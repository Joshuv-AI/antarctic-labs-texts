Protocol for dorsal ovariectomy and elastase-induced abdominal aortic aneurysm in rats
Authors: Mohamed S. Zaghloul, Santiago Elizondo-Benedetto, Louai Alrata, Dina Ibrahim, Nicholas Speller, Bera Koklu, Batool Arif, Mohamed A. Zayed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryWomen have increased abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) growth rates and a higher rupture risk, yet hormonal effects remain unclear. We present a protocol combining dorsal ovariectomy with elastase-induced AAA formation in female rats. We describe bilateral dorsal ovariectomy, a 42-day hormone depletion period, and ventral elastase infusion for AAA induction. This approach prevents adhesions encountered with traditional ventral techniques, increasing experimental feasibility and success.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Sastriques-Dunlop et al.1

Before you begin

Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA) is a life-threatening disease involving progressive dilation of the abdominal aorta, resulting in 150,000-200,000 deaths/year globally and >15,000 deaths/year in the US alone.2 Known as a silent killer, AAA has a subtle onset and remains asymptomatic in up to 80-90% of patients until rupture, which carries an 85% mortality rate even after acute intervention.3 With regard to gender, AAA is four-fold more prevalent in males; however, AAAs in women are more severe with increased growth rate and higher mortality due to rupture.4 In fact, rupture occurs at smaller diameters in women, is four times more likely during surveillance, and has a three times greater likelihood of mortality.5 The exact mechanisms underlying this increased severity in women remain unclear but potentially involve differences in aortic wall composition, hormonal influences, and smaller baseline aortic diameters leading to rupture at lower absolute sizes.

Several approaches have been used in rat models to explore sex-related hormonal differences, including pharmacological inhibition of estrogen and ovariectomy.4,6,7,8 Ovariectomy offers several advantages including increased reliability, reproducibility, reduced experimental cost, and fewer confounding off-target effects compared to pharmacologic inhibitors. Ovariectomy models for AAA in mice and rats typically use a ventral approach, which creates peritoneal and retroperitoneal adhesions around the AAA and decreases experimental feasibility in subsequent AAA procedures.4 Our protocol presents a dorsal ovariectomy approach in a female rat AAA model to ameliorate these issues.

This protocol describes a specific technique for dorsal ovariectomy combined with elastase-induced AAA formation in adult female Sprague-Dawley rats. While developed for this rat strain, the approach may be adapted for other rat strains with appropriate adjustments for anatomical differences.

Institutional permissions

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis (Protocol ID: 22-0178). The rats were housed at 21°C in a 12/12 hour light/dark cycle and had access to food and water ad libitum. During the surgical procedures, the core body temperature was monitored and maintained at 37°C–38°C using an electronic heating pad. At the conclusion of the studies, live animals were euthanized following IACUC-approved protocols.

Before conducting any animal experiments using this protocol, researchers must obtain approval from their own IACUC and adhere to all relevant guidelines and regulations for the ethical use of animals in research.

Anesthesia and analgesia

Timing: 15 min 7.Prepare isoflurane anesthesia system:a.Fill the isoflurane vaporizer with isoflurane.b.Connect the oxygen supply to the anesthesia system. The system should deliver 3–4% isoflurane in 100% oxygen at 1 L/min for induction, and 1.5%–2% isoflurane in 100% oxygen at 0.5 L/min for maintenance.c.Set up the induction chamber and the nose cone on a clean surface.d.Test the anesthesia system to ensure proper functioning.8.Prepare analgesic agents:a.Buprenorphine SR (sustained-release, 1 mg/kg) for subcutaneous (SC) injection: Use the stock solution at the appropriate concentration for subcutaneous injection. Store buprenorphine at room temperature (20°C–25°C) in a locked, controlled substance cabinet protected from light. Once opened, mark the expiration date according to institutional guidelines. Buprenorphine SR stock concentration: 1 mg/mL. For 1 mg/kg dosing, administer 1 mL/kg body weight subcutaneously (e.g., 250 g rat = 0.25 mL or 250 μL; 300 g rat = 0.30 mL or 300 μL). CRITICAL: Do not dilute the buprenorphine stock solution to maintain consistency in dosing.

β-aminopropionitrile preparation

•Prepare a 0.3% (w/v) β-aminopropionitrile (BAPN) solution by dissolving BAPN in the rats' drinking water.•Begin BAPN treatment three days prior to AAA induction surgery and continue until tissue harvest at 14 days post-AAA induction, providing it as the sole drinking source with free access. Rats typically consume 20–30 mL/day.•Store prepared solution in a refrigerator and prepare fresh weekly.

Ovariectomy procedure

Timing: 1 week

AAA induction (42 days post-ovariectomy)

Timing: 75 min

Timing: 75 min

The following steps outline the procedure for inducing AAAs in rats using porcine pancreatic elastase (PPE). This protocol should be performed 42 days after the ovariectomy surgery to ensure the depletion of endogenous estrogen levels. The 42-day (6-week) waiting period serves two critical purposes: (1) it ensures complete estrogen depletion, as circulating estrogen levels reach their nadir approximately 4-6 weeks post-ovariectomy in rats based on previous studies,4,9 and (2) it allows rats that undergo ovariectomy at 2 weeks of age to reach 8 weeks of age for AAA induction, which is the standard age for this procedure and enables direct comparison with male rats if needed.

Estrogen depletion is best confirmed through anatomical verification: (1) during ovariectomy surgery, carefully examine the ovaries, oviducts, and uterine horns to ensure complete ovarian transection, and (2) during AAA surgery, briefly examine the oviducts and uterine horns to confirm absence of ovarian remnants. While not necessary for this protocol, additional confirmation methods such as histological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Figure 5) or serum estrogen testing may be valuable for projects requiring more rigorous hormonal verification. Expected outcomes

Following successful completion of this protocol, researchers can expect several key outcomes that validate the effectiveness of the combined dorsal ovariectomy and elastase-induced AAA model. By Day 7 post-ovariectomy, the surgical incisions should show signs of proper healing, with no evidence of infection, dehiscence, or excessive inflammation. By Day 14, the ovariectomy incisions should be fully healed, with minimal scaring and no signs of complications.

The elastase infusion procedure should produce consistent and reproducible infrarenal AAAs in ovariectomized rats. Based on our previous experience with elastase-induced AAA models, the PPE perfusion should result in a localized dilation of the abdominal aorta, with an increase in aortic diameter of up to 100% compared to baseline measurements.1,10 The induced AAAs should be consistently located in the infrarenal segment of the abdominal aorta, between the renal arteries and the iliac bifurcation. The success rate of AAA induction is similar between ovariectomized and sham-operated groups (approximately 90%–95% in both groups). However, ovariectomized rats develop significantly larger fusiform AAAs with greater aortic diameter expansion compared to sham-operated controls, demonstrating the protective role of estrogen against AAA progression.

Transabdominal ultrasound assessments on Day 7 and Day 14 post-AAA induction should reveal a progressive increase in AAA diameter compared to the initial post-induction measurements. The AAAs should maintain a fusiform shape, with a smooth, regular appearance on ultrasound imaging. Blood flow within the aneurysmal segment should remain patent, with no evidence of thrombosis or occlusion as confirmed by ultrasound.

Upon surgical exposure of the abdominal aorta on Day 14, the presence of a distinct, localized aneurysmal dilation should be visually evident. The maximum AAA diameter measured using microscope software should be significantly greater than the baseline aortic diameter, consistent with the ultrasound findings.

The surgical procedures (ovariectomy and AAA induction) should be well-tolerated by the animals, with a low incidence of intraoperative and postoperative complications. Postoperative mortality should be minimal, with most rats surviving until the designated endpoints (Day 7 and Day 14) for assessment and tissue harvesting. By achieving these expected outcomes, the protocol will provide a reliable and reproducible model for studying the development, progression, and pathophysiology of AAAs in the context of estrogen deficiency. The generated data and tissue samples will be valuable for investigating the underlying mechanisms and potential therapeutic interventions for this critical vascular condition. The actual values for AAA diameter, growth rates, and complication frequencies may vary depending on factors such as animal age, strain, and specific experimental conditions. Appropriate controls (e.g., sham-operated or non-ovariectomized animals) should be included to contextualize the findings and account for potential confounding variables.

Limitations

While this protocol provides a valuable tool for studying AAAs in the context of estrogen deficiency, there are several limitations to consider:

Surgical complexity and skill requirements: The ovariectomy and AAA induction procedures described in this protocol are technically challenging and require a high level of surgical expertise. In particular, creating the aortotomy for PPE infusion is a delicate step that demands precision and care. Any mistakes or inaccuracies during this process can lead to inadequate pressurization of the AAA, compromising the model’s validity and reproducibility. Slight variations in surgical technique, such as the placement of ligatures or the control of PPE infusion pressure, can also influence the outcomes and introduce variability between animals and experiments. To mitigate these risks, extensive training and practice are crucial for surgeons performing these procedures. Standardization of techniques and the use of experienced personnel can help minimize operator-dependent effects and maintain high success rates.

Influence of anesthesia on physiological parameters: The repeated anesthesia sessions required for ultrasound assessments and surgical procedures may have an impact on the animals' vital signs, such as blood pressure and heart rate. These alterations in physiological parameters could potentially influence the development and progression of AAAs, as well as the overall health and recovery of the animals. Researchers should be aware of these potential confounding effects and take appropriate measures to monitor and control for them, such as including sham-operated controls and carefully recording vital signs throughout the experimental period.

Limited assessment time points: The protocol specifies two main assessment time points (Day 7 and Day 14 post-AAA induction), which may not fully capture the dynamic nature of AAA progression and remodeling. While these time points provide valuable insights into the early stages of AAA development, they may not reflect the long-term changes and potential complications associated with the condition. Additional intermediate or long-term assessments could offer a more comprehensive understanding of the aneurysm development process and the impact of estrogen deficiency over time. Researchers should consider incorporating further time points or extending the study duration, depending on their specific research questions and objectives.

Despite these limitations, the protocol offers a novel and potentially valuable approach for investigating AAAs in the context of estrogen deficiency. By combining dorsal ovariectomy with the well-established PPE infusion model, this protocol aims to provide new insights into the interplay between hormonal factors and AAA pathophysiology. Careful experimental design, appropriate control groups, and cautious interpretation of the findings can help mitigate the limitations and enhance the value of the generated data. Researchers should be aware of these constraints when planning experiments and drawing conclusions from the results obtained using this protocol.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Difficulty locating or isolating the ovaries during ovariectomy.

Potential solution

Use gentle, blunt dissection techniques to carefully navigate through the adipose tissue and identify the ovaries. Ensure adequate lighting and magnification to visualize the anatomical structures clearly. If needed, consider using a surgical microscope or magnifying loupes to assist in the identification and isolation of the ovaries.

Problem 2

Excessive bleeding during ovariectomy.

Potential solution

Pay close attention to the vasculature surrounding the ovaries and use careful dissection techniques to avoid damaging blood vessels. Ensure proper ligation of the ovarian arteries and veins using appropriate suture materials and techniques. If excessive bleeding occurs, apply gentle pressure with a sterile gauze or cotton swab and use electrocautery or hemostatic agents to achieve hemostasis.

Problem 3

Difficulty creating the aortotomy for PPE infusion.

Potential solution

Use a well-maintained, sharp 30-gauge needle to create the aortotomy. If the aortotomy is not large enough, carefully dilate it using the tips of sharp, fine forceps. Avoid using scissors, as they may damage the posterior aortic wall or cause leakage of the infused PPE. Practice the technique on non-survival animals to refine your skills.

Problem 4

Inadequate AAA pressurization during PPE infusion.

Potential solution

Ensure that the catheter is securely placed within the aortotomy and that there are no leaks or gaps allowing the PPE solution to escape. Use an electronic infusion pump to precisely control the infusion rate and maintain constant pressure during the infusion. Continuously monitor the aortic diameter and infusion pressure to ensure adequate dilation.

Problem 5

Premature aneurysm rupture during PPE infusion.

Potential solution

Carefully monitor the aortic diameter and infusion pressure during the infusion process. Use an electronic infusion pump to precisely control the infusion rate and pressure. If signs of impending rupture are observed (e.g., rapid expansion, thinning of the aortic wall), immediately stop the infusion and proceed with the closure of the aortotomy.

Problem 6

Difficulty visualizing the AAA during ultrasound assessments.

Potential solution

Ensure that the animal is properly positioned and that the abdominal area is shaved and cleaned to optimize ultrasound transmission. Use a high-frequency transducer and adjust the focal depth to improve image quality. Gently apply pressure to displace overlying structures and obtain clearer views of the AAA.

Problem 7

Poor wound healing or infection following surgery.

Potential solution

Maintain strict aseptic techniques throughout the surgical procedures, including proper sterilization of instruments and materials. Place the rats in clean cages post-surgery and change the cages regularly. If any complications are observed, such as inflammation or discharge, apply a local antibiotic cream and consult the animal facilities staff. If the condition worsens, euthanize the animal as needed.

Problem 8

Hematoma formation due to improper ligation of the oviduct.

Potential solution

Ensure proper ligation of the oviduct and carefully inspect for any small bleeding branches before returning the uterine horn to the abdominal cavity. Monitor the animal for signs of hematoma formation, such as swelling or changes in skin color. If a hematoma is suspected, perform a ultrasound examination and consider surgical intervention if necessary.

Problem 9

Hind limb ischemia or tail necrosis due to inadequate removal of elastase or air bubbles.

Potential solution

Thoroughly squeeze the aorta to remove any residual elastase and air bubbles before closing the aortotomy. Maintain the animal’s body temperature using a heating pad during the procedure, but avoid overheating, which can alter blood pressure. Monitor the animal for signs of hind limb ischemia or tail necrosis post-surgery.

Problem 10

Inadequate aneurysmal shape post-PPE infusion.

Potential solution

Ensure adequate placement and tightening of the proximal and distal aortic ligations. Verify that all aortic branches within the isolated segment are properly ligated. Inspect the aortotomy for any leaks or punctures in the aortic wall. If necessary, gently tighten the ligations and continue the infusion while monitoring for improvements in aneurysmal shape.

Problem 11

Weight loss after surgery.

Potential solution

If significant weight loss is observed post-surgery, anesthetize the rat and examine the abdomen for signs of inflammation or internal bleeding. Transfer the rat to a clean cage and ensure an adequate supply of food and water. House the rat in a separate cage to minimize stress and competition for resources. Monitor the rat’s weight and overall health closely and consider euthanasia if the condition does not improve.

By addressing these specific problems and providing targeted solutions, researchers can more effectively troubleshoot issues that may arise during the execution of this protocol. Regular monitoring, attention to detail, and adherence to best practices can help minimize the occurrence of these complications and ensure the success of the experiments.
