Effects of dopaminergic medication on upper limb motor function, strength and hand dexterity in people with Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Parisa Alaei, Daryl J. Wile, Jennifer M. Jakobi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Dopaminergic medication improves affected upper limb muscle strength, and hand dexterity in people with Parkinson’s disease.•Motor symptom severity was linked to pegboard performance during OFF medication.•Muscle strength is important for hand dexterity in people with Parkinson’s Disease.

Introduction

Motor symptoms of the upper and lower limbs have distinctive effects on functional abilities in persons with Parkinson's disease (PD). Considering lower limb functional measures, strength accounts for 18 % to 24 % of the variability in gait performance, balance, and chair-rise time [1]. Although many patients present with motor symptoms affecting the upper limbs, and the differential effect of the disease on extensor and flexor muscle strength is well established [2], the role of muscle strength on upper limb functional ability remains less understood.

Dopaminergic medications, such as levodopa, effectively control motor symptoms but many patients struggle with motor symptom response fluctuations [3,4]. In the upper limb, muscle strength is reduced during the “OFF” phase compared with “ON” phase [2,5]. However, these studies did not assess voluntary activation, which measures the efficacy of activating muscles during an isometric maximum voluntary contraction (MVC). Thus, the decrease in maximal strength may arise from perceptions associated with symptoms during the OFF phase, or a physiological inability to activate muscle. Comprehensive neuromuscular measures, including MVC, voluntary activation and twitch contractile properties, offer an opportunity to study effects of dopaminergic treatment on muscle strength, activation and functional ability in people with PD.

The clinical correlates of motor symptoms in an “ON” and “OFF” phase of medication can be assessed using instruments such as the Movement Disorder Society revision of the Unified Parkinson Disease Rating Scale motor subscore (MDS-UPDRS III). This scale provides a comprehensive measure of tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia in the limbs, as well as axial motor functions, including gait and balance dysfunction [6]. In addition to motor symptom scores, upper limb motor performance can be measured in people with PD using pegboard dexterity tasks [7]. The Grooved Pegboard Test (GPT) and the Purdue Pegboard Test (PPT) are widely used assessments for manual and bimanual dexterity in neurological conditions, and correlate with progression of motor symptom deficits [8,9]. The GPT captures upper-limb control, rigidity, and bradykinesia [10] and is correlated to dopaminergic denervation in people with mild to moderate PD symptoms [11]. The PPT is utilized to assess movement of the arm, hand, and fingers [7,12,13]. Comparative performance ON and OFF dopaminergic treatment in people with PD have not been reported. By combining these assessments of motor symptom deficits with neuromuscular activity and hand dexterity tests, we can gain deeper understanding of the effects of the ON and OFF phases of antiparkinsonian medication on upper limb function.

The aim of this study was to explore the influence of dopaminergic medication in persons with PD through investigation of upper limb muscle strength, contractile function and pegboard test performance in the more affected side. We expected medication-related improvements in motor symptom deficits, strength, and hand dexterity (measured by the pegboard tests).

Methods

Participants

All participants were diagnosed with PD and on neurologist prescribed levodopa-carbidopa medication. Participants were recruited from Parkinson’s Disease support groups, as well as advertisement in the Kelowna Movement Disorder Clinic. Participants completed two identical sessions, in randomized order, ON and OFF medication. The ON phase was evaluated at least 60–90 min after participants took their physician prescribed levodopa dose, whereas the OFF phase followed a minimum 12-hour withdrawal from dopaminergic medication. The MDS-UPDRS-III was assessed for each participant in the ON and OFF medication phase, including a total score and a score specific to the self-reported more affected upper limb. The Hoehn and Yahr rating was performed in the OFF phase. Individuals with cardiac conditions, any recent major injuries, or surgeries to the limb being tested within the last six months, or neurological disorders other than PD were excluded. This study received approval through the harmonized review (H22-02896) for the Clinical Research Ethics Board of the University of British Columbia and the Interior Health and conformed to the standards set by the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to starting the study.

Experimental set-up and procedure

To undergo neuromuscular testing, participants were seated in a custom-built elbow flexion dynamometer. The chair was set such that the feet of the participants were supported on a flat surface, the knees and hips flexed to 90°, the shoulder was slightly abducted (∼15°), and the elbow was flexed to 110°. Participants gripped a manipulandum with the hand of the self-reported more affected side while their forearm rested on a padded support in a neutral forearm orientation. A non-distensible strap was used to secure the participants into the chair to minimize extraneous movement. The force measurements were displayed in real-time on a 52 cm monitor positioned 1 m in front of the participant with the center of the screen at eye level (Fig. 1a). Two 4 cm × 4 cm carbon–carbon stimulation electrodes were coated in electrode gel (Parker Spectra 360 Electrode Gel, Parker Lab Inc., Fairfield, NJ, USA) and positioned over the proximal and distal aspects of the more affected biceps brachii muscle belly. A constant voltage stimulator (DS7AH: Digitimer. Ltd., Welwyn Garden City, Hertfordshire, UK) was used to deliver square wave pulses (200 µs) for the evaluation of biceps brachii contractile properties and voluntary activation [14]. To determine the stimulation intensity, the electrical current was adjusted while monitoring muscle response through palpation. The threshold was monitored through noticeable contraction of antagonist muscles or when force decreased with an increasing current. The intensity was then increased by 10 % to ensure supramaximal stimulation. Ten single pulses were delivered at the supramaximal intensity approximately one second apart to evoke twitches at rest.

To perform MVC, participants were instructed to pull-up as hard and as fast as possible against the chair manipulandum. They practiced MVCs without stimulation, and then performed three to five isometric elbow flexion MVCs, each held for approximately 5 s with three electrical stimuli applied preceding the MVC while the participant was relaxed (resting twitch), during the MVC (interpolated twitch), and following the MVC (post MVC). Each MVC was separated by 2 min of rest.

Manual dexterity tests

The manual dexterity tasks included GPT and PPT. The GPT (Lafayette Instruments, 32025, Lafayette, IN) consists of a pegboard with 25 slotted key-shaped holes arranged in various orientations in a 5 x 5 grid. Participants inserted the key-shaped pegs into these holes. The most affected side was measured. When using their right hand, the participants performed the task left to right, beginning with the top row. If using their left hand, the directionality was from right to left commencing with the top row, and the duration of time to place the pegs was recorded. The PPT Lafayette Instruments, #32020, Lafayette, IN) consists of a board with two vertically aligned parallel rows of 25 circular holes each and small metallic pegs. The pegs were placed in the outer cup on the top side being tested, and participants were instructed to remove the pegs individually from the cup and place them into the holes from top to bottom as quickly as possible. The number of pegs placed in 30 s was recorded.

Data analysis

Twitch contractile properties were analyzed offline (Spike 2, v10.04 software, CED, Cambridge, England) (Fig. 1b), and quantified as: Peak tension (maximum force amplitude of the twitch); Time to peak tension (time duration from the rise in twitch force to its peak); Half relaxation time (time from peak tension to when the twitch force amplitude decreases to half of its maximum); and Contraction duration (sum of TPT and HRT). MVC was measured as the highest amplitude of force output achieved, corresponding to the voluntary activation reported. Normalized MVC is calculated by dividing the MVC by the individual's body weight.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 26 (SPSS Ver 26, IBM, Amrok, NY, USA). Shapiro-Wilks normality test demonstrated that the data were normally distributed, and thus paired t-tests were used to determine the influence of dopaminergic medication on the measures of PD motor symptom deficit, neuromuscular properties, and hand dexterity measures in the most affected upper limb of persons with PD. Pearson correlations were conducted to examine explanatory variables between hand dexterity scores and MVC, normalized MVC, and MDS-UPDRS III scores. To correct for multiple comparisons the dependent variables that were independent of each other (voluntary activation, post activation potentiation and twitch contractile properties) a Benjamin-Hochberg false discover rate (FDR) correction procedure was applied to control for Type 1 error. The degree of correlation coefficient was interpreted as follows: very strong (0.90–1.00), strong (0.70–0.89), moderate (0.40–0.69), weak (0.10–0.39), and negligible (0.0–0.10). The probability level for statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 and values are reported as means ± standard deviations.

Discussion

Strength, contractile properties and hand dexterity in the more affected upper limb of people with PD were compared between the ON and OFF phases of dopaminergic medication. Although the motor symptom scores (27 %), and hand dexterity tasks improved (11 % GPT; 34 % PPT) as well as strength increased (4 % elbow flexor MVC), electrically induced contractile properties did not differ between ON and OFF phase of medication. Normalized strength had a moderate correlation with hand dexterity OFF and ON medication. Performance on the GPT and PPT had a moderate correlation with motor symptom deficit in the OFF phase but not in the ON phase.

Neuromuscular properties

Consistent with findings from lower limb studies [1], both elbow flexion MVC and normalized MVC were significantly higher ON versus OFF medication. The use of peripheral nerve stimulation to assess both twitch contractile properties and post-activation potentiation in the upper limb ON and OFF medication is novel. In our study, treatment phase did not influence these neuromuscular properties (PT, TPT, HRT, CD and post activation potentiation), suggesting that observed changes in strength ON medication are not due to peripheral muscle alterations but a centrally mediated phenomenon.

Measuring voluntary activation through peripheral stimulation offers a level of surety that the MVC was executed and measured during optimal drive [15]. There were no differences in voluntary activation ON and OFF medication for elbow flexion, distinct from the reduction in voluntary activation of knee extensors seen by Folland et al. [16] Increased upper limb MVC in our study was not due to inadequate voluntary activation OFF medication or due to increased voluntary activation ON relative to OFF medication. Differences between studies might arise from muscle groups studied or the well-established limitations with voluntary activation, inclusive of the stimulation protocol, calculation used, patient tolerability to stimulation, and the sensitivity of superimposed twitch amplitude measures [[17], [18], [19]]. Future studies should consider the use of voluntary activation to enhance comprehensive understanding of strength production in persons with PD between lower and upper limbs.

Dopaminergic medication was associated with a significant increase in elbow flexor MVC. The locus of influence of medication on strength cannot be ascertained from this study; factors may include increased neural drive to the motoneuron pool or enhanced spinal motor neuron excitability, as observed in animal models [20]. This increase in neural excitability may culminate in adaptations in spinal motor neuron output [21] and positively impact voluntary force through adaptations in muscle coordination, motor unit recruitment or activity across the pool of motoneurons. These nuances cannot be ascertained with voluntary activation and require consideration in future research.

In this study we normalized MVC to address differences in body composition between sexes (n = 5 females; n = 11 males), and between persons (e.g. due to exercise participation, physical labour) both of which impact strength. This allowed a comprehensive assessment of the relationship of dopaminergic medication, MVC and functional measures of manual dexterity. A significant improvement in pegboard test scores was observed ON treatment. Festini et al. [22] demonstrated improved GPT performance during the ON phase of the more affected side; we additionally measure effects on the PPT. Muscle strength increased 1–17 % in people with PD, and was further associated with improved pegboard scores. Naturally, elbow flexion strength contributes to performance on such tasks and is known to have functional impact in PD [1].

Disease severity

As a clinical measure of motor symptom severity, MDS-UPDRS III scores improved, as expected, ON treatment; however, in contrast to MVC we did not see correlation of ON phase MDS-UPDRS III score with GPT or PPT performance. This aligns with previous research by Sage et al. [10] in mild to moderate PD using grooved pegboard; however, the relationship was not explored during the OFF phase, or with the PPT. Herein we observed moderate correlation with the GPT and PPT in the OFF phase. This highlights how many processes contribute to complex motor task performance, and clinical tests may capture some elements but may not effectively measure others. We note the possibility that the central drive to activate the motoneuron pool may have variable treatment responsiveness in PD, and may contribute to relatively ‘levodopa unresponsive’ symptoms and poorer functional treatment response for some people with PD. The reasons for these variations may, in part, relate to central effects of dopaminergic medication on strength that are not well measured with current clinical tools and deserve further study.

Attention and cognition are important factors affecting hand-dexterity influenced by dopaminergic medication [23] but was not directly assessed in our study. This may be illustrated by comparing modestly increased performance on the GPT (11 %) and substantial improvement in PPT (34 %) OFF versus ON medication. Differences include the need to rotate pegs into position before placement in the GPT that elevates cognitive load relative to the PPT [12,24,25]. The PPT involves placing round pegs into round holes, which lessens the cognitive challenge associated to orientation.

Conclusion

Antiparkinsonian medication improves motor symptom deficits and also improves upper limb muscle strength, and hand dexterity. However, there is more nuance in how medication related to functional tasks like pegboard test performance between ON and OFF medication. Functional benefits of dopaminergic medication may relate to subject specific effects of medication on strength, central drive of motor output, and cognition and attention. The association of muscle strength with both the GPT and PPT both OFF and ON medication suggests that strength is an important element of upper limb functional ability in persons with Parkinson’s disease irrespective of whether they are ON or OFF treatment. The modest sample size is a limitation of this study that may restrict the detection of more subtle effects. The absence of formal cognitive assessment (e.g., MoCA, MMSE) also limits determination of cognition on manual dexterity performance; however, the patterns observed provide a strong foundation for future investigation of cognitive contributions to manual dexterity ON and OFF prescribed levodopa in persons with PD. Assessments in this study focused on the clinically more affected upper limb, and hand dominance was not specifically accounted for. Motor deficits in PD are generally more pronounced on the affected side regardless of dominance, and thus the potential influence of handedness on motor performance cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies should further explore whether hand dominance modulates these outcomes.
