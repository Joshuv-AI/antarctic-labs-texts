Comparison of essential drug lists: concordance, differences, and the need for core lists in times of crisis
Authors: Lilly Josephine Bindel, Roland Seifert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prioritization of essential drugs is important for rational pharmacotherapy, public health, drug availability, and medical education. While the Model List of Essential Medicines (EML) of the World Health Organization (WHO) is considered the international reference, other drug lists focus on supply-related or educational purposes. Seven drug lists were compared. Drug names were extracted, harmonized, and analysed for their presence on each list. Assessed were the number of listings per drug, the concordance between the lists, and the comparison of the EML 2025 with other lists. In addition, frequently prescribed drugs in Germany were compared with the summarized drug list. In total, 1061 single drugs were identified. Most drugs were listed on one to three drug lists (n = 824; 77.6%), whereas 49 drugs were listed on six or seven lists. These highly concordant drugs mainly covered broad-use symptomatic care, long-term treatment of chronic disorders, and emergency situations. Several frequently prescribed drugs and relevant indication areas were not represented within the highly concordant core. Essentiality strongly depends on the purpose, scope, and regional context of a drug list. Broad concordance can support the relevance and recognition of established drugs across different contexts, but does not prove therapeutic value or essentiality. Therefore, comparison with other drug lists provides an additional perspective, but should be combined with drug-specific assessment of therapeutic value, safety, prescription relevance, feasibility, and global need. Especially in times of crisis, effort is needed for the development of national and international core essential drug lists.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00210-026-05698-8.

Introduction

The choice of effective, appropriate, and comparably safe drugs for a certain condition is a key principle of rational pharmacotherapy (Maxwell 2009; Tahvanainen et al. 2024). However, because of the large variety of available drugs and treatment options, it is not always simple to choose the most beneficial drug (Kim and Prasad 2021). In addition, the increasing availability of newly developed and often expensive drugs makes rational pharmacotherapy not only a question of individual treatment decisions but also highly relevant for the whole healthcare system and public health (Ghinea 2024; Rajkumar 2020; WHO 2026).

The large number of available drugs raises the question of which medicines are most essential and should therefore be prioritized (WHO 2025a). This is important in regard to ensuring their availability, preventing supply shortages, supporting rational prescribing, and educating medical professionals about clinically relevant drugs. Therefore, it is necessary to identify drugs with high therapeutic value, broad clinical relevance, or particular importance for severe or frequent diseases. Such drugs have to be recognized not only for individual patient care but also for drug supply, medical education, and preparedness of health care systems.

The concept of essential medicines was introduced by the World Health Organization (WHO) to support the selection of medicines that satisfy the global healthcare needs. The WHO Model List of Essential Medicines (EML) was first published in 1977 and has since been regularly revised and expanded according to changing disease burdens, therapeutic standards, and public health priorities (WHO 2025a, 2025b). It is considered the international reference list for the selection of essential medicines and provides a global framework for national medicine selection, supply, availability, and rational pharmacotherapy (Peacocke et al. 2022; WHO 2025a). However, multiple other drug lists have been created for national, regional, supply-related, or educational purposes. These lists may partly overlap with the EML but may also differ because of their specific scope, regional focus, or intended use.

In previous analyses, our research group evaluated the evolution and the appropriateness and therapeutic value of long-retained drugs of the EML (Bindel and Seifert 2026a, 2026b). In the present analysis, seven drug lists with essential, supply-related, or educational purposes were compared to identify which drugs are recognized as essential or particularly relevant in multiple settings. This comparison allows the identification of drugs with high concordance across lists with different purposes, which may indicate broad clinical and public health relevance. Furthermore, the drug lists were compared with the EML 2025 (WHO 2025b) as reference list to provide an additional perspective on the EML drug listings. By this approach, drugs already listed on the EML can be supported by their presence on other lists, drugs listed only on the EML can be identified for critical re-evaluation, and drugs not listed on the EML but included in several other drug lists can be recognized as possible candidates for future assessment. Thus, the aim of the present analysis was to identify highly concordant drugs, to assess the agreement of the EML 2025 with the other analysed drug lists, and to evaluate whether cross-list concordance may support the identification of drugs for a standardized key essential drug list. Especially in times of crisis—war, supply shortages, epidemics, and increasing healthcare costs, a transparent procedure for defining key essential drugs may be important for rational pharmacotherapy, medical education, supply security, and healthcare preparedness.

Methods

Data setting and scope

The present analysis compared seven drug lists created by governmental organizations, non-governmental organizations, professional societies, and expert consensus. Included were (A) the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines 2025 (EML) (WHO 2025b), (B) the list of supply-relevant drugs from the German Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (BfArM) (BfArM 2026), (C) the drug and biological essential medicines list based on Executive Order 13,944 from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) (FDA 2022), (D) the drug list of the IMPP/MFT for the second state examination in medical studies in Germany (IMPP 2024), (E) the list of indispensable drugs from the German Society of Internal Medicine (DGIM) (DGIM 2026), (F) the UK core drug list from Baker et al. The selection of these seven drug lists was based on their different purposes and their direct relevance for the present comparison. The EML 2025 (WHO 2025b) was included as the international reference list for essential medicines. The BfArM (2026), FDA (2022), and DGIM (2026) lists were included because they focus on supply-relevant or indispensable drugs in national health care contexts. The IMPP (2024), Baker et al. (2010), and Donker et al. (2023) lists were included because they represent drug lists created for medical education and training. Thus, the included drug lists covered the three relevant perspectives of global essentiality, supply assurance, and medical education. Other national essential drug lists, particularly from LMICs, were not included, since the aim of this study was not to perform a global comparison of national EML adaptations, but to compare drug lists with different scopes and purposes. We acknowledge that this selection resulted in a stronger representation of high-income country contexts, particularly Germany, Europe, and the United States.

The scope of the analysis was to compare drugs that are considered essential or particularly relevant in terms of global need, supply assurance, or educational purposes. In this context, educational purposes were defined as the selection of drugs that should be known by medical students, young physicians, or prescribers because of their relevance for pharmacological understanding, prescribing, clinical decision-making, or examination requirements. For each drug, it was assessed whether it was present or absent on each of the included drug lists. Based on this, the number of drug lists on which each drug was included was counted. This allowed the identification of drugs listed on only one drug list, drugs shared by several lists, and drugs with high concordance across most or all compared lists.

Data extraction and analysis

The listed drugs of each drug list were extracted manually by one author (L.J.B.) and, if necessary, translated into English. Afterwards, drug names were harmonized by summarizing synonyms and removing duplicate listings. Spelling variants and synonyms were merged when it could be ensured that they referred to the same drug, with synonyms provided in parentheses. Salt forms were summarized when they referred to the same active drug, e.g. lithium and lithium carbonate. In cases of uncertainty whether two drug names or drug forms referred to the same drug, they were retained as separate entries. Duplicate listings caused by different doses, formulations, routes of administration, or application forms were not counted separately. Fixed-dose combinations (FDCs) were separated into their individual drugs to avoid counting both as single substances and as combination products. For example, artemether + lumefantrine was included as artemether and lumefantrine, and hydrochlorothiazide + valsartan was included as hydrochlorothiazide and valsartan. If one of the drugs was also listed separately, the duplicate entry was removed. FDCs with a distinct established name were retained as one entry, e.g. cotrimoxazole.

For the EML 2025 (WHO 2025b), all drugs from the main Model List of Essential Medicines were included, while alternative drugs mentioned within the sections were not included as independent listings. Similarly, for the IMPP/MFT drug list (IMPP 2024), only the representative drugs were included, while additionally mentioned alternatives were not counted separately. For the FDA list (FDA 2022), the device section was excluded. For Baker et al. (2010), the listed commonly prescribed drugs were allocated and included in the comparison.

After extraction and harmonization, a combined drug summary was created. For each drug, the presence on the seven drug lists was documented and the total number of listings was calculated. The resulting dataset was used to describe the extent and overlap of the drug lists, to identify and evaluate highly concordant drugs listed on six or seven drug lists, and to assess the concordance of the EML 2025 (WHO 2025b) with the other analysed drug lists. Concordance was interpreted as an indicator of repeated recognition across different drug lists, but not as a proof for essentiality. Therefore, high concordance was not considered to replace drug-specific assessment of therapeutic value, safety, disease burden, affordability, feasibility, supply vulnerability, and public health need.

To ensure consistent terminology, the term “drug lists” was used for all analysed lists included in the present study. The term “Essential Medicines List” was used when referring specifically to the WHO EML. The term “key essential drug list” was used when referring to the need for a standardized list of particularly relevant drugs for medical education, supply assurance, and crisis preparedness.

Comparison with prescription relevance

To additionally relate cross-list concordance to current prescription relevance, drugs from the TOP30 drug preparations with highest prescription numbers in Germany according to the 'Drug Prescription Report 2025' (Ludwig et al. 2026) were compared with the summarized drug list. This comparison was included as an illustrative national example and was not intended to represent global prescription relevance. For each of these drugs, it was assessed whether it was included in the combined dataset and how many of the seven analysed drug lists contained the respective drug. This comparison was used to identify frequently prescribed drugs that were also highly concordant, as well as frequently prescribed drugs that did not reach the threshold of six or seven listings.

Results

Purpose of analysed drug lists

In the analysis, seven drug lists were included (Table 1). Although all lists aim to prioritize drugs considered important or essential in their specific context, they differ in their extent, scope, regional coverage, and revision period.

Regarding the scope of the drug lists, most included drug lists focused on the selection of essential medicines for ensuring their availability and supply. While the EML 2025 (WHO 2025) focuses on a global selection of drugs as a recommendation for national authorities, the drug lists from the BfArM (2026), the DGIM (2026), and the FDA (2022) focus on a country-specific prioritization, for Germany and the United States, respectively. Thus, these lists mainly reflect public health, supply security, and national or international prioritization of drugs. The second group of drug lists focuses on medical education, with the selection of the most important drugs that are particularly relevant for medical professionals. This includes the drug list of the IMPP/MFT (IMPP 2024) for the second state examination in medical studies in Germany, the list by Baker et al. (2010) on medical education in the UK, and the list of Donker et al. (2023) covering the whole of Europe. These educational lists therefore have a narrower scope, since they aim to define drugs that should be known by medical students or young physicians, rather than drugs that are primarily selected for supply-related reasons. However, no drug list gives a prioritized core list of drugs, although two drug lists, including EML 2025 (WHO 2025b) and IMPP (2024), provide exemplary drugs along with possible alternatives.

Some of the lists are updated regularly, including the EML every two years, and the drug lists of the DGIM and BfArM every year. The drug list for the medical state examination from the IMPP (2024) is required to be revised every two years. In contrast, the drug list of the FDA (2022) was created because of an executive order of the United States government, without the requirement of regular updating. The drug lists for medical education from Donker et al. (2023) and Baker et al. (2010) were created by a Delphi consensus, without a specific revision date or regular update interval being defined. For all drug lists, it was stated that no conflict of interest is present.

The majority of drugs were listed within one drug list only (n = 431; 40.5%), while only 11 drugs were found on all compared lists. Having a look at the distribution, the majority of drugs were included within one to three drug lists (n = 824; 77.6%), a moderate number on four to five lists (n = 187; 17.6%), and only a minor part on six or all drug lists (n = 49; 4.6%). (2023) showed the lowest share of unique drugs, with 4.8%. Thus, the FDA (2022) list contained a comparatively large proportion of drugs with low concordance, while the Donker et al. The EML 2025 showed a broad distribution across the different levels of concordance with the other drug lists. Overall, 482 drugs of the EML 2025 were included in the comparison. Of these, 97 drugs were only listed on the EML 2025 (one drug list), corresponding to 20.1% of all included EML drugs. In contrast, 385 drugs were also found on at least one other drug list, with 49 EML drugs being listed on six or seven of the compared drug lists.

Regarding the proportional distribution, the highest shares were found for drugs listed on three or four drug lists, with 21.0% and 21.8%, respectively. In contrast, 11.0% of EML drugs were listed on five drug lists, 7.5% on six drug lists, and only 2.3% were found on all seven drug lists. Therefore, although the EML 2025 represents an internationally oriented list of essential medicines, only a small proportion of its drugs belonged to the highly concordant core across all compared lists.

Having a look at the retained drugs of the EML 2025 from the 1 st EML in 1977 (Bindel and Seifert 2026a), this share was even higher. Among these retained drugs, 6.3% were listed on all seven drug lists compared with 2.3% for the complete EML 2025, and 15.0% were listed on six of seven drug lists compared with 7.5% for the complete EML 2025. 21.3% of retained drugs were included in six or seven drug lists, whereas this proportion was only 9.8% for all EML 2025 drugs. However, also in these retained drugs, the proportion of drugs included in one to three drug lists remained quite high, with 42.5%. Nevertheless, compared with the complete EML 2025, the retained drugs showed a clear shift toward higher concordance. 39.4% of retained drugs were listed on five to seven drug lists, compared with 20.8% of all EML 2025 drugs.

Discussion

Characterization of highly concordant drugs across the compared drug lists

Overall, 49 drugs were particularly frequently listed on the compared drug lists, including 11 drugs that were listed on all seven drug lists and 38 drugs that were listed on six of the seven drug lists. The included drugs were quite heterogeneous and covered a variety of drug classes and indications, ranging from infectious diseases, cardiovascular disorders, pain management, and respiratory diseases to inflammatory, gastrointestinal, and psychiatric conditions (Table 3). Many of these drugs are used for frequent conditions, e.g. paracetamol for pain relief and fever, or have a broad range of indications, e.g. prednisolone for inflammatory conditions and hormone substitution (Ayoub 2021; Whittaker et al. 2025). Despite this heterogeneity, three recurrent patterns of usage and importance could be distinguished (Table 4, Fig. 4). These patterns describe broad characteristics of highly concordant drugs and should be understood as overlapping concepts rather than as strict drug categories. The first group includes drugs that are primarily relevant for broad-use symptomatic care. These drugs are often used for common, usually uncomplicated disorders and are frequently applied for symptomatic relief across different indications, such as pain, fever, nausea, diarrhea, cough, or mild infections. Some of these drugs are available over-the-counter (OTC) and may be used without direct consultation of a medical professional. Examples are ibuprofen, loperamide, or acyclovir (Ngo and Bajaj 2024; Baker 2007; Klysik et al. 2020). Their frequent occurrence across the compared drug lists may therefore reflect their broad utility, high familiarity in clinical practice, and relevance for common healthcare needs. The second group includes drugs with an important role in the long-term treatment of chronic disorders. These drugs are used for conditions requiring continuous or recurrent treatment and may relieve symptoms, improve long-term outcomes, or prevent disease-related complications. In contrast to drugs used mainly for symptomatic care, these drugs are often part of a broader treatment strategy that may consist of several drugs and regular follow-up. Examples are insulin and metformin for diabetes mellitus (Mozrelatou et al. 2026; Pathak et al. 2019), or ramipril for arterial hypertension and cardiovascular risk reduction (Jones et al. 2025). Their frequent occurrence across the compared drug lists therefore underlines the importance of chronic disease management as a central component of essential pharmacotherapy.

The third group includes drugs that are particularly important in acute and emergency treatment situations. These drugs are commonly used in specific clinical situations and often require administration by medical professionals. Due to their rapid onset, high efficacy, or specific mechanism of action, they may reverse potentially life-threatening situations, such as anaphylaxis or bradycardia, e.g. epinephrine and atropine (Wigginton et al. 2025), or may prevent serious complications, e.g. amiodarone and alteplase in acute cardiovascular or thromboembolic events (Sorodoc et al. 2024; NINDS 2024). Their inclusion in many drug lists highlights that those essential medicines are not only defined by frequent use but also by their importance in time-critical and high-risk situations.

However, it is important to consider that these patterns are not distinct and can overlap, particularly because drugs are often used for multiple indications and in different clinical settings. For example, acetylsalicylic acid can be used as an OTC drug for headache, but may also be taken daily for the prevention of cardiovascular events (Patrono 2022). Similarly, GCR agonists, MOR agonists, antibacterial drugs, or cardiovascular drugs may be relevant in both acute and long-term treatment contexts. In addition, some drugs may be comparably safe and suitable for self-medication, whereas others require professional administration, clinical monitoring, or recurrent therapeutic drug monitoring. Overall, the high concordance of these 49 drugs appears to be mainly reasoned by their broad utility, their relevance for common or serious disorders, and their efficacy in important treatment situations. This highlights their importance in regard to public health need, medical education, and availability. However, the highly concordant drug selection should not be misunderstood as an unquestioned collection of the most useful or relevant drugs. Several included drugs may be regarded as problematic in selected indications or from a current pharmacotherapeutic perspective. This applies, for example, to digoxin and glyceryl trinitrate, which were already discussed as potentially outdated or obsolete retained EML drugs (Bindel and Seifert 2026a). Furthermore, cotrimoxazole may be questioned because of the sulfonamide component and the comparable high risk of type IV reactions (Preyra et al. 2025; Coppry et al. 2022), hydrocortisone may be less suitable for strong immunosuppression compared with more potent GCR agonists like mometasone (Spada et al. 2018), paracetamol has to be considered critically because of hepatotoxicity (Chidiac et al. 2023), and lactulose may be limited by gastrointestinal adverse effects, especially flatulence, while macrogol may represent a better tolerated alternative (Guest et al. 2008). These examples show that broad listing across several drug lists does not automatically prove current therapeutic superiority or an unquestionable role in pharmacotherapy.

In addition, some important therapeutic areas and highly used drugs were not represented within the highly concordant core. For example, no drug for depressive disorders (e.g. SSRI) was included among the 49 highly concordant drugs, and lithium was also not part of this core, despite its established role in bipolar disorder. Furthermore, no H1R antagonist, no PPI, no vitamin D preparation, no β1-AR antagonist, and no antispasmodic drug such as butylscopolamine were listed in this highly concordant drug group. This is particularly relevant because the comparison with German prescription data as an illustrative national example (Drug Prescription Report 2025, Germany, TOP30 drug preparations with highest consumption) (Ludwig et al. 2026) showed that several frequently prescribed drugs were listed on the summarized drug list, but did not reach the threshold of six or seven lists (Table S2). This includes atorvastatin, bisoprolol, formoterol, metoprolol, pantoprazole, apixaban, beclometasone, empagliflozin, candesartan, metamizole, torasemide, and dapagliflozin. Thus, some drugs with high clinical relevance were not identified as highly concordant drugs by the present approach.

Reasons for differences and similarities between drug lists

The analysed drug lists are quite distinct in some aspects; however, they also share a large number of similarities. Although many drugs were listed on only one or two compared lists, a considerable share was found on most drug lists (Table 3 and S1). This indicates that the composition of the lists is shaped by both list-specific determinants and more general principles of pharmacotherapy. Several aspects may therefore contribute to the observed differences and similarities.

Differences in listed drugs can first be attributed to variations in the scope of the drug lists. Drug lists for educational purposes likely focus on representative drugs, important mechanisms of action, and the coverage of relevant diseases (Baker et al. 2010; Donker et al. 2023; IMPP 2024). However, they may not cover the whole spectrum of clinically important pharmacotherapy. In contrast, drug lists that focus on the assurance of availability and the prevention of supply shortages may aim to include a broader range of clinically relevant drugs (WHO 2025b; BfArM 2026; DGIM 2026). These lists may therefore include not only highly essential medicines, but also therapeutic alternatives, drugs for specific clinical settings, or medicines required to maintain treatment options during supply shortages.

Another important aspect is country-specific need and public health prioritization. Some diseases or treatment situations are more relevant in particular regions, e.g. tropical diseases or the need for anti-venoms, resulting in a more specific or niche use of some medicines (Fattah et al. 2025; Piggott et al. 2022). In other countries, a higher prevalence of certain diseases (Lo Sardo et al. 2023) may further influence which drugs are prioritized. Furthermore, the healthcare system structure and available resources determine the complexity of treatment regimens, the affordability of medicines, and the availability of costly or newer drugs (Dos-Santos-Silva et al. 2022; Oldfield et al. 2025; Cylus and Pearson 2016; Wouters et al. 2025). Thus, drug costs may also contribute to differences between drug lists, particularly in countries or settings where financial resources limit the inclusion, supply, or regular availability of certain medicines. This may especially affect newer, expensive, or highly specialized drugs, even if these drugs have therapeutic value. However, the extent to which drug costs were explicitly considered differed between the analysed drug lists and was not systematically assessed in the present comparison. Therefore, differences between drug lists may reflect not only medical relevance, but also differences in regional disease burden, infrastructure, affordability, and access.

Cultural and practice-related differences may further contribute to variation between the drug lists. These include beliefs and attitudes toward medicines, national guidelines, medical education, and prescribing traditions, which can influence the preference for certain treatment options (Table 5). For example, some drugs may be widely used in one setting but withdrawn or uncommon in another, as shown for metamizole in Germany compared with the United States (Sebode et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2020). Morphine illustrates another relevant pattern, with overuse in some high-income settings and underuse in many LMICs (Roques et al. 2022; Jayawardana et al. 2021). Similarities between drug lists are likely due to the fact that many diseases occur globally and that similar pathophysiological processes require similar pharmacological approaches. Infectious diseases, pain, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, asthma, acute emergencies, and inflammatory disorders are relevant across many healthcare systems, although their prevalence and treatment possibilities may differ (IHME 2025). Therefore, drugs with broad utility, well-established efficacy, and relevance for frequent or severe diseases are more likely to be included in several different lists. In addition, pharmacological principles are largely universal. Drugs such as anti-infectives, analgesics, antihypertensives, bronchodilators, anticoagulants, or emergency medicines address central mechanisms that are relevant independent of region or healthcare system. This may explain why a core group of drugs was repeatedly identified across the compared lists, even when the underlying purpose of the lists differed.

Furthermore, globalization and internationalization of biomedical research and clinical practice (Kész and Balatoni 2026) likely contribute to the observed similarities. International guidelines, shared evidence from clinical trials, global pharmacovigilance, and international consensus processes may lead to similar treatment approaches, especially for long-established drugs. As a result, many medicines that are effective, widely known, and embedded in clinical practice are selected across different types of drug lists.

Evaluation of the EML 2025 by comparison with other drug lists

The WHO Model List of Essential Medicines (EML) is considered the international reference list for essential medicines (Fattah et al. 2025). Therefore, the EML 2025 (WHO 2025b) was evaluated in relation to the other drug lists to identify particularly valuable drugs that are already listed on the EML, to identify drugs that are not listed on the EML but are included in many other drug lists and may therefore be suggested for future consideration, and to evaluate drugs that are listed only on the EML and not on any other compared drug list (Table S3).

Overall, 49 drugs were identified as broadly listed across the compared drug lists, since they were included in six or seven drug lists, and were also listed on the EML 2025. These drugs include, for example, paracetamol, salbutamol, metronidazole, insulin, ramipril, morphine, epinephrine, and diazepam. As described earlier, these drugs cover a broad spectrum of therapeutic fields, including common symptomatic treatment, chronic disease management, anti-infective treatment and emergency medicine (see chapter “characterization of highly concordant drugs”). A large number of these drugs, 27 in total, were already listed on the first edition of the EML in 1977 and are therefore considered as “retained drugs” (Bindel and Seifert 2026a, b). The presence of these drugs on many drug lists with varying scopes strengthens their importance for public health and pharmacotherapy, although this does not represent a direct proof of their therapeutic value. However, two of these highly concordant drugs were previously identified as potentially outdated or obsolete, including digoxin, which was listed on six drug lists, and glyceryl trinitrate, which was listed on all seven drug lists (Bindel and Seifert 2026a). This high concordance appears to contradict the previous assessment of these retained EML drugs. However, it may be explained by different indications or clinical roles in the compared drug lists than in the EML. Alternatively, it may indicate that some questionable or historically established drugs are retained not only on the EML but also on other drug lists, despite a need for critical re-evaluation.

This aspect is important for the interpretation of concordance. A high number of listings can support the relevance of a drug, but it may also reflect historical establishment, prescribing tradition, or delayed removal from drug lists. Therefore, highly concordant drugs should not automatically be interpreted as therapeutically superior drugs. They should rather be regarded as drugs with broad recognition across different contexts, which still require an individual pharmacological and clinical assessment.

In contrast, 98 drugs were observed that were solely listed on the EML and not on any further compared drug list. This may be interpreted as a hint toward a lower relevance of these drugs in the contexts covered by the other drug lists. However, a sole listing on the EML does not automatically indicate obsolescence or limited therapeutic value. The EML differs from the other drug lists in its inclusion principles, particularly by its focus on global public health needs, the prioritization of LMIC needs, and the inclusion of medicines for country-specific, neglected, or otherwise underrepresented diseases (WHO 2025a). Therefore, the EML may include medicines that are highly relevant in specific global health contexts, but less relevant in high-income countries, to which most other compared drug lists refer. This aspect is particularly important for drugs used for tropical diseases, neglected infectious diseases, reproductive health, vaccines, antidotes, and public health interventions. In these cases, the absence from other drug lists may reflect differences in regional need, health-system context, or list scope rather than a lack of value. Nevertheless, among the drugs listed only on the EML, six drugs were identified as outdated in prior analyses, including amodiaquine, dimercaprol, griseofulvin, melarsoprol, oxamniquine, and procaine benzylpenicillin (Bindel and Seifert 2026a). For these drugs, the lack of listing on all other compared drug lists strengthens the consideration for deletion or at least critical re-evaluation in future EML revisions.

Furthermore, 27 drugs were listed on the majority of the other drug lists, namely on four or five drug lists, but were not listed as independent medicines on the EML 2025. This group includes drugs such as apixaban, diclofenac, metoprolol, pantoprazole, tramadol, gabapentin, cefepime, daptomycin, or ticagrelor. Importantly, some of these drugs are not completely absent from the EML, since they are included as alternatives to listed essential medicines, but not as individually listed medicines (WHO 2025b). This applies to 13 drugs. In contrast, 14 drugs are not included on the EML at all. The frequent occurrence of these drugs on other drug lists may support their consideration for future inclusion on the EML. However, this should not be interpreted as an automatic recommendation for inclusion. Some drugs may be omitted because their relevant indications are already covered by other representative or comparable medicines on the EML. Therefore, these drugs should be interpreted as candidates for further evaluation rather than direct additions to the EML.

Limitations

Some limitations of this analysis have to be considered. Seven drug lists and one consumption report were included, and most of them refer to high-income country contexts or medical education in Europe and the United States. Therefore, the comparison may not fully represent global drug prioritization, particularly regarding LMICs. Furthermore, the analysed drug lists differed in their update procedures and publication years. Therefore, differences between the lists may partly reflect differences in actuality and update status, rather than only differences in medical relevance. Furthermore, data extraction and harmonization were conducted by one author (L.J.B.). Although the harmonization procedure was performed according to predefined principles, this may have introduced some uncertainty, particularly for synonyms, salt forms, spelling variants, and borderline cases.

The analysis was based on the presence or absence of drugs on the respective lists. Thus, no weighting according to clinical importance, frequency of use, strength of evidence, or disease burden was performed. Concordance between drug lists can therefore support the relevance of a drug, but it cannot prove therapeutic value. Dosing, formulations, and routes of administration were not considered, and FDCs were included as single drugs. This approach allowed a standardized comparison between the lists, but may have reduced differences between drugs that are used in distinct clinical settings or formulations.

Conclusions

The comparison of the seven analysed drug lists showed that the consideration of a drug as being essential strongly depends on the purpose, scope, and regional context of the respective list (Table 1). Therefore, differences between the lists should not only be interpreted as differences in drug importance, but also as a consequence of different priorities regarding public health, supply security, medical education, regional need, and update procedures.

Although 1061 single drugs were identified in total, the majority of drugs was listed on only one to three drug lists (Table 2 and S1). In contrast, only a small core of 49 drugs was listed on six or seven of the compared lists (Table 3). These highly concordant drugs covered broad-use symptomatic care, long-term treatment of chronic disorders, and acute or emergency treatment. Their frequent occurrence across drug lists with different scopes highlights their broad utility and their importance for public health, medical education, and drug availability. At the same time, the small size of this highly concordant core suggests that agreement between drug lists is limited and that many drugs are relevant only within specific scopes or healthcare contexts. Furthermore, this highly concordant drug list does not represent an unquestionable collection of the most essential and therapeutically important key drugs. Some highly concordant drugs may require critical re-evaluation, while several relevant therapeutic areas and highly prescribed drugs were not included.

The comparison of the EML 2025 with the other drug lists showed that broad concordance can support the relevance of already included medicines, particularly for long-established drugs that were retained from the first EML (Table S3). However, absence from other lists should not directly be interpreted as low therapeutic value. The EML has a global scope and therefore includes drugs that may be less relevant in the mainly high-income country contexts of the other analysed lists. Nevertheless, drugs listed only on the EML, especially those previously identified as outdated, may require critical re-evaluation in future revisions. In addition, several drugs not listed as independent medicines on the EML were included in the majority of other drug lists. These drugs may represent candidates for further assessment, but their frequent occurrence on other lists should not be interpreted as an automatic recommendation for inclusion.

Overall, the concordance between the analysed drug lists reflects a balance between universal pharmacological and clinical principles on the one hand and country-specific, cultural, educational, and supply-related priorities on the other hand (Tables 4 and 5). The comparison with other drug lists can therefore provide a useful additional perspective for evaluating essential medicines and for identifying drugs that are highly established, potentially overlooked, or in need of re-evaluation. However, this approach cannot replace a drug-specific assessment of therapeutic value, public health relevance, feasibility, and global need.

The present findings highlight that a relevant key essential drug list should not be based on concordance alone. It also the consideration of current therapeutic value, safety, prescription relevance, supply vulnerability, practical feasibility, and the coverage of important indication areas. Especially in times of crisis, such as war, supply shortages, epidemics, or sudden increases in healthcare demand, there is a need for a transparent and regularly updated procedure to define national and international key essential drug lists. Such a procedure should include pharmacological assessment, public health relevance, supply vulnerability, clinical indispensability, and practical feasibility. Thus, a key essential drug list should not only reflect historical or cross-list concordance, but should be an actively maintained instrument for rational pharmacotherapy, medical education, supply security, and preparedness of healthcare systems.
