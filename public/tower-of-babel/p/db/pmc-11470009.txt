Assessment of heart rate measurements by commercial wearable fitness trackers for early identification of metabolic syndrome risk
Authors: Sujeong Mun, Kihyun Park, Jeong-Kyun Kim, Junho Kim, Siwoo Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic syndrome increases the risks of cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers. The early detection of metabolic syndrome is clinically relevant, as it enables timely and targeted interventions. In the current study, we aimed to investigate the association between metabolic syndrome and heart rate measured using wearable devices in a real-world setting and compare this association with that for clinical resting heart rate. Data from 564 middle-aged adults who wore wearable devices for at least 7 days with a minimum daily wear time of 20 h were analyzed. The results showed significantly elevated all-day, sleeping, minimum, and inactive heart rates in men with pre-metabolic or metabolic syndrome compared with those in normal individuals, whereas sleeping heart rate and heart rate dips were significantly increased and decreased, respectively, in women with metabolic syndrome. After adjusting for confounders, every 10-beats-per-minute increment in all-day, sleeping, minimum, and inactive heart rates in men corresponded to odds ratios of 2.80 (95% confidence interval 1.53–5.44), 3.06 (1.57–6.40), 4.21 (1.87–10.47), and 3.09 (1.64–6.29), respectively, for the presence of pre-metabolic or metabolic syndrome. In women, the association was significant only for heart rate dips (odds ratio = 0.49 [95% confidence interval 0.25–0.96] for every 10% increment). Models incorporating inactive or minimum heart rate in men and heart rate dip in women demonstrated better fits, as indicated by lower Akaike information criterion values (170.3 in men and 364.9 in women), compared with models that included clinical resting heart rate (173.4 in men and 369.1 in women). Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS), characterized by the co-occurrence of metabolic abnormalities including central obesity, hypertension, elevated glucose levels, and abnormal lipid profiles, is substantially associated with increased risks of developing cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and cancer, as well as all-cause mortality1,2. The prevalence of MetS is growing in most countries, although the rates vary depending on the criteria used to define MetS3. As early identification of MetS risk facilitates timely interventions and targeted preventive measures, certain indicators of early-stage MetS have been suggested, including serum uric acid, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, muscle strength, and heart rate (HR)4.

Among these proposed measures, HR is a fundamental vital sign that is routinely measured alongside body temperature, respiratory rate, and blood pressure (BP)5. While resting HR has been extensively studied and shown to be substantially associated with cardiovascular disease6 and metabolic abnormalities7,8, other HR metrics, such as HR during sleep, have received less attention in the context of MetS. A 2017 meta-analysis of 16 observational studies reported a 28% increase in MetS risk per 10-beats-per-minute (bpm) increment in resting HR9. Similarly, elevated HR during sleep, such as those observed in individuals with diabetes or hypertension10,11, and reduced nighttime HR dips are associated with increased cardiovascular risk12,13. However, the relationship between HR during sleep and MetS remains underexplored.

By 2028, the number of wearable fitness tracker users worldwide is projected to reach 348 million14. With the increasing use of commercial wearable fitness trackers, such as those incorporating photoplethysmography, continuous HR data can now be easily, non-invasively, and cost-effectively collected. These devices can capture real-time, long-term HR data, and contextual information such as physical activity level and sleep status. Importantly, wearable fitness trackers have demonstrated acceptable accuracy compared with standard criterion measures such as electrocardiography or chest straps for daily activities, as well as high accuracy during sleep and rest15.

Emerging studies have begun to incorporate data from wearable fitness devices into metabolic risk assessments. While many of these studies have focused on lifestyle behaviors such as physical activity levels and patterns16–18, some have also included HR data19,20. For instance, Ballinger et al. described a semi-supervised deep neural network model for predicting cardiovascular risk using data from wearable devices, including step counts and HR. However, the nature of the deep learning system has raised questions regarding the interpretability of this model19. Lim et al. demonstrated the usefulness of resting HR measured by wearable devices in identifying individual metabolic risk markers20. Nevertheless, most of these previous studies did not fully explore the potential of different HR metrics obtained from wearable devices, including minimum HR, HR dips, or sleeping HR, in predicting MetS and its early stages.

Recent evidence has highlighted the potential of wearable device-derived HR metrics other than resting HR in relation to metabolic risk. For instance, Tochikubo et al. found that individuals with hypertension had higher minimum sleeping HR than normotensive individuals11. Additionally, Zhou et al. used high-resolution HR features obtained from wearable devices to develop machine-learning models to predict several metabolic risk factors21. Rykov et al. incorporated multiple HR indices, including resting and average HR, and their differences to analyze their associations with individual metabolic risk biomarkers. The authors demonstrated that some of these HR indices were substantially associated with body composition biomarkers, although they failed to detect any significant associations with blood biomarkers related to metabolic risks22. Despite these findings, comprehensive research on the association between wearable device-derived HR indices and MetS is lacking, particularly investigations of HR during sleep and across multiple inactive periods throughout the day rather than single-point measurements, which may provide valuable insights into early metabolic dysfunction.

To address these gaps, the current study aimed to investigate the associations between MetS, including its early stage (PreMetS), and various HR indices, including inactive, sleeping, minimum, and all-day HR, and HR dip, derived from wearable devices. Additionally, we sought to compare these associations with those for clinical resting HR, which has traditionally been considered the gold standard in HR-based metabolic risk assessment. By examining HR metrics derived from continuous monitoring in real-world settings, this study offers the potential to identify more sensitive markers for early detection of MetS and to advance the use of wearable technologies in clinical practice.

Results

Participant characteristics

Among the 759 participants who were issued a device, HR data were available at 1-min intervals for 736 participants owing to technical issues such as access token expiration. Among these, 104 participants were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria on the wear time (≥ 20 h/day) and period (≥ 7 days), and 68 were excluded because they were taking antihypertensive medications. Therefore, we analyzed data from 564 participants. The mean ± standard deviation of included day counts for each participant was 13.1 ± 1.6 days, whereas the daily wear time was 23 ± 0.5 h.

The characteristics of the study participants according to the presence of PreMetS or MetS are presented in Table 1. Among men, individuals with PreMetS or MetS had significantly elevated all-day, sleeping, minimum, inactive, and clinical resting HRs compared with individuals categorized as normal (without PreMetS or MetS). There were no statistically significant differences in HR indices between individuals with PreMetS and those with MetS. Conversely, women diagnosed with MetS demonstrated significantly increased sleeping HR and decreased HR dip compared with women categorized as normal.

Associations between HR indices and PreMetS or MetS

Figure 1 presents the results of logistic regression analyses of the associations between HR indices and the likelihood of having PreMetS or MetS, with normal individuals used as the reference group. In men, the significant associations observed in the unadjusted models were retained in the fully adjusted models. The odds ratios (ORs) of all-day, sleeping, minimum, inactive, and clinical resting HR in relation to PreMetS or MetS were 2.80 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.53–5.44), 3.06 (95% CI 1.57–6.40), 4.21 (95% CI 1.87–10.47), 3.09 (95% CI 1.64–6.29), and 2.43 (95% CI 1.37–4.56), respectively, for every 10-bpm increment in each HR index in the fully adjusted models. Notably, the wearable device-driven indices showed more significant P-values compared with clinical resting HR. In women, while several HR indices were significant in the unadjusted models, only HR dip remained significant in the fully adjusted model, with an OR of 0.49 (95% CI 0.25–0.96) for every 10% increment.

In men, among the fully adjusted and best subset models, including each HR index, the lowest Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) values were observed in models that included inactive or minimum HR (AIC: 170.3, BIC: 226.1), which were even lower than those of the model including clinical resting HR (AIC: 173.4, BIC: 229.2). In women, among the fully adjusted models, the lowest AIC and BIC values were observed in the model that included HR dip (AIC: 364.9, BIC: 440.7), whereas the corresponding values for the model with clinical resting HR were 369.1 and 445.0 respectively. HR dip was selected in the best subset model based on AIC but not in that based on BIC (Supplementary Table S1).

The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values and cutoff points for HR indices, which were statistically significant in the unadjusted models, are presented in Supplementary Table S2. In men, the AUCs for all-day, sleeping, minimum, inactive, and clinical resting HRs were 0.667, 0.668, 0.679, 0.682, and 0.650, respectively. In women, the AUCs for sleeping HR, HR dip, and minimum HR were 0.585, 0.617, and 0.583, respectively.

Supplementary Figure S1 presents a comparison of the ORs of HR indices for MetS, with non-MetS (PreMetS and normal state) as the reference group. In both men and women, all associations in the adjusted models were not statistically significant. Additionally, results obtained from logistic regression analyses of the associations between HR indices and the likelihood of having PreMetS or MetS—with normal or PreMetS, respectively, as the reference category—are presented in Supplementary Fig. S2. Among men, after adjusting for relevant covariates in the fully adjusted models, increased all-day, sleeping, minimum, and inactive HRs were significantly associated with increased likelihoods of having PreMetS or MetS when compared with normal conditions. Additionally, an increased clinical resting HR was significantly linked with a higher probability of having PreMetS (but not MetS). Notably, the likelihood of having MetS versus PreMetS did not exhibit a significant increase in all HR indices, suggesting that discernible HR alterations may already be present in the PreMetS stage. Conversely, some of the associations were significant in women, although these findings were not consistent across all models.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the associations between HR indices obtained from wearable devices and PreMetS or MetS in middle-aged Korean individuals. Our findings indicated that the changes in HR associated with MetS were identifiable even in the early stages of MetS. The altered levels of HR indices derived from wearable devices, encompassing increased all-day, sleeping, minimum, and inactive HRs in men and reduced HR dip in women, were significantly associated with increased likelihoods of PreMetS or MetS, independent of potential confounding factors. Clinical resting HR demonstrated significant associations only in men.

Among the wearable device-derived HR indices used in this study, inactive HR, defined as a valid HR while the participant is awake with no movement (0 steps) for at least 30 min, is the closest, by definition, to resting HR, which is the gold standard index for HR. Among the regression models of each HR index for PreMetS or MetS in men, the model that included inactive HR had the lowest AIC, BIC, and P-values. Moreover, the AUC was the highest for the model that included inactive HR. Although clinical resting HR is associated with MetS7,9, the association between inactive HR derived from wearable devices and MetS has not been extensively evaluated. Only a few studies have reported associations between wearable device-derived inactive HR and individual metabolic risk markers21,22. By assessing both clinical resting HR and wearable device-derived HR indices, we were able to compare their utility in predicting PreMetS or MetS. Interestingly, the models that included inactive, minimum, all-day, and sleeping HRs in men demonstrated lower AIC and BIC and higher AUC values than the models that included clinical resting HR. In women, the best subset model did not include clinical resting HR, while HR dip was selected in the model based on AIC. These results suggest that the utility of HR indices from wearable devices is superior, or at least comparable, to that of conventional methods used to monitor metabolic risk by measuring resting HR in clinical settings. This finding aligns with those of a previous study demonstrating that wearable device-derived inactive HR can provide comparable value for predicting cardiovascular risk markers compared with clinical resting HR20, as well as a study that found night-time HR measured using an ambulatory device was a better predictor of cardiovascular events than clinical resting HR in patients with hypertension12, a finding that highlighted the clinical importance of continuous monitoring rather than a single-point measurement of HR.

Notably, the AIC, BIC, and P-values, as well as the AUC for minimum HR, were the closest to those for inactive HR. Although research regarding minimum HR in relation to MetS is scarce, minimum HR is correlated with coronary heart disease severity and progression, whereas average or maximal HR shows no such associations23,24. These findings, along with our results, may be related to findings that minimum HR is associated with autonomic nervous function25,26, body mass index (BMI)26, and high BP11.

In our study, higher sleeping HR was associated with increased risks of PreMetS or MetS, high BP, large WC, and high FBG levels in men and high FBG levels in women. Although the HR measured during sleep is relatively understudied compared with resting HR, some studies have reported the usefulness of sleeping HR, HR dip, or minimum HR in predicting cardiovascular events or metabolic risks11,12,27,28. In the present study, we found that while the HR dip was not associated with any metabolic risk in men, the HR index was significantly associated with MetS in women. Several previous studies have reported that the association between resting HR and MetS risk can be only detected in men29,30, and a systematic review reported that the association between resting HR and the risk of cardiovascular disease was stronger in men than in women31. In our study, although significant associations were not detected with other HR indices in women, significant changes in sleeping HR and HR dip were observed with regards to the existence of PreMetS or MetS. Moreover, HR dip was included in the best subset model based on AIC. This finding suggests that sleep-related changes in daily HR fluctuations may be relatively more important than resting HR in women.

Previous studies on the association between resting HR and MetS have yielded mixed findings regarding its relationship with individual MetS components. For instance, in a study of apparently healthy non-obese men, resting HR was not associated with any MetS component29. In contrast, other studies have identified significant associations between resting HR and multiple MetS components, although the specific components differed across studies7,32,33. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in participant characteristics, such as age, sex, ethnicity, and sample size, and analytical approaches, including HR categorization, confounder adjustments, and stratification. In the present study, we analyzed a sample of middle-aged participants (mean age: 46 years), stratified by sex, and adjusted relevant confounders, including the four other MetS components not being examined. Our findings revealed significant associations between resting HR and elevated WC and FBG levels in men. Furthermore, multiple HR indices obtained from wearable devices were significantly associated with elevated BP, WC, and FBG level in men, while these associations were weak in women. Additional studies are required to confirm these findings.

The over-activity of the sympathetic nervous system may partly explain the mechanisms underlying the association between HR and metabolic risks34–36. HR is primarily regulated by the autonomic nervous system, with the sympathetic nervous system increasing HR and the parasympathetic nervous system suppressing it37. Hyperactivity of the sympathetic nervous system is strongly associated with insulin resistance, a key factor in MetS owing to its impact on glucose and lipid metabolism38. Furthermore, sympathetic hyperactivation is associated with increased abdominal visceral fat39. Leptin, a hormone produced by adipose tissue, also stimulates sympathetic nerve outflow, which can increase BP and HR40. Other explanations include the relationship of HR with cardiovascular fitness or increased inflammation41–44. Cardiovascular fitness, frequently measured as maximal oxygen uptake, is inversely related to MetS incidence41, and HR is an important predictor of cardiovascular fitness42. Elevated HR could also predict chronic inflammatory status44, which may be a central mechanism underlying MetS pathophysiology43.

Despite evidence establishing the link between resting HR and increased risks of cardiovascular diseases, resting HR remains a slightly overlooked risk factor and has yet to be incorporated into widely recognized indices used to assess cardiovascular risk45. Moreover, investigations of HR indices other than resting HR, such as sleeping HR, minimum HR, and HR dip, remain comparatively scarce. However, with the growing accessibility of 24-h HR monitoring owing to the increasing use of wearable devices among the general population, the relevance of HR indices is expanding. Therefore, the clinical importance of HR indices in predicting metabolic risks warrants greater attention. In the present study, we investigated the associations of PreMetS or MetS with various HR indices from all-day monitoring of HR obtained from wearable devices. Possible confounders (including demographic, socioeconomic, and lifestyle characteristics) were adjusted in the analysis to evaluate the independent associations. The results of the comparative analysis with clinical resting HR were also used to estimate the relative utility of wearable device-derived HR indices. Our results suggest that the changes in HR related to MetS may already exist in the early stages of MetS. A previous study that included middle-aged Japanese males also reported that elevated HR seemed to precede the development of full-blown MetS in individuals with PreMetS46. Therefore, changes in HR may be a useful predictor of MetS and could be used as a screening index for individuals at high risk for MetS.

This study has some limitations. First, it was difficult to reach causal inferences of the associations detected because of the cross-sectional design of the study. Second, the study sample may not be representative of the residents of Daejeon because, due to practical constraints, only some Korean Medicine Daejeon Citizen Cohort (KDCC) participants were issued a device and included in the analysis. This may limit the generalizability of the study findings. Third, although adjustments were made for possible confounders in the analysis, other unmeasured or unknown factors may have impacted the association between HR and metabolic risk. Acute infections, stress, and diet (including the consumption of caffeine or alcohol during the tracking period) were not investigated, and the possibility of these factors affecting HR cannot be ruled out. However, with ORs of 4.21 (95% CI 1.87–10.47) and 2.80 (95% CI 1.53–5.44), which were the largest and smallest OR values obtained for HR indices derived from wearable devices in men, and 0.49 (95% CI 0.25–0.96), which was the only significant OR in women, the unmeasured confounders would need to be associated with both HR and MetS with an OR of 3.52 (95% CI 2.08–5.93), 2.73 (95% CI 1.78–4.10), or 2.21 (95% CI 1.17–3.41), respectively, to entirely explain the present findings47. Fourth, our study used HR data from a consumer-grade wearable device based on photoplethysmography. Although the accuracy of this device is generally acceptable for most common activities and is deemed suitable for use in population-based studies, data discrepancies are possible, particularly during activities such as cycling and resistance training15. In the present study, except for all-day HR and HR dip, other HR indices were calculated using measurements obtained solely during rest or sleep, times during which HR measurements are reportedly more accurate15. Additionally, we used median rather than mean values during the first stage of HR index calculation to reduce the effect of possible measurement errors from motion artifacts.

In conclusion, our study results suggest that HR changes linked with MetS are detectable even at the early stages of MetS. The HR indices derived from the wearable devices, i.e., inactive or minimum HRs in men and HR dip in women, were significantly associated with PreMetS or MetS. Moreover, the models incorporating these factors demonstrated better fits than those incorporating clinical resting HR. HR monitoring using wearable devices demonstrates promise as a tool for assessing MetS risk and may facilitate its early detection. However, further validation in diverse populations is needed to fully establish the clinical utility of HR monitoring using wearable devices for MetS risk assessment.

Methods

Study participants

The current study analyzed data from the KDCC study. As described previously48, the KDCC study was conducted in Daejeon City, South Korea, and utilized stratified cluster sampling to recruit adults between 30 and 55 years of age without cancer or cardiovascular diseases. From October 2020, participants who agreed to wear a commercial wearable activity and HR tracker (Fitbit Inspire 2, Fitbit, San Francisco, California, USA) were requested to wear the device on their wrists for 2 weeks. Data were downloaded through the programming interface of the Fitbit application. We considered only the days on which the device was worn for at least 20 h20,49, as determined from the HR data recorded for each minute, and included participants who wore the device for ≥ 7 days (5 weekdays and 2 weekend days). Data obtained for up to 2 weeks (10 weekdays and 4 weekend days) were retrieved from the participants. As some antihypertensive medications have a potential effect on HR, participants taking these medications were excluded from the analysis.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Dunsan Korean Medicine Hospital of Daejeon University (approval number: DJDSKH-17-BM-12) and the Korea Institute of Oriental Medicine (approval number: I-1703/002-002). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study. All participants were informed that their data would be de-identified using a unique code assigned to each participant. The included participants consented to allow their data (including health data collected from wearable devices for up to 2 weeks and stored de-identified on our server) to be used for research purposes. The study was conducted according to the principles in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical measurements and laboratory tests

Height and body weight were measured using a digital stadiometer (BSM370, InBody, Seoul, South Korea). BMI was then calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of the height (m2). WC and hip circumference were measured at the umbilicus and the broadest point of the gluteal region, respectively, using a measuring tape (Hoechstmass-Rollvix, Germany).

BP and clinical resting HR were measured using an automatic BP cuff (FT-500R PLUS, Jawon Medical, South Korea), with the participants sitting upright after resting for at least 5 min. The measurements were repeated after a rest period of at least 1 min. The analysis used the mean values of the two measurements for both BP and HR.

Venous blood samples were collected in the morning following an overnight fast; after 30 min, the samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 3450 rpm and transported to Seoul Clinical Laboratories (Seoul, South Korea) within 24 h.

Socioeconomic and lifestyle variables

Education level was classified as high school or lower, university, and graduate school or higher. Household income (measured in 10,000 won/month) was classified as < 300, 300–399, and 400–499. Regarding marital status, the participants were classified as married, unmarried, or other. For smoking status, the participants were classified as current smokers, past smokers, or non-smokers. Alcohol consumption status was classified using the average volume of daily alcohol consumption, according to the World Health Organization report. Participants were classified as non-drinkers (0 g/day), responsible drinkers (0.1–39.99 g for men and 0.1–19.99 g for women), hazardous drinkers (40–59.99 g for men and 20–39.99 g for women), or harmful drinkers (≥ 60 g for males and ≥ 40 g for females)50. Physical activity levels were evaluated using the Korean version of the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire51 and classified as low, moderate, or high. For women, menopause was defined as the absence of menstrual bleeding within the last 12 months.

Definitions of MetS and PreMetS

This study used the criteria described in the modified Third National Cholesterol Education Program/Adult Treatment Panel52, with modification of the WC cutoff for Koreans53. These criteria included WCs ≥ 90 cm for men and ≥ 85 cm for women, triglyceride level ≥ 150 mg/dL or using dyslipidemia medication, high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol levels < 40 mg/dL for men or < 50 mg/dL for women, systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 85 mmHg, and FBG ≥ 100 mg/dL or using antihyperglycemic medication. Participants who satisfied at least three of the criteria were defined as having MetS, whereas those who satisfied two of the criteria were defined as having PreMetS54.

HR indices derived from wearable fitness devices

HR indices were derived from data collected at 1-min intervals. To summarize all-day, sleeping, and inactive HRs, the median values for the corresponding periods in each day were calculated to reduce the possible effects of measurement errors from motion artifacts. The mean daily values represented the participants’ corresponding HR indices. The sleep period was defined from sleep onset to sleep offset according to the device, excluding periods defined as “wake” (in “stages” sleep logs), “restless,” “awake” (in “classic” sleep logs), or “unknown,”, and short wakes classified as “shortData.” The inactive period was defined as the awake period during which the participants were inactive (no steps) and had valid HR values (not 0) for at least 30 min before they began moving again55. The minimum HR was defined as the mean of the daily minimum HR obtained from 5-min rolling median HR values. HR dip was defined as the mean value of daily HR dips and calculated as follows: daily HR dip = ([daily awake HR – daily sleeping HR]/daily awake HR) × 10056. The daily awake HR was defined as the median value measured during periods other than the sleep period each day. The accuracy of the HR measured by Fitbit devices is reportedly acceptable compared with the criterion measures57. Although the accuracy of HR measurements during activities such as cycling or resistance training was lower than that during other types of activities, the accuracy of HR measurements during daily living activities, sleep, and rest was deemed acceptable and, thus, may be suitable for measuring HR in population-based studies15.

Statistical analyses

Differences between groups were assessed using the analysis of variance with post-hoc Scheffé analysis for normally distributed data and the Kruskal–Wallis test with post-hoc Dunn test for non-normally distributed data. Multiple logistic regression models were used to estimate the association of each HR index with PreMetS or MetS, with adjustment for confounders (including age, BMI, education level, household income, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, physical activity level, and menopause status [in women]). Multiple logistic regression models were also used to estimate the association between each HR index and each MetS component, with adjustment for the other four components in addition to the aforementioned confounders. As lower AIC and BIC values indicate better model fit, these values were used for best subset selection to identify the best combination of predictors among all covariates included in the fully adjusted models. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The integration of 1-min data derived from wearable devices and calculation of HR indices were performed using Python (version 3.9.5). All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).
