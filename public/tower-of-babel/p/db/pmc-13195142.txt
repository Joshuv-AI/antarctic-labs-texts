Lateral reinforcement of anastomoses enhances mechanical strength in fragile neonatal oesophageal tissue
Authors: Yaohui Guo, Akihiro Yasui, Aitaro Takimoto, Akinari Hinoki, Chiyoe Shirota, Takahisa Tainaka, Wataru Sumida, Satoshi Makita, Hizuru Amano, Takuya Maeda, Daiki Kato, Hiroki Ishii, Kazuki Ota, Jiahui Liu, Eijiro Maeda, Takeo Matsumoto, Hiroo Uchida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnastomotic leakage is a major complication in neonatal oesophageal surgery, particularly in fragile tissues such as long-gap oesophageal atresia. In this study, we evaluate a simple suture modification, known as lateral reinforcement, to improve anastomotic stability and reduce leakage. Mechanical testing was performed using artificial oesophagus models and rat intestinal tissue, comparing standard sutures with and without lateral reinforcement. Tearing force and displacement were measured using a load–displacement device and motion-tracking software. Lateral reinforcement increased tissue-tearing resistance 3.5-fold in artificial models and 2.3-fold in animal models relative to standard sutures. Reinforced constructs also showed greater displacement before failure, indicating improved stress distribution and reduced focal tension. Clinically, the technique was applied in six thoracoscopic repairs of oesophageal atresia complicated by intraoperative tearing, with no postoperative leakage observed. These results suggest that lateral reinforcement enhances mechanical strength and may reduce anastomotic failure in high-risk neonatal cases. Further clinical evaluation using larger cohorts is needed to assess long-term healing and potential effects on stricture formation.Level of evidence: III.Keywords: Oesophageal atresia, Anastomosis reinforcement, Suture technique, Tissue biomechanics, Neonatal surgery, Anastomotic leakage preventionSubject terms: Oesophagus, Paediatric research, Neonatology, Biomedical engineering

Introduction

Oesophageal atresia (EA) repair in neonates is technically challenging because the oesophageal tissue is fragile and prone to tearing under anastomotic tension. Previous analyses of gastrointestinal anastomoses have identified anastomotic tension as an independent predictor of leakage, with an approximately 10-fold increase in odds reported in a large clinical series1. In EA surgery, excessive anastomotic tension similarly increases the risk of stenosis and leakage2–4, particularly in long-gap cases.

However, longitudinal tearing due to tissue fragility can also occur in non–long-gap cases, especially when the oesophagus is thin or delicate. Clinically, anastomotic tension is widely recognised as a key contributor to anastomotic complications, and multiple approaches have been proposed to achieve a more “tension-free” repair in paediatric oesophageal surgery, including devices and techniques designed to reduce local stress at the suture line5. In parallel, reinforcement suturing has been associated with reduced leakage in other gastrointestinal anastomoses, suggesting that mechanical stabilisation of the suture line may be beneficial when tissue is fragile or tension is unavoidable6. Furthermore, variability in applied longitudinal force has been demonstrated even in simulated oesophageal anastomosis settings, highlighting the need for techniques that are robust to differences in intraoperative handling7. Given the increasing adoption of minimally invasive approaches in EA repair and the importance of optimising technique to minimise procedure-related morbidity8, quantitative biomechanical evaluation of reinforcement strategies is warranted. Therefore, we aimed to determine whether a lateral reinforcement stitch redistributes local stress and increases resistance to tearing under traction. Representative clinical cases are included to illustrate intraoperative management of tension-related tearing, rather than to serve as a cohort analysis of long-gap EA.

To achieve this, we used biomimetic materials and performed animal experiments to examine the mechanical properties of the reinforcement technique and to identify an optimal anastomosis approach for fragile neonatal oesophagi.

Results

Tearing tension in artificial and animal models

In the artificial simulation, tearing tension differed by suture technique. The standard 5-mm bite (group C) required 0.43 ± 0.16 N, and the 8-mm bite (group L) required 0.55 ± 0.29 N, showing no significant difference. In contrast, lateral reinforcement markedly increased tear resistance. The reinforced 5-mm bite (group R) required 1.49 ± 0.49 N, and reinforcement placed directly over a laceration (group T) required 1.25 ± 0.40 N, with no significant difference between R and T. Compared with group C, tearing tension increased 3.5- and 2.9-fold in groups R and T, respectively (Fig. 1A).

L: Suture with an 8-mm bite. R: Suture after lateral reinforcement with a 5-mm bite. T: Suture after lateral reinforcement of the laceration with a 5-mm bite.

Animal testing yielded similar findings. Tearing tension was 0.41 ± 0.14 N in group C and 0.40 ± 0.11 N in group L. Groups R and T required 0.93 N to tear (0.93 ± 0.21 N and 0.93 ± 0.40 N, respectively). Thus, reinforcement increased tear resistance approximately 2.3-fold compared with group C (Fig. 1B).

Displacement at tearing in artificial models

Displacement was measured from the initial suspension point until tearing. The normal suture group displaced 15.55 mm (95% confidence interval [CI], 10.90–18.08 mm), whereas the lateral reinforcement group displaced 42.41 mm (95% CI, 22.71–55.80 mm). Reinforcement approximately doubled displacement tolerance (p = 0.0079; Fig. 2).

Displacement at the moment of tearing of the artificial simulation models. The displacement of the suture line’s point of impact in the lateral reinforcement group was twice as long as that in the general suture group (p = 0.0079).

Clinical outcomes

The technique was applied in six thoracoscopic EA repairs complicated by intraoperative longitudinal tearing (Fig. 3). Four patients were male and two female, with a median age of 18.5 days (range, 1–94.25 days). Median birth weight was 1968 g (1590–2670 g) and operative weight 2682 g (1943.25–3482.25 g). EA types were 2-1-2-1 (A-B-C-D classification). No postoperative leaks occurred. Anastomotic strictures requiring balloon dilatation developed in 67% (4/6) of patients, higher than the usual 20%–30%, but all eventually achieved normal oral feeding after serial dilatation (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that lateral reinforcement substantially enhances the tensile strength of anastomoses in fragile oesophageal and intestinal tissues. Mechanical resistance to tearing was 3.5-fold higher in the artificial oesophagus model and 2.3-fold higher in the animal models compared with standard suture techniques, indicating a marked improvement in mechanical strength with reinforcement. These findings underscore the potential of lateral reinforcement as an effective strategy to reduce anastomotic leakage in neonatal and paediatric surgeries, especially in high-tension cases such as long-gap EA.

Recent biomechanical studies have elucidated how suture configuration, bite length, and load distribution influence anastomotic strength. Oetzmann von Sochaczewski et al.9,10 demonstrated in porcine oesophageal models that optimising suture geometry and bite length significantly improves anastomotic strength and elongation tolerance. Drescher et al.11 showed in rat oesophagogastric anastomoses that stress concentration along the suture line contributes to tissue tearing. Similarly, Cui and Urschel12 reported that variations in suture configuration alter local force distribution, affecting anastomotic stability. Toczewski et al.13 further found that reinforced suture constructs redistribute tensile load, increasing failure load and allowable elongation in ex vivo oesophageal models. Collectively, these studies highlight that local stress fields and geometric tension distribution determine anastomotic integrity.

Taken together, prior studies have primarily evaluated whole-anastomosis performance (e.g., burst or leak pressure and elongation), whereas our study complements this literature by quantifying local tear initiation and propagation at the needle–tissue interface. Compared with prior work evaluating complete oesophageal anastomoses, our experimental design intentionally isolated a single suture–tissue interaction to quantify tear initiation and propagation at the needle–tissue interface. Because the initial stitch is placed before mechanical load is distributed across the anastomosis, it may be exposed to higher local tension; therefore, evaluation of a single stitch has practical relevance. In contrast, a full anastomosis distributes force across multiple stitches and tissue layers, such that burst or leak pressure primarily reflects overall sealing and load sharing, whereas our tearing test targets a distinct failure mechanism related to longitudinal traction. The two-layer polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) hydrogel was selected as a reproducible, layered, and tearable substrate for controlled comparisons, although it cannot reproduce perfusion or healing responses. Rupture or tearing under supraphysiologic traction was used to quantify a controlled mechanical safety margin when traction is unavoidable intraoperatively, rather than to directly predict postoperative leakage.

Our findings extend this biomechanical foundation by showing that lateral reinforcement disperses tensile stress across a broader load-bearing area, reducing peak stress on the primary suture line. This mechanism explains the greater tissue displacement at failure in the reinforced group and aligns with prior finite-element and experimental data indicating that increasing tissue–suture contact area enhances rupture resistance. Thus, we provide quantitative evidence that a simple modification in suture placement can meaningfully strengthen anastomoses under high-tension conditions.

Neonatal tissues, characterised by high water content and inherent fragility, pose unique challenges for high-tension anastomoses14. A retrospective study of 74 children with EA identified gap length as an independent risk factor for leakage15. Evidence syntheses similarly show that anastomotic tension is a key determinant of leak risk, although reliable intraoperative quantification remains limited. Meta-analytic data indicate that reinforcement suturing reduces leakage across techniques (pooled odds ratio approximately 0.26; 95% CI 0.18–0.37)16,17. Despite trials exploring fibrin sealants and various biological or synthetic materials, most have not achieved significant reductions in leakage18,19, emphasising the value of surgical technique refinement and improved technical control. Liddy et al.7 demonstrated considerable variability in intraoperative suture force even among experienced surgeons, underscoring the importance of controlled longitudinal force application. These clinical insights are consistent with evidence linking excessive tension and increased gap length to leakage15,20. The lateral reinforcement technique directly addresses these risks by uniformly redistributing tension. In cases where longitudinal tearing occurred during high-tension anastomoses, small horizontal sutures followed by deeper reinforcement stitches effectively prevented extension of the tear. This approach, used in six cases of thoracoscopic EA repair with intraoperative tearing, resulted in no postoperative leakage (Fig. 3). However, anastomotic stricture requiring balloon dilation occurred in four of six patients (67%), higher than reported rates of approximately 20–30%4. Although leakage was avoided, this suggests that while lateral reinforcement improves acute stability, stricture risk may persist. This may be due to focal ischemia or tension concentration at the mucosal layer, which requires further investigation. Importantly, all affected patients resumed normal oral intake after repeated dilation, indicating treatable strictures without long-term functional compromise. Future prospective studies are needed to determine whether reinforcement also affects clinically significant stenosis rates.

Ischemia may also contribute to impaired anastomotic healing. In vivo perfusion studies suggest that anchoring sutures can attenuate tension-related impairment of blood flow and help preserve microvascular perfusion at the anastomotic site21. Moreover, increasing tissue tension has been shown to compromise local perfusion and promote ischemia, which may adversely affect anastomotic healing22,23. Importantly, Walsh et al. reported a markedly low leak rate in esophagogastric anastomoses using anchoring sutures to reduce tension and stabilize the conduit24. Although derived from adult surgery, these findings support the concept that tension management and preservation of perfusion can be clinically actionable and provide a complementary biological rationale for reinforcement strategies. Together with our biomechanical results, this literature strengthens the plausibility that reinforcement may improve anastomotic robustness under high-tension conditions; future studies should further evaluate the interaction between reinforcement geometry, tissue perfusion, and healing in vivo.

In clinical settings, maintaining a uniform 2-mm spacing between reinforcement and primary sutures is challenging; in practice, spacing is typically 2–3 mm. Although Fig. 3 may appear to show wider spacing due to 3-mm surgical forceps, the true distance was approximately 2.5 mm. Therefore, clinical placement closely mirrors the experimental model. These successful clinical outcomes support the biomechanical principles demonstrated in our experiments.

This study has some limitations. First, artificial substrates and rat tissues cannot fully replicate the viscoelastic properties, layered microarchitecture, or healing responses of the human neonatal oesophagus. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as proof of concept regarding local suture–tissue mechanics. To address this limitation, future studies should incorporate additional bench-top testing using an artificial oesophagus under controlled wet conditions, including cyclic loading, to generate reproducible mechanical data, followed by validation in a large-animal model (e.g., porcine) that permits assessment of healing dynamics.

Second, the clinical cohort included only six cases, representing all eligible patients during the study period; therefore, the clinical results should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Larger prospective studies are required to validate these findings and determine statistical significance. Although biomechanical research in paediatric oesophageal surgery remains limited, our interdisciplinary collaboration with mechanical engineering experts enabled robust interpretation of the observed stress redistribution effects. Additional biomechanical modelling studies are planned to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms and strengthen external validation.

In conclusion, lateral reinforcement substantially increases the mechanical strength of fragile tissues, offering a promising method to reduce anastomotic leakage in neonatal and paediatric surgeries. By integrating biomechanical principles with clinical observations, this study demonstrates how lateral reinforcement distributes tension more evenly and enhances tissue tolerance to high stress. This simple, practical technique may improve anastomotic safety in high-tension, fragile-tissue scenarios.

Methods

Experimental materials and anastomotic technique

A two-layer PVA hydrogel oesophageal model was used for biomimetic simulation. The model consisted of separable inner and outer layers (0.5 mm each) and demonstrated high stretchability, softness, and tearability, closely resembling neonatal oesophageal tissue (Fig. 4A). Small intestines from 8-week-old Sprague Dawley rats (Japan SLC Inc., Shizuoka, Japan) were used for in vivo testing. Rats were housed under controlled temperature (23 °C) and a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum food and water. Ten rats (five males, five females) were included. Sample size calculations were based on pilot data for failure load, with a coefficient of variation (CV) of 0.12–0.15. We targeted a relative standard error (SE/mean) of < 5%, calculated using SE/mean = CV/√n, which yielded a required sample size of n ≥ (CV/0.05)² (approximately 6–9 specimens). Accordingly, we selected n = 10 specimens per group to ensure adequate precision while adhering to the principles of replacement, reduction, and refinement (3Rs).

(A) An artificial oesophagus model with a two-layer structure made of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) hydrogel. (B) The small intestine of the SD rat used in the animal experiments. In the animal model, a total of four segments were collected. Starting 5 cm proximal to the terminal ileum, 3-cm sections of the proximal intestine were obtained sequentially.

All studies employed PDS 5 − 0 RB-3 sutures. Rats underwent intraperitoneal anaesthesia using Domitol (0.375 mg/kg), midazolam (2.0 mg/kg), and betanorphine (2.5 mg/kg). Carbon monoxide inhalation was used for euthanasia. Four experimental groups were created (n = 10 per group): normal suture with 5-mm bite (C), normal suture with 8-mm bite (L), 5-mm suture after lateral reinforcement (R), and 5-mm suture after reinforcement of a simulated laceration (T) (Fig. 5). For animal samples, four contiguous 3-cm intestinal segments were harvested starting 5 cm proximal to the terminal ileum (Fig. 4B).

Normal suture with a 5-mm bite (group C). Suture with an 8-mm bite (group L). Suture after lateral reinforcement (blue line segment) with a 5-mm bite (group R). Suture after lateral reinforcement (blue line segment) of the laceration with a 5-mm bite (group T). Suture needle position (red asterisk). Blue line: strings.

To avoid ischemia, only the intestinal tube was mobilised, and four sutures were placed while preserving mesenteric attachment. The mesenteric connection was severed immediately before traction testing. All samples measured 18 mm, and the lateral reinforcement stitch width was 2 mm. All procedures were performed under standardised environmental conditions.

Force measurements

The models were mounted on a motorised vertical force–displacement testing system (FSA-ZTA-500 N, IMADA Co., Aichi, Japan; resolution 0.1 N, 0.2% error). The stand moved at 60 mm/min. The starting point was defined as the moment the suture tightened with zero tension (Fig. 6A). Tearing onset at the suture or reinforcement site marked the endpoint (Fig. 6B). Tearing force was confirmed using load–displacement curves (Fig. 7A, B) and video recordings.

(A) Origin of the measurement: the suture is pulled tight. (B) End of the measurement: the beginning of tearing at the model with dots.

(A) Load–displacement graph of the force–displacement measuring system. Point of beginning to tear (red asterisk). (B) Time–displacement graph of the force–displacement measuring system.

Displacement analyses

To assess how lateral reinforcement increases tensile tolerance, four rows and three columns of tracking points were marked on the artificial model using oil-based dye (Fig. 6B). Tension testing was conducted as above with a 5-mm bite, and displacement data were captured using DIPP-Motion V/3D software (DITECT Co. Ltd., Japan). Automated and manual point-tracking generated XY displacement values, enabling comparison between standard and reinforced suture configurations.

Clinical application

The reinforcement technique was applied in select neonatal cases where longitudinal tearing occurred during thoracoscopic anastomosis. After closing the tear with a horizontal stitch, a deeper lateral reinforcement stitch was placed to prevent further extension. Indications, operative steps, and postoperative outcomes were recorded (Table 1).

Statistical analyses

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0 (macOS). Graphs were created in Microsoft Excel 2021 and Prism 9 (GraphPad Software). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation and 95% CIs. Group comparisons used one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test. Displacements were analysed at 0.1 N intervals and compared with the Mann–Whitney U-test. Significance was set at p < 0.05.
