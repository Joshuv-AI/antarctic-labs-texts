Aniline-induced refractory methemoglobinemia in polytrauma: successful management with erythrocytapheresis
Authors: Karolína Lečbychová, Marcela Káňová, Paula Dzurňáková, Šárka Blahutová, Tereza Romanová, Denis Buršík
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMethemoglobinemia is a life-threatening condition characterized by the inability of hemoglobin to transport oxygen efficiently. Background

Methemoglobinemia is a rare but life-threatening condition characterized by the oxidation of hemoglobin's ferrous iron (Fe2⁺) to ferric iron (Fe3⁺), forming methemoglobin (MetHb). Unlike normal hemoglobin, MetHb cannot bind or transport oxygen, resulting in impaired oxygen delivery to tissues. In healthy individuals, MetHb constitutes less than 1% of total hemoglobin. However, elevated levels of MetHb, particularly above 30%, can cause hypoxia, cyanosis, and, in severe cases, death if untreated [1].

The diagnosis is based on patient history, detection of a saturation gap (difference between arterial and peripheral saturations, SaO2 and SpO2), supported by an oxygen saturation discrepancy (SpO₂ around 85%) with clinical signs of cyanosis, and confirmation using CO-oximetry, which directly measures MetHb levels [2, 3].

Red blood cells counteract MetHb formation primarily through the enzymatic activity of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH)-dependent cytochrome b5 reductase, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH)-MetHb reductase, and glutathione/NADPH. The production of NADPH depends on the proper function of the enzyme glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), whose mutation is one of the congenital causes of MetHb [4].

Acquired cases are more commonly encountered, triggered by exposure to oxidative agents such as aniline dyes, nitrites, or certain medications (e.g. sulfonamides, dapsone, and benzocaine) [5]. Aniline dyes are lipophilic compounds metabolized into phenylhydroxylamine, an oxidative metabolite that can be repeatedly formed and further contributes to methemoglobin formation and red cell hemolysis [6].

Treatment typically involves methylene blue, a synthetic compound that works as a cofactor of NADPH-MetHb, creating leukomethylene blue, which reduces MetHb by reducing Fe3⁺ to Fe2⁺ [7]. The initial dose is 1–2 mg/kg and can be repeated up to 7 mg/kg. However, methylene blue can cause hemolysis and may be ineffective in ongoing oxidative stress or significant cases of MetHb rebound. Other treatment options include glucose as a substrate for NADPH, ascorbic acid, and N-acetylcysteine to promote glutathione function as a cellular antioxidant [8]. Alternative therapies are described, such as hyperbaric oxygen or exchange erythrocytapheresis [9–12].

Case presentation

A 39-year-old man suffered extensive injuries after an explosion in a chemical factory, which caused him to fall into a barrel containing aniline. On arrival of the emergency medical services (EMS), the patient was conscious (GCS 14) but confused, complaining of back and right arm pain. Initial treatment included decontamination, analgesia, and sedation for manipulation and securing of the airway, given the risk of progression of suspected chemical burn injuries of the airways because of the mechanism of injury. After initiation of positive pressure ventilation, there were diminished breath sounds on the left side and hypotension, which required chest decompression for pneumothorax. The patient was immobilized with cervical collar and pelvic binder and put into vaccum mattress. He was also hemodynamically unstable (MAP < 65 mmHg) despite fluid administration and decompression and required vasopressor support to keep MAP > 65 mmHg during transport to a trauma centre.

At the trauma centre, examination revealed greyish discolouration of the skin, SpO₂ of 85%, heart rate 120/min, and hypotension (blood pressure 90/50 mmHg on noradrenalin 0,2 mcg/kg/min), capillary refill more than 2 s, and the patient was sedated (RASS −5) and on mechanical ventilation. The FAST exam revealed a pneumothorax also on the right side, followed by bilateral insertion of chest drains. Burn injuries (15% of the total body surface [TBSA], 12% of the first-degree, and 3% of the second-degree) were observed on the legs, mons pubis, and buttocks. Imaging (CT) ruled out head injury but confirmed fractures of the pelvis, sacrum (symphyseolysis, sacroiliac joint diastasis), retroperitoneal haematomas without active leak, and fracture of the right elbow. ABG on arrival showed significant metabolic acidosis (pH 7.28, lactate 5.3 mmol/L, BE −4.8), hemoglobin (Hb) 134 g/L, and MetHb levels exceeding 20%, which were unmeasurable due to the limit of the point-of-care (POCT) machine available in the hospital. A saturation gap of 9% further supported methemoglobinemia. Trauma severity according to the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) was graded ≥ 3 (chest, extremities/pelvis), and the Injury Severity Score (ISS) was a total of 29, and combined with physiologic variable (systolic blood pressure ≤ 90 mmHg), fulfilling the Berlin definition of polytrauma [13].

Initial treatment with 200 mg of methylene blue was administered, followed by 100 mg of methylene blue and 10 g of ascorbic acid. Treatment included fluid administration, administration of procoagulation material (4 g of fibrinogen), four units of red blood cells (RBCs), and two units of fresh frozen plasma (FFP) for hemodynamic instability. Despite this, the patient remained hemodynamically unstable, requiring combined vasopressor support (norepinephrine and vasopressin). Due to metabolic derangement, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was initiated. Repeat blood analysis showed persistently elevated MetHb levels, severe tissue hypoxia, coagulopathy, and refractory metabolic acidosis.

Due to the lack of significant clinical improvement, automated red blood cell exchange was performed 24 h after admission. During the 4-h procedure, 4701 mL of blood was exchanged, and 17 units of leukocyte-depleted RBCs were transfused. The patient's clinical condition improved significantly following the intervention, with no adverse side effects, and no further erythrocytapheresis was necessary. He was eventually weaned off vasopressors and CRRT. However, lactate levels normalized over the next 48 h, and MetHb levels declined rapidly post-exchange but returned to normal only 90 h after the injury (Fig. 1). Once the patient's condition stabilized, he underwent planned surgical interventions to fix the fracture. Later, nosocomial infections, including Aspergillus fumigatus pneumonia and sepsis caused by haemolytic E. coli, complicated the hospitalization. After 67 days of hospitalization, the patient was discharged to a rehabilitation centre. During follow-up, he reported good physical fitness, weight recovery, and no significant psychological sequelae.

Discussion

Acquired methemoglobinemia is often caused by exposure to oxidative agents such as aniline dyes. Aniline can cause toxicity through local skin, corneal contact, inhalation, or ingestion, all of which were possible routes of exposure in our patient. Due to refractory methemoglobinemia, probably caused by the ongoing production of phenylhydroxylamine and the overwhelmed patient's redox systems, methylene blue had only a temporary effect [14]. Other pharmacologic options include N-acetylcysteine, glucose, and ascorbic acid in high doses [15].

Furthermore, the lower the Hb and the higher the levels of MetHb, the less functional Hb is available, leading to functional anemia [16]. Concurrent hemorrhage and coagulopathy further limited oxygen delivery.

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy was contraindicated due to pneumothorax, leaving erythrocytapheresis as a possible alternative. Although erythrocytapheresis is not routinely used for methemoglobinemia, the literature supports its role in refractory cases in patients with G6PD deficiency [12, 17, 18], and even case reports in patients without this deficiency describe no significant adverse events [19–22]. However, none of the case reports in the literature describe its use in refractory methemoglobinemia caused by aniline dye exposure in a patient with polytrauma having multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS).

Conclusion

This case highlights the complexity of treating methemoglobinemia in the setting of polytrauma and ongoing hemorrhagic, traumatic, and initially obstructive shock. Early recognition and treatment with methylene blue are essential, but refractory cases may require alternative approaches. Erythrocytapheresis proved to be a safe and effective therapy, restoring tissue oxygenation quickly, permanently eliminating dysfunctional erythrocytes, and improving clinical outcomes within a couple of hours. This case underscores the importance of considering advanced therapies in refractory methemoglobinemia and adds to the growing evidence supporting erythrocytapheresis as a valuable option in aniline intoxication.
