Repeated oral administration of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) does not cause reproductive or developmental toxicity in rats
Authors: Xuan Li, Aditya Kulkarni, Hiroaki Yamada, Yusuke Yamashita, Young-il Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) occurs naturally in a variety of foods and has a long history of dietary consumption, yet its effects on reproduction and development have not been evaluated in a guideline-compliant toxicological study. We examined the reproductive and developmental endpoints of repeated oral gavage GABA administration in Sprague Dawley (SD) rats using an enhanced OECD (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development) 421–compliant design, in which groups of 20 males and 20 females received daily doses of 0, 625, 1250, or 2500 mg/kg/day GABA. The OECD 421 protocol was modified to include male dosing lasting 89–92 days, covering the pre-mating, mating, and post-mating periods. Females were dosed for 50–63 days from pre-mating through Lactation Day 12, with protocol-based adjustments for reproductive status. No treatment-related mortality, systemic toxicity, or organ pathology was observed. Estrous cyclicity, mating performance, fertility index, implantation, gestation, parturition, and maternal behavior remained unchanged. Offspring viability and developmental landmarks showed no treatment-related differences. Hormonal assessments revealed no pattern indicative of reproductive or thyroid disruption. Overall, repeated oral GABA administration in SD rats up to 2500 mg/kg/day produced no adverse effects on parental health, reproductive outcomes, or offspring development.Keywords: Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), Reproductive toxicity, Developmental toxicity, OECD 421, Oral administration, Safety

Introduction

Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) is a naturally occurring, non-protein amino acid widely present in prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms. In vertebrates, GABA is synthesized endogenously from glutamate via glutamate decarboxylase (GAD), while additional GABA can be obtained from the diet, particularly from fruits and vegetables, grain-based products, edible fungi, and fermented foods [1], [2]. In both the brain and the peripheral systems, GABA is quickly metabolized through the GABA shunt pathway, primarily catalyzed by GABA transaminase (GABA-T) and succinic semialdehyde dehydrogenase (SSADH) [3]. GABA is well known for its role as the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter in regulating neuronal excitability, synaptic transmission, and neuroendocrine function. Brain GABA levels are under control of active production and transport mechanisms associated with feedback from local brain extracellular GABA concentrations and likely less influenced by peripheral GABA concentrations [4], [5], [6]. Beyond its classical neurophysiological actions, GABA also participates in peripheral processes including modulation of insulin secretion, immune cell activity, and gastrointestinal tract functions [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]. In recent years, GABA has gained attention as a functional dietary supplement due to its proposed benefits in reducing stress and anxiety [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], alleviating hypertension [17], [18], [19], [20], improving sleep quality [13], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], and enhancing cognitive health [26], [27]. Commercially available GABA is manufactured by microbial fermentation, enzymatic conversion, or chemical synthesis. Modern industrial processes predominantly use microbial fermentation, often employing lactic acid bacteria genera capable of converting monosodium glutamate into GABA via GAD activity [3], [28].

As a substance widely abundant in nature, GABA has been a long-standing component of the human diet. This is especially evident in Asia, which maintains a rich tradition of safely consuming GABA-rich foods, including fermented soy products, germinated brown rice, and specific teas and vegetables [2]. Regulatory bodies in major Asian markets, such as China, Japan, South Korea, Thailand, Vietnam, and Philippines recognize the use of GABA as a food or functional ingredient, permitting its inclusion in GABA-enriched products under defined conditions [29], [30]. Labeling analyses indicate that doses of approximately 100–750 mg/day are commonly used in supplements marketed in these countries. In the United States, GABA qualifies as a dietary ingredient under section 201(ff)(1)(D) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act). GABA is included in the United Natural Products Alliance (UNPA) list of dietary ingredients prior to the enactment of Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA) in 1994 and has been incorporated into a wide range of products marketed as dietary supplements [3]. As of 17 November 2025, the Dietary Supplement Label Database (DSLD) listed 1883 products declaring GABA on their labels [31]. Analyses of dietary supplement labeling listed in DSLD indicate recommended intakes ranging from approximately 45 mg to 3 g/day, with most products recommending about 600–750 mg/day of GABA in divided doses [3]. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)’s Substances Added to Food database also identifies GABA as an approved substance used for technical purposes, such as a flavoring agent or adjuvant [32]. GABA has also been reviewed by the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) in the context of its use as a food additive or potential contaminant. JECFA concluded that the tissue levels of GABA resulting from its use as a flavoring agent would be biologically insignificant and therefore not pose a safety risk [33]. As GABA is a naturally occurring amino acid present in various foods and produced endogenously by the human body, major international food safety authorities have not established a formal Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) as they do for synthetic additives. Nonetheless, following standard toxicological frameworks where the ADI is derived by applying a 100-fold uncertainty factor to the no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) [34], the ADI for GABA has been calculated to be 25 mg/kg body weight based on safety data from rodent models [35], [36].

Animal toxicology data consistently indicate a low toxicity profile for orally administered GABA. A review by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in 1997 noted no signs of toxicity in chronic rodent and canine studies at doses up to 1 g/kg/day [37]. Later, acute toxicity studies in rats showed no clinical signs or mortality after a single oral dose equivalent to 1000 mg/kg GABA, establishing an LD₅₀ > 5000 mg/kg for the test material [38], consistent with earlier reports of even higher LD₅₀ values in mice (12,000 mg/kg) [39]. Sub-chronic exposure in rats for 28 days at approximately 36–50 mg/day (estimated based on mean daily food consumption) by ad libitum feeding revealed no treatment-related effects on body weight, food intake, hematology, clinical chemistry, or organ histopathology; minor increases in testis weight were observed but were considered toxicologically insignificant as it fell within historical ranges and lacked correlated pathological findings [40]. A 13-week study with doses up to 2500 mg/kg/day in SD rats reported good tolerability, with only mild, transient clinical signs at the highest dose and no dose-dependent changes in clinical pathology or organ morphology [35]. Recently, a 2025 pre-clinical mouse study demonstrated that long-term oral GABA supplementation at 10–20 mg/kg/day produced anxiolytic effects without any detectable adverse outcomes, including no alterations in proteomic or immunomodulatory profiles [41].

Clinical studies, although most of them were not originally designed as safety trials, collectively provide strong evidence that oral GABA is well tolerated in humans. A wide dosing range (10–18,000 mg/day) of GABA in various matrices, such as fermented milk, natural product extracts, and purified GABA supplement, have been evaluated over periods of 4 days to 13 weeks [19], [42], [43], [44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50]. At low doses (10–75 mg/day), GABA intake for 4–12 weeks was not associated with adverse effects, with no changes in vital signs, body weight, hematological or blood chemistry parameters, or urinalysis findings ( [19]; Tanaka, 2009; [50]). Repeated intake of 200–300 mg/day for 4–13 weeks was likewise well tolerated, showing no clinically meaningful changes in physical examinations, blood or urine parameters, or metabolic markers, and no adverse events at 200–250 mg/day [45], [48], [49]. At 300 mg/day, only mild, transient symptoms (abdominal discomfort, headache) were observed in a limited number of subjects [42]. Even substantially higher exposures, including 2 g three times daily for 7 days or short-term intake up to 18 g/day, did not result in serious adverse effects [43], [44], with only transient discomforts such as dizziness and sore throat reported [46]. Recently, a 6-month randomized trial in adults with long-standing type 1 diabetes evaluated a controlled-release oral GABA formulation at 200–600 mg/day and similarly reported no major safety concerns; aside from isolated, non–dose-dependent, transient elevations in liver enzymes, clinical chemistry, urinalysis, vital signs, neurological assessments, and electrocardiography findings remained unaltered from baseline [51]. Collectively, these findings suggest that oral GABA is generally well tolerated at commonly used doses in human studies. While some symptoms have been reported at doses ≥ 300 mg/day, these effects appear to be sporadic, transient, and generally mild. Studies evaluating gram-level doses have typically involved relatively short dosing durations; therefore, longer-term safety at these higher doses remains less well characterized. Across the available studies, including those with durations of up to 6 months, no consistent safety concerns have been identified.

Although GABA has been extensively evaluated in clinical and toxicological settings, these studies have not examined its potential effects on fertility, reproduction, or developmental processes. It is recognized that testis, spermatozoa and ovaries possess GABA receptors, and GABA may play a role in reproduction [52], [53], [54], [55]. To clarify whether repeated oral intake of GABA influences reproductive or developmental outcomes in vivo, we conducted a screening study to evaluate the potential effects of GABA produced through Lactobacillus hilgardii-mediated fermentation on mating performance, fertility, gestation, and postnatal development in rats. The study employed an OECD (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development) Test Guideline 421-aligned but enhanced design, which incorporated extended dosing and additional endpoints in males to better cover the timing associated with spermatogenesis.

Materials and methods

Ethics and compliance

This study was conducted in compliance with the OECD Principles of Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) (ENV/MC/CHEM(98)17), Ministerial Ordinance on Good Laboratory Practice for Nonclinical Safety Studies of Drugs, and 21 CFR Part 58—Good Laboratory Practice for nonclinical studies. All experimental procedures involving animals were performed in strict accordance with ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines, the Act on Welfare and Management of Animals (Act No. 105 of 1973, as amended by Act No. 39 of 2019), the Standards relating to the Care and Management of Laboratory Animals and Relief of Pain (Notice No. 88 of 2006, as amended by Notice No. 84 of 2013), and the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th Edition)[56]. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Trans Genic Inc., IWATA Research Institute (Approval No. 24–0288 A). Efforts were made to minimize the number of animals used and to reduce their suffering.

Test article and characterization

GABA used in this study (Lot No.: 3L09), marketed as PharmaGABA®, was provided by Pharma Foods International Co., Ltd. (Kyoto, Japan). PharmaGABA® is derived from glutamate via a fermentation process using an organism originally designated as Lactobacillus hilgardii K-3. As with other lactobacilli found in fermented foods, Lactobacillus hilgardii K-3 has subsequently determined via genetic analysis to be identified as Levilactobacillus senmaizukei belonging to the group Lactobacillus brevis [57], [58]. PharmaGABA® is a water-soluble, white to pale yellow powder and was stored in bulk at ≤ 15 °C in an air-tight container protected from light. Independent compositional analysis (Sekisui Medical Co., Ltd., Ibaraki, Japan) confirmed a purity of 100.0% ± 1.0% (mean ± standard deviation) at study initiation. Post-study analysis indicated a purity of 96.8%, demonstrating preservation of chemical integrity of GABA throughout the study period under the storage conditions employed (Supplemental Method S1, Figure S1).

Distilled water (Otsuka Pharmaceutical Factory, Inc., Tokushima, Japan) served as the vehicle for the GABA dosing formulations. Dosing formulations of 62.5, 125, and 250 mg/mL were prepared by dissolving GABA in distilled water and stored in amber glass bottles under refrigerated conditions until administration. Stability of the dosing formulations at 10 and 250 mg/mL was independently verified by Sekisui Medical Co., Ltd. (Ibaraki, Japan) following 10 days of refrigerated storage and subsequent storage for up to 24 h at room temperature. The dosing formulations were administered under conditions for which stability was assured.

Animals

Sprague–Dawley rats [Crl:CD(SD)] were obtained from Jackson Laboratory Japan, Inc (Yokohama, Japan). Male rats were purchased at 6 weeks of age and dosing commenced at 7 weeks of age. Female rats were received at 7 weeks of age. Estrous cycles were monitored throughout the pre-dosing period, and dosing was initiated when the animals were 10 weeks of age. Animals were housed in a barrier facility under controlled environmental conditions (19–25°C, relative humidity 35–70%, 12-h light/dark cycle, ≥ 12 air changes/hour) with ad libitum access to standard laboratory diet (CRF-1, Oriental Yeast) and tap water. Animals were housed two per cage during acclimation and then individually housed after group assignment, except during mating when one male and one female were paired. Pregnant females were individually housed through gestation and lactation with appropriate nesting and enrichment materials.

Study design

The study was conducted in compliance with OECD Principles of Good Laboratory Practice and followed OECD Guideline for the Testing of Chemicals No. 421:: Reproduction/Developmental Toxicity Screening Test (2016) [59], with an extended dosing period in males to ensure coverage of a full spermatogenic cycle.

Male and female Crl:CD(SD) rats were randomly allocated to four groups receiving 0 (vehicle only), 625, 1250, or 2500 mg/kg/day of GABA. A previous 13-week study showed only minor changes in body weight and food consumption at 2500 mg/kg/day in rats with no observed toxicity [35]. Accordingly, 2500 mg/kg/day was selected as the high dose in the present study, with 1250 and 625 mg/kg/day chosen using a 2-fold interval. Each group consisted of 20 males and 20 females. This group size exceeds the minimum of 10 animals per sex per group recommended in OECD Test Guideline 421 and was selected to increase the number of reproductive observations, to improve the robustness of the analysis, and to accommodate for the assessment of the additional endpoints. Group assignment was performed using a computerized stratified randomization based on body weight measured on the day prior to dosing. Body weights of the assigned animals were within ±20% of the sex-specific mean at allocation. Female rats with regular estrous cycles during the pre-dosing period were eligible for assignment. Animals with irregular estrous cycles or inadequate body weight gain during acclimation were excluded.

Male rats were dosed once daily for 89–92 consecutive days, consisting of a 54-day pre-mating period, a 14-day mating period, a 21-day post-mating period, and an additional 0–3 days to accommodate necropsy scheduling. Female rats were dosed once daily for 50–63 consecutive days, including a 14-day pre-mating period, a mating period of up to 14 days or until copulation, and the period from copulation through gestation and until Lactation Day 12. For females with no evidence of copulation after a 14-day mating period, dosing continued for a total of 53 consecutive days (i.e., 14 days pre-mating, 14 days mating, and 25 days after the mating period). For females that copulated but did not deliver, dosing was conducted for 40–43 consecutive days, consisting of 14 days pre-mating, a mating period of up to 14 days (or until copulation), and dosing through Gestation Day 24. The group assignment and dosing regimen are illustrated schematically in Fig. 1.

The test article was formulated in distilled water as aforementioned and administered by gavage at a dose volume of 1.0 mL/100 g body weight, adjusted according to the most recently measured body weight. The study was conducted as the facility of Trans Genic Inc., IWATA Research Institute (Iwata, Japan).

Observations, measurements, and examinations

Clinical observation

Clinical observations were conducted twice daily (before and after dosing) throughout the dosing period. On the day of scheduled necropsy, animals were observed once prior to the necropsy procedures. Clinical observations consisted of cage-side examinations for mortality, morbidity, general appearance, posture, behavior, respiration, and any other signs of toxicity.

Body weight

Male rats were weighed on Days 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, 49, 56, 63, 70, 77, 84, and 88 of dosing, and body weight gain from Day 0 to Day 88 was calculated. Male body weight was also recorded on the day of necropsy (Days 89–92). Female rats were weighed on Days 0, 7, and 14 of dosing, and body weight gain from Day 0 to Day 14 was calculated. For females that did not copulate, body weight was additionally recorded on Days 21, 28, 35, 42, 49, and 53 (necropsy day). For females that copulated, body weight was recorded on Gestation Days 0, 7, 14, and 20, and body weight gain from Gestation Day 0 to Day 20 was calculated. For females that delivered, body weight was recorded on Lactation Days 0, 4, and 13 (necropsy day), and body weight gain from Lactation Day 0 to Day 13 was calculated.

Food consumption

Food consumption was measured for all animals on the same days that body weight was recorded throughout the dosing period. For females, food consumption was additionally measured on Gestation Day 18 and Lactation Day 1 (rather than Lactation Day 0). The mean daily food consumption (g/day) for each animal was calculated from the difference between the amount of food supplied and the amount remaining at each measurement interval. Food consumption was not monitored during the mating period.

Estrous cycle

The estrous cycle of each female was monitored daily from the initiation of dosing until copulation. The estrous cycle length for each female was determined as the number of days from one estrous phase to the day before the next estrous phase (for females showing consecutive estrous days, the first estrous day was used as the starting point). The mean estrous cycle length and the incidence of abnormal cycles (cycles of not 4 or 5 days) were calculated for the females observed. In addition, vaginal smears were examined on the morning of necropsy.

Mating

Females were monitored for estrous cyclicity for 14 days prior to mating. Each female was paired overnight with a single male from the same dose group (1:1 mating) for up to 14 days. Copulation was confirmed the following morning by the presence of a vaginal plug or sperm in a vaginal smear. The day sperm was detected was designated as Gestation Day 0.

The copulation index was calculated for each group as follows:

Copulation index (%) = (Number of females that successfully copulated/Number of females paired for mating) × 100

Natural delivery and offspring

All copulated females were allowed to deliver naturally. From Gestation Day 20 to Day 25, animals were checked daily for signs of delivery between 08:30 and 10:00. For females that completed delivery during this period, or for those that began delivery during the observation window and completed it later the same day, the day on which delivery was completed was designated as Lactation Day 0. For females that initiated delivery after 10:00, completion of delivery was confirmed the following morning. Females that had not delivered by 09:00 on Gestation Day 25 were weighed and euthanized. The uterus of each animal was stained with 10% ammonium sulfide solution to determine the presence of implantation sites. As no implantation sites were detected in any of these females, they were regarded as non-pregnant. Dams that delivered were necropsied on Lactation Day 13. At necropsy, the numbers of corpora lutea and implantation sites were recorded and examined for macroscopic abnormalities. The gestation period (defined as the interval from Gestation Day 0 to Lactation Day 0), as well as the fertility index, gestation index, implantation index, and delivery index, were calculated in accordance with standard reproductive performance assessment procedures as follows:

Fertility index = (Number of pregnant females / Number of successfully copulated females) × 100

Gestation index = (Number of dams with live newborns / Number of pregnant females) × 100

Implantation index = (Number of implantation sites / Numbers of corpora lutea) × 100

Delivery index = (Number of newborns / Number of implantation sites) × 100

Each litter was examined immediately after delivery to determine the total number of offspring (live pups, dead pups, and cannibalized pups). The live birth index on Lactation Day 0 was calculated as:

Live birth index (%) = (Number of live offspring at birth / Total number of offspring delivered) × 100

Offspring were sexed on Lactation Day 0, and the sex ratio was determined for each litter and for each treatment group. The sex ratio of each group was calculated as:

Sex ratio (group) = Number of live male offspring in the group / Total number of offspring in the group

Sex ratio (litter) = Number of live male offspring in the litter / Total number of offspring in the litter

Live offspring were examined for external anomalies, and the incidence of external anomalies was calculated as:

Incidences of external anomalies (%) = (Number of live offspring with external anomalies / Number of live offspring examined) × 100

On Lactation Day 4, litters containing nine or more live pups were standardized to eight pups per litter (four males and four females when possible) by random selection. Before culling, anogenital distance (AGD) was measured for all live pups using a digital caliper. At least two culled pups of each sex, when possible, were anesthetized for blood collection, and serum samples were pooled per dam and stored at −80°C until hormone determination. Remaining culled pups were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia followed by hypothermia and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin.

Live pups were weighed individually on Lactation Days 0, 4, and 13, and mean body weights were calculated for each sex within each litter. The number of live and dead pups was recorded daily through Lactation Day 13, and the general condition of live pups was monitored. The number of nipples/areolae in male pups was recorded on Lactation Day 13. Dead pups were preserved in Bouin's solution, and cannibalized pups were discarded. The viability index on Lactation Day 4 and Day 13 was calculated respectively as:

Viability index Day 4 (%) = (Number of live offspring on Lactation Day 4 / Number of live offspring at birth) × 100

Viability index Day 13 (%) = (Number of live offspring on lactation D13 / Number of live offspring on Lactation Day 4 after adjustment) × 100

Selected hormones

Serum hormone levels were measured in selected animals. Pooled serum samples were obtained from at least two pups of each sex per litter on Lactation Day 4 and again from two pups of each sex per litter on Lactation Day 13. Serum was also collected from all dams on Lactation Day 13 and from all males at scheduled necropsy after completion of the dosing period. Serum samples were stored at −80°C and analyzed within three months of collection. Testosterone (T), estradiol (E2), progesterone (P4), thyroxine (T4), and triiodothyronine (T3) were measured by using a chemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer (ADVIA Centaur CP, Siemens), while thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was determined by ELISA (LIBIS Rat TSH ELISA Kit, FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan). For dams whose progesterone concentrations exceeded the upper assay limit (60 ng/mL), samples were diluted and re-measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Pathological examination

Pathological evaluation included gross necropsy, organ weight measurement, and histopathological examination. Animals scheduled for necropsy were euthanized by exsanguination under isoflurane anesthesia following blood collection. Serum prepared from collected blood was used for hormone measurements, and plasma was used for GABA quantification. A complete gross necropsy was performed by examining the external surface, body orifices, and internal organs in the thoracic, abdominal, pelvic, and cranial cavities, and any macroscopic abnormalities were recorded.

In males, the testes, epididymides, seminal vesicles (including coagulating glands), prostate, levator ani–bulbocavernosus muscle, Cowper’s glands, glans penis, and thyroid glands were weighed, and relative organ weights were calculated using terminal body weight. Tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, and the testes and epididymides were prefixed in modified Davidson’s solution. One side of the testes, epididymides, seminal vesicles, and prostate was used for histopathology, while the contralateral side of these reproductive organs and the brain were retained for GABA quantification or sperm evaluation and stored frozen.

In females that delivered, necropsy was performed on Lactation Day 13. The number of implantation sites was recorded, and the ovaries, uterus, and thyroid glands were weighed and fixed, with relative organ weights calculated. The right ovary and uterine horn were processed for histopathology, while the remaining side and the brain were retained for GABA quantification and stored frozen. Females that did not copulate were necropsied on the day after final dosing, and females that copulated but did not deliver were necropsied on Gestation Day 25; in both cases, the same organs and tissues collected from dams were fixed for histological evaluation.

On Lactation Day 13, two pups of each sex per litter were euthanized following blood sampling for serum hormone measurement, and an additional two pups of each sex per litter, when feasible, were used for plasma collection for GABA quantification. Brains from one male and one female pup per litter were collected for GABA quantification and stored frozen, and thyroid glands from one male and one female pup per litter were fixed in formalin for histopathological evaluation.

Fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Testes were additionally evaluated using Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining to assess spermatogenic stage. Histopathological examinations were performed on all males and females in all dose groups, and qualitative evaluation of spermatogenesis was also conducted in males without evidence of mating or fertility.

Sperm examination

At necropsy, the left cauda epididymis of each surviving male was collected and weighed for sperm analysis; the remaining epididymis was retained for GABA quantification. Sperm count was evaluated using a computer-assisted sperm analysis system (SMAS, DITECT), and sperm morphology was assessed microscopically.

For sperm count, the left cauda epididymis was placed in modified human tubal fluid (mHTF) medium containing bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated at 37°C to allow sperm to disperse into the medium. The sperm suspension was then diluted and analyzed using SMAS, and the number of sperm was expressed as the number of sperm per gram of cauda epididymis.

For sperm morphology evaluation, an aliquot of the sperm suspension was stained with Eosin-Y, smeared onto glass slides, air-dried, and examined microscopically. Approximately 200 sperm per male were evaluated, and the percentage of morphologically abnormal sperm was calculated as:

Abnormal sperm (%) = (Number of sperm with abnormal morphology / Total number of sperm observed) × 100

GABA quantification

GABA concentrations were measured in surviving males at necropsy and in dams on Lactation Day 13. In males, plasma, brain, and reproductive organs (testes, epididymides, seminal vesicles, and prostate) were analyzed, and in dams, plasma, brain, ovaries, and uterine horn were examined. For offspring euthanized on Lactation Day 13, GABA levels were determined in plasma and brain tissue. Organs and plasma collected for GABA analysis were stored at −80°C until sample preparation and analysis. Prepared samples were also stored at −80°C and analyzed within four weeks of preparation. GABA was quantified using a GABA ELISA kit (ImmuSmol SAS, Bordeaux, France), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The assay quantifies γ-aminobutyric acid itself and therefore detects GABA present in biological matrices irrespective of its origin, including GABA absorbed following administration of the test article.

Statistical analysis

Pairwise comparisons between each GABA-treated group and the vehicle control were performed for body weight, body weight gain, food consumption, mean estrous cycle length, numbers of corpora lutea and implantation sites, gestation length, numbers of offspring delivered, live and dead offspring counts, cannibalism on Lactation Day 0, sex ratio per litter, implantation and delivery indices, incidence of external anomalies, live birth index on Lactation Days 0, 4, and 13, anogenital distance on Lactation Day 4, nipple retention in male pups on Lactation Day 13, organ weights and relative organ weights, hormone levels, sperm parameters, and GABA levels in organs and plasma. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Bartlett’s test, respectively. When assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were met, data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s test for comparisons with the control group. When either assumption was violated, Steel’s test was used instead. The significance level for the Shapiro−Wilk test, Bartlett’s test, and one-way ANOVA was two-sided at 5%. The significance level for the Steel’s test was two-sided at 5% and 1%. The gestation index, copulation index, and fertility index were analyzed using the χ² test, with two-sided significance levels of 5% and 1% applied for comparisons between each GABA-treated group and the vehicle control. The incidence of irregular estrous cycles and the sex ratio per group were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test, also at two-sided significance levels of 5% and 1%. During lactation, offspring data were analyzed on a per-litter basis, except for anogenital distance and nipple retention, which were analyzed using individual pup data. Data from non-pregnant females were excluded from analyses of gestational and litter outcomes. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Effects of repeated dose of GABA on parental animals

Clinical signs

No deaths or treatment-related clinical signs were observed in either sex throughout the dosing period. Routine cage-side examinations showed no changes in general appearance, posture, behavior, respiration, or any other signs of toxicity. Similarly, no abnormalities were recorded during pre-necropsy observations on the day of scheduled necropsy.

Body weight

Body weight of the male rats increased steadily throughout the 88-day dosing period across all groups. The body-weight trajectories of the GABA-treated groups (625, 1250, and 2500 mg/kg/day) closely overlapped with that of the vehicle control, and no statistically significant differences were observed between any groups at any time point. In line with this, total body-weight gain did not differ significantly between any GABA-treated group and the control (Fig. 2A). In females, no significant differences in weight changes or weight gain were noted among dose groups during the pre-mating, gestation, or lactation periods (Fig. 2B).

Food consumption

Food consumption of the male rats remained generally constant throughout the 88-day dosing period across all groups (Fig. 3, upper panel). A significantly lower value was only recorded in the 2500 mg/kg/day group from Day 0 to Day 7 of dosing (p-value ≤ 0.05, Dunnett’s test). In females, food consumption during the pre-mating, gestation, and lactation periods was comparable among groups (Fig. 3, lower panels), though lower consumption was observed in the 2500 mg/kg/day group during early gestation (Day 0–7) (p-value ≤ 0.05, Dunnett’s test) and late lactation (Day 4–13) (p-value ≤ 0.01, Steel’s test).

Gross necropsy observations

There were no treatment-related lesions in either sex. In males, one animal from the control group whose mating partner was infertile exhibited small testes and epididymides. No abnormalities were observed in three males from the control group with unsuccessful mating. A minor focal depression in the right kidney was observed in one animal from the high-dose group. In females that delivered normally, white patches in the stomach (high-dose group), diverticula in the small intestine (low-dose group), hepatodiaphragmatic nodules in the liver (mid-dose group), focal depressions in the kidneys (control and mid-dose groups), ovarian cysts (mid-dose group), and uterine cysts (control group) were observed. Vaginal cysts were noted in one non-pregnant female from the control group, and one non-pregnant female from the mid-dose group. Focal depressions in the kidneys were observed in one female from the control group with unsuccessful mating (Supplemental Table S1A-D). These findings are considered spontaneous lesions commonly observed in laboratory rats [60], [61], [62], [63] and were detected in both GABA-treated groups and the vehicle control group.

Histopathological observations

Histopathological examination was performed on the testes and epididymis of males and the ovaries of females. In one male from the vehicle control group with an infertile partner, mild atrophy and vacuolation of seminiferous tubules, minimal interstitial edema, minimal Leydig cell hyperplasia in the testes, and mildly decreased sperm with cell debris in the epididymal lumen were observed. In one male from the 625 mg/kg/day group, minimal tubular atrophy was observed. In another male from the same group, moderate dilatation in the testes was noted. No lesions of the testes were noted in the 1250 mg/kg/day or 2500 mg/kg/day groups. Minimal inflammatory cell infiltration in the epididymides were observed across the control and three dosing groups. Qualitative assessment of spermatogenesis showed incomplete spermatogenesis in one control animal and one 625 mg/kg/day animal, consistent with tubular atrophy. In dams, minimal ovarian mineralization was observed in the vehicle control, 625 mg/kg/day, and 1250 mg/kg/day groups, but not in the 2500 mg/kg/day group. No histopathological findings were noted in ovaries of non-pregnant or non-copulated females (Supplemental Table S2). These lesions are commonly observed in laboratory rats and were detected in both GABA-treated groups and the vehicle control group and were therefore considered unrelated to the test article.

Testosterone (T), estradiol (E2), progesterone (P4), and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels remained comparable among groups. Discussion and conclusion

GABA is widely used as a functional dietary supplement due to its proposed benefits in areas such as stress modulation, sleep quality, and cognitive function. Previous toxicological evaluations have consistently demonstrated low systemic toxicity of orally administered GABA, as briefly reviewed in the introduction section. These studies collectively provide important context supporting the overall biological safety profile of GABA. Nevertheless, no study to date has systematically examined the effects of repeated oral administration of GABA on fertility, reproductive outcomes, or offspring development in a controlled toxicological framework. The present study evaluated the potential effects of repeated oral GABA administration on fertility, reproductive performance, and developmental outcomes in rats under conditions closely aligned with OECD Test Guideline 421. The study followed the OECD Test Guideline 421 with the exception of extended premating exposure in males for 88 days and in females for at least 14 days, followed by assessments of mating behavior, fertility, gestational progression, parturition, and lactation. This design enabled the evaluation of potential treatment-related effects on both parental animals and their offspring and incorporated additional hormone and male reproductive measures.

In this study, parental observations showed no evidence of systemic toxicity. Neither mortality nor treatment-related clinical signs occurred in any group, and body-weight gain remained consistent between the control and GABA-treated groups throughout the dosing period. Although food consumption was transiently reduced in males and females at 2500 mg/kg/day at varying time points, these decreases were not sustained and did not translate into reductions in body weight or clinical condition, suggesting they were of minimal biological relevance. Organ weights, including reproductive organs, were generally comparable between groups. A single reduction in the absolute weight of the levator ani–bulbocavernosus muscle in high-dose males occurred without corresponding changes in relative weight or any histopathological correlates, arguing strongly against toxicological significance. Gross necropsy findings, such as kidney depressions, ovarian or uterine cysts, stomach patches, and intestinal diverticula, appeared sporadically across both the control and GABA-treated groups and showed no dose-related pattern. Histopathological findings, including interstitial edema, vacuolation of seminiferous tubules, Leydig cell hyperplasia in the testes, and decreased sperm with cell debris in the epididymal lumen, were limited to a single male in the vehicle control group with an infertile partner. Seminiferous tubular atrophy was observed in both the control and low-dose groups, while no lesions of the testes were noted in the higher-dose groups, and minimal inflammatory cell infiltration in the epididymides was observed across the control and all dose groups. In dams, minimal ovarian mineralization was observed except in the highest-dose group. Taken together, the occurrence in concurrent controls, lack of dose- or exposure-related trends, and sporadic distribution suggest that these findings represent background or spontaneous lesions related to inherent biological variability rather than test article–related effects [60], [61], [62], [63], [66]. Overall, the parental data demonstrate that repeated oral GABA administration did not induce systemic, organ-level, or clinical toxicity.

Reproductive function was similarly unaffected by treatment. Copulation indices were 100% in all GABA-treated groups, slightly exceeding the rate observed in controls. Fertility indices showed no significant differences among groups, and infertility events appeared sporadically, with no dose-dependent trend. Estrous cyclicity was preserved, with only a small and non–dose-related reduction in mean cycle length in the mid-dose group; importantly, this variation was not accompanied by impaired mating success, conception, or gestation, suggesting that reproductive endocrine function remained intact. Key functional outcomes, including gestation index, implantation success, delivery index, and live birth index, were also unaffected by GABA exposure. No increases in pre- or perinatal loss were observed, and maternal behavior, including care of pups during lactation, was normal across all groups.

Offspring development through lactation was likewise unaltered by GABA administration. Viability indices on Days 4 and 13 of lactation did not differ between groups, and no treatment-related external abnormalities were observed. A single case of a thread-like tail in one pup from the 625 mg/kg/day group represented an isolated and spontaneous malformation without treatment relationship. Sensitive endocrine-related developmental markers, including anogenital distance and nipple/areola counts [67], [68], were unchanged across all GABA-treated groups, indicating that GABA exposure did not interfere with androgen-dependent sexual differentiation. Postnatal body weights did not differ among groups on Days 0 and 4; however, pups in the 2500 mg/kg/day group showed significantly lower body weights on Day 13. Despite this statistical difference, the values remained within the historical control range at the test facility (Day 14 data used as reference due to the absence of Day 13 historical data; Supplemental Table S4). Moreover, this change emerged only at the late lactation stage, was relatively modest, and did not coincide with alterations in survival, external morphology, maternal behavior, or endocrine profiles. In accordance with the interpretation outlined in ICH S5 (R3) [69], such isolated reductions in pup body weight, particularly when limited to a single time point, are often interpreted as non-adverse and may reflect natural variation among litters rather than developmental toxicity, especially in the absence of accompanying deficits. In addition, at gross necropsy on Day 13 of lactation, dilated renal pelvis was observed in both male and female pups. Because this finding is a spontaneous lesion with a reported background incidence of approximately 2% in SD rats [65], and the maximum incidence observed in pups from the GABA-treated groups was 1.72% (1/58), which falls within this background range, and because occurrences were detected in both the concurrent control and GABA-treated groups without a dose-related pattern, the finding was considered unrelated to GABA treatment and non-adverse.

In endocrine assessments, reproductive hormones (testosterone, estradiol, and progesterone) of the parental males remained unaltered. Thyroid hormones (T3 and T4) were elevated at the mid and high doses in parental males, yet these changes were not accompanied by alteration of TSH or by thyroid histopathological alterations. Physiologically, increases in circulating free T3 and T4 are expected to inhibit pituitary TSH release through a negative feedback mechanism [70], [71], and the relationship between thyroid hormones and TSH is log-linear rather than linear, such that even very small fluctuations in T3 or T4 can produce marked changes in TSH [72], which were not seen in the present study. Indeed, the thyroid gland possesses a substantial capacity to compensate for changes in hormonal and metabolic conditions as reflected by transient increases or decreases in activity. The high degree of inherent variability of these hormones is underscored by OECD guidelines, which allow a coefficient of variation (CV) of up to 25% for T3 and T4 and 35% for TSH, irrespective of age of the rats [73]. The increases observed in T3 and T4 in this study fall within a range where analytical and biological noise frequently obscures treatment-related signals. Therefore, collectively, the increases in T3 and T4 in the absence of TSH changes, functional impairment, or corroborating pathology are consistent with adaptive or compensatory, non-adverse responses, rather than hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid (HPT) axis disruption. Furthermore, changes in thyroid gland morphology, rather than isolated hormone fluctuations, are considered among the most reliable indicators of biologically meaningful HPT-axis disruption [73]. Taken together, the absence of TSH suppression, the lack of thyroid histopathological changes, and the known adaptive capacity of the thyroid axis support the conclusion that the observed increases in T3 and T4 represent adaptive, non-adverse physiological responses rather than toxicologically relevant disruption of the HPT axis. In parental females, circulating levels of T3, T4, TSH, estradiol, and progesterone remained comparable among groups. Hormone levels in offspring showed several isolated changes, including increased TSH in males at 625 mg/kg/day on Day 13 and increased T3 in female pups at mid and high doses on Day 4, as well as a reduction in T4 in females at 625 mg/kg/day on Day 13. However, these variations were inconsistent across dose levels, sexes, and time points, and did not correspond to a recognizable pattern of thyroid axis perturbation. Importantly, no developmental landmarks or structural endpoints suggested endocrine disruption.

Sperm quality and morphology also remained unaffected by the test article. Neither sperm counts nor sperm abnormality rates differed between groups, and no treatment-related changes were observed in the structure of testes or epididymides upon microscopic examination. Pathological findings that did occur were present in both control and all dose groups and represented naturally occurring variations rather than test article-related effects. These results provide strong evidence that spermatogenesis and male reproductive capacity were not adversely affected under repeated GABA exposure.

Analysis of tissue GABA levels provided additional mechanistic context. Plasma GABA levels increased with dose, confirming systemic absorption, while GABA levels in reproductive organs showed modest elevations in some groups. In contrast, GABA concentrations measured in the plasma of offspring on Lactation Day 13 did not show a dose-dependent increase, providing no indication of systemic exposure to the test article in the offspring. Interestingly, brain GABA levels decreased in males at the mid and high doses and in females at the lowest dose; however, these changes were not accompanied by neurological signs or histopathological findings. Mechanistically, brain GABA homeostasis is regulated primarily by local synthesis, reuptake, and transport processes, and efflux of GABA from the brain to the blood across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) has been demonstrated, whereas peripheral GABA exhibits minimal BBB penetration [74], [75], [76]. Consequently, plasma GABA levels do not necessarily reflect brain GABA status, and peripheral elevations may coexist with unchanged or variable brain GABA levels [77], [78] without implying neurotoxicity. Moreover, baseline brain GABA concentrations are known to be dynamically regulated within individuals, with modest temporal fluctuations reported under physiological conditions, as demonstrated by longitudinal neurochemical and in vivo 1H magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) studies; such variability occurs in the absence of neurological impairment and reflects normal neurochemical regulation rather than dysfunction [79]. From a safety perspective, an isolated neurochemical change in the absence of accompanying neurobehavioral findings, neuropathological correlates, or a coherent dose–response pattern, the observed decreases in brain GABA are most appropriately interpreted as non-adverse and not indicative of neurotoxicity [66].

Some recent studies have suggested that exposure to GABA may adversely affect very early pregnancy stages under certain conditions. Tian et al. reported that oral administration of a very high GABA dose (12.5 mg/g or 12.5 g/kg body weight) to mice during the preimplantation period reduced the number of implantation sites and increased the proportion of morphologically degenerated or developmentally retarded embryos; in vitro, GABA at 10–50 μg/μL inhibited preimplantation embryo development in a GABAB receptor-dependent manner [80]. In a study by Kovaříková et al., expression of multiple GABAA and GABAB receptor subunits was identified in mouse oocytes and preimplantation embryos, and exposure of cultured blastocysts to GABA at 2–10 mM or to synthetic GABA receptor agonists (10–100 μM) increased cell death [81]. These studies collectively suggest that excessive activation of maternal or embryonic GABA receptors may compromise preimplantation development and implantation under highly pharmacological exposure conditions. However, several factors limit the direct extrapolation of these findings to typical oral GABA intake in humans or to the dosing paradigm used in the present study. First, the in vivo dose used in Tian et al. (12.5 g/kg) corresponds to a human equivalent dose (HED) of approximately 1.0 g/kg, or 60 g/day for a 60-kg adult ( [82]; U.S. [83]), while an extreme supplemental intake for human is 18 g/day as reviewed by the United States Pharmacopeia (USP) [3] and the highest dose tested in the present study was 2500 mg/kg/day, which is equivalent to approximately 0.4 g/kg/day or 24 g/day for a 60-kg adult. Thus, the mouse dose at which implantation defects were observed represents an exposure that substantially exceeds both our highest NOAEL in rats and the upper range of human use. Second, in both Tian et al. and Kovaříková et al., preimplantation embryos were either directly bathed in high GABA concentrations in vitro (millimolar or tens of mg/mL) or exposed in vivo during a very narrow, highly sensitive window, thereby bypassing many of the pharmacokinetic constraints (absorption, metabolism, distribution, and placental transfer) that operate after oral intake. In fact, in the present study plasma GABA concentrations in male rats from the highest dose group averaged 370.2 ng/mL with a maximum observed value of < 600 ng/mL, physiological serum GABA concentrations in humans are typically in the submicromolar range (approximately 0.1–1.0 μM) [84], [85], and available clinical and toxicological data do not support sustained systemic GABA levels approaching the millimolar concentrations used in the embryo culture experiments [46]. In contrast to Tian et al. and Kovaříková et al., the present study directly assessed fertility, implantation, gestation, and postnatal development in a guideline-conformant reproductive toxicity model. In a whole-animal setting with intact maternal metabolism and placentation, doses up to 2500 mg/kg/day did not reproduce the implantation failures or preimplantation embryo toxicity reported under the extreme exposure conditions used by Tian et al. and Kovaříková et al.

The present study was conducted using an enhanced OECD 421–compliant design to evaluate potential effects of repeated oral GABA administration on reproductive performance and postnatal development. Certain endpoints assessed in more extensive reproductive or developmental toxicity studies were not evaluated. In particular, detailed prenatal developmental examinations, including fetal external, visceral, and skeletal evaluations as performed in OECD Test Guideline 414, as well as multigenerational reproductive outcomes and long-term reproductive function of offspring as addressed in OECD Test Guideline 416 or OECD Test Guideline 443, were not examined. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted within the scope of the endpoints evaluated in this study. Additionally, it should be noted that the test article used in this study was GABA produced through a defined microbial fermentation process. Therefore, consideration of material characteristics (e.g., production method, raw material safety, matrix profile, etc.) remains of great importance when interpreting the applicability of these results.

The present study provides the first in vivo evaluation of the reproductive and developmental safety of repeated oral GABA administration, thereby extending the existing body of evidence that substantiates the systemic safety of GABA as an orally consumed functional ingredient. Among the evaluated endpoints, although several statistically significant differences (primarily at the p ≤ 0.05 level) were observed, such as isolated hormonal changes, minor organ weight variations, or reduced pup body weight at a single time point, none exhibited a coherent dose–response relationship, were accompanied by corroborative functional or morphological alterations, or formed a biologically meaningful pattern indicative of toxicity. Consistent with the regulatory evaluation frameworks for reproductive toxicity, which emphasize integrated, weight-of-evidence interpretation and caution against over-reliance on single, isolated statistical outcomes, this study employed an OECD 421-aligned design that incorporated extended dosing and additional endpoints in males, and demonstrated that repeated oral administration of GABA produced through Lactobacillus hilgardii-mediated fermentation up to 2500 mg/kg/day (the NOAEL) in rats does not adversely affect parental health, reproductive function, pregnancy outcomes, offspring development, reproductive organ integrity, endocrine profiles, or sperm quality.
