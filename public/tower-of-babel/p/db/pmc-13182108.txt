Health-related quality of life and associated psychosocial and clinical factors among mothers whose neonates admitted to neonatal intensive care units in Amhara Regional State Referral Hospitals, Northwest Ethiopia: a multicenter study
Authors: Alemken Eyayu Abuhay, Berihun Agegn Mengistie, Fisseha Yetwale Kassie, Getnet Azanaw, Tadele Amare Zeleke, Chilot Kassa Mekonnen, Degsew Ewunetie Anteneh, Gebeyehu Nigussie Amare, Serku Abate Mihret, Alemu Kefale Berihun, Mengstu Melkamu Asaye

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMothers’ health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is frequently compromised when their neonates are admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), leading to psychological distress, sleep disturbances, and impaired maternal–infant bonding. These adverse experiences may be further intensified by neonatal clinical complications and maternal psychosocial vulnerabilities. Despite this, empirical evidence on HRQoL and its associated psychosocial and clinical determinants among Ethiopian mothers remains very limited. Therefore, this study aimed to assess mothers’ HRQoL and to identify the associated psychosocial and clinical factors among mothers of neonates admitted to NICUs in referral hospitals of the Amhara Regional State, Northwest Ethiopia.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted from December 12, 2023, to March 15, 2024 among 636 mothers selected via systematic random sampling in the NICUs of selected referral hospitals. Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) was measured using the validated WHOQOL-BREF instrument, comprising 23 items in four domains. The mean score of each domain and the total HRQoL score (range 0–92) were calculated, with higher scores indicating better quality of life. Data were collected via structured interviews and hospital chart review. Simple and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to identify psychosocial and clinical factors associated with HRQoL.ResultsA total of 651 eligible mothers were approached, of whom 636 participated, yielding a response rate of 97.7%. The overall mean (± SD) score of mothers’ health-related quality of life was 41.87 ± 3.47. The mean scores (± SD) for the physical, psychological, social, and environmental domains were 12.65 ± 2.09, 13.86 ± 1.99, 2.60 ± 1.04, and 13.72 ± 2.02, respectively. Factors significantly associated with mothers’ HRQoL included being married (β = 4.19; 95% CI: 3.02, 5.37), history of antenatal care follow-up at hospitals (β = -1.17; 95% CI: -1.99, -0.36), gestational age ≤ 34 weeks (β = -1.97; 95% CI: -3.21, -0.72), low birth weight (β = -1.15; 95% CI: -3.26, -1.06), receiving information about the neonate’s health status (β = 1.12; 95% CI: 1.03, 2.01), length of NICU stay (β = -0.24; 95% CI: -0.28, -0.04), and poor social support (β = -0.89; 95% CI: -1.40, -0.38).ConclusionsThe findings of this study demonstrate that mothers whose neonates were admitted to the NICU experience reduced health-related quality of life. Therefore, to improve maternal quality of life, a range of effective interventions is needed, including strengthening social support and providing timely information about the health status of their neonates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02534-8.Keywords: Ethiopia, Health-related quality of life, Mothers, Neonatal intensive care unit, Neonates

Introduction

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an individual’s perception of their position in life in the context of culture, value systems, goals, expectations, standards, and concerns, encompassing physical, psychological, and social domains [1]. Maternal HRQoL reflects women’s well-being during the postpartum period and can be influenced by obstetric complications (e.g., preterm labor, preeclampsia), socio-demographic characteristics (e.g., age, income, education), and clinical factors such as mode of delivery, gestational age, and neonatal birth weight [2]. There are several factors contributing to HRQoL among mothers with neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). These factors include sociodemographic characteristics, obstetric and neonatal factors, psychological well-being, social support, and healthcare-related factors such as the quality of care and counseling received [3]. Studies have shown that maternal age, education, and income level can significantly influence HRQoL [4]. Obstetric factors, including parity, mode of delivery, and complications during pregnancy, have also been linked to variations in maternal HRQoL [5]. Additionally, the mental health of mothers, particularly the presence of stress, anxiety, or depression, plays a crucial role in determining their quality of life during the neonatal period [6]. Social support from family, friends, and healthcare providers has consistently been associated with improved maternal HRQoL outcomes [7]. The admission of a newborn to a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) is a significant maternal stressor that can adversely affect HRQoL. Mothers of NICU-admitted neonates often experience emotional distress, sleep disturbances, physical strain, and impaired bonding with their infants, while neonatal factors such as diagnosis at admission, prematurity, and low birth weight further contribute to reduced maternal HRQoL [8, 9]. Globally 30 million newborns each year require quality special or intensive inpatient newborn care [10]. A recent systematic review found that anxiety is highly prevalent among parents of infants admitted to neonatal units, affecting approximately 42% within the first month postpartum and remaining elevated at around 26% between one month and one year [11]. Symptoms of post-traumatic stress were similarly common, affecting about 40% of parents during the first month, 25% between one month and one year, and roughly 27% beyond one year after birth [12].

Studies have shown that maternal HRQoL improves when healthcare providers provide timely and adequate information about the infant’s condition, emphasizing the importance of communication and support [6]. Moreover, 94% of mothers report health issues such as back pain, exhaustion, and fatigue, all associated with lower HRQoL [13]. Concerns about infant safety, uncertainty regarding growth and development, financial stress, social isolation, and the NICU hospitalization experience itself have been identified as additional determinants of maternal HRQoL [14].

The NICU environment further influences maternal well-being. Continuous noise, disturbing visual stimuli from monitors and equipment, unpleasant chemical odors, and limited communication with staff are all linked to poorer maternal HRQoL [9]. Evidence indicates that postpartum mothers’ HRQoL is substantially reduced when their neonates are admitted to NICUs for medical conditions [15]. Furthermore, diminished HRQoL also contributes to postpartum depression, which affects approximately 14%–17.8% of cases [4]. Studies in different countries have reported that 51.2% to 73.7% of postpartum mothers experience reduced HRQoL. Among mothers with preeclampsia, 26% to 52.6% reported negative impacts on mental health due to inadequate social support and limited access to healthcare services, resulting in lower HRQoL [16–18].

Globally, mothers of NICU-admitted neonates actively participate in caregiving, seeking medical attention, engaging with family support systems, and learning about their infants’ needs. They are encouraged to maintain visits, address social concerns, and contribute to the cleanliness and safety of the NICU environment [19]. Despite growing global evidence on maternal experiences in NICU settings, empirical data on NICU-specific maternal health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and its psychosocial and clinical determinants remain limited in Ethiopia and sub-Saharan Africa. Most existing studies in Ethiopia focus on general postpartum HRQoL, without isolating the unique challenges faced by mothers of NICU-admitted neonates. This highlights a critical knowledge gap, demanding context-specific investigation that considers both clinical and psychosocial dimensions. To address this gap, the study was conceptualized using the stress-coping model and social support theory, which propose that maternal stressors, such as neonatal admission to the NICU, can negatively affect health outcomes, but these effects may be alleviated by coping strategies and social support. According to this framework, maternal psychosocial factors (e.g., marital status, perceived social support) and neonatal clinical factors (e.g., gestational age, birth weight, length of NICU stay) interact to influence maternal health-related quality of life (HRQoL). This model guided the selection of study variables and informed the interpretation of how both maternal and neonatal factors contribute to HRQoL outcomes in the NICU context. Accordingly, this study aimed to assess health-related quality of life and its associated psychosocial and clinical factors among mothers whose neonates were admitted to NICUs in selected referral hospitals of the Amhara Regional State, Northwest Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study design

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among mothers with neonates admitted to selected comprehensive specialized referral hospitals in the Amhara Regional State, Northwest Ethiopia. The study employed an observational design to assess maternal experiences and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) at a single point in time. Because the study was observational and cross-sectional in nature, clinical trial registration was not required. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Review Committee prior to the commencement of data collection. Written informed consent was secured from all study participants before enrollment to ensure voluntary participation and confidentiality.

Study period

The study was conducted over a period of thirteen (13) weeks, from December 12, 2023, to March 15, 2024.

Study setting

The study was conducted in selected comprehensive specialized referral hospitals of the Amhara Regional State, Northwest Ethiopia. The region has eight tertiary-level public hospitals that provide comprehensive inpatient, outpatient, emergency, delivery, and neonatal intensive care services.

Each hospital serves both its designated catchment population and referred cases from surrounding districts and zones, with catchment populations ranging from approximately two to five million people. NICU infrastructure and service capacity vary across hospitals. Smaller facilities, such as Debre Tabor and Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals, have approximately 15–20 NICU beds, whereas larger institutions, such as the University of Gondar and Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals, have more than 40 NICU beds. These variations are also reflected in differences in equipment availability, staffing levels, and patient load, which may influence maternal experience and HRQoL. To ensure homogeneity in patient care systems and referral patterns, only public hospitals were included in the study, while private hospitals were excluded. Four hospitals were selected using simple random sampling from the eight eligible tertiary hospitals to ensure representativeness and logistical feasibility for data collection. Source and study population

The source population consisted of all mothers whose neonates were admitted to NICUs in Amhara Regional State Referral Hospitals. The study population included all mothers discharged during the study period from the randomly selected hospitals who met the inclusion criteria.

Eligibility criteria

Mothers whose neonates had been admitted to the NICU for at least two weeks were included. This threshold was chosen because the WHOQOL-BREF instrument requires a minimum two-week exposure to a stressor to capture a meaningful and stable measure of psychological and physical impact on maternal HRQoL [6, 13, 19–24]. Mothers were interviewed immediately before discharge to ensure consistent recall. Mothers who were critically ill (e.g., those with postpartum eclampsia) or who had acute or life-threatening medical conditions that prevented effective communication at the time of interview were excluded from the study. Mothers of twins or multiple births were included.

Sample size determination and sampling procedures

The sample size for this study was determined using a single population mean formula, assuming a 95% confidence level, a standard deviation (SD) of 6.2 obtained from the pilot study (as no previous study was found in a similar population), and a 0.5 margin of error [25].

Where n = required total sample size, α = level of significance, z = standard normal distribution curve value for 95% confidence level = 1.96, and SD = standard deviation. Finally, by considering a 10% contingency for non-response, the final sample size became 591 + 59.1 = 651. This sample size of 651 provides 80% statistical power at a 95% confidence level to detect a minimum effect size (odds ratio) of 1.5 for factors associated with maternal health-related quality of life, ensuring sufficient power to examine the primary psychosocial and clinical determinants (Fig. 2).

Flow of participant inclusion and exclusion (N = 636)

Participants were selected independently within each hospital using systematic random sampling. Proportional allocation based on NICU admission volume was applied to ensure representativeness across hospitals. Since the study did not use cluster or multistage sampling at the participant level, a design effect was not incorporated into the sample size calculation. Four hospitals were selected from the eight comprehensive specialized hospitals in the Amhara Regional State using simple random sampling (Fig. 1). This ensured representativeness across different geographic zones and referral systems. The selection of four hospitals was justified by logistical feasibility and sufficient representation of regional service diversity.

Within each selected hospital, systematic random sampling was employed to identify study participants. Systematic random sampling was preferred over consecutive sampling to minimize potential selection bias arising from daily fluctuations in NICU admissions. The number of mothers whose neonates were admitted to the NICU during the previous three months was approximately 500 at the University of Gondar, 350 at Debre Markos, 450 at Debre Tabor, and 400 at Tibebe Ghion, giving a total of 1,700 mothers. A proportional allocation method was applied to ensure that each hospital contributed to the total sample size according to its patient load. The sampling interval (k) was calculated as (k = N/n = 1700/651 = 2.6 ~ 3).

The first participant at each hospital was selected by lottery, and every third eligible mother was subsequently recruited until the required sample was reached. The proportionally allocated sample sizes were University of Gondar (191), Debre Markos (134), Debre Tabor (172), and Tibebe Ghion (154). Mothers who declined participation were not replaced but were recorded as non-responders, and sampling continued with the next eligible participant according to the k-interval sequence. Study variables

The Dependent (outcome) variable for this study was maternal health-related quality of life (HRQoL). The independent variables comprised socio-demographic factors (age, residence, mother’s and partner’s educational level, employment status, religion, and marital status), obstetric factors (number of pregnancies, number of previous births, mode of delivery, family size, desirability of the pregnancy, bad obstetric history, previous low birth weight or preterm birth, previous neonatal death, and antenatal care), NICU-related factors (maternal stress and level of health facility satisfaction), and newborn-related factors (gestational age at birth, sex of the neonate, birth weight, admission diagnosis, and length of neonatal stay). The conceptual framework of this study was informed by the multidimensional definition of health-related quality of life proposed by the World Health Organization, which conceptualizes quality of life as encompassing physical, psychological, social, and environmental domains [26]. Guided by this framework and existing empirical evidence, maternal health-related quality of life was hypothesized to be influenced by sociodemographic characteristics, obstetric history, NICU-related factors (such as maternal stress and satisfaction with care), and newborn-related clinical characteristics. These factors may independently or jointly affect maternal physical, psychological, environmental, and social well-being [27].

Operational definitions

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) Maternal HRQoL was measured using the WHOQOL-BREF instrument, which contains 23 items across four domains: physical (7 items), psychological (6 items), social relationships (2 items), and environmental (8 items). The raw scores of the items were summed to produce domain-specific and total HRQoL scores, with a total possible score range of 0–92, where higher scores indicate better quality of life. The total HRQoL score was treated as a continuous outcome variable in the analysis. For descriptive purposes, HRQoL scores were categorized as low (< mean − 1 SD), moderate (mean ± 1 SD), and high (> mean + 1 SD) [28].

Preterm birth

A baby is considered preterm if it’s born before 37 weeks of pregnancy; in this study, preterm birth is determined by gestational length and measured from the first day of the LNMP and early ultrasound findings [29].

Term birth

A full-term pregnancy is defined as a pregnancy that lasts between 37 weeks and 41 weeks, measured by the duration of gestation from the first day of LNMP [30].

Maternal stress at NICU Maternal stress in the NICU was assessed using the 34-item Parental Stressor Scale: Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (PSS-NICU). The scale comprises three subscales: sights and sounds, infant appearance and behavior, and parental role alteration. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all stressful) to 5 (extremely stressful). Subscale scores were calculated by summing the items within each subscale, and the total score was calculated by summing all 34 items, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress [31]. The instrument was translated into Amharic using a forward–backward translation procedure to ensure language uniformity. The internal consistency of the scale in the current study was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient.

Level of health facility satisfaction was assessed using a structured 15-item questionnaire developed in a previous study [32]. The items addressed three domains: quality of healthcare providers’ communication and care, physical environment of the facility, and availability of necessary services. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied), with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction. Mothers were classified as satisfied if their total score was greater than or equal to the mean score and not satisfied if their score was below the mean. The instrument was pretested prior to data collection, and its internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha.

Mothers’ perceived social support

Maternal social support was measured using two questions on perceived support from family, friends, and community. Scores below the mean were classified as poor social support, and scores ≥ the mean as good social support [33].

Social support

Social support was assessed using the three-item Oslo Social Support Scale (OSSS-3) a validated instrument [34]. This evaluates the number of close supporting individuals, the degree of concern and interest shown by others, and the perceived availability of practical assistance. The scale was translated into Amharic using a forward–backward translation procedure to ensure linguistic equivalence and pretested prior to data collection. The total score ranges from 3 to 14, with higher scores reflecting stronger maternal social support during the NICU stay. The internal consistency of the scale in the current study was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha.

Low birth weight (LBW)

defined as a newborn whose weight at birth is below 2,500 g, determined within the first hour after delivery using a properly standardized infant scale, irrespective of gestational age [35].

Data collection method and procedure

Data were collected using a structured and pretested questionnaire administered through face-to-face interviews, and additional clinical information was obtained from medical chart reviews. The WHOQOL-BREF was culturally adapted and translated into Amharic, with back-translation into English to ensure linguistic consistency. Four midwives with a Bachelor of Science degree served as data collectors, and an additional four midwives with the same qualification served as supervisors. All data collectors and supervisors received two days of training on the study objectives, ethical considerations, interviewing techniques, accurate documentation, and overall data collection procedures prior to fieldwork. Each interview lasted approximately 30–40 min, and supervisors monitored data collection daily to ensure completeness and consistency. The tool comprised socio-demographic characteristics and present and past obstetric history. Three items were negatively worded and reverse-coded during analysis. One item was culturally and religiously sensitive among postpartum mothers. During the pilot study, participants indicated that sexual activity is culturally and religiously prohibited in the early postpartum period. Therefore, the item “How satisfied are you with your sex life?” was removed from the questionnaire after the pilot study and consultation with senior researchers [36].

A validated version of the HRQoL tool for use in Ethiopia was employed. In 2022, a team of experts, including gynecologists, epidemiologists, and midwife specialists, evaluated the tool’s face and content validity among postpartum mothers with preeclampsia in the Sidama region, assessing readability, clarity, intelligibility, and applicability [37]. WHOQOL-BREF was selected over SF-36 for its shorter length, cultural adaptability, and multidimensional coverage of its domains.

Data quality controls

Two days of training was provided for data collectors and supervisors about the purpose of the study, the content of the questionnaires, and all the study protocols to be followed throughout the data collection process. The questionnaire was piloted on 15% (58) of subjects at Felgehiwot Referral Hospital NICU ward to determine the standard deviation, language clarity, and consistency of the questionnaire. The internal consistency of the instrument was assessed by calculating Cronbach’s alpha (overall HRQoL domain α = 0.92, physical domain α = 0.72, mental domain α = 0.82, social domain α = 0.82, and environmental domain α = 0.85). During data collection, the questionnaire was checked for completeness on a daily basis by the supervisor and principal investigator.

Data processing and analysis

The data were checked for completeness and cleanliness, then coded and entered into a computer using EpiData version 4.6 software and exported to SPSS version 20 for analysis. Both descriptive and analytical statistical procedures were performed. Descriptive statistics such as means, medians, frequencies, proportions, tables, and figures were used to describe the characteristics of the study participants and present the study findings. For analytical statistics, the total HRQoL score for each study participant was calculated from the five-point Likert scale items. Mean scores were computed using the study sample data, and the sample mean was used as the reference for analysis. Population-based reference datasets were not applied because validated reference values for postpartum mothers with neonates admitted to the NICU are not available in Ethiopia or comparable low-income settings. This limitation highlights the need for future research to develop population-based HRQoL reference values for this population. Categorical variables were coded into dummy variables for regression analysis. Like with marital status, which had four categories (Single, Married, Divorced, Widowed), was coded by selecting single as the reference category (0). The other categories were coded as 1 if the participant belonged to that category and 0 otherwise: Married = 1 if married, 0 otherwise; Divorced = 1 if divorced, 0 otherwise; Widowed = 1 if widowed, 0 otherwise.

The presence of a statistical association between the dependent and independent variables was initially screened using simple linear regression analysis. Candidate variables with a p‑value of less than 0.25 were retained for inclusion in the multiple linear regression models to avoid excluding potentially important predictors, as recommended in regression modeling literature [38]. The model assumptions of linear regression (normality tests, linearity, equality of variance (homoscedasticity), multicollinearity, and outliers) were checked and fulfilled. Hence, histograms, skewness, and kurtosis were examined to determine the shape of the distribution of the data and residuals. For that, they indicated that the data and residuals were approximately normally distributed. Statistically, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (p-value = 0.12) was non-significant, indicating that standardized residuals were normally distributed. Linearity was checked by generating separate scatter plots with the dependent variable on the y-axis and the independent variables on the x-axis. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and all values were < 10, indicating the absence of multicollinearity among the independent variables. Outliers were examined using boxplots and Cook’s distance. The goodness-of-fit of the regression model was evaluated using the adjusted coefficient of determination (R²) and the F-test. In the multiple linear regressions, a p-value of 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval was used to determine the significance of the association [39]. In addition to reporting statistical significance, effect size measures were computed to quantify the magnitude of associations in the multiple linear regression models. Cohen’s f² values were calculated using the formula f² = (R² included − R² excluded) / (1 − R² included). The effect size was interpreted as small (f² = 0.02), medium (f² = 0.15), or large (f² = 0.35), based on Cohen’s guidelines [40]. Minimal missing data (< 5%) were handled using listwise deletion because the missing values were assumed to be missing completely at random (MCAR).

Finally, multiple linear regression analysis was fitted to identify independent variables that were significantly associated with the overall HRQoL of mothers whose neonates were admitted to the NICU using the “enter method” [41] Independent variables that have a significant association were expressed using an adjusted unstandardized β coefficient at a 95% confidence interval, and factors with a p-value < 0.05 at a 95% confidence interval were considered statistically significant. Data from all referral hospitals were pooled for analysis to estimate the overall level of women’s satisfaction with health-facility-related factors.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of mothers

A total of 651 mothers whose neonates were admitted to NICUs in Amhara Regional State Referral Hospitals were approached, of whom 636 provided complete responses, yielding a response rate of 97.69% (95% CI: 96.3–99.1%). Fifteen mothers were excluded due to incomplete responses or withdrawal from participation. The primary reasons for withdrawal included sudden neonatal transfer to another facility, maternal emotional distress during data collection, and early self-discharge before the interview was completed. Most respondents were housewives, (398; 63.5%), and married, (604; 95%). Approximately 41.3% had primary education, and (479; 76.4%) were Orthodox Christians. More than half (51.4%) resided in rural areas. The median age of mothers was 26 years (IQR ± 3.6), and the mean age of mothers was 26.4 ± 3.6 years (range 18–42) (Table 1).

Women’s satisfaction with health-facility-related factors

Regarding health-facility-related factors, (362; 56.9%) of the respondents reported dissatisfaction with the availability of chairs. A total of (299; 47.0%) mothers reported dissatisfaction with the cleanliness and services of the NICU toilets and showers, while (291; 45.8%) mothers reported dissatisfaction with the overall cleanliness and safety of the NICU environment. The latter variable was assessed using a single Likert-scale item measuring mothers’ perception of the general cleanliness and safety conditions of the NICU. A high proportion of participants reported dissatisfaction with the availability of rooms for expressing breast milk (443; 69.7%) and resting or sleeping rooms for parents (415; 65.3%). Additionally, (278; 43.7%) of the mothers were dissatisfied with the information provided regarding NICU rules and regulations. Overall, more than half of the participants reported poor satisfaction with health-facility-related factors (Table 4).

Regarding health care provider–related factors, many mothers experienced stress related to communication and interaction with staff. Specifically, (244; 38.4%) of the participants reported that staff spoke or explained information too quickly, while (251; 39.5%) indicated that medical terms used by staff were difficult to understand. Additionally, (233; 36.6%) of mothers reported receiving contradictory information about their neonate’s condition, and (201; 31.6%) felt that they were not given enough information about tests and treatments. Difficulty in obtaining information or help during visits or phone calls was reported by (299; 47.0%) of respondents, and (273; 42.9%) did not feel confident about changes in their baby’s condition. Regarding staff behavior and interaction, (352; 55.3%) of mothers observed that staff appeared worried about their baby, while (279; 43.9%) perceived that staff acted as if they did not want parents around. Moreover, (264; 41.5%) of respondents reported that staff did not understand their baby’s behavior or special needs, and (253; 39.8%) indicated that they were not provided with enough information overall. In terms of the overall relationship with health care providers, (344; 54.1%) of mothers reported experiencing stress during interactions with staff (Table 5).

Health-related quality of life of mothers

The overall mean (± SD) HRQoL score among mothers was 41.87 ± 3.47. Among the four domains, the lowest mean score relative to its maximum possible score was observed in the social relationships domain (2.60 ± 1.04 out of 8; 32.5%; 95% CI: 2.52–2.68), whereas the highest relative scores were recorded in the psychological domain (13.86 ± 1.99 out of 24; 57.8%; 95% CI: 13.69–14.00) and environmental domain (13.72 ± 2.02 out of 32; 42.9%; 95% CI: 13.56–13.87). When interpreted based on the study’s predefined criteria, 46.4% of mothers were classified as having low HRQoL (Fig. 4). Presenting the domain scores as a percentage of the maximum possible score allows for a more accurate comparison of maternal HRQoL across domains with different score ranges.

Factors associated with mothers’ health-related quality of life (HRQoL)

Multiple linear regression analysis showed that the overall model was statistically significant (F = 7.99, p ≤ 0.0001) and explained 58.4% of the variance in mothers’ HRQoL (Adjusted R² = 0.584). Variables entered into the regression model included maternal age, marital status, pregnancy status, ANC follow-up, place of ANC follow-up, mode of delivery, gestational age at birth, birth weight, receiving information about the baby’s health status, length of NICU admission, perceived social support, and level of maternal stress were included in simple linear regression analysis.

In the multivariable linear regression, factors associated with maternal HRQoL included being married, having ANC follow-up at hospitals, gestational age ≤ 34 weeks, low birth weight, receiving information about the health status of the baby, length of NICU stay, and poor perceived social support (Table 6; Fig. 5). These factors collectively explained 58.4% of the variance in maternal HRQoL. Being married was positively associated with maternal HRQoL; on average, married mothers had a 4.19-point higher HRQoL score compared to unmarried mothers (β = 4.19; 95% CI: 3.02, 5.37; f² = 0.19), holding other variables constant. Conversely, mothers who received ANC follow-up at hospitals had a 1.17-point lower HRQoL score than those who attended private clinics or hospitals (β = − 1.17; 95% CI: − 1.99, − 0.36). Mothers who delivered before 34 weeks had a 1.97-point reduction in HRQoL compared to those who delivered after 37 weeks (β = − 1.97; 95% CI: − 3.21, − 0.72).

In addition, mothers of low-birth-weight neonates had a 1.15-point lower HRQoL score than mothers whose infants had normal birth weight (β = − 1.15; 95% CI: − 3.26, − 1.06). However, receiving information about the health status of the baby increases the mothers’ HRQoL by 1.12 (β = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.03, 2.01, f2 = 0.11) as compared to their counterparts. We also explored correlations with household income and use of healthcare services. While income showed a positive but non-significant association with HRQoL, use of healthcare services including ANC follow-up and place of care demonstrated significant associations, highlighting the role of access to quality healthcare in maternal well-being. Each additional day of NICU stay was associated with a 0.24-point decrease in maternal HRQoL (β = -0.24, 95% CI: -0.28, -0.04, f2 = 0.16). Finally, lower perceived social support was significantly associated with reduced HRQoL among mothers (β = -0.89, 95% CI: -1.40 to -0.38, f² = 0.23), indicating that, on average, mothers with lower social support had HRQoL scores 0.89 points lower than those with higher social support.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and associated psychosocial and clinical factors among mothers with neonates admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) in referral hospitals of the Amhara Regional State. The findings indicated that a substantial proportion of mothers experienced poor HRQoL. Among the HRQoL domains, social relationships were the most affected, whereas psychological and environmental domains showed comparatively higher scores. Furthermore, the overall maternal HRQoL observed in this study was lower than that reported in studies conducted in other regions of Ethiopia and in some low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The overall mean (± SD) score of HRQoL was found to be 41.87 ± 3.47. Besides, the mean score (± SD) of physical, mental, social, and environmental sub-domains was determined to be 12.65 ± 2.09, 13.86 ± 1.99, 2.60 ± 1.04, and 13.72 ± 2.02, respectively.

The mean HRQoL score observed in this study was lower than that reported in other Ethiopian studies conducted in Oromia [17], Bale Zone [42], Arba Minch [16], Jimma [43], and Sidama region [37] and median (IQR) score of 52 (65 − 42), as well as in Pakistan [44], and India [45]. However, direct comparison of absolute mean scores should be interpreted cautiously because different studies used different measurement instruments and scoring systems. For example, the present study employed the WHOQOL-BREF tool, whereas several of the cited studies used the SF-36 questionnaire [16, 18, 46, 47], which differs in structure, domain composition, and scoring range. Therefore, differences in numerical mean scores may reflect methodological variation rather than true differences in HRQoL levels.

Among studies that used similar instruments, variations in HRQoL may be explained by differences in study populations and clinical contexts. Unlike many of the comparison studies that assessed HRQoL among general postpartum mothers in community or facility-based settings, the present study focused specifically on mothers whose neonates were admitted to the NICU. The stress, uncertainty, and emotional burden associated with neonatal hospitalization may substantially reduce maternal HRQoL. Differences observed even within Ethiopia may be attributable to variations in socioeconomic status, access to healthcare services, availability of psychosocial support, neonatal clinical conditions, and healthcare facility environments. These contextual factors may influence maternal well-being and contribute to the lower HRQoL observed in the current study.

This study also demonstrated that variables such as being married, ANC follow-up at hospitals, gestational age ≤ 34 weeks, low birth weight (< 2500 g), receiving information about the health status of the baby, length of NICU stay, and poor perceived social support were significantly associated with maternal HRQoL.

Marital status was significantly associated with maternal HRQoL. Married mothers had a higher mean HRQoL score compared with unmarried mothers, consistent with findings from a study conducted in Malawi [48]. This association may be explained by the greater availability of emotional, social, and financial support from partners, which can alleviate stress and enhance coping capacity during the neonatal hospitalization period.

Furthermore, on average, mothers who received antenatal care (ANC) at public hospitals reported lower HRQoL than those attending private clinics, consistent with other studies in Ethiopia [37]. This may be related to higher patient volume and longer waiting times in hospital settings, which can increase fatigue, stress, and dissatisfaction with care, thereby negatively affecting maternal well-being.

Mothers of very preterm neonates (GA ≤ 34 weeks) had significantly lower HRQoL scores during the NICU stay compared to mothers of term neonates, a finding that is consistent with studies conducted in the United States [20], Malaysia [2] and Australia [6], which also reported poorer maternal quality of life among mothers of very preterm infants. This could be due to the fact that mothers of very preterm neonates often face higher levels of stress and anxiety due to the fragility and potential health complications of their babies, and they are worried about being unable to care for and protect their newborn [49].

Additionally, mothers are faced with weeks or months of lengthy hospital stays for their newborns in the NICU, prolonged separation from their newborns, an overcrowded NICU environment, and changes in their regular lives (job, family life, and financial status). Therefore, addressing the unique needs and challenges faced by mothers of very preterm neonates during the NICU stay is crucial to providing adequate support and promoting maternal well-being through compassionate, respectful, and caring peer support groups, informing them about newborn status, and assisting them with coping strategies.

Receiving information about the health status of the baby was associated with an increase of 1.12 units in mothers’ HRQoL scores compared to those who did not receive such information. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in France [50], Australia [51] and Brazil [52]. This can be explained as being informed about the health status of the baby can alleviate stress, empower mothers, strengthen the mother-child bond, and provide access to support, all of which can contribute to a higher improvement in HRQoL compared to mothers who do not receive such information.

The length of NICU stay was the other factor that was statistically associated with the mothers’ HRQoL. The mothers’ HRQoL dropped by 0.24 for each additional day spent in the hospital. This study finding was in agreement with studies done in the United States [53], Malaysia [2] and Portugal [54]. An extended hospital stay in the NICU environment may increase maternal stress and anxiety, limit opportunities for social support, and contribute to physical and emotional exhaustion. In addition, prolonged hospitalization may impose financial strain on families.

These factors collectively may negatively affect mothers’ health-related quality of life. It is essential for healthcare providers to recognize the impact of prolonged NICU hospitalization on mothers and to implement structured support strategies, including psychological counseling, clear communication regarding the infant’s condition, facilitation of social support, and appropriate discharge planning to address mothers’ physical, emotional, and social needs during this critical period.

This study also reported that low birth weight (< 2500 g) was a significant negative predictor of maternal health-related quality of life as compared to normal-weight neonates. This could be due to the fact that mothers of LBW neonates are under constant worry, anxiety, and fear of potential complications, which can take a toll on the mother’s mental well-being and HRQoL. Additionally, mothers of LBW neonates in the NICU may need prolonged hospitalization and increased caregiving demands, including feeding schedules, medication administration, and coordination of care with healthcare providers, which leads to physical and emotional drain, affecting the mother’s HRQoL. This finding was consistent with studies conducted in the United States of America and Portugal [53, 54]. In sum, providing comprehensive support services, counseling, and resources to mothers of low-birth-weight neonates in the NICU can help address their unique needs, enhance coping mechanisms, and improve their overall well-being and HRQoL during this challenging period. This finding was supported by studies done on postpartum mothers in Ethiopia [37].

Finally, having poor social support, from either parents or friends, significantly decreased the average HRQoL as compared to their counterparts. This finding was supported by studies done in Ethiopia [17, 55]. Insufficient social support is associated with lower health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among mothers whose neonates are admitted to the NICU. During their infant’s hospitalization, mothers often have limited social interaction and support.

Cultural factors in Ethiopia may further influence maternal HRQoL, as mothers might underreport available support or overemphasize the burden of caregiving due to societal expectations and traditional postpartum practices. Additionally, maternal health literacy plays a critical role in shaping HRQoL by influencing the understanding of neonatal conditions, adherence to care recommendations, and effective communication with healthcare providers. Notably, the social domain recorded the lowest mean HRQoL score.

To enhance interpretability, domain scores were also examined using established categorization criteria (e.g., low, moderate, and high based on mean ± 1 SD), which indicated that the social relationships domain had comparatively lower scores than the other domains. This suggests that social support and interpersonal functioning may be particularly affected during NICU hospitalization. However, this domain includes fewer items and has a lower maximum possible score than the other domains; therefore, the lower mean score should be interpreted with attention, as it may reflect the scale structure rather than a true difference in HRQoL. This finding highlights the importance of interventions that strengthen maternal social support, such as peer support, structured group-based programs, and family involvement, which have been shown to improve social support and maternal psychosocial outcomes in the postpartum period [56].

Overall, maternal HRQoL in this Ethiopian NICU setting is influenced by marital status, ANC follow-up location, neonatal prematurity, low birth weight, length of NICU stay, social support, and access to information. These findings underscore the need for culturally sensitive and context-specific interventions that address both the psychosocial and practical challenges mothers face during neonatal hospitalization. Improving hospital care processes, enhancing NICU staff communication skills through targeted health communication training, and providing structured psychosocial support could meaningfully improve maternal HRQoL in NICU environments.

Moreover, the regression analysis revealed medium to large effect sizes for key predictors, particularly marital status, social support, and duration of NICU stay, indicating substantial practical significance beyond statistical findings. These results reinforce the importance of interventions aimed at strengthening social support networks, facilitating clear and empathetic communication, and minimizing prolonged NICU stays to promote maternal well-being during the critical period of neonatal hospitalization [57].

The regression model explained 58.4% of the variance in maternal HRQoL, indicating a substantial combined effect of the predictors. Psychosocial factors such as marital status, social support, and NICU stay length showed moderate to large contributions to HRQoL, whereas clinical factors, including birth weight and ANC follow-up, demonstrated smaller but still meaningful effects. These findings highlight the importance of both maternal psychosocial conditions and neonatal clinical characteristics in shaping mothers’ quality of life during NICU hospitalization.

Strength and limitation of the study

This study has several strengths. First, it was a multicenter, institution-based study with a high response rate (97.69%) and an adequate sample size, which enhances the representativeness and generalizability of the findings to mothers whose neonates are hospitalized in NICUs at referral hospitals in the Amhara Regional State. Additionally, the study provides valuable quantitative information on maternal health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in the Ethiopian context, where information on this topic is limited.

However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. The cross-sectional design of the study prevents causal inferences between predictors and maternal HRQoL. Recall bias may have affected responses regarding obstetric history, and social desirability bias could have influenced mothers’ reporting during face-to-face interviews. The study did not include fathers or other caregivers, which limits the understanding of family-level influences on maternal HRQoL. Furthermore, qualitative perspectives were not captured, which could have provided richer insights into mothers’ experiences in the NICU. Cultural adaptation, such as the removal of the sex-life item from the WHOQOL-BREF tool, may reduce comparability with international studies. Residual confounding by unmeasured variables may exist, which could lead to overestimation or underestimation of the associations between predictors and maternal HRQoL.

Another limitation is the absence of population-based reference datasets for health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among mothers with neonates admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) in Ethiopia or other similar low-income settings in Africa. Due to this limitation, the study relied on the sample mean as the reference for analysis, which may limit the ability to compare the findings with standardized population norms. In addition, the single-country setting limits the generalizability of the findings to other regions or healthcare contexts.

Future studies using longitudinal designs, mixed-methods approaches, and the inclusion of paternal perspectives are recommended to address these gaps.

Conclusions and recommendations

This multicenter study is the first in the Amhara region to assess maternal health-related quality of life during neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) hospitalization. The findings indicate that mothers’ overall HRQoL during NICU stay was reduced, with significant associations observed with marital status, receiving antenatal care (ANC) at public hospitals, gestational age ≤ 34 weeks, low birth weight, receiving information about the neonate’s health, length of NICU stay, and perceived social support.

Practical implications include integrating routine maternal HRQoL screening in NICUs, enhancing psychosocial support, and improving hospital infrastructure, such as resting rooms and dedicated parental rest areas. Based on the study findings, targeted health communication training for NICU staff and strengthened antenatal programs to prevent preterm birth and low birth weight are recommended. These interventions may help mitigate maternal stress, improve bonding with the newborn, and enhance overall HRQoL.

Additionally, future mixed-methods research in Ethiopia is needed to explore contextual and cultural factors affecting maternal well-being. Future studies should also consider developing population-based HRQoL reference values for mothers with neonates admitted to NICUs in low-income settings. Establishing such normative data would facilitate more meaningful comparisons across studies and improve the interpretation of HRQoL outcomes in this population.

Furthermore, maternal HRQoL assessments may benefit from using complementary or condition-specific HRQoL instruments alongside the WHOQOL-BREF. This approach could provide a more detailed and context-specific assessment of maternal well-being, particularly when evaluating interventions or exploring specific domains of health. The findings may inform policies and interventions aimed at improving maternal health and contribute to achieving Sustainable Development Goals related to maternal well-being.
