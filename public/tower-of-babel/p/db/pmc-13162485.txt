Background

Preeclampsia (PE) is a multisystem and hypertensive disorder that disproportionately affects pregnant women after 20 weeks of gestation [1]. PE is defined as a systolic blood pressure of at least 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure of at least 90 mmHg, associated with proteinuria, maternal organ dysfunction, and uteroplacental dysfunction [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), PE is the third leading cause of maternal mortality, accounting for 16% of global maternal deaths, and approximately 5 to 8% of pregnant women developed PE in 2023 [2]. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is among the WHO regions that have high proportions of women with PE, ranging from 1.8% to 16.7% [3]. In the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), the prevalence of PE depends on the season and varies across cities: 15% in Kinshasa and 4% in Lubumbashi [4].

Severe PE is a life-threatening form of PE associated with maternal and fetal complications. Maternal complications include eclampsia, stroke, cerebral edema, visual disturbances, thrombopenia, acute kidney injury, and pulmonary edema. Fetal complications of pregnancy include placental abruption, postpartum hemorrhage, prematurity, stillbirth, and neonatal death [5]. These complications negatively impact the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goal 3, targets 3.1 and 3.2 in SSA, where maternal mortality ratio is 442 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births instead of the required 70 deaths or less per 100,000 by 2030 live births, and the neonatal mortality rate is 32 per 1,000 live births instead of the required 12 or less per 1,000 live births by 2030 [6].

In recent years, the discovery of neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) has been a milestone for the diagnosis of PE. NGAL is a glycoprotein whose level increases within two hours when kidney damage occurs. Compared with conventional tests such as blood pressure measurement and proteinuria, NGAL is highly sensitive in detecting kidney injury in pregnant women with PE [7]. Much evidence from Europe has revealed a significant association between NGAL levels and PE in pregnant women [8–10]. This evidence demonstrates the importance of NGAL in predicting severe PE and complications associated with PE.

Emerging evidence suggests that urinary NGAL thresholds derived from Western populations, which may not be directly generalizable to African populations due to important biological, environmental, and epidemiological differences [8–10]. Contextual and genetic factors may influence the expression and circulating levels of NGAL [8]. In addition, prevailing environmental factors in many African countries may alter baseline inflammatory responses and renal biomarker profiles. These factors include dietary patterns, higher exposure to infectious diseases such as malaria, typhoid fever, and parasitic diseases . Also, these factors could potentially modify NGAL production or clearance, leading to different baseline concentrations and diagnostic thresholds compared with populations in which most biomarker studies have been conducted. Consequently, generating context-specific evidence in African populations is essential to determine whether NGAL retains similar diagnostic performance and to establish appropriate thresholds for distinguishing moderate from severe PE [8, 10].

Kinshasa bears the highest proportion of pregnant women with PE [4]. However, there is a dearth of literature on urinary NGAL levels among pregnant women, which has been documented to be highly sensitive in distinguishing pregnant women with moderate PE from those with severe PE [7, 9]. This study aimed to examine the association between urinary NGAL and severe PE, as well as the discriminatory ability of urinary NGAL between moderate and severe PE among pregnant women in Kinshasa.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a multicenter hospital-based cross-sectional survey, carried out from January to July 2024.

Study area

The study was undertaken in four health facilities in Kinshasa in the DRC, including the General Hospital of Makala, the University Hospital of Kinshasa, the Ngaliema Clinic and Saint Joseph Hospital. These health facilities were chosen because of their high patient attendance and their placement within the Ministry of Health in Kinshasa.

Study population

Pregnant women living in Kinshasa for at least six months whose diagnosis of PE was confirmed. Women with chronic kidney disease and those with twin pregnancies were excluded from this study.

Sampling

A convenience sampling approach was used to recruit pregnant women who visited the four healthcare facilities during the study period. A minimum sample size of 214 pregnant women with PE was estimated via the population proportion formula. To estimate the sample size, we used a 15% estimated prevalence of PE among pregnant women in Kinshasa. This prevalence was derived from a study conducted by Elongi et al. [9]. Margin of error of 5% and critical value at 95% confidence interval (Z1-/2 = 1.96). A nonresponse rate of 10% was assumed.

Data collection

Four data collectors, all of whom were medical doctors, were trained for three days to ensure adherence to the standard operating procedures, informed consent, handling data collection tools, quality assurance, and strict confidentiality. The questionnaire was designed via the Open Data Kit (ODK) and then transferred to Kobo Collect to streamline the data collection and reduce incomplete responses. The questionnaire was accurately translated into Lingala, the most spoken local language in Kinshasa, by specialists in linguistics from the faculty of Letter at the University of Kinshasa using a back-translation approach and was pretested before the data collection started. To ensure data integrity, all the responses were reviewed daily to verify completeness and correctness. Any unclear responses were promptly clarified with data collectors on the same day, allowing issues to be resolved before the next day’s data collection.

Data were recorded electronically on Android tablets and saved on the Kobo Collect server. Sociodemographic and clinical data included age, profession, parity, gravidity, history of abortion, antenatal care visit, lower limb edema, dipstick urinalysis, history of PE, and history of diabetes mellitus, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure. Biological data included urinary NGAL.

Urine samples for the measurement of NGAL were collected in the birthing room, waiting room during prenatal care visits, or during hospital stays from participants at the time of clinical evaluation for hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Sampling was performed at a gestational age of ≥ 20 weeks of gestation, consistent with the period during which PE typically manifests. In participants diagnosed with PE, urine collection was conducted after the clinical diagnosis was established but prior to the initiation of major therapeutic interventions, in order to assess urinary NGAL levels in relation to PE severity. Skilled birth attendants, or any other healthcare provider in charge of obstetric care, and pregnant women were provided with clear instructions to avoid errors during sample collection. Fresh urine samples were stored at the University Hospital of Kinshasa laboratory in refrigerators with frozen packs. The preserved urine samples were then properly homogenized for a 10-second period. The samples were then examined within six hours for NGAL measurement. Data related to maternal and fetal complications included eclampsia, placental abruption, acute pulmonary edema, ascites, intrauterine fetal death, intrauterine growth restriction, preterm birth, and acute fetal distress.

Operational definitions

PE was moderate when SBP was < 160 mmHg and/or DBP was < 110 mmHg and high when SBP was ≥ 160 mmHg and/or DBP was ≥ 110 mmHg.

Urinary NGAL was normal when its value was less than 131.7 ng/ml and high when its value was 131.7 ng/ml or greater.

Data management and analysis

Data cleaning, coding, and analysis were conducted via STATA version 18. Because a 10% nonresponse or incomplete data rate was anticipated during sample size estimation, procedures were established to manage missing data during the analysis phase. Data completeness was assessed for all variables prior to analysis. Variables with missing values were examined to determine the pattern and proportion of missingness. A complete-case analysis approach was applied, since the proportion of missing data was minimal (< 5%), all variables were categorized and summarized as percentages. Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test, when expected cell counts were less than 5, were used to compare proportions between groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of urinary NGAL to discriminate between moderate and severe PE. The diagnostic performance of urinary NGAL was quantified using the area under the ROC curve (AUC) with corresponding 95% confidence interval. The optimal cut-off value for NGAL was determined using the Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1) [11], which identifies the threshold that maximizes the combined sensitivity and specificity for detecting severe PE. Modified poisson regression with robust standard errors was applied, due to the high proportion of women with severe PE, to examine the association between urinary NGAL and severe PE. All the underlying regression assumptions were verified. A Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) was developed, based on the literature review and clinical knowledge, using the DAGitty software to identify the minimally sufficient set of confounders (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study assessed the association between urinary NGAL and severe PE, as well as the ability of urinary NGAL to discriminate moderate and severe PE among women in Kinshasa. This study provides context-specific evidence from a resource-limited setting on the relevance of urinary NGAL in PE. More than half of the participating women were aged 30–39 years. Diabetes mellitus was significantly associated with urinary NGAL. However, history of PE was not significantly associated with urinary NGAL. Eclampsia was the most observed maternal complication. Urinary NGAL was not significantly associated with severe PE among women in Kinshasa. Also, there was no significant difference in urinary NGAL levels between women with moderate PE and those with severe PE (p = 0.432).

Middle-aged women are the most affected by PE

In this study, most women with PE were aged 30–39 years. In SSA, advancing maternal age is associated with a greater body mass index in women. Women aged 30–39 years may experience metabolic syndrome, including hypertension. A similar pattern has been documented in other settings. For example, a 2025 cross-sectional survey conducted by Boadu et al. in Ghana [12] revealed that women aged 30–39 years had greater odds of having PE than adolescent women did. In 2020, Benjelloun et al. in Morocco [9] and Kapisyzi et al. in Mali [12] reported a relatively high frequency of PE among women aged 30–39 years in their respective countries. The similarity between our finding and findings from these studies could be attributed to the fact that the women included in these studies may share the same physiological and environmental commonalities, since they all live in SSA. Several studies, however, have reported conflicting findings [12, 13]. In 2015, Essome et al. in Cameroon reported more cases of PE among women aged 29 years or younger [13]. Early menarche has been associated with early onset of PE at a relatively young age. Nevertheless, this study’s finding highlights the need to screen for PE and promote counseling for PE in every middle-aged woman. Additionally, policy-makers should incorporate screening for metabolic syndrome in routine healthcare services prior to PE among middle-aged women.

History of diabetes mellitus was associated with PE

History of diabetes mellitus was the only variable significantly associated with urinary NGAL levels (p = 0.044). This finding suggests that underlying metabolic disorders may contribute to renal tubular stress or injury, which is reflected by increased NGAL concentrations. Diabetes mellitus is known to induce subclinical kidney damage and endothelial dysfunction, even in early stages, which could amplify the renal impairment observed in PE. Therefore, the higher proportion of diabetes mellitus among women with high urinary NGAL may indicate a shared pathophysiological pathway involving renal and vascular dysfunction, reinforcing the role of NGAL as a marker of kidney injury in this population.

In this study, history of PE showed borderline significance, probably due to the small sample size of this study. Although not statistically significant at the conventional 5% threshold, this trend is clinically meaningful and suggests a possible link. Several studies have documented positive association between history of PE and new episodes of PE [12, 13]. The persistence of endothelial dysfunction resulting from previous PE increases the risk of developing recurrent PE episodes [13]. Similar pathogenic pathways result in a vicious cycle in women with PE. In LMICs, the recurrence of PE could be due to a short inter-pregnancy interval and an unchanged lifestyle. Practically, healthcare professionals should prioritize the prevention and screening of PE in women with a history of PE.

Eclampsia is the most frequent maternal complication of PE

Eclampsia was the top reported maternal complication among the participating women. This finding indicates weaknesses in emergency obstetric care and prenatal surveillance, as well as a lack of screening for eclampsia among women with PE. Similar studies have documented the same pattern [14, 15]. In contrast, a few studies have reported other PE-related complications [16–18]. In 2011, Gokhan et al. in Turkey reported that HELLP syndrome was the most common maternal complication among women with PE [19]. These study’s findings are different from ours in that HELLP syndrome is underdiagnosed in many countries in SSA. On the other hand, in settings with enough resources or referrals, better diagnostics and early seizure prevention increase the likelihood of HELLP syndrome being a severe consequence while decreasing the incidence of eclampsia [20]. This finding has several implications for the increased frequency of eclampsia among women with PE. At the clinical level, this study emphasizes how critical it is to improve monitoring of pregnant women with hypertension diseases, guarantee regular availability and prompt delivery of magnesium sulfate, and increase the early diagnosis of PE during antenatal care. Maternal health services should build referral mechanisms, increase the surveillance of hypertension problems, increase training for frontline health workers in emergency obstetric care, and incorporate standardized screening procedures for PE. Initiatives for community-based education are also crucial for encouraging early care-seeking and halting the development of serious illness.

Surprisingly, no association was found between urinary NGAL and severe PE. Variations in genetic background, including polymorphisms affecting inflammatory and renal injury pathways, may influence the expression urinary NGAL among pregnant women in Kinshasa. Given the borderline significance of p-value (p = 0.056) and lack of power to detect a significant association, the study’s finding should be interpreted with caution. This finding is further supported by a poor discriminatory performance of urinary NGAL between moderate and severe PE among participating women, contradicting a body of evidence in the literature [21–23]. Furthermore, low sensitivity and specificity of urinary NGAL observed in this study may reflect the fact that NGAL is a marker of renal injury and systemic inflammation rather than a biomarker specific to PE. Elevated NGAL levels may therefore occur in other conditions associated with inflammation or renal stress, which may reduce its specificity [24]. In addition, the diagnostic performance of urinary NGAL may be influenced by the timing of measurement, population characteristics, and the selected threshold used to define elevated levels. Nevertheless, these findings contribute to this evolving body of literature, suggesting that urinary NGAL should not be used alone, since it has limited capacity to discriminate moderate and severe PE. A combination of urinary NGAL with clinical manifestations and other biological markers could be useful for the diagnosis of severe PE. These include blood pressure, proteinuria, creatinine levels, and symptoms. More powered and longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the ideal urinary NGAL cutoff values, evaluate its additional predictive value over traditional clinical markers, and analyze its temporal variations during pregnancy.

Strengths and limitations

An important contribution of this study is the generation of data from an African population, where biomarker evidence remains limited. Most previously proposed urinary NGAL thresholds have been derived from cohorts in Western and Asian countries. This study therefore contributes to filling this evidence gap and underscore the need for larger multicenter studies in African populations to establish context-specific reference values and diagnostic cutoffs. Also, this is the first study to explore both severe PE and urinary NGAL in the DRC. The data were gathered from four large healthcare facilities. The use of biological samples via standardized handling enhances the reliability of urinary NGAL measurement. The use of modified Poisson regression, because of the high proportion of women with severe PE, was relevant for interpreting the findings. Limitations were encountered in this study. Owing to the study design, the temporal sequence in the association between urinary NGAL and severe PE could not have been assessed. The convenience sampling approach was used due to the hospital-based nature of this study. This sampling approach and the non-response rate of 10% have introduced selection bias, which limits the generalizability of the study’s findings. Even if several confounders were controlled for, the findings were not free from residual confounders. The urinary NGAL cutoff stems from other populations, which is not applicable for women living in Kinshasa.

Conclusion

This study revealed that urinary NGAL is not associated with severe PE among pregnant women in Kinshasa. The ability of urinary NGAL to discriminate between moderate and severe PE was poor, suggesting that urinary NGAL alone might not accurately represent the clinical severity of PE. These results reveal potential population-specific pathophysiological variations and the need for locally validated biomarkers in SSA, in contrast to evidence from high-income nations. Powered cross-sectional studies using probabilistic sampling approaches and longitudinal cohort studies are needed to assess the discriminatory ability and predictive value for discriminating moderate and severe PE and predicting progression to severe PE and maternal–fetal complications, as well as establishing appropriate NGAL cutoff values for African populations.
