ACHO virtual assistant and digital care delivery for treatment adherence: a qualitative study of care practices and representations among nursing professionals
Authors: Jerónimo Luengo Polo, David Conde Caballero, Borja Rivero Jiménez, Lorenzo Mariano Juárez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPopulation ageing, together with the rise of chronic conditions, comorbidities, and polypharmacy, poses major challenges for ensuring therapeutic adherence among older adults. In this context, digital technologies emerge as potential support tools, although their integration into nursing practice warrants further investigation.MethodsThis qualitative study explored the experiences of primary care nurses in Extremadura (Spain) using a virtual voice assistant (Assistant on Care and Health Offline, ACHO) to support therapeutic adherence among older adults. In-depth interviews were conducted to examine nurses’ views on ACHO’s usability and the incorporation of digital technologies into daily care delivery.ResultsParticipants found ACHO intuitive and valued its offline functionality and custom features. Some saw opportunities for promoting independent living while improving medical data collection and follow-up care. However, concerns emerged regarding the potential weakening of fundamental professional values, such as empathy and face-to-face interaction, as well as risks to data confidentiality and demands for additional training and workload.ConclusionsDespite positive feedback on usability, nursing staff’s deep-rooted professional values and ideological reservations remain significant barriers to the broader adoption of digital technologies in primary care.

Introduction. The Assistant on Care and Health Offline (ACHO) in the context of digital care delivery

Population ageing is a demographic phenomenon with far-reaching implications that pose complex challenges [1, 2]. In Europe, the population aged 65 and over is projected to grow from 90.5 million in 2019 to 129.8 million by 2050. Within the European Union, the proportion of this age group is expected to rise from 20% of the total population in 2019 to 30% by 2069 [3]. Although individual health trajectories are shaped by a variety of factors [4], the impact of demographic ageing on overall population health – and the resulting strain on healthcare systems – are both significant and unavoidable [5]), primarily due to the rising prevalence of chronic conditions and comorbidities [6–8]. By 2035, it is estimated that 9% of the population in this age group will be living with long-term health conditions, while 35% will experience some degree of social isolation [9].

Older adults present a high prevalence of chronic conditions, particularly cardiovascular, metabolic, musculoskeletal, neurodegenerative, and mental health disorders. While primary care prioritises non-pharmacological interventions – such as a balanced diet, physical exercise, and cognitive stimulation – these measures are sometimes insufficient. Consequently, the frequent need for pharmacological treatment, often involving multiple drugs, contributes to the widespread phenomenon of polypharmacy – commonly defined as the concurrent use of five or more medications by a single patient on a daily basis [10, 11]. Currently, the prevalence of polypharmacy exceeds 23% among individuals aged 65 and older [12], and these figures may be higher among older adults in rural contexts [13, 14]. Evidence suggests that older adults exhibit lower rates of medication adherence compared to younger populations [15]. The presence of polypharmacy further exacerbates this issue [16]. According to estimates from the World Health Organization (WHO), between 30% and 50% of older adults do not adhere to their prescribed treatments [17].

The magnitude of this problem has prompted various strategies to improve therapeutic adherence, particularly among older adults. Emphasis has been placed on the role of social and family support networks [18], the implementation of psychosocial interventions [19], and the development of personalised patient education programs [20] In recent years, there has also been a rise in the implementation of technology-based interventions [21, 22]. With the growing prevalence of smartphones [23] and the expanding potential of Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies [24, 25], the use of virtual assistants to support tasks such as medication reminders and medical appointment monitoring has become increasingly common [26]. Some studies have suggested that the use of these devices could improve therapeutic adherence [27] and have identified the most effective user interfaces [28, 29]. Existing challenges include enabling these assistants to overcome identified barriers while also functioning as reliable sources of information for healthcare providers.

While considerable attention has been given to the usability of technology by older adults and the challenges they may face in engaging with it, recent findings suggest that healthcare professionals’ assessments of technology use in their clinical practice may also play a critical role in the effectiveness of these interventions. In the case of nursing professionals, several studies have identified positive models of technology acceptability while also highlighting challenges such as the need for additional training, increased costs, fatigue resulting from acquiring new skills [30, 31], and concerns regarding patient data confidentiality [32]. Indeed, caregivers could be instrumental in ensuring the success of digital care delivery models [33], identifying strengths and weaknesses that extend beyond technological issues. The emergence of AI represents a transformative revolution, and understanding the role of healthcare professionals as potential facilitators of its adoption is a key consideration.

This study explores ideological and social representations among a group of primary care nurses in the region of Extremadura (Spain), focusing on the use of the Assistant on Care and Health Offline (ACHO)1 [34]. The main feature of this open-source voice assistant is that it does not require an internet connection, since all the information is hosted on the device. Consequently, it can be used irrespective of connectivity quality, which benefits adults with limited computer literacy or those residing in rural and remote areas with poor network coverage.

ACHO is designed to integrate its information management functionalities within the digital care delivered by nursing professionals, who are responsible for inputting data via an application – or instructing patients and their family members on how to do so. After a home visit or an on-demand consultation, information such as medication schedules or upcoming appointments can be recorded in the assistant. The process is straightforward for nursing staff, allowing them to use a digital platform that enhances treatment adherence and provides access to records that supplement the patient’s medical history. The system’s workflow consists of three phases: (1) The healthcare professional or designated caregiver inputs the data; (2) the data is transmitted from the application to the voice assistant via Bluetooth; and (3) the assistant delivers reminders for events such as medication administration or medical appointments Following the reminder, ACHO prompts the user to confirm medication intake. If the assistant does not receive a positive response, it will save this information for later review by a family member or caregiver. The assistant’s design can be culturally adapted: versions currently under development allow reminders to be recorded by influential figures – such as doctors, nurses, or family members – and medications to be customised with the users’ “emic” labels – e.g., “the one in the red box” or “the one for cholesterol” [34, 35].

Methods

This qualitative study analysed the experiences of primary care nurses when using the virtual assistant, with two primary objectives: (1) To evaluate the device’s usability and identify areas for improvement, and (2) to explore their views on the routine integration of technology into their care delivery practices. The study examined the practical barriers, challenges, and opportunities associated with the implementation of this technology to improve therapeutic adherence and medical appointment management; it also explored the perceptions and ideologies surrounding the integration of this kind of technology within nursing care delivery. The research was guided by the following questions: What are the perceptions and representations of primary care nurses regarding the use of a voice assistant to support therapeutic adherence? What professional and ethical factors shape their evaluation of its usability? The analysis of these perceptions, barriers, and potential opportunities was conducted using an interpretative approach informed by phenomenological, hermeneutic, and constructivist perspectives. This framework guided the analysis of discourses, attending both to their textual features and the contexts in which they were produced.

Participants and settings

Fieldwork was conducted across six rural communities and two urban centres in the Spanish region of Extremadura, an area characterised by a significant percentage of ageing population. The ageing index in this region rose from 39.47 at the beginning of the 1980s to 125.49 in 2020, with an annual increase of more than two points in recent years. This significant increase, combined with other sociodemographic characteristics – such as dispersed municipalities, emigration, and low population density – places older adult care at the forefront of regional concerns.

Participant selection was purposively organised to obtain a non-representative but pluralistic sample, incorporating diversity across multiple categories – including rural versus urban settings, gender, years of professional experience, and age (Table 1). The sample comprised a total of 14 primary care nurses, who represent the first point of access to Spain’s healthcare system and provide comprehensive, ongoing, and community-based care. We used a mixed sampling approach, integrating random selection with the snowball sampling technique.

Initial participants were identified through nursing professional networks and contacted via email and phone. They were provided with detailed information about the study objectives, as well as aspects such as voluntary participation, confidentiality, and data management. These issues were further detailed in writing through the informed consent form. Interested nurses provided written informed consent before participating in the study. Initial participants were then asked to refer colleagues who might also be eligible, in accordance with the snowball sampling technique. This approach allowed us to reach participants from different locations and professional backgrounds, while ensuring that all potential participants received adequate information to make an informed decision.

Socio-demographic profile of the participants

The inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old and possess a nursing degree with experience in primary care. A total of 34 h of interview recordings were collected, along with numerous informal conversations with nursing staff in different settings. The majority of interviewees were women (68.7%), reflecting the gender distribution typical within the nursing profession. The sample consisted of an equal number of professionals working in both rural and urban settings. The sole exclusion criterion was not having used the virtual assistant for the time period specified in the study protocol.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted between March 2024 and January 2025. Each participant received a virtual assistant device and a mobile phone preloaded with the application for a minimum of 48 h to evaluate its usability and potential. Subsequently, a semi-structured interview was conducted [36, 37] – with an interview guide (Table 2). The design of this interview guide was based on the existing academic literature on the topic. Consistent with the study’s inductive approach, the researchers remained open to incorporating into the interviews emerging topics not initially anticipated [38].

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This research project was approved by the Bioethics and Biosafety Committee of the University of Extremadura (143/2020). The study was conducted in compliance with the ethical guidelines established by the Declaration of Helsinki and the Belmont Report.

All participants provided written informed consent after being fully apprised of the study’s objectives, the terms of their participation, and their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. The confidentiality of participants’ personal data was ensured throughout the entire research process. The first author anonymised the participants’ data, while the rest of the research team worked exclusively with the coded dataset.

Data analysis

The interviews were audio-recorded and fully transcribed, with participants’ notes incorporated into the transcripts. These were analysed with the qualitative data analysis software ATLAS.ti (version 25.0.1 for Windows). The systematisation and interpretation process followed the guidelines of thematic analysis [39] informed by grounded theory [40]. Following the initial reading of the transcripts, preliminary analytical categories were identified and coded. These were then compared and grouped into concepts and final categories, based on insights from existing literature. The relationships between categories and subcategories were analysed in a process of selective coding [41] to define the themes presented in the results. To ensure data quality, validity, and accuracy, the analysis process was triangulated: two researchers independently conducted analyses using a combined deductive-inductive approach. The deductive categories were based on the thematic blocks of the interview guide, which in turn were informed by a review of relevant literature on digital health, nursing care delivery, and technology acceptance. After coding, the researchers compared results and resolved any discrepancies through discussion, consulting a third team member when necessary. The inductive process allowed for the identification of emerging themes not captured by the initial categories. Results were then summarised by thematic block, incorporating both deductive and inductive findings to ensure rigour and consistency in the analysis. Various theoretical perspectives were also integrated into the inductive process.

Results

The study included 16 primary care nurses, with a mean age of 52.6 years and an average of 28.5 years of professional experience. A total of 16 interviews were conducted, each lasting approximately 47 min. On average, patients reported using the ACHO virtual assistant five times, with a mean of three patients per nurse, reflecting varying levels of engagement with the tool.

Usability of the ACHO system and future developments

The analysis of nursing staff’s user experiences with ACHO provided valuable insights into the assistant’s usability, helped identify previously overlooked design issues, and informed future development. The interviews revealed minor software reliability issues, such as data synchronisation problems and failure to recognise user responses. This feedback was incorporated into the testing processes. Overall, interviewees found the assistant simple and intuitive to use, emphasising its portability and offline functionality as particularly valuable features: “It’s very interesting for our villages, as many [patients] have a mobile phone at home now, but only that, no [internet] connection.”It’s easy, it’s very intuitive and works well, like, there is no problem there, there is no problem there, no, nothing, nothing. Also, it is very intuitive, like, it is easy, [if] you can manage Jara [a computer management system], we can manage this, what I also see is that the device is easy to handle, you can take it anywhere you want. If you go, if the patient goes somewhere else, they can just take it with them and carry on. (Interview 3, lines 45–50)

If you go, if the patient goes somewhere else, they can just take it with them and carry on. (Interview 3, lines 45–50)

Suggestions for improvement included issues like the voice, which “should be clearer and speak slower, as sometimes it can be difficult to understand.” Future developments aim to address this by incorporating recordings of more “familiar” voices or professionally produced audio. The application will also offer adjustable font sizes and will be more inclusive. Among other suggestions, interviewees recommended that medical appointment reminders should account for the time needed to travel to the healthcare facility.Imagine a woman who lives an hour away – she should receive the reminder an hour beforehand so she knows when she needs to leave. Ideologies on the routine incorporation of technology into nursing care delivery practices

The interviews revealed diverse attitudes towards technology in general and its use in professional settings. All interviewed nurses reported using smartphones for messaging, social media, and email applications – both personal and work-related – although they acknowledged using email less frequently: “I basically only use the computer for emails and online training courses.” There is broad agreement that age is a limiting factor – usage frequency tended to decrease with increasing user age – although some individual exceptions were noted. Their accounts reflected past experiences of rejecting technological innovations, such as digital medical records and the local health service’s electronic system.It has always been this way. I remember colleagues who refused to use Jara. They would say, ‘What’s this for?’ And they refused to use it. I remember that, when I first arrived at the health centre, some communications were still being sent by fax because some colleagues refused to open their email. (Interview 14, lines 31–34)

It has always been this way. I remember colleagues who refused to use Jara. They would say, ‘What’s this for?’ And they refused to use it. I remember that, when I first arrived at the health centre, some communications were still being sent by fax because some colleagues refused to open their email. (Interview 14, lines 31–34)

The reluctance towards technology among the interviewed nursing staff cannot be attributed solely to age. While the use of digital technologies in their daily work has increased since the COVID-19 pandemic, some hesitation remains – although attitudes are changing over time. To facilitate acceptance, a key aspect is that the learning process remains straightforward and that appropriate training is provided.What I mean is that we are going to have to change our mindset when it comes to work – as I said before, I am a nurse who carries a bag and takes blood pressure, but the buzz of digital technology is increasing… and we have to adapt. Having said that, it has to be simple, easy, and there has to be training on how to use it. (Interview 6, lines 56–59)

Technology: distance or closeness

A third theme encompasses nursing staff perspectives on the integration of technology and the potential loss of empathy and personal connection with patients. Technology is often portrayed through metaphors highlighting its “coldness” or the erosion of the caring identity associated with face-to-face interactions.I think these practices detract from what is important in the profession, which is delivering care in person, touching people. It would be more impersonal, just another number – we would be like someone who phones to identify them, to say, ‘Hi, look, it’s so-and-so calling…’ Because they would not even know who I am, because they would actually not know the nurse. (Interview 10, lines 92–96)

I think these practices detract from what is important in the profession, which is delivering care in person, touching people. It would be more impersonal, just another number – we would be like someone who phones to identify them, to say, ‘Hi, look, it’s so-and-so calling…’ Because they would not even know who I am, because they would actually not know the nurse. (Interview 10, lines 92–96)

Most interviewees viewed this as a potential risk, although one that could be mitigated by recognising the technology as a complementary tool – rather than a replacement for traditional home visits or close personal contact.In normal circumstances, it would be a complementary help […] Because you have to visit the patient anyway – to monitor their blood pressure and glucose levels, you just check the device too while you’re there, and that’s it, you know? It would be an ideal complement because when I used to visit them at home, at the end I’d have to check their medication [I’d say] ‘Why did you have to get the medicines out? You don’t need to do that.’ Now they go to the pharmacy and start taking medicines out randomly. I don’t think this will detract from a close personal relationship, it will only make other things easier. (Interview 1, lines 49–55)

Technology can enhance monitoring and therapeutic adherence; however, for some, the foundation of nursing care remains rooted in direct, face-to-face interaction. Indeed, it is recognised that individuals often seek medical attention not just for health issues but as a way to cope with issues such as loneliness.… you know, here, even more so in villages, what really matters is the relationship, let’s say, between nurses and patients, and for some [patients] even if they say, ‘Phew, I have to go there to have my blood pressure taken,’ they come because, whether you like it or not, they interact […] If they were at home, they go for a walk, walk around a bit… Out there, they meet people, they interact, they talk a little because sometimes what there is – it’s loneliness, it’s loneliness and […] you don’t need so many… so many technological devices to keep them from coming. On the contrary, perhaps they prefer to come, hang there for a bit if they have to wait or… or not… Or they meet someone on the way there and cheer up. (Interview 13, lines 103–111)

Most interviewees expressed concern that incorporating digital technologies into their care delivery practices could undermine a fundamental aspect of the nursing profession – personal interactions and connectedness. However, as mentioned above, this fear could be easily addressed. A more significant issue that emerged from the analysis involved the broader challenges and difficulties related to implementing both the ACHO assistant and digital technologies more generally.

Barriers and difficulties

A key theme in the participants’ accounts is the emphasis on the challenges posed by integrating technology into their care delivery routines. Although they maintained a generally open attitude towards technology – at least in abstract or theoretical terms – throughout the interviews, the analysis of their discourses revealed a strong focus on perceived problems, which could hinder the adoption of technological innovations. Their accounts stressed the importance of technologies being “straightforward” and “not making us learn too many things.” For the technological shift to be accepted, a key concern is that it remains user-friendly and intuitive, and provides tangible benefits for nursing staff – at least equal to those for patients. In this context, the advanced mean age of primary care staff is often cited, along with other factors that aim to justify their lack of interest in adopting new technologies.This requires an effort on the part of nurses, who have to undergo training and learn to use things they don’t always understand – at a time when staff are extremely burnt out, especially since the pandemic, because they are burnt out physically, but also psychologically. (Interview 16, lines 61–64)

As the discussion shifts from a theoretical framework to the practical implementation of ACHO within care delivery practices, additional questions emerge. The interviewees stressed the importance of receiving adequate training, which should be either offered by the public health services or financially supported by the technology’s developers. They also emphasised the challenges related to the need for additional training and its associated costs – e.g., who will provide the training and whether it will occur during or outside of working hours – placing a strong focus on potential obstacles. Discussion

This study found that, although nurses generally perceived the ACHO application as easy and intuitive to use, they expressed hesitation about fully integrating it into their routine practice. At the same time, participants expressed concerns that reliance on digital tools could compromise the care relationship and reduce empathy in nurse–patient interactions. These results highlight the tension between technological support and the relational aspects central to nursing, underscoring the need to balance efficiency with the preservation of personalised care.

The scientific literature highlights usability as a critical factor in the success of technologies such as voice assistants, particularly among populations with low digital literacy [42]. For the ACHO assistant, identified usability challenges are related to software reliability and limitations in voice recognition, particularly in cases of changes in prescribed medication routines. This emphasises the need for tailored adjustments to improve performance. Similar voice recognition issues have been noted in previous studies [43]. pointed out that both users and support staff experienced frustration while using a similar virtual assistant, despite the device’s overall ease of use, primarily due to voice recognition issues. This finding is supported by a study involving older adults that observed that such errors posed a significant barrier to use, elevating user stress and substantially reducing the device’s effectiveness [44].

Future improvements proposed for the ACHO assistant – such as custom settings to adjust the tone of voice or the font size – are also supported by recent literature A study [44] emphasised the importance of tailoring assistants to the user’s individual context, noting that personalised voice recordings enhanced acceptance and sustained use – particularly among older adults. Importantly, given that their mode of communication is similar to that of humans, it is necessary to consider how the assistant’s perceived personality affects user experiences [45]. Another work [46] suggested that patient-centred designs were perceived as more useful, particularly when the assistant aligned with its users’ cultural and linguistic contexts, allowing for a more seamless interaction.

Attending to the perspectives of nursing staff is a key issue, particularly as the rise of AI is poised to reshape healthcare significantly. The analysis of the participants’ accounts revealed cautiously conservative attitudes towards technology, consistent across both urban and rural settings. This may pose a significant barrier to the adoption and implementation of innovations in the healthcare sector. This finding is reinforced by recent studies underscoring the direct influence of nursing professionals’ ideological orientations on the adoption and integration of new technologies. For instance, research pointed out that resistance to change was a key factor contributing to nursing staff’s reluctance to adopt electronic healthcare systems, even when their potential benefits were acknowledged [47]. Similarly, other work highlighted concerns over the potential loss of clinical skills with the introduction of new technologies, suggesting a defensive attitude towards the automatisation of tasks previously conducted by humans [48].

Although some studies report a positive attitude among nursing staff towards technological change [31, 49], the prevailing tendency generally edges towards caution – or even rejection – when such changes challenge established practices or core professional values, such as personal interaction and caregiving. This tension between acknowledging the functional value of technology and expressing ideological resistance is also reflected in our study’s findings, which reveal a gap between the perceived usefulness of technological tools and the willingness to incorporate them into routine practice. A clear example of this tension is the limited integration of technology use in degree-level nursing education in countries such as Spain. Although some universities have incorporated relevant subjects into their academic programmes, this integration remains inconsistent. In many cases, these technologies are addressed in a cross-cutting manner or embedded within other subjects, without dedicated focus on the use of digital tools in care delivery. A recent report [50] indicates that only 30% of Spanish universities have integrated advanced technologies into their health sciences curricula. This lack of integration underscores the tension between acknowledging the utility of emerging technologies and resisting their implementation – particularly when they are perceived as threatening core professional values related to care delivery. This challenge, central to the future of healthcare, has been highlighted in numerous studies – including some that address the implications of AI [51, 52].

Another significant barrier is the requirement for technologies to be intuitive and user-friendly, minimising the time healthcare professionals must spend learning new digital skills. Several authors note that while professionals acknowledge the potential of voice assistants to enhance patient care delivery, the lack of adequate training remains a significant barrier, as it may lead to increased workload and extended working hours for healthcare staff [53]. In a study [31] nearly three-quarters of participants identified inadequate training and the time required to develop additional skills as major barriers to technology adoption. In our study, nearly half of the nurses interviewed reported limited technological proficiency and stressed the time required to improve these skills – which reflects the broader lack of training in digital technologies in their professional education.

Another key concern identified in our study is the risk of compromising patient data confidentiality – a concern also highlighted in previous research. Some works identify confidentiality concerns as a key barrier to the adoption of digital technologies in healthcare settings [32]. This concern is particularly heightened with voice assistants, given their interactive nature and the perception that they might be “listening” constantly. A research study noted that while nearly all participants acknowledged the transformative potential of voice assistants in care delivery, concerns about data confidentiality remained a significant issue [54]. Indeed, 85% of participants believed that these concerns were unlikely to subside in the near future, particularly if commercial development of such devices continued without strict regulatory standards. This represents a significant barrier that must be addressed carefully in future devices, particularly in contexts where patient trust and regulatory compliance are crucial for delivering high-quality patient care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. Firstly, the sample is small and non-representative, limiting the extent to which the results might be generalised. We acknowledge that the snowball sampling technique may introduce selection bias. Nonetheless, this approach allowed us to reach participants from diverse locations and professional backgrounds, providing valuable insights into nursing staff experiences with the ACHO system. The mean age of the participants (52.6 years) may also introduce a potential bias, as older age could be linked to lower familiarity with digital technologies and possible gaps in related training processes. Moreover, the sample size does not support the examination of potential differences based on gender or geographic context (rural vs. urban), thus limiting the analysis of variables that could influence the outcomes. While the study design does not allow for an assessment of the intervention’s effectiveness, it does suggest that nursing staff attitudes may present a potential barrier. Given the qualitative design of the study, the results may not be applicable to the broader population of nursing professionals. Nevertheless, the findings may be transferable to similar contexts, particularly in rural primary care settings or in environments with limited technological resources. The detailed description of the context and participant characteristics may enable readers to evaluate the applicability of these results to other comparable settings. Consequently, more comprehensive research is needed to assess the practical impact and efficiency of this virtual assistant further.

Conclusions

Qualitative analysis of user experiences with the virtual assistant helped identify usability improvements and inform future development, using inductive models that remained open to previously unanticipated possibilities. ACHO was perceived as an interesting, comprehensive, and user-friendly tool for primary care nursing practice. Although new features were suggested, ACHO was considered a viable option in rural areas with no internet access.

In theory, the participating nurses were open to adopting new technologies; however, when confronted with concrete examples from the living lab, they focused primarily on the challenges they posed. Their willingness to embrace new technologies depended on them being easy to use and requiring minimal training.

This study identified two key concerns: (1) A weakening of the nurse–patient relationship, including the loss of empathy and personal connection, and (2) potential risks to patient data and confidential information. Nursing professionals appear to exhibit a conservative habitus, favouring traditional methods and showing resistance to the incorporation of new practices. Some interviewees associate care delivery with face-to-face interaction and relational values such as empathy and closeness, which they view as incompatible with technology – perceived as cold and distant. This may act as a barrier to the broader adoption of tools like the ACHO assistant or other emerging AI-based technologies.
