Usefulness of intervention using interlesion pressure ratio in renal artery stenosis due to multifocal fibromuscular dysplasia: a case report
Authors: Yosuke Matsumoto, Shoji Kawakami, Jun-ichiro Nishi, Costantino Mancusi, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRenal artery stenosis (RAS) due to fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) is a known cause of secondary hypertension. Percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty (PTRA) is an established treatment. However optimal procedural endpoints remain controversial. The Pd/Pa ratio—the mean distal renal artery pressure (Pd) to the mean aortic pressure (Pa)—has been proposed to guide interventions, its role as an endpoint remains to be validated. This report describes the clinical utility of the Pd/Pa ratio in our patient.Case summaryA 36-year-old Asian woman presented with incidentally diagnosed hypertension (185/115 mmHg). Introduction

Renal artery stenosis (RAS) is a known cause of secondary hypertension and is frequently associated with fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD), a non-atherosclerotic, non-inflammatory vascular disorder.1,2 A prompt diagnosis and management of FMD-induced hypertension are crucial for preventing poor long-term outcomes.3 Percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty (PTRA) can improve renal perfusion, lower blood pressure (BP), and preserve renal function in patients with RAS-induced drug-resistant hypertension.4 Although procedural success is usually determined based on angiographic and haemodynamic improvements, the ideal endpoint for FMD-related RAS remains undefined. The Pd/Pa ratio, defined as the mean distal renal artery pressure (Pd) to mean aortic pressure (Pa), is considered useful for assessing the physiological impact of the RAS.5 However, its use as a procedural endpoint remains insufficiently supported. Herein, we describe a case of secondary hypertension due to multifocal (beaded) FMD in which Pd/Pa measurements before and after PTRA contributed to guide successful intervention.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old Asian woman (body mass index, 20.9 kg/m²) was diagnosed with hypertension during a gynaecological visit for dysmenorrhoea. She reported no symptoms such as snoring or daytime somnolence. Family history of hypertension or cardiovascular risk factors was unremarkable. The only previous medical concern was dysmenorrhoea. Further, she had no history of chronic medication use, including contraceptives or analgesics. Previous health check-ups did not reveal any abnormalities. Her BP at the gynaecology visit was 185/115 mmHg. She was started on azilsartan 20 mg and amlodipine (5 mg), but the calcium channel blocker was discontinued because due to palpitations and dizziness. Despite treatment, home BP remained at approximately 170/100 mmHg.

Echocardiography showed left ventricle (LV) septal and posterior wall thickness of 10.1 mm and 10.7 mm, respectively, with a preserved ejection fraction (60%). However, diastolic dysfunction was suggested by E/A 1.11, E/e′ 11.6, tricuspid regurgitant pressure gradient 23 mmHg, and left atrial volume index 42 mL/m2 (Figure 1A–C). Renal ultrasonography revealed a slightly enlarged left kidney (12.0 cm) and an elevated peak systolic velocity (PSV) of 300.5 cm/s in the right renal artery, raising the suspicion of RAS (Figure 1D–F). Laboratory tests performed 3 weeks after discontinuing ARB therapy showed normal renal function but elevated plasma renin activity and aldosterone levels. 3D-CT angiography demonstrated early bifurcation of the right renal artery into the upper and lower branches with suspected stenosis in the lower pole branch (Figure 2). Calcification was absent. The left renal artery was normal, and no other vascular abnormalities were observed.

Evaluation by three-dimensional computed tomographic angiography. Suspicion of stenosis was noted in part of the right renal artery with no indication of adrenal arteries.

Percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty was indicated because of persistent drug-resistant hypertension caused by FMD. Using a 6 Fr sheath in the right femoral artery, selective renal angiography confirmed early bifurcation and high-grade stenosis in the lower pole branch (Figure 3A; Supplementary material online, Video S1). The lesion showed a multifocal pattern typical of FMD (Figure 3B). Intravascular ultrasound (AnteOwl WR™, Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) using a 0.014-inch guidewire (Jupiter™, Boston Scientific Corporation, Marlborough, MA, USA) revealed preserved intima and fibrous medial thickening (Figure 3C). The diameters of the reference vessels were 2.75 mm. Pressure guidewire (COMET™ II, Boston Scientific Corporation, Marlborough, MA, USA) showed a Pd/Pa ratio of 0.44 (Figure 3D and E).

(A) The first renal branch arises at 5.0 mm from the renal artery ostium. (B) Multifocal (beaded) stenosis is visible distal to the lower pole branch. (C) The stenosis reveals fibrous narrowing without calcification. (D and E) Interlesion pressure ratio measurement indicated a Pd/Pa ratio of 0.44. Pd/Pa ratio, ratio of the mean distal pressure to the lesion and mean proximal pressure.

To relieve flow limitation, angioplasty was performed with a 2.5 × 13 mm balloon (Aperta™, NIPRO Corporation, Osaka, Japan) inflated to 24 atm, followed by a 3.0 × 20 mm balloon (Coyote™, Boston Scientific Corporation, Marlborough, MA, USA) at 6 atm (Figure 4A–C). Post-angioplasty angiography showed only slight luminal improvement (Figure 4D and E; Supplementary material online, Video S2). However, the Pd/Pa increased to 0.84 (Figure 4F), indicating marked haemodynamic improvement. Based on these results, the procedure was completed without stenting.

(D) Angiography before dilation and (E) after dilation showed only slight improvement. (F) The Pd/Pa ratio measured after balloon dilation significantly improved to 0.84. PTRA, percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty; Pd/Pa ratio, ratio of mean distal to lesion pressure and mean proximal pressure.

The patient received dual-antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 100 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg daily) for 3 months. One month later, the azilsartan treatment (20 mg) was resumed. Her home BP stabilized at approximately 125/75 mmHg. At the 1-year follow-up, the PSV in the right renal artery had improved to 165.3 cm/s, with well-controlled BP using monotherapy.

Discussion

This case report provides two key clinical insights. First, Pd/Pa measurements before and after PTRA may be a useful endpoint in FMD-related RAS even when the angiographic changes are subtle. In this patient, a post-procedural Pd/Pa of 0.84 correlated with good BP control at 1 year. Second, in multifocal FMD, in which angiography alone may underestimate the effect of angioplasty, the Pd/Pa ratio can help guide therapy and avoid unnecessary stenting.

Although PTRA is an established option for FMD-associated RAS, a Pd/Pa threshold of <0.90 has been proposed for identifying lesions with significant flow limitation.6,7 Pd/Pa assessment provides a functional evaluation of the haemodynamic significance of RAS, with progressively lower values reflecting greater impairment of renal perfusion.8,9 However, a consensus on the optimal endpoint of intervention is lacking. Previous reports have relied primarily on the angiographic findings; however, this approach may not reflect true physiological improvements.6 In our case, despite minimal visual improvement, Pd/Pa rose from 0.44 to 0.84, and hypertension was controlled without additional therapy.

Of note, this case shows that a post-procedural Pd/Pa < 0.90 may still result in favourable outcomes. This aligns with the observations in coronary angioplasty, where balloon-induced delayed lumen enlargement may continue after the procedure.10 It is also possible that the passage of the pressure wire in the tortuous, beaded vessel temporarily compressed the lesion, falsely lowered Pd/Pa ratio and underestimated the improvement.

Anatomical factors may also contribute to procedural decision making. In patients of FMD presenting multifocal RAS, it is well recognized that angiographic severity may not correlate with the pressure gradient across the lesion.7 The patient’s renal artery showed an early bifurcation 5.0 mm from the aortic origin, which is an uncommon variant combined with the distal location of the multifocal FMD.11,12 These features result in smaller target vessels, which increases the risks of stenting. Balloon angioplasty guided by Pd/Pa ratio alone proved to be both effective and safe.

Conclusion

In cases of multifocal FMD-related RAS, Pd/Pa measurement before and after PTRA may offer a reliable physiological endpoint, especially when angiographic results are inconclusive. Pd/Pa measurements may help guide interventions and support long-term BP control while avoiding unnecessary stenting.
