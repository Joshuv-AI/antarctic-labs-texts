Conjunctival Lymphangiectasia is Likely Lymphatic–venous Lesions: A Clinicopathologic and Immunohistochemical Study
Authors: Huda Al Ghadeer, Catherine Wang, Hind Alkatan, Rajiv Khandekar, Azza Al Maktabi, Deepak P. Edward
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:To report the clinical presentation, histopathological and immunohistological features of conjunctival lymphatic–venous lesions.METHODS:This was a retrospective review of 15 patients with symptomatic conjunctival lymphatic–venous lesions. The clinical features and histopathologic features of the excised tissues were reviewed. Immunohistochemical staining with antibodies against CD34 to label vascular endothelium and D2-40 to label lymphatic endothelium was performed.RESULTS:All patients had unilateral conjunctival involvement. The mean age was 48.9 ± 18.9 years, with more women affected (67%). No patient had impaired visual acuity secondary to the lesion. The location of the lesion was temporal in 67% of patients. The presenting symptoms included conjunctival swelling, discomfort and/or foreign-body sensation, and tearing. All lesions were excised, and none recurred. All excised lesions showed dilated channels that were lined by a flattened endothelium staining positive with CD34, surrounded by edematous lamina propria. Larger ectatic lymphatic channels demonstrated scattered D2-40 staining in the endothelial cell lining and patchy CD34 staining within the endothelial cell cytoplasm. D2-40 and CD-34 immunoreactivity did not overlap in the same cells.CONCLUSION:The clinical features and outcomes of the lesions in this large cohort were similar to those reported in the literature. However, the mixed immunoreactivity of the endothelial cells lining these ectatic lymphatic channels in the conjunctiva suggests that these channels are lymphatic–venous lesions. We suggest that these channels be termed conjunctival lymphaticovenous malformation rather than lymphangiectasia, which suggests ectasia of existing lymphatics. Future studies are needed to understand these lesions and their histopathologic origins.

Introduction

Vascular tumors of the conjunctiva are less frequent than those of the other periocular tissues, accounting for only 4% of nonmelanocytic tumors of this tissue.[1] Vascular lesions include lymphangioma, lymphangiectasia, capillary hemangioma, pyogenic granuloma, acquired sessile hemangioma, Kaposi’s sarcoma, varix, cavernous hemangioma, racemose hemangioma, glomangioma, and hemangiopericytoma.[2345] The clinical differentiation of these tumors is based on the patient’s medical background, as well as certain typical clinical and histopathologic features of the lesion. Conjunctival lymphangiectasia is a rare condition in which the normal lymphatic vessels are dilated and prominent within the bulbar conjunctiva.[67] It accounts for <1% of all conjunctival lesions in the general population.[8] There is little published information on this condition but it is presumably caused by obstruction of lymphatic channels which leads to the “dilated channel” appearance.[9] It occurs in two forms: a diffuse enlargement of lymphatics that appears clinically as chemosis or focal dilated lymphatics that manifest as a cyst or a series of cysts “string of pearls.”[10] Although it often resolves spontaneously, occasionally the conjunctiva becomes chronically swollen, leading to tear film disruption.[9] Generally, these lesions are asymptomatic, but symptoms may include ocular irritation, dryness, epiphora, blurred vision, and pain.[11] If there is bleeding into the lymph channels, it is called hemorrhagic lymphangiectasia.[1213]

While the origins of orbital lesions are not well established, studies have shown the presence of lymphatic components in the orbit and within the eye, including the conjunctiva and ciliary body.[1415] Therefore, as normal conjunctiva has lymphatics and morphologically by light microscopy these lesions appeared as dilated lymphatics, the lesions have been called lymphangiectasia.[67] In this study, we examined 15 cases of ectatic lymphatic-like channels, describing the clinical presentation and distinctive histological and immunophenotypical characteristics of this condition.

Methods

This is a retrospective study that was conducted after approval by the Institutional Review Board of King Khaled Eye Specialist Hospital, and followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants have provided written informed consent to participate in this study. We reviewed the clinical records and histopathology files of patients referred to our tertiary eye care center from 2011 to 2021. We identified 15 cases with the diagnosis of conjunctival lymphangiectasia that underwent excision. The clinical and demographic data, including medical/surgical history, ophthalmic examination, tissue diagnosis, and follow-up after surgery, was collected. Light microscopic examination was performed on excised tissue. We examined the lesions for the presence of fibrosis in the surrounding stroma, presence of lymphoid follicles or infiltrates in association with ectatic channels, and hemorrhage within the lymphatic lumen. Immunohistochemistry was performed using monoclonal antibodies raised against Lymphatic Vessel Endothelial Receptor 1 (rabbit antihuman D2-40-1, 1:100, Abcam, Cambridge, MA) and vascular endothelial cell marker mouse antihuman CD-34 (1:100, Dako, Carpinteria, CA) using indirect immunohistochemistry with 3-3’ diaminobenzidine as the chromogen. CD34 labels vascular endothelial cells and D2-40 labels lymphatic endothelial cells.[16] Staining of endothelial cells in aberrant lymphatic channels, normal blood vessels, and normal lymphatics of conjunctival stroma were assessed qualitatively.

Results

The hospital database identified 15 patients who had excision of the lesion performed as shown in Table 1. The mean age was 48.9 ± 18.9 years. It was more common in women 10 (67%), and the left eye 10 (67%). All cases were unilateral (100%) with 20/20 visual acuity in the affected eye. The lesion was temporal to cornea in 10 (67%) eyes. The presenting complaint included conjunctival swelling (n = 11), discomfort and/or foreign body sensation (n = 8), and tearing (n = 7). Pathologic findings

By light microscopy, the conjunctival epithelium in all the lesions appeared unremarkable. Normal conjunctival lymphatics were determined by the vessel caliber and positive D2-40 staining showed lymphoid follicles in proximity to these channels. The lymphatic channels were of varying sizes, with some larger channels appearing multiloculated and located at varying levels of the substantia propria and not just the superficial stroma where normal lymphatics are located. The lymphatic channels were lined by a monolayer of flattened cells and had larger more convoluted lumens than normal conjunctival lymphatics, which were located in the superficial substantia propria and had smaller lumens [Figure 2]. The conjunctival stroma appeared normal in the majority of the cases (86.7%). Two cases showed mild fibrosis surrounding abnormal channels. Scattered mature lymphocytes and plasma cells were seen in seven cases, but these were not associated with the lymphatic channels, and they did not appear to represent an inflammatory reaction in the stroma. Only one case showed isolated lymphoid follicles associated in proximity with ectatic lymphatic channels.

Immunohistochemistry with CD34 antibody staining demonstrated positive staining within endothelial cell cytoplasm of normal vessels in the conjunctival stroma. The endothelium of the larger ectatic lymphatic channels also demonstrated patchy CD34-positive staining within the endothelial cell cytoplasm [Figure 3]. D2-40 staining showed positive staining of the normal conjunctival lymphatic endothelium as expected [Figure 3]. However, in the 15 specimens, the endothelial cells lining the larger ectatic lymphatic channels showed patchy, scattered D2-40 immunoreactivity, and in general fewer cells appeared D2-40 positive that labeled with CD-34 [Figure 4]. On comparing D2-40 immunoreactivity with CD-34 immunoreactivity in sections where the same ectatic channels could be identified, the immunoreactivity with the two antibodies did not overlap the same cells. There were also some cells that lacked both CD34 and D2-40 immunoreactivity.

Discussion

In this large series, we describe the clinicopathological features of ectatic lymphatic channels in the conjunctiva and through immunohistochemistry, we show that these lesions have both lymphatic and vascular endothelial cells. Currently, there is limited published information on the clinical features of conjunctival lymphangiectasia lesions, with even less information about the histology.[9] They also described that the surrounding lamina propria can be edematous, with scattered inflammatory cells or show fibroblastic proliferation or scarring, depending on the underlying pathophysiology.[9] These characteristics were seen in our study, and we also identified an interesting pattern of CD34 and D2-40 immunoreactivity. D2-40 is a known sensitive and specific marker of lymphatic endothelium while CD34 is an established marker of vascular endothelium.[1617] Smaller lymphatics identified by histological features stained positively with D2-40 as expected. However, large ectatic channels with histologic features consistent with “lymphangiectasia” lacked uniform D2-40 immunoreactivity in the endothelial cells lining the channels. Furthermore, these endothelial cells lining the large ectatic channels stained positively with anti-CD34 antibody, indicating a vascular origin.[16] Based on our immunohistochemical findings, we suggest that these enlarged channels represent lymphaticovenous malformations and not dilation of existing lymphatics of the conjunctiva, which are mainly located in the superficial portions of the conjunctival stroma.[18] These lymphatic–venous channels in this study were present both in the superficial and deeper layers of the conjunctival stroma. Lymphaticovenous malformations are a range of abnormal vascular anomalies, varying between dominantly venous or dominantly lymphatic characteristics.[192021] This spectrum of presentations stems from a common lineage of vascular endothelial cell types that are influenced by likely both biochemical and mechanical signaling processes.[21] Since the orbit does not have significant lymphatics besides the skin, conjunctiva, lacrimal gland, and the perineural area, the origin of orbital lymphaticovenous malformations are likely primary orbital varies and/or lymphatic or venous systems of the periorbital structures.[212223242526]

The clinical features of these lesions were consistent with the limited published studies on conjunctival lymphangiectasia.[919] The symptoms associated with these lesions include ocular irritation, dryness, epiphora, blurred vision, and pain.[9] Common presenting features in our patients included conjunctival swelling (73.3%) followed by ocular discomfort (53.3%) and then epiphora (46.7%). No recurrence was observed within 3 years, which is consistent with previous smaller studies and literature review on conjunctival lymphangiectasia.[9] Clinically, these lesions described in the report are distinguished from conjunctival lymphangioma with variable clinical features.[5] Lymphangiomas can simultaneously affect the nasal cavity, potentially causing concurrent conjunctival hemorrhage and epistaxis, and be a rare component of hereditary systemic lymphangiomatosis.[2728]

Despite the limited sample size, this is the largest case series with the most complete histopathological descriptions to date. Of note, previous studies examining “conjunctival lymphangiectasia” were either singular case reports or had smaller case numbers.[912] In addition, most of the recently published literature on this subject focuses on treatment modalities, rather than histopathology.[293031]

Conclusion

Our study found that lesions that are conventionally identified as conjunctival lymphangiectasia have immunohistopathological findings that suggest they are not completely lymphatic in origin. One could argue that D2-40 immunoreactivity was lost as malformed channels became dilated and CD34 was expressed at a later stage of ectasia. However, it is more plausible that, similar to the orbit, the endothelial cells lining these channels are heterogeneous based on the location of these channels and pattern of staining. We therefore suggest that the term conjunctival lymphaticovenous malformations be used to describe this condition rather than lymphangiectasia, which suggests ectasia of existing vessels. Future studies can look at these conjunctival lymphatic–venous lesions and further examine them in the context of orbital lymphaticovenous malformations.
