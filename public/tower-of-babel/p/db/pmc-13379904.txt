Percutaneous Aspiration of Ascending Aortic Mural Thrombus Under Intra-Aortic Intracardiac Echocardiographic Guidance
Authors: Ahmad Al-Sahli, John Fish, Daniel Marcusa, Alexander Ghannam, Michael Moront, Stephanie Younes, Ahmad Younes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAscending aortic mural thrombus can be highly mobile and cause systemic embolization, even without aneurysm or advanced atherosclerosis.Case SummaryA 57-year-old man with coronary artery disease, prior coronary artery bypass grafting, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and recent orthopedic surgery presented with chest tightness and cough. Computed tomography angiography revealed a multilobulated ascending aortic mural thrombus measuring 26 × 11 mm. Despite heparin therapy, he developed acute right internal carotid artery occlusion requiring thrombectomy. Follow-up imaging showed persistent mobile thrombi, and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was limited. Percutaneous debulking using the AngioVac system with intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) guidance successfully removed the thrombus without complications.DiscussionThis case demonstrates a novel percutaneous approach for ascending aortic thrombus removal using intra-aortic ICE to overcome TEE blind spots, allowing safe, targeted aspirations.Take-Home MessageTEE has blind spots in the proximal ascending aorta. ICE provides real-time guidance, facilitating safer real-time procedural visualization and AngioVac thrombus aspirations.

History of Presentation

A 57-year-old man with coronary artery disease with prior coronary artery bypass grafting, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and recent orthopedic surgery, presented with 1 week of shortness of breath and cough. Physical examination was unremarkable, and oxygen saturation was 97% on room air. Chest computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed an irregular mural thrombus in the ascending aorta proximal to the great vessels, without evidence of aneurysm or dissection (Figure 1).Figure 1Preprocedural Computed Tomography Angiography(Left) CTA axial view demonstrated multilobulated mural thrombus in the ascending aorta (red circle), maximum size 26 × 11 mm. Past Medical History

The patient's medical history was notable for coronary artery disease with subsequent triple-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with ongoing tobacco use, and ischemic cardiomyopathy with now-recovered ejection fraction. Additional comorbidities included hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia.

Investigations

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated multiple layered mural thrombi in both the ascending and descending thoracic aorta but no mobile mass, though there was incomplete evaluation of the distal ascending aorta given acoustic shadowing from the trachea and right mainstem bronchus.1 Heparin was initiated in the emergency department and maintained without interruption. Despite therapeutic anticoagulation, the following day the patient developed an acute neurologic change and was found to have a right internal carotid artery occlusion, for which he underwent mechanical thrombectomy with successful reperfusion. A repeat CTA of the chest performed the next day demonstrated persistent mobile thrombi measuring approximately 1.3 cm and 1.2 cm, along with occlusion of the right common iliac artery.

Management

After the patient's stroke, mechanical thrombectomy was performed for the right internal carotid artery occlusion, but persistent thrombus on repeat imaging led to a multidisciplinary discussion including the cardiac surgery, neurology, and cardiovascular teams. Given recurrent embolization and thrombus mobility, the group elected to offer percutaneous debulking using the AngioVac system (AngioDynamics). Given this would be a redo sternotomy with patent bypass grafts and recent large-territory stroke, we chose an endovascular attempt first.2 This decision was complicated by the inability to use TEE for procedural guidance, challenges with cerebral protection because of clot position, and significant peripheral arterial disease with occlusion of the right common iliac artery.

Given the presence of atherosclerotic disease, the patient's arteries were unable to accommodate a 22-F Gore DrySeal. After serial dilation, an F18 AngioVac cannula outer sleeve was advanced into the artery. A 17-F return cannula was placed in the right common femoral vein for blood return. Venous blood return was chosen given normal ejection fraction and short expected pump time. The initial plan for intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) in the aorta via femoral access was abandoned however, given the right iliac occlusion, and an 8-F × 30-cm left brachial artery sheath was placed. ICE was advanced via the brachial sheath into the aorta and allowed for thrombus localization and procedural guidance. The AngioVac cannula was then advanced under combined fluoroscopic and ICE, with flows gradually increased to 4 to 5 L/min. A large mobile thrombus was successfully aspirated, though some residual broad-based thrombus remained (Figure 2). Hemostasis was achieved using Perclose (Abbott) and Angio-Seal (Terumo) devices, and the patient remained hemodynamically stable throughout the procedure. Outcome and Follow-Up

There were no embolic or vascular complications during the procedure. Postprocedure, the patient remained hemodynamically stable, with no new neurological deficits. Follow-up CTA demonstrated irregular filling defects within the distal ascending aorta, consistent with the residual broad-based adherent thrombus, no evidence of penetrating aortic ulcer (Figure 3). Pathology from aspirated material confirmed the diagnosis of thrombus. Hypercoagulable work-up was negative for any underlying thrombophilia.Figure 3Postintervention Computed Tomography AngiographyCTA 48 hours postprocedure demonstrated irregular filling defects within the distal ascending aorta consistent with the residual broad-based adherent thrombus, with no evidence of penetrating aortic ulcer. Discussion

Aortic mural thrombus in a nonaneurysmal ascending aorta is rare and poses a significant risk of systemic embolization.3 Management is complex and typically individualized, with options including systemic anticoagulation, open surgical thrombectomy, and endovascular approaches. Choice of therapy depends on thrombus mobility, embolic complications, and patient-specific operative risk. The AngioVac system consists of an aspiration cannula that comes in different curves and 2 different sizes, 22-F and 18-F. For this case, we used the F18 cannula (18-F; allows for an 85° curvature). The system also consists of an extracorporeal circuit designed for short-term use during aspiration procedures. It enables active removal of fresh, soft thrombi or emboli through aspiration, offering a less invasive therapeutic option. The system filters and reinfuses the patient's blood through a return cannula that is typically 15-F to 17-F, helping to minimize blood loss and maintain hemodynamic stability during the procedure.4,5 Given the patient's prior sternotomy, significant comorbidities, and recent cerebrovascular event, surgical risk was elevated, supporting an initial percutaneous strategy. ICE was essential in this case, compensating for the well-recognized blind spot of TEE in the proximal ascending aorta and allowing real-time visualization and procedural guidance for safer catheter manipulation.

Several important teaching points emerge from this case. First, TEE has limitations, particularly in evaluating the distal ascending aorta and proximal arch, where blind spots may obscure aortic pathology, including thrombi. Second, multimodality imaging including CTA and ICE performed via a brachial approach provided procedural guidance and visualization. This allowed us to plan vascular access accordingly. Third, the AngioVac system, typically used for thrombi in the right atrium or vena cava, was successfully adapted for ascending aortic thrombus removal using femoral arterial access. Site of blood reinfusion whether arterial or venous becomes important when blood is aspirated from the left side of the heart. Based on our experience, venous return is generally well tolerated except in cases of significantly reduced cardiac output. Lastly, the risk of stroke remains a major concern during catheter manipulation near mobile thrombi, and this case highlights an ICE-guided technique, ramping up flow and maintaining high flows to minimize embolic risk.

Conclusions

In carefully selected patients with mobile ascending aortic mural thrombus who are not optimal surgical candidates, percutaneous AngioVac aspiration guided by ICE represents a feasible and less invasive alternative. Understanding the limitations of TEE and implementing appropriate imaging strategies are critical to optimizing safety and procedural success.
