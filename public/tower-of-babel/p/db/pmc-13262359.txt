OmpK35/36 modulate the virulence of hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae by regulating capsular polysaccharide export
Authors: Susu Wu, Ying Zhang, Ying Sheng, Xiaobo Ma, Xinyi Yang, Bin Ma, Dakang Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the impact of outer membrane proteins 35 (OmpK35) and 36 on the virulence of Klebsiella pneumoniae and the corresponding mechanisms.MethodsSerotype K1 strain NTUH-K2044 was utilized to create single-deletion mutants (ΔompK35 and ΔompK36) and a double-deletion mutant (ΔompK35/36), alongside their complementation variants. Multiple analyses were performed, including capsule staining, transmission electron microscopy, serum resistance assays, phagocytosis tests, and infection models using mice and Galleria mellonella.ResultsThe mutants retained hypercapsules similar to NTUH-K2044, but looser capsule structures revealed by transmission electron microscopy. All mutants exhibited decreased virulence, with median lethal doses under 5 × 10⁴ colony forming units in mice. Specifically, only ΔompK35/36 showed reduced serum resistance compared to the wild strain, similar for the anti- phagocytosis capabilities. Additionally, ΔompK35 induced liver inflammation, whereas ΔompK36 and ΔompK35/36 exerted milder effects. Despite these differences, all mutants displayed normal growth rates and upregulated expression of gnd and wcaJ, crucial for capsular polysaccharide (CPS) biosynthesis.ConclusionsThe hypercapsule, composed mainly of CPS, plays a crucial role in the hypervirulence of K. pneumoniae. OmpK35/36 may modulate the virulence of K. pneumoniae by regulating CPS export.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05212-3.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae, a notorious member of “ESKAPE” pathogens, is widely distributed in nature and poses significant health threat because of its ability to cause severe infections [1, 2]. Clinically, K. pneumoniae ranks as the third most common bacterial pathogen associated with infections and the fourth leading cause of bacteria-associated mortality [3]. Its remarkable capacity for antibiotic resistance and the evolution of hypervirulence have presented escalating challenges to global health [4, 5].

However, the pathogenesis of K. pneumoniae infections remains partially understood, regardless of its importance. Host defense mechanisms, including serum killing and phagocytosis, play a crucial role in determining an infection [6]. Conversely, to evade host immunity, K. pneumoniae employs a variety of virulence strategies including CPS [6], which constitute hypercapsule, a key determinant of the bacterium’s hypervirulence [7]. Moreover, the biosynthesis, export, and anchoring of CPS are tightly regulated by virulence genes rmpAs, wzy, manC, galF, wcaJ, gnd, rcsA/B, and wzi [4].

Further, the underlying mechanisms about outer membrane proteins (OMPs) OmpK35 and OmpK36 in modulating antibiotic resistance and virulence in K. pneumoniae remain unclear, despite their implications [6, 8], whose absence can enhance drug resistance [9] while paradoxically leading to a significant reduction in virulence.

In this study, isogenic mutants (ΔompK35, ΔompK36, and ΔompK35/36) and their complementation strains (ΔompK35 + ompK35, ΔompK36 + ompK36, and ΔompK35/36 + ompK35/36) were constructed using hypervirulent K1 strain NTUH-K2044, which were further utilized in murine and Galleria mellonella infection models, with the aim of elucidating the effects of OmpK35 and OmpK36 on CPS export and their contributions to the virulence of K. pneumoniae.

Materials and methods

Strains and plasmids

K. pneumoniae strain NTUH-K2044 was provided by Professor Jin-Town Wang from National Taiwan University Hospital (Taiwan, China). NTUH-K2044, a representative hypervirulent strain with accession number AP006725.1, is classified as sequence type 23 and serotype K1 and exhibits resistance only to ampicillin. K. pneumoniae strain HS11286 (accession number: CP003200.1; serotyped as K47) was initially isolated from a patient with pneumonia at Huashan Hospital. NTUH-K2044 was utilized as the positive control, while HS11286 was the negative control.

The plasmids used in this study included pCas (apramycin resistance, utilized for gene knockout), pSGKP (rifampicin resistance, also used for gene knockout), and pBAD24 (apramycin resistance, employed for gene complementation).

Knockout and complementation of ompK35/36 in NTUH-K2044

The CRISPR-Cas9 gene deletion method [10] was employed to construct mutants ΔompK35, ΔompK36, and ΔompK35/36 using the wild strain NTUH-K2044. The primers utilized for this process are listed in Table S1. The successful knockouts were confirmed through regular PCR, electrophoresis, and sequencing.

pBAD24 was used to construct complementation mutants ΔompK35 + ompK35, ΔompK36 + ompK36, and ΔompK35/36 + ompK35/36 by electroforming mutants ΔompK35, ΔompK36, and ΔompK35/36. The primers used in this process are also detailed in Table S1. Complementation was verified using standard PCR, electrophoresis, and sequencing.

String test

K. pneumoniae strains were incubated overnight at 37 °C on 5% sheep blood agar plates (Shanghai Chromagar Microbiology Technology, Shanghai, China). Further, a standard loop was used to stretch the cultures [11], with viscous string longer than 5 mm being considered a positive result.

Morphological determination

K. pneumoniae strains were incubated overnight at 37 °C on 5% sheep blood agar plates, and the diameters of colonies were measured.

Capsule staining

K. pneumoniae strains were cultured overnight on 5% sheep blood agar plates and subsequently stained using a capsule staining kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (BASO, Zhuhai, China). Capsules were examined under a Leica light microscope with a 100× objective lens.

Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining

K. pneumoniae strains were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) overnight and subsequently PAS staining was performed to detect exopolysaccharides (EPS) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (BASO, Zhuhai, China).

Fitness analysis

The fitness of K. pneumoniae strains was assessed [12], which were first cultured in LB broth until an OD600 of 0.5 was reached, and then diluted to OD600 of 0.001 and incubated at 37 °C under aerobic conditions. The OD600 was monitored every 30 min.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted to analyze the expression of gnd and wcaJ using an ABI 7500 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA), with 16 S rRNA serving as the internal control. The primers utilized in the assays are detailed in Table S1. The experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (FS-Q1002; FOREVER STAR, Beijing, China). Total RNAs of each sample were harvested nine times, followed by qRT-PCR analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM was performed using an HT7700 Electron Microscope (HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan), as previously reported [13]. K. pneumoniae strains were cultured on 5% sheep blood agar plates overnight before analysis. The samples were all processed once but more than five fields of each sample were inspected for TEM analysis.

Human neutrophil killing assay and serum killing assay

The human neutrophil killing assay was conducted in accordance with the referenced protocol [14]. The neutrophils were collected from three healthy volunteers and mixed thoroughly immediately for the human neutrophil killing assay.

The serum killing assay protocol was adapted from the reference [15]. The fresh sera were pooled from twenty healthy volunteers, equal volume for each volunteer, which were used immediately for the serum killing assay.

Lethality test

BALB/c mice were procured from Zhejiang Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology. The mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. The tested strains were grown in LB broth to an OD600 of 0.5 and then washed once before being adjusted to 1 × 107 colony forming unit (CFU)/ml in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Six-week-old pathogen-free female BALB/c mice were randomly assigned to groups, with four mice in each group, and were inoculated intraperitoneally with 100 µl of the K. pneumoniae strains [16]. The mice were monitored for 14 days. Median lethal dose (LD50) values and survival curves were then yielded as the reference. The LD50 of NTUH-K2044 was assessed using inocula of 102, 103, and 104 CFUs. To make further comparisons among all the groups, inocula of 5 × 103 and 5 × 104 CFUs were administered, with K. pneumoniae being classified as hypervirulent when its LD50 was ≤ 107 CFUs. The typical hypervirulence threshold is ≤ 106 CFUs while a direct bacterial suspension is used [2]. Our study employed once-washed bacterial suspension, which attenuated the virulence for one gradient.

The lethality test was also conducted using wax moth (Galleria mellonella) larvae (Huiyude Biotech, China) through methodology suggested [17, 18]. The tested strains were cultured in LB broth until reaching an OD600 of 0.5. The bacterial suspensions were then adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 107 CFU/ml using PBS, with PBS being the blank control. Surveillance time points after post-infection were established at 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h.

Hematoxylin & Eosin (HE) staining

On the 3rd day the mice were infected with K. pneumoniae, they were euthanized through CO₂ inhalation, followed by decapitation. Their livers and spleens were dissected and fixed with 10% formalin. 5 μm-thick paraffin sections were prepared using a Microtome (Leica, shanghai, China). HE staining was performed before microscopy.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (Dotmatics, USA). The difference was considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. The statistical methods included one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test, the Kruskal-Wallis test, and Dunn’s multiple comparisons test.

Discussion

Traditionally, OMPs in K. pneumoniae are regarded as being associated with antibiotic resistance rather than virulence [8, 19–23]. OmpK35 and OmpK36, which form trimeric β-barrel pores and constitute the major porins of K. pneumoniae [24, 25], are homologs of Escherichia coli OmpF and OmpC, respectively [24]. Notably, OmpK35 forms wider pores than OmpK36 [25]. Our study reveals that the absence of either OmpK35 or OmpK36 partially impedes CPS export, resulting in decreased capsule density and diminished virulence.

The hypervirulent strain NTUH-K2044 typically exhibits an LD50 of < 10² CFU in mice [26]. However, after a single PBS wash (to homogenize tested strains while adjusting concentrations), its LD50 increased to 10³ CFU, prompting us to set the hypervirulence threshold at 10⁷ CFU [2]. Whereas the LD50 values of ΔompK35, ΔompK36, and ΔompK35/36 mutants remained below 5 × 10⁴ CFU (retaining hypervirulence status), their virulence significantly reduced compared with that of NTUH-K2044. This attenuation is in line with prior reports [24, 27, 28] and is partially supported by findings of reduced serum resistance and impaired anti-phagocytic activity [22, 29]. Only dual absence of OmpK35 and OmpK36 impaired serum resistance and anti-phagocytosis significantly (Fig. 3).

Consistent with findings from clinical and intraperitoneal infection models [26, 30, 31], the liver was more severely impacted than the spleen. After the PBS wash, the virulence of NTUH-K2044 and its mutants decreased by one gradient, and therefore, no pyogenic abscesses were observed; however, inflammatory responses persisted. TEM analysis revealed that ΔompK35, ΔompK36, and ΔompK35/36 mutants displayed looser and more radially dispersed capsules than those of NTUH-K2044, although capsule staining confirmed their hypercapsule phenotype. Given that hypercapsulation is essential for hypervirulence [7], this structural alteration probably contributes to their reduced virulence.

The looser capsules of OMP-deficient mutants suggest either impaired CPS export or decreased synthesis. Whereas OMPs are classically viewed as import channels for nutrients and antimicrobials [26, 32], our data challenge this paradigm. Notably, no growth defects were observed in the mutants, even after prolonged monitoring. Additionally, the upregulation of CPS biosynthesis genes (gnd and wcaJ) indicated increased CPS production; the looser capsules suggest the presence of significant CPS in capsules and a slight blockage in export. The growth speeds of ΔompK35, ΔompK36, and ΔompK35/36 did not decline compared with that of NTUH-K2044, providing stronger evidence for steady growth speeds than previous reports, as our study included more monitoring points in the growth curves [8]. Therefore, OmpK35 and OmpK36 appear to serve a dual function: facilitating nutrient uptake, while also mediating CPS export.

Although CPS is a cornerstone of hypervirulence in K. pneumoniae [7], its exact synthetic routes are still veiled. K. pneumoniae encompasses 79 serotypes [6], with NTUH-K2044 (K1) potentially synthesizing EPS via four pathways [4], similar to E. coli [33]. Among these, the Wzx/Wzy pathway is the predominant route. In the study, the three mutants all presented longer strings but looser capsules. The positive string test is more dependent on EPS than on CPS. EPS and CPS are both polysaccharides and may partially use the same synthetic pathway. We hypothesize that CPSs are heterogeneous in structure and molecular weight, with a subset being exported through OmpK35/36 pores. However, the precise biosynthetic pathways of CPS remain to be fully elucidated, particularly when considering the other serotypes.

Although TEM and gene expression data support the involvement of OmpK35/36 in CPS efflux, current evidence cannot distinguish between direct channel effects and indirect effects. The deletion of OmpK35/36 may indirectly regulate CPS efflux by altering the periplasmic environment or inducing envelope stress responses (such as the σE/Cpx pathway) [34, 35]. Therefore, we infer that OmpK35/36 is the key determinant, but whether it is a direct efflux channel still needs to be verified.

In addition, other certain limitations still exist in this study. First, only one K1 strain was focused in this investigation, while K. pneumoniae demonstrates significant variability across various characteristics. Second, a comprehensive quantification of CPS was lacked, which could have provided additional evidence to support the blocking effects observed in the ΔompK35, ΔompK36, and ΔompK35/36 mutants. Moreover, there is not a precise method appropriate for distinguishing slight changes of CPS production in this paper. The glucuronic acid method [36] was performed but failed to verify the slight changes (data not shown).

Conclusions

This study underscores the pivotal roles of OmpK35 and OmpK36 in the export of CPS in K. pneumoniae. The absence of these proteins compromises capsule integrity, resulting in a significant reduction in virulence and a diminished likelihood of pyogenic liver abscess formation. This finding redefines their functions, extending beyond mere antibiotic resistance to encompass critical aspects of bacterial pathogenesis. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for unraveling the virulence and pathogenicity of K. pneumoniae infections.
