ABSTRACT
IntroductionEffective insulin therapy relies on proper injection technique and the correct use of insulin delivery devices. Introduction

Insulin therapy is a cornerstone of diabetes management, and its effectiveness depends heavily on proper injection technique and adherence to best practices for insulin delivery devices. One area of persistent concern is insulin pen needle reuse, a behavior that continues to be common despite clear recommendations against needle reuse from international diabetes care organizations.

Previous studies have documented high rates of pen needle reuse across populations, with convenience, cost, and habit cited as the most frequent reasons [1, 2]. However, some of these studies, particularly those derived from indirect calculations of insulin use and device supply rather than direct patient reporting may have limitations in accuracy, by relying on assumptions about insulin consumption and reuse frequency rather than firsthand behavior. In contrast, self-reported data collected through surveys can provide a more direct understanding of reuse practices.

Reuse undermines sterility and has been associated with adverse injection-site outcomes such as lipohypertrophy, bleeding, bruising, and pain, which in turn can contribute to impaired insulin absorption and glycemic variability [3, 4].

While insulin injection education is considered standard of care at therapy initiation, evidence suggests that ongoing reinforcement is neglected and that healthcare professionals (HCPs) may not routinely review injection technique during follow-up visits [6]. This gap creates a behavioral challenge in diabetes self-management: patients may be aware of recommendations but continue to engage in reuse due to cost pressures, environmental concerns, or perceived safety based on prior experience. Importantly, recent work has highlighted the role of targeted messaging and trusted sources, such as endocrinologists, primary care physicians (PCPs), and diabetes educators, in modifying needle reuse behavior [5, 6].

This study sought to evaluate current patterns of pen needle reuse among U.S. adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) who inject insulin at least twice per day and report reusing a pen needle at least once before switching to a new needle. It also aimed to explore patient-reported reasons for reuse, characterize adults with higher reuse frequency, including demographics and clinical factors, and identify the most effective educational and motivational strategies for reducing reuse. By surveying a large national sample, this study adds to existing literature on injection behaviors and provides actionable insights for clinical education and patient-support interventions.

Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

A cross-sectional online survey was conducted through Qualtrics between June 27 and July 16, 2025, using the Thrivable proprietary health panel database. This panel includes over 79,000 individuals in the United States living with diabetes or serving as caregivers for people with diabetes (PWD).

Institutional review board (IRB) review and formal participant consent were not required for this research under U.S. federal regulations. Under 45 CFR 46.102(e) and 45 CFR 46.104(d), research involving fully de-identified, anonymous survey data without any identifiable private information does not meet the regulatory definition of human subjects research and is exempt from IRB oversight. While Thrivable members provide identifiable private information at the time of panel registration, this information was not accessed or used in the survey analysis. All survey responses used for this study were fully de-identified, and no identifiable data were included in the manuscript. All participants were existing members of the Thrivable panel and had previously provided consent to participate in research activities and share de-identified data in accordance with the Thrivable Research Consent and Privacy Policies (appended in the electronic supplementary material). In accordance with these policies, participants agreed that their de-identified responses may be used for research, presented publicly, or published in scientific journals. No identifying information was disclosed, accessible to authors outside of Thrivable, or included in any responses used in this manuscript. At the beginning of the survey, participants were also informed that by continuing, they were agreeing to these policies, and advancing past the first page therefore constituted electronic informed consent. Participants were free to discontinue participation at any time.

To maximize high-quality data, the Thrivable panel employs a robust quality validation system that leverages SEON-supported checks and evaluates condition- and behavior-specific attributes to flag nonsensical therapy combinations, inconsistent responses over time, and other indicators of low-quality participation.

Data Analysis

Prior to analysis, data cleaning procedures were conducted to ensure data quality, including removal of responses flagged by Qualtrics as potential bots or low-quality submissions using built-in reCAPTCHA technology, as well as responses containing nonsensical open-ended text. Nonsensical responses were defined as entries consisting primarily of gibberish or text unrelated to the question prompt. All exclusions were made prior to conducting the analysis.

Data collection, management, and analyses were conducted using Qualtrics. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sample characteristics and survey responses. Subgroup analyses were conducted to compare responses by diabetes type (T1D vs. T2D), age groups, gender, race/ethnicity, and reported levels of needle reuse (2–5 times vs. ≥ 6 times). Statistical significance for subgroup differences was assessed using pairwise Z-tests and chi-square tests at a 95% confidence interval. All statistically significant segmentation findings reported throughout reflect differences at p < 0.05. Statistically significant segmentation findings are also denoted in the figures with capitalized lettering. Respondents reported their exact age in an open-text field and were grouped into predefined ranges for analysis and reporting purposes.

Participant demographics

The majority of participants (85.0%) had been diagnosed ≥ 5 years ago and most had been on insulin therapy for an extended period (75.6% injecting insulin for ≥ 5 years), indicating a high-risk profile for complications and ongoing disease management challenges [7]; 1.8% of participants reported using basal insulin only, injecting at least two times per day.

Patterns of Pen Needle Reuse

Among participants who started the survey and met the initial eligibility criteria, 50.0% qualified based on reported pen needle reuse. Among those who qualified and completed the survey, reuse of 2–5 times was most frequently reported (70.2%). However, 29.8% of all qualified participants indicated reusing a pen needle ≥ 6 times (high reuse group), and a subset (13.4%) reported reuse of > 10 times. As there is no official or validated categorization for the number of pen needle reuses, the threshold for defining the higher reuse group (≥ 6 uses) was determined based on clinical experience.

Reuse patterns differed by diabetes type. Participants with T2D were significantly more likely to reuse needles 2–3 times before replacement (52.8% T2D vs. 44.0% T1D), whereas participants with T1D were significantly more likely to report higher levels of reuse (> 10 times; 17.6% T1D vs. 9.2% T2D) (Fig. 2). Reasons for Needle Reuse

The most frequently cited reasons for pen needle reuse were convenience (64.2%), habit (reusing needles for a long time without issues; 46.2%), environmental/waste concerns (40.8%), and cost (40.6%). Convenience emerged as one of the most influential factors, with 61.4% of those who selected it ranking it as their primary reason for reuse.

Participants with T1D were more likely than those with T2D to report convenience (71.2% T1D vs. 57.2% T2D), cost (45.6% T1D vs. 35.6% T2D), forgetting to bring an extra pen needle when leaving home (32.8% T1D vs. 22.4% T2D), not receiving enough needles with their prescription (17.6% T1D vs. 10.0% T2D), habit (52.4% T1D vs. 40.0% T2D), and environmental/waste concerns (49.2% T1D vs. 32.4% T2D) as reasons for reuse. Qualitative Insights on Reasons for Pen Needle Reuse

Participants provided open-ended comments elaborating on their reasons for reusing pen needles. These qualitative responses reinforced convenience and logistical burden as key drivers of reuse. One participant noted, “I take multiple injections a day. It is more difficult to carry 7–10 needles in my case…it’s easier to reuse the needle already attached if I am in a hurry, at a restaurant, driving, etc.”. Another reported, “So I don’t have to carry so much stuff with me all the time. It’s just too much to have to worry about all the time”. Others described challenges related to disposal of used needles, stating: “It is hard to find a place to dispose of needles, and I do not want to carry used needles in my purse.”

Injection-Site Issues and Educational Messaging

The majority of participants (60.6%) reported seeing an endocrinologist as their primary diabetes HCP, with those with T1D being significantly more likely to see an endocrinologist (67.6% T1D vs. 53.6% T2D).

Despite most participants reporting initial insulin education (86.8%) as well as education on changing the needle with every injection (66.8%), the majority indicated limited follow-up regarding injection practices. Specifically, 73.6% indicated their injection sites were never examined by a HCP, 72.8% reported their injection technique had never been reviewed, and 66.2% reported their HCP had never asked them about injection site problems. Participants themselves, however, frequently observed issues, with nearly half (49.4%) reporting sometimes noticing persistent lumps or bumps at injection sites. The most commonly experienced injection-related issues overall were bleeding/bruising (66.6%) and pain (54.2%). Participants with T1D were more likely than those with T2D to report lumps or bumps under the skin where they inject (45.2% T1D vs. 35.6% T2D), pain (65.6% T1D vs. 42.8% T2D), and bleeding/bruising (79.2% T1D vs. 54.0% T2D). Across all seven educational messages, the majority of participants indicated they would be extremely or somewhat likely to change their reuse behavior. The A1c improvement message stood out, with the highest proportion of likely responses (77.2%) and the lowest proportion of unlikely responses (10.1%) among the messages (28.9% extremely likely, 48.3% somewhat likely, 12.8% neutral, 7.4% somewhat unlikely, 2.7% extremely unlikely).

Discussion

Overall Findings & Implications

This survey highlights that pen needle reuse remains widespread among U.S. adults with diabetes, despite most receiving initial education at therapy initiation. Our findings align with prior research documenting high rates of reuse [1, 2, 6, 8], reinforcing that knowledge alone is insufficient to change behavior. Previous studies have shown that reuse compromises needle sterility, which increases the risk of pain, bleeding, bruising, and lipohypertrophy. These injection-site issues may in turn impair insulin absorption and contribute to greater glycemic variability [3, 4]. This underscores the importance of reinforcing single-use injection practices.

High-Frequency Reuse Characteristics

Participants with T1D, long-duration of diagnosis, and those who are white were more likely to report high reuse. The higher frequency of needle reuse observed among participants with T1D may be partly explained by their greater number of daily injections, which represents a potential confounding factor. The higher-frequency reuse patterns are concerning since individuals in this group are at greater risk of injection-site complications and inconsistent insulin absorption [3, 4, 9]. Targeted interventions are therefore particularly important for high-frequency reusers to reduce potential adverse outcomes, and are discussed further below.

Reasons for Needle Reuse

Consistent with previous studies, participants cited convenience, habit, and cost as primary drivers of reuse [1, 2], with convenience emerging as the leading reason. Convenience often reflects the perceived ease of reusing the same needle rather than carrying or disposing of additional supplies. Open-ended responses further underscored the role of convenience, particularly challenges related to carrying and disposing of multiple pen needles throughout the day. These patterns were even more pronounced among individuals in the high-reuse group, with convenience, habit, and environmental/waste concerns being particularly prominent, indicating that sustainability may be a secondary but meaningful motivator for behavior change (cited as a reason for reuse by 40.8% overall). Participants with T1D are more likely to report intentional or practical reasons for reuse (e.g., convenience, cost, habit, forgetting to bring extra needles, etc.), while those with T2D are more likely to cite knowledge gaps or fear-based concerns as reasons. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive strategies that address both individual behavior and systemic barriers, such as co-pay support programs, insurance coverage improvements, and environmentally friendly disposal programs, to complement educational strategies.

Impact of Educational Messages

Importantly, we found that targeted educational messaging, particularly around A1c reduction and lipohypertrophy, was most-strongly motivating overall as compared to other messaging frameworks, with high-reuse participants showing similar trends, suggesting that message framing can influence patient behaviors. These results support prior work emphasizing the role of clinician-patient communication and tailored education in reducing unsafe injection practices [1]. Educational messaging also resonates differently among those with T1D and T2D, with those with T1D being more motivated by reducing lipohypertrophy, whereas those with T2D are more likely to be influenced by messages about A1c, sterility, diabetes self-management best practices, diabetes expert recommendations, and avoiding pain, bleeding, and bruising.

Gaps in Clinical Reinforcement

The lack of ongoing clinician reinforcement is notable. More than 70% of participants reported never having their injection sites examined or their technique reviewed after insulin initiation, echoing concerns raised in the literature about gaps in routine diabetes care [1, 2]. This is particularly notable among the high-reuse individuals, who were significantly more likely than those using needles 2–5 times to report never having their injection technique reviewed by their HCP (68.9% 2–5 uses and 81.9% ≥ 6 uses). Similarly, while 77.0% of participants report receiving education on the importance of site rotation, a substantial proportion (43.3%) report injecting exclusively into the abdomen. These findings highlight a persistent gap between knowledge and practice, underscoring the need for reinforcement and follow-up clinical care. Closing this gap represents a key opportunity to reduce unsafe injection practices and needle reuse through longitudinal patient support programs, refreshers at follow-up visits, and campaigns centered on the most effective motivational messages. For example, “Safe and Simple” campaigns could emphasize both safety benefits and the ease of proper injection technique. Educational materials could also be provided for HCPs to share with patients, including content suitable for waiting rooms. These findings further suggest a clear opportunity for HCPs to take a more proactive role in reinforcing proper injection techniques by routinely educating patients on single-use needle recommendations, and partnering with pharmacists to encourage support and open communication around single use. Such efforts also align with established frameworks such as the Forum for Injection Technique and Therapy Expert Recommendations (FITTER) Forward initiative, which emphasizes ongoing injection technique education for HCPs, PWD, and caregivers [10].

Clinical guidelines recommend Diabetes Self-Management Education and Support (DSMES) at four critical timepoints, including at diagnosis, annually and/or when not meeting treatment targets, when complicating factors develop, and when transitions in life and care occur [11]. Given that the majority of participants have been diagnosed with diabetes for ≥ 5 years, many likely encountered more than one of these recommended opportunities for education. However, the lack of reported follow-up and injection-technique review suggests that these represent critical missed opportunities for ongoing DSMES in routine clinical care.

Role of Trusted Sources and Peer Influence

Our findings also underscore the importance of trusted sources: endocrinologists and diabetes care and education specialists emerged as the strongest influencers for behavior change, though peer influence was more prominent among those who are younger and those with T1D. These insights suggest that a multi-channel approach, combining professional reinforcement with peer-driven messaging, may be most effective. Notably, while existing literature has established that peer support can significantly impact diabetes self-management behaviors [12], our study uniquely highlights how peer influence could specifically affect needle reuse behaviors, an area previously underexplored. This underscores the need for tailored interventions that leverage peer influence to address specific self-management practices.

Access to Specialty Care

Of note, about 40% of participants overall, with 46% of those with T2D and 30% of those with T1D, report they do not see an endocrinologist as their primary diabetes HCP. Given the complexity of diabetes management, this highlights potential challenges when care is provided in settings with limited time or specialized resources to reinforce safe and effective injection practices. Since specialists often do not have the capacity to see more patients, and a significant portion of diabetes care occurs in non-specialist offices, solutions and education efforts should also target these settings, not just specialty clinics. These findings reinforce the importance of both patient education and HCP resources to support safe and effective insulin use.

Tips to Prevent Reuse

To help prevent pen needle reuse, interventions can be implemented across multiple levels, with both PWD and HCPs taking several practical steps. PWDs should plan ahead by packing an adequate supply of pen needles before leaving home, ideally more than they expect to need, to avoid situations that encourage reuse. At the PWDs level, education should also include personalized messages about specific benefits of single-use needles and obstacles to single use of needles that were identified in this study to be impactful.

HCPs play an important role in educating PWD about proper injection techniques and the importance of removing and safely discarding pen needles after each injection, as well as in providing ongoing reinforcement of skin assessment and correct injection practices, including the importance of single-use needles, through routine injection site checks. Ensuring that prescriptions provide a sufficient quantity of pen needles, and that pharmacies dispense enough to support single use with each injection and refill, may further reduce the likelihood of reuse. Additionally, pharmacists can help monitor pen needle prescription fulfillment, creating opportunities to identify and address potential reuse practices. Ongoing skin and reuse assessments, patient education, and the distribution of patient education materials as visual reminders for home use in between visits could also help to decrease and prevent reuse.

At the system level, electronic health record reminders can prompt HCPs to perform injection site checks and deliver injection technique education. Finally, at the industry level, the development of educational opportunities and materials can support consistent messaging about the importance of single-use needles for people with diabetes, their family members, and HCPs. For example, the use of colorful handouts in appropriate language and reading level, enhanced with pictures, would aid in reuse education and prevention.

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights, this study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall bias, misreporting of behavior, and social desirability bias. The sample was drawn from an online panel and is not fully representative of the broader U.S. diabetes population, limiting generalizability. Given the range of insulin pen types available, minor inconsistencies in self-reported brand names were reconciled using participants’ verified therapy profiles on the Thrivable platform. Additionally, segmented analyses should also be interpreted with caution, as differences between groups may reflect underlying sociodemographic or clinical characteristics rather than the primary segmentation variable.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that needle reuse remains widespread among U.S. adults with diabetes, and education at therapy initiation alone is insufficient to encourage safe and effective injection practices. Ongoing reinforcement through targeted educational messaging, particularly those emphasizing A1c improvement and prevention of injection-site complications, may help drive behavior change. Such efforts are likely to be most effective when delivered by trusted HCPs and complemented by peer-driven messaging and support. The findings also underscore the need for comprehensive strategies that address both individual behaviors and systemic barriers, pairing continued patient education with solutions such as enhanced coverage and environmentally responsible disposal programs, to promote sustained adherence to safe injection practices.
