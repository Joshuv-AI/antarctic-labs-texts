Altered right inferior frontal gyrus-based functional connectivity associated with inhibition through swimming exercise in children with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder
Authors: Li Ding, Wei Zhong, Lihan Chen, Huijuan Shen, Lin Chen, Kaihua Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAttention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a common clinical developmental disorder. Aerobic exercise effectively improves the inhibition function of ADHD children, but its intervention mechanism is not clear yet. This study aimed to explore the brain mechanisms of aerobic exercise improving inhibitory function in children with ADHD.Methods20 school-age children with ADHD were studied. Before and after swimming exercise three times a week for 8 weeks, resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) and Flanker task tests were performed respectively. fMRI analysis focused on the right inferior frontal gyrus (rIFG), and the whole-brain functional connectivity (FC) was calculated based on rIFG. Paired t-test was used to analyze the FC value and the behavioral results of the Flanker task. And the correlations between the FC and the behavioral changes before and after swimming exercise were analyzed.ResultsThis study found that the brain regions with significant differences in rIFG-based FC values included rIFG, right inferior parietal lobule, and cerebellum posterior lobe. The response time after exercise was significantly shorter than that before exercise, while the accuracy after exercise was higher than before. And, the FC changes within rIFG were negatively correlated with the response time changes and positively correlated with the accuracy changes of the Flanker task.ConclusionThis study suggests that the FC within rIFG and the FC between rIFG and right inferior parietal lobule are related to the improvement of inhibition function in ADHD children.Keywords: Aerobic exercise, Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, Inhibition, Functional magnetic resonance imaging, Functional connectivity

Background

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is an early-onset, common childhood developmental disorder characterized by age-inappropriate attention deficits, hyperactivity, and impulsivity. Meta-analysis showed that the total prevalence rate of ADHD in Chinese children was 5.6%, 7.7% in boys, and 3.4% in girls [1]. ADHD is not only a childhood cognitive and behavioral disorder but also persists into adulthood [2], causing a significant burden on children’s school and life. In the treatment of children with ADHD, a large proportion of parents refuse to give their children medication for fear of side effects, and children with mild ADHD can be effectively relieved by non-pharmacological treatment. Therefore, it is essential to find an effective non-pharmacological approach.

Barkley proposed the response inhibition model theory that deficits of response inhibition in ADHD lead to secondary impairments in other executive functions, resulting in symptoms such as attention deficit, hyperactivity, and impulsivity [3]. Inhibition plays an important role in the academic, social-emotional, and self-regulatory development of normally developing children and adolescents [4–6], and impairment of inhibition in children with ADHD can have a greater impact on academic performance than other components of executive function [7, 8] and can lead to personality disorders, antisocial behavior, and substance abuse [9, 10]. Therefore, it is of great significance to improve inhibitory functioning in children with ADHD.

Aerobic exercise is an effective means of improving inhibitory function in children with ADHD. Long-term exercise can effectively improve overall fitness and exert long-lasting effects on cognitive function in children. A meta-analysis collected 314 studies and found that exercise intervention could effectively increase executive function in children and adolescents with ADHD, of which inhibitory control and cognitive elasticity demonstrated the most significant improvements. Long-term moderate-intensity exercise is currently the most recommended model for children with ADHD [11]. It was found that exercise based on open-motor skills improved the inhibitory function of children with ADHD compared to exercise based on closed-motor skills. In open-motor movements children invest more cognitive resources in information processing, and behavioral inhibition such as dominant response inhibition, sustained response inhibition and interference inhibition are actively engaged in the brain during exercise, thus promoting the improvement of inhibitory function in children with ADHD [12]. In addition, children with ADHD can effectively improve their impaired inhibitory function by engaging in moderate to vigorous intensity, open-motor skill-based exercise for 30 min three times a week [13]. Especially, some studies have proved swimming exercise could effectively promote the inhibition of children with ADHD [14, 15]. Although some studies have demonstrated the effects of aerobic exercise on inhibitory function in children with ADHD, the brain mechanisms involved are not yet understood.

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is a well-established technique for studying brain cognition and is widely used in cognitive deficits such as ADHD. fMRI studies have demonstrated the importance of inhibitory control in a number of areas, including prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate gyrus, supplementary motor areas, and anterior insula lobe [16]. Particularly right inferior frontal gyrus (rIFG) plays an important role in inhibitory control [17]. rIFG is directly related to response inhibition, and this region may be a more advanced brain region that regulates response inhibition and is therefore considered to be the core brain region of response inhibition. It has been suggested that rIFG is a node of inhibition signals, involved in the pause and cessation of the action response [18, 19], and also involved in slowing down the action response [20]. Therefore, in this study, we used rIFG of the AAL template as the seed point for the whole-brain functional connectivity (FC), took the classical inhibitory Flanker task for pre-post comparisons, and correlated FC with behavioral outcomes to explore the brain mechanisms of aerobic exercise improving inhibitory function in children with ADHD.

Materials and methods

Participants

Twenty children (11 boys & 9 girls) were all diagnosed from the ADHD Clinic of Changzhou Children’s Hospital of Nantong University, and all participants met the diagnostic criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders version IV. The inclusion criteria were: 6 to 10 years-old (average 8.2 ± 1.3); right-handedness; with an IQ above 80 (Wechsler Intelligence test); without receiving medication for ADHD; without any severe brain trauma; without any mental disorders (autism, schizophrenia) by Kiddie-SADS-present and lifetime.

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Changzhou Children’s Hospital of Nantong University, and written informed consent was obtained from the parents/guardians of all subjects.

Twenty typically developing children (TDC, 10 boys & 10 girls) were included in the study as the control group. They also met the inclusion criteria above. The average age of TDC was 8.1 ± 0.8, and there was no significant difference of age between children with ADHD and TDC (Table 1). The relatively small sample size of this study did indeed lead to limitations in the statistical analysis.

Swimming exercise program

The standard of moderate intensity of aerobic exercise needed children to achieve 60 ~ 69% of the maximum heart rate (220-age). The heart rate was monitored in real-time, and the target heart rate with moderate intensity was measured by Polar A370 fitness tracker [21]. After diagnosis in the ADHD Clinic, their parents were instructed to supervise ADHD children for three times of 30 minutes’ moderate intensity swimming exercises every week for 8 weeks. The heart rate was required to reach a moderate intensity and the training lasted for 30 min. The parent supervised the whole program.

Flanker task

The Flanker task is recognized as a classical paradigm for evaluating inhibitory function and is regularly used in a lot of studies on children [22, 23]. Before the formal test, the children should get the accuracy of more than 85%a in the practice part. A series of English letters appeared on the screen under two conditions: congruent (FFFFF and LLLLL); and incongruent (LLFLL and FFLFF). The children were asked to identify the middle letter by pressing the F or L key as soon as possible. The two conditions were equally and randomly presented. The formal test was composed of two blocks, and each block contained 48 trials, in which the duration of fixation was 500 ms, the duration of letter presentation was 1000 ms, and the stimulation interval was 2000 ms. The shorter response time represented better inhibitory control.

Image acquisition

The fMRI images were acquired by a Siemens 1.5-Tesla Magnetom Avanto scanner. These data were acquired using echo-planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: slice number = 33, repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 40 ms, flip angle = 90°, thickness/gap = 6.0/1.2 mm, field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm, matrix = 64 × 64, 180 volumes (6 min). High-resolution T1-weighted three-dimensional images were acquired sagittally, covering the entire brain using the following parameters: TR = 414 ms, TE = 11 ms, flip angle = 90°, in-plane resolution = 256 × 256, FOV = 240 mm × 240 mm and thickness/gap = 5.0/1.5 mm.

fMRI data analysis

The first ten volumes of fMRI data were discarded to avoid transient signal changes and to allow children to get used to the scanning noises. The data were then preprocessed using a toolbox for Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI) [24]. The preprocessing included the following procedures: (1) slice timing correction; (2) head motion correction; (3) spatial normalization to a standard template (Montreal Neurological Institute; MNI) and re-sampling (3 × 3 × 3 mm3); 5) removal of linear trend; 6) FC calculation seeded on rIFG (MNI coordinates of X = 50, Y = 16, and Z = 22) [25]; 7) spatial smoothing with a Gaussian kernel of 6 mm full width at half maximum was done after FC calculation. The data which had 3.0 mm and 3.0 degree in max head motion will be excluded and no data was excluded in the study.

Statistical analysis

DPABI software was also used for paired t-test analysis of standardized FC brain maps of ADHD children before and after aerobic exercise, and the threshold was set as P < 0.001 (before correction), and only the regions with no less than 20 continuous voxels were considered statistically significant, and the threshold was set as P < 0.05 after Alphasim correction. The results after paired t-test were superimposed on the Ch2 template for viewing. The comparison of response time and accuracy of the Flanker task before and after exercise and the correlations between the FC changes and the response time and accuracy changes were analyzed by SPSS 22.0 software.

One-way ANOVA was used to compare the rIFG-based FC values of the three groups of ADHD children before and after exercise and TDC, and the threshold was set as P < 0.001 (before correction), and only the regions with no less than 20 continuous voxels were considered statistically significant, and the threshold was set as P < 0.05 after Alphasim correction. Post hoc was done at the same time by the multiple comparison of Bonferroni.

The brain regions with significant differences in rIFG-based FC values before and after swimming exercise included right inferior frontal gyrus, right inferior parietal lobule, and cerebellum posterior lobe (after AlphaSim correction, Fig. 1; Table 2). Comparison of the behavioral results of the Flanker task before and after swimming exercise

The response time of the Flanker task was 21.29 ± 5.04ms before exercise and 14.57 ± 4.37ms after exercise. And the accuracy was 0.868 ± 0.066 before exercise and 0.924 ± 0.051 after exercise. There were significant differences of the response times (t=−4.16, P = 0.001) and the accuracy (t = 3.02, P = 0.004) before and after exercise.

Discussion

It has been shown in a particular way that moderate to intense aerobic exercise is a beneficial intervention for the executive functions in children with ADHD. A moderate-intensity exercise session benefits processing speed and inhibitory control in adolescents diagnosed with ADHD [11, 26]. Response inhibition is an important part of executive function and normal children have the ability to control their behavioral inhibition to better adapt to society when there are errors in behavior or changes in the environment. However, children with ADHD have impaired response inhibition and poor self-management skills, resulting in a reduced ability to interrupt or delay their response to target behaviors and a lack of self-control. The inhibition function of children with ADHD could be enhanced by aerobic exercise intervention. Using the rIFG brain region associated with inhibition as a seed point for functional connectivity, it was found that the FC within rIFG and the FC between rIFG and right inferior parietal lobule were increased compared to the pre-intervention period, and the FC between rIFG and cerebellum posterior lobe was decreased compared to the pre-intervention period, and the changes of the FC within rIFG were correlated with the behavioral changes.

Previous studies have shown that the inferior frontal gyrus is a key brain region involved in response inhibition [27–29], and also plays an important role in error monitoring [30]. There are not many studies related to the growth and development of the IFG in response inhibition, but some studies have shown that: during response inhibition tasks, activation of left IFG is enhanced in adults compared to adolescents, while activation of rIFG is enhanced in adolescents compared to adults [31]; and activation of the dorsolateral prefrontal lobe is enhanced in children compared to adults [32]. Regular physical training is associated with increased grey matter volume in the prefrontal cortices, which support a range of executive functions including inhibitory control [33]. For the inferior frontal gyrus, a key brain region for response inhibition, activation is also stronger in the right than in the left. It is clear that rIFG is relatively more important in response inhibition [28, 29]. The present study found that the FC within rIFG of children with ADHD was enhanced by swimming exercise, demonstrating the importance of rIFG in inhibiting cognitive development in children with ADHD. The results of FC and behavioral studies also revealed that FC within rIFG was negatively correlated with the response time and positively correlated with the accuracy of the Flanker task, further suggesting that this brain region is probably the most important brain region influencing inhibition in children with ADHD and swimming exercise improves the inhibitory function of children with ADHD by improving the function of rIFG.

Right inferior parietal lobule is an important brain region for the default mode network (DMN). When there is external stimulation, DMN is in a state of inhibition; when the brain is in a resting waking state, DMN is active [34–36]. Increased DMN activity is associated with poorer inhibitory control, thus physical training’s decrease of DMN may have an indirect benefit on inhibitory control mechanisms [33]. So the right inferior parietal lobule also has a great association with the inhibitory function. Through a certain period of swimming exercise, the FC between rIFG and right inferior parietal lobule was enhanced, suggesting that when children with ADHD need to focus their attention, the inhibitory function of DMN in which the right inferior parietal lobule is located is more pronounced, which is more conducive to the activation of the dorsal attention network that manages attention and enhances attention cognition in children with ADHD. Therefore, the improving of inhibitory function of children with ADHD by swimming exercise is also closely related to right inferior parietal lobule.

Cerebellum has a role in sensory-motor and vestibular control, but also in cognitive, emotional, and autonomic functions. There are important loops between cerebellum and the cerebral cortex, particularly the cerebellum posterior lobe, which has an impact on children’s cognition [37–39]. The present study found a decrease in the FC between rIFG and cerebellum posterior lobe in children with ADHD after swimming exercise, possibly as a compensatory effect of enhanced other connections. This phenomenon may also be attributed to the simultaneous activation of the prefrontal cortex and suppression of cerebellar function during swimming exercise.

The ANOVA analysis showed that the brain regions with significant differences in rIFG-based FC values before and after aerobic exercise and TDC included rIFG and right inferior parietal lobule. The post hoc multiple comparison of Bonferroni showed that the FC within rIFG of ADHD children before exercise was lower than ADHD children after exercise and TDC; the FC of rIFG and right inferior parietal lobule of ADHD children before exercise was lower than ADHD children after exercise. The study found that FC of ADHD before exercise were both lower than ADHD after exercise and TDC, indicating that aerobic exercise actually improved the inhibition function of children with ADHD. Previous studies used the fixed algorithms of the whole brain to compare the brain function changes before and after skipping exercise [40]. This study adopted the swimming exercise mode, added the TDC group for comparison and carried out specific research on the key brain regions of inhibition in methodology. Based on these studies, we will design more targeted aerobic exercise methods to better improve the inhibitory function and increase the observation of clinical symptom improvement of ADHD children.

Conclusions

This study suggests that FC alterations within rIFG and the FC between rIFG and right inferior parietal lobule are related to the improvement of inhibition function in ADHD children, which provides a very good basis for further clinical intervention. The limitation of this study is a relatively small sample size. Further studies with larger sample size are therefore needed to confirm our findings.
