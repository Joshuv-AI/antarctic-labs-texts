Chemotherapy-induced senescent nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells suppress NK cell-mediated antitumor immunity by upregulating EBI3 via mtDNA-cGAS-STING pathway
Authors: Lin Liang, Siyi Liu, Yanling Li, Feng Zeng, Qian He, Zhenxin Wang, Jun Deng, Yanhong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chemotherapy is one of the important means to improve the 5-year survival rate of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) patients, and commonly used chemotherapy drugs include cisplatin and gemcitabine. Previous studies have shown that chemotherapy can induce some tumor cells to exhibit a senescent phenotype, thereby altering the tumor microenvironment towards tumor immune suppression and tumor recurrence. However, the role and detailed mechanism of chemotherapy in the senescence of NPC cells are not yet clear, and further in-depth research is needed. This study focuses on the mechanism of cisplatin-induced cellular senescence, a major form of chemotherapy-induced senescence, in the progression of NPC. Our study found that cisplatin induces tumor cell senescence, upregulates EBI3 expression, and promotes abnormal secretion of IL-35 to mediate tumor immune escape. Specifically, senescent NPC cells release mtDNA due to mitochondrial damage, which in turn activates the cGAS-STING signaling pathway. Activation of this pathway induces the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65, which directly binds to the EBI3 promoter and significantly upregulates EBI3 expression. Subsequently, EBI3 binds to the p35 subunit to form IL-35, which activates the JAK-STAT1 pathway of NK cells through IL-12Rβ2/GP130 receptors, inducing upregulation of the inhibitory receptor NKG2A and thereby weakening the anti-tumor function of NK cells. This study reveals for the first time a novel molecular mechanism by which cisplatin-induced senescent cells, a representative of chemotherapy-induced senescent cells, regulate NK cell anti-tumor function through the EBI3/IL-35 axis, and identifies EBI3 as a key molecular target involved in chemotherapy-induced senescence-mediated immune suppression in NPC, providing important experimental evidence for developing targeted immunotherapy strategies for NPC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02848-6.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) originates from the nasopharynx and exhibits significant differences in histology, epidemiology, molecular pathogenesis, and treatment response compared to other head and neck tumors [1]. In 2020, there were 133,354 new cases of NPC worldwide, with 80,008 deaths. Its etiology is complex, involving genetic susceptibility, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection, and environmental risk factors. NPC is a rare malignant epithelial tumor that is mainly distributed in southern and southeastern China [2]. However, NPC patients are often diagnosed at the locally advanced or metastatic stage, and recurrence and distant metastasis are common causes of death. Current treatment strategies mainly include radiation therapy and platinum-based chemotherapy, and some specific patients may also receive targeted therapy and immunotherapy [3, 4]. Although radiotherapy and chemotherapy have improved the survival rate of locally advanced patients to some extent, approximately 30% of patients still experience recurrence or metastasis [5, 6]. Therefore, in-depth exploration of the potential mechanisms underlying NPC recurrence and metastasis is of great significance for developing new treatment strategies.

Cellular senescence is a stable state of cell cycle arrest that can be triggered by various internal or external stimuli, including telomere shortening, oncogenic and genotoxic stress, hypoxia, and nutrient deficiency [7–9]. Currently, cellular senescence has been listed as one of the hallmarks of cancer [10]. Interestingly, cancer cells also undergo senescence during anti-cancer treatments such as chemotherapy and radiation therapy, a phenomenon known as therapy-induced senescence (TIS) [11, 12]. Although multiple studies have investigated the association between TIS and tumor progression in recent years, there is still a lack of in vivo evidence for the clinical prognostic significance of TIS [13–15]. Research has shown that after undergoing anti-cancer treatment, senescent tumor cells (STC) tend to accumulate in the tumor and produce a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [16, 17]. Increasing evidence suggests that SASP secreted by STC affects the malignant biological behavior of adjacent cells through paracrine signaling, leading to adverse consequences [9, 17–19]. Notably, most current research primarily focuses on the cytokines and chemokines of SASP, while little is known about other cytokines outside of SASP. Importantly, recent studies have shown that senescent cells (SnCs) in tissues upregulate ganglioside GD3, thereby gaining immune privilege and evading the killing of natural killer (NK) cells [20]. However, further clarification is needed on how STC affects immune evasion. Additionally, senescent cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) have a bidirectional effect on tumor progression [21, 22]. Therefore, it is particularly important to explore more precise molecular targets or pathways that mediate the harmful effects of senescent cells, in order to maximize the balance of senescent cells and facilitate anti-tumor therapy.

Epstein-Barr virus inducible gene 3 (EBI3) is a member of the interleukin-12 (IL-12) family and shares structural homology with the IL-12p40 subunit [23]. EBI3 can bind to different subunits to generate various cytokines. Specifically, EBI3 binds to IL23A (p19) to form IL39, to IL12a (p35) to form IL35, and to IL27 (p28) to form IL27 [24, 25]. Among these, IL-27 is secreted by activated antigen-presenting cells and plays an important role in the anti-tumor process. It can promote the function of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [26] and NK cells [27, 28], but studies have also shown that it can inhibit DC function [29, 30]. IL-35 is mainly produced by regulatory T cells or B cells (Treg or Breg) and epithelial-derived tumor cells with pro-tumor properties. It expands Treg cells and inhibits CD4+CD25 effector T cells [31], and can also activate immunosuppressive cells derived from CD11b+GR+ bone marrow in the TME, thereby helping tumor cells achieve immune escape [32]. The latest research has found that the STING-IL-35 axis in B cells can reduce the proliferation of NK cells and weaken NK driven anti-tumor responses [25]. However, the specific mechanism of EBI3 in the microenvironment of NPC is not fully understood, and its importance in regulating NK cells has not been reported in NPC.

In this study, we found that chemotherapy drugs can trigger the senescence of NPC cells both in vivo and in vitro. Compared with non-senescent cells, senescent cells promote the recruitment of NK cells and affect their mediated anti-tumor immunity. Specifically, senescent cells promote tumor progression by suppressing NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Mechanistically, we found that senescence NPC cells trigger mitochondrial damage, releasing mitochondrial mtDNA and activating the cGAS-STING signaling pathway. Activation of this pathway promotes NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation, which binds to the promoter region of EBI3, upregulating the expression of EBI3. Upregulated EBI3 promotes the secretion of IL-35 by binding to the p35 subunit. IL-35 activates the JAK-STAT1 signaling pathway through its receptor IL-12Rβ2-GP130, thereby upregulating the expression of NKG2A and ultimately affecting the function of NK cells. Therefore, EBI3 is expected to become a key molecular target for therapeutic cellular senescence, providing a highly promising strategy for improving the therapeutic effect of NPC.

Results

Cisplatin-induces senescence of nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells

Recent studies have found that DNA damage caused by chemotherapy not only leads to cytotoxic death but also induces primary cells and tumor cells to enter a senescent state [33, 34]. Further research shows that after anti-cancer treatment, STC may gradually accumulate in tumor tissues and have a dual effect of inhibiting or promoting tumor development [35, 36]. To confirm the clinical relevance of cellular senescence in NPC, we collected paired nasopharyngeal tumor tissue samples from 10 NPC patients before and after standard clinical anti-cancer therapy. The clinical and pathological characteristics of the patients are detailed in Table S5. IHC staining results showed that the expression of senescence markers p16 and p21 was significantly increased in post-treatment specimens relative to the pre-treatment group (Fig. 1A and B). This observational evidence indicates that elevated senescence marker expression is present in NPC tissues following clinical anti-tumor treatment.

To evaluate whether chemotherapy drugs can induce senescence of NPC cells in vitro, we treated HK1 and HONE1 with cisplatin (a commonly used drug for NPC treatment). Firstly, we determined the IC50 of cisplatin (CDDP) and found its optimal therapeutic concentration to be 1.0 µg/mL (Supplementary Figure S1A). In the experiment, we observed that after treating NPC cells with 1.0 µg/mL cisplatin for 24 h and stopping treatment, about 80% of NPC cells showed morphological changes of enlargement and flattening on the third day, which is a typical characteristic of cell senescence (Supplementary Figure S1B) [37]. To further determine the inducing effect of senescence, we examined the activity of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal), a widely recognized cellular senescence biomarker. The experimental results of the β-galactosidase staining kit for cellular senescence showed that, compared with NPC cells not treated with cisplatin, the proportion of SA-β-Gal positive cells in NPC cells treated with cisplatin was increased (Fig. 1C and D). Additionally, we used flow cytometry and immunofluorescence experiments to detect the probe Xite™β-D-galactopyranoside, which is used to evaluate cellular senescence status, and evaluated cellular senescence status. The results of flow cytometry showed that, compared with the NPC cells untreated with cisplatin, the fluorescence intensity of Xite™β-D-galactopyranoside in the NPC cells treated with cisplatin increased significantly (Supplementary figure S1C). Correspondingly, the immunofluorescence results also showed that cisplatin significantly increased the fluorescence intensity of Xite™β-D-galactopyranoside in NPC cells (Supplementary figure S1D). Notably, in the group with sequential CDDP-ABT263 treatment, cisplatin did not significantly increase the fluorescence intensity of Xite™β-D-galactopyranoside (Fig. 1E and F). This was attributed to ABT263-induced apoptosis of senescent cells, where membrane disruption of apoptotic cells abrogated β-galactosidase staining; no massive death of normal cells was observed in this treatment window. Additionally, although cisplatin at this concentration significantly inhibited the growth of NPC cells (Supplementary figure S1F, S1G), it did not activate apoptosis. There was no difference in the expression of cleaved PARP and cleaved Caspase-3 between the cisplatin treatment group and the control group (Supplementary figure S1H). Therefore, in subsequent in vitro experiments, unless otherwise specified, the concentration of cisplatin used to induce senescence in both HK1 and HONE1 cells was 1.0 µg/mL. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the chemotherapeutic drug cisplatin can induce senescence in NPC cells in vitro.

Cell cycle arrest is an important characteristic of senescent cells. Therefore, we further investigated the cell cycle distribution of NPC cells treated with cisplatin. The results of flow cytometry analysis showed that approximately 50%-60% of the cisplatin-treated cells were arrested in the G2/M phase (Fig. 1G and H). Since the DNA damage response plays a crucial role in inducing cellular senescence, we evaluated the DNA damage marker γH2AX as well as the senescence-associated p53/p21 pathway. The results of immunoblotting showed that the expression levels of p53, p21, γH2AX, and p16 in NPC cells treated with cisplatin were significantly increased (Fig. 1I). Additionally, RT-qPCR results also showed that the mRNA levels of p53, p21, p16, and p27 were significantly increased in NPC cells treated with cisplatin (Fig. 1J). We also detected the expression of DNA damage marker γH2AX using laser confocal microscopy, and the results showed that compared with the control group, the expression of γH2AX was significantly upregulated in NPC cells treated with cisplatin (Supplementary figure S1I). The senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) is another important characteristic of senescent cells. Therefore, we used RT-qPCR to detect the expression of SASP factors in NPC cells. The results showed that cisplatin treatment led to a significant increase in the mRNA levels of SASP factors (including IL-6, IL-8, CXCL10, TNFα, CCL5, etc.) (Fig. 1K). In summary, these results further confirm that the chemotherapy drug cisplatin can induce the senescence of NPC cells in vitro.

To verify cisplatin-induced cell senescence in vivo, we subcutaneously inoculated HK1 cells into BALB/c-nude mice, and initiated intraperitoneal administration of cisplatin or normal saline on the 7th day post inoculation (once every 3 days, 3 times total) to establish an in vivo cell senescence model (Fig. 1L). To validate this model, we sacrificed the mice on the 21st day post inoculation, collected xenograft tumor tissues, and performed immunohistochemical staining. The results showed markedly increased expression of p16 and p21 in the cisplatin-treated group (Fig. 1M, Supplementary Figure S1J).Correspondingly, the results of tissue RT-qPCR further confirmed that, compared with the tissues of the control group mice, the expression of p53, p21, p16 and p27 in the tissues of mice treated with cisplatin was significantly increased (Fig. 1N). In conclusion, through both in vivo and in vitro experiments, we have confirmed that the chemotherapy drug cisplatin can induce the senescence of NPC cells.

Cisplatin-induced senescence of NPC cells recruits NK cells and inhibits their killing of cancer cells, thereby promoting the progression of NPC cells

To explore the potential mechanism of cisplatin-induced senescence in NPC cells, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on in vitro cultured NPC cells from the non-senescent group (Ctrl) and the senescent group (Se). The results showed that a total of 250 genes were differentially expressed (DESeq2 padj < = 0.05 | log2FoldChange |>=1.0), of which 146 genes were upregulated and 104 genes were downregulated in the senescence group (Se) (Supplementary figure S2A). In the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), we found that senescence NPC cells significantly affect NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, suggesting that senescence may affect the recruitment and function of NK cells (Supplementary figure S2B). Additionally, recent research has found that replicative senescent cells recruit NK cells and inhibit their function, indicating a close relationship between senescent cells and NK cells [20]. However, the relationship between chemotherapy-induced senescent NPC cells and NK cells remains unclear. To explore the effect of senescent NPC cells on NK cell recruitment, we co-cultured the culture supernatants of the non-senescence group (Ctrl) and the senescence group (Se) with NK-92MI cells, and used Transwell migration assay to determine the chemotaxis of NK-92MI cells (Fig. 2A). The results showed that compared with the non-senescence group (Ctrl), the culture supernatant of the senescence group (Se) promoted the chemotaxis of NK-92MI cells (Fig. 2B).

To further explore the effect of cisplatin-induced senescence of NPC cells on the function of NK cells, we first pretreated NK-92MI cells with the conditioned media from non-senescent (Ctrl) and senescent (Se) NPC cells, respectively. After complete removal of the pretreatment media, the pretreated NK-92MI cells were co-cultured with NPC target cells. We then evaluated the effector functions of NK-92MI cells by analyzing the expression of the degranulation marker CD107a, as well as the intracellular levels of TNF-α, GZMB and IFN-γ (Fig. 2C). The results of flow cytometry showed that compared with the non-senescent group (Ctrl), the conditioned medium of the senescent group (Se) inhibited the expression of the degranulation marker CD107a in NK-92MI cells (Fig. 2D and E). Additionally, compared with the non-senescent group (Ctrl), the culture supernatant of the senescent group (Se) significantly inhibited the expression of the intracellular cytokines TNF-α, GZMB and IFN-γ in NK-92MI cells (Fig. 2F and G, Supplementary figure S3A-S3D). Correspondingly, the ELISA assay also showed that compared with the non-senescent group (Ctrl), the culture supernatant of the senescent group (Se) significantly inhibited the secretion of TNF-α, GZMB and IFN-γ in NK-92MI cells (Fig. 2H, Supplementary figure S3E). The results of RT-qPCR also showed similar results (Supplementary figure S3F). These results indicate that cisplatin-induced senescent NPC cells can significantly inhibit the killing function of NK cells. Next, we examined the effect of cisplatin-induced senescent NPC cells on the tumor cell killing mediated by NK cells. We first co-cultured the culture supernatants of the non-senescent group (Ctrl) and the senescent group (Se) with NK-92MI cells, and then co-cultured the pre-treated NK-92MI cells with NPC cells at an effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 5:1 (Fig. 2I). Flow cytometry was used to detect the death of NPC cells. The results showed that compared with the non-senescent group (Ctrl), the culture supernatant of the senescent group (Se) inhibited the killing effect of NK-92MI cells on NPC cells (Fig. 2J and K).

To further investigate whether cisplatin-induced senescence of NPC cells impairs NK cell-mediated anti-tumor activity in vivo, we constructed a GFP-LUC-HK1 stable cell line for in vivo bioluminescent tracing of tumor cells. NKG mice were subcutaneously co-inoculated with GFP-LUC-HK1 cells plus non-senescent Ctrl-HK1 or senescent Se-HK1 cells, and divided into two cohorts with or without tail vein injection of NK-92MI cells (Fig. 2L). Tumor cell growth was assessed by in vivo bioluminescent imaging, and flow cytometry was used to detect the proportion and activation status of NK cells in tumor tissues as well as the death level of GFP-LUC-HK1 cells.

In the cohort without NK-92MI injection, no significant difference in the average luciferase fluorescence intensity of GFP-LUC-HK1 cells was observed between Ctrl-HK1 and Se-HK1 groups. In the cohort with NK-92MI injection, the Se-HK1 group showed a markedly increased average fluorescence intensity of GFP-LUC-HK1 cells compared with the Ctrl-HK1 group (Fig. 2M). Flow cytometry analysis showed consistent results: no notable differences in NK cell proportion, activation marker (CD107a, GZMB) expression or GFP-LUC-HK1 cell death were found between the two groups without NK-92MI injection. In the presence of NK-92MI cells, the Ctrl-HK1 group significantly promoted GFP-LUC-HK1 cell death relative to the Se-HK1 group; meanwhile, the Se-HK1 group had an increased proportion of tumor-infiltrating NK cells with decreased CD107a and GZMB expression, indicating impaired NK cell cytotoxic function (Fig. 2N and P, Supplementary figure S4A, S4B).

These results demonstrate that cisplatin-induced senescence of NPC cells inhibits NK cell-mediated anti-tumor cytotoxicity in an NK cell-dependent manner. Senescent NPC cells impair the killing function of NK cells in the tumor microenvironment, thereby reducing tumor cell death and promoting NPC tumor growth in vivo.

Cisplatin-induced senescence of NPC cells promotes the recruitment of NK cells and inhibits the killing of cancer cells through the expression of EBI3

To identify the genes with differential expression during the process of cellular senescence, we analyzed the results of the RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) described above. The results showed that a total of 250 genes were differentially expressed (DESeq2 padj < = 0.05, |log2FoldChange|>=1.0), among which 146 genes were upregulated and 104 genes were downregulated in the senescent group (Se), as previously stated (Supplementary figure S2A). To find the genes with the most significant and important differential expression, we sorted them according to the log2FoldChange enrichment fold and the GSEA heat map. By intersecting the top 20 genes ranked by the log2FoldChange enrichment fold with the top 20 genes ranked by the GSEA heat map, we identified 8 common genes: SLC38A4, EBI3, CLDN1, INHBA, CLTRN, AOX1, KRT6A, and NUPR1 (Fig. 3A). We then used RT-qPCR to verify the expression of these 8 genes. The results showed that compared with the non-senescent group (Ctrl), the mRNA level of EBI3 in the senescent group (Se) was most significantly upregulated (Fig. 3B). Additionally, we performed RT-qPCR on the xenograft tumor tissues from the cell senescence model induced in mice and the control xenograft tumor tissues. The results showed that compared with the control xenograft tumor tissues, mRNA of EBI3 was significantly upregulated in the senescent xenograft tumor tissues in mice (Fig. 3C). Immunoblotting results also showed that compared with the non-senescent group (Ctrl), the protein level of EBI3 in the senescent group (Se) was significantly upregulated (Fig. 3D). These results indicate that during cisplatin-induced senescence of NPC cells, EBI3 expression is significantly upregulated.

To verify the role of EBI3 in senescent cells, we designed three sgRNAs targeting EBI3 using the CRISPR-Cas9 technology. The results of immunoblotting showed that the knockdown effect of sgRNA-02 was relatively pronounced, so we selected this sgRNA for subsequent experiments (Fig. 3E). HK1/HONE1 cells were first transfected with sgNC/sgEBI3 lentivirus and selected for stable cell lines, then treated/untreated with CDDP for 24 h. The cisplatin-containing medium was subsequently removed, and the cells were cultured for an additional 3 days. The results showed that EBI3 gene knockdown significantly inhibited CDDP-induced senescent cell morphology and markedly reduced the proportion of SA-β-Gal-positive cells (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Figure S5A). Flow cytometry results also indicated that after cisplatin treatment, knockdown of the EBI3 gene (sgEBI3) significantly reduced the fluorescence intensity of Xite™β-D-galactopyranoside (Supplementary figure S5B). Additionally, RT-qPCR results showed that after CDDP treatment, sgEBI3 significantly downregulated the mRNA levels of p21, p16,p27 and p53 (Supplementary figure S5C), while overexpression of EBI3 significantly upregulated the mRNA levels of these genes (Supplementary figure S5D). These results indicate that EBI3 plays an important role in cisplatin-induced cellular senescence.

To further explore whether chemotherapy-induced senescence of NPC cells promotes the recruitment of NK cells and inhibits NK-mediated tumor cell killing through the expression of EBI3, we first used the GSCA database (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA/) to predict the relationship between the expression of EBI3 and the infiltration of NK cells in head and neck tumors. The results showed that in head and neck tumors, the expression of EBI3 was positively correlated with the infiltration of NK cells (Supplementary figure S6A). Then, we co-cultured the culture supernatants of the untreated group (Ctrl), cisplatin-treated wild-type (WT) cells, and cisplatin-treated sgEBI3 cells with NK-92MI cells respectively. The results of flow cytometry showed that after treatment with cisplatin, NPC cells with sgEBI3 restored the expression of the degranulation marker CD107a in NK-92MI cells (Fig. 3G and H, Supplementary figure S6B). Additionally, after treatment with cisplatin, NPC cells with sgEBI3 significantly restored the expression of the intracellular cytokines TNF-α, GZMB, and IFN-γ in NK-92MI cells (Fig. 3I and K, Supplementary figure S6C-S6F). Correspondingly, the results of ELISA and RT-qPCR also confirmed the above findings (Fig. 3L and M, Supplementary figure S6G, S6H). We also examined the effect of sgEBI3 on NK cell-mediated cancer cell killing. We co-cultured the culture supernatants of the untreated group (Ctrl), cisplatin-treated WT cells, and cisplatin-treated sgEBI3 NPC cells with NK-92MI cells, and then co-cultured the pre-treated NK-92MI cells with NPC cells. Flow cytometry was used to detect the death of NPC cells. The results showed that after treatment with cisplatin, NPC cells with sgEBI3 significantly restored the killing effect of NK-92MI cells on NPC cells (Fig. 3N, Supplementary figure S6I). In conclusion, these results further indicate that cisplatin-induced senescence of NPC cells inhibits NK-mediated cancer cell killing through EBI3, thereby promotes the progression of NPC.

Cisplatin-induced cancer cell senescence upregulates EBI3 via the mtDNA-cGAS-STING signaling pathway

To thoroughly elucidate the molecular mechanism of the up-regulated expression of EBI3 during the process of cisplatin-induced senescence of NPC cells, we conducted a series of studies. Our previous results showed that compared with the non-senescent group (Ctrl), the mRNA level of EBI3 in the senescent group (Se) was significantly upregulated, suggesting that senescent cells may regulate EBI3 expression at the transcriptional level. To determine whether senescent cells directly regulate the transcription of EBI3, we constructed a plasmid containing the EBI3 promoter region and verified it using a luciferase reporter gene assay. The results showed that EBI3 transcription was significantly induced and upregulated in senescent NPC cells (Se) compared with non-senescent control cells (Ctrl) (Fig. 4A). Next, we further explored the potential mechanism underlying the upregulation of EBI3 transcription levels. We used two online tools, hTFtarget (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/hTFtarget#!/) and MotifMap (https://motifmap.ics.uci.edu/), to predict the transcription factors that might bind to the EBI3 promoter region. The analysis results showed that both tools predicted that the transcription factor RELA (NF-κB p65) could potentially bind to the EBI3 promoter (Fig. 4B). This finding suggests that NF-κB p65 may regulate the transcription level of EBI3 by directly binding to its promoter. To verify whether NF-κB p65 directly binds to the EBI3 promoter region, we used the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) to predict the binding site of NF-κB p65 on the EBI3 promoter and designed corresponding primers for chromatin immunoprecipitation ChIP-PCR. The results of the ChIP-PCR experiment confirmed that NF-κB p65 can specifically bind to the-1956 bp to -1934 bp site in the EBI3 promoter region (Fig. 4C and D), providing direct evidence for the direct regulation of EBI3 transcription by NF-κB p65.

Since NF-κB p65 needs to be phosphorylated to enter the nucleus and bind to the gene promoter to exert its transcriptional regulatory activity, we detected the phosphorylation level of NF-κB p65 and its nuclear translocation by immunoblotting. The results showed that compared with the cells in the non-senescent group (Ctrl), the phosphorylation level of NF-κB p65 in the cells of the senescent group (Se) was significantly increased(Fig. 4E). Additionally, after separating the cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins of NPC cells, we found that compared with the cells in the non-senescent group (Ctrl), more p-NF-κB p65 was translocated into the nucleus in the cells of the senescent group (Se) (Fig. 4F). Moreover, laser confocal microscopy also observed that compared with the non-senescent group (Ctrl) cells, the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 was more significant in the senescent group (Se) cells (Fig. 4G). To further validate the role of NF-κB p65 in EBI3 transcriptional regulation, we treated cells with the IKK inhibitor BAY 11-7082. The results showed that after treatment with BAY 11-7082, the mRNA level of EBI3 was significantly reduced (Fig. 4H, Supplementary Figure S7A), and its transcriptional activity was correspondingly inhibited (Fig. 4I, Supplementary Figure S7B). These results further confirm the mechanism by which NF-κB p65 regulates EBI3 expression in senescent cells by directly binding to the EBI3 promoter region.

To further explore the detailed mechanism of NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation, we referred to recent research reports indicating that mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is released into the cytoplasm during cellular senescence. This cytoplasmic mtDNA can activate the cGAS-STING pathway [20]. Therefore, we evaluated the enrichment of mtDNA in the cytoplasm. First, we performed immunofluorescence (IF) experiments using the pDsRed2-Mito vector (a mitochondrial tracer vector) and an antibody against mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) [38]. The results showed that compared with the non-senescent group (Ctrl), the cytoplasm of cells in the senescent group (Se) exhibited increased TFAM expression (Fig. 4J). This indicates that mitochondrial DNA may have been released into the cytoplasm. To further verify this, we used RT-qPCR to assess the relative abundance of mitochondrial-encoded ATP synthase 8 (MTATP8, a gene encoded by mtDNA) [39]. The results showed that the level of MTATP8 in the cytoplasm of the senescent group (Se) increased significantly (Fig. 4K), which further confirmed the release of mitochondrial DNA in the cells of the senescent group. Additionally, we conducted RNA-seq transcriptome analysis on both the non-senescent group cells and the senescent group cells, and evaluated the changes in related signaling pathways using GSEA. The results showed that the gene sets related to the NF-κB signaling pathway and the cGAS-STING signaling pathway were significantly enriched in the cells of the senescent group (Se) (Supplementary figure S7C). This result indicates that senescent cells may regulate gene expression by activating these signaling pathways. Immunoblotting experiments further support this conclusion. We found that the expression levels of key proteins in the NF-κB signaling pathway and the cGAS-STING signaling pathway were upregulated in the cells of the senescent group (Se) (Fig. 4L). These results suggest that senescent cells activate the cGAS-STING pathway by releasing mitochondrial DNA, which in turn promotes the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 and upregulates the expression of EBI3.

To further validate the role of the cGAS-STING pathway in the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 and the upregulation of EBI3, we treated non-senescent and senescent NPC cells with the cGAS inhibitor RU.521. Laser confocal microscopy analysis showed that treatment with RU.521 effectively inhibited the cGAS-STING signaling pathway and suppressed the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 (Fig. 4M). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that treatment with RU.521 significantly decreased the phosphorylation level of NF-κB p65 (Fig. 4N). Importantly, treatment with RU.521 also significantly reduced the mRNA level of EBI3 (Fig. 4O) and inhibited the promoter activity of EBI3 (Fig. 4P). These results collectively indicate that senescent cells activate the cGAS-STING pathway through the release of mitochondrial DNA, which in turn promotes the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 and upregulates the expression of EBI3.

To address whether cGAS-STING pathway inhibition (via RU.521) completely blocks cisplatin-induced senescence, we supplemented experiments using our established model: HK1/HONE1 stable cell lines (sgNC/sgEBI3) were treated with CDDP to induce senescence, then incubated with cGAS inhibitor RU.521, and senescence phenotypes were assessed by flow cytometry-based SA-β-Gal (Xite™) detection and RT-qPCR of senescence markers (p53/p21/p16); the results showed that the senescent (Se) group had elevated SA-β-Gal-positive cells and upregulated p53/p21/p16 compared with the control (Ctrl) group, while RU.521 treatment (Se + RU.521) reduced the proportion of SA-β-Gal-positive cells and attenuated the upregulation of p53/p21/p16 but did not restore them to Ctrl levels, confirming that inhibition of the cGAS-STING pathway, which suppresses EBI3, does not completely block cisplatin-induced senescence and only partially alleviates senescence phenotypes, consistent with the effects of EBI3 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 8A,8B).

Cisplatin-induced senescence of NPC cells upregulates EBI3, promotes IL-35 secretion, activates the STAT1-NKG2A axis, and inhibits NK cell anti-tumor immunity

EBI3 is a subunit of the immunosuppressive cytokines IL-27 and IL-35 and belongs to the IL-12 family. EBI3 can combine with different subunits to generate different cytokines: it assembles with IL23A (p19) to form IL39, with IL12a (p35) to form IL35, and with IL27 (p28) to form IL27 [24, 25]. Therefore, in senescent and non-senescent NPC cells, we used RT-qPCR to detect the mRNA levels of IL12a (p35), IL27 (p28), and IL23A (p19). The results showed that in senescent cells, the expression of IL12a (p35) was not affected, the expression of IL23A (p19) was upregulated, while the expression of IL27 (p28) was almost undetectable (Fig. 5A). In senescent NPC cells, the enhanced IL-35 secretion is dependent on the marked upregulation of EBI3 (regulated by the mtDNA-cGAS-STING-NF-κB pathway). Although the mRNA expression of IL12a (p35) shows no significant alteration, its stable basal expression level enables efficient heterodimerization with the upregulated EBI3 to promote the formation and secretion of IL-35, which in turn mediates tumor immune suppression.ELISA results further showed that, compared with the non-senescent group (Ctrl), the secretion of IL-35 in the cell supernatant of the senescent group (Se) was significantly increased (Fig. 5B). Moreover, in both HK-1 and HONE1 cell lines, cisplatin-induced IL-35 secretion was notably reduced by EBI3 knockout (Fig. 5C), confirming that EBI3 is essential for IL-35 upregulation during NPC cell senescence. In contrast, IL-39 secretion showed no significant difference between the Ctrl and Se groups (Supplementary Figure S9A). These results collectively indicate that during NPC cell senescence, upregulated EBI3 binds to IL-12α (p35) to drive IL-35 secretion.This further suggests that during the senescence process of NPC cells, the upregulation of EBI3 binds to IL12a (p35) and then leads to the secretion of IL-35. To explore whether cisplatin-induced senescent NPC cells inhibit NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity by upregulating EBI3 to secrete IL-35, we conducted the following experiments. sgEBI3 NPC cells were treated with cisplatin for 24 h and cultured for another 3 days to obtain senescence-associated conditioned medium (CM). NK-92MI cells were then incubated with this CM with or without recombinant IL-35 (r-IL-35), followed by harvesting the pretreated NK-92MI cells for co-culture with target NPC cells (Fig. 5D). The results showed that, after treatment with cisplatin, compared with the sgEBI3 NPC cells without r-IL-35, the sgEBI3 NPC cells with r-IL-35 inhibited the expression of the degranulation marker CD107a in NK-92MI cells (Fig. 5E). Additionally, the expressions of cytokines TNF-α, GZMB and IFN-γ in NK-92MI cells were also suppressed (Fig. 5F and G, Supplementary figure S9B, S9C). Correspondingly, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that in NK-92MI cells co-cultured with CDDP-treated sgEBI3 NPC cells, supplementation with r-IL-35 significantly downregulated the mRNA expression of TNF-α, IFN-γ and GZMB (Fig. 5H). Consistently, ELISA results confirmed that the secretion of TNF-α, IFN-γ and GZMB in the cell supernatant was also markedly reduced upon r-IL-35 treatment (Fig. 5I). We also examined the effect of EBI3 in senescent cells on NK cell-mediated cancer cell killing via IL-35. The results showed that, compared with the group of sgEBI3 NPC cells without r-IL-35, the sgEBI3 NPC cells with r-IL-35 significantly inhibited the tumor cell killing mediated by NK-92MI cells (Fig. 5J). Specifically, in both HK1 and HONE1 sgEBI3 NPC cell models, supplementation with r-IL-35 resulted in a marked reduction in the proportion of PI-positive dead tumor cells.This result indicates that IL-35 plays an important immunomodulatory role during the interaction between NPC cells and NK cells, potentially affecting the killing ability of NK cells against tumor cells by suppressing their cytotoxic function.

Given that the natural killing activity of NK cells primarily depends on the dynamic balance between activating and inhibitory receptors, and that the expression and activity of these receptors determine the activation state of NK-92MI cells [40], we hypothesized that IL-35 might influence NK cell functions via regulating the expression of these receptors, and this regulatory effect might be mediated by the EBI3-IL35 pathway. To determine whether senescent NPC cells affect the activating and inhibitory receptors of NK-92MI cells through the EBI3-IL35 pathway, we conducted a series of experiments.

First, NK-92MI cells were incubated with conditioned media with conditioned media from non-senescent and senescent NPC cells, respectively. The mRNA levels of seven activating receptors (NKG2D, NKP30, CD226, NKG2C, NKP44, NKP46, and CD244) and six inhibitory receptors (NKG2A, LAG3, TIM3, TIGIT, PVRIG, and CD94) were quantified. The results showed that, compared with NK-92MI cells incubated with conditioned medium from non-senescent NPC cells, those exposed to conditioned medium from senescent NPC cells exhibited significantly downregulated mRNA levels of five activating receptors (CD226, NKG2C, NKP44, NKP46, and CD244) and upregulated mRNA levels of five inhibitory receptors (NKG2A, LAG3, TIM3, TIGIT, and PVRIG) (Supplementary Figure S10A, S10B), completing the preliminary screening of differential receptors.

Furthermore, to screen for receptors regulated in an EBI3-dependent manner, NK-92MI cells were incubated with culture supernatants from the untreated control group (non-senescent NPC cells), cisplatin-induced senescent wild-type (WT) NPC cells, and cisplatin-induced senescent sgEBI3 NPC cells, respectively. RT-qPCR was used to detect the mRNA levels of the differentially expressed receptors identified in the preliminary screening. The results showed that CD226 was the only activating receptor downregulated by EBI3, while NKG2A, LAG3, TIM3, and TIGIT were the inhibitory receptors upregulated by EBI3 (Fig. 6A).

Next, to further confirm the receptors directly regulated by IL-35, we first treated sgEBI3 NPC cells with cisplatin for 24 h, removed the original medium, continued culturing for 3 days, and then collected the cell culture supernatants (with or without the addition of recombinant IL-35, rIL-35). NK-92MI cells were incubated with the above conditioned supernatants, followed by RT-qPCR quantification of mRNA levels for the activating receptor CD226 and inhibitory receptors NKG2A, LAG3, TIM3, CD96 and TIGIT. The results showed that CD226 was the only activating receptor downregulated by IL-35, and NKG2A was the only inhibitory receptor upregulated by IL-35(Fig. 6B). Given that NKG2A is a core inhibitory receptor that plays a dominant role in regulating NK cell cytotoxicity, and our previous findings showed that EBI3 knockdown inhibits NK cell function, we selected NKG2A as the key target for subsequent mechanistic investigations.Additionally, flow cytometry results further confirmed that the expression of NKG2A was co-regulated by IL-35 and EBI3 (Fig. 6C and D). In conclusion, this study found that senescent NPC cells significantly affect the expression of the inhibitory receptor NKG2A on NK cells through the EBI3-IL35 axis, thereby potentially regulating the functions of NK cells.

The receptors for IL-35 include GP130-GP130, IL-12Rβ2-IL-12Rβ2, IL-12Rβ2-GP130, and IL-12Rβ2-IL-27Rα. When IL-35 binds to its receptor, it activates members of the Janus kinase family and promotes the phosphorylation of members of the signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) family, thereby regulating the expression of target genes [32]. Therefore, we hypothesized that IL-35 secreted by senescent cells might affect the transcription of NKG2A through binding to its receptor. To verify this hypothesis, we first determined whether IL-35 activates the IL-35 receptor subunits. We used RT-qPCR to detect the mRNA levels of GP130, IL-12Rβ2, and IL-27Rα. The results showed that, compared with sgEBI3 NPC cells without the addition of r-IL-35, NK92MI cells treated with r-IL-35 upregulated the expressions of GP130 and IL-12Rβ2. This indicates that IL-35 activates the IL-35 receptor subunits (Fig. 6E). Since the binding of IL-35 to its receptor activates the phosphorylation of STAT1 or STAT4, which in turn regulates target genes, we further determined whether STAT1 or STAT4 could regulate NKG2A. We treated the cells with a STAT1 inhibitor and a STAT4 inhibitor, respectively, and found that the expression of NKG2A was restored only when the cells were treated with the STAT1 inhibitor. This indicates that IL-35 binds to its receptor to activate the phosphorylation of STAT1, which then regulates the expression of NKG2A (Fig. 6F and G). Subsequently, we conducted a ChIP assay to investigate the binding of STAT1 to the promoter of NKG2A. The results showed that IL-35 induced the binding of STAT1 to the − 1889 site of NKG2A (Fig. 6H and I). These results indicate that chemotherapy-induced senescence of NPC cells upregulates EBI3, promotes the secretion of IL-35, activates the STAT1-NKG2A signaling axis, and inhibits NK cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity (Fig. 6J).

Discussion

Traditional chemotherapeutic drugs, cisplatin and gemcitabine, are the main treatment regimens for NPC [3, 41, 42]. An increasing number of studies have found that chemotherapeutic drugs can induce tumor cell senescence. Cellular senescence has long been generally regarded as a mechanism for preventing tumorigenesis [40, 43–46]. However, growing evidence suggests that senescent cells can stimulate tumor growth and, in some cases, promote malignancy [47]. Specifically, senescent cells promote tumor progression by remodeling the extracellular matrix, enhancing angiogenesis, and establishing an immunosuppressive TME [18, 48]. However, due to the complexity of cellular senescence, the indiscriminate elimination of senescent cells cannot achieve the desired anti-tumor effect. It is crucial to find more precise targets to achieve more satisfactory anti-cancer effects. In this study, we found that cisplatin treatment-induced senescence of NPC cells upregulates the expression of EBI3, which in turn inhibits the anti-tumor immunity of NK cells. This indicates that EBI3 is a key brake on anti-tumor immunity in the senescent microenvironment of NPC cells.

We found that the expression levels of p16 and p21 were upregulated in tumor tissues of NPC patients who received conventional clinical anti-cancer treatment. In addition, the chemotherapeutic drug cisplatin could induce senescence of NPC cells in vitro, which is consistent with previously reported results [49, 50]. Previous studies have demonstrated that senescent tumor cells promotes T-cell infiltrationinto the TME, thereby converting “cold tumors” into “hot tumors“ [51]. Furthermore, senescent cells exhibit strong immunogenicity and can effectively activate CD8+T cell-mediated anti-tumor immune response [52]. Other evidence suggests that IL-6 secreted by senescent tumor cells modulates tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) by upregulatig CD73 expression via the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway, ultimately suppressing anti-tumor immunity [50]. Recent studies revealed that senescent cells in humans and mice exert potent immunosuppressive functions in vivo, including GD3-dependent pathway-mediated inhibition of NK cell activity against both senescent cells and cancer cells [20]. However, the role of chemotherapy-induced senescence of NPC cells on anti-tumor immunity remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that cisplatin-induced senescence NPC cells recruit NK cells while simultaneously suppressing their anti-tumor function. Our findings bridge the gap in understanding the interplay between NPC cell senescence and NK cell activity within the TME.

The senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) is the most prominent feature of senescent cells and exerts pro-tumorigenic effect on surrounding cancer cells via a non-cell-autonomous manner [21, 53–55]. Indeed, we observed that cisplatin-induced senescent NPC cells exhibit enhanced SASP secretion. Notably, we identified upregulated expression of Epstein-Barr virus-induced gene 3 (EBI3) during NPC cell senescence. EBI3 is a glycoprotein and a structural subunit homolog of interleukin 12 (IL-12), sharing homology with the IL-12p40 subunit [56]. EBI3 can dimerize with distinct subunits to form cytokines: it combines with IL12a (p35) to form IL-35 [57], with IL27 (p28) to form IL-27 [58], and wth IL23A (p19) to form IL-39 [59]. In this study, senescent NPC cells showed unaltered IL-12α (p35) expression, upregulated IL-23 A (p19), and negligible IL-27 (p28) levels compared to non-senescent controls. ELISA revealed elevated IL-35 secretion in senescent (Se) cell supernatants relative to non-senescent (Ctrl) groups, whereas IL-39 secretion remained unchanged. Intriguingly, despite IL-23 A (p19) upregulation in senescent cells, IL-39 secretion was unaffected, suggesting alternative regulatory mechanisms. Collectively, our data demonstrate that NPC cell senescence upregulates EBI3, which binds IL-12α (p35) to promote IL-35 secretion. IL-35 is an immunosuppressive cytokine that modulates dysfunctional T cells in immune disorders, activates myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and regulates immune-related inflammatory factors [32]. Here, we show that senescent cell-derived IL-35 activates the JAK-STAT1 pathway via IL-35 receptors (GP130 and IL-12Rβ2) on NK cells, upregulating the inhibitory receptor NKG2A and thereby suppressing NK cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity. This mechanism differs from recent reports that the STING-IL-35 axis in B cells impairs NK proliferation and anti-tumor responses [60], highlighting the direct role of tumor cell senescence in IL-35-driven NK suppression. Furthermore, our study reveals that senescent tumor cells can secrete IL-35 (via upregulating EBI3 and p35), which represents a previously underappreciated component of the senescent secretome with potent NK cell-suppressive activity.

Mechanistically, we utilized the hTFtarget and MotifMap databases to predict transcription factors (TFs) binding the EBI3 promoter region, identifying NF-κB as the sole TF co-regulating EBI3 transcription. Further investigations revealed that senescent cells activate the cGAS-STING signaling pathway via mtDNA release, driving NF-κB nuclear translocation and subsequent EBI3 transcriptional upregulation. Mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of cellular senescence [20, 61, 62]. Recent studies demonstrate that cytosolic mtDNA in senescent cells activates cGAS-STING to exacerbate senescence and neurodegeneration [63]. Notably, minor mitochondrial outer membrane permeability (miMOMP) a BAX/BAK-dependent process distinct from apoptosis induces low-level mtDNA leakage, triggering cGAS-STING-mediated SASP expression without cell death [20]. Our findings align with these reports but reveal a novel downstream effector: NF-κB-dependent EBI3/IL-35 axis. Critically, we demonstrate that cGAS-STING activation in senescent NPC cells specifically promotes NF-κB nuclear translocation, which orchestrates EBI3 transcription, enhances IL-35 secretion, and ultimately suppresses NK cell anti-tumor immunity. This extends beyond prior links between cGAS-STING and SASP, establishing a direct mechanistic bridge to immune evasion via IL-35.

Notably, this study has several limitations. (1) In assessing cellular senescence in NPC patient tissues, we were restricted to evaluating p16 and p21 expression in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples, without measuring SA-β-Gal activity, the gold-standard senescence marker. (2) The EBI3 knockdown model was established as a mixed stable cell pool via puromycin selection of lentivirus-transfected cells, with three independent sgRNAs achieving significant partial knockdown instead of complete knockout of EBI3. Although monoclonal cell lines combined with TIDE analysis could generate a more precise gene editing profile, this technical optimization was not performed in the present study and will be addressed in subsequent research. Notably, partial EBI3 knockdown almost completely reversed the SA-β-Gal-positive senescent phenotype. We speculate this is because EBI3 acts as a threshold regulator or key effector node in the cisplatin-induced senescence signaling pathway with a non-linear dose-dependent function; when its expression drops below a critical threshold, the pro-senescent program cannot be effectively initiated or sustained, leading to a marked phenotypic rescue effect. (3) This study focuses on the molecular mechanism underlying the regulation of NK cell anti-tumor function by cisplatin-induced senescent NPC cells, clarifying the core role of the mtDNA-cGAS-STING-NF-κB-EBI3/IL-35 signaling axis. However, this mechanism has only been validated in the cisplatin-induced tumor senescence model, and its applicability to senescence induced by other chemotherapeutic drugs or radiotherapy remains unexplored. Furthermore, the in vivo experiments employed a co-implantation model using ex vivo cisplatin-pretreated senescent NPC cells, which allowed specific interrogation of the intrinsic functions of cisplatin-induced senescent NPC cells while excluding confounding factors associated with systemic cisplatin administration, such as direct cytotoxicity, systemic immune dysregulation, and multi-organ toxicity. While this approach provides a well-controlled experimental system, it does not fully recapitulate the complex in vivo dynamics of chemotherapy-induced senescence, and future studies employing systemic chemotherapy models will be valuable for further validation. (4)This study mainly investigated how senescent NPC cells regulate the function of tumor microenvironment-infiltrated NK cells via the EBI3/IL-35 axis, but did not explore the key factors mediating NK cell recruitment to tumor sites after treatment. The observed increased NK cell infiltration is presumed to be associated with the intrinsic immunomodulatory effects of chemotherapeutic agents and inflammatory mediators released by senescent tumor cells, while the specific cytokines/chemokines responsible for this recruitment remain uncharacterized. (5) Additionally, due to the lack of NPC-specific immune infiltration datasets in public databases, the correlation between EBI3 expression and NK cell infiltration was preliminarily assessed using the HNSC cohort from the GSCA database as an exploratory step; this association was further directly validated through a series of NPC-specific functional experiments and animal models in the present study.(6) Due to experimental resource constraints, all NK cell functional assays were performed using the classic NK-92MI cell line, a standard model in tumor immunology research, without validating the findings in primary NK cells isolated from human peripheral blood.

In summary, this study provides the first evidence that senescent NPC cells recruit NK cells yet suppress their anti-tumor immune function. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that senescent NPC cells induce mitochondrial damage, leading to mtDNA release and subsequent activation of the cGAS-STING-NF-κB axis. Nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 upregulates EBI3 expression by binding to its promoter. Elevated EBI3 dimerizes with the p35 subunit to enhance IL-35 secretion, which then activates the JAK-STAT1 pathway via the IL-12Rβ2-GP130 receptor complex on NK cells. This cascade upregulates the inhibitory receptor NKG2A, impairing NK cell activity (Fig. 7). This positions EBI3 inhibitors or monoclonal antibodies as promising therapeutic agents, offering a novel and targeted strategy for NPC treatment.

Experimental section

Cell lines and cell culture

The human NPC cell line HONE1 was preserved by the Cancer Research Institute of Central South University (Hunan), while the human NPC cell line HK1 was donated by Professor Hong Yi from Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. Human NK92MI cells were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Technology Co., Ltd. The NPC cell lines HONE1 and HK1 were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Procell, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/ml penicillin streptomycin. NK92MI cells were cultured using NK-92MI cell specific culture medium (Procell, China). All cells were cultured at 37 ℃ and 5% CO₂, and regular mycoplasma testing was conducted to ensure no mycoplasma contamination.

Reagents and antibodies

Cisplatin (CDDP) (HY-17394), ABT263 (HY-10087), Cyclophosphamide (HY-17420), and IL-35 Protein, Human (HY-P70338) were purchased from MCE, USA. Xite™ β-D-galactopyranoside (14030) was purchased from AAT Bioquest, USA. IKK inhibitor (BAY11-7082) (T1902), cGAS inhibitor (RU.521) (T5486), STAT1 inhibitor (Fludarabine) (T1038), and STAT4 inhibitor ((R)-Lisofylline)(T21694) were purchased from TargetMol, China. D-Luciferin potassium salt (ST196) was purchased from Beyotime, China. CFSE (C34554) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA. The antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Induction of senescent cells

Unless otherwise stated, senescent cells were treated with 1 µg/ml cisplatin for 24 h, as previously reported [50]. The treatment was terminated after 24 h, and cellular senescence was evaluated on the third day.

Conditioned medium (CM)

To obtain CM, NPC cells that had been induced with CDDP for 24 h were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS for two days. On the third day, the medium was replaced with a culture medium specifically designed for NK-92MI cells. After 24-hour incubation, the serum-free medium was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (MilliporeSigma). For IL-35 treatment, 100 ng/mL of IL-35 was added to the CM, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 16 h. The CM was then used for cell culture. For treatment with STAT1 inhibitor/STAT4 inhibitor, 10 µM of STAT1 inhibitor and 10 µM of STAT4 inhibitor were added to the CM, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h.The CM was subsequently used for cell culture.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) Staining

As previously reported [19, 50, 64, 65], fresh frozen sections of tumor tissues or the indicated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio) for 15 min, and then stained with a Senescence β-galactosidase Staining Kit (C0602, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Microscopy was used to capture images for analyzing the percentage of SA-β-Gal positive cells.

Green β-D-galactopyranoside Fluorescence Staining

Cells were seeded and cultured to desired confluency for subsequent experimental treatments. For staining, the culture medium was removed, and Xite™ Green β-D-galactopyranoside working solution (20 µM) was added to cell plates. Plates were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ in the dark for 15–45 min, then washed twice with pre-cooled PBS. For fluorescence microscopy: stained cells were directly imaged with an FITC filter set. For flow cytometry: cells were trypsinized, centrifuged (1000 × g, 5 min) to collect pellets, resuspended in PBS as a single-cell suspension, and fluorescence intensity was detected via flow cytometry with a 530/30 nm filter (FITC channel); data were analyzed with FlowJo.

Infection

According to the manufacturer’s recommendation, lentiCRISPR v2 (EBI3) was transfected into cells by using Neofect DNA transfection reagent (Neofect Biotech, China). Three days later, successfully transfected cells were selected using puromycin. Subsequently, the efficiency of sgEBI3 was evaluated by immunoblotting. The sgEBI3 sequence used in this study is listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Generation of lentiviruses and infections

The lentivirus was transfected into HEK 293T cells using pMDG.2, psPAX2, and lentiCRISPR v2 (EBI3) to generate a large number of lentiviruses carrying sgEBI3. The virus was collected at 48, 72, and 96 h after transfection. Cell debris was removed through centrifugation and filtration. The supernatant containing the virus was then stored fresh or as frozen aliquots supplemented with 8 µg/mL polybrene (C0351, Beyotime) on dry ice at -80 °C. Receptor cells were incubated with lentivirus for 8 h and then screened with puromycin (ST551, Beyotime) after 3 days.

CCK-8 assay

The CCK-8 assay (C0005, TargetMol) was used to detect cell viability. Briefly, 5.0 × 10³ NPC cells were inoculated into a 96-well plate. At the designated time point, 10 µl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 ℃ in the dark for 2 h. The absorbance was then measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Colony formation assay

As previously reported [66], NPC cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10³ cells per well in a 6-well plate and treated with or without CDDP at the indicated concentration for 24 h, followed by two weeks of further culture. After washing with PBS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio) at room temperature for 30 min and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Biyun Tian, Shanghai, China) for 15 min. The number of cell colonies was counted using Image J software.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Measure the concentrations of IL-35, IL-39, IFN-γ, GZMB, and TNF-α in the supernatant of the culture medium using ELISA kits (ELK Biotechnology, ELK2745; ELK Biotechnology, ELK9171; ELK Biotechnology, ELK1036; ELK Biotechnology, ELK1658; ELK Biotechnology, ELK1190) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells using a total RNA extraction reagent (M5101, NCM Biotech). Complementary DNA was reverse-transcribed using HiScript III RT SuperMix(+ gDNA wiper) (#R323,Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RT-qPCR reaction was performed using AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) in a Bio-Rad CFX96 qPCR instrument. GAPDH was used as an internal reference. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Tables 3 and were synthesized by Changsha Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP analysis was performed using a ChIP assay kit (P2083S, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, NPC cells were cross-linked with 37% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min, followed by sonication to shear the chromatin. Then, immunoprecipitation was carried out overnight with the NF-κB antibody pre-bound to Protein G Dynabeads. The beads were washed, and the eluted DNA/protein complex was treated with 20 µg of proteinase K at 55 °C for 2 h. Cross-linking was reversed at 65 °C overnight. The purified DNA was digested with RNase A and then quantified by RT-qPCR using the specific primers listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Dual luciferase reporter gene assay

NPC cells were transfected with a firefly luciferase reporter plasmid and a control vector containing the Renilla luciferase gene (pRLTK). The Renilla luciferase construct served as an internal control, and the firefly luciferase values were standardized based on this. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the firefly and Renilla signals were quantified using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (DL101, Vazyme).

RNA sequencing and gene enrichment analysis

Senescent and control HK1 cells were collected and sent to Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd. for RNA sequencing analysis. The P-values were calculated by edgeR (v3.24). The false discovery rate (FDR) was obtained through multiple testing and adjustment of the P-values. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with padj ≤ 0.05 and |log2FoldChange|≥1.0. GSEA was performed using the fgsea package.

Immunoblotting

Immunoblotting analysis was performed as previously described [66, 67]. Briefly, proteins were extracted from NPC cell lines, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). The samples were then mixed with SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis sample loading buffer (P0015, Beyotime) and denatured at 100 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, the proteins were separated on an SDS-polyacrylamide gel and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. The membrane was then incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Chemiluminescence of the blots was detected using NcmECL Ultra (P10100, NCM Biotech). The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescence staining

If mitochondrial staining is required, pDsRed-Mito should be transfected into the cells. The cells are then placed on glass coverslips. Wash the cells twice with PBS, then fix them with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS at room temperature for 15 min, and permeabilize them with 0.25% Triton-X100 in PBS at room temperature for 20 min. Incubate the cells overnight at 4 °C with antibodies diluted in PBS (1:100). The next day, wash the cells three times with PBS, add the corresponding secondary antibodies to the samples, and incubate at room temperature for 30 min. Stain the cell nuclei with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images are acquired using an LSM980 confocal laser-scanning microscope. The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunohistochemical staining

Prepare paraffin-embedded tumor samples and sections according to the previously described method [68]. In brief, tissue slices were boiled with citrate buffer for antigen repair. The tissue slices were then blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h and incubated overnight with the primary antibody at 4 °C. The following day, the tissue slices were incubated with the secondary antibody at 37 °C for 30 min. The K-Viewer was used for analysis. The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The expression of EBI3, p16, and p21 was detected using the Vectastain Elite ABC-HRP kit.

Quantification of mtDNA release

Cells were resuspended in 170 µl of digitonin lysis buffer composed of 150 mM NaCl (Bio Sharp, BL542A), 50 mM HEPES (Beyotime, C0217), and 25 µg/ml digitonin (Beyotime, ST1272). After incubation and centrifugation, the supernatant containing cytosolic mtDNA was diluted at a ratio of 1:15 for subsequent qPCR amplification. The corresponding cell pellet was subjected to total DNA extraction using the TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (TIANGEN Biotech). The relative level of cytosolic mtDNA was then normalized to the total mtDNA content obtained from the pellet for final quantification.

Subcellular component separation

Follow the manufacturer’s instructions (P0028, Beyotime) to collect and wash the cells with PBS. Add 20 µL of precipitate to 200 µL of cytoplasmic extraction reagent A containing PMSF. After a series of operations, centrifuge the mixture to obtain cytoplasmic protein. Add 50 µL of nuclear extraction reagent containing PMSF to the precipitate, and centrifuge again to obtain the nuclear protein in the supernatant.

NK cell killing assays

Collect the cell-conditioned medium and co-culture it with NK92MI cells. After co-culturing for 6 h, the NK92MI cells are then co-cultured with NPC cells labeled with CFSE for another 4 h. Use flow cytometry to analyze CFSE+PI+ positive cells.

Transwell migration assays

Suspend NK92MI cells in NK-92MI cell-specific culture medium at a concentration of 2 × 105 cells per milliliter. In a Transwell plate (transparent polycarbonate membrane Transwell insert, diameter 6.5 mm, porosity 3 μm; Corning, 003415), 600 µl of conditioned medium from senescent or non-senescent cells was added to the bottom of the well, and 100 µl of cell suspension was added to the insert. The cells migrated at 37 °C for 4 h under 5% CO2. After incubation, cell counting was performed to evaluate the recruitment rate of NK92MI cells.

Flow cytometry

Evaluate the degranulation of NK cells by detecting the expression of CD107a on the cell surface. Briefly, NK cells were co-incubated with target cells in a 1:1 ratio for 1 h in the presence of anti-human CD107a antibody (328607, BioLegend). Then, Cell Activation Cocktail (with Brefeldin A) (423303, BioLegend) was added at a dilution of 1:1500, and the incubation was continued for 4 h. The cells were then collected and stained with surface anti-CD45 antibody (982304, BioLegend) for analysis by flow cytometry.

To detect intracellular tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and granzyme B (GZMB), co-cultured NK cells were stained with anti-CD45 before fixation and permeabilization, followed by staining with anti-IFN-γ (502526, BD Pharmingen), anti-TNF-α (554513, BD Pharmingen), and anti-GZMB (372206, BD Pharmingen) for analysis.

Collect NK92MI cells co-cultured with tumor cell conditioned medium or NK92MI cells with/without r-IL-35 added, stain with anti-NKG2A (375113, BioLegend), and evaluate the expression of NKG2A on the surface of NK-92MI cells. Stained cells were analyzed using FACSCelesta flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software v10.1 (FlowJo, RRID: SCR_008520).

Mouse tumor model and efficacy evaluation

BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old) were purchased from Guangdong Weitong Lihua Experimental Animal Center. NKG mouse (6-week-old) were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Inc., China. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Central South University.

Cisplatin-induced senescenc model: 100 µL PBS containing 5 × 106 HK1 cells was injected subcutaneously on the right flank of BALB/c nude mice. When the tumor volume reached 50 mm³, treatment was initiated with intraperitoneal injections of 5 mg/kg cisplatin, administered a total of four times. Tumor size was measured every 3 days. After 21 days, the mice were euthanized.

NKG mouse model: This study first established a stable cell line by transducing human nasopharyngeal carcinoma HK1 cells with a lentiviral vector co-expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) and luciferase (LUC), followed by two weeks of selection with 2 µg/mL puromycin to obtain a monoclonal GFP-LUC-HK1 cell pool. Subsequently, 6‑ to 8‑week‑old female NKG mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5 per group): the Ctrl‑HK1 group (co‑inoculated subcutaneously with 5 × 10⁵ GFP‑LUC‑HK1 cells plus an equal number of untreated control HK1 cells); the Se‑HK1 group (co‑inoculated with the same number of GFP‑LUC‑HK1 cells and cisplatin‑induced senescent HK1 cells); and two corresponding NK‑cell treatment groups (using the same inoculation schemes, with intravenous injection of 5 × 10⁶ NK‑92MI cells on day 7 and day 14 after inoculation). All cells were resuspended in 100 µL of a 1:1 mixture of Matrigel and serum‑free medium and injected subcutaneously into the right dorsal flank of each mouse. On day 21, D-fluorescein potassium salt solution (ST196, Beyotime) was administered intraperitoneally at a dosage of 10 µL per gram of body weight. After 10–20 min, when the fluorescence signal reached its strongest and stable state. The average luciferase signal intensity within the region of interest was quantified using Living Image software (units: photons/sec/cm²/sr).

Clinical specimens

NPC tissue specimens were obtained from Xiangya Medical College Affiliated Cancer Hospital/Hunan Provincial Cancer Hospital of Central South University. This study analyzed specimens from a total of 10 NPC patients, including specimens before and after treatment. All samples were collected with the informed consent of patients, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Medical College Affiliated Cancer Hospital/Hunan Provincial Cancer Hospital of Central South University, in accordance with the ethical standards set forth in the Helsinki Declaration. Clinical variables such as age, race, and disease stage were not controlled. The patient characteristics are shown in Supplementary Table 5.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used to determine significant differences. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was performed. All experiments were conducted with at least three replicates, and data are presented as mean ± SD.
