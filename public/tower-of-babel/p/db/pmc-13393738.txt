Delayed repair of singly transected and segmental-loss peripheral nerve injuries in rat sciatic nerves using optimized PEG-fusion protocols and stored viable peripheral nerve allografts
Authors: Alexa N Olivarez, Cathy Z Yang, Liwen Zhou, Michelle Mikesh, Cameron L Ghergherehchi, Henry A Garcia, Alexander M Schafer, Guhan Periyasamy, Phuong Vy Nguyen, Yiming Hao, Arjun Agarwal, Varun Gokhale, Rhea Sood, Neha Nagarajan, Sushant Sanklipur, George D Bittner

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSingly transected and segmental-loss peripheral nerve injuries (ST- and SL-PNIs) produce immediate loss of sensorimotor functions. Polyethylene glycol (PEG)-fusion repairs performed immediately following ST- and SL-PNIs improve long-term outcomes in animal models, with SL-PNIs repaired using fresh or 1-to-3 day stored viable peripheral nerve allografts (VPNAs). Here, we propose several optimizations for the existing PEG-fusion protocols to repair ST- and SL-PNIs with a delay of 24–48 h post-injury, using longer stored VPNAs for SL-PNIs, both conditions critical for translating PEG-fusion to the clinic.MethodsOptimized PEG-fusion protocols to repair ST- and SL-PNIs of the rat sciatic nerve include the additional steps of topical application of low-dose epinephrine and/or local administration of methylprednisolone. Electrophysiological recordings were used to assess the success of surgeries, and Sciatic Functional Index and end-point nerve morphology were performed to assess long-term recovery. For longer VPNA storage, a static storage protocol was established, and the viability of VPNAs was assessed electrophysiologically prior to transplantation and morphologically.ResultsTogether, our optimized PEG-fusion and static storage protocols successfully repair ST- and SL-PNIs with a delay of at least 24 h, with SL-PNIs repaired using VPNAs stored for at least 3 weeks. Topical application of low-dose epinephrine stops intraoperative bleeding and improves behavioral recovery. Local administration of methylprednisolone following PEG-fusion eliminates microvasculitis and further improves behavioral recovery.ConclusionsThe findings in this study using rats, along with the ongoing PEG-fusion clinical trials, suggest the feasibility of clinically translating delayed PEG-fusion repair for ST- or SL-PNIs in humans, and support further investigation into the storage of VPNAs at tissue banks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08573-5.Keywords: Sciatic nerve ablation, Polyethylene glycol, Graft storage, Static cold storage, Sciatic functional index, Epinephrine, Methylprednisolone

Introduction

Singly transected and segmental-loss peripheral nerve injuries (ST- and SL-PNIs) produce immediate loss of sensorimotor functions in animal models and humans [1–5]. The current clinical approach to repair such injuries uses neurorrhaphy to directly microsuture cut ends for ST-PNIs or to bridge the gap for SL-PNIs using autografts, allografts, or synthetic materials [6–8]. In principle, PNI repairs could be performed within 48 h, similar to allograft repairs using organs or other tissues. However, in current clinical practice, PNI repairs are typically delayed for weeks, in part because the distal segment inevitably undergoes rapid Wallerian degeneration (WD) within a few days, and functional recovery depends on slowly regenerating (1–2 mm/day) proximal axons [9–11]. These regenerating axons often take months-to-years to reinnervate target tissues that may have irreversibly atrophied within weeks to months. These conditions often produce poor-to-none behavioral recovery, especially for SL-PNIs, both experimentally and clinically [12, 13].

In contrast, polyethylene glycol (PEG)-fusion repair of ST- and SL-PNIs, with SL-PNIs bridged with viable peripheral nerve allografts (VPNAs), produces significantly better behavioral outcomes in rat sciatic PNI models as assessed by the Sciatic Functional Index (SFI) and von Frey assays [14–19]. PEG-fusion uses a series of well-specified bio-engineered solutions, one of which is 50% w/w 3.35 kDa PEG that non-specifically fuses closely apposed, open, viable donor and host axons. About 40–60% of axons in a sciatic nerve successfully achieve fusion within seconds to minutes that restores their cytoplasmic and electrophysiological continuity across SL-PNIs and prevents their WD. These PEG-fused axons immediately and non-specifically reinnervate many distal sensorimotor targets and produce faster and better behavioral recovery within weeks compared to Negative Controls (NCs) that solely rely on regeneration by outgrowth from surviving proximal axons.

However, existing experimental PEG-fusion protocols have been largely limited to immediate repairs of ST- and SL-PNIs, with SL-PNIs using fresh or short-term-stored VPNAs [14–20]. No study has examined pharmacological or surgical modifications of PEG-fusion protocols to improve outcomes following delayed interventions [21, 22] and/or the use of long-term-stored VPNAs, both conditions critical for translating PEG-fusion to the clinic.

In this study, we hypothesized that: (1) PEG-fusion can successfully repair ST- and SL-PNIs following delays of at least 24–48 h post-injury; (2) ex vivo viability of axons in VPNAs can be greatly extended to at least 9 weeks using a static cold storage protocol, compared to the previously reported 9-day time using a daily replacement protocol [23]; and (3) PEG-fusion using statically stored VPNAs can successfully repair SL-PNIs with and without delays.

Our data on rat sciatic nerve ST- and SL-PNIs reported herein are consistent with our hypotheses. Furthermore, we report optimizations of PEG-fusion protocols that improve repair outcomes. Specifically, we show that topical application of low-dose epinephrine during surgical procedures greatly reduces intraoperative blood accumulation [24–27] and improves behavioral recovery. Additionally, local administration of methylprednisolone (MP)—a corticosteroid that we find promotes Schwann cell proliferation and remyelination and decreases inflammatory infiltration as previously reported [28–30]—eliminates microvasculitis and further improves behavioral recovery. In summary, our findings suggest that our improved PEG-fusion protocol may make PEG-fusion more clinically feasible and significantly improve clinical outcomes following SL-PNIs using delayed PEG-fusion of statically stored VPNAs.

Results

Topical epinephrine application during surgery improves behavioral recovery following delayed PEG-fusion repair of ST-PNIs in Sprague Dawley (SD) rats

SD rats underwent ST-PNIs and received PEG-fusion repairs between 24 and 48 h post-injury (Fig. 1). PEG-fused and NC groups repaired immediately (0-hour) following ST-PNIs served as controls. All delayed repair groups had much more intraneural bleeding (Supplementary Video 1) compared to the 0-hour (non-delayed) control groups. Bleeding could not be stopped via compression which could damage the nerve. This excessive bleeding impaired visualization and added extracellular calcium to the site of PEG-fusion. Extracellular calcium can prematurely seal axons within minutes [31] and prevent proper PEG-fusion [32].

We confirmed that irrigating the surgical site with epinephrine in diluted Normosol-R (dNR) as needed throughout the surgery produced more rapid vasoconstriction and hemostasis [33] than dNR without epinephrine (Supplementary Video 1). Epinephrine application did not affect PEG-fusion of axons, as confirmed by the rapid (within 1–2 min) restoration of in vivo compound action potential (CAP) conduction across the lesion site in all PEG-fused groups (Fig. 1A). Figure 1B shows 24-hour delayed PEG-fusion repairs with and without local application of epinephrine performed by the same surgeon. PEG + Epi group had significantly improved overall SFI scores compared to the 0-hour NC group (p < 0.0001, Mixed-effects) starting at 4 weeks post-operatively (PO) (p < 0.05, Tukey’s); no difference was found between the PEG without epinephrine group and the 0-hour NC group. Similar trends were found for the 24-hour and 48-hour delayed PEG-fused repair groups with and without epinephrine (data not shown).

Figure 1C summarizes the SFI recovery of all delayed PEG-fused groups, including both repairs performed with and without epinephrine. Immediate (0-hour) and delayed PEG-fused groups all exhibited significantly better overall SFI scores compared to the 0-hour NC group (p < 0.01, Mixed effects; Fig. 1C). We have previously defined successful SFI recovery following immediate repairs of ST-PNIs by whether or not a rat reached and maintained an SFI score better than − 69 (three standard deviations above the NC average; [16]). We confirmed that the 0-hour NC rats rarely have successful SFI recovery in this study, with only one rat recovering to an SFI score of -68.6. The successful SFI recovery rates for PEG-fused groups were 80%, 69%, 75%, and 78% for 0-, 24-, 36-, and 48-hour delays, respectively, and these rates were all significantly higher (p < 0.0001, Chi-square) compared to that rate for the 0-hour NC group (4.3%) following ST-PNIs.

Behavioral recovery following delayed PEG-fusion repair with epinephrine of SL-PNIs in Lewis-host/SD-donor rats

We performed all SL-PNI repairs in this study exclusively with Lewis host rats to eliminate premature euthanasia due to self-mutilation that often occurred for host SD rats described in previous studies [17, 18, 34]. We observed more bleeding in Lewis rats than SD rats following SL-PNIs, but this bleeding was well managed by local application of epinephrine described in the previous section.

Lewis host rats received SL-PNIs and were repaired 24 h post-injury (Fig. 2). PEG-fused and NC groups repaired at 0-hour following SL-PNIs served as controls (note that these controls were different from those of Fig. 1). All rats were repaired using fresh donor VPNAs from SD rats. In vivo CAP conduction was restored immediately in all PEG-fused rats but not in NC rats (Fig. 2A). 0-hour PEG-fused rats exhibited significantly better overall SFI scores compared to 0-hour NC rats (p < 0.001, Mixed effects; Fig. 2B), consistent with previous reports [18, 35].

The 24-hour-delay PEG-fused group had significantly higher (p < 0.05, Tukey’s; Fig. 2B) SFI scores than the 0-hour NC group at 6 weeks PO but exhibited a decline in recovery of SFI scores starting at 7 weeks PO. To better describe this decline, 24-hour-delay PEG-fused rats were divided into three subgroups based on their SFI recovery (Fig. 2C): (1) Successful PEG subgroup: animals that recovered and maintained an SFI score of at least − 79 (three standard deviations above the NC average); (2) Poor PEG subgroup: animals that never recovered to an SFI score of -79; and, (3) Partially Effective PEG subgroup: animals that temporarily recovered to -79 but failed to maintain that recovery. Our analysis showed that 38% of 24-hour-delay PEG-fused rats could be classified as Successful PEG, 31% as Partially Effective PEG, and 31% as Poor PEG. The 0-hour NC group was not divided because no rat recovered to an SFI score of -79 or better. The successful SFI recovery rate (38%) for the 24-hour-delay PEG-fused group after SL-PNIs was significantly better than that rate for the 0-hour NC group (0%; p < 0.001, Chi-square) but also significantly worse than that rate for the 0-hour PEG-fused group (69%; p < 0.01, Chi-square).

Delayed repair with epinephrine successfully maintains many PEG-fused axons independent of SFI recovery in Lewis-host/SD-donor rats

Morphological analyses on semi-thin, plastic-embedded sections of the proximal, VPNA, and distal sciatic nerve segments at 12 weeks PO assessed whether axonal survival and myelination status correlated with SFI recovery (Fig. 2D).

All three of 24-hour-delay PEG-fused subgroups had significantly higher (p < 0.0001, Tukey’s) axonal diameters for their donor VPNA segments and distal segments compared to the 0-hour NC group (Fig. 2E). All these 24-hour-delay PEG-fused subgroups, independent of their SFI recovery status, had many large-diameter (≥ 3 μm) axons, indicating successful PEG-fusion during surgeries that rescued many axons from WD. In contrast, 0-hour NC nerves had mostly small diameter (< 3 μm) regenerating axons with thin myelin sheaths and a few large-diameter regenerating axons, as previously reported for 0-hour NC rats at ≥ 12 weeks PO [36, 37].

Myelinating Schwann cell densities were significantly higher (p < 0.0001, Tukey’s) in all three of 24-hour-delay PEG-fused subgroups compared to unoperated sciatic nerves (Fig. 2F), confirming that Schwann cells proliferate following SL-PNI repairs independent of SFI recovery status [38]. However, among these three subgroups, Successful PEG rats had significantly higher (p < 0.05, Tukey’s) Schwann cell density in their VPNA compared to Poor PEG rats, i.e., Successful PEG-fused rats had better graft myelination. In summary, these data showed that poorer SFI recovery was correlated with poorer myelination but not poorer axonal survival.

Improved SFI recovery following delayed repair with epinephrine correlates with less microvasculitis

Microvasculitis in the peripheral nervous system may occur as a secondary response to acute inflammation [39], resulting in blocked small endoneurial blood vessels (bBVs). Microvasculitis was not observed in 0-hour PEG-fused or NC rats in this study (Fig. 2D) or in previous studies [35, 40]. However, microvasculitis was observed in all three of 24-hour-delay PEG-fused subgroups independent of the extent of their SFI recovery. bBV diameters in VPNA segments and distal segments were the highest (p < 0.01, Tukey’s) in Partially Effective PEG rats and Poor PEG rats (Fig. 2G), respectively, suggesting more hyperemia. Successful PEG rats exhibited significantly lower overall bBV percentages than both Partially Effective PEG rats and Poor PEG rats (p < 0.05 and p < 0.001, Two-way ANOVA; Fig. 2H). The highest percentages of bBVs were observed in VPNA segments (p < 0.01, Tukey’s) compared to proximal or distal segments for all 24-hour-delay PEG-fused subgroups. Poor PEG rats exhibited a significantly higher (p < 0.01, Tukey’s) bBV percentage than Successful PEG rats distally, i.e., poorer SFI recovery was correlated with greater acute inflammation.

Pilot study of drug candidates that can reduce inflammation

To reduce acute inflammation and improve myelination, we conducted a pilot study investigating different classes of corticosteroids [28, 41, 42], as well as progesterone [43], that have been used to treat peripheral nerve and other injuries and pathologies. We applied low doses (40 µL/kg) of four drug candidates—MP [29, 30], prednisolone [44], dexamethasone [45–47], and progesterone—to the sciatic nerve following immediate PEG-fusion repair of ST-PNIs prior to closing the surgical site. PEG-fusion with MP produced the best sciatic nerve morphology and neuromuscular junction occupation compared to PEG-fusion alone or with any other drug candidate (Supplementary Fig. 1) at 1 week (Fig. 3A) and 3 weeks PO (Fig. 3B), as assessed by plastic-embedded nerve semi-thin sections and immunohistochemical staining. Thin cross-sections showed that MP improved myelination following PEG-fusion at 1 week PO (Fig. 3C). Overall, despite small sample sizes that limited statistical power, PEG-fusion with MP across three PO time points (1, 3, and 6 weeks PO) consistently showed increased distal axon density (Fig. 3D), better soleus muscle fiber innervation (Fig. 3E), improved sciatic nerve composition (Fig. 3F), and promoted myelination (Fig. 3G-H) compared to PEG-fusion alone.

In vivo CAP conduction was restored within 1–2 min in both 24-hour-delay and 48-hour-delay PEG-fused rats prior to MP application (Fig. 2A). Ex vivo nerve viability is maintained for up to 9 weeks using a static cold storage protocol

Previously, we used a daily replacement protocol to produce short-term-stored VPNAs that could be subsequently successfully PEG-fused [19, 23]. To extend the storage time, we modified a “gold standard” organ preservation technique—static cold storage—that reduces cell metabolism and degeneration [48]. We compared this static protocol against other protocols in which nerves were stored at 4 °C in dNR and normal saline (NS) and were tested daily or weekly with solution replacement. Sciatic VPNAs were obtained from outbred SD, SD-eGFP, and inbred Lewis rats.

Ex vivo CAP recordings were observed for up to 9 weeks when VPNAs were statically stored in dNR (Fig. 5A). CAP peaks at 6–9 weeks had smaller amplitudes compared to shorter static storage times. 100% of VPNAs statically stored for 1 week in dNR conducted CAPs (Fig. 5B), while 0% of those stored for 1 week in NS conducted CAPs. VPNAs statically stored for 1 week in NS were also stiff (see Supplementary Video 2), which was not observed in VPNAs stored in dNR even after 6 or 9 weeks. Percentages of conducting VPNAs in dNR were 100% at 3 weeks, 83% at 6 weeks, and 50% at 9 weeks. Storage duration was not affected by the donor strain or sex (p > 0.05, Log-rank test). The overall survival of conducting VPNAs statically stored in dNR was significantly better than in NS (p < 0.0001, Log-rank test). In contrast, no VPNAs conducted CAPs after 3 weeks in dNR using a weekly replacement protocol (Fig. 5C). Axonal survival in dNR with weekly replacement was significantly better than axonal survival in dNR with daily replacement (p < 0.001, Log-rank test) using historical data (Fig. 5D). Taken together, VPNAs stored in a given solution performed best using a static storage protocol, next best using a weekly replacement protocol, and worst using the previous daily replacement protocol.

The morphology of statically stored VPNAs was analyzed according to previously established criteria [19]: (1) axons with well-organized myelin and axoplasm, (2) axons with abnormal myelin, (3) axons with abnormal axoplasm, (4) axons with abnormal myelin and axoplasm, and (5) disintegrated axons (Fig. 5E). Transmission electron microscope images showed that most axons have abnormal myelin (myelin blebbing) after 3 weeks of storage in dNR (Fig. 5F). Neurofilaments were usually well-organized and densely packed, and many mitochondria were present.

The percentages of axons in each morphological category were quantified (Fig. 5G), and pair-wise statistical comparisons were calculated (Table 1). The percentage of well-organized axons significantly decreased following static storage in both solutions (p < 0.0001, Tukey’s). However, many well-organized axons and axons with abnormal myelin were preserved in dNR (13.9% and 56.4%, total 70.3%) after 3 weeks of storage. As previously postulated [19], these two axonal populations may be contributing to CAP conduction. In contrast, only 7.2% of intact axons and 20.1% of axons with abnormal myelin were present in NS after one week; both values were significantly lower than those observed for storage in dNR (p < 0.0001, Tukey’s). Furthermore, disintegrated axons were prominent after 1 week of storage in NS at 27.8%; this value was significantly higher than 1.8% after 1 week and 18.8% after 3 weeks of storage in dNR (p < 0.01, Tukey’s).

VPNAs statically stored for 1 to 9 weeks in dNR were then used for PEG-fusion repair of SL-PNIs without any delay in Lewis-host/SD-donor rats (Fig. 6). PEG-fusion repair of SL-PNIs using 3-week statically stored VPNAs with a 24-hour-delay achieves successful behavioral recovery in Lewis-host/SD-donor rats

After determining optimal VPNA storage times for transplantation, we performed 24-hour-delay PEG-fusion repair of SL-PNIs using 3-week-stored VPNAs in Lewis-host/SD-donor rats (Fig. 7). Data for the 0-hour PEG-fused and NC groups from Fig. 2 are plotted again in Fig. 7 for easier visualization of statistical comparisons. Discussion

Summary of results

As documented in greater detail below, this study demonstrates that (1) PEG-fusion repair of ST and SL-PNIs delayed for up to at least 48 h can be successful; (2) VPNAs can be stored for at least three weeks and then successfully PEG-fused to repair SL-PNIs; and (3) localized application of epinephrine and MP increase the success of PEG-fusion repairs of ST- and SL-PNIs.

PEG-fusion repair delayed up to 48 h post-injury successfully restores recovery of voluntary behaviors following both ST- and SL-PNIs (Tables 2 and 3). Acute inflammation, especially the disruption of the blood-nerve barrier, between the time of injury and repair produces more intraoperative bleeding during surgical operations [49]. Topical low-dose epinephrine stops intraoperative bleeding much sooner than saline alone and improves SFI recovery rates following ST-PNIs.

Acute inflammation following PNIs can produce hypoxia/ischemia injury, microvasculitis, and the development of neuropathic pain [28]. Reducing hypoxia and restoring blood flow alleviates neuropathic pain and increases limb usage as assessed by von Frey assays [49, 50]. Our results are consistent with these findings, i.e., poorer SFI recovery correlates with more blood vessel blockage and poorer myelination in 24-hour-delay PEG-fused rats. A low-dose, one-time local application of anti-inflammatory MP eliminates this vascular occlusion and improves limb usage and recovery as assessed by SFI assays.

VPNAs can be statically stored ex vivo in dNR solution for months and remain viable, with all stored VPNAs having excellent CAPs at 3 weeks and some VPNAs having CAPs at 9 weeks. Many axons in 3 weeks statically stored VPNAs had intact axoplasm/myelin or only abnormal myelin. Stored VPNAs can be successfully PEG-fused to repair SL-PNIs for at least 24-hour post-injury. The improved storage duration using the static storage protocol was significantly better than weekly or daily replacement protocols [19, 23]. Longer successful storage with static storage may be due to reduced mechanical disturbances and temperature fluctuations. The improved storage duration enables VPNAs to be optimally stored for weeks to possibly months in rats. Ongoing translational efforts in VPNA storage have been made using nerves harvested from rabbits, voles, and pigs. Preliminary results of one ongoing study showed that our static storage protocol similarly maintained viability of VPNAs harvested from pig nerves of different diameters, modalities, and lengths (3–8 cm) that better match those of human nerves than rat nerves. Validation of these results for human peripheral nerves may support the potential establishment of VPNA tissue banks.

Extending ex vivo nerve viability

We observed that longer VPNA storage times correlated with progressively degraded axonal morphology in 4 °C dNR. Ex vivo CAPs, axonal morphology, and behavioral recovery data were the best for PEG-fused VPNAs statically stored for three weeks. This relationship suggests that fewer axons remain viable for PEG-fusion at longer storage times. Ex vivo CAPs were still recordable after 6 weeks of static storage, but viable axons were fewer in number and behavioral recovery was poor. This result might be due to PEG-fused VPNAs stored for 6 weeks and beyond having fewer viable axons that may be insufficient to support behavioral recovery. Future work using statically stored VPNAs might examine ways to reduce ex vivo WD to maximize the number of viable axons available for PEG-fusion. This result might be achieved by using inhibitors against SARM1 [51] or using donor VPNAs harvested from WldS rats [52].

Additionally, we observed that VPNAs statically stored in 4 °C NS, but not 4 °C dNR, appeared stiff after 1 week. This finding suggests that solution composition affects tissue integrity and the survival of non-axonal cellular components, such as endothelial cells and fibroblasts. Static storage using alternative solutions that provide metabolic support might extend VPNA viability.

Current clinical procedures to repair PNIs by neurorrhaphy

Current best clinical practices to repair ST- and SL-PNIs employ (1) viable autografts obtained from other previously intact nerves, usually mostly sensory in axonal composition, (2) decellularized donor anucleate allografts, or (3) non-allogeneic synthetic conduits that are all microsutured (neurorrhaphy) to the host epineurium at both ends of the gap [3, 8, 53, 54]. Each of these methods creates a non-allogenic bridge that attempts to guide surviving proximal axons to distal nerve segments that have undergone WD and does not require immunosuppression. However, these methods are problematic in that recovery of sensorimotor function depends entirely upon slow (1–2 mm/day) axonal regeneration by outgrowth from surviving proximal axons [55, 56]. These procedures also do not prevent WD of axons in distal host segments or atrophy and fibrosis of denervated tissues. Autografts are typically regarded as the “gold standard” for such poor and slow repairs [3, 53] but also result in loss of sensory perceptions and sometimes donor site neuroma and pain [50, 57–61]. Autograft cabling using multiple segments of smaller diameters to repair larger diameter SL-PNIs is also a difficult surgical procedure that produces variable, often poor, long-term outcomes.

Advantages of using VPNAs vs. other bridging materials for PEG-fusion repair of SL-PNIs

Advantages of using PEG-fused VPNAs to repair SL-PNIs of larger-diameter mixed nerves (e.g., sciatic) are as follows: (1) Compared to autografts that are usually smaller-diameter sensory nerves, VPNAs can be much more closely matched with the injured nerve for function, composition, diameter, axonal number, and axonal density. When axonal density in the bridge matches or exceeds that of the injured nerve, the number of donor/host axons that can be PEG-fused across the lesion sites is maximized (40–60%). (2) VPNAs do not require secondary surgery with associated autograft morbidity at explant sites. (3) VPNAs are peculiar immunologically/ functionally compared to other transplanted tissues [14, 62], requiring less immunosuppression in the absence of PEG-fusion and no immunosuppression with successful PEG-fusion [15, 35, 63]. These preclinical rodent data are consistent with data from two clinical case studies [64, 65] and two clinical trials on PEG-fusion repair of ST- and SL-PNIs using autografts [66, 67], in which the time course and extent of recovery of two-point discrimination in humans are similar to SFI and von Frey behavioral recovery in rats in this and other studies [16, 18, 35, 40, 68]. Importantly, these clinical repairs using autografts were performed ~ 20 h post-injury on average. This timing is consistent with the 24-to-48-hour repair window established in our preclinical VPNA model.

Optimization of previous PEG-fusion repair protocols to repair ST- and SL-PNIs

Table 4 details the steps in the previous PEG-fusion protocol used for immediate repair (not delayed), as well as optimizations developed in this study. Previous PEG-fusion protocols consist of the sequential administration of four pharmaceutical agents in solution: 1) hypotonic Ca2+-free saline, 2) 0.5-1% methylene blue in distilled water, 3) 50% 3.35 kDa PEG in distilled water, and 4) isotonic Ca2+-containing saline. Additional steps to optimize PEG-fusion now include local applications of epinephrine and MP. These optimized steps are critical to clinically advance previous PEG-fusion protocols to repair SL-PNIs with PEG-fused VPNAs. These optimized procedures may also further improve outcomes for PEG-fusion of ST-PNIs and more distal SL-PNIs currently repaired with cable autografts in ongoing Phase III clinical trials.

PEG-fusion repair of ST- and SL-PNIs: characteristics and cellular/molecular mechanisms

PEG-fusion repair of ST- and SL-PNIs by VPNAs has recently received much national and international attention as a novel and potentially clinically translatable technology. Data from rats as an experimental small animal model system show that both wild-type and isogeneic rats with 5–10 mm sciatic SL-PNIs are successfully repaired with fresh PEG-fused VPNAs with no delays (0-hour PEG). These previously-published data, and data obtained herein with a delay in repair and use of statically stored VPNAs to repair SL-PNIs, can be summarized as follows:

(1) PEG-fusion protocols re-establish axoplasmic/axolemmal continuity of 40–60% of all axons that originally innervated the denervated sensory/motor structures within minutes. This rapid reinnervation is non-specific and produces immediate functional recovery of CAPs and compound muscle action potentials [16, 40]. Repairs using optimized PEG-fusion protocols performed with 24-to-48-hour delays and 3-week-statically-stored VPNAs produce similar results.

(2) PEG-fusion repairs prevent WD of the 40–60% successfully PEG-fused axons, prevent atrophy of muscle fibers reinnervated by these PEG-fused axons, and maintain morphology and functions of neuromuscular junctions [16, 40]. Fast and slow axonal transports are re-established in successfully PEG-fused axons within days to weeks [17, 69]. Without PEG-fusion, anucleate donor and distal axons undergo WD, and other donor cells/tissues (Schwann cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, connective tissue) are replaced by host viable cells/tissues [9, 14]. Repairs using optimized PEG-fusion protocols performed with 24-to-48-hour delays and 3-week-statically-stored VPNAs produced similar axon morphology and adequate myelination.

(3) PEG-fusion repairs subsequently restore up to 100% of pre-injury sensorimotor behavior as assessed by SFI and von Frey tests in 2–8 weeks [16, 40]. This behavioral recovery is almost certainly due to plasticity that extensively rewires CNS/PNS circuits, a phenomenon typically not otherwise observed beyond prenatal/early postnatal stages [17, 69]. Repairs using optimized PEG-fusion protocols performed with 24-to-48-hour delays and 3-week-statically- stored VPNAs produced similar SFI recovery rates significantly better than Negative Control rates.

(4) PEG-fusion of VPNAs is a unique and novel immunological system. PEG-fused VPNAs, in the absence of tissue-matching or immunosuppression, have comparable immunogenicity to NC autografts [35, 70, 71]. Compared to NC VPNAs, PEG-fused VPNAs have much less immunogenicity, as shown by downregulated T cell-associated transcripts, downregulated macrophage-associated transcripts, and downregulated transcripts driving antigen presentation. Furthermore, PEG-fused VPNAs display upregulation of transcripts for extracellular matrix components important for regeneration and repair. Local application of MP further reduces this reduced immune response to PEG-fused VPNAs.

The immunotolerance of PEG-fused VPNAs is almost certainly due primarily to host fast axonal transport that delivers host proteins to replace donor-specific antigens in the 40–60% successfully PEG-fused axons in donor VPNAs and secondarily to some immunoprotective effects of 3.35 kDa PEG per se [35]. The acquisition of host immune characteristics prevents the WD of successful PEG-fused axons and eliminates the need for tissue matching or systemic immunosuppression—unlike traditional allogeneic transplants (e.g., viable heart, liver, kidney, skin), which require lifelong immunosuppression to preserve functional donor cells. Using epinephrine and MP, the optimized PEG-fusion protocol maintained the immunotolerance of PEG-fused VPNAs despite delay and storage and produced behavioral recovery. Graft rejection is unlikely in the Partially Effective PEG animals in this study, as their behavioral loss occurred much later than typical rejection windows.

Limitations

This study did not directly compare PEG-fusion efficacy across different nerve gap lengths of SL-PNIs. Our use of 0.5–1 cm ablations and VPNA lengths are much shorter than would be used in human studies, albeit the lengths are comparable if one considers the overall size of each species. We also did not have NC groups repaired with delay or stored VPNAs, although we would expect that data from these NC groups would be no better than our 0-hour NC group. Additionally, anti-inflammatory drugs other than MP or similar corticosteroids not well characterized in this study might improve PEG-fusion. We did not do dose-response curves for MP and epinephrine, albeit we used dosages within recommended ranges for humans. Our pilot data showed that higher concentration of epinephrine further reduced bleeding (data not shown). MP dosage was intentionally kept low in this study to investigate its efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects. Future studies should investigate whether higher doses of MP within recommended ranges further improve success. We did not examine possible synergistic or antagonistic effects of MP and epinephrine, albeit when used in combination our results were better than when only one drug was used. We did not examine effects beyond 12 weeks, albeit such times are a significant portion of the life span of a rat comparable to many years of human life spans. Lastly, our findings are limited to small rodent animal models. We are currently researching VPNA repair using swine as a large animal model that has larger and longer nerve bundles, immune responses, and vascular dynamics more similar to Homo sapiens. These data are needed to further validate the clinical translatability of these optimized PEG-fusion protocols involving delay in repair of ST- and SL-PNIs and statically stored VPNAs to repair SL-PNIs.

Conclusions

We show that many proximal and distal host axons remain viable to be PEG-fused for both ST- and SL-PNIs for at least 48 h post-injury. We also show that rat VPNAs can be statically stored for at least 9 weeks. PEG-fusion of VPNAs stored up to 3 weeks with 0-hour to at least 24-hour delays following SL-PNIs produces satisfactory behavioral recovery rates. Future studies using human VPNAs, as shown with rats in this study, could help determine the feasibility of developing donor VPNA tissue banks for clinical use instead of using autografts, anucleate allografts, or conduits. These storage and delay times almost certainly can be extended, greatly improving the clinical application of PEG-fusion technology. Optimized PEG-fusion protocols using local application of epinephrine and anti-inflammatory MP greatly reduced bleeding and acute inflammation, improved morphology of PEG-fused nerves, and produced better behavioral recovery. These small doses, one-time local applications produce minimal systemic concentrations of PEG, MP or epinephrine.

Taken together, the present findings for delayed repairs and storage of VPNAs, as well as the ongoing clinical trial success using PEG-fused autografts [66, 67], may support a transformative medical advance in the clinical management of SL-PNIs. Currently, standard clinical practices delay surgical nerve repairs for days to weeks due to the status of patient general conditions and/or the lack of surgical facility or personal availability. This delay is based on assumptions that functional and behavioral outcomes of ST- or SL-PNIs are solely dependent on slow axonal regeneration and are not affected by delayed repairs to ST- or SL-PNIs. However, our results, along with previous clinical trials using PEG-fused autografts, challenge these assumptions and the subsequent clinical paradigm. If confirmed in larger model systems like swine, our optimized PEG-fusion protocol using VPNAs applied within a few days post-injury before host distal axons degenerate, would produce significantly improved functional outcomes within weeks, rather than months or years (if ever) with the current paradigm. Earlier surgical intervention using PEG-fusion would warrant serious consideration in future clinical practice. Most other clinical practices for PNI management, such as controlling neuropathic pain and rehabilitation, would likely remain unchanged.

Methods

Animals

All experimental procedures adhered to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th ed., National Research Council, 2011; [72]) and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at The University of Texas at Austin (AUP20252200278, approved 02/07/2025). A total of 286 rats (162 SD, 7 SD-eGFP, and 117 Lewis) were used in this study. Male (250–500 g) and female (225–300 g) rats 3–12 months old were housed 2–3 per cage and maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle with food and water given ad libitum. Surgical and behavioral procedures were performed during the light cycle.

Storage solution preparation

Two sterile solutions were used to store sciatic nerves as VPNAs: NS (0990-7983-09; ICU Medical, San Clemente, CA, USA) and dNR. dNR was made by diluting Normosol (0990-7967-09; ICU Medical) with ddH2O from ~ 270 mOsm to 250–255 mOsm. Osmolarity was always measured using a freezing point osmometer (Model 3300; Advanced Instruments, Norwood, MA, USA). Both storage solutions contained 100 U/mL of penicillin and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin (P4333; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Static cold storage of VPNAs and electrophysiological confirmation of viability in acute animal studies

SD, SD-eGFP, and Lewis rats were anesthetized with 4% isoflurane (RXISO-250; Animal Health International, Roanoke, TX, USA) using an inhaled anesthetic system (Handlebar Anesthesia, Pflugerville, TX, USA) followed by an intracardiac KCl injection for acute harvest of sciatic nerve tissue. The skin was shaved and disinfected by alternate wiping with 70% ethanol and Betadine several times. A 1.5–2 cm incision was made through the skin and biceps femoris to expose the sciatic nerve. Sciatic nerve segments (VPNAs; n = 141) were harvested (1.8–2.5 cm long) from both hindlimbs and stored at 4 °C in 2 mL sterile Eppendorf tubes filled with 1.5 mL of designated solutions.

Three different storage protocols were used:

Static storage. Storage solutions were neither replaced nor mechanically disturbed for the duration of storage. Ex vivo CAPs were recorded on the day of harvest (day 0) and at the end point of storage.

Static storage. Storage solutions were neither replaced nor mechanically disturbed for the duration of storage. Ex vivo CAPs were recorded on the day of harvest (day 0) and at the end point of storage.

Weekly replacement. Storage solutions were replaced weekly. Ex vivo CAPs were recorded on day 0 and weekly at times of replacing storage solutions.

Weekly replacement. Storage solutions were replaced weekly. Ex vivo CAPs were recorded on day 0 and weekly at times of replacing storage solutions.

Daily replacement. Storage solutions were replaced daily. Ex vivo CAPs were recorded on day 0 and daily at times of replacing storage solutions.

Daily replacement. Storage solutions were replaced daily. Ex vivo CAPs were recorded on day 0 and daily at times of replacing storage solutions.

For ex vivo CAP recordings, VPNAs were briefly placed in a custom 3D-printed chamber (ABS polymer; Craftbot XL machine) on sterilized titanium wires connected to stimulating and recording electrodes. VPNAs were stimulated with incremental voltages from 0 to 8 V using 0.1 ms square wave depolarizations given at 1 Hz with a 0.1 ms delay from a sweep-triggering pulse using a PowerLab 4/35 (AD Instruments, Sydney, Australia). CAPs were recorded using a Dual Bio Amp (AD Instruments). Stimulus amplitudes were limited to 1 V on day 0 and 8 V beyond day 0.

Surgical procedures and electrophysiological confirmation of PEG-fusion in chronic animal studies

Sciatic nerve injury Rats were anesthetized with 4% isoflurane and maintained with 1–2% isoflurane throughout the surgery. Rats were given preemptive carprofen at 5 mg/kg via subcutaneous injection. For local anesthetics, lidocaine (≤ 7 mg/kg; 16479689; Animal Health International) diluted in a solution of 0.025 mg/mL epinephrine (E-4000-02; Animal Health International) prepared in dNR was given 4–5 min prior to skin incision and sciatic exposure as described above via subcutaneous injection at the mid-thigh level. Ca2+-containing normal saline (0.9% NaCl containing 2 mM CaCl2) was used to hydrate the nerve as needed. After saline was removed, the sciatic nerve at the mid-thigh level was either transected or ablated (4-mm segment removal) using fine dissection scissors. After sciatic nerve injury, rats received immediate or delayed repairs as described below.

Immediate repairs The injury site was irrigated with 0.5% methylene blue followed by flushes with dNR. For ST-PNI repairs, sciatic nerve stumps were carefully trimmed and closely apposed with at least four 10 − 0 microsutures through the epineurium. For SL-PNI repairs, size-, sex-, and orientation-matched fresh or statically stored VPNAs were trimmed to ~ 5 mm and closely apposed to recipient sciatic nerve ends with at least four 10 − 0 microsutures through the epineurium. For rats receiving a PEG-fusion protocol, the surgical area was submerged in a sterile solution of 50% w/w 3.35 kDa PEG (88276-250G-F, Sigma-Aldrich) in ddH2O for 2 min to non-specifically fuse the closely apposed, open axonal ends. NC rats underwent neurorrhaphy but were not treated with PEG. For NC and PEG-treated rats, the surgical area was then irrigated 3–4 times with Ca2+-containing normal saline to accelerate Ca2+-induced vesicle accumulation and seal any remaining open axons [31]. All muscles were closed with 5 − 0 sutures, and the skin was closed with wound clips. Neosporin was applied to the incision area. Rats recovered from surgery on heating pads and were returned to standard housing. Rats received 5 mg/kg carprofen every 24 h for 72 h PO.

Delayed repairs: The muscles and the skin were closed as described above immediately after sciatic nerve injury. The rats were allowed to recover, and carprofen was given every 24 h PO, as described above for immediate sciatic nerve repairs. After 24, 36, or 48 h post-injury, the wound was re-opened to expose the injured nerve. A solution of 0.025 mg/mL epinephrine prepared in dNR was used as needed throughout the procedure to stop bleeding/maintain hemostasis until incision closure. ST- and SL-PNI repairs were performed as described above. Methylprednisolone acetate (MP: 70121-1573-01; Medline Industries, Northfield, IL, USA), prednisolone (P6004-5G, Sigma-Aldrich), progesterone (P3962-5G, Sigma-Aldrich), or dexamethasone (1126, TOCRIS, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was applied to the surgical site to sufficiently coat the exposed sciatic nerve at 40 µL/kg of animal weight. The incision was closed again, and rats were returned to standard housing and received 5 mg/kg carprofen every 24 h for 72 h PO.

In vivo electrophysiology: In vivo CAPs were recorded before sciatic injuries and immediately following nerve repairs prior to anti-inflammatory drug application. Stimulating and recording electrodes were placed proximally and distally to the lesion site and were ≥ 1 cm apart. Sciatic nerves were stimulated at 1 V using 0.1 ms square wave depolarizations at 1 Hz with a 0.1 ms delay from the trigger signal using a PowerLab 4/35 and recorded using a Dual Bio Amp. All CAP data were normalized with respect to the stimulus artifact recorded for immediate PEG-fusion repair groups.

Morphological analyses

For stored VPNAs, 3–4 mm segments from the middle of the VPNA were harvested. For end-point nerve samples, 3–4 mm of proximal host nerve, donor VPNA (if applicable), and distal host nerve each 0.5–1 mm away from the suture sites were harvested. Nerve samples were fixed and embedded as previously described [15, 18, 35, 40]. Glass-knife thick Sect. (0.5 μm) were obtained using a UC7 ultramicrotome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), stained with toluidine blue, and imaged on a Zeiss Axiovert 200 M fluorescent light microscope with an HR3 camera (Hebron, KY, USA). Diamond-knife (DDK, Wilmington, DE, USA) thin Sect. (70 nm) were obtained, mounted on 200 mesh formvar-carbon grids (01801, Ted Pella, Redding, CA, USA), and imaged on a JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Peabody, MA, USA).

Nerve morphometric analyses were performed using ImageJ (version 1.53 m, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) on randomly chosen ROIs for nerves harvested at the time of sacrifice. Axon diameter, g-ratio, and axon density for 150–200 axons from at least three ROIs per sample were analyzed in at least 2 samples per treatment group [18, 35, 40]. Nerve composition was quantified using a previously established assessment method [40] to characterize myelinated axon, myelin, degrading debris, and interspace. Microvasculitis analysis, blocked blood vessel (bBV) percentage and vessel diameter were analyzed from 10 to 200 vessels from 4 to 9 ROIs in 3–4 animals per group. Schwann cell counts and densities were analyzed from > 50 nuclei from 9 to 12 total ROIs from 3 to 4 animals per group. For ex vivo stored VPNA samples, > 200 axons from 3 ROIs from 3 to 5 nerve samples per group were categorized based on previously established criteria [19].

Immunohistochemical analysis

Soleus muscles, innervated by the tibial branch of the sciatic nerve, were fixed and stained as previously described at 1, 3, or 6 weeks PO following PEG-fusion or NC repairs of ST-PNIs [16]. Muscle sections stained with anti-SV2 and NF-M (for nerve) and bungarotoxin (for acetylcholine receptors) were imaged on a Zeiss LSM 710 confocal microscope with a 40x oil immersion lens, and max-intensity compressed z-stacks of images were analyzed in ImageJ. Labeled axons that were in contact with acetylcholine receptors were considered to innervate the receptor at the neuromuscular junction.

SFI behavioral analyses

SFI tests were performed as previously described [17, 18, 35]. Rats were handled and trained for at least three sessions prior to surgery to acclimate them to the testing apparatus and procedure. SFI tests were performed weekly for up to 12 weeks PO by testers blinded to the experimental groups. Rat hind paws were marked with red ink (right, unoperated side) and blue ink (left, operated side). Rats were placed on one end of a slightly inclined board (1.52 m long, 10.2 cm wide) lined with white papers and allowed to run back to the other end that contained a home cage. Inked paw prints were analyzed for paw length, total toe spread, and intermediate toe spread to compute SFI scores. Two successful trials without stopping or hesitation for three consecutive steps were gathered for each rat at each testing time point, and SFI scores were obtained by averaging the two successful trials. Unoperated rats exhibited SFI scores between + 30 and − 30. Depending on the success of recovery, the injured hind paw often had lower (more negative) SFI scores than − 30.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Data from male and female rats were pooled for both SFI and morphological analyses because previous studies reported no sex-related difference [17, 18, 35]. SFI data were analyzed by mixed-effects analysis followed by post hoc Tukey’s multiple comparisons. The success rates of PEG-fusion surgeries were compared and analyzed using Chi-square tests. Survival of conducting VPNAs in ex vivo storage was analyzed using Log-rank tests. Morphological data were analyzed using either one-way or two-way ANOVAs followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons, as stated in figure legends. A 95% confidence interval was used for all analyses. All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).
