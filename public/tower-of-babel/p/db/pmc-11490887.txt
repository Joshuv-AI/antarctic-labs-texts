Iron regulatory protein two facilitates ferritinophagy and DNA damage/repair through guiding ATG9A trafficking
Authors: Ting Zhang, Qian Liu, Quan Chen, Hao Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrace elemental iron is an essential nutrient that participates in diverse metabolic processes. Dysregulation of cellular iron homeostasis, both iron deficiency and iron overload, is detrimental and tightly associated with disease pathogenesis. IRPs-IREs system is located at the center for iron homeostasis regulation. Additionally, ferritinophagy, the autophagy-dependent ferritin catabolism for iron recycling, is emerging as a novel mechanism for iron homeostasis regulation. It is still unclear whether IRPs-IREs system and ferritinophagy are synergistic or redundant in determining iron homeostasis. Here we report that IRP2, but not IRP1, is indispensable for ferritinophagy in response to iron depletion. Mechanistically, IRP2 ablation results in compromised AMPK activation and defective ATG9A endosomal trafficking, leading to the decreased engulfment of NCOA4-ferritin complex by endosomes and the subsequent dysregulated endosomal microferritinophagy. Moreover, this defective endosomal microferritinophagy exacerbates DNA damage and reduces colony formation in IRP2-depleted cells. Collectively, this study expands the physiological function of IRP2 in endosomal microferritinophagy and highlights potential crosstalk between IRPs-IREs and ferritinophagy in manipulating iron homeostasis.Keywords: iron, IRP2, ferritinophagy, ATG9A, AMPK

Trace elemental iron is indispensable for numerous biological processes, including but not limited to DNA replication and damage/repair, electron transfer and oxidative phosphorylation, and oxygen transportation (1). Iron deficiency would lead to the disruption of these processes, and is tightly associated with the pathogenesis of diverse human diseases, especially iron deficiency anemia, which seriously endangers the health of newborns, children, and pregnant women (2). However, iron overload is also toxic. It has been well characterized that the bioactive iron could support the Fenton reaction and catalyze lipid peroxidation to initiate ferroptosis, a newly defined programmed cell death that is implicated in the pathogenesis of organ injuries and degenerative diseases (3). Thus, a primary challenge for organisms and cells is maintaining an optimal level of iron, which is sufficient to support those critical biological processes while avoiding cytotoxicity due to iron overload.

Iron homeostasis in mammalian cells is elaborately orchestrated by sophisticated regulatory mechanisms to coordinate iron absorption, utilization, storage, and efflux. These processes are meticulously dominated at the translational level by IRPs (iron regulatory proteins)-IREs (iron-responsive elements) system, although some other mechanisms still exist. IRPs, including IRP1 and IRP2, determine the molecular fate of mRNAs harboring cis-regulatory RNA hairpin structures in the UTR (untranslated region) known as IREs, and the majority of these IREs-harboring mRNAs mainly encode iron metabolism associated proteins (4). Specifically, when intracellular iron is sufficient, IRP1 binds to an [4Fe-4S] iron-sulfur cluster, loses the IREs-binding activity, and alternatively functions as the aconitase in tricarboxylic acid cycle by catalyzing the conversion of citrate to isocitrate (5). IRP2 is ubiquitinated by SKP1-CUL1-FBXL5 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex for proteasomal degradation in an iron- and oxygen-dependent manner (6, 7, 8). When intracellular iron is insufficient, IRP2 escapes from ubiquitination and stabilizes, while IRP1 disassociates from the iron-sulfur cluster and displays as an apoprotein. Consequently, both IRPs bind to IREs of target mRNAs, leading to the translational suppression of target mRNAs when IREs locate in 5′ UTR, as is the case for mRNAs encoding FTH (ferritin H-chain) and FTL (ferritin L-chain) for iron storage, FPN1 (Ferroportin) for iron export, as well as ALAS2 (Aminolevulinate synthase 2) for iron utilization, or the protection of target mRNAs from endonucleolytic cleavage when IREs locate in 3′ UTR, as is the case for mRNAs encoding TfR1 (Transferrin receptor 1) and DMT1 (Divalent metal transporter 1) for iron uptake. Therefore, the activated IRPs-IREs system facilitates iron absorption and restrains iron efflux, iron utilization, and iron storage, thereby maintaining an optimal intracellular iron level (9, 10).

In addition to IRPs-IREs system, NCOA4 (Nuclear receptor coactivator 4)-mediated ferritinophagy is another important approach to maintaining cellular iron homeostasis. Intracellular iron is largely sequestered by ferritin nanocages in the cytosol. Ferritin is a ubiquitously expressed heteropolymer composed of 24 FTH and FTL, possesses capacity to carry up to approximately 4500 iron atoms in the hollow cavity in each particle (11), and thus prevents the potential cytotoxicity of labile iron during iron sufficiency (12). Ferritin-bound iron could be mobilized in response to iron starvation through lysosomal degradation of ferritin, namely ferritinophagy (13, 14). NCOA4, previously known as a transcriptional coactivator for several nuclear receptors (15), was recently characterized as the specific receptor of ferritinophagy. When cellular iron is deficient, NCOA4 binds to ferritin and delivers ferritin to be encompassed by lysosomes for the following degradation and free iron release (16, 17). Under the condition that iron is sufficient, NCOA4 is ubiquitinated for proteasomal degradation by E3 ligase HERC2, due to the elevated iron occupancy in its C-terminal domain, thus ensuring a lower NCOA4 expression and a basal activity of ferritinophagy to leave more ferritin for iron storage (18).

It was reported that the C-terminal domain of NCOA4 is responsible for its interaction with a conserved surface arginine (Arg23) on FTH (18, 19). NCOA4 depletion strongly suppresses lysosomal engulfment of ferritin. However, the exact mechanism for the lysosomal engulfment of the NCOA4-ferritin complex is still under controversy. The earlier studies reported that NCOA4 delivers ferritin to be encompassed by autophagosomes (which subsequently fuse with lysosomes) through directly interacting with LC3 or the homologous. Biochemical analysis confirmed the direct interaction between NCOA4 and LC3 family. No typical LIR (LC3-interacting region) has been characterized in the NCOA4 molecule (16), albeit some receptors interact with the LC3 family in an LIR-independent manner (20). A following study by Goodwin and colleagues reported that NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy is independent of the classical macroautophagy machinery, as depletion of critical components involved in LC3 lipidation, including ATG16L1, ATG14, ATG7, and ATG3, gave rise to a negligible change in ferritin turnover. Alternatively, a non-classical ESCRT (endosomal sorting complexes required for the transport)-mediated delivery system and some autophagy genes, especially ATG9A (Autophagy related 9A), are indispensable for NCOA4-ferritin transportation and the subsequent ferritinophagy (21). This was further confirmed by another study from Prof. Noboru Mizushima’s group (22). The authors presented compelling evidence to suggest that NCOA4 drives ferritin phase separation to form liquid-like NCOA4-ferritin condensates, which are incorporated into endosomes for the subsequent endosomal microferritinophagy (22).

Both IRPs-IREs and ferritinophagy are crucial mechanisms for maintaining cellular iron homeostasis. Whether there is any crosstalk between these two pathways is unclear. In this study, we critically suggested that IRP2, but not IRP1, is indispensable for ferritinophagy in response to iron depletion. Importantly, this IRP2-dependent ferritinophagy is mediated by endosomal microferritinophagy rather than selective macroferritinophagy. Mechanistically, the compromised AMPK activation during IRP2 ablation results in defective ATG9A trafficking onto endosomes, leading to disorders in endosomal engulfment of NCOA4-ferritin complex and the subsequent endosomal microferritinophagy. In addition, we also showed that this IRP2-mediated ferritinophagy is important for DNA damage/repair and genome integrity maintenance. In summary, this study concludes that IRP2 orchestrates endosomal microferritinophagy and DNA damage/repair via AMPK-ATG9A axis.

Results

IRP2 is indispensable for ferritin turnover via ferritinophagy

To dissect the potential regulation of ferritinophagy by IRPs, IRP1, and IRP2 knockdown HeLa cells were constructed, respectively. Treatment with iron chelator depletes intracellular iron and triggers ferritin degradation. When sc (scramble control) cells were challenged with iron chelator DFP (deferiprone) or DFO (deferoxamine), a notable degradation of the ferritin FTL subunit was observed. IRP1 knockdown led to a negligible change in this FTL degradation (Fig. S1, A and B). To verify that this ferritin degradation was mediated by ferritinophagy, the lysosomal inhibitor Baf-A1 (bafilomycin A1) was used. Both in sc and IRP1 knockdown cells, Baf-A1 supplementation largely suppressed the DFO-induced degradation of the ferritin FTH subunit (Fig. S1C). Furthermore, ferritin engulfment by lysosomes was measured by analyzing the potential colocalization of ferritin-LAMP2. Immunofluorescence staining clearly showed comparable colocalizations of FTL-LAMP2 in sc and IRP1 knockdown cells during DFO treatment (Fig. S1D). On the contrary, the knockdown of IRP2, another master iron regulatory protein, significantly decelerated the degradation of ferritin subunits in HeLa cells during iron depletion (Fig. 1A). This was further confirmed by utilizing IRP2 KO (knockout) cells. IRP2 KO in HeLa cells and MEFs (mouse embryonic fibroblasts) dramatically suppressed iron chelators-induced degradation of ferritin subunits (Fig. 1, B–E). Administration of Baf-A1 strongly inhibited FTH degradation in WT cells, further confirming that this DFO-induced ferritin degradation was indeed ferritinophagy (Fig. S1, E and F). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining showed that IRP2 KO obviously decreased FTL-LAMP2 colocalization upon iron depletion, suggesting a notable reduction of ferritin engulfment by lysosomes (Fig. 1F). We also measured ferritinophagy by analyzing the colocalization between lysosomes and ferritinophagy receptor NCOA4. IRP2 KO largely decreased NCOA4-LAMP2 colocalization during iron depletion (Fig. 1G).

Quick transition from iron sufficiency to iron depletion by administration of FAC (ferric ammonium citrate) followed by challenge of iron chelator DFO could substantially trigger ferritinophagy (16). Ferritin subunits were highly expressed during FAC administration and then quickly degraded in response to DFO challenge in WT cells. IRP2 KO in HeLa cells and MEFs largely abrogated ferritin turnover in this ferritinophagy model (Fig. 1, H and I), further suggesting that IRP2 is indispensable for ferritin turnover via ferritinophagy.

IRP2 manipulates ferritinophagy via endosomal microferritinophagy rather than selective ferritin macroautophagy

IRP2 ablation leads to iron deficiency, which is expected to trigger ferritinophagy for ferritin-bound iron mobilization. Therefore, the defective ferritinophagy in IRP2 ablated cells seems incomprehensible. To dissect the underlying mechanism for which IRP2 manipulates ferritinophagy, we first analyzed the NCOA4-ferritin interaction. Immunofluorescence staining showed that iron depletion triggered the formation of NCOA4-and FTL-positive puncta in the cytosol， whereas IRP2 ablation gave rise to a comparable colocalization between NCOA4 and FTL, both under steady state and under iron-depleted condition (Fig. 2A). Co-immunoprecipitation analysis further demonstrated an equivalent interaction of NCOA4-FTH (Fig. 2B), suggesting that IRP2 ablation failed to alter NCOA4-ferritin interaction. Lysosome-mediated ferritinophagy includes two distinct pathways, namely autophagosome-mediated ferritin macroautophagy and autophagosome-independent endosomal microferritinophagy (22). To uncover which route is for IRP2 manipulating ferritinophagy, we analyzed the engulfment of ferrtin by autophagosomes or endosomes. Immunofluorescence staining showed a comparable translocation of FTL into RFP-LC3-positive autophagosomes in the control cells and IRP2 ablated cells, both upon the treatment of DFO and DFO plus the lysosomal inhibitor CQ (chloroquine) (Fig. 2C). More importantly, we noticed that a large amount of FTL puncta were not surrounded by, or even proximity to, the autophagosomes, both in WT and IRP2 ablated cells (Fig. 2C). Similarly, IRP2 ablation led to a comparable colocalization between NCOA4 and RFP-LC3-positive autophagosomes (Fig. 2D). On the contrary, decreased translocation of FTL into RAB7-RFP-positive endosomes was observed in IRP2 KO cells during iron depletion (Fig. 2E). Importantly, large amounts of FTL puncta were indeed engulfed by, or neared to, the endosomes, in WT cells (Fig. 2E). IRP2 ablation also led to a lower colocalization of NCOA4 and RAB7-RFP-positive endosomes during iron depletion (Fig. 2F). Taken together, these results indicated that IRP2-dependent ferritinophagy in response to iron chelation is mediated by endosomal microferritinnophagy rather than ferritin macroautophagy.

AMPK determines endosomal microferritinophagy

AMPK and mTOR are two major upstream signaling regulators of autophagy. To explore the potential regulation of IRP2-mediated ferritinophagy by these two kinases, the activities of AMPK and mTOR in IRP2 ablated cells under iron depletion were analyzed by detecting AMPK phosphorylation level and S6K (a major downstream substrate of mTOR kinase) phosphorylation level, respectively. Comparable S6K dephosphorylation in response to iron depletion was observed in sc and IRP2 knockdown cells (Fig. S2, A and B), suggesting that IRP2 was unable to manipulate mTOR activity. An obvious AMPK activation in response to iron depletion was observed in control cells. On the contrary, both IRP2 knockdown and KO in HeLa cells led to a dramatic decrease in this AMPK phosphorylation (Fig. 3, A and B). Similarly, the same phenotype was observed in IRP2 KO MEF cells (Fig. 3C), suggesting that IRP2 is functionally important for AMPK activation in response to iron depletion.

To characterize the potential regulation of ferritinophagy by AMPK, the AMPK inhibitor C. C (Compound C) was employed. C. C pretreatment resulted in a significant reduction in DFO-induced FTL degradation in HeLa cells (Figs. 3D and S3A), as well as a decreased colocalization of FTL-LAMP2 (Fig. S3B). Similarly, AMPK KO resulted in a significant reduction in ferritin degradation in response to both iron depletion (Fig. S3C) and the quick transition from iron sufficiency to iron depletion (Fig. S3D). The supplementation of Baf-A1 further confirmed that this DFO-induced ferritin degradation was mainly dependent on ferritinophagy (Fig. S3E). Immunofluorescence staining showed that AMPK KO led to a decreased colocalization of FTL-LAMP2 (Fig. S3F) and NCOA4-LAMP2 (Fig. S3G), suggesting a reduction in the lysosomal engulfment of NCOA4-ferritin complex. Furthermore, we also investigated the potential regulation of AMPK in endosomal microferritinophagy. Immunofluorescence staining showed that AMPK ablation led to reduced translocation of FTL and NCOA4 into RAB7-RFP-positive endosomes (Fig. S3, H and I). Hence, AMPK activation is indispensable for iron starvation-induced endosomal microferritinophagy.

In addition, the potential involvement of AMPK in IRP2-mediated endosomal microferritinophagy was explored. The AMPK inhibitor C. C largely suppressed ferritinophagy both in sc and IRP2-depleted cells (Fig. 3D). Reversely, administration of the AMPK activator AICAR (5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide) mandatorily drove AMPK activation in IRP2 knockdown cells, as evidenced by the increased AMPK phosphorylation level. Simultaneously, AICAR pretreatment partially restarted FTL degradation in IRP2 ablated cells (Fig. 3E), as well as the colocalization of FTL-LAMP2 (Fig. 3F) and NCOA4-LAMP2 (Fig. 3G) in IRP2 KO cells. More importantly, mandatory activation of AMPK partially restored the translocation of FTL and NCOA4 into RAB7-positive endosomes (Fig. 3, H and I) in IRP2 KO cells, suggesting an indispensable role of AMPK activation in IRP2-dependent endosomal microferritinophagy. Moreover, a comparable colocalization between NCOA4 and FTL was observed in WT cells and AMPK KO cells, both under steady state and under iron-depleted condition (Fig. S4A). The co-immunoprecipitation analysis further demonstrated an equivalent interaction of NCOA4-FTH in these two cells (Fig. S4B). Collectively, these data highlight a notion that AMPK primarily modulates the engulfment of NCOA4-ferritin complex by endosomes/lysosomes, which is important for IRP2-dependent endosomal microferritinophagy during iron depletion, rather than directly manipulating the formation of NCOA4-ferritin condensates.

Compromised AMPK-mediated ATG9A trafficking is responsible for the defective endosomal microferritinophagy in IRP2-depleted cells

By using a genome-scale pooled CRISPR-Cas9 screening, Goodwin and colleagues suggested that ferritinophagy requires some autophagy core components, especially ATG9A and TAX1BP1 (4). It has been reported that TAX1BP1 serves as a scaffold protein and mediates the endosomal trafficking of NCOA4-ferritin complex by directly interacting with NCOA4 (22, 23). ATG9A is a multi-spanning membrane protein, and its trafficking between TGN (trans-Golgi network) and post-Golgi vesicles may provide lipids for autophagosome formation, which is essential for bulk autophagy both in yeast and mammals (24, 25, 26). Additionally, ATG9A is crucial for some types of selective autophagy, including mitophagy and xenophagy (27, 28). Genetic ablation of ATG9A dramatically disrupted ferritin degradation in response to iron depletion (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the supplementation of Baf-A1 confirmed that this DFO-induced ferritin degradation was mainly dependent on ferritinophagy (Fig. S5A). More importantly, ATG9A KO led to the reduced colocalization between FTL and RAB7-positive endosomes, as well as the colocalization between NCOA4 and RAB7-positive endosomes (Fig. 4, B and C), suggesting an involvement of ATG9A in endosomal microferritinophagy.

By using an immunofluorescence staining assay, we found that a part of ATG9A located onto LAMP2-positive lysosomes and RAB7-positive endosomal compartments in WT cells during iron depletion (Fig. 4, D and E). This ATG9A trafficking onto lysosomes and endosomes was sharply disturbed in C. C-pretreated WT cells and in IRP2 KO cells during iron starvation (Fig. 4, D and E). In addition, ATG9A trafficking was restored to some extent in IRP2 KO cells when the administration of AICAR (Fig. 4, D and E). Moreover, loss of AMPK resulted in a similar disruption of ATG9A trafficking during iron depletion (Fig. 4, F and G), suggesting a notion that IRP2-mediated ATG9A trafficking is dependent on AMPK activation.

How AMPK manipulated ATG9A trafficking during endosomal microferritinophagy is the next question. It was reported that AMPK phosphorylates ATG9A at the S761 residue on the C terminus, which is critical for ATG9A trafficking under hypoxic stress (29). When WT ATG9A was reintroduced into ATG9A KO cells, partial colocalization between ATG9A and lysosomal marker LAMP2 was observed (Fig. 5A). Similarly, WT ATG9A was also partially colocalized with endosomal marker RAB7 (Fig. 5B). These colocalizations were largely reduced when a S761A mutant was expressed in ATG9A KO cells (Fig. 5, A and B). Furthermore, re-introduction of WT ATG9A, but not the S761A mutant, restored iron depletion-induced ferritinophagy in ATG9A KO cells, as shown by the colocalization of FTL-LAMP2 (Fig. 5C) and ferritin degradation (Figs. 5D and S5B). More importantly, the expression of WT ATG9A, but not the S761A mutant, restarted the endosomal engulfment of ferritin (Fig. 5E), further indicating that ATG9A phosphorylation at the S761 residue was important for iron depletion-induced endosomal microferritinophagy in mammalian cells.

Co-immunoprecipitation analysis suggested the interaction between ATG9A and RAB7. S761A mutant presented a relatively lower affinity to RAB7 when compared to WT ATG9A (Fig. 5F). In addition, WT ATG9A could partially colocalize with NCOA4, while a relatively lower colocalization between ATG9A and NCOA4 was observed when the S761A mutant was expressed in ATG9A KO cells (Fig. 5G). More importantly, IRP2 KO resulted in less interaction between ATG9A and RAB7 or NCOA4 (Fig. 5H). Overall, these data suggested that ATG9A functions as a scaffold protein to mediate the endosomal trafficking of NCOA4-ferritin complex by interacting with RAB7 and NCOA4. Furthermore, we also shed light on the defective AMPK-ATG9A axis in IRP2-depleted cells results in disturbed ATG9A trafficking and endosomal microferritinophagy.

IRP2-mediated endosomal microferritinophagy is crucial for the maintenance of genome integrity

Iron is crucial for broad-spectrum biological processes, including the maintenance of genome integrity, as iron is an essential co-factor for many enzymes involved in DNA damage/repair. To test whether IRP2-mediated endosomal microferritinophagy is involved in the maintenance of genome stability, the DNA damage levels in WT and IRP2 ablated cells were analyzed by immunofluorescence staining of γH2AX, the distinctive DNA damage marker. IRP2 KO resulted in an obvious increase in γH2AX foci formation during iron depletion when compared to WT cells, suggesting an elevated level of DNA damage upon IRP2 depletion. More importantly, administration of AMPK activator AICAR decreased this DNA damage under iron-depleted condition, even in IRP2 KO cells (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, AMPK depletion generated the same phenotype, as evidenced by more γH2AX foci formation during iron depletion (Fig. 6B). Re-introduction of WT ATG9A, but not the S761A mutant, largely decreased γH2AX foci in ATG9A KO cells during iron depletion (Fig. 6C). Additionally, colony formation assay showed that IRP2 KO and AMPK KO resulted in a decreased number of colony formations. AICAR administration could partially restore colony number of IRP2 KO cells (Fig. 6, D and E). Taken together, these data suggested an important role of IRP2-mediated endosomal microferritinophagy in sustaining DNA damage/repair and supporting cell survival.

Discussion

Intracellular iron must be precisely managed to avoid the cytotoxicity of both iron deficiency and iron overload. IRPs-IREs and NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy are two elegant mechanisms for maintaining intracellular iron homeostasis. In this study, we provided conclusive evidence to suggest that IRP2, but not IRP1, is functionally important for NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy in response to iron depletion. Mechanistically, IRP2 is indispensable for iron depletion-induced AMPK activation. IRP2 ablation decreases ATG9A-RAB7 interaction and disturbs ATG9A endosomal trafficking, leading to the defective engulfment of NCOA4-ferritin complex by endosomes for the following endosomal microferritinophagy. Besides, IRP2-mediated endosomal microferritinophagy and iron recycle is important for DNA damage/repair to maintain genome integrity and support cell survival (Fig. 7). Overall, this study highlights a novel mechanism for IRP2 dominating iron homeostasis via endosomal microferritinophagy.

Schematic diagram of IRP2 regulating NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy. In WT cells, AMPK activation facilitates ATG9A translocation from Golgi to lysosomes/endosomes, leading to the engulfment of NCOA4-ferritin complex by endosomes and ensuring the endosomal microferritinophagy. IRP2-AMPK-ATG9A axis is critical for iron recycle to sustain DNA damage/repair and maintain genome integrity. In the absence of IRP2, compromised AMPK activation results in defective ATG9A endosomal trafficking, leading to the decreased engulfment of NCOA4-ferritin complex by endosomes, dysregulated endosomal microferritinophagy and the subsequent exacerbated DNA damage.

IRP1/2 are two well-characterized iron regulatory molecules. By binding iron metabolism-related mRNAs that harbor IREs, IRP1/2 determines the molecular fate of these target mRNAs. Previous studies utilizing genetically engineered mice suggested complementation of these two IRPs in modulating iron homeostasis and iron metabolism. Conventional double KO of IRP1 and IRP2 resulted in embryonic lethality (30), and conditional KO of both IRP1 and IRP2 in the liver (31) or intestine (32) led to mortality a few weeks after birth. On the contrary, mice with a single deletion of IRP1 or IRP2 are viable, and a relatively mild phenotype in iron imbalance was manifested in these single KO mice (33, 34, 35), suggesting that these two IRPs function redundantly. More specifically, mice lacking IRP1 develop normally, with mild abnormality of iron metabolism only in the kidney and brown fat (35), while mice lacking IRP2 show relatively more serious phenotypes, as evidenced by the microcytosis with abnormal iron metabolism in the liver, duodenum, and spleen (34), as well as elevated red cell protoporphyrin IX and adult-onset progressive neurodegeneration (33, 36). In addition, the regulatory mechanisms for IRP1 and IRP2 determining iron homeostasis differ sharply. Specifically, the IREs-binding activity of IRP1 is competitively inhibited by the iron-sulfur cluster occupancy on IRP1 apoprotein, while the IREs-binding activity of IRP2 is mainly determined by IRP2 stability. However, recent studies reported that the iron-sulfur cluster also modulates the IREs-binding activity (37) and stability (8) of IRP2. Overall, these studies strongly suggest that IRP2 is the master regulator of iron homeostasis in mammalian cells, whereas IRP1 mainly functions as an aconitase in vivo and is absent in IREs-binding for manipulating iron homeostasis (38). In addition, NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy is another important approach to maintaining iron homeostasis both in vitro and in vivo. Genetic ablation of NCOA4 in mice results in iron accumulation in the liver and spleen, as well as elevated transferrin saturation and mild microcytic hypochromic anemia (39, 40). In this study, we clearly showed that ablation of IRP2, but not IRP1, leads to decelerated ferritin turnover and decreased ferritin translocation into lysosomes, suggesting an incredible capacity of IRP2 in fine-tuning ferritinophagy. However, whether this IRP2-mediated ferritinophagy is dependent on the IRE-binding activity of IRP2 is still unclear. Therefore, further investigation on the crosstalk between IRPs-IREs and ferritinophagy is necessary.

Increasing number of studies have uncovered the importance of AMPK activation in selective autophagy, including xenophagy (41), mitophagy (42, 43), ER-phagy (44) and lipophagy (45). In this study, we suggested that AMPK activation is important for IRP2-dependent ferritinophagy in response to iron starvation. Importantly, inhibition of AMPK activation by administration of the chemical inhibitor C. C or genetic ablation of AMPK largely suppresses ferritinophagy, whereas enforced activation of AMPK by challenge of the activator AICAR restores ferritinophagy in IRP2 ablated cells. It has been reported that AMPK is able to fine-tune microautophagy to mediate the turnover of lipid droplets (46) and proteasomes (47, 48). Recently, a study from Prof. Noboru Mizushima’s group suggested that ferritinophagy could be achieved by endosomal microautophagy (22). In this study, compelling evidence further showed that IRP2-AMPK axis modulates ferritinophagy in an endosomal microautophagy-dependent manner. Iron depletion-mediated AMPK activation is partially attenuated during IRP2 ablation, while no obvious difference in ATP production was observed in IRP2-depleted cells, neither under a steady state nor under iron-starved condition (data not shown). An alternative mechanism dependent on glycolysis contributes to AMPK activation (49), and IRP2 modulates several metabolic pathways including glycolysis (50). Therefore, it is supposed that IRP2 manipulates AMPK activation by modulating glycolysis in response to iron depletion.

In all autophagy core components, ATG9A is the sole multi-spanning membrane protein. However, its exact molecular function is only partially understood. ATG9A trafficking between TGN and post-Golgi compartments, including endosomes and highly mobile ATG9A positive vesicles (51), is proposed to sustain autophagosome nucleation, formation, and expansion (26, 52) by supporting the biosynthesis of PI4P (phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate) (53) and transferring phospholipids (54, 55). Previous studies reported that ATG9A trafficking is modulated by dynamic phosphorylation mediated by AMPK (29), ULK1 (56, 57) and other kinases. Particularly, AMPK phosphorylates ATG9A at the S761 residue to facilitate ATG9A trafficking in response to metabolic stress (29). In this study, defective ATG9A trafficking onto endosomes was observed in IRP2 and AMPK-depleted cells. Re-introduction of WT ATG9A, but not the S761A mutant, partially restored ferritinophagy in ATG9A null cells, suggesting that ATG9A phosphorylation is crucial for ferritinophagy. Mechanistically, ATG9A phosphorylation during iron depletion is critical for ATG9A-RAB7 interaction and ATG9A endosomal trafficking, which is supposed to mediate the endosomal engulfment of NCOA4-ferritin complex and following endosomal microferritinophagy. This is consistent with previous reports that ATG9A could interact with RAB7 (57) and transport to RAB7-positive endosomes (58). Therefore, IRP2 manipulates endosomal microferritinophagy through AMPK-ATG9A axis and the following ATG9A-RAB7 interaction.

Iron sustains enzymatic activity of proteins involved in DNA replication and damage/repair, thus plays an important role in maintaining genome integrity and supporting cell growth. Previous studies reported that both IRP2- and NCOA4-mediated iron bioavailability are crucial for DNA damage/repair and cell survival (59, 60, 61). In this study, we analyzed DNA damage in IRP2, AMPK, and ATG9A ablated cells, and the results suggested that IRP2-AMPK-ATG9A axis is important to facilitate DNA damage/repair and support cell survival. In summary, this study concludes that IRP2 orchestrates endosomal microferritinophagy and genome integrity via AMPK-ATG9A axis.

Experimental procedures

Cell culture and transfection

HeLa and MEF cells were maintained in our lab, cultured in DMEM (Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium, C11995500BT, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (fetal bovine serum, 03. U16001DC, EallBio) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Lipofectamine 8000 reagent (C0533, Beyotime) was used for cell transfection. Briefly, the culture medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing FBS and penicillin-streptomycin before transfection. Transfection reagent and DNA plasmid were mixed into Opti-MEM Medium (31,985,070, Gibco). After gently mixing, the solution was dropped into the cell medium. The medium was discarded and fresh medium was added 6 h after transfection. Follow-up experiments were performed 24 h after transfection.

Regents and antibodies

The following regents were used in this study: DFP (379,409), DFO (D9533), FAC (RES20400-A7), CQ (C6628), and AICAR (A9978) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich; Baf-A1 (S1413) and C. C (S7840) was purchased from Selleck Chemicals. The following primary antibodies were used in this study: anti-IRP1 rabbit monoclonal antibody (20272S), anti-IRP2 rabbit monoclonal antibody (37135S), anti-FTH rabbit monoclonal antibody (4393S), anti-AMPK rabbit polyclonal antibody (2532S), anti-P-AMPK rabbit monoclonal antibody (2535S), anti-S6K rabbit polyclonal antibody (9202S) and anti-P-S6K rabbit monoclonal antibody (9204S) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology; anti-LAMP2 mouse monoclonal antibody (sc-18822) and p-Histone H2A.X mouse monoclonal antibody (sc-517348) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology; anti-Myc mouse monoclonal antibody (M4439) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich; anti-Actin rabbit monoclonal antibody (AC026), anti-Myc rabbit monoclonal antibody (AE070) and anti-RAB7 rabbit monoclonal antibody (A12308) were purchased from ABclonal; anti-FTL rabbit polyclonal antibody (10727-1-AP) and anti-GFP mouse monoclonal antibody (66002-1-Ig) were purchased from ProteinTech. The secondary antibodies used for western blotting in this study included HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (7074S) and HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (7076S), which were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. The fluorescent secondary antibodies used for immunofluorescence staining in this study included goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 (A11001), goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 594 (A11032), goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488 (A11034) and goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 594 (A11012) antibodies, which were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Plasmids and site-direct point mutagenesis

RFP-LC3 and ATG9A-Myc were reported previously (56, 62). The CDS regions encoding NCOA4 and RAB7 were amplified by PCR from a human cDNA bank and cloned into a peGFP-N3 vector and DsRed vector, respectively. The NCOA4-GFP and RAB7-RFP plasmids were sequenced and extracted from E. coli by using a plasmid DNA purification kit.

Site-directed mutagenesis was carried out to construct ATG9A-S761A mutant. Briefly, upstream and downstream primers containing the desired mutation were synthesized. The upstream primer is 5′- GATGAGAGTGGAGAACGCGCCCCTGATGAAGGGGGAGAGGGCG-3′, and the downstream primer is 5′-CCCTTCATCAGGGGCGCGTTCTCCACTCTCATCACTCTCCCGG-3′. 20 ng WT ATG9A-Myc plasmid as template, corresponding primers and Pfu DNA Polymerase (600,135, Agilent) were employed for site-directed mutagenesis PCR according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The PCR product was processed with endonuclease DpnI (R0176L, New England Biolabs) to degrade template ATG9A-Myc plasmid. The subsequent product was transformed into competent E. coli cells, and positive clones were sequenced to confirm the mutagenesis.

Construction of stable cell lines

ATG9A KO HeLa cell was maintained in our lab (56, 63). IRP2 KO MEF cell was reported previously (50). AMPKα1/α2 double KO HeLa cell was kindly gifted by Dr Fengli Wang from Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, China.

Plasmids expressing hairpin siRNA were constructed by inserting pairs of annealed DNA oligonucleotides into pLKO.1-puro vector. The targeting sequence to IRP1 is 5′-GCAGGATTGTTAGCAAAGAAA-3′, and the targeting sequence to IRP2 is 5′-GATCTTACAGTTGACCATTCT-3′. A non-targeting sequence, 5′- ACTACCGTTGTTATAGGTG-3′, was used as a sc. pCas9-sgRNA plasmid expressing Cas9 together with sgRNA targeting IRP2 was constructed by inserting pairs of annealed DNA oligonucleotides into pCas9-sgRNA vector. The sgRNA to IRP2 is 5′-AACAAGACCCGTATTGAGTA-3′. To construct stable knockdown or KO cell lines, HeLa cells were transfected with pLKO.1-IRP1, pLKO.1-IRP2 or pCas9-sgIRP2 plasmid, and placed under puromycin selection. Monoclone was selected and the knockdown efficiency or KO was determined by western blotting.

ATG9A rescue was performed in ATG9A KO cells. Plasmid expressing WT ATG9A-Myc, the S761A mutant, or empty pcDNA4-To-Myc vector were transfected into ATG9A KO cells. 100 μg/ml zeocin was added for selection for 7 days. The surviving cells were sub-cultured in DMEM/FBS medium supplemented with 50 μg/ml zeocin and used for the indicated experiments.

SDS-PAGE and western blotting

After indicated transfection or treatment, cells were washed with PBS and lysed with NP40 lysis buffer (P0013F, Beyotime Biotechnology) containing phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktail on ice for 30 min. Samples were centrifuged to collect the supernatant as whole cell lysates. The protein concentrations were measured and lysates were boiled for 15 min after adding 6 × loading buffer. An equivalent quantity of protein from each sample was resolved on SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Then the membrane-bound proteins were blocked with 5% non-fat milk or 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h, subsequently incubated with indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, immunoreactive protein bands were visualized by adding chemiluminescence regents (PA110, TianGen). The relative Actin-normalized protein levels were quantitatively analyzed via the band density by ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining and confocal microscopy imaging

Cells were plated in a 24-well plate preplaced with a sterilized glass coverslip in each well. After the indicated treatment, the medium was discarded and cells were fixed with DMEM supplementing with 4% paraformaldehyde at 37 °C for 15 min. Next, the cells were permeabilized in 0.2% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 15 min, blocked with PBS containing 5% FBS for 30 min, incubated with indicated primary antibody overnight at 4 °C and corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h, successively. The samples were washed with PBS three times, mounted in an antifade mountant, and images were captured with an N-STORM confocal microscope. The brightness and contrast were adjusted equally in all images for one independent experiment. The colocalization between two fluorescence signaling in cells was quantified by ImageJ software. Data represent the mean ± SEM from three independent experiments, with >200 cells counted per condition.

Immunoprecipitation

Cells were seeded in 15 cm2-plates. After indicated transfection or treatment, cells were washed with PBS, scraped, and lysed with pre-cold NP40 lysis buffer supplementing with phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktail on ice for 30 min. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000g for 15 min to collect the whole cell lysates. 100 μl supernatant was left in a new centrifuge tube as input, and the remaining lysate was incubated with the indicated antibody overnight at 4 °C. Protein-A/G plus sepharose (sc-2003, Santa Cruz) was washed twice with pre-cold PBS, added into the lysate-antibody mixture, and maintained at 4 °C with rotation for 2 h. Subsequently, the precipitants were washed 3 times with pre-cold PBS, and the immune complexes were eluted with 2 × loading buffer containing 1% SDS for 5 min at 95 °C. The samples were subjected to Western blotting analysis.

Colony formation assay

IRP2 KO and AMPK KO cells were digested with trypsin, and 200 cells per well were plated into 12-well plates. Cells were cultured for 12 days with or without indicated treatment. The medium was changed every 2 days until the colonies were visible. The colonies were fixed with DMEM containing 4% paraformaldehyde at 37 °C for 15 min and stained with 0.04% crystal violet-2% ethanol in PBS at room temperature for 30 min. Images were captured and colony number was counted.

Statistical analysis

For quantitative analysis presented as histograms, values were obtained from at least three independent experiments and are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Statistical significance between two groups was determined by an unpaired Student’s t test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism eight software.
