Left Atrial Appendage Closure and Direct Oral Anticoagulation
Authors: Thomas A. Boyle, David J. Callans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Before interpreting any stroke prevention trial, clinicians must first decide what stroke and bleeding risks they consider clinically meaningful. Neither statisticians nor regulators can answer that question. Without contextualizing clinically meaningful differences, event rates and noninferiority margins are little more than abstractions.

Clinicians who treat atrial fibrillation (AF) have been through this exercise before. Modern anticoagulation has been remarkably successful, but not without its trade-offs. In the now historical primary prevention warfarin trials, patients treated with placebo experienced stroke rates of 4.5% per year compared to 1.4% with warfarin.1 The pooled absolute risk reduction of 3.1% per year and relative risk (RR) reduction of 68% were transformational. They were also compelling enough to convince countless individuals with AF to take a nightly pill, monitor their therapeutic levels with invasive testing, modify their diets, and live with increased bruising and bleeding. As it turns out, many people will go to great lengths to reduce their risk of stroke.

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) subsequently improved upon warfarin. Trials demonstrated significantly reduced stroke or systemic embolism compared to warfarin (RR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.73-0.91), and even reduced all-cause mortality (RR: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.85-0.95).2 These data, along with convenience, have given DOACs a clearly favored position in guidelines and contemporary clinical practice. Nevertheless, warfarin remains in use for nonvalvular AF because of lower cost and, presumably, because some consider its shortcomings clinically modest.3

In this issue of JACC: Advances, Chan et al4 present a meta-analysis of 4 randomized trials comparing left atrial appendage closure (LAAC) to DOAC therapy in patients with AF. The analysis includes 5,890 patients enrolled in CHAMPION-AF (Left Atrial Appendage Closure or Anticoagulation for Atrial Fibrillation), CLOSURE-AF (Left Atrial Appendage Closure or Medical Therapy in Atrial Fibrillation), OPTION (Comparison of Anticoagulation with Left Atrial Appendage Closure after AF Ablation), and PRAGUE-17 (Left Atrial Appendage Closure vs Novel Anticoagulation Agents in Atrial Fibrillation) and represents the most comprehensive randomized comparison of these treatment strategies to date.5, 6, 7, 8 Although differences in patient populations, devices, and postprocedural antithrombotic regimens introduce heterogeneity, the availability of nearly 6,000 randomized patients provides an important opportunity to examine the overall balance of efficacy and safety.

The pooled efficacy results consistently favored DOAC for stroke prevention. There were 103 strokes in patients treated with LAAC compared to 86 with DOAC, corresponding to a RR of 1.19 (95% CI: 0.90-1.58). There were 85 ischemic strokes with LAAC compared to 62 with DOAC (RR: 1.36; 95% CI: 0.99-1.89). One way to interpret these efficacy results is that they would fail most reasonable noninferiority margins, in which the crucial statistic is the upper bound of the 95% CI. Historically, the major DOAC trials established noninferiority margins of approximately 1.4 when comparing DOACs with warfarin. Viewed through that lens, the pooled estimates do not support a claim of equivalent stroke protection. They allow for the possibility of substantially worse efficacy. In fact, after excluding the OPTION trial, which enrolled a uniquely low-risk population post-ablation, LAAC was associated with a significantly higher rate of ischemic stroke (RR: 1.45; 95% CI: 1.03-2.06).

The pooled safety results consistently favored LAAC for nonmajor bleeding—and for major bleeding when procedural bleeding is excluded. Nonmajor bleeding was reduced by nearly half, from 12.8% to 7.0% (RR: 0.53; 95% CI: 0.4-0.71). The absolute reduction is substantial and these outcomes matter to patients. Recurrent epistaxis, bruising, gastrointestinal bleeding, medication interruptions, hospital visits, and anticoagulation anxiety all contribute meaningfully to quality of life. However, the bleeding story warrants careful examination. The reduction in bleeding was not accompanied by significant reductions in overall major bleeding or intracranial hemorrhage, the events most feared by patients and prescribers. This should not be surprising. Some bleeding is attributable to patient factors rather than medication or procedure exposure. This is illustrated by the Low-Dose Edoxaban in Very Elderly Patients with Atrial Fibrillation (ELDERCARE-AF) trial9 of low-dose edoxaban vs placebo in high-risk patients, which demonstrated 45% per year rate of all bleeding and 1.8% per year major bleeding in the placebo arm. With LAAC, patients are subjected to their baseline bleeding risk, procedural risk, and additional ongoing risk of antithrombotic therapy, usually with aspirin.

In summary, these trials showed low overall stroke rates with modern therapy: 3.5% in these trial patients treated with LAAC and 2.9% with DOAC. According to the CIs, LAAC could be similar or as much as 58% worse than DOAC therapy for preventing overall stroke and 89% worse for preventing ischemic stroke. The corresponding benefit for these patients was a pooled 5.8% absolute reduction in nonmajor bleeding. That is, perhaps, a reasonable trade-off in a “shared decision-making” framework. However, that discussion must include several other data points that were not included in this meta-analysis. Namely, 1% to 3% rates of device leaks > 3 mm, 1% to 4% rates of device-related thrombus, and 2% to 6% rates of serious procedure- or device-related adverse events in these 4 highly controlled trials. Device leaks and thrombi may directly increase stroke risk in the device population. Even if they do not, they represent novel medical issues that require monitoring or treatment. In this way, LAAC introduces procedure- and device-specific risks that can be mitigated but never entirely eliminated.

Despite frequent appeals to careful patient selection, the subgroup analyses in this meta-analysis failed to identify significant subgroup interactions. The exception was that the bleeding benefit of LAAC was more pronounced in male patients, though female patients were relatively under-represented. The hope that increasingly sophisticated patient selection will reveal a subgroup in whom LAAC is unequivocally superior remains largely aspirational. At present, the characteristics that most strongly support LAAC use are those related to intolerance of long-term anticoagulation. Of course, there are likely patients in this category for whom the best option is to avoid intervention altogether.

Chan and colleagues have assembled a comprehensive randomized comparison of LAAC and DOAC therapy currently available. Their findings reinforce an important principle that has occasionally been obscured by enthusiasm for device-based stroke prevention: LAAC and anticoagulation are not interchangeable therapies. Patients choosing LAAC de-escalate their long-term antithrombotic therapies and experience less nonmajor bleeding, potentially at the cost of reduced protection from stroke. For patients with certain bleeding risk profiles or preferences, the trade-off may be appropriate. For the larger population of patients who tolerate DOAC therapy well, however, the accumulated randomized evidence continues to support anticoagulation as the gold standard.
