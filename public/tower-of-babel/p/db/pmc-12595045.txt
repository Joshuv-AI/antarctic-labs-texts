Comparative analysis of geometry, relative stabilities, optoelectronic and thermochemical properties of [CuX+2]λ and [CuX-Ag-Au]λ nanoalloy clusters (λ = 0, ± 1; X = 1 − 13): a DFT approach
Authors: Shayeri Das, Prabhat Ranjan, Tanmoy Chakraborty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA comparative study of geometry, relative stabilities, optoelectronic and thermochemical properties of [CuX-Ag-Au]λ and [CuX+2]λ (λ = 0, ± 1; X = 1 − 13) nanoalloy clusters is performed by using density functional theory (DFT) technique. The ground state configuration as well as low lying isomers of these clusters are analyzed. Binding energies, fragmentation energies and second-order difference in energies of these clusters show an odd-even alteration behavior with the size of the cluster. Result exhibits that incorporation of Ag and Au atoms in [CuX+2] enhances the energy gap of overall system in most of the cases. Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital (HOMO) - Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital (LUMO) energy gap of [CuX+2] and [CuX-Ag-Au] are found between 0.870 and 3.490 eV and 1.237–3.196 eV respectively. The result shows that HOMO-LUMO energy gap of [CuX-Ag-Au] clusters are in the optimum range needed for photovoltaic devices, it signifies that these clusters may be considered as potential candidate for optoelectronic and photovoltaic devices. Data reveals that [CuX-Ag-Au] at X = 4 show maximum value of HOMO-LUMO gap, Vertical Ionization Energy (VIE), and hardness whereas minimum value of softness. However, at X = 13 it has demonstrated minimum value of HOMO-LUMO gap, and hardness whereas maximum value of softness and electrophilicity index. It is observed that [Cu14] and [Cu11-Ag-Au] clusters have significant electron infusion abilities due to large Vertical Electron Affinity (VEA) value. Optical properties – optical electronegativity, refractive index, dielectric constant as well as infrared and Raman spectra of these clusters are analyzed. For [CuX+2], minimum and maximum refractive index is found in case of [Cu10] and [Cu14] respectively, whereas for trimetallic clusters, [Cu13-Ag-Au] and [Cu4-Ag-Au] show the maximum and minimum refractive index. Thermochemical properties like thermal energy, heat capacity, zero-point correction and entropy of these systems increase with the cluster size, whereas Gibbs free energy follows reverse pattern.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23559-x.Keywords: Density functional theory, Trimetallic nanoalloy clusters, Cu4-Ag-Au, HOMO-LUMO gap, Photovoltaic devicesSubject terms: Chemical physics, Condensed-matter physics, Theoretical chemistry, Condensed-matter physics, Theory and computation

Introduction

Recent years have seen a significant increase in study on nanoalloys clusters because of the multitude and intricate nature of their size, shape, composition and physico-chemical characteristics1. Since the nano-dimension and amalgamation impacts have enriched the chemical and physical properties, the effectiveness of the nanoalloys for their extensive applications in catalytic processes, optical technology, nanoelectronics, data retention and sensing devices, hydrogen generation and production as well as quantum medicine dependent on it2. Thus, comprehending the geometry and physico-chemical properties of nanoalloys is vital for building up new intriguing possibilities for both present and future technological uses3. Clusters differ significantly from solid-state materials, existing as collections of nanoscale dimensions and occupying a middle ground among molecules as well as bulk substances. They demonstrate a variety of distinctive physical and chemical characteristics encompassing structural, electronic, and thermodynamic aspects. Metallic clusters have garnered substantial attention in research endeavors. Their diverse applications in fields like biology, catalysis, and semiconductors have spurred significant advancements in both experimental and theoretical studies focused on clusters4–9. Prior to the potential usage of metal nanoclusters in prospective nano-technological applications, it can be crucial to comprehend the complex relationship among the atomic and electrical frameworks4–9. The evolution of cluster attributes from small to large clusters, for example, is one of the most fascinating issues in this respect9. Understanding further general cluster features begins with comprehensive structural analysis. The lowest energy structure of transition metal clusters is hence the focus of considerable study8–13. However, analysis of equilibrium geometries, and of atomic arrangements and configurations in transition metal clusters, remains a challenging task.

In the last few years, metallic clusters have shown considerable applications in optoelectronic, magnetic and photovoltaic devices14–19. Due to the ease with which functional nanoparticles of industrial significance are being developed, mono-metallic and bimetallic nanoalloy clusters have currently been at the forefront of scientific research2,20–22. As a result, a wide range of prospective technological applications have been envisaged23–25. Additionally, studies have demonstrated the feasibility of modulating their physico-chemical features for specific uses, including healthcare applications, catalytic activities, and various commercial uses in alimentary and semiconductor sectors, such as metallic semiconductor-based nano-contacts26–30. The relationship between the dimensions, shape and composition of nanoalloy clusters and their physico-chemical characteristics make structure-based investigations an inevitable topic in materials science31. When bimetallic or trimetallic clusters consist of two or more chemical substances, the bigger clusters exhibit a high divergence of topologies that correspond to crystalline and non-crystalline encapsulating patterns32. In comparison to similar mono-metallic and bimetallic clusters, it is established that trimetallic clusters have substantially greater catalytic effectiveness and sensitivity33,34. Furthermore, the dimension, conformation, and molecular arrangement of trimetallic clusters can be employed to alter their physical and chemical characteristics35.

Polak and Rubinovich36,37 reported ternary nano clusters Ni-Cu-Al, Ni-Cu-Rh and Ni-Cu-Pd by using free-energy concentration expansion techniques. They found that a significant order-disorder shift develops at high temperature, contingent on the dimension, geometry and mixing of the cluster, because of the intense Ni-Al correlations that increase the chemical enforcement into the cluster center. The overall form of the computed free-energy interfaces suggests that the alloying characteristics of ternary alloy clusters are substantially distinct from those of the primary alloys. Toshima et al.33 reported Pd-Ag-Rh nanoparticles through experimental technique and observed that the hydrogenation of olefin is catalyzed by these trimetallic systems with greater precision than by comparable monometallic and bimetallic systems. Zhang et al.34 investigated Pt-Ru-Co nanoparticles using experimental technique. Authors reported that in comparison to the bimetallic system Pt-Ru, trimetallic system Pt-Ru-Co possess greater catalytic behavior against methanol degradation. Fang et al.38 studied Au-Pd-Pt nanoparticles through experimental and density functional theory (DFT) method. Authors reported that trimetallic substances are particularly well-suited for catalytic processes due to their topology and electronic behaviors. They also highlighted the utilizations of these trimetallic nanoparticles in detectors, hydrogen production, and catalysis. Trimetallic clusters PtxRuyMz (M = Fe, Ni, Cu, Mo; x + y + z = 4) and its interaction with oxygen atoms are investigated by using DFT method39. It is found that trimetallic cluster Pt2RuM possess greater catalytic abilities for oxygen dissociation, and the response constraints reveal that trimetallic clusters exhibit more substantial catalyst efficacy than bimetallic clusters. Khanal et al.40 investigated Ag-Pd-Pt nanoparticles through experimental and theoretical technique. Authors observed that silver atoms are easily segregated from the surface and Ag and Pt atoms are alloyed in the outermost area, which influences stability and ability to catalyze. Liu et al.41 reported trimetallic Pt-Pd-Rh system by using genetic algorithms and found that Rh atoms likely to congregate inside, whereas Pt and Pd atoms choose to segregate on their outermost layers. Pd atoms show superior surface segmentation in comparison with Pt atoms. Dzhurakhalov et al.42 reported Ag-Cu-Co nanoparticles using Monte Carlo technique and found that the depth of the silver layer impacts the arrangement of cobalt and copper in the center of trimetallic clusters, where silver consistently constitutes the surface layer. Pacheco-Contreras et al.43 reported ternary cluster AglAumPtn, where l + m + n = 13, 19, 33 and 38 using theoretical approach. Authors observed that fragmentation exhibits dimension-dependent activities, having a distinctive shift from combined to segmented complexes at 38-atoms clusters dimension. Guzman-Ramirez et al.44 studied physico-chemical properties of FexCoyNiz where x + y + z = 7 through DFT technique. Zhang et al.45 investigated FeAlAun, n = 1–6 using DFT method and found that the relative binding energy of FeAlAun is high compared to Aun+2, which suggests that incorporation of Fe and Al atoms may enhance the stability of gold clusters. Tao et al.46 studied trimetallic Pt-X-Cu system (X = Au, Ag, Pd and Rh) using theoretical approach. Wang et al.47 reported Pt-Pd-Cu nanoalloys through DFT technique. Du et al.48 investigated Ag-Pd-Pt and Au-Pd-Pt clusters using DFT and found that Ag-Pd-Pt clusters have more diverse geometrical arrangements in comparison with Au-Pd-Pt. Taran and his group3,49–51 studied several trimetallic nanoalloys like Cu-Au-Pt, Pd-Ag-Pt and Pt-Ni-Ag by using DFT method.

Studies on noble metallic clusters, especially on mixed clusters of Cu, Ag and Au have received considerable attention in materials science community52–55. These nanoalloy clusters are highly effective for a variety of applications because of their intriguing optical, electronic, magnetic, catalysts and electrochemical capabilities56–64. It is feasible to enhance the distinct features of these metallic clusters via changes to their atomic nature, geometry, configuration and ligands protection55,65–67. Wu et al.68 reported Cu-Ag-Au to investigate the geometric structures. They found that copper and silver atoms tend to prefer positions in the inner layers and on the surface of clusters, respectively. Meanwhile, gold atoms showed a preference for the middle layers and tended to mix with Cu and Ag atoms. It is useful for understanding the geometric characteristics of these clusters and their potential implications for cluster stability, reactivity, and electronic properties. Chen et al.69 performed a study on photo luminescent metal organic compounds and reported that the structures exhibit a unique 3D framework. Cheng et al.35 investigated Ag-Cu-Au clusters through Monte Carlo simulation technique and found that the segregation phenomena with respect to the atoms is mainly due to their variable surface energies. Authors also observed that the bulk and composition have insignificant consequence on the segregation phenomenon. Posada-Amarillas et al.31 studied CulAgmAun (l + m + n = 6) clusters by using DFT technique. They found that the presence of such structures can be explained through orbital hybridization and bonding orbitals. The study predicted that stable structures would display variations in the HOMO-LUMO (Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital - Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital) gap based on the composition of the cluster. From the above literature, it is observed that the study on trimetallic clusters mainly Cu-Ag-Au is limited and as per our knowledge thermochemical properties of trimetallic clusters as well as cationic and anionic state for Cu-Ag-Au by using DFT approach is not yet reported.

In this report, geometry, optoelectronic and thermochemical characteristics of trimetallic nanoalloy clusters [CuX-Ag-Au]λ (X = 1–13, λ = 0, ± 1) have been studied using DFT approach. The computed results of [CuX-Ag-Au]λ are compared with the [CuX+2]λ and discussed in detail. Relative stabilities of these nanoalloy clusters are also calculated. All the optimized geometry shows positive harmonic frequency. The result shows that these trimetallic clusters may be suitable for optoelectronic and photovoltaic devices.

Computational technique

Density Functional Theory (DFT) is the most prevalent computational technique to understand the reactivity, optoelectronic as well as other physico-chemical characteristics of bimetallic and trimetallic nanoalloy clusters70. DFT has evolved into more and more prevalent nowadays to quantify the electrical characteristics of clusters, especially for use in optoelectronic and photovoltaic systems71–80. In the present report, [CuX-Ag-Au]λ (X = 1–13, λ = 0, ± 1) trimetallic nanoalloy clusters are studied using DFT technique. Computational software Gaussian 1681 is used for the optimization of these trimetallic nanoalloy clusters. Exchange correlation Becke, three parameter, Lee-Yang-Parr (B3LYP) and basis set LANL2DZ is selected for geometry optimization. Although LANL2DZ is economical, it may produce some inaccurate findings for some parameters like bond energies and energetics of reaction phase. Nevertheless, if particular basis sets are applied, most of the parameters may be calculated precisely82–85. The physical and chemical characteristics of trimetallic clusters are efficiently studied by researchers using B3LYP/LANL2DZ in the literature39,86–89. Density of States, IR and Raman spectra of these systems are obtained using GaussSum90.

Results and discussions

Equilibrium structure

In this section, equilibrium geometries of [CuX-Ag-Au]λ (X = 1–13, λ = 0, ± 1) trimetallic nanoalloy clusters are discussed. All the optimized structures of these trimetallic clusters exhibit positive harmonic frequency. The pursuit for the ground state configuration of these trimetallic clusters involves optimizing a number of isomers. Structure of lowest energy of [CuX+2]λ (λ = 0, ± 1; X = 1 − 13) are reported in Supplementary Information (SI) Figure- S1, S2, and S3. The optimized low-lying isomers of pure Cu clusters are in line with the previous report104. Coordinates (X, Y, Z) of ground state configuration of [CuX+2]λ and [CuX-Ag-Au]λ (λ = 0; X = 1 − 13) are also presented in SI -Table S1 and Table S2. There are a number of reports in which genetic stochastic search techniques105–110 as well as global search algorithms111–113 is presented to find the lowest energy structure of clusters. In this work, for the optimization of [CuX-Ag-Au]λ first ground state configurations of [CuX+2]λ by using functional B3LYP/LANL2DZ within DFT framework is optimized. After obtaining the ground state configurations of [CuX+2]λ, one Cu atom is replaced with Ag and second Cu atom is replaced with Au atom to form [CuX-Ag-Au]λ. These Ag and Au atoms are placed on all possible sites of parent [CuX+2]λ system to obtain the lowest energy structure of [CuX-Ag-Au]λ.

[CuX-Ag-Au]λ (X = 1–13, λ = 0)

The pursuit for the ground state configuration of [CuX-Ag-Au] (X = 1–13) trimetallic clusters involves optimizing a number of isomers however, only four lowest energy geometries for each system is presented in Figure 1. The four most stable isomers for neutral state of X = 1 are observed between 0 and 0.415 eV and have doublet spin. The ground state configuration (N-1-a) shows triangular geometry with Cs symmetry. The other three low-lying isomers are of linear structure having C∞ v symmetry. The difference in optimization energies of structures N-1-b and N-1-c is found as 0.015 eV. In case of neutral state of X = 2, ground state configuration i.e. N-2-a is observed as a quadrilateral structure with C2v symmetry. N-2-b is obtained after replacing the location of Cu and Ag in N-2-a. However, the optimization energy of N-2-b is 0.271 eV higher than N-2-a. Isomers N-2-c and N-2-d are obtained from N-1-a by adding one Cu atom and shifting the position of Ag and Au atoms. As compared to N-2-a, N-2-c and N-2-d show high energy levels as 0.574 eV and 0.641 eV respectively. It is noticed that the energy variance between N-2-c and N-2-d is only 0.067 eV. All the isomers for X = 2 possess singlet spin. Lowest energy geometry as well as low lying isomers of X = 3 are shown in N-3-a to N-3-d. The most stable structure bears CS spin symmetry and doublet spin forming a trapezium shape. The second low-lying structure with C1 spin symmetry and doublet spin has a butterfly shape with energy difference of 0.171 eV with ground state. N-3-c is obtained from N-3-a after exchanging the position of Cu and Au atom. N-3-d is designed from N-2-a after the addition of one Cu atom and shifting the position of Au. It has quadrilateral geometry. N-3-c and N-3-d have an energy difference of only 0.063 eV. The three most stable isomers for X = 4 are found from N-3-a.

The ground state as well as three most stable low-lying isomers for X = 10 in the energy range of 0 to 0.241 eV. [CuX-Ag-Au]λ (X = 1–13, λ= +1)

The ground-state structure of cationic [CuX-Ag-Au]λ (X = 1–13, λ= +1) nanoalloy clusters are shown in SI- Figure S4. The cationic structure for X = 1 displays a triangular structure with Cs symmetry as well as singlet spin. Lowest energy structure for X = 2 have a quadrilateral ground-state structure with C₂v symmetry and doublet spin. It is obtained from N-2-a through switching the location of Cu and Ag. For [Cu3-Ag-Au]+1, N-3-c is observed as the ground state configuration. It has Cs symmetry and singlet spin. In case of X = 4, neutral and cationic state has same geometry. For X = 5, N-5-b is attained as the lowest energy structure for cationic state. In case of X = 6 to 10 and 12, ground state configuration of cationic state of these clusters has similar configuration as that of neutral clusters. For X = 11, third most stable cluster (N-11-c) of neutral state is observed as lowest energy structure of cationic state. Similarly, for X = 13, second most stable system (N = 13-b) is observed as the lowest energy geometry for cationic state.

[CuX-Ag-Au]λ (X = 1–13, λ= −1)

The ground-state structures of anionic [CuX-Ag-Au]λ (X = 1–13, λ= −1) nanoalloy clusters are shown in SI-Figure S5. For X = 1, it adopts a triangular structure with Cs symmetry and singlet spin. Anionic X = 2 has a quadrilateral ground-state structure with Cu atoms placed diagonally, while X = 3 forms a trapezium-shaped with Ag and Au placed at base vertices. The most stable structure of X = 4 is pyramidal, a modification of ground state configuration of X = 3. In case of X = 5, lowest energy geometry has a hexagonal structure with Ag and Au atoms placed at opposite vertices, while ground state structure of anionic X = 6 is same as N-6-b with C₁ symmetry and doublet spin. The lowest energy structure of anionic X = 7 is obtained from X = 6 structure by addition of one Cu atom and changing the position of Ag atom. It has C₁ symmetry and singlet spin. Further modification of the anionic X = 7 structure, with an additional Cu atom and variation in Au and Ag position, results in the ground-state structure of anionic X = 8, which shares a similar symmetry but possesses a doublet spin. For anionic X = 9, the ground-state structure is three-dimensional geometry, with C₁ symmetry and singlet spin. Anionic X = 10 exhibits a planar configuration with double hexagons, while anionic X = 11 has a non-planar ground-state structure with C₁ symmetry and singlet spin. In the case of anionic X = 12, the ground-state structure is non-planar, with C₁ symmetry and doublet spin. Lastly, anionic X = 13 presents a planar structure with C₁ symmetry and singlet spin.

Fragmentation energies and Second-order difference in energies of [CuX+2]λ and [CuX-Ag-Au]λ for λ = 0 exhibit an intriguing odd-even alternation pattern. Results suggest that clusters with an even number of atoms are more stable in comparison with their neighbor clusters consist of odd number of atoms. In case of [CuX-Ag-Au] clusters, maximum fragmentation energies and Second-order difference in energies is observed at X = 6. It indicates that Cu6-Ag-Au is thermodynamically stable as compared to other clusters considered in this study. As a result, Cu6-Ag-Au stability might be an indicative of a prevailing structural impact, where the quality of molecules rather than electrons determines stability. It shows that this cluster may create an efficient, symmetrical structure that increases stability115.

HOMO-LUMO gaps and DFT based descriptors

The HOMO-LUMO energy gap is a crucial factor for investigating the electronic characteristics of metallic nanoalloy clusters. It signifies the least amount of energy needed for an electron to transit from an occupied to an unoccupied orbital. Clusters chemical stability is strongly influenced by HOMO-LUMO gap, where clusters with smaller gaps signify a higher sensitivity to interference from the outside. The HOMO-LUMO gap of [CuX+2]λ and [CuX-Ag-Au]λ for λ = 0 are presented in Table-1, whereas for λ= +1 and − 1, it is listed in SI- Table S3. It is noted that nanoalloy clusters consist of even number of total atoms in system are more stable and have a larger energy gap in comparison with their corresponding adjacent systems with an odd number of total atoms. While the exception for [CuX+2] is noted at X = 10 and 12, and in case of [CuX-Ag-Au] exception is observed at X = 10. It is noted that incorporation of Ag and Au atoms in pure [CuX+2] nanoalloy clusters enhances the energy gap of clusters in most of the cases.

In case of neutral clusters of Cux-Ag-Au, HOMO-LUMO gap varies from 1.237 eV to 3.196 eV. Maximum and minimum value of HOMO-LUMO gap for neutral clusters occurs at X = 4 and X = 13 respectively. For cationic state of [Cux-Ag-Au]+, HOMO-LUMO gap fluctuates between 1.307 eV to 3.140 eV. Maximum and minimum value of HOMO-LUMO gap for cationic clusters occurs at X = 6 and X = 13 respectively. For anionic state of [Cux-Ag-Au]−, HOMO-LUMO gap fluctuates between 0.074 eV to 2.472 eV. Maximum and minimum value of HOMO-LUMO gap for anionic clusters occurs at X = 1 and X = 5 respectively. HOMO-LUMO gap is directly associated with the stability of clusters. It is reported that clusters with maximum HOMO-LUMO gap exhibit maximum stability and vice-versa. Among the studied trimetallic clusters, maximum stability is observed for [Cu4-Ag-Au], [Cu6-Ag-Au]+, and [Cu-Ag-Au]−. In contrast to their comparable trimetallic clusters [CuX-Ag-Au], the HOMO-LUMO energy gap of [CuX+2] is shown to be high for X = 4, 8, 9, 11 and 13. In case of [CuX+2] maximum and minimum energy gap is observed at X = 8 and 12 respectively. Balbuena et al.116 reported HOMO-LUMO energy gap of Cu5, Cu7, Cu8 and Cu11 as 2.49 eV, 1.05 eV, 2.049 eV and 1.45 eV respectively. Posada-Amarillas et al.31 reported HOMO-LUMO energy gap of Cu6 and Cu4AgAu as 1.888 and 1.834 eV respectively.

In case of CuAg, the reported HOMO-LUMO gap is 3 eV117, incorporation of Au atom in CuAg decreases the gap considerably to 1.675 eV, making the cluster more suitable for solar cell applications. The ideal photovoltaic materials have an energy gap between 1.0 and 1.8 eV118. Dipole moment (in Debye) of these clusters for neutral systems is presented in Table 2. In case of [CuX-Ag-Au] maximum and minimum dipole moment is observed at X = 4 and 13 respectively, which is in the similar line with the HOMO-LUMO energy gap.

Conclusion

Structure, relative stabilities, optoelectronic as well as thermochemical characteristics of [CuX+2]λ and [CuX-Ag-Au]λ (λ = 0, ± 1; X = 1 − 13), nanoalloy clusters are investigated through DFT approach. Results of these systems are outlined as:

Ground state as well as low lying isomers of [CuX+2]λ and [CuX-Ag-Au]λ (λ = 0, ± 1; X = 1 − 13) nanoalloy clusters are studied and discussed.

Ground state as well as low lying isomers of [CuX+2]λ and [CuX-Ag-Au]λ (λ = 0, ± 1; X = 1 − 13) nanoalloy clusters are studied and discussed.

Relative stabilities - binding energies, fragmentation energies, as well as second-order differences in energies of [CuX+2]λ and [CuX-Ag-Au]λ are computed. It is found that binding energy of [CuX-Ag-Au] shows an odd-even fluctuation pattern with the value of X. In contrast to its adjacent system with an even number of X, data shows that trimetallic clusters [CuX-Ag-Au] exhibits a low binding energy value for odd values of X, nevertheless, a notable exception is noted for X = 9 and 11. The binding energy of [CuX-Ag-Au] is found high in comparison with their corresponding [CuX+2] clusters.

Results suggest that clusters with an even number of atoms have higher fragmentation energy values in comparison with their neighbor clusters consist of odd number of atoms.
