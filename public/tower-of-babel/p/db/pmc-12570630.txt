Simultaneous pulmonary and intracardiac bone cement embolism after vertebroplasty: a case report and literature review
Authors: Jiaqi Pu, Jian-Qing He, Qun Yi, Haixia Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary embolism (PE) is a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Pulmonary cement embolism (PCE), a rare form of PE, typically arises from bone cement leakage, a recognized complication of percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) and kyphoplasty. To reach the pulmonary artery, cement fragments must first pass through the heart cavities, where some fragments may remain, resulting in intracardiac cement embolism (ICE). Due to its subtle clinical presentation, PCE and ICE may go undetected by clinicians, posing a serious threat to patient safety.Case reportWe present a rare case of PCE and ICE following PVP caused by bone cement leakage. A 52-year-old woman presented to West China Hospital of Sichuan University with symptoms of dyspnea and chest tightness. She had a history of undergoing PVP for vertebral fracture (L4). PCE and ICE were confirmed through computed tomography pulmonary angiography and echocardiogram. The patient underwent open-heart surgery and showed a good recovery after a six-month follow-up.ConclusionsThis case highlights the crucial importance of clinical vigilance for both pulmonary and intracardiac cement embolism in patients presenting with respiratory or cardiac symptoms following vertebroplasty. Our experience demonstrates the essential role of prompt diagnosis through CTPA and echocardiography, while confirming that open-heart surgery can effectively manage life-threatening intracardiac cement emboli. These findings emphasize the value of systematic post-procedural monitoring when cement embolism is suspected, providing important clinical insights for managing this rare but serious complication.

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, with an estimated annual incidence of 60–120 cases per 100,000 population in the United States, resulting in 60,000–100,000 deaths each year [1]. Although most cases originate from deep venous thrombosis (DVT), the expanding use of vertebral augmentation procedures has led to increased recognition of non-thrombotic emboli, particularly pulmonary cement embolism (PCE) [2]. PCE has become a well-documented complication. In contrast, the concurrent occurrence of PCE with intracardiac cement embolism (ICE) represents a distinctly rare phenomenon, with only sporadic case reports documenting this dual embolism pattern [3, 4].

PCE is closely associated with kyphoplasty and percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP), a minimally invasive and widely used technique for treating osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF) and osteolytic spinal metastases (OSM) [5]. Bone cement leakage leading to PCE is a recognized complication of PVP and kyphoplasty. To reach the pulmonary artery, cement fragments must first pass through the heart cavities, where some fragments may remain, resulting in ICE in addition to PCE [6]. Although ICE and PCE are potentially serious complications of PVP and kyphoplasty, they are often overlooked during diagnosis due to their relatively low incidence [7]. Reports of pulmonary and intracardiac embolism caused by bone cement leakage are relatively uncommon. In this report, we present a rare case of PCE and ICE following PVP and provide a literature review to explore the clinical characteristics and strategies for managing this condition (Table 1).

Case report

A 52-year-old woman presented to the chest pain center of the emergency department at West China Hospital of Sichuan University, complaining of dyspnea and chest tightness. Physical examination revealed a conscious and alert patient. Her vital signs were as follows: blood pressure 170/99 mmHg, heart rate 78 beats per minute, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute, and body temperature 36.7 °C. Cardiovascular examination indicated a regular heart rate with no murmurs. Respiratory examination revealed no dry or moist rales in either lung, and no edema was observed in the lower limbs. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable. In terms of the medical history, during the PVP procedure for L4 vertebral fracture performed at a local hospital on the previous day, the patient developed acute-onset dyspnea immediately following bone cement injection, which persisted until the current presentation.

Laboratory examination revealed a positive D-dimer (4.84 mg/L FEU) with an elevated fibrinogen equivalent unit (FEU) of 4.05 mg/L. Arterial blood gas analysis, performed while the patient was breathing 33% oxygen through a nasal cannula, showed mild hypoxemia (pO₂ 85.5 mmHg, pCO₂ 30.6 mmHg, SaO₂ 95%). Creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) was mildly elevated at 3.56 ng/ml (reference value ≤ 2.88 ng/ml), and cardiac troponin T (cTNT) was 20.2 ng/L (reference range 0–14 ng/L) (Table 2), while brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) was within the normal range. A 12-lead electrocardiogram was normal, and other laboratory parameters, including hemoglobin, white blood cell count, platelet count, liver function, and kidney function were all within normal limits.

Based on the patient’s symptoms and the results of physical and laboratory examinations, PE was suspected by the attending doctor in the emergency department and computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) and an echocardiogram were subsequently arranged for the patient. Multiplanar reconstructed images of CTPA shows high-density foreign material in the segmental and subsegmental branches of pulmonary artery (Fig. 1). Axial view of non-contrast Chest CT (mediastinal window) shows high-density foreign material. in the branches of the Left Descending Pulmonary artery and right ventricle (with adjacent artifacts due to high density material in right ventricle) (Fig. 2). CTPA reformatted images in maximum intensity projection shows high-density foreign material in the branches of the pulmonary artery and the right ventricle (Fig. 3). The transthoracic echocardiogram (Fig. 4) suggested multiple hyperechoic masses (likely bone cement; largest 10 mm) adherent to the tricuspid valve chordae in the RV, with preserved valve function and no RV strain. Pulmonary outflow is unobstructed, and biventricular function remains normal. Currently hemodynamically stable. CTPA and echocardiography demonstrated no evidence of concomitant thrombus formation adjacent to the cement emboli or within the pulmonary vasculature. Based on the history of PVP surgery and the above findings, a diagnosis of PCE and ICE was confirmed. After immediate consultation with doctors from the Department of Cardiovascular Surgery, an immediate open-heart surgery to remove the bone cement was suggested and agreed upon by the patient.

The patient underwent cardiopulmonary bypass-assisted intracardiac foreign body removal under general anesthesia. Surgical findings confirmed the preoperative echocardiographic identification of a 4 cm hardened object adherent to the tricuspid valve chordae tendineae within the right ventricle, consistent with bone cement. Approximately 80 mL of pale yellow pericardial fluid was noted upon opening the pericardium. The procedure followed standard cardiac surgical protocol: systemic heparinization was achieved, followed by aortic and bicaval cannulation to establish extracorporeal circulation. After aortic cross-clamping and delivery of cardioplegia, a right atriotomy provided direct visualization of the foreign body. The cement fragment was carefully extracted while preserving the native valve apparatus. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography confirmed complete removal of the foreign material and demonstrated preserved tricuspid valve function without significant regurgitation. The patient was successfully weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass with stable hemodynamics and maintained sinus rhythm throughout the procedure. Immediate postoperative transthoracic echocardiography documented normal biventricular function (LVEF 66%) and the absence of intracardiac shunts or pericardial effusion.

The surgical procedure proceeded smoothly under general anesthesia and extracorporeal circulation. During the surgery, approximately 80 ml of pale yellow fluid was found in the pericardial cavity. Upon opening the right heart, a 4 cm-long strip of a hard substance was discovered in the right ventricle, attached to the chordae tendineae below the tricuspid valve. The foreign body was successfully removed. The patient’s symptoms of dyspnea and chest tightness were relieved after the surgery. During the six-month postoperative outpatient follow-up period, the patient exhibited complete resolution of both dyspnea and chest tightness. Serial echocardiographic examinations and CTPA revealed complete absence of residual emboli in the pulmonary arterial system and cardiac chambers. The patient maintained therapeutic anticoagulation with warfarin throughout the recovery period, demonstrating excellent clinical outcomes.

Discussion

While PE is widely recognized as life-threatening, rare etiologies like cement embolism (PCE) post-vertebroplasty are frequently overlooked [8]. Cement migration can cause simultaneous PCE and ICE - an exceptionally rare but fatal complication. Current evidence shows PCE incidence varies 1–25% [5, 7, 9] while ICE occurs in only 3.9% of cases, with merely 8.3% becoming symptomatic [10]. Our case of dual embolism not only exemplifies this rare phenomenon but more importantly demonstrates open-heart surgery’s efficacy as a life-saving intervention. Given the ongoing debates regarding pathogenesis and management of such cases, we conducted a systematic literature review of PCE/ICE following vertebral augmentation procedures to better characterize this clinical entity (Table 1).

Our analysis of 10 representative PCE/ICE cases [3, 4, 11–18] (Table 1) demonstrates consistent clinical patterns that contextualize our case findings. The reviewed cohort showed marked female predominance (90%, 9/10 [3, 4, 11–17] with ages spanning 52–96 years, where PVP accounted for 80% of causative procedures [4, 11–16, 18]. Clinically, chest pain emerged as the most frequent presenting symptom (50% of cases) [3, 4, 12, 16, 19], followed by dyspnea (30%) [3, 4, 11], while imaging confirmed pulmonary artery involvement in all cases [3, 4, 11–18] and concurrent cardiac embolism in 30% (3/10) [3, 4, 16]. Treatment approaches varied significantly based on embolism location and severity, ranging from conservative management (30%) [12, 14, 19] and anticoagulation (30%) [11, 13, 17] to surgical intervention (40%) [3, 4, 15, 16], with the single mortality case occurring in a patient presenting with acute respiratory distress syndrome managed surgically [15].

Our case provides three key novel insights to this field: First, it represents one of the few documented instances where cement emboli simultaneously occupied both the segmental pulmonary arteries and right ventricular cavity. Second, the acute symptom onset within minutes of cement injection reinforces the need for immediate intraoperative vigilance. Most significantly, our successful open-heart intervention challenges current conservative tendencies by demonstrating that surgical retrieval can achieve complete emboli removal and symptom resolution in such complex cases.

Anatomy plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of PCE and ICE. There are two main pathways by which PCE can form: cement leakage into the paravertebral venous system and the inferior vena cava system [20, 21]. Iwanaga et al. found that bone cement is more likely to be injected into the inferior vena cava system rather than into the vertebral venous plexus within the vertebral canal [21]. Once in the inferior vena cava, the cement can travel to the right ventricle, leading to PCE. However, the vertebral venous plexus (VVP) serves as an important route for S-type cement leakage [22]. This serpentine pathway through the valveless VVP, influenced by venous hemodynamics and patient positioning, may facilitate cement migration toward the central circulation. The anatomical pathways observed in our case align with current understanding of cement migration [21]. Intraoperative findings confirmed cement entry through the inferior vena cava system, consistent with previous report [21].

Previous studies have tried to explore the potential risk factors for PCE [20, 23]. In terms of age, findings have varied across different studies [7, 8, 24]. A prospective study reported a median age of 74.78 years (range 48–93), consistent with another retrospective study showing a median age of 72.1 years (range 61–94) [7, 24]. These findings are similar to ours. We speculate that this may be related to the increased susceptibility of older patients to compression fractures due to osteoporosis, leading to a higher likelihood of cement injections. Regarding gender, in the cases reviewed in our study, 9 out of 10 were female, it seems that women may be more prone to PCE [3, 4, 11–17]. Similarly, Schmidt et al. observed that 55.4% of patients in their cohort were female [8]. However, Sun et al. found that gender had no significant effect on PCE, despite the higher incidence of OVCF in older women [20]. This suggests that the higher proportion of female patients in our study may be attributed to the higher prevalence of osteoporosis among women, rather than an inherently higher incidence of PCE. For ICE, Fadili et al. [10]. found that 31.6% of ICE cases were located in the right atrium (RA) and 38% in the right ventricle (RV), indicating that ICE in the RV may be more common—a finding consistent with our case. Other contributing factors to PCE may include involvement of the thoracic vertebrae, higher cement volumes injected per level, treating more than three vertebrae per session, and venous cement leakage [20]. Surgeons should therefore carefully monitor the volume of bone cement injected during the procedure to minimize the risk of PCE and reduce mortality. Clinicians should not overlook the diagnosis of PCE in either male or female patients, especially those with PVP procedure-related risk factors.

The clinical manifestations of PCE vary widely [3, 11–14]. Some PCE patients exhibit symptoms of embolism [3, 14, 23], such as cough and chest pain, while others remain asymptomatic [7, 25]. The symptoms of PCE may result from several factors. First, pulmonary vascular occlusion can obstruct the local lobar branches of the lungs. Second, the allergic toxins released by polymethyl methacrylate, which was a common component of cement, may stimulate the release of histamine and activate coagulation factors, thereby damaging vascular endothelial cells and promoting thrombosis [15]. In the cases we reviewed [3, 4, 11–17, 19], imaging examinations, including CT/CTPA and echocardiography, were instrumental in confirming the diagnosis of PCE. Regarding the clinical manifestations of ICE, most patients were asymptomatic; however, symptomatic ICE cases tend to involve concomitant cement embolism in the pulmonary arteries [10]. ICE-related symptoms range from mild chest pain, syncope, or moderate dyspnea to life-threatening conditions such as acute respiratory distress and cardiac tamponade [26]. Therefore, when patients present with symptoms like cough and chest pain during or after a PVP procedure, imaging examinations should be considered to establish the diagnosis.

There are no clear guidelines for the treatment of PCE and ICE. For PCE, researchers have proposed three treatment options depending on the location of the embolism: observation with clinical follow-up, anticoagulation, or surgical embolectomy [7, 9, 16, 27]. Close observation is recommended for asymptomatic peripheral embolisms [7, 9], while anticoagulant therapy may be appropriate for symptomatic peripheral embolisms [27]. Mansour et al. suggested that all symptomatic patients should receive anticoagulation therapy [28]. Additionally, a meta-analysis recommended treating thrombotic pulmonary embolisms with initial intravenous heparin followed by six months of coumarin therapy [29]. In addition to coumarin derivatives, newer oral anticoagulants (NOACs) such as direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) may also be considered as potential treatment options for bone cement embolism [30]. However, the optimal dosage and duration of anticoagulant therapy remain uncertain and warrant further investigation. For symptomatic central embolisms, surgical embolectomy is suggested [3, 4, 16], since medicinal reopening of the occludes vessels seems to be impossible. However, the decision to proceed with surgery should be individualized, taking into account the patient’s baseline cardiopulmonary function, overall clinical stability, and personal preferences, while carefully weighing the risks (e.g., perioperative complications) and benefits (e.g., rapid revascularization) against alternative approaches, such as thrombolysis or anticoagulation alone. The optimal timing of intervention should also be considered, as delayed surgery may reduce efficacy, whereas early intervention in high-risk patients may carry greater procedural risks. Regarding ICE, conservative management is recommended for asymptomatic patients, while open-heart surgery is often indicated for patients with moderate to life-threatening symptoms, particularly in cases involving tricuspid valve injury or cardiac perforation [10, 29, 31], which was the situation in our case. Our case reinforces that surgical intervention becomes imperative when: (i) symptoms develop intraoperatively, (ii) emboli involve cardiac structures, and (iii) clinical deterioration occurs despite medical management. The comprehensive 6-month follow-up protocol we implemented detected no late complications, suggesting this may represent a good surveillance strategy.

The prognosis of PCE or ICE has been minimally reported in the literature. In the cases reviewed here [3, 4, 11–17, 19], only 1 out of 10 patients died [15], while two cases did not report clinical outcomes [13, 19], and the remaining patients survived [3, 4, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17]. One study in the general population found that PCE after PVP did not increase mortality risk compared to non-PCE patients [23]. Another autopsy study similarly demonstrated no association between PCE and mortality [18]. Additionally, case reports in our study suggest that patients with PCE tend to have favorable outcomes [3, 4, 11, 12, 16, 17]. Consistent with PCE, a retrospective study with a mean follow-up of 8.1 ± 19 months (range 0.1–108 months) found that most ICE cases had uneventful outcomes [10]. While these studies provide some evidence regarding the relationship between PCE or ICE and clinical outcomes, it is important to note that the existing literature on this topic remains limited. Further research and long-term follow-up are needed to better understand the prognosis of PCE and/or ICE.

Conclusions

This case highlights the crucial importance of clinical vigilance for both pulmonary and intracardiac cement embolism in patients presenting with respiratory or cardiac symptoms following vertebroplasty. Our experience demonstrates the essential role of prompt diagnosis through CTPA and echocardiography, while confirming that open-heart surgery can effectively manage life-threatening intracardiac cement emboli. These findings emphasize the value of systematic post-procedural monitoring when cement embolism is suspected, providing important clinical insights for managing this rare but serious complication.
