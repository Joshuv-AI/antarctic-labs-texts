Exploring the extremes: The cosmic laboratory
Authors: Yang Huang, Shunxuan He, Liang Ge, Zhengyang Li, Yongna Mao, Jifeng Liu
Published: 2025

Introduction

That which extends in all directions—above, below, and to the four cardinal points—is termed Yu; that which spans from antiquity to the present is termed Zhou. Collectively, Yuzhou (the universe) constitutes a natural “laboratory of four extremes”—combining ultra-macroscopic scales, ultra-microscopic scales, the most extreme physical conditions, and the deepest interdisciplinary integration—forming the stage for continuous exploration across astronomy, physics, biology, and beyond (see Figure 1 for an overview).

From the Big Bang to humanity

From the ultra-macroscopic perspective, our exploration of the universe begins with the familiar solar system and extends outward—across the Milky Way, into galaxy clusters, and all the way to the edge of the observable cosmos. Gazing across spacetime, from the first light of the Big Bang to the weaving of galaxies and the emergence of life, a grand evolutionary epic spanning tens of billions of years awaits our discovery.

From the ultra-microscopic perspective, the vast universe also functions as a natural laboratory for microscopic phenomena, a grand stage where the microscopic gives rise to the macroscopic. How did atoms and molecules emerge from primordial high-temperature plasma? How did these molecules and dust grains coalesce into stars, planets, and other large-scale structures? A deeper inquiry follows: from inorganic matter to organic compounds, from complex molecules to conscious, intelligent life—does this chain of transformation represent a unique miracle of Earth, or is it a universal “equation of life” that pervades the cosmos? Are we alone? This remains one of the most profound and captivating questions in the study of the universe.

Equally significant are the universe’s extreme physical conditions. Black holes, neutron stars, and white dwarfs manifest gravity, density, and magnetic fields that vastly exceed anything attainable on Earth. The sun, as our nearest star, also provides a natural laboratory where extreme plasma and magnetic processes can be directly observed and studied. These naturally occurring laboratories grant us rare access to physical regimes that push the limits of current theory. In this four-extreme laboratory, new phenomena emerge, and the boundaries of physical law are continually probed.

Opportunities and prospects for development

A full realization of the universe as a four-extreme laboratory requires deep multidisciplinary integration and collaboration, together with a coordinated system of observational facilities employing diverse methods.

From the perspective of astronomical research, the boundaries between physics, chemistry, biology, and astronomy are increasingly blurred and have gradually become intertwined. Physics provides the fundamental framework for understanding the birth, evolution, and extreme phenomena of celestial bodies; chemistry reveals the pathways of matter transformation, molecular formation, and even the emergence of life from the interstellar medium to planetary surfaces; and biology drives the search for extraterrestrial life, challenging life’s definition. To support these grand theoretical inferences and complex phenomenon simulations, powerful computational physics and precise mathematical modeling have become indispensable foundations.

Facing the deluge of data and unprecedented complexity brought by leaps in observational technology at cosmic scales, the role of artificial intelligence has undergone a fundamental transformation: it is no longer merely a simple auxiliary tool but must be internalized as a new dimension of our cognition of the universe, serving as an extension of thought for deciphering deep-space mysteries and uncovering hidden laws.

Yet even the most advanced analytical tools rely on robust observational data, as precision measurement remains the cornerstone of discovery. In recent years, China has developed a network of comprehensive and world-class facilities. On the ground, the Five-Hundred-Meter Aperture Spherical Radio Telescope (FAST), the largest single-dish radio telescope globally, detects faint signals from pulsars, fast radio bursts, and potentially extraterrestrial sources.1 The Large Sky Area Multi-Object Fiber Spectroscopic Telescope (LAMOST) conducts extensive spectroscopic surveys, mapping the chemical composition and kinematics of galactic stars.2 Rising from the western Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, the Wide Field Survey Telescope (WFST), also known as “Mozi Xuntian,” captures cosmic transient events with exceptional precision and speed. In space, a new generation of telescopes is expanding our observational reach. The China Space Station Telescope (CSST) is being developed as a high-resolution, wide-field observatory to provide deep, panoramic views of the universe. The SVOM mission, a Sino-French collaboration, focuses on high-energy transient phenomena such as gamma-ray bursts.3 The Einstein Probe (EP, “Tian Guan”) is a soft X-ray telescope monitoring short-lived, high-energy events associated with compact objects like black holes and neutron stars.4 For solar physics, China operates several major facilities, including the New Vacuum Solar Telescope (NVST) for high-resolution ground-based imaging, and space-based observatories such as the Advanced Space-based Solar Observatory (ASO-S) and the Chinese Hα Solar Explorer (CHASE), which monitor solar magnetic activity, flares, and coronal dynamics.

Looking ahead, the Global Open Transient Telescope Array (GOTTA) aims to establish a worldwide network for monitoring transient astronomical events. Endorsed by UNESCO’s “International Decade of Science for Sustainable Development,” this initiative seeks to enhance accessibility and coordination in time-domain astronomy on a global scale.5 Together, these facilities are forming an integrated observational system that spans the entire electromagnetic spectrum and bridges multiple spatial and temporal scales. This transformation, from isolated facilities to interconnected infrastructure, lays the foundation for the next wave of scientific discovery.

To fully unleash and expand the capabilities of this systematic network, it relies not only on continuous exploration and breakthroughs within the scientific community but also on deep engagement from the industrial sector—particularly in the development of new technologies and methods for large-scale scientific facilities, as well as in supporting efficient and intelligent manufacturing systems. Only by advancing science and engineering in tandem and integrating theory with practice can we build a powerful observational infrastructure for the future.

Frontiers and challenges

The cosmic laboratory of astronomy not only belongs to astronomers but also serves as a cutting-edge platform shared by many fundamental disciplines, such as physics, chemistry, and biology. The wave of deep multidisciplinary integration poses challenges to the innovation of our thinking and the reconstruction of scientific research paradigms. To promote cross-disciplinary research, we propose the following key topics.

Frontier intersections of astronomy and physics

Within the universe’s natural laboratory, the fusion of astronomy and physics is fostering new scientific opportunities. Cosmic extremes pose core questions: what is the nature of dark matter and dark energy? Is gravity valid at extreme scales? As modern physical theories collide with unprecedented observational data, a new cosmic picture is emerging—phenomena such as gravitational wave events, fast radio bursts, and neutron star mergers continuously challenge established frameworks. Against this backdrop, identifying the most breakthrough “key physical quantities” will be the focus of future competition: is it the precise measurement of the Hubble constant or the detection of dark matter particles? All of this will unfold on the grand stage of deep space.

Evolutionary growth of the universe and matter

From fundamental particles to the emergence of life, critical gaps persist in the evolutionary chain of the universe and matter. From a multidisciplinary perspective, there is an urgent need to fill these gaps, such as the continuity of molecular evolution in the interstellar medium or the coupling mechanisms between early star formation and galactic structure growth; how does the sun’s magnetic activity and solar wind shape the planetary environment necessary for life? At the same time, some cross-disciplinary questions are profoundly reshaping our scientific paradigm: is the transition from inorganic to organic, from molecules to life, universal? To systematically track the entire evolutionary process, we urgently need an integrated system that deeply coordinates observations and experiments, enabling multi-scale dynamic tracing from particles and molecules to galaxies and life.

Technology and intelligence driving the future

Faced with the faintness and complexity of cosmic signals, technology and intelligence will become the core driving forces of future exploration. A new generation of measurement technologies is rapidly emerging, promising to open unprecedented observational windows and capture physical quantities previously inaccessible. Who will be the first to realize the practical application of these technologies? Will it be new detectors driven by advanced materials or distributed telescope systems with adaptive capabilities? Meanwhile, AI is evolving from a mere tool to an extension of cognition, redefining how we discover the universe: from target identification and transient detection to automated modeling and physical interpretation, AI is gradually participating in—and even leading—the transformation of astronomical thought.
