A novel tension-slide double intramedullary cortical button technique for distal biceps tendon reconstruction: a case series
Authors: Jacqueline K. Kobayashi, William R. Aibinder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComplete distal biceps ruptures are primarily treated with direct repair; however, patients with chronic ruptures, poor tissue quality, or tendon deficiency may need to be treated with reconstruction. Prior studies have shown dual intramedullary cortical button fixation has the highest load to failure and unicortical fixation decreases the risk for injuring the posterior interosseous nerve intraoperatively. The tension-slide technique aids in tensioning the distal biceps tendon repair and significantly decreases bone-to-tendon gap formation. The aim of this study is to present the clinical outcomes of a novel reconstruction technique that utilizes dual intramedullary cortical button fixation with a tension-slide technique.MethodsThree male patients underwent a distal biceps reconstruction and were followed for 10-19 months. An anterior curvilinear incision is made over the antecubital crease, and 2 3.2-mm unicortical drill holes are placed in the radial tuberosity. A semitendinosus allograft is fixed distally with 2 intramedullary cortical buttons. Using the tension-slide technique, the allograft is shuttled to the radial tuberosity in an onlay fashion. The allograft is secured proximally using a Pulvertaft weave with the elbow at 80° of flexion. Post-operative range of motion, strength, Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand score, Mayo Elbow Performance Score, and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons Shoulder scores were obtained.ResultsTwo patients sustained a chronic distal biceps tendon rupture, and one patient had a failed primary distal biceps tendon repair prior to undergoing the reconstruction procedure. Age ranged between 52 and 55 years. The average post-operative Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand score was 8.3 (range, 0-25.0), Mayo Elbow Performance Score was 95 (range 85-100), and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score was 96 (range 88-100). Full range of motion was obtained in all 3 patients. Post-operative strength was evaluated, and bicep flexion strength was 5/5 in all patients. Supination strength was 5/5 in 2 patients and 4+/5 in one patient. The mean time to surgery was 9.7 months (range, 8-13 months). There were no post-operative complications.ConclusionWe present a technique for distal biceps reconstruction that has similar patient-reported outcomes scores as prior studies with high patient satisfaction while implementing techniques that have shown to significantly decrease the gap between the bone–tendon junction, maintaining the posterior cortex of the radial tuberosity, and safely utilizing distal fixation methods with the highest load to failure. Our early experience with this technique has shown encouraging clinical results.

Distal biceps ruptures occur in about 2.55 per 100,000 patients per year. These injuries most commonly present in 40- to 60-year-olds and have a slight tendency to affect the dominant arm. More than 90% of patients who sustain these injuries are male.12 The mechanism of injury is usually from a sudden and forceful eccentric force against a flexed elbow.7 Nonoperative management is reserved for low-demand and elderly patients who may not safely be able to undergo surgery.25 However, nonoperative treatment has been found to have lower patient-reported satisfaction and loss of 30%-40% of supination strength, 20%-30% loss of flexion strength, and significant supination endurance.9,15,17 Surgical treatment may be indicated for active patients with injuries in their dominant arm or chronic tears that fail nonoperative treatment.

Patients who present with delayed treatment or who decide to have surgery after failing nonoperative treatment may be candidates for reconstruction.25 Chronic injuries may lead to tendon retraction, tissue atrophy or degeneration, inadequate tendon length (<2 cm), and scar formation, which may limit ability to perform a direct repair.6,23 If the distal biceps tendon is retracted, there are a few surgical options including tenodesis of the biceps tendon to the brachialis, mobilization of the tendon and primary repair, or reconstruction using a tendon graft.6 Tenodesis to the brachialis has not been shown to restore supination strength or endurance13,17 and depending on severity of adhesions, the distal bicep tendon may require extensive dissection and still may fail to reach the radial tuberosity, even with hyperflexion of the elbow.2,22 As a result, multiple reconstruction options have been described. Furthermore, the failure rate of primary repair has been reported between 1.5% and 5.4%.1,5,10,11,24,26 In these cases, a revision with a reconstruction may be an option.

There are multiple proximal graft fixation techniques as well as several prior studies describing and comparing different distal fixation techniques using bone tunnels, interference screws, suture anchors, and cortical button fixation.2, 3, 4,20,22,23,25 In addition, different autograft and allograft options have been described including palmaris longus, gracilis, tibialis anterior, flexor carpi radialis, Achilles tendon, and semitendinosus.2,4,22,25

The aim of this study is to present the clinical outcomes of a novel reconstruction technique utilizing a semitendinosus allograft, fixed distally with a tension-slide technique using double intramedullary cortical button fixation, and secured proximally with a Pulvertaft weave. To our knowledge, this is the only case series of distal biceps reconstruction reported utilizing this surgical technique.

Methods

Following institutional review board approval, a retrospective review of a single-surgeon cohort was performed. We identified 3 patients who underwent distal biceps tendon reconstruction with the described technique by the senior author (W.R.A) between June 2023 and March 2024. The patients were followed for a mean follow-up of 15.6 months (range, 10-19 months) post-operatively. The indications for reconstruction as well as the time from injury to surgery were recorded. Post-operative clinical outcomes including strength with manual muscle testing and range of motion (ROM) were assessed, in addition to functional outcome measures, complications, and reoperations. The primary functional outcome measures were obtained using the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand score, Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS), and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons scores.

The cohort included 3 patients, all of whom were males. The mean age was 53.7 years (range, 52-55 years). Two patients had a chronic distal biceps tendon rupture that was initially treated nonoperatively, and one patient sustained a rerupture of a previous primary distal biceps tendon repair using a bicortical button with an interference screw. The mean time to surgery from injury was 9.7 months (range, 8-13 months).

Surgical technique

The patient is placed in a supine position with the arm out on a hand table. The procedure can be performed with a regional anesthetic and monitored anesthesia care or with general anesthesia per preference. The arm is prepped and a sterile tourniquet is placed on the upper arm. A curvilinear incision is utilized over the antecubital fossa aiming toward the radial tuberosity (Fig. 1). The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve is identified with careful dissection and protected during the entirety of the case. The remnant distal biceps tendon is identified, and adhesions are removed.Figure 1Intraoperative image of a left arm with a planned extensive curvilinear incision centered over the antecubital fossa.

Intraoperative image of a left arm with a planned extensive curvilinear incision centered over the antecubital fossa.

The radial tuberosity is then dissected and débrided of all soft tissue and assessed with the arm in maximal supination to protect the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN). A 4-mm burr is used to decorticate the radial tuberosity. Using a 3.2-mm pin, 2 unicortical drill holes are placed 1 cm apart from proximal to distal centered on the radial tuberosity (Fig. 2). The appropriate position can be confirmed with fluoroscopy.Figure 2Left arm in maximal supination after 2 unicortical holes were placed on the radial tuberosity with a 3.2-mm drill approximately 1 cm apart.

Left arm in maximal supination after 2 unicortical holes were placed on the radial tuberosity with a 3.2-mm drill approximately 1 cm apart.

Two No. 2 nonabsorbable polyethylene sutures (FiberWire; Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA) are used, using a different color for each button. This technique utilizes 2 2.6 mm × 7 mm distal biceps buttons (Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA). The sutures are loaded through the buttons in a double loop fashion. One limb of the suture is threaded through one side of the cortical button and back through the opposite side. The loop that is created is maintained and the same strand of the suture is threaded again in the same direction as the first pass, maintaining the original loop but tightening down the second loop. This results in one looped suture and 2 free limbs that can easily be shuttled through the button (Fig. 3). This is repeated with the second colored suture and the other cortical button. Using the insertor, the cortical buttons are placed in the intramedullary position within the radial tuberosity. The button is then released from the inserter, and the cortical buttons are flipped against the anterior cortex of the radial tuberosity. This position is confirmed with radiographic imaging (Fig. 4).Figure 3Loading the button by passing the No. 2 nonabsorbable suture through the button up one hole and back out the second (left), then passing the same end in the same direction again to create 2 loops (middle), and then putting the second pass tight to maintain the first loop leaving one suture loop and 2 free limbs (right).Figure 4Radiographic image confirming the placement of 2 intramedullary cortical button fixations after the primary reconstruction procedure.

Radiographic image confirming the placement of 2 intramedullary cortical button fixations after the primary reconstruction procedure.

A frozen semitendinosus allograft is opened and placed in a warm saline solution. Then, the allograft is passed through the 2 suture loops, and the 4 free limbs are passed from deep to superficial through the allograft on either side of the suture loops using a needle (Fig. 5). Then, using a tension-slide technique, the 4 limbs are pulled to carefully shuttle the graft down to the radial tuberosity in an onlay technique. The free limbs are tied, and the graft is secured onto the radial tuberosity (Fig. 6). This creates 4 points of fixation with the 2 loops compressing the allograft to the tuberosity and the 2 knots securely compressing the graft as well. The fixation is tested by using the graft to lift the patient's arm off the surgical table.Figure 5(Left) the semitendinosus allograft is passed through the 2 suture loops after the cortical buttons have been placed in the radial tubersoity. The elbow is then reduced to about 80° of flexion and 80° of supination during the proximal graft fixation. The proximal aspect of the allograft is fixed to the native biceps tendon using a Pulvertaft weave technique with each limb of the suture in a 90-degree fashion to each other. The fixation is secured with No. 2 nonabsorbable figure-of-eight sutures (Fig. 7). Once the fixation is complete, the resting tension of the elbow should be around 60° of flexion.Figure 7Pulvertaft weave technique of the allograft to the native biceps tendon, secured with No 2 nonabsorbable sutures.

Pulvertaft weave technique of the allograft to the native biceps tendon, secured with No 2 nonabsorbable sutures.

The wound is irrigated after the tourniquet is deflated and adequate hemostasis is attained. The wound is closed with a subcuticular closure in a layered fashion. The patient is placed in a long-arm splint with the elbow in 90° of flexion post-operatively. Indomethacin SR 75 mg is prescribed for heterotopic ossification prophylaxis and omeprazole 20 mg daily for gastrointestinal prophylaxis for 21 days. Follow-up is arranged at two weeks, where the patient is transitioned to a hinged elbow brace at 90° of flexion for 1 week and extended 10°-15° every week until the patient reaches full extension, usually by 6-8 weeks post-operatively. Formal physical therapy is started at six weeks with active ROM, and strengthening starts three months post-operatively.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as means and ranges. Only descriptive statistics were reported given the case series nature of this study.

Results

Full ROM was obtained in all 3 patients compared to their unaffected arm. All patients were able to achieve supination of 80° and pronation of 80°. Bicep flexion strength was evaluated, and 5/5 strength was obtained in all patients. Supination strength was 5/5 in 2 patients and 4+/5 in one patient (Table I). All patients had a normal hook test and normal symmetric rise-and-fall test post-operatively. The average Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand score was 8.3 (range, 0-25.0), MEPS was 95 (range, 85-100), and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons shoulder ccore was 96 (range, 88-100) (Table II). There were no post-operative complications reported. Discussion

In this case series, all patients who underwent this procedure had good functional outcomes and high patient satisfaction, with no known post-operative complications. The 2 patients who underwent this procedure for chronic distal biceps tendon ruptures had better post-operative function and satisfaction scores compared to the patient who had a previously failed primary repair, which could be secondary to having undergone a second surgical procedure and possible higher rates of soft-tissue adhesions that may have formed prior to the reconstruction procedure. Nonetheless, this patient was satisfied and improved compared to before reconstruction. This surgical technique described uses a semitendinosus allograft, proximal fixation with a Pulvertaft weave technique, and fixed distally with a tension-slide technique and 2 intramedullary cortical button fixations. The combination of a tension-slide technique with double intramedullary cortical button fixation has not previously been described.

Sethi et al18 initially described the tension-slide technique to decrease the suture bridge between the tendon–bone junction after distal fixation. They found using a standard cortical button can cause up to a 7-mm suture bridge between the tendon and bone and when the construct is cycled, there can be up to 3 mm of displacement, which is thought to compromise strength and healing. When using the tension-slide technique, the sutures are threaded through the cortical button and the distal tendon, and the free limbs are utilized to shuttle the tendon directly down onto the radial tuberosity, which significantly reduces the gap formation and motion at the repair site.19 This technique was originally described using an 8.0-mm cannulated reamer on the anterior cortex of the radial tuberosity to allow for flush seating of the end of the distal biceps tendon.18 However, we describe a technique that utilizes the tension-slide technique with an onlay fashion to avoid using a cannulated reamer on the anterior cortex of the radial tuberosity, while also decreasing any gap formation between the tendon–bone junction.

Regarding options for distal fixation, Mazzocca et al16 performed a cadaveric laboratory study and found the Endobutton (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA) has the highest load to failure at 440 N compared to suture anchors, bone tunnels, and interference screws. Furthermore, Siebenlist et al performed a biomechanical study comparing double vs. single intramedullary cortical button fixation and found the double intramedullary cortical button fixation has the highest load to failure at 455 N compared to 275 N with the single intramedullary fixation. The authors hypothesize the double cortical button fixation may offer a wider and more anatomic restoration of the footprint of the distal biceps tendon during repair. There was also no significant difference between load to failure between a single intramedullary and single extramedullary cortical button fixation. This study supports using intramedullary button fixation, which requires the surgeon to drill unicortically and avoid drilling bicortically, which decreases the risk of injuring the PIN.21 Thus, our described surgical technique requires only drilling unicortically while utilizing the fixation with the highest load to failure. Furthermore, using small cortical buttons allows ease of flipping in an intramedullary position, and placement can occur distal and proximal to an interference screw in revision settings.

Regarding options for proximal graft fixation techniques, both the onlay and weave techniques have been described. The onlay technique drapes the graft over the native biceps muscle and uses a running locking suture around the proximal graft. The weave technique weaves the tendon graft through the muscle belly of the native biceps tendon proximal to the muscle–tendon junction. This fixation is reinforced by placing sutures at the site where the graft is passed through the native biceps tendon.14 A systematic review was performed by Litwoski et al which evaluated the clinical outcomes and complication rates after distal biceps tendon reconstruction by graft source and proximal graft fixation techniques. They found no significant difference in outcomes or complication rates between using an onlay vs. weave technique for proximal graft fixation.14

There have been several different autograft and allograft options previously described. Litwoski et al evaluated the different allograft vs. autograft options and found there were no significant difference in outcomes or complications rates between the 2 types of grafts. However, the difference in complications that were identified were related to the harvest site morbidity when utilizing autografts.14 Thus, the senior author prefers to use allograft.

Regarding the outcomes of distal biceps reconstruction, Snir et al evaluated the clinical outcomes of chronic distal biceps reconstruction and found Achilles tendon allograft and cortical button fixation were most commonly used and demonstrated significant improvement in the patients' pre-operative and post-operative MEPS. Two of the patients developed post-operative PIN palsies, but no other complications. All patients gained full ROM and reported 4.75/5 mean flexion and supination strength.22 Darlis et al2 also evaluated distal biceps reconstruction using Achilles tendon allograft, but with suture anchors for distal fixation, and found all patients developed 5/5 strength in flexion and supination post-operatively and obtained a mean supination strength of 87% compared to their contralateral extremity.

Regarding semitendinosus grafts, Hendy et al8 analyzed the outcomes of distal biceps reconstruction with allograft compared to primary distal biceps repair. This study evaluated 14 Achilles tendon allografts and 32 semitendinosus allografts with either bone tunnel, suture anchor, or suture button fixation, and were matched to patients who underwent primary distal biceps repair, and found no significant difference in post-operative functional outcome scores, ROM, or complications.8

Frank et al4 compared delayed distal biceps reconstruction utilizing semitendinosus autograft with a transosseous tunnel compared to delayed primary repair and found delayed reconstruction had slightly worse functional outcomes scores compared with delayed primary repair; however, there was no difference in strength, ROM, or complications. Wiley et al27 also evaluated distal biceps reconstruction utilizing semitendinosus autograft and found flexion and supination strength and endurance were restored within normal range post-operatively compared to the nonoperative group. Our study demonstrates our surgical technique has similar and comparable outcomes to prior studies. All patients who underwent the described technique gained full ROM, full flexion strength, and near normal supination strength. Patients had improvement in their post-operative functional outcome scores and there have been no reported complications.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. This study was performed by a single surgeon at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the results. The retrospective nature of this study creates a risk for bias. Although, this study describes a novel reconstruction technique, the sample size is small and there is no control or comparison with an alternative technique.

Conclusion

Patients with chronic distal biceps tendon ruptures can be treated using a semitendinosus allograft reconstruction with a Pulvertaft weave fixation and a tension-slide technique with double intramedullary cortical button fixation. The technique described in this study has similar post-operative outcomes as prior studies and high patient satisfaction while implementing techniques that have shown to significantly decrease the gap between the bone–tendon junction while maintaining the anterior cortex of the radial tuberosity and safely utilizing distal fixation methods with the highest load to failure.
