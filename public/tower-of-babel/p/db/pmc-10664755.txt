Long-Term Temporospatial Complementary Relationship between Degradation and Bone Regeneration of Mg–Al Alloy
Authors: Liangwei Chen, Ziyu Yan, Tiancheng Qiu, Jianhua Zhu, Guanqi Liu, Jianmin Han, Chuanbin Guo
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The utilization of guided tissue regeneration membranes is a significant approach for enhancing bone tissue growth in areas with bone defects. Biodegradable magnesium alloys are increasingly being used as guided tissue regeneration membranes due to their outstanding osteogenic properties. However, the degradation rates of magnesium alloy bone implants documented in the literature tend to be rapid. Moreover, many studies focus only on the initial 3-month period post-implantation, limiting their applicability and impeding clinical adoption. Furthermore, scant attention has been given to the interplay between the degradation of magnesium alloy implants and the adjacent tissues. To address these gaps, this study employs a well-studied magnesium–aluminum (Mg–Al) alloy membrane with a slow degradation rate. This membrane is implanted into rat skull bone defects and monitored over an extended period of up to 48 weeks. Observations are conducted at various intervals (2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 weeks) following the implantation. Assessment of degradation behavior and tissue regeneration response is carried out using histological sections, micro-CT scans, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The findings reveal that the magnesium alloy membranes demonstrate remarkable biocompatibility and osteogenic capability over the entire observation duration. Specifically, the Mg–Al alloy membranes sustain their structural integrity for 8 weeks. Notably, their osteogenic ability is further enhanced as a corrosion product layer forms during the later stages of implantation. Additionally, our in vitro experiments employing extracts from the magnesium alloy display a significant osteogenic effect, accompanied by a notable increase in the expression of osteogenic-related genes. Collectively, these results strongly indicate the substantial potential of Mg–Al alloy membranes in the context of guided tissue regeneration.

Introduction

Vertical bone defects and advanced horizontal atrophy pose significant challenges in bone augmentation.1,2 Approximately 40% of osseointegrated implants require guided bone regeneration (GBR) as a crucial part of patient rehabilitation.3 GBR materials can be categorized into nonabsorbable and absorbable materials. Absorbable materials like collagen membranes often lack sufficient support capacity and are prone to deformation due to soft tissue compression.4−6 On the other hand, nonabsorbable materials, such as titanium mesh, necessitate a secondary surgery for removal and may induce a “stress barrier effect” after implantation.7,8 Desirable GBR membranes should exhibit traits such as biocompatibility, absorbability, appropriate mechanical and physical attributes, capacity for creating space, osteogenesis, and antibacterial prowess.5 Various approaches, including pH-responsive coatings and nanoscale functional coatings, have been explored to enhance these capabilities. These efforts have been validated to yield robust bone therapeutic outcomes.9−11 In recent years, magnesium alloys have emerged as promising materials for GBR membranes, due to their excellent biocompatibility, degradability, and high mechanical strength.12,13 Magnesium alloys not only maintain a specific spatial structure but also eliminate the need for secondary surgical intervention, offering the potential to replace collagen membranes and titanium alloys as a new generation of biological implant materials.

Extensive research has demonstrated the excellent biocompatibility of magnesium and its successful applications in various medical fields. Magnesium alloy fixation screws have found application in orthopedic procedures such as bunion valgus correction,14 treatment of distal radius fractures,15 and fixation of vascularized bone grafts.16 Within the cardiovascular realm, biodegradable magnesium alloy metal scaffolds are routinely implanted in vascular lesions.17 Nevertheless, in the context of oral and maxillofacial treatments, the degradation rate of magnesium alloy must be moderated to ensure its relative integrity during the initial month, facilitating bone tissue growth. Furthermore, the thickness of GBR membranes is confined to the range of 100–200 μm. The MgAl alloy system demonstrates a degradation rate below 0.8 mm/a, enabling it to uphold its integrity within the oral cavity over an extended time frame. Numerous studies have corroborated the excellent biocompatibility and heightened corrosion resistance attributes of this magnesium alloy system.18−20 Therefore, we selected the Mg–Al alloy system to investigate the effects of magnesium alloy GBR membranes.21−23 The AZ91 Mg–Al alloy, characterized by its basic phases, namely, the α-phase (Mg matrix) and the β-phase Mg17Al12, has undergone extensive examination as a biodegradable biomaterial in both in vitro and in vivo settings.24−26 In this investigation, a minor quantity of the antibacterial element Ag at 0.12 wt % was introduced into the AZ91 magnesium alloy. This incorporation serves to enhance the yield strength of the alloy while having no impact on its degradation rate.27−29

The beneficial effects of magnesium in promoting bone defect repair and facilitating osteogenesis through the local microenvironment have been well-documented.30−35 For optimal tissue regeneration, it is crucial to align the degradation rate of magnesium alloy in GBR with the repair rate of alveolar bone tissue. Excessive hydrogen production and the creation of a highly alkaline microenvironment, which can impede tissue healing and result in premature loss of mechanical support properties of the membrane, may occur with rapid degradation.36,37 Conversely, a decrease in degradation rate prolongs the degradation period. However, existing in vivo studies have predominantly focused on the initial weeks or months following implantation.38−40 Consequently, the long-term effects of magnesium alloy on the local microenvironment and the relationship between tissue reactivity and in vivo degradation of Mg membranes remain insufficiently investigated and understood. Bridging this knowledge gap is vital to determine the clinical potential of Mg alloy GBR membranes and to develop more suitable magnesium alloy GBR membranes.

In this study, we conducted in vitro extraction experiments and used a rat skull bone defect model to observe the degradation properties and biological characteristics of degradable magnesium alloy materials. We investigated the long-term temporospatial complementary relationship between the degradation of degradable magnesium alloy membranes and their osteogenesis performance, providing a reference for the development of degradable magnesium alloy membranes suitable for GBR applications.

Materials and Methods

Sample Preparation and Characterization

The experimental materials were provided by Central South University, featuring an alloy composition denoted as Mg-9Al-0.5Zn-0.27Mn-0.12Ag (Table 1). The ingot was processed by hot ring rolling and the sample was obtained on the ring for research. The solution treatment (T4) was conducted at 683 K for 12 h, followed by air quenching at 298 K. Aging treatment (T6) of the solution-treated samples was performed at 448 K for 18 h, also followed by air quenching at 298 K.

Membranes with a diameter of 5 mm were cut to 130 μm thickness by mechanical wire cutting and then ground with 2000 grit silicon carbide (SiC) paper to 110 μm thickness. Subsequently, all samples were cleaned by ultrasonic with acetone and ethanol and sterilized with ultraviolet irradiation (UV lamp, predominantly 254 nm) for 30 min before they were used in vitro and in vivo.

Characterization of the microstructure was carried out using optical microscopy (OM) (Model BX51M, Olympus, Japan) and phases of the Mg alloys were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Model D8, Bruker, Germany).

Electrochemical tests were conducted using an electrochemical workstation (Model Princeton PAR2273, PAR, USA) in SBF at 37 ± 0.5 °C. A three-electrode cell, using samples as the working electrode (10 mm × 10 mm of exposed area), a platinum rod as the counter-electrode and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as the reference electrode, was employed for electrochemical measurements. First, the open circuit potential (OCP) curves were obtained for 30 min. Then, the potentiodynamic polarization was carried out at a scanning rate of 5 mV s–1 with the potential of −250 mV ∼ +500 mV versus OCP, and the values of Ecorr and Icorr were calculated using the Tafel extrapolation method.

Cell Culture and Preparation of Magnesium Alloy Extract

hBMSCs were isolated following a previously established method41 and cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Tauranga, New Zealand) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and 95% air and subcultured upon reaching 80%–90% confluence. Cells from passages 3–5 were utilized in this study.

Sample plates were submerged in the complete culture medium described above under cell culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2, 95% humidity, and 80 rpm) for 24 h to obtain extracts. The specimen-to-solution volume ratio was set at 1.25 cm2/mL, as determined by ISO 10993-5. After incubation, the medium and extraction solution were combined in ratios of 7:1, 3:1, and 1:1 to produce diluted and undiluted conditional extraction media. Extracts from each sample were then stored at 4 °C for subsequent in vitro cell experiments. Five groups were established: α-MEM control (NC), 1/8 Mg extract (1/8 Mg), 1/4 Mg extract (1/4 Mg), 1/2 Mg extract (1/2 Mg), and original Mg extract (1 Mg).

Cell Viability Assessment

hBMSCs were seeded at 1 × 104 cells/mL in 96-well plates. After 1 day for complete cell attachment, the growth medium was discarded and replaced with sample extraction condition medium. Then they were further incubated for 0, 1, 3, and 5 days. At each time point, the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo Molecular Technology, Japan) assay was used to evaluate the number of viable cells. The CCK-8 working solution was prepared by adding 10% CCK-8 solution to the α-MEM medium. After reaction with mixed working solution for 2 h, the supernatants were sucked into a new 96-well plate and the optical density (OD) of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Tek, USA).

In Vitro Osteogenic Differentiation of hBMSCs

Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) Assay

ALP staining was performed to assess osteogenic differentiation. Vitamin C (50 mg/mL), β-glycerophosphate (10 mM), and dexamethasone (100 nM) (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were added to diluted extracts and control media to produce osteogenic media (OM). hBMSCs were seeded at 1.5 × 106 cells/well in 6-well plates and switched to osteogenic media after a day for full cell attachment. After 7 days of osteogenic induction using the mentioned medium, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, they were stained with BCIP/NBT ALP Color Development Kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Extracellular Matrix Mineralization Assay

Alizarin Red S (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) staining was used to detect the extent of mineralization nodules. Cells were cultured similarly to the method described earlier and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After 21 days of osteogenic induction, cells were stained with Alizarin Red S for 30 min at room temperature.

Quantitative Real-Time-Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)

Osteogenic gene expression, including bone morphology protein-2 (BMP-2), collagen-I (Col-I), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and osteocalcin (OCN), was evaluated via qRT-PCR. Cells were cultured as described earlier. After 7 days, total RNA was extracted using the Trizol (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) method and quantified with a Nanodrop 8000 (Thermo Fisher, USA). Then, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa Code No. RR036A). The synthesized cDNA was combined with SYBR Green Mastermix and primers to quantify target gene expression. Gene analysis was performed using GAPDH as a housekeeping gene, with the 2–ΔΔCT method employed for normalization and relative quantification. Primer sequences are listed below in Table 2.

Degradation and Histological Evaluation in Vivo

Surgical Procedure

Thirty (30) 8-week-old SD rats were used in this study, with the study protocol approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University (LA2020513). Animals were anesthetized using intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg body weight). The surgical sites were shaved and sterilized with 3 vol % povidone-iodine and 75 vol % ethanol. The skin, subcutaneous tissue, and periosteum along the midline were incised, and the bone was exposed through blunt dissection. Two 6-mm full-thickness bone defects were created on both sides of the parietal bone midline using a saline-cooled trephine drill. Bone debris was carefully removed from each defect by rinsing with saline. Mg-9Al-0.5Zn-0.27Mn-0.12Ag alloy membranes were inserted into the defects (Figure 1), and tissues were sutured in layers.

Each defect was washed with saline to remove bone debris; (c) Mg-9Al-0.5Zn-0.27Mn-0.12Ag alloy membranes were inserted into the defects; (d) suturing of the wound.

Radiographic Evaluation (Micro-CT Analysis)

Micro-CT was used to assess new bone formation and remaining alloy material volumes after degradation. An Inveon MM system (Siemens, Munich, Germany) was utilized for this analysis. Images were captured at an effective pixel size of 8.82 μm, voltage of 80 kV, current of 500 μA, and exposure time of 1500 ms for each of the 360° rotational steps. Acquired axial images were imported into Inveon Research Workplace software (Siemens, Knoxville, TN, USA) for three-dimensional (3-D) reconstruction. New bone formation and alloy material volumes were calculated.

Histological Processing

Samples were dehydrated through a series of ascending alcohol concentrations (50, 60, 70, 80, 90, and 99 vol %) and embedded in autopolymerizing MMA resin (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Nondecalcified specimens were sectioned into 150–200 μm thick slices using a diamond saw (STX-202A, Shenyang Kejing Auto-Instrument Co., Ltd., Shenyang, China), adhered to resin slides, and ground to a thickness of ∼50 μm. The ground specimens were then stained with Methylene Blue trihydrate and van Gieson stain. Images were captured under a light microscope at 1.25× total magnification for overall evaluation and 10× total magnification for local assessment. New bone formation areas were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

The histological section evaluation index primarily includes inflammation indicators, new bone formation, alloy material degradation, and surrounding tissue responses to the implant materials.

In Vivo Degradation Characterization (SEM Examination)

After embedding in MMA resin, samples were cut using a diamond saw (Model STX-202A, Shenyang Kejing Auto-Instrument Co., Ltd., Shenyang, China) to reveal the implant and bone, then sectioned using an automated microtome (Model RM 2255, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) to obtain a smooth, fresh surface.

SEM samples were C-coated using an auto fine coater (Model JFC-1600, JEOL, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Surface morphology was observed with a Quanta 200 FEG SEM (FEI) in backscattered electron mode. Degradation product composition was analyzed using EDS, line scanning, and map scanning.

Statistical Analysis

Results are presented as mean ± standard error. D’Agostino-Pearson’s test indicated the non-normal distribution of values. Consequently, statistical analysis was performed using nonparametric analysis (Kruskal–Wallis) with a preset significance threshold of α = 0.05. Notable distinctions among groups were identified through Dunn’s posthoc test. Data analysis was executed using the SPSS software.

Results

Materials Characterization and In Vitro Corrosion Test

The microstructure of the sample was detected by optical microscopy. The average grain size of the sample was ∼50 μm. Due to the aging treatment, the precipitate was produced in the α-Mg matrix (the black part in Figure S1A in the Supporting Information). The precipitate was confirmed as β-Mg17Al12 by XRD (Figure S1B in the Supporting Information).

In order to test the corrosion rate of Mg–Al alloy in vitro, electrochemical experiments were carried out in SBF at 37 °C, and the Tafel curve is shown in Figure S1C in the Supporting Information. The measured Ecorr was −1.528 V and the value of Icorr was 2.9 × 10–4 mA/cm2.

In Vitro Biocompatibility

In order to assess the biocompatibility of the Mg–Al alloy in vitro and investigate whether the degradation products of magnesium alloy can stimulate osteogenic differentiation by creating a microenvironment similar to bone development after magnesium alloy implantation, we cultured BMSCs in a conditioned medium containing magnesium alloy extract. Cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 assay with different extract concentrations (Figure 2A). All groups showed an increase in OD from day 1 to day 5 of incubation. The cell viability of the 1/8 Mg, 1/4 Mg, 1/2 Mg, and 1 Mg groups was comparable to that of the NC group. None of the groups exhibited cytotoxicity, including the 1 Mg group. Subsequently, the osteogenic effect was evaluated through ALP activity, ARS staining, and RT-PCR. Extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity are crucial indicators of osteogenic differentiation. As depicted in Figure 1B, ALP activity increased gradually with higher extract concentrations. The undiluted extract group exhibited the highest ALP activity. ECM deposition was evaluated using Alizarin Red staining, and the trend observed in ARS staining was consistent with that in ALP staining (Figure 1B). The extract groups significantly increased the area of red staining, with the 1 Mg group showing the most intense staining.

To further assess the osteogenic induction effects of the magnesium alloy extract, we examined the RNA levels of several osteogenesis-related genes using RT-PCR after treating the cells. As anticipated, the expression of RUNX2, Col-I, and OCN genes was significantly higher in the MgAl extract group, reaching its peak in the 1 Mg group (Figure 1C). Although BMP2 did not show a noticeable difference among the low-concentration magnesium alloy extract groups and the negative control group, the 1 Mg group still exhibited enhanced expression. Overall, these findings confirm that the magnesium alloy extract does not compromise cell viability and promotes the osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs in vitro, particularly in the undiluted magnesium alloy extract group.

In Vivo Bone Regeneration

Clinical Observation

Six hours after the surgical procedure, the experimental animals gradually regained mobility without any signs of cracks or bleeding in the surgical area. By day 2, the animals resumed normal activities, exhibited good mental states, maintained regular diets, and had normal urine and feces production. There were no instances of implant discharge, allergic reactions, or toxic reactions.

Post-operative examination revealed slight local swelling in most experimental animals between 3 and 7 days after surgery, without any noticeable gas accumulation or inflammatory exudate. The swelling subsided within 2 weeks, and the wound tissue showed satisfactory healing. Throughout the observation period, no abnormalities were observed in the animals’ daily behaviors, including eating and activity levels, and there were no instances of animal mortality.

Micro-CT Analysis

In order to assess the osteogenic effect and degradation rate of the magnesium alloy membrane in vivo, micro-CT was used to measure the volumes of newly formed bone and residual magnesium alloy membranes. Figure 3A shows the 3D reconstructed images of bone defects with newly regenerated bone. Over the first 12 weeks, new bone gradually formed from the margin to the center of the defect. Quantitative CT analysis indicated a progressive increase in new bone volume, although no significant difference was observed between 2 and 12 weeks (p > 0.05). By 24 weeks, more than half of the bone defect areas covered by the magnesium alloy membranes were filled with new bone, resulting in a considerable reduction in the residual defect area. The BV value was significantly higher compared to that at 12 weeks (Figure 3C). At 48 weeks, the majority of bone defects in the experimental animals were almost completely filled with new bone, and the BV value exhibited a considerable increase, compared to week 24.

The micro-CT 2D reconstructed images revealed a gradual and uniform degradation behavior of the magnesium alloy during the initial 8 weeks. Micro-CT quantitative analysis indicated a gradual decrease in the volumes of the magnesium alloy membranes during the first 8 weeks post-implantation, although no significant difference was observed. The magnesium alloy membranes maintained their integrity well, without evident collapse or cracks. By the eighth week, some portions of the magnesium alloy began to fracture. The volume of the residual magnesium alloy membrane decreased without a significant difference (Figure 3D). At 24 weeks, further membrane degradation was observed, and quantitative analysis revealed a significant decrease in the volume of the residual membrane, with most of the magnesium alloy films disintegrating. The volume of the magnesium film significantly decreased, compared to the previous time point. By 48 weeks, membrane degradation became evident, with the original shape barely discernible but still occupying a portion of the volume. The volume of the residual magnesium alloy decreased to less than a fifth of its initial volume.

Curve fitting and Pearson’s correlation analysis were performed to examine the relationship between magnesium alloy degradation and bone formation. A significant correlation was observed between degradation and bone regeneration, with a Pearson’s correlation coefficient of −0.850 (p < 0.01).

Histological Evaluation

Histological analysis was carried out to examine continuous bone tissue reactions at different time points, aiming to demonstrate the biological response at the bone-implant interface. Figure 4 presents histologically stained images of the bone-membrane interface. During the initial 8 weeks post-implantation, the magnesium alloy exhibited good integrity with minimal degradation and corrosion products. Bubbles were observed surrounding the alloy, which was enveloped by dense fibrous connective tissue. The connective tissue contained numerous newly formed small blood vessels, capillaries, and well-organized fibroblasts, with no significant aggregation of inflammatory cells.

At the 12-week mark, some portions of the magnesium alloy had fractured, and the corrosion product layer on the alloy surface had thickened. Although bubbles were still visible, their quantity had decreased. The fibrous connective tissue appeared thinner compared to earlier stages, with no notable accumulation of lymphocytes or plasma cells. New bone extended multiple branches toward the alloy, resulting in increased thickness and volume. However, there was no statistically significant difference between 2 and 12 weeks (p > 0.05). By 24 weeks, most of the magnesium alloy membranes had bent or fractured. The corrosion product layer was noticeably thicker, compared to 12 weeks, indicating fragmentation and disintegration. The volume of new bone exhibited a significant increase compared to week 12 (p < 0.05). New bone covered a substantial portion of the defect area, demonstrating ongoing activity. Numerous branching processes at the front of the new bone were interconnected by fibrous connective tissue.

After 48 weeks, the magnesium membranes had nearly completely degraded. There was a significant increase in new bone formation (p < 0.01), and the bone defect areas were completely covered. Additionally, new bone was visible above the magnesium membrane, and no remarkable infiltration of inflammatory cells was observed in the fibrous layer in any of the sections.

EDX Line Scanning

The distribution of key elements (Mg, Al, Ca, P) in the relevant sections was analyzed using elemental line scanning of the same set of cross sections. The corresponding maps and regional BEIs are shown in Figure 5.

Energy-dispersive X-ray line scan data of the cross sections of the residual magnesium membranes; Red, blue, and yellow represent the elements magnesium, calcium, and phosphorus, respectively.

After 2 weeks, slight degradation of the magnesium membranes was observed, with the presence of Ca and P in the degradation layer. Over time, the magnesium membranes gradually degraded, and the degradation layer became thicker. By 8 weeks, the degradation layer had reached a thickness of 40 μm, with the ongoing deposition of Ca and P.

At 24 weeks, numerous corrosion cracks were observed in the magnesium membranes, which had disintegrated but still maintained their integrity. Near the magnesium membranes, a substantial amount of new bone had formed. By 48 weeks, the magnesium membranes had fragmented into multiple pieces and lost their integrity. The height and width of the Mg peak had significantly decreased. In contrast, wide and continuous Ca and P peaks filled the degradation layer, indicating that the elements Ca and P had almost fully penetrated the magnesium alloy. Abundant new bone formation was also evident around the magnesium membrane.

EDX Mapping

The characterization of the composition of the degradation layer formed on the alloy surfaces after 2–48 weeks is crucial for understanding the degradation behavior and biological performance of materials. This was achieved by performing EDX mapping, as shown in Figure 6.

Energy-dispersive X-ray mapping data of the cross sections of the residual magnesium membranes; Red, green, blue, and purple colors represent the elements Mg, Al, Ca, and P, respectively.

The mapping analysis revealed that the magnesium alloy substrate primarily consisted of elemental Mg with a small amount of elemental Al. However, in the degradation product layer, the Mg content was significantly reduced, while the concentrations of Al, Ca, and P elements were enriched. Initially, after 2 weeks, only a thin surface layer of the magnesium alloy had corroded, while the matrix remained largely intact. Over the following 4 and 8 weeks, the corrosion product layer gradually thickened, accompanied by a notable increase in the concentrations of elemental Al, Ca, and P. During this period, the corrosion process followed a regular pattern. However, at 12 weeks, the surface corrosion of the magnesium alloy intensified, leading to the formation of corrosion cracks throughout the entire alloy in some areas. Despite this, a significant portion of the matrix remained undegraded. By 24 weeks, only a small fraction of the original magnesium alloy remained intact, as most of the alloy had undergone degradation. The corrosion product layer formed during the degradation process began to disintegrate while still retaining its original form. Finally, at 48 weeks, the degradation of the magnesium alloy was almost complete, with the majority of the alloys disintegrating, and only small amounts of localized Mg, Al, Ca, and P elements were detected.

Discussion

Previous studies have investigated various methods to reduce the initial biodegradation rate of magnesium alloys, but most of these approaches were not suitable for oral guided bone regeneration.13,15,32,42−46 In this study, we focused on the Mg–Al alloy system, using several methods to examine its degradation rate and osteogenesis effects in vivo, aiming to establish the long-term temporal relationship between degradation and bone regeneration. Furthermore, we conducted in vitro osteogenic induction experiments to demonstrate the osteogenic effect of the magnesium alloy.

Both the in vivo bone defect implantation and the in vitro extract experiments confirmed the ability of the magnesium alloy to promote new bone formation. Previous studies have emphasized the importance of conducting long-term research to validate the impact of biological properties on segmental defect regeneration.30,44,45,47 In our study, dynamic observations of hard tissue sections and micro-CT imaging clearly illustrated the consistently robust growth activity of bone tissue at the bone-implant interfaces throughout the 48-week degradation period following magnesium implantation. During the degradation of the magnesium alloy, the formed alkaline microenvironment combined with the release of Mg ions could effectively promote the osteogenesis of stem cells.48−50 Of note, in this study, new bone continuously regenerated as the magnesium alloy slowly degraded, even at 24 and 48 weeks post-implantation, implying that the Mg–Al alloy holds promise for tissue regeneration among elderly individuals. Consistently, in the in vitro experiments using the indirect contact method (sample extracts), the undiluted extract demonstrated excellent osteogenic effects on hBMSCs, and cell viability remained consistently above 95% in all CCK-8 assay measurements, indicating no cytotoxicity. The released degradation products, mainly magnesium ions, significantly improved the expression of osteogenesis-related genes, such as Type I collagen and Runx2, aligning with previous research on the osteogenic potential of magnesium.12,45 However, there was no significant increase in BMP-2 expression, possibly due to the insufficient increase in magnesium ion concentration in the extract to alter BMP-2 expression.51−53

Magnesium alloy possesses a dual nature as it can benefit bone formation through its degradation products, but its high degradation rate can rapidly reduce mechanical strength and generate excessive gas, which can impact tissue regeneration.32,33,43,54,55 Notably, in this study, the corrosion of the Mg–Al alloy exhibited uniform and gradual progression, and the gas generation slowed down upon the formation of a corrosion product layer, mainly consisting of Al, Ca, and P elements. Although we observed rapid degradation and accumulation of hydrogen gas in the first stage, which could hinder new bone formation in the Mg–Al alloy as reported before,43,44 healing of the bone defect was still highly active due to the suitable concentration of Mg ions released by Mg–Al alloys, as shown in SEM-EDS results. Multiple factors (such as the elemental composition, distribution, and thickness of the corrosion product layers) could affect the degradation rate of the magnesium alloy in vivo.56,57 Our research has revealed, for the first time, the degradation process, composition, and distribution of the degradation product layers of Mg–Al alloy GBR membranes implanted in vivo for up to 48 weeks. Ideally, scaffolds should maintain relative integrity for 12 weeks and then gradually be absorbed after bone healing.58 In this study, Mg–Al alloy exhibited a suitable rate and uniform corrosion products, maintaining its relative integrity in the first 12 weeks, confirming its significant potential in the clinical transformation application in oral and maxillofacial and orthopedic surgery.

The degradation rate of magnesium alloys must be carefully controlled to ensure their suitability for GBR membranes in the oral and maxillofacial field. Many factors affected by magnesium alloy degradation, such as space-making ability, mechanical properties, the release of Mg ions, and production of hydrogen gas, have a strong correlation with osteogenesis properties.32,42,44,45 Rapid degradation can lead to higher local concentrations of Mg ions, excess hydrogen accumulation, and extrusion of healing space, while degradation rates that are too slow will lead to a space-occupying effect.59−61 Therefore, it is crucial to match the degradation rate of the alloy with the growth rate of the bone tissue. In this study, correlation analysis between bone formation and magnesium alloy residue within 48 weeks revealed a strong negative correlation with a correlation coefficient of −0.85, indicating a significant adaptation between magnesium alloy degradation and tissue repair. In addition to the Mg–Al alloy system, more corrosion-resistant solutions, such as coatings, could be simultaneously applied to achieve a better temporospatial complementary relationship between bone regeneration and the degradation of the magnesium alloy membrane.62,63 Hence, with good mechanical properties, degradability, and osteogenic properties, the Mg–Al alloy can be used in various clinical applications, such as augmenting alveolar bone deficiencies and repairing large bone defects and peri-implant defects. It still has great potential, even in challenging bone defects in the elderly.

Regarding the presence of Al, although concerns have been raised about its potential adverse effects, our study did not find direct and conclusive evidence linking Al to disease.64,65 It is important to acknowledge the limitations of our study, including the use of an animal model that may differ from human physiology. Additionally, we observed relatively active osteoblasts even at 48 weeks, which warrants further exploratory research in follow-up experiments.

Conclusions

The degradation rate of the magnesium alloy membranes had a persistent impact on the promotion of osteogenesis during extended implantation. The membranes effectively retained their shape during the initial three months and continued to stimulate osteogenesis even after a duration of 12 weeks. This study establishes a foundation for the future clinical implementation of biodegradable magnesium alloy membranes and provides valuable insights and guidance for the application of Mg-based GBR.
