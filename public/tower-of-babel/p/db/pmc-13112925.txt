Long-term outcomes and bleeding risk factors in pancreatic portal hypertension: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yingjie Zhang, Kun Yin, Yanglin Pan, Gui Ren, Jun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic portal hypertension (PPH) is a distinct form of sinistral (left-sided) portal hypertension, primarily resulting from pancreatic diseases such as acute or chronic pancreatitis and pancreatic tumors. These conditions lead to splenic vein distortion, compression, and inflammatory thickening, which can progress to luminal obstruction and thrombosis. This thrombosis impairs venous return, elevates splenic vein pressure, and ultimately causes splenomegaly, hypersplenism, and left-sided portal hypertension. Current knowledge regarding the long-term prognosis of PPH is primarily derived from small-scale descriptive studies and case reports. Thus, large-scale, prospective cohort studies are needed to better characterize its natural history and outcomes.ObjectiveThis retrospective cohort study aimed to determine the incidence of variceal bleeding (the primary complication) in patients with PPH and to identify its risk factors.MethodsA total of 207 patients diagnosed with PPH secondary to acute or chronic pancreatitis who were managed at Xijing Hospital between 2015 and 2023 were included. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings (e.g., thrombosis, stenosis, or occlusion of the splenic, portal, or superior mesenteric vein) were collected retrospectively. All patients were prospectively followed for the clinical endpoint of variceal bleeding. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify independent risk factors for variceal bleeding.ResultsThe crude incidence of bleeding was 11.6% (24/207). In contrast, Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed a cumulative incidence of approximately 30% at 3 years. On multivariate Cox regression analysis, smoking (hazard ratios [HR] 3.26, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.39–7.66, p = 0.007), chronic pancreatitis (HR 2.69, 95% CI: 1.04–6.91, p = 0.041), and diabetes mellitus (HR 2.83, 95% CI: 1.20–6.65, p = 0.017) were independent risk factors for bleeding. Conversely, a higher platelet count conferred a protective effect (HR 0.99 per 10^9/L increase, 95% CI: 0.98–1.00, p = 0.008). Of note, the clinical relevance of this per 10^9/L increase is likely limited. Among the intervention subgroups, bleeding occurred in 1 of 4 patients (25%) receiving endoscopic prophylaxis and in 1 of 15 patients (6.7%) on oral anticoagulation, whereas none of the 3 patients treated with non-selective beta-blockers (NSBBs) experienced bleeding. Formal statistical comparisons were not performed due to small and unequal sample sizes.ConclusionThis study suggests smoking, chronic pancreatitis, and diabetes mellitus as independent risk factors may be associated with variceal bleeding in pancreatitis-associated PPH, while a higher platelet count conferred a protective effect might confer protection. These findings provide preliminary insights for risk stratification. These findings enable risk stratification by highlighting modifiable factors and underscore the need for vigilant monitoring in high-risk patients, but require validation in prospective studies.Keywords: Pancreatic portal hypertension, Acute pancreatitis, Chronic pancreatitis

Introduction

Pancreatic portal hypertension (PPH) is a distinct form of extrahepatic portal hypertension, primarily caused by pathologies such as chronic pancreatitis (CP), acute pancreatitis (AP), pseudocysts, or neoplasms that compress or invade the splenic vein [1–3]. This pathological process causes venous obstruction, stenosis, and thrombosis, leading in significant pressure elevation within the splenoportal venous system. Consequently, PPH is one of the most common forms of extrahepatic portal hypertension [4].

In contrast to cirrhosis-related portal hypertension, PPH exhibits divergent anatomical, hemodynamic, and clinical features [5]. Its progression and prognosis are largely determined by the course of the underlying pancreatic disease. Despite often being considered a relatively ‘benign’ condition, PPH can, however, be complicated by life-threatening gastroesophageal variceal bleeding [6]. Consequently, variceal bleeding is a leading cause of emergency hospitalization and poor prognosis in these patients.

Beyond the acute threat of bleeding, sustained portal hypertension and hypersplenism in PPH can lead to chronic sequelae that adversely affect quality of life and long-term survival. Although current research focuses on optimizing diagnosis, treating underlying causes, and managing acute bleeding, robust data are lacking regarding the long-term bleeding risk in PPH. In particular, large-scale, long-term follow-up data from Chinese populations are lacking, and the independent risk factors for bleeding events—especially initial episodes—have not been systematically identified.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a retrospective cohort study. This study aimed to characterize the natural history of PPH and identify its risk factors by (1) determining the long-term incidence of variceal bleeding in patients with pancreatitis-associated PPH; (2) identifying independent risk factors for bleeding in this population; and (3) evaluating the efficacy of existing prophylactic strategies in preventing the first variceal bleed among asymptomatic patients. The findings from this study are expected to inform risk stratification for hemorrhage and guide clinical management, ultimately improving long-term outcomes.

Methods

Patients

Patients consecutively admitted with pancreatitis-associated PPH at Xijing Hospital between January 2015 and December 2023 were included in this retrospective study. We collected demographic characteristics (including age, sex, body mass index [BMI], and history of smoking and alcohol use) and clinical data (including etiology, disease severity, presenting symptoms, and laboratory parameters) from electronic medical records.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Xijing Hospital (Approval No. KY20250196). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of PPH secondary to AP or CP; (2) age between 18 and 80 years at diagnosis.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) history of pancreatic or splenic surgery; (2) evidence of portal hypertension from non-pancreatic causes (e.g., cirrhosis); (3) diagnosis of pancreatic neoplasm; (4) presence of any established hematological diagnosis (e.g., leukemia, lymphoma, or coagulation disorders), excluding transient laboratory abnormalities; (6) incomplete clinical records or loss to follow-up.

Diagnosis and definitions

AP was diagnosed according to the Revised Atlanta Classificationand international consensus [7], meeting at least two of the following three criteria: (1) abdominal pain consistent with AP; (2) serum lipase or amylase activity at least three times the upper limit of normal; (3) characteristic imaging findings of AP on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or abdominal ultrasonography.

CP was diagnosed based on the presence of any of the following criteria [8]: (1) histopathological findings diagnostic of CP; (2) imaging evidence on cross-sectional imaging (e.g., CT, MRI/MRCP) demonstrating pancreatic calcifications or ductal calculi; (3) characteristic pancreatic ductal changes or calculi detected on endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP); (4) endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) findings meeting established diagnostic criteria for CP; (5) a positive secretin stimulation test result.

PPH was diagnosed according to the following criteria [9]: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of acute or chronic pancreatic disease; (2) imaging evidence on cross-sectional imaging (CT or MRI) or Doppler ultrasound demonstrating splenic vein thrombosis or obstruction, with the presence of perigastric or perisplenic collateral circulation; (3) direct visualization of gastric varices by esophagogastroduodenoscopy, which was performed in all patients; (4) exclusion of other causes of portal hypertension.

Imaging definitions for vascular involvement [10]: (1) stenosis: a reduction of ≥ 50% in the diameter of the splenic, portal, or superior mesenteric vein compared to the expected normal diameter; (2) occlusion: complete absence of contrast opacification of the vessel lumen with no flow on Doppler ultrasonography or contrast-enhanced imaging, typically resulting from thrombosis; (3) thrombosis: the presence of an intraluminal filling defect on CECT or MRI, which may cause partial or complete occlusion.

A bleeding event was defined as meeting all the following criteria [11, 12]: (1) a documented clinical history of hematemesis or melena; and (2) clinical and/or endoscopic exclusion of alternative non-variceal upper gastrointestinal bleeding sources (e.g., ulcer, Mallory-Weiss tear).

Outcome measures

The observation period began on the date of PPH diagnosis. The primary endpoint was the first episode of variceal bleeding. Patients who did not experience the endpoint were censored at the date of their last clinical contact.

To characterize the vascular anatomy, the splenoportal mesenteric venous system and gastric varices were evaluated using CECT or MRI. All images were independently reviewed by two investigators (including radiologists), with any discrepancies resolved by consensus with a senior radiologist.

Statistical analyses

Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared with the Mann-Whitney U test (also known as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, which was employed when > 20% of cells had an expected count < 5.

Time-to-event analysis for the first bleeding event was performed using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models. The time origin was defined as the date of PPH diagnosis. Patients who experienced upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) were considered to have reached the endpoint, while those without an event were right-censored at their last follow-up date. Variables with a significance level of p < 0.05 in the univariable analysis were used as the criterion for inclusion in the initial multivariate model. The results are reported as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 27.0). Cumulative incidence curves were generated with GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0).

Result

Baseline characteristics

A total of 412 patients with pancreatitis-associated PPH were initially identified between January 2015 and December 2023. After applying the exclusion criteria, 205 patients were excluded due to the following reasons: cirrhosis (n = 43); pancreatic malignancies or benign tumors (n = 101); hematological disorders (n = 33); incomplete clinical data (n = 14); and lost to follow-up (n = 14). Thus, the final study cohort consisted of 207 patients. Based on the occurrence of bleeding during follow-up, patients were stratified into a non-bleeding group (n = 183) and a bleeding group (n = 24), as summarized in the patient flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study flow chart

The study included 207 patients with pancreatitis-associated PPH. The median age was 49 years (IQR 37–58), and 70.5% (146/207) were male. Regarding etiology, AP accounted for 51.7% (107/207) of cases and CP for 48.3% (100/207). The etiologies included: biliary (32.9%, 68/207); hypertriglyceridemia-induced (24.6%, 51/207); alcoholic (10.6%, 22/207); idiopathic (11.1%, 23/207); and other causes (20.8%, 43/207). The most frequently reported clinical symptom was abdominal pain (79.2%, 164/207), followed by fever (12.1%, 25/207), jaundice (5.8%, 12/207), and anemia (2.9%, 6/207). Splenomegaly was documented in 67.6% (140/207) of patients. The prevalence of hypersplenism, splenic infarction, and ascites was 1.4% (3/207), 1.4% (3/207), and 2.9% (6/207), respectively.

Over a median follow-up of 34.1 months (IQR 22.9–43.9), variceal bleeding occurred in 24 patients (11.6%). Baseline characteristics differed significantly between the bleeding and non-bleeding groups in smoking history and the type of pancreatic disease (AP vs. CP). However, the groups were comparable in other parameters (Table 1).

Risk factor analysis for bleeding in PPH

Univariable analysis indicated that smoking history, CP, diabetes mellitus, platelet count (PLT), prothrombin time (PT), and albumin level (ALB) were significantly associated with bleeding events (p < 0.05). These variables were included in a multivariable Cox regression model. After adjustment, smoking (HR 3.26, 95% CI 1.39–7.66, p = 0.007),CP (HR 2.69, 95% CI 1.04–6.91, p = 0.041), and diabetes mellitus (HR 2.83, 95% CI 1.20–6.65, p = 0.017) emerged as independent risk factors. A higher PLT was associated with a statistically significant, though modest, reduction in risk (HR 0.99 per 10^9/L increase, 95% CI 0.98–1.00, p = 0.008). PT and ALB were not significantly associated with bleeding in the multivariable model (p > 0.05). The full details of the multivariable analysis are presented in Table 2.

Distribution of varices in PPH

Evaluation of variceal distribution revealed that isolated gastric varices (IGV) were the most common pattern, accounting for 92.3% (191/207) of patients, followed by combined gastroesophageal varices in 7.7% (16/207). Further analysis revealed that some of these 16 GOV patients had portal vein or superior mesenteric vein thrombosis, which may explain the extended pattern of varices. This suggests that the extent of thrombosis in PPH can influence the type of varices.

Follow-up outcomes

Over a median follow-up of 34.1 months (IQR 22.9–43.9), variceal bleeding occurred in 24 patients, yielding a crude incidence of 11.6%. However, Kaplan-Meier analysis, which accounts for variable follow-up time, revealed a cumulative incidence of approximately 30% at 3 years (Fig. 2). Four deaths were recorded, all attributed to non-pancreatic causes based on clinical assessment.

Cumulative incidence curve of bleeding events

Among the 24 patients with index bleeding, initial management included: conservative medical therapy (n = 2; 1 rebleeding), endoscopic hemostasis (n = 6; 2 rebleeding), angiographic intervention (n = 11; 2 rebleeding), and surgical intervention (n = 5; 0 rebleeding). Notably, all 5 patients who experienced rebleeding were subsequently managed surgically, and no further rebleeding occurred during follow-up, suggesting that surgery effectively controlled refractory bleeding (Table 3).

Discussion

This study defined the clinical course of pancreatitis-associated PPH by investigating the incidence and risk factors for variceal hemorrhage. The crude incidence of bleeding was 11.6% (24/207); however, Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed a 3-year cumulative incidence of approximately 30%, highlighting a substantial long-term risk. Multivariable Cox regression analysis established smoking, CP, and diabetes mellitus as independent risk factors, whereas a higher platelet count conferred a protective effect. This finding is consistent with the previously reported range of 10–20% [13], reinforcing that PPH carries a substantial hemorrhage risk, which is distinct from that of cirrhotic portal hypertension, particularly in patients with CP. The significant differences observed in smoking history and pancreatic disease type (AP vs. CP), despite general comparability in other baseline characteristics, highlight their critical importance in risk stratification.

Among the 24 bleeding patients, 18 (75%) presented with hematemesis and 6 (25%) with melena. The absence of mortality may be attributed to the exclusion of cirrhosis patients, who constitute a high-risk group for variceal bleeding deaths. This underscores the distinct profile of PPH compared to cirrhotic portal hypertension.

The high prevalence of abdominal pain (79.2%) and splenomegaly (67.6%) is a hallmark of the underlying pathophysiology of PPH [14]. Abdominal pain commonly indicates ongoing pancreatic inflammation or fibrosis, whereas splenomegaly stems directly from splenic venous congestion secondary to portal hypertension and may also be indicative of splenic vein thrombosis, a known risk factor for hemorrhage. In contrast, the low incidence of ascites (2.9%) aligns with the extrahepatic nature of PPH, which largely preserves hepatic synthetic function compared to cirrhotic portal hypertension, underscoring its role as a key discriminator in the differential diagnosis [15].

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis identified smoking, CP, diabetes mellitus, and platelet count as independent risk factors for bleeding. Of these, smoking emerged as a particularly strong risk factor (HR 3.26, 95% CI 1.39–7.66), an understudied association in prior PPH literature. We hypothesize that this risk is mediated via smoking-induced pancreatic fibrosis, endothelial dysfunction, and a chronic pro-inflammatory state, mechanisms that collectively promote portal hypertensive bleeding [16, 17]. These findings strongly support integrating smoking cessation as a cornerstone of risk modification strategies in PPH management. Regarding chronic pancreatitis, an elevated bleeding risk was observed in patients with CP (HR 2.69, 95% CI 1.04–6.91, p = 0.041) compared to those with AP, a finding attributed to the chronic inflammation and progressive vascular injury inherent to CP [18, 19]. This risk differential is substantiated by prior studies reporting a gastrointestinal bleeding probability of 50–72% in CP with splenic vein thrombosis [1, 2, 20], versus 12.5–17.8% in AP [21, 22], demonstrating a substantially higher bleeding risk in CP-associated PPH. Consequently, patients with pancreatitis-associated PPH and underlying CP, particularly those with additional risk factors (e.g., smoking), may benefit from enhanced endoscopic surveillance. For individuals deemed at highest risk, early prophylactic intervention with non-selective beta-blockers or endoscopic variceal ligation may be considered based on an individualized risk-benefit assessment, although its efficacy specific to PPH requires future validation. Diabetes mellitus was an independent risk factor for bleeding (HR 2.83, 95% CI 1.20–6.65, p = 0.017). Pathophysiologically, diabetes-associated microangiopathy, endothelial dysfunction, as well as coagulation abnormalities may synergistically increase vascular fragility in the setting of portal hypertension [23, 24]. A higher platelet count conferred a protective effect (HR 0.99 per 10^9/L increase, 95% CI 0.98–1.00, p = 0.008) Compensatory mechanisms may explain this: although splenomegaly accelerates platelet destruction, it may trigger reactive thrombopoiesis, producing younger, hyperfunctional platelets [25–27]. Alternatively, a preserved platelet count might contribute to the stabilization of portosystemic collaterals through localized microthrombosis. While these mechanisms are plausible, the modest effect size (HR 0.99) warrants cautious interpretation, as it may be influenced by unmeasured confounding. Collectively, these findings indicate a complex relationship between platelet count and bleeding risk in PPH that may not be directly comparable to that in cirrhotic portal hypertension.

Only 22 of the 207 patients (10.6%) with PPH received primary prophylaxis (NSBBs, endoscopic therapy, or oral anticoagulation). The small sample size in each intervention subgroup (n ≤ 15) precluded robust statistical comparison of bleeding rates between treatments, precluding definitive assessment of their therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, the role of primary prophylaxis in PPH remains undefined. Although NSBBs are recommended as first-line therapy for primary prophylaxis in cirrhotic portal hypertension per consensus guidelines [17], and endoscopic therapies have established efficacy in cirrhotic patients [28], their benefit in PPH has not been established. To address this knowledge gap, future studies should focus on: (1) conducting comparative effectiveness research among different prophylactic interventions; (2) identifying patient- and disease-specific factors to guide therapy selection; and (3) optimizing intervention timing and developing individualized treatment protocols.

Isolated gastric varices (IGV) predominated (191/207, 92.3%), while gastroesophageal varices (GOV) were less common (16/207, 7.7%). This distribution is a hallmark of the sinistral portal hypertension collateral pattern in PPH and carries significant clinical implications. Of clinical importance, EUS studies demonstrate that gastric varices possess thinner walls and reduced elasticity compared to esophageal varices [29], thereby rendering them more susceptible to rupture under elevated portal pressure [30]. Consequently, patients with gastric varices, particularly those with additional risk factors such as smoking or CP, warrant enhanced endoscopic surveillance. For individuals identified as high-risk, primary prophylactic therapy warrants consideration.

Among the 107 patients with acute pancreatitis, 27 (25.2%) experienced recurrent episodes during follow-up. However, no significant association with bleeding risk was observed in this subgroup, though no formal statistical comparison was performed due to the limited sample size.

Kaplan-Meier analysis that accounts for varying follow-up times demonstrated a time-dependent increase in the cumulative incidence of bleeding, reaching approximately 30% at 3 years. By comparison, the crude proportion of bleeding was 11.6% (24/207), which likely underestimates the true long-term risk as it does not account for the limited median follow-up and right-censoring. Notably, this follow-up duration may be insufficient to capture the full spectrum of long-term sequelae, given the chronic and progressive nature of CP and its well-documented potential for late vascular complications, such as splanchnic vein thrombosis [31, 32]. This potential underestimation may particularly affect patients at risk for late-onset complications, a frequent limitation in longitudinal studies of chronic diseases where extended follow-up is crucial for accurate risk assessment. Therefore, large-scale, prospective multicenter studies with extended follow-up are essential to precisely define the natural history of CP-associated PPH, which is crucial for developing evidence-based long-term management strategies. Several important limitations should be acknowledged. The moderate sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly for the analysis of prophylactic interventions. The single-center retrospective design and non-randomized treatment allocation may introduce selection bias, such as the preferential use of anticoagulation in patients with thrombosis.

In conclusion, this study suggests that smoking, CP, and diabetes mellitus may be associated with variceal bleeding in patients with pancreatitis-associated PPH, while a higher platelet count might confer protection. These findings provide preliminary insights for risk stratification but require validation in prospective, multicenter studies.

The efficacy of specific interventions, such as non-selective beta-blockers or endoscopic variceal ligation, requires validation in large-scale, randomized controlled trials to inform evidence-based management guidelines for this distinct form of portal hypertension.
