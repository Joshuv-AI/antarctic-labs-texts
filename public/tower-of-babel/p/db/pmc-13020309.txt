Dusp15 modulates mtHsp70 Thr116 phosphorylation state to preserve mito-UPR and attenuate cardiac dysfunction in diabetic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Yan Liu, Hongshuo Shi, Chun Li, Haowen Zhuang, Yinglong Li, Xiuling He, Ying Jiang, Zhiyu Jin, Zhijiang Guo, Sangbing Ong, Yuanyuan Wang, Xing Chang, Guipeng An, Junyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) involves cardiac dysfunction/remodeling with mitochondrial stress and impaired mitochondrial proteostasis. The role of dual-specificity phosphatases (DUSPs) in these processes remains unclear. We examined whether Dusp15 modulates diabetic cardiac injury and whether mtHsp70/mito-UPR-linked proteostasis is involved.MethodsDCM was induced in mice by high-fat diet (HFD) combined with low-dose streptozotocin (STZ). We studied cardiomyocyte-specific Dusp15 knockout (Dusp15Cko) mice, a Dusp15 gain-of-function line, and high-glucose-treated HL-1 cardiomyocytes. Cardiac function/remodeling were assessed by echocardiography and molecular/histological analyses. Dusp15-mtHsp70 signaling was interrogated by protein interaction assays and mtHsp70 Thr116 genetic models.ResultsDusp15 was reduced in diabetic hearts and associated with impaired contractility. Dusp15 gain-of-function improved cardiac function and reduced remodeling/inflammation, whereas Dusp15Cko worsened diabetic injury, indicating a cardiomyocyte-necessary role for Dusp15. Dusp15 associated with mtHsp70 and supported mtHsp70-linked mitochondrial proteostasis/mito-UPR in cardiomyocytes. Genetically, mtHsp70T116A knock-in mice were substantially protected from diabetic cardiac dysfunction/remodeling. Finally, dapagliflozin (DAPA) improved diabetic cardiac outcomes, and its benefit was reduced in Dusp15Cko mice, suggesting Dusp15 as an important mediator.ConclusionDusp15 is a stress-responsive regulator that protects against diabetic cardiac dysfunction and remodeling through mtHsp70-associated mito-UPR signaling. Targeting the Dusp15–mtHsp70 axis may represent a therapeutic strategy for diabetic cardiomyopathy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03125-z.

Introduction

Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) represents a severe cardiovascular complication of diabetes, characterized by ventricular enlargement, myocardial hypertrophy, and impaired cardiac function independent of coronary artery disease or hypertension [1, 2]. The diagnosis of DCM involves detecting impaired ventricular relaxation and contractility, reduced ejection fraction, myocardial hypertrophy, and excessive extracellular matrix deposition [3]. The pathogenesis of DCM is multifaceted, involving disrupted metabolism, aberrant inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular stress, and advanced glycation end-products [4–8]. These factors collectively contribute to progressive cardiac damage and dysfunction. Despite tight glycemic control being the cornerstone of diabetes management, it remains insufficient to prevent or reverse DCM progression [9–11], highlighting the urgent need to elucidate underlying mechanisms and develop therapies that optimize glucose homeostasis while conferring cardioprotection.

The dual-specificity phosphatase (Dusp) family comprises enzymes that dephosphorylate both tyrosine and serine/threonine residues, thereby regulating critical cellular signaling pathways [12]. In diabetes, Dusps influence glucose metabolism and insulin signaling through diverse mechanisms. For instance, Dusp1 expression is downregulated in the myocardium of streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats, contributing to cardiac dysfunction [13–15]. Deletion of Dusp2 protects against diet-induced obesity and hepatic steatosis by improving glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity [16]. Dusp3 deficiency reduces high-fat diet (HFD)-induced weight gain and hepatic steatosis by inhibiting adipocyte differentiation and enhancing HDAC1/2 phosphorylation and activity [17]. Conversely, Dusp5 knockout exacerbates diabetes-associated symptoms in mice with type 1 and type 2 diabetes through AMPK-mediated inhibition of autophagy and GRP-78-driven endoplasmic reticulum stress [18]. In contrast, Dusp9 overexpression restores IRS-1 tyrosine phosphorylation, enhancing insulin signaling, improving glycemic control, and reducing hepatic steatosis in obese diabetic mice [19]. Furthermore, deletion of Dusp26 results in elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and oxidative damage, thereby exacerbating diabetic nephropathy [20]. Collectively, the Dusp family plays pivotal roles in regulating glucose metabolism, insulin resistance, inflammation, apoptosis, and mitochondrial function in diabetic contexts. Recent studies have highlighted the cardioprotective properties of the Dusp family in various cardiovascular diseases, including ischemia/reperfusion injury [21], septic cardiomyopathy [22, 23], cardiac hypertrophy [24], and doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity [25]. While the involvement of the Dusp family in DCM has been preliminarily investigated [26, 27], the specific members that act as primary regulators and their downstream mechanisms remain poorly understood.

The mitochondrial unfolded protein response (mito-UPR) constitutes a protective cellular mechanism activated by the accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins within mitochondria [28]. This adaptive response restores mitochondrial function by enhancing the expression of mitochondrial chaperones and proteases, thereby promoting proper protein folding and facilitating the degradation of damaged proteins [29]. Among these chaperones, mtHsp70 (also known as mortalin or GRP75) plays a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial proteostasis by facilitating proper folding of nascent mitochondrial proteins and mediating the degradation of misfolded proteins [30, 31]. Studies have demonstrated the role of mtHsp70 in mitigating oxidative stress by detoxifying ROS in diabetic nephropathy [32]. Notably, mtHsp70 expression is significantly downregulated in yellow catfish fed an HFD, whereas choline supplementation restores mito-UPR activity, enhances mitochondrial respiration, and promotes fatty acid β-oxidation [33]. Similarly, in DCM, mtHsp70 downregulation disrupts mitochondrial proteostasis and impairs protein import efficiency [34]. Cardiac-specific mtHsp70 transgenic mice in a db/db diabetic background exhibit improved cardiac function, restored nuclear-encoded mitochondrial protein import, and enhanced mitochondrial performance compared to wild-type db/db mice [34]. Recent findings have revealed that mtHsp70 undergoes extensive post-translational modifications, with phosphorylation occurring at more than 50 serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues [35]. These modifications serve as critical determinants of mtHsp70 stability [36]. For instance, c-Src-mediated phosphorylation of Hsp60, a known mtHsp70 partner, at Y227 is associated with its proteasomal degradation [37], while aggregated α-synuclein impairs mtHsp70 function by facilitating its proteasomal degradation [38]. These observations suggest that phosphorylation-mediated post-translational modifications are key drivers of mtHsp70 degradation.

Despite evidence of reduced mtHsp70 expression in diabetic conditions, the underlying mechanisms and upstream regulators governing its phosphorylation remain poorly understood. Given the dephosphorylation activity of Dusps, it is compelling to investigate whether specific Dusp family members regulate mtHsp70 in the context of DCM. Elucidating this relationship may provide novel insights into mitochondrial dysfunction in DCM and identify promising therapeutic targets for this devastating complication of diabetes.

Methods

Mice

Cardiomyocyte-specific Dusp15 knockout (Dusp15Cko) mice were generated by crossing floxed Dusp15 mice (C57BL/6JGpt-Dusp15em1Cflox/Gpt, stock no: T021933; GemPharmatech, San Diego, CA, USA) with α-MHC (alpha myosin heavy chain)-Cre transgenic mice (α-MHCCre+). These lines were maintained on a C57BL/6J background and backcrossed with C57BL/6J wild-type mice for at least six generations before being used for experiments. In all experiments involving this line, Dusp15flox/flox; α-MHCCre+ (Dusp15Cko) mice were compared with Dusp15flox/flox littermate controls lacking the Cre transgene on the same C57BL/6J background. Transgenic Dusp15-overexpressing mice (Dusp15Tg) were engineered by Cyagen (Santa Clara, CA, USA) and backcrossed to C57BL/6J for at least six generations before use. For experiments with Dusp15Tg mice, age- and sex-matched C57BL/6J wild-type (WT) mice from the same colony were used as controls. A schematic representation of the construction of Dusp15Cko and Dusp15Tg mice is shown in Supplemental Figure 1. All mouse lines were maintained on a C57BL/6J background under specific pathogen-free conditions at Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine, with a 12-h light/dark cycle, controlled temperature and humidity, and unrestricted access to food and water.

To model DCM [39], we employed an HFD combined with low-dose streptozotocin (STZ) injections, as previously described with modifications [40]. To minimize experimental variability arising from hormonal fluctuations in female animals, only male mice were used in this study. Briefly, C57BL/6J mice were fed an HFD (Research Diets, D12492) for 8 weeks to induce insulin resistance, followed by intraperitoneal administration of STZ (35 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, S0130) dissolved in sodium citrate buffer (50 mmol/L, pH 4.5) for three consecutive days. The mice continued on the HFD for an additional 16 weeks. Control mice received a standard chow diet (Research Diets, D12450J) and citrate buffer injections. The HFD consisted of 60% fat, 20% protein, and 20% carbohydrates, while the control diet comprised 10% fat, 20% protein, and 70% carbohydrates. Blood glucose levels and body weights were monitored biweekly, and diabetes was defined as random blood glucose exceeding 16.7 mmol/L on two consecutive measurements (Supplemental Fig. 2). Cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography.

After the completion of STZ injections, hyperglycemia was confirmed 2 weeks later. At this point, when STZ treatment had been completed for 4 weeks and the second 4-week period of HFD feeding had been completed, diabetic and age-matched non-diabetic mice were randomized to receive biweekly intraperitoneal injections of DCEM1 (60 mg/kg; Cat. No. DC74309, DC Chemicals) or vehicle for 12 weeks [41]. For assessing cardioprotection, dapagliflozin (DAPA, 40 mg/kg/day) was administered via daily intraperitoneal injection into diabetic mice. At the experimental endpoint, whole blood was collected via cardiac puncture using heparin (10 U/mL) as an anticoagulant, and plasma was isolated by centrifugation. Hearts were perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove blood, weighed, and either processed for histological analysis or snap-frozen at − 80 °C for subsequent molecular and biochemical studies.

Histological analysis

Mouse heart tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 μm thickness, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for structural assessment. Collagen deposition was visualized using Sirius Red or Masson's Trichrome staining according to standard protocols. Oil Red O staining and periodic acid-silver methenamine (PASM) staining were performed to assess diabetes-induced injury in the liver and kidney, respectively. Quantification of Sirius Red- or Masson's Trichrome-positive areas was performed using ImageJ software and expressed as the percentage of the total section area.

Cell culture

HL-1 cardiomyocytes, obtained from the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resource (Beijing, China), were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For hyperglycemic stress induction, cells at 80–85% confluence were exposed to high glucose (HG, 30 mmol/L) for 48 h. To block mito-UPR activity, HL-1 cells were pretreated with DCEM1 (20 μM, Cat. No. DC74309, DC Chemicals) for 1 h prior to HG exposure. To assess the half-life of mtHsp70, cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX, 100 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for various time intervals, followed by Western blot analysis. To inhibit Dusp15 phosphatase activity, cells were pretreated with sodium orthovanadate (SO, 10 mM, Cat. No. HY-D0852, MedChemExpress) for 6 h before HG exposure. Proteasome activity was modulated using either the inhibitor MG132 (2 μM, Cat. No. HY-13259, MedChemExpress) or the activator betulinic acid (10 μM, Cat. No. HY-N1451, MedChemExpress) for 6 h prior to HG treatment. DAPA (10 μM) was administered 24 h before HG treatment to evaluate cardioprotective effects.

Cardiomyocyte isolation and functional assessments

Ventricular cardiomyocytes were isolated using an enzymatic dissociation protocol. Briefly, mice were heparinized (500 IU/kg, i.p.) and anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane (#100150, Shanghai Yuyan Instruments Co., Ltd). Following thoracotomy, hearts were rapidly excised and immersed in ice-cold, oxygenated Ca2⁺-free Tyrode's solution containing (in mmol/L): 137 NaCl, 5.4 KCl, 1.2 MgCl₂, 1.2 NaH₂PO₄, 10 glucose, and 20 Hepes (pH 7.35). The aorta was cannulated and perfused using a Langendorff apparatus with oxygenated Tyrode's solution at 37 °C for 5 min, followed by digestion buffer containing 0.7 mg/mL collagenase II (#4176, Worthington) supplemented with taurine and 2,3-butanedione monoxime. Once the heart became flaccid, the ventricles were dissected, and the myocardial tissue was mechanically dispersed. Calcium was reintroduced incrementally to a final concentration of 1.2 mM for electrophysiological analyses and 1.8 mM for measurements of contractility and calcium transients [42–44].

Isolation of cardiac endothelial cells and fibroblasts

Cardiac endothelial cells (ECs) and fibroblasts were isolated from adult mouse hearts as previously described with minor modifications. Briefly, mice were heparinized and euthanized, and hearts were rapidly excised, rinsed in ice-cold PBS to remove blood, minced into small pieces, and digested at 37 °C in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing collagenase type II (1–2 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) and DNase I (0.1 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) with gentle agitation for 20–30 min. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in PBS/0.5% BSA and subjected to magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) using anti-CD31 (PECAM-1) microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The CD31+ fraction was collected as cardiac ECs, and the CD31− fraction was plated in fibroblast growth medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin).

Western blot and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) analyses

Western blot and co-IP analyses were performed as previously reported. Samples were lysed using RIPA buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 50 mM Tris, pH 8.0) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay. Protein lysates were separated on SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to PVDF membranes (MerckMillipore, IPVH00010), and probed with primary antibodies listed in Supplemental Table 1. Immunoreactive bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence and quantified by densitometry.

For co-IP, lysates were prepared using IP buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% Triton X-100, and protease inhibitors) and incubated at 4 °C for 1.5 h. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min, supernatants were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Flag M2 affinity gel (Sigma-Aldrich). Beads were washed extensively with IP buffer, and immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer and analyzed by Western blot.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Heart tissues were processed for TEM following established protocols. Samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h at 4 °C, washed in phosphate buffer, and post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h. Specimens were stained with 2% uranyl acetate, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in epoxy resin (Embed812 EMS). Ultrathin Sects. (60 nm) were cut using a Leica Ultra Cut UCT ultramicrotome, contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and imaged using a JEM-1400 Plus transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) at 80 kV.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from heart tissues or cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Homogenized samples were mixed with chloroform, shaken vigorously, and centrifuged to isolate the aqueous phase, which was then precipitated with isopropanol. RNA pellets were washed with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and dissolved in nuclease-free water. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by spectrophotometry. Reverse transcription was performed using M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen), and qRT-PCR was carried out using SYBR Select Master Mix (Roche) on the ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used are detailed in Supplemental Table 2.

Echocardiography

Cardiac function was evaluated using the Vevo® 2100 High-Resolution Ultrasound System (FUJIFILM VisualSonics). Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and positioned supine on a heated platform for imaging. Parasternal long-axis views were obtained to measure left ventricular (LV) dimensions and wall thickness in systole and diastole using M-mode echocardiography. Apical four-chamber views were used to assess mitral inflow velocity (E wave) and mitral annular tissue velocity (E′ wave) by pulsed-wave Doppler, with the E/E′ ratio calculated to estimate LV diastolic function. Heart rate and body temperature were continuously monitored throughout imaging procedures.

LC–MS/MS proteomic analysis

Proteomic profiling was conducted on cardiac tissue samples. Tissues were homogenized in SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.6) and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad). Following tryptic digestion, peptides were fractionated using the Thermo Scientific™ Pierce™ High pH Reversed-Phase Peptide Fractionation Kit and analyzed with a Bruker timsTOF mass spectrometer coupled to an Evosep One liquid chromatography system. Data-independent acquisition (DIA) was employed for peptide identification and quantification, with Spectronaut™ software used for spectral library searches. Results were filtered based on a Q value cutoff of 0.01, ensuring a false discovery rate (FDR) below 1%.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Gene sets corresponding to known Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways and biological processes from the Gene Ontology (GO) database were utilized for enrichment analysis. Differential gene expression was assessed using the Java-based GSEA platform (version 3.0), employing the "Signal2Noise" metric. Pathways or processes with a p-value < 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched.

KEGG pathway analysis

Enrichment analysis for KEGG pathways was conducted using Fisher's exact test to identify significant associations with differentially expressed genes. Pathway annotations were obtained from the KEGG database, and statistical significance was defined as p-values < 0.05.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed in pre-chilled methanol/acetone (1:1) at − 20 °C for 10 min, rinsed three times with PBS, and blocked with 5% normal goat serum (Eurobio, Les Ulis, France) at room temperature for 15 min. Primary antibodies (Supplemental Table 1) were applied overnight at 4 °C in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100. After washing with PBS, samples were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL), and visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica SP5X).

Lentiviral vector construction and packaging

Lentiviral vectors for overexpressing or silencing Dusp15 and mtHsp70 were generated using standard molecular cloning techniques. Coding sequences for murine Dusp15 and mtHsp70 were synthesized (RuiBiotech, Beijing) and verified by Sanger sequencing. Overexpression constructs were cloned into the pInducer20 vector (Addgene #44012), while short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences for knockdown experiments were inserted into the GV248 lentiviral backbone (Genechem, Shanghai). Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells co-transfected with the lentiviral plasmids and helper plasmids (pRSV-Rev, pMDLg/pRRE, and pMD2.G) using polyethyleneimine (PEI). Viral supernatants were harvested 48 h post-transfection, treated with Benzonase (50 U/mL; EMD Chemicals) to eliminate residual DNA, filtered through 0.45 μm filters, and titered using a p24 ELISA kit (Cell Biolabs, #VPK-107-5).

Cell transfection

Plasmid transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 70–80% confluence, and transfection complexes were prepared by mixing plasmids with Lipofectamine 3000 in Opti-MEM medium. Transfection of microRNA (miRNA) mimics or inhibitors (Ribobio, China) was carried out using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, USA) at a final concentration of 100 nM.

Plasmid construction and expression

Recombinant adenoviruses were constructed by cloning full-length or truncated sequences of Dusp15 and mtHsp70 into appropriate expression vectors. Constructs included Myc-tagged Dusp15 and its domain deletion mutants, as well as HA-tagged mtHsp70 mutants lacking specific functional domains. All constructs were sequence-verified and transduced into HL-1 cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. Cell viability was assessed to confirm the absence of cytotoxicity. Plasmids were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 for expression studies and subsequent biochemical analyses.

Mitochondrial ROS and membrane potential measurements

For in vitro analyses, HL-1 cells were loaded with MitoSOX Red (5 μM; Thermo Fisher Scientific) in Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) at 37 °C for 25 min in the dark to measure mitochondrial superoxide production. Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using the JC-1 probe (Biotium, #30001) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescence was measured both qualitatively by confocal microscopy and quantitatively by flow cytometry, with emission recorded at 580 nm upon excitation at 510 nm.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of IL-6, TNFα, MCP1, and norepinephrine were quantified using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Briefly, whole blood was collected, allowed to clot at room temperature, and centrifuged to isolate serum; aliquots were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Mouse IL-6 and TNFα were measured using Quantikine ELISA kits (R&D Systems/Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, MN, USA). MCP1 was measured using a pre-coated sandwich ELISA (LEGEND MAX™, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Norepinephrine (noradrenaline) was determined using a competitive ELISA with extraction/acylation (Noradrenaline ELISA Fast Track, LDN Labor Diagnostika Nord, Nordhorn, Germany). The activity of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9) in cardiac tissue homogenates was quantified using a commercially available MMP9 ELISA kit (eBioscience, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Samples and standards were assayed in duplicate, concentrations were calculated from standard curves, and values were normalized to serum volume.

Protein stability and pulse-chase analysis

To assess protein stability, HL-1 cells transduced with lentiviral constructs were serum-starved (0.5% FBS) for 24 h before treatment with cycloheximide (100 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) at specified time intervals. Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche), and lysates were analyzed by Western blot to determine protein degradation kinetics. Pulse-chase experiments involved radiolabeling HL-1 cells with [35S]-methionine (50 μCi/mL) in methionine-free medium, followed by chase periods in complete medium. Immunoprecipitation with protein G beads and specific antibodies enabled subsequent analysis of protein turnover by autoradiography and Western blot.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Sample sizes for both biological and technical replicates are specified in the figure legends. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests, while multiple group comparisons were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with appropriate post-hoc tests (Tukey's or Bonferroni). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), as indicated in the figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Dusp15 is a key biomarker of the Dusp family in diabetic cardiomyopathy

To identify pivotal regulatory proteins implicated in the pathogenesis of DCM, we performed transcriptomic analysis using the publicly available dataset GSE161931, which contains expression profiling data from heart tissue of db/db mice—a well-established model of spontaneous diabetic cardiomyopathy—with BKS mice serving as healthy controls (Fig. 1A). Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses (Fig. 1B, C) identified "protein tyrosine/serine/threonine phosphatase activity" and "MAP kinase phosphatase activity" as prominently enriched molecular functions (MF), alongside mitochondrial-related biological processes (BP) and cellular components (CC). Given the established association between the Dusp family and phosphatase activity [12], we conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the MF term "protein tyrosine/serine/threonine phosphatase activity" to identify contributing genes (Fig. 1D). To further substantiate the role of Dusp15 in regulating myocardial dysfunction associated with DCM, we examined its relationship with the "heart contraction" phenotype-a biological process markedly downregulated in DCM. Single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) scores for "heart contraction" were correlated with the expression levels of Dusp family genes, identifying the top ten Dusps most strongly associated with this phenotype (Fig. 1E, F). Notably, Dusp15 expression exhibited a significant positive correlation with "heart contraction," suggesting its critical role in maintaining cardiac function in DCM.

To elucidate the mechanistic role of Dusp15 in DCM, we analyzed the single-cell transcriptomic dataset from a DCM mouse model [45]. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analyses (Fig. 1G–I) delineated distinct cardiac cell subpopulations, with cell identities validated through marker gene expression heatmaps (Fig. 1J). Examination of Dusp15 expression across these cell subpopulations revealed pronounced downregulation of Dusp15 in DCM, particularly within cardiomyocytes (Fig. 1K). Consistent with these findings, quantitative PCR and Western blot analyses validated a significant reduction in Dusp15 expression in our DCM mouse models (Fig. 1L, M). These results underscore the central role of Dusp15 in the molecular mechanisms underlying DCM and highlight its potential as a promising therapeutic target for mitigating disease progression.

Echocardiography showed that HFD/STZ caused marked systolic and diastolic dysfunction in WT mice, whereas these impairments were substantially attenuated in Dusp15Tg mice (Fig. 2D). Consistently, cardiomyocytes isolated from diabetic Dusp15Tg hearts displayed improved shortening/relaxation properties compared with diabetic WT cardiomyocytes (Fig. 2E). Histological and molecular analyses further demonstrated that HFD/STZ-induced myocardial disarray, fibrosis, and inflammatory responses were markedly reduced in Dusp15Tg hearts (Fig. 2F–P), accompanied by preservation of myocardial ultrastructure and attenuation of injury biomarkers (Fig. 2Q–R).

Because the Dusp15Tg line is not strictly cardiomyocyte-specific, we directly examined Dusp15 expression in non-cardiomyocyte populations. Endothelial cells (ECs) and cardiac fibroblasts (CFs) were isolated and analyzed by immunoblotting. In WT mice, hyperglycemia only modestly affected Dusp15 levels in ECs and did not significantly change Dusp15 in CFs (Supplemental Figure 3B–C). Notably, Dusp15 expression was increased in both ECs and CFs from Dusp15Tg hearts (Supplemental Figure 3B–C), indicating that transgene enrichment is not confined to cardiomyocytes. Functionally, ECs in the Dusp15Tg setting showed improved stress resistance under high glucose, and perivascular fibrosis was reduced in Dusp15Tg hearts after HFD/STZ (Supplemental Figure 3D–F). Together, these data support an overall cardiac protective phenotype in Dusp15Tg mice, to which non-cardiomyocyte compartments-particularly ECs-may contribute.

To test whether cardiomyocyte Dusp15 is required to limit hyperglycemia-induced cardiac injury, we generated cardiomyocyte-specific Dusp15 knockout mice (Dusp15Cko). Importantly, Dusp15 ablation was verified in isolated cardiomyocytes from Dusp15Cko hearts, whereas Dusp15 levels were preserved in ECs and CFs isolated from the same hearts (Supplemental Figure 1B), confirming cardiomyocyte-restricted recombination. Under HFD/STZ, Dusp15Cko mice exhibited exacerbated cardiac dysfunction and myocardial injury compared with Dusp15f/f controls (Supplemental Figure 4A–F). Collectively, these data indicate that Dusp15Tg mice display cardiac-level protection with potential multi-cellular contributions, while cardiomyocyte Dusp15 is required to constrain hyperglycemia-induced myocardial injury.

Suppression of mito-UPR due to Dusp15 deficiency in DCM

To investigate Dusp15's protective mechanisms in DCM, we analyzed downstream pathways using single-cell RNA-seq data [45] from 16,490 cardiac cells from 6 healthy controls and 16,095 cells from 6 HFD/STZ-induced diabetic mice (Supplemental Fig. 5A). In the DCM group, ventricular cardiomyocytes were stratified into high-Dusp15 (hiDusp15) and low-Dusp15 (loDusp15) subgroups based on median Dusp15 expression levels (Supplemental Figure 5A). Gene Ontology analysis revealed DCM-associated disruptions in mitochondrial organization, matrix, and nucleoid, with enriched pathways including chaperone activity, heat shock protein binding, and unfolded protein response (UPR) (Supplemental Figure 5B). KEGG analysis identified dysregulated pathways including oxidative phosphorylation, cardiac muscle contraction, diabetic cardiomyopathy, and pyruvate metabolism (Supplemental Figure 5C). GSEA further highlighted mito-UPR-associated pathways, including mitochondrial protein import and cellular stress responses (Supplemental Figure 5D). Single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) scoring demonstrated that higher mito-UPR activity aligned with the hiDusp15 subgroup in DCM ventricular cardiomyocytes (Supplemental Figure 5E–F). Broad analysis across cardiac cell types revealed elevated UPR activity, most prominently in ventricular cardiomyocytes (Supplemental Figure 5G). These findings underscore Dusp15's critical role in regulating mitochondrial unfolded protein response (mito-UPR) and stress adaptation during DCM progression.

To unravel the mechanisms by which Dusp15 overexpression confers cardioprotection, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to evaluate transcriptional changes in the hearts of diabetic WT and Dusp15Tg mice. Of the 2081 genes exhibiting significant expression changes (log₂FC > 1.5), 765 reached statistical significance (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3A). Differential expression analysis revealed 483 upregulated and 282 downregulated genes (Fig. 3A). To evaluate potential compensation within the Dusp family, we analyzed Dusp members at both transcript and protein levels. In cardiac RNA-seq (HFD/STZ WT vs. Dusp15Tg), no Dusp transcript showed significant differential expression (Supplementary Figure 6A). At the protein level, HFD/STZ was associated with reduced Dusp1 and increased Dusp2/4/5/10/22, whereas Dusp15 overexpression did not further alter these changes (Supplementary Figure 6B). These data argue against a major Dusp-family compensation detectable at the mRNA level or a broad reshaping of the measured Dusp protein profile, although subtle activity-based compensation cannot be excluded. Pathway enrichment analysis using the KEGG revealed significant activation of protein export and digestion pathways in diabetic Dusp15Tg hearts, indicating that Dusp15 overexpression enhances protein handling and degradation capacity under diabetic conditions (Fig. 3B). GO Biological Process analysis highlighted prominent enrichment of pathways related to response to unfolded protein, mitochondrial UPR, and mitochondrial organization, while Cellular Component analysis suggested marked enrichment of genes associated with mitochondrial envelope, mitochondrial inner membrane, and mitochondrial matrix (Fig. 3C). Additionally, GO Molecular Function analysis demonstrated enrichment of heat shock protein binding in Dusp15Tg hearts (Fig. 3C). GSEA further corroborated the activation of pathways linked to "response to unfolded protein," "mitochondrion organization," "protein digestion and absorption," "protein export," "mitochondrial unfolded protein response," and "heat shock protein binding" in the hearts of diabetic Dusp15Tg mice (Fig. 3D–I). Notably, within the category of "heat shock protein binding," GSEA identified a strong association between Dusp15 overexpression and increased expression of mtHsp70 (Fig. 3I), a key regulator of mitochondrial proteostasis. mtHsp70 links Dusp15 to mito-UPR preservation and protects against hyperglycemia-induced myocardial dysfunction

To identify a proximal node linking Dusp15 to mito-UPR preservation, we focused on mtHsp70. Dusp15 overexpression did not change mtHsp70 mRNA (Supplemental Figure 8A) but restored mtHsp70 protein levels reduced by HFD/STZ, whereas Lonp1 was not rescued (Supplemental Figure 8B). In HL-1 cardiomyocytes, mtHsp70 knockdown abolished Dusp15-mediated preservation of mito-UPR signaling under high glucose (Supplemental Figure 8C). Consistently, cycloheximide chase assays showed that high glucose accelerated mtHsp70 turnover, which was attenuated by Dusp15 overexpression (Supplemental Figure 8D), supporting post-transcriptional stabilization. Broad pharmacologic modulation (MG132, betulinic acid, and sodium orthovanadate) showed changes consistent with our model; however, because these agents are not highly specific, the findings are presented as supportive evidence rather than definitive mechanistic proof (Supplemental Figure 8E-F). Co-IP and domain-mapping analyses further supported a Dusp15-mtHsp70 interaction in heart tissue and HG-treated HL-1 cells, requiring the Dusp15 phosphatase domain and the mtHsp70 nucleotide-binding domain (Supplemental Figure 8G-K). Consistently, Dusp15ΔPD accelerated HG-induced mtHsp70 loss, and orthovanadate weakened Dusp15-mtHsp70 association with a concomitant reduction of mtHsp70 (Supplemental Figure 9), supporting a role for Dusp15 phosphatase activity in maintaining mtHsp70 stability under hyperglycemic stress.

To test whether mtHsp70 is a determinant of diabetic cardiac injury, we generated mtHsp70-overexpressing (mtHsp70Tg) mice and cardiomyocyte-specific mtHsp70 knockout (mtHsp70Cko) mice. In vivo, mtHsp70Tg mice were protected from HFD/STZ-induced cardiac dysfunction, fibrosis, and inflammatory responses, whereas mtHsp70Cko mice exhibited the opposite phenotype (Supplemental Figures 10A-E and 11A-E). In HL-1 cardiomyocytes, mtHsp70 overexpression mitigated, while mtHsp70 knockdown exacerbated, high glucose-induced mitochondrial dysfunction as reflected by ΔΨm collapse and mtROS accumulation (Supplemental Figures 10F-G and 11F-G).

mtHsp70 Thr116 phosphorylation state mediates Dusp15-associated mitochondrial protection under hyperglycemic stress

Having established mtHsp70 as a key node linking Dusp15 to mito-UPR preservation, we next asked whether the phosphorylation status of mtHsp70 contributes to its stability and to the protective phenotype. Sequence inspection identified a conserved TFY motif encompassing Thr116-Tyr118 (Supplemental Figure 12A). Immunoblotting indicated that HG increased the mtHsp70 Thr116 phospho-signal, whereas Tyr118 was not detectably altered in vitro or in vivo (Supplemental Figures 12B and 13). Dusp15 overexpression reduced the HG-associated Thr116 phospho-signal, and this reduction was blunted by orthovanadate; given its limited specificity, this finding is presented as supportive, not definitive, evidence (Supplemental Figure 13).

To functionally interrogate the Thr116 site, we employed phosphorylation-defective (mtHsp70T116A) and phosphomimetic (mtHsp70T116D) mutants. mtHsp70T116A delayed HG-induced mtHsp70 loss and prolonged mtHsp70 half-life, whereas mtHsp70T116D diminished the stabilization associated with Dusp15 overexpression (Supplemental Figure 12C-D). Consistently, mtHsp70T116A or Dusp15 overexpression improved mitochondrial integrity under HG (restored ΔΨm and reduced mtROS), while these effects were not observed in mtHsp70T116D-expressing cells (Supplemental Figure 14A-B). Likewise, Dusp15-mediated preservation of cardiomyocyte viability and mito-UPR signaling was reduced in the mtHsp70T116D context (Supplemental Figure 14C-D).

Together, these data indicate that Thr116 phosphorylation of mtHsp70 is a critical determinant of hyperglycemia-induced mitochondrial stress and myocardial dysfunction in DCM, while residual fibrosis likely reflects additional mtHsp70-independent pro-fibrotic pathways and/or multicellular contributions to remodeling.

Similarly, DAPA reduced diabetes-induced myocardial fibrosis and inflammatory responses in WT hearts, while these benefits were largely lost in Dusp15Cko mice (Fig. 6J–O). Mechanistically, DAPA was associated with preservation of mtHsp70 and attenuation of the HG-induced phosphorylation signal, together with restoration of mito-UPR activity in WT hearts; these effects were blunted in Dusp15Cko mice (Supplemental Figure 16A-B). Collectively, these results support that Dusp15 is required for the full cardioprotective response to DAPA and implicate normalization of the Dusp15/mtHsp70 axis as a therapeutic entry point for DCM.

Discussion

In this study, using complementary genetic models and multi-level analyses, we identify Dusp15 as a stress-responsive regulator linked to mitochondrial proteostasis in experimental diabetic cardiomyopathy. Across HFD/STZ-induced DCM, Dusp15 levels track with cardiac contractile performance, and gain- and loss-of-function perturbations consistently modulate myocardial dysfunction, fibrosis, inflammatory responses, and mitochondrial stress. These data support a model in which impaired Dusp15-mtHsp70-mito-UPR signaling contributes to diabetic cardiac remodeling, and restoring this axis confers protection.

The mitochondrial unfolded protein response (mito-UPR) is an adaptive program that safeguards mitochondrial proteostasis under stress [28, 29]. In the heart, mito-UPR supports mitochondrial function [30, 31], whereas in DCM these protective mechanisms are compromised and contribute to apoptosis, fibrosis and contractile dysfunction [33, 34]. Our results extend this framework by linking Dusp15 to mtHsp70-associated mito-UPR competence during diabetic stress.

A recent study nominated Dusp15 as a potential DCM biomarker [46], motivating our mechanistic work. Using Dusp15-based stratification, we linked Dusp15 to mito-UPR-related programs and mtHsp70-associated mitochondrial proteostasis beyond canonical MAPK-centric DUSP signaling [12, 47]. Dusp family proteins are dual-specificity phosphatases that can modulate phosphorylation-dependent signaling and protein function [12], and Dusp15 represents a member with distinct regulatory properties and cellular targets [48]. Although Dusp15 showed the strongest association with cardiac contractility among DUSPs, RNA-seq and DUSP protein profiling did not support a coordinated compensatory response across other DUSPs in Dusp15 gain-of-function hearts; however, because DUSPs act at the protein/enzyme level, we restrict this statement to the absence of broad transcriptomic or measured DUSP-protein remodeling, and activity-based compensation remains possible.

We note several considerations that temper mechanistic interpretation. First, the pharmacologic tools used to probe proteostasis/phosphatase dependence (MG132, betulinic acid, and orthovanadate) are broad-acting and not highly specific; therefore, these results should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive evidence for the precise degradation machinery or phosphatase requirement. Second, the cardioprotection observed in Dusp15Tg mice cannot be attributed to cardiomyocytes alone, as Dusp15 is also elevated in non-cardiomyocyte compartments (including ECs and fibroblasts), and we did not systematically profile additional cardiac cell populations or extra-cardiac tissues; thus, endocrine/neurohumoral or immune-cell contributions cannot be excluded and the Dusp15Tg phenotype should be interpreted as a heart-level benefit with multicellular contributions. In parallel, while the Dusp15Cko model supports necessity of cardiomyocyte Dusp15 for limiting hyperglycemia-induced myocardial injury, it does not establish that cardiomyocyte-restricted gain-of-function is sufficient to reproduce the full transgenic protection. Third, because the mtHsp70T116A/A allele is global, extra-cardiac contributions cannot be fully excluded even though systemic metabolic disturbance and gross hepato-renal injury were comparable across genotypes, arguing against major systemic confounding. Finally, the HFD/STZ model and DCEM1 (used as a pharmacologic stressor of mito-UPR) may not fully recapitulate human DCM progression and lack selectivity; accordingly, the Dusp15-mtHsp70-mito-UPR axis likely represents a major but not exclusive mechanism. Future work will prioritize cell type-specific/conditional models and orthogonal, site-resolved validation (e.g., LC–MS/MS confirmation of Thr116 phosphorylation and biochemical controls using established phosphatase substrates) to strengthen substrate-level evidence and clarify compartment-specific effects.

In conclusion, our data associate Dusp15 with mtHsp70-linked mitochondrial proteostasis and preservation of mito-UPR competence in experimental DCM. Protection in mtHsp70T116A knock-in mice supports in vivo relevance and motivates future site-resolved validation and upstream kinase mapping. Dapagliflozin improved cardiac outcomes in association with increased Dusp15 and preserved mtHsp70, supporting translational interest.
