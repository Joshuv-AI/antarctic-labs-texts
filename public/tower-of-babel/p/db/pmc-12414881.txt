US Poison Center Encounters for Psilocybin-Related Exposures: 2013-2022
Authors: Juan Carlos C Montoy, Ralph C Wang, Allison R Coker, Craig G Smollin, Brian T Anderson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesGiven the increasing use of psilocybin-containing substances across a variety of use settings, understanding the potential risks is imperative for informing public health policy, health care providers, and consumers. Poison centers (PCs) receive calls following exposures to potential toxins to support the detection, prevention, and treatment of toxin-related health emergencies. This report assesses trends in PC encounters of psilocybin and a subset of other comparator substances.MethodsA retrospective study of PC encounters documenting exposure to psilocybin, other psychedelic substances (lysergic acid diethylamide, mescaline/peyote, and hallucinogenic plants), or toxic plants and mushrooms from 2013 to 2022 was performed. The primary outcome was the occurrence of psilocybin-related encounters, with and without other coingestants. Psilocybin-related encounters were presented overall and stratified by age groups, and the clinical outcomes were described.ResultsThere were 6933 PC encounters for psilocybin-containing substances between 2013 and 2022. PC encounters for psilocybin-containing substances (alone or with coingestants) increased over time—from 477 in 2013 to 1441 in 2022. Psilocybin-related encounters increased over the study period among all age groups (years) and were most common in the 18 to 24 and 25 to 44 year-old age groups. Across all years, the number of psilocybin-related encounters was similar to those for lysergic acid diethylamide and mescaline/peyote, and far lower than those for other mushrooms and toxic plants.ConclusionFrom 2013 to 2022, there was a 3-fold increase in psilocybin-related PC encounters, nearly all of which occurred since 2019. A similar pattern was not observed with other substances. Although the number of encounters remains low, this trend could continue as psilocybin use increases.Keywords: psilocybin, psychedelic, mushrooms, magic mushrooms, toxicology

The Bottom Line

Psilocybin use in both medical and nonmedical settings has increased over the last decade, and use has been associated with immediate harms. The number of poison centers encounters in the United States for psilocybin-associated harms (including sole psilocybin ingestions and polysubstance ingestions) increased from a baseline of 477 encounters in 2013 to 1441 in 2022; the trend for yearly encounters was flat through 2019, then increased by 308 encounters per year. Most cases were male (71%), and over 1/3 were among those aged 18 to 24 years, although increases in incidence were seen for all age categories (years) and genders. Most encounters (87%) received care in a healthcare facility; among those, most were treated and released, although 10% were admitted to a critical care unit.

Introduction

Background

Psilocybin-containing substances have a long history of medical and nonmedical use, and their use is increasing.1,2 With renewed interest in psilocybin for therapeutic, recreational, and/or religious purposes, use is expected to continue to rise.3, 4, 5

Psilocybin is typically derived from Psilocybe spp. “magic mushrooms” and is ingested in the form of the fruiting bodies themselves, tea, tincture, or some other preparation.6 This manuscript refers to any such psilocybin-containing preparation as “psilocybin.” There are concerns about potential harms associated with psilocybin use including risk of acute behavioral toxicity, persisting psychosis, disturbance of perception and mood, and major cardiovascular events.7,8 Many of these harms have only been evaluated using survey self reports or in the context of clinical trials, which differ significantly from nonmedical use. Since 2019, several US states and cities have decriminalized or deprioritized enforcement of laws regulating the use of psilocybin; thus, raising concerns about public health risks. Given the increasing use of psilocybin across a variety of use settings, understanding the type of risks—and their incidence—is imperative for guiding clinical care and public policy.

Importance

Poison centers (PCs) receive calls from individuals at home and from medical providers for patients in emergency departments (EDs) or other medical settings following exposures to potential toxins to support the detection, prevention, and treatment of toxin-related health emergencies. PCs have served this public health role for several decades; the National Poison Data System (NPDS) has recorded all PC cases since 2006. Thus, the NPDS provides a valuable opportunity to investigate trends. A prior study using national poison center data showed a decline in psilocybin mushroom exposures from 2004 to 2016.9 A more recent investigation identified increased calls in adolescents and young adults that started in 2019.10

Goals of This Investigation

This analysis sought to describe PC calls related to psilocybin for a recent 10-year period (2013-2022) across a broad population to assess trends in encounter volume over time.

Methods

The NPDS integrates data from any of the 55 PCs within the United States. Data include call origination location (eg, home and medical facility), patient characteristics, severity of outcome, and level of medical care needed. Data are recorded by PC staff in real time.

Inclusion Criteria

PC encounters originating from January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2022, were queried for exposure to psilocybin and select other substances using Poisindex codes. Psilocybin-related encounters were identified using code 058000, “mushrooms, hallucinogenic (psilocybin and psilocin).” Additionally, lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) (027000), mescaline/peyote (100000), and “plants: hallucinogenic” (093000) were included to serve as reference psychedelics. To provide context and address the possibility that psilocybin mushrooms were coded nonspecifically, “mushrooms: unknown” (032000) and plants: other toxic types” (097686) were included as separate drug categories. Each encounter comprised the initial call to the PC and any follow-up calls related to the same case.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the number of encounters per year reporting psilocybin ingestion, including cases with and without coingestants (i.e., at least one other substance reported). Secondary outcomes include characteristics of the encounters, including call origination location (healthcare facility, own residence, and workplace), management site (nonhealthcare facility, healthcare facility, and other), level of care (eg, no medical care, treated and released, critical care unit, and psychiatric facility), and medical outcome (no effect; minor effect; moderate effect; major effect; death; not followed, nontoxic; not followed, minimal clinical effects possible; unable to follow, potentially toxic; unrelated effect; and confirmed no-exposure). Additional data included gender, reason for exposure (unintentional, intentional, other, adverse reaction, and unknown), and treatment in a healthcare facility. The patients were grouped as per the following age categories: 0-4, 5-12, 13-17, 18-24, 25-44, 45-64, and ≥65 years. Some age observations in the dataset were recorded as ≤19 or ≥20 years and are presented as such.

Analysis

Psilocybin-related encounters per year are presented overall and stratified by gender and age category (years). Descriptive statistics are presented as counts and percentages. The yearly change in psilocybin-related encounters over the study period was estimated using linear regression; this was conducted for all psilocybin-related encounters and separately for each of psilocybin-related encounters with and without coingestants. Noting that the data appeared to have an inflexion point and a linear model may not capture changes over time, we also ran regressions using piecewise linear splines with a knot at 2019. All analyses were performed in Stata SE 18.0 (College Station).

Results

Between 2013 and 2022, there were 6933 PC encounters that were psilocybin related. During the same time period, there were 98,874 encounters for exposures to toxic plants (other) and mushrooms (other) without exposure to psilocybin (Fig 1).

Of the 6933 psilocybin-related encounters, 4729 (68.2%) were psilocybin-only ingestions; 2204 (31.8%) involved at least one other drug ingestion (Table 1; further detail is provided regarding coingestants in Supplementary Appendix 1). Encounters occurred most commonly in the 18 to 24 years age group, followed by the 25 to 44 years and 13 to17 years age groups (Fig 2). The majority (4938, 71.2%) of psilocybin-related encounters occurred for males during the study period as well as for each year.

Psilocybin-related encounters per year increased from 477 in 2013 to 1441 in 2022 (202% increase) with the majority of this increase occurring since 2019. The linear regression estimate for the yearly change in encounters during the study period was an increase of 95.6 encounters per year (95% CI, 41.6, 149.7), comprised 30.2 psilocybin-only encounters (95% CI, 15.5, 44.9) and 65.4 encounters with at least one other substance (95% CI, 25.9, 104.9). Psilocybin-related encounters increased among all age groups, including the groups for 0 to 4 years and 5 to 12 years. Among the 0 to 4 years and 5 to 12 years age groups, 346 of 348 (99.4%) and 136 of 172 (79.1%), respectively, were for unintentional exposures (Supplementary Appendix 2). In the piecewise linear model, there was no change in psilocybin-related encounters per year (13.6, 95% CI, −75, 34.8) from 2013 to 2018 and an increase of 307.7 cases per year (95% CI, 262.7, 352.7) from 2019 to 2022.

Table 2 displays the characteristics of psychedelic PC encounters, stratified by psilocybin, LSD, and mescaline/peyote. The majority of psilocybin-related encounters were placed in health care facilities (69.2%), followed by the patient’s own residence (24.0%). Similarly, the majority (81.0%) of LSD-related encounters were placed from a health care facility, and 13.0% from the patient’s own residence. Most patients with psilocybin-related encounters were treated and released from the health care facility (60.2%); 18.3% were admitted to a hospital, and 4% were admitted to a psychiatric care facility. These characteristics are reported separately for psilocybin-related cases involving no other substance or at least one other substance in Supplementary Appendix 3.

Limitations

A major limitation of inferences from PC datasets is that consulting PCs is voluntary, even in medical centers, and the degree to which these results underestimate the actual number of patients who experienced psilocybin-related reactions or sought medical care related to psilocybin or psychedelic ingestion is unknown. Relatedly, this sample could be biased toward reporting more severe cases. Second, substance coding relies on information conveyed by the caller, which may be incomplete or inaccurate. Calls to PCs may have increased over time if identification and knowledge of psilocybin by the public and/or clinicians increased over the study period, which may have occurred given the increased general awareness of psilocybin. Conversely, increased public and healthcare provider awareness and experience may have led to a decreased likelihood of PC calls for similar events, as has been seen with other substances.11 Further, dose and frequency of use are not included in the NPDS data, so we were unable to comment on the types of ingestions that lead to these calls, such as frequent microdosing or one large dose, which may have different risk profiles.12 Lastly, as only a targeted subset of psychedelic substances and unrelated substances were assessed here, this report does not contextualize the relative impact of these observed changes compared to the overall population or incidence of PC calls.

Discussion

Psilocybin-related PC encounters increased 3 fold from 2013 to 2022, with a significant increase in incidence since 2019. This pattern was not observed in the set of other psychedelic drugs queried; those patterns varied over time, with the incidence of encounters for each nonpsilocybin substance rising and falling during the study period. The total number of psilocybin-related encounters was similar to that of LSD and much smaller than the number of encounters for nonhallucinogenic toxic plants or nonpsilocybin mushrooms.

Within this increase in psilocybin-related encounters was a larger percentage increase in encounters for psilocybin with coingestants than encounters for psilocybin-only, although the incidence of both types of encounters increased over time. There was no age group (years) that appeared to drive the increase in encounters, and the incidence appeared to increase similarly for females and males. The severity of outcome for psilocybin-related encounters was similar to that for the other psychedelic substances studied here in terms of management within a health care facility (versus home or other) and level of care within health care facilities.

The use of NPDS data offers advantages over other observational methods. PCs cover all of the United States, and the NPDS provides a framework for robust data collection, transmission, and analysis for all PCs. This role is unique as other systems, such as the National Syndromic Surveillance Program or Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project cannot yet identify psilocybin exposures; in the case of National Syndromic Surveillance Program, no syndrome has yet been defined and thus cannot be observed, and in the case of enterprise data such as Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project, psilocybin exposures are not identifiable because International Classification of Diseases codes are not specific to psilocybin but instead grouped together with similar drugs (eg, F16.X hallucinogen disorder and T40.9X poisoning by hallucinogen).

Prior studies of psilocybin exposures using NPDS include investigations in adolescents and young adults, individual states, or earlier time periods.9,10,13 Our findings corroborate the earlier finding in adolescents in which the trend for psilocybin-related encounters was flat for several years before beginning to increase over the last few years, and we expand this finding to other age groups (years). Of note, we found increases among both young and elderly people, 2 groups that have been relatively unstudied. In contrast to a study conducted from 2000 to 2016, which found that psilocybin-only calls declined after 2004, we found increases in encounters for both psilocybin-only and psilocybin with coingestants, with majority of this increase occurring after 2019; LSD did not show a similar pattern of an increase since 2019. However, we observed similar trends in level of care and medical outcomes, with the majority of calls treated and released, and low prevalences of major effects and death in both psilocybin and LSD groups.9 Recent work focusing on a single state (Texas) found a decrease in psilocybin-related encounters, but this was over a different time period and limited to single-substance exposures, which does not capture all relevant encounters (Forrester 2020). This study builds on this prior work by providing a more comprehensive assessment of PC encounters for psilocybin using recent national data across all age groups (years).

Most psilocybin-related PC encounters also engaged in-person care at a healthcare facility, and patients experienced a wide range of outcomes. Although most patients were discharged, a quarter of the patients were admitted to a hospital ward, intensive care unit, or psychiatric facility, suggesting high severity among a substantial proportion of these encounters. The number of psilocybin-related ED visits during this period is unknown; therefore, we are unable to comment on the frequency of PC consults among psilocybin-related ED encounters. Psilocybin use may have increased because of several factors, including increased public awareness of potential therapeutic effects, increases in societal acceptability, drug policy changes, or changes in stressors and responses to them; these and other factors may influence future trends. As use increases and as provider knowledge of psilocybin and its possible effects including hallucinations, gastrointestinal symptoms, and serotonergic symptoms, the role that PC consults play may evolve, as it did with opioids.11

PC encounters for psilocybin exposures have increased across all ages (years) and genders over the past 10 years, with a trend of increased incidence starting in 2019. Although the absolute number of encounters remains well below those for nonspecific mushrooms or plants, this trend could continue as recent and pending psilocybin-related policies may lead to increased psilocybin use. Continued surveillance is critical to inform consumer choices, clinical care, and public policy.
