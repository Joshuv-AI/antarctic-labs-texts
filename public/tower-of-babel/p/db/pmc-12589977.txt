Impact of Triglycerides and Remnant Particles on Hemodynamic Valve Deterioration After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Revathy Sampath-Kumar, Erika Padilla, Michael Wilkinson, David Torres Barba, Niki Aramburo, Ehtisham Mahmud, Ori Ben-Yehuda, Pam Taub
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of cardiometabolic factors, such as atherogenic lipids, on hemodynamic valve deterioration (HVD) after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has not been studied.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to investigate the association between cardiometabolic factors and HVD in patients following TAVR.MethodsThe UC San Diego Health internal Society of Thoracic Surgeons/American College of Cardiology Transcatheter Valve Therapy registry was used to identify patients who underwent TAVR from May 2013 to December 2022 with echocardiographic follow-up. The primary outcome was moderate or severe HVD, defined by Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 guidelines, with all-cause death treated as a competing risk.ResultsA total of 555 patients (median age 81 years, 38.7% female, 32.3% diabetes, median lipoprotein(a) 17 mg/dL, 52.8% with low-density lipoprotein >70 mg/dL) were included. Most patients were on statins (74.8%), whereas few were on other cardiometabolic therapies. HVD occurred in 6.3% of patients at a median of 3.7 years. The cumulative incidence of HVD was higher in patients with elevated triglycerides (>100 mg/dL) (HR: 3.81; 95% CI: 1.75-8.29; P < 0.001) and remnant cholesterol (HR: 1.03; 95% CI: 1.02-1.04; P < 0.001). Elevated triglycerides were associated with a higher annualized rate of change in peak aortic jet velocity (0.01 m/s/y, IQR: -0.07-0.18, vs −0.02 m/s/y, IQR: -0.13-0.09; P = 0.027). HVD was not associated with other cardiometabolic factors.ConclusionsElevated triglycerides and remnant cholesterol were associated with a higher cumulative incidence of HVD, and elevated triglycerides were associated with a higher annualized rate of change in peak aortic jet velocity. Optimizing cardiometabolic risk factors may be a strategy for improving TAVR valve longevity that warrants future study.Key words: bioprosthetic valve failure, cardiometabolic health, transcatheter aortic valve replacement

Aortic valve replacement remains the sole definitive treatment for severe aortic stenosis. As the number of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) procedures increases and indications expand to include younger, lower-risk patients, long-term valve durability becomes critical.1,2 Currently, no pharmacologic treatments exist to reduce the incidence or halt the progression of hemodynamic valve deterioration (HVD), which ultimately necessitates reintervention.

The mechanism of HVD is multifactorial, involving repetitive leaflet mechanical stress, fibrocalcific degeneration and remodeling, and inflammation, all resulting in the disruption of bioprosthetic leaflet tissues.3 Similar mechanisms affect the native aortic valve and result in aortic stenosis.4,5 Despite immobilization of the native stenotic valve leaflets, persistent biologic activity of the native aortic valve after TAVR may also contribute to bioprosthesis degeneration.6 A study of explanted TAVR valves showed that leaflet thickness is directly correlated with implant duration, with fibrosis occurring as early as 60 days postimplant.7 Although longer-term data are scarce, the reported incidence of bioprosthetic valve failure after TAVR is up to 10% at 5 years and 25% at 10 years.8, 9, 10

Limited studies have evaluated predictors of HVD following TAVR, with varying definitions of HVD and conflicting results. Native aortic valve calcium volume, residual aortic regurgitation postimplant, valve-in-valve procedures, smaller valve size, younger age, female sex, and higher body mass index have been associated with higher rates of HVD in some studies but not in others.10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Among patients who underwent surgical aortic valve replacement, a dysmetabolic profile and insulin resistance were associated with the development of HVD.15 Elevated lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)) and oxidized phospholipids have been implicated in the accelerated progression of native aortic valve stenosis, and elevated Lp(a) has been associated with bioprosthetic aortic valve degeneration.16, 17, 18, 19 Although randomized controlled trials of lipid-lowering therapies have not demonstrated a change in native aortic valve disease progression,20, 21, 22 an exploratory analysis of the Further Cardiovascular Outcomes Research With PCSK9 Inhibition in Subjects With Elevated Risk trial showed a reduced risk of worsening aortic stenosis or aortic valve replacement with proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitor therapy in patients with elevated Lp(a).23

The role of cardiometabolic factors such as atherogenic lipids on outcomes after TAVR have not been studied. We thus evaluated the impact of cardiometabolic factors on HVD and hemodynamic progression among patients who underwent TAVR at a large academic medical center with echocardiographic follow-up.

Methods

Data source

The UC San Diego Health internal Patient Analysis and Tracking System (PATS, Axis Clinical) was used to obtain data on patients who underwent TAVR from May 2013 to December 2022 at UC San Diego Health. UC San Diego is an academic tertiary and quaternary referral hospital system. TAVR procedures were performed by attending interventional cardiologists and cardiothoracic surgeons with or without interventional cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery fellows.

Echocardiograms were obtained pre-TAVR, within 7 days post-TAVR, and at follow-up ≥ 30 days from the date of TAVR. Echocardiograms were performed by trained sonographers according to the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines for the evaluation of prosthetic valve function.24 Echocardiograms were interpreted by attending cardiologists board certified in echocardiography. Echocardiographic data were extracted from the electronic medical record or the institutional echocardiography database and merged with registry data using unique patient identifiers. For each patient with echocardiographic follow-up ≥1 year, the annualized rate of change in mean transvalvular pressure gradient and peak aortic valve jet velocity across the bioprosthetic valve was calculated. This was determined by dividing the difference between baseline and last follow-up measurements and the follow-up time in years.

Cardiometabolic laboratory values and prescribed medications closest to the TAVR date (either before or after, median 43 days) were manually obtained from the electronic medical record and merged with registry data using unique patient identifiers. Lipid panels were obtained in the fasting state. Remnant cholesterol was calculated by subtracting low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol from total cholesterol. Non-HDL cholesterol was calculated by subtracting HDL from total cholesterol. The Institutional Review Board of the University of California, San Diego approved the study (#809424).

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was moderate or severe HVD as determined by serial echocardiography according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 guidelines.25 Moderate HVD was defined as an increase in mean transvalvular gradient ≥10 mm Hg resulting in a mean gradient ≥20 mm Hg or the new occurrence or increase of ≥1 grade of intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation resulting in greater than or equal to moderate aortic regurgitation. Severe HVD was defined as an increase in mean transvalvular gradient ≥20 mm Hg resulting in a mean gradient ≥30 mm Hg or the new occurrence or increase of ≥2 grades of intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation resulting in severe aortic regurgitation.

Statistical methods

Categorical data are presented as percentages and number of patients. Chi-square and Fisher exact tests were used as appropriate to compare categorical variables between groups. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Since none of the continuous variables met the assumption of normality, they are presented as median and IQR, and group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. HVD was treated as a categorical binary outcome to compare cardiometabolic laboratory values and echocardiographic findings between patients with and without HVD. Univariable Fine and Gray regression with all-cause death as a competing risk was used to identify predictors of HVD in a time-to-event analysis.26 We tested all cardiometabolic factors, including Lp(a), total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, non-HDL, triglycerides, remnant cholesterol, hemoglobin A1c, and BMI, as predictors in the model. Cardiometabolic factors that were significant in the univariable model were then tested in a multivariable Fine and Gray regression with all-cause death as a competing risk using baseline patient and procedural characteristics determined as confounders a priori. Censoring occurred at the date of the latest echocardiographic follow-up. Analyses were performed using SAS Studio release 3.81 (SAS Institute Inc); a 2-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient selection and follow-up

A total of 604 adult patients underwent TAVR at UC San Diego Health from May 2013 to December 2022. We excluded 38 patients who did not have echocardiographic data available ≥30 days post-TAVR, and 11 patients who died within 30 days post-TAVR. The final study cohort comprised 555 eligible patients who had a median duration of echocardiographic follow-up of 1.9 years (IQR: 0.5-3.7 years). During follow-up, 35 patients developed HVD and 520 patients did not (Figure 1).

Rate and cardiometabolic predictors of hemodynamic valve deterioration

Moderate or severe HVD occurred in 35 patients (6.3%) during follow-up, with median time to HVD of 3.7 years (IQR: 1.9-5.0 years). Bioprosthetic aortic valve stenosis was the most common cause (26 patients), followed by intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation (5 patients) and combined stenosis and regurgitation (4 patients). Patients with HVD had higher left ventricular ejection fraction before TAVR (66%, IQR: 52% to 76%, vs 62%, IQR: 54% to 68%; P = 0.028) and borderline significantly higher aortic valve peak velocity within 7 days of TAVR (2.5 m/s, IQR: 2.1-2.9 m/s, vs 2.3 m/s, IQR: 2.0-2.7 m/s; P = 0.054).

Patients with HVD had higher triglycerides (118 mg/dL, IQR: 102-157 mg/dL, vs 92 mg/dL, IQR: 69-124 mg/dL; P < 0.001), remnant cholesterol (24 mg/dL, IQR: 20-32 mg/dL, vs 18 mg/dL, IQR: 14-25 mg/dL; P < 0.001), total cholesterol (166 mg/dL, IQR: 138-187 mg/dL, vs 147 mg/dL, IQR: 121-172 mg/dL; P = 0.018), and non-HDL (107 mg/dL, IQR: 85-142 mg/dL, vs 95 mg/dL, IQR: 73-118 mg/dL; P = 0.027) compared to patients without HVD (Table 2). There was no difference in other cardiometabolic laboratory values between groups.

The cumulative incidence of HVD, treating all-cause death as a competing risk, was higher in patients with elevated triglycerides (>100 mg/dL) (HR: 3.81; 95% CI: 1.75-8.29; P < 0.001) (Central Illustration) and remnant cholesterol (HR: 1.03; 95% CI: 1.02-1.04; P < 0.001). After adjusting for valve-in-valve status, valve diameter, age, sex, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, dialysis dependence, prior myocardial infarction, and congestive heart failure, elevated triglycerides (HR: 4.55; 95% CI: 1.94-10.71; P < 0.001) and remnant cholesterol (HR: 1.04; 95% CI: 1.03-1.05; P < 0.001) remained statistically significant predictors for HVD (Table 4). HVD was not associated with other cardiometabolic factors.

Hemodynamic progression

Among 370 patients with echocardiographic follow-up of at least 1 year, those with HVD had significantly higher annualized rates of change in peak aortic jet velocity (4.06 m/s/y, IQR: 2.35-9.34 m/s/y, vs −0.22 m/s/y, IQR: −1.93 to 1.72 m/s/y; P < 0.001) and mean transvalvular pressure gradient (0.27 mm Hg/y, IQR: 0.17-0.80 mm Hg/y, vs −0.02 mm Hg/y, IQR: −0.23 to 0.18 mm Hg/y) compared to those without HVD (Table 3).

Patients with elevated triglyceride levels (>100 mg/dL) demonstrated a significantly higher annualized rate of change in peak aortic jet velocity (0.01 m/s/y, IQR: −0.07 to 0.18 m/s/y, vs −0.02 m/s/y, IQR: −0.13 to 0.09 m/s/y; P = 0.027) compared to those with normal triglyceride levels. A borderline significant increase in the annualized rate of change in mean transvalvular pressure gradient was also observed in patients with elevated triglycerides (0.12 mm Hg/y, IQR: −0.85 to 1.72 mm Hg/y, vs −0.26 mm Hg/y, IQR: −1.34 to 1.10 mm Hg/y; P = 0.050) compared to those with normal triglyceride levels.

Discussion

In patients who underwent TAVR with a median echocardiographic follow-up of 1.9 years (IQR: 0.5-3.7 years), we found that elevated triglycerides were associated with a higher cumulative incidence of HVD and a higher annualized rate of change in peak bioprosthetic aortic jet velocity. Remnant cholesterol was also associated with a statistically significant but modest increase in the cumulative incidence of HVD. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the impact of cardiometabolic factors including atherogenic lipids on bioprosthetic valve function after TAVR. These findings are particularly relevant given the increasing number of young patients undergoing TAVR who may outlive their bioprostheses. Modern TAVR valves demonstrate acceptable hemodynamic function for at least 5 years, but long-term data remain limited.27 Whether the association between triglycerides, remnant cholesterol, and HVD is causal or reflects a shared underlying pathogenic mechanism remains to be determined.

Despite our modest sample size and low event rate, the association between elevated triglycerides and remnant cholesterol with HVD remained statistically significant in both unadjusted and adjusted models, accounting for all-cause death as a competing risk. Elevated triglycerides and remnant cholesterol have been associated with an increased risk of native aortic stenosis, diabetes, ischemic heart disease, and stroke.28, 29, 30

The remnants of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins contain a four-fold higher cholesterol content per particle compared to LDL and can traverse the endothelial barrier.28 Unlike LDL particles, cholesterol in triglyceride-rich lipoprotein remnants can be directly taken up by macrophages, promoting atherosclerotic plaque formation and inflammation.28 Similar mechanisms may facilitate HVD by accumulation of lipid particles within the bioprosthetic valve leaflets, initiating a cascade of local inflammation leading to fibrocalcific remodeling and impaired valve hemodynamics.

We did not observe statistically significant associations between HVD and other cardiometabolic factors examined, including Lp(a). Although a previous study reported an association between elevated Lp(a) and bioprosthetic aortic valve degeneration,18 our study may have been underpowered to detect a similar effect. In addition, the role of oxidized phospholipids, which are largely carried by Lp(a) and have been implicated in the progression of native aortic stenosis, could not be evaluated due to lack of routine measurement.17

The metabolism of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins is known to be influenced by dietary fat and cholesterol intake, as well as lifestyle factors.28,31 Current guidelines recommend dietary modification and weight loss for the management of hypertriglyceridemia and elevated remnant cholesterol.32 While expert consensus guidelines recommend cardiac rehabilitation following TAVR, real-world implementation remains suboptimal, with low participation rates and significant hospital-level variation.33,34 Furthermore, although lifestyle modification and cardiovascular risk factor reduction are recommended, they are not routinely emphasized in valve clinics. There are also no established lipid treatment goals for patients following TAVR. Our study revealed a high prevalence of overweight/obesity and elevated LDL cholesterol among the study population, along with potential underutilization of newer cardioprotective therapies, such as PCSK9 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors.

Our findings generate the hypothesis that management of cardiometabolic risk factors may improve long-term TAVR valve durability, analogous to the secondary prevention strategies employed in patients with coronary artery disease after percutaneous coronary intervention. Multicenter studies are necessary to confirm these findings, and randomized prospective trials are needed to determine whether modifying cardiometabolic risk factors through lifestyle interventions or pharmacotherapy can impact TAVR valve longevity. The incorporation of standardized cardiometabolic data collection, including fasting lipid profiles, into TAVR registries is critical for future research and clinical translation. In the interim, increased emphasis on cardiometabolic health in post-TAVR follow-up care is warranted.

The present study provides novel evidence linking elevated triglycerides and remnant cholesterol to HVD following TAVR. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive cardiometabolic risk management in this patient population and highlight the need for further research to develop strategies for maximizing long-term TAVR valve durability.

Study Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the single-center, retrospective design, relying on registry data from TAVR procedures performed at one academic medical center in the United States, limits the generalizability of our results. Second, the modest sample size and relatively low incidence of HVD may have resulted in insufficient statistical power to detect other smaller but potentially important interactions. Third, the nonstandardized and limited duration of echocardiographic follow-up could have introduced bias. Furthermore, lipids were not measured uniformly in relation to the date of TAVR, and variable fasting duration could affect the comparability of lipid measurements. In addition, although statistically significant, the absolute differences in triglycerides and remnant cholesterol were small, and the effect size from elevated remnant cholesterol was modest. These limitations underscore the need for prospective, multicenter studies to validate our findings and investigate whether lowering triglyceride and remnant cholesterol levels can improve TAVR valve durability.

Conclusions

In this study, we found that elevated triglycerides and remnant cholesterol were associated with a higher cumulative incidence of HVD and that elevated triglycerides were associated with a higher annualized rate of change in peak bioprosthetic aortic jet velocity. Optimizing cardiometabolic risk factors may be a strategy for improving TAVR valve longevity that warrants future study.
