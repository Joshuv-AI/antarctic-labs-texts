A new CT-based classification system for posterior cruciate ligament tibial avulsion fractures: a nationwide multicenter study
Authors: Yi zheng, Xinda zhang, Yingzhen Niu, Ziqi Shang, Baohai Yu, Junchao Zhao, Jiajun Zhao, Xueliang Hu, Yubin Long, Jinchuan Pu, He En, Li Shu, Jiangtao Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Meyers-McKeever two-dimensional classification has been widely used in diagnosing and treating posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fractures since its introduction in 1959. However, because of image overlap and obscuration on knee radiographs, the type of avulsion fracture injury is often not identified, which can lead to misdiagnosis. CT has a higher overall confidence in size assessment, displacement, and degree of comminution of the posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fracture block compared with X-ray. Therefore, this study aims to propose a new classification system based on CT for posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fractures.MethodsThis multicenter study evaluated 523 patients with posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fractures admitted between July 2018 and July 2023 at nine medical centers. The new CT-based classification system, the Dong classification system, includes 2 major types and 7 subtypes. Furthermore, the inter- and intra-observer reliability of this classification system were compared to the Meyers - McKeever classification system by 4 observers (Kappa coefficient, K-value) to assess the reproducibility of the Dong-classification system for posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fractures.ResultsA total of 446 patients were eligible for inclusion criteria; 29 patients (6%) could not be classified through the Meyers - McKeever classification system, but all the cases were classifiable through the Dong classification system. The most common type of posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fracture is Type Ic, with 176 cases, accounting for 40%, followed by Type Ib, with 141 cases, accounting for 32%. Non-displaced fractures, including Type Ia and Type IIa, totaled 55 cases, representing 12%. The Meyers-McKeever classification had a mean K-value of 0.593 for inter-observer reliability and a mean K-value of 0.896 for intra-observer reliability. However, the Dong classification had a mean K-value of 0.768 for inter-observer reliability and 0.910 for intra-observer reliability.ConclusionThe CT-based 3D Dong classification is the first proposed 3D CT classification of posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fractures, which helps analyze the type of avulsion fracture and whether it is combined with a tibial plateau fracture from a 3D level. This classification shows higher inter- and intra-observer reliability than the Meyers - McKeever classification and can be used as a complement to the Meyers - McKeever and Schatzker classifications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09025-9.

Introduction

The posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) is shaped like a footprint and is twice as strong as the anterior cruciate ligament. It starts at the lateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle and ends at the posterior part of the intercondylar ridge of the tibia, about 10 mm below the slope of the tibial articulating surface [1]. The main function of the PCL is to limit the posterior translation of the tibia relative to the femur. Given the wide and strong attachment of the PCL to the tibia, post-traumatic avulsion fractures often occur at this site [2]. Posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fractures (PCLAF) of the tibia have been reported in the literature to be rare in western countries. However, it is more common in India and China, which may be associated with a higher incidence of two-wheeler accidents [3]. PCLAF is a specific type of fracture within the knee joint that is difficult to diagnose and treat. The treatment of PCLAF depends on the type of fracture, size, displacement, degree of comminution, and direction of displacement, as well as the integrity of the attached PCL [4, 5].

The classification guides treatment, and the current international mainstream classification is the Meyers - McKeever classification proposed in 1959, which can be divided into three types. Type I: mild displacement of the anterior margin of the fracture fragment or no displacement. Type II: Displaced anterior 1/3 − 1/2 of the fracture fragments with a beak-like appearance. Type III: fracture fragment completely separated from the intercondylar eminence; III+: fracture fragment completely separated from the intercondylar eminence and rotated [6]. However, the Meyers-McKeever classification initially typed tibial intercondylar crest fractures and did not systematically typify PCLAF and guide treatment. While Griffith (2004) distinguished between ACLAF and PCLAF using the Meyers-McKeever classification system, the literature does not precisely describe how many millimeters of displacement of the fracture block constitutes a non-displaced fracture. Moreover, the size of avulsion range of fracture mass was not considered [7].

The Meyers-McKeever classification is a two-dimensional classification that makes it difficult for the clinician to assess the type of injury in PCLAF by preoperative lateral knee X-ray. In general, PCLAF can be detected by ordinary knee X-ray, but it is difficult to accurately judge fractures with small displacement, because the anatomical position of PCL tibial insertion is about 10 mm below the joint line, to be precise, at the bottom of the groove formed at the middle back of the medial lateral tibial platform. Therefore, when the fracture is displaced < 10 mm, it is difficult to detect the fracture mass from the lateral X-ray of the knee joint. Kendall and Berg et al. described the intraoperative finding of an avulsion fracture block as a comminuted fracture, whereas preoperative radiographs showed an uncomminuted fracture [8, 9]. The author also frequently founded a significant number of patients with preoperative CT showing PCLAF but no sign of fracture on preoperative radiographs. Therefore, the preoperative X-ray assessment of PCLAF is not accurate, and there may be missed diagnosis or inaccurate diagnosis. With the popularity of CT and three-dimensional reconstruction, CT is preferred for the assessment of the size, displacement, and degree of comminution of the posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fracture block compared with X-ray. The treatment of PCLAF has been limited to open surgical repair with the aim of achieving anatomical repositioning and stiff internal fixation, as was the case with other fracture types. However, arthroscopic treatment of PCLAF has become increasingly popular due to the advent of arthroscopic techniques. According to the literature, about 16.8% of PCLAF patients sustain concomitant meniscal injuries. Arthroscopy has the advantage of simultaneous treatment of concomitant injuries. The Meyers-McKeever classification is poor guidance for arthroscopic treatment of PCLAF as it is outdated. Moreover, it disregards a significant factor: the extent of the PCL tibial attachment avulsion. Therefore, the avulsion of the fracture block is not entirely isolated to the posterior part of the intercondylar ridge and often extends beyond the posterior part of the intercondylar ridge involving the articular surface of the tibial plateau or the intercondylar ridge. The Meyers-McKeever classification does not encompass all of PCLAF. Therefore, the PCLAF classification requires updating to help surgeons better understand, record, and communicate information regarding PCLAF.

An ideal classification system should include most fracture types, be straightforward for the clinician, and have satisfactory inter- and intra-observer reliability. Moreover, it should facilitate a comprehensive assessment of fracture type, which may be more clinically relevant since a finer classification system may help in surgical decision-making. This study aims to present a three-dimensional CT-based classification system for PCLAF, to report the incidence of each type, and to compare its reproducibility with the Meyers-McKeever classification.

Materials and methods

Patients

The study was approved by the institutional ethical review board. The authors who selected the cases were not involved with the observers who evaluated the cases. A total of 523 patients with posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fractures admitted between July 2018 and July 2023 at nine medical centers were included in this study. There were 202 patients in the Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University, 67 patients in the Cangzhou Combined Hospital of Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine, 87 patients in the Tianjin Hospital of Tianjin, 34 patients in the First Central Hospital of Baoding, 30 patients in the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University; 14 patients in the Sixth Hospital of the Affiliated University of Medical Sciences of Xinjiang; 29 patients in the People’s Hospital of the Bazhou City of Xinjiang; 45 patients in the Military Hospital of the 81 st Group of the Army; and 15 patients in the Henan Provincial People’s Hospital.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) Patients aged 10–80 years and diagnosed with posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fracture; (ii) The patient has complete imaging data: standard preoperative X-rays (knee in the front and side view), computed tomography (CT) images including sagittal, coronal as well as axial images and 3D reconstructions, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) incomplete patient imaging X-ray, CT), (ii) bone defects or tibial plateau deformities, and (iii) pathological fractures. This study conformed to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki and has been reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University (IRB number: KE 2024-100-1).

Description of the tibial avulsion fracture of posterior cruciate ligament Classification

Anatomical definition of the posterior intercondylar fossa: The upper articular surface of the tibia (tibial plateau) consists of two smooth, flat surfaces or condyles (medial and lateral) that are attached to the femoral condyles. They are separated in the middle by the intercondylar elevation, consisting of the medial and lateral intercondylar nodes (also known as the tibial spines). The intercondylar elevation is designated anteriorly as the anterior intercondylar fossa and posteriorly as the posterior intercondylar fossa. The anterior cruciate ligament and the anterior horn of the medial meniscus attach to the anterior intercondylar fossa. The posterior intercondylar fossa is the site of attachment of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus and the posterior cruciate ligament. Whether the PCLAF was confined to the posterior intercondylar fossa served as the critical diagnostic criterion in our classification system (Fig. 1).

3D CT was reconstructed using RadiAnt DICOM Viewer software. Preoperative imaging CTs of all patients were imported into the RadiAnt DICOM Viewer in the DICOM format, and 3D reconstruction was performed to create images of the tibial plateau only (Fig. 2). Senior surgeons initially proposed a CT-based classification system for PCLAF based on the anatomical basis, the extent of the avulsion fracture, the integrity of the fracture block, the degree of displacement of the fracture block, the presence or absence of a combined tibial plateau fracture, and clinical experience. The final classification system for PCLAF, the 3D-Dong classification system (Table 1), was then proposed after a retrospective analysis of all archived CT images and several discussions among the nine participating institutional reviewers.

Classification of tibial avulsion fracture of posterior cruciate ligament based on CT

PCLAF was classified into 2 different types and 7 subtypes (Fig. 3). Type I was a PCLAF block in the posterior intercondylar fossa. Type II was defined as an avulsion fracture block extending beyond the posterior intercondylar fossa and involving the intercondylar eminence, the anterior intercondylar fossa, or the medial and lateral tibial plateaus (Fig. 1). Type I was divided into 3 subtypes, with type Ia defined as a non-displaced fracture (displacement ≤ 3 mm) with the avulsion fracture block located in the posterior intercondylar fossa. Displacement measurement was performed as follows: the CT scan level with the largest displacement was screened, and the displacement distance was positioned at the distance from the posterior cortical tip of the fracture fragment to the base of the fracture [10] (Figure 4). Type Ib was defined as a displaced fracture where the avulsion fracture block was partially connected to the tibial plateau (hinge mechanism, displacement > 3 mm). Type Ic was defined as a completely displaced fracture or a comminuted fracture of an avulsed fracture block. Type II was further divided into four subtypes. Type IIa was defined as a non-displaced fracture with an avulsed fracture mass extending beyond the posterior intercondylar fossa (displacement ≤ 3 mm). Type IIb was defined as a displaced fracture (displacement > 3 mm) with an avulsed fracture block extending beyond the posterior intercondylar fossa. Type IIc was localized as the avulsion of the fracture block involving the anterior intercondylar fossa or comminuted fracture of the fracture block. Type IId was defined as a posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fracture combined with a medial and/or lateral tibial plateau fracture.

Reproducibility assessment

A useful classification system must be reliable and accurate. Reliability reflected the accuracy of the classification system and generally refers to intra- and inter-observer reliability. Four professionally trained orthopedic clinicians were recruited to classify all the included cases of posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fractures using the Meyers-McKeever classification and 3D CT-based Dong classification. Imaging information for this study was provided by senior surgeons from nine medical centers who participated in the patient admission. Observers were not involved in the identification of patients with PCLAF or any treatment received in this study. Observers were trained, and discussed, and queries were clarified using both classification systems to standardize the assessment process. Observers were asked to memorize the content of the two classification systems and understand the principles of classification. Observers were asked to classify all fracture model maps covering all typical fracture types in the Meyers-McKeever and 3D CT-based Dong classifications. When all typical fracture types were correctly diagnosed and described, it was assumed that the observer was familiar with both classification systems. Each observer operated independently on 2 occasions at 4-week intervals, and cases were presented randomly to avoid recall errors.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software 27.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The kappa coefficient (K-value) was used to determine inter- and intra-observer agreement, with K-values expressed as 95% confidence intervals (CI). Then we analysesd inter- and intra-observer reliability of the Meyers-McKeever and 3D CT-based Dong classifications. The levels of agreement based on the K-values were evaluated according to the guidelines proposed by Landis and Koch [11] which assigned subdivisions according to the strength of agreement: slight agreement (0.01 to 0.20), fair agreement (0.21 to 0.40), moderate agreement (0.41 to 0.60), substantial agreement (0.61 to 0.80), and excellent agreement (0.81 to 1.00).

Results

Classification

A total of 523 cases of PCLAF were included in this study, and 446 cases were finally included by excluding cases with incomplete imaging data. There were 279 males and 167 females with a mean age of 42 ± 16.72 years (range 10–80 years), 235 on the left and 211 on the right. 29 patients (6%) could not be classified through the Meyers-McKeever classification, and all cases could be classified through the 3D CT-based Dong classification. The most common of the new classifications were types Ic and Ib, with 176 cases, 40%, and 141 cases, 32%. The next most common types were type IIb (45 cases, 10%), type Ia (33 cases, 7%) and type IIa (22 cases, 5%). Types IIc and IId were less common, with 15 cases (3%) and 14 cases (3%) (Table 2). Complete MRI examinations were performed in 230 patients, 46 of whom involved injuries to the medial and lateral collateral ligaments or meniscus. Among these 46 patients, the proportion of type I PCLAF with ligament and meniscus injury was 38%, and the proportion of type II PCLAF with ligament and meniscus injury was 62%.

Discussion

In conclusion, we have developed a PCLAF classification based on 3D CT, which is the first multicenter and largest study in the reported literature. The Dong classification is supported by an anatomical basis and radiological measurements, and the application of 3D reconstruction allowed for the improvement of the PCLAF classification. The Dong classification is recommended to characterize fractures in PCLAF.

The Meyers-McKeever classification system has been widely used in the clinical diagnosis and treatment of PCLAF since it was proposed in 1959. However, this classification is primarily relevant to intercondylar eminence fractures in children and is based on two-dimensional planar X-ray imaging, providing insufficient information about the fracture. In 1977, Zaricznyj further proposed type IV fractures as comminuted fractures [12] and refined the Meyers-McKeever classification in two dimensions. In 2004, Griffith made a distinction in the Meyers-McKeever intercondylar augmentation fracture classification by creating the ACLAF and PCLAF classifications. Furthermore, Griffith stated that CT scans may be used to accurately depict the fracture margins, comminution degree, and the extent of avulsion [7]. This system is known as the current PCLAF classification. In 2005, Lubowitz classified type III fractures into type IIIa, whereby fracture fragments involve only the ACL and the attached bone block, and type IIIb, in which the fracture fragments include the entire intercondylar eminence [13]. Despite the efforts of the aforementioned authors, the Meyers-McKeever classification system still has shortcomings. These include an inadequate coverage of PCLAF types, the absence of stereoscopic assessment in two-dimensional classification, and an unclear definition of non-displaced fracture displacement. Twenty-nine cases of types that could not be encompassed by the Meyers-McKeever classification were found in this study. Therefore, this study proposes a new classification system and attempts to address the shortcomings of previous classifications. The classifications and the concepts proposed by the above-mentioned scholars also provide a solid foundation for the new classification.

There is a high incidence of concomitant injuries in PCL avulsion fractures, and in a study by Hooper [3] et al. It was statistically found that 16.8% of PCL avulsion fractures were associated with concomitant meniscus injuries, and 19.1% of patients had concomitant injuries to other ligaments. Pardiwala [14] reported that preoperative magnetic resonance imaging in 28% of patients with tibial avulsion fractures of the PCL showed meniscal injury or intra-articular free osteochondral fragments. It suggests that clinicians should strongly suspect the presence of concomitant injuries involving PCLAF. This finding was confirmed by our study, which also found different percentages of type I and type II avulsion fractures combined with meniscal or ligament injuries, with 38% of type I PCLAF combined with injuries and 62% of type II. This finding may be because the Type II PCLAF block breaks through the posterior intercondylar fossa and involves a larger area that is more prone to meniscal and other ligamentous or structural injuries. According to these findings, arthroscopic treatment of PCLAF does resolve meniscal, ligament, or cartilage injuries at the same time, which cannot be addressed with open treatment. Therefore, avulsion fracture blocks were classified into type I and type II according to whether they break through the posterior intercondylar fossa, which may influence the choice of surgical procedures.

Previous classifications did not clearly define the distance by which the imaging fracture block was displaced, making it difficult to determine if a fracture block was non-displaced. Torisu [15] noted that if the fracture block is displaced > 10 mm, postoperative instability of grade II or higher may occur, and therefore, surgery should be performed. Zhao [16] recommended that all fracture blocks displaced > 5 mm should be treated surgically. Yoon [10] recommended that PCLAF displacement < 6.7 mm be considered for consevative treatment. Gui [17] used an avulsion fracture block displacement of 3 mm as the boundary between surgical and non-surgical treatment. Scholars believe that undisplaced PCL tibial avulsion fractures can be treated conservatively, whereas displaced PCL tibial avulsion fractures (≥ 3 mm) require surgical intervention to restore biomechanics and joint stability, prevent non-union of the fracture, and prevent osteoarthritis. Avulsion fracture block displacement is critical in deciding between surgical or non-surgical treatment. Measurement of posterior tibia transposition (PTT) relaxation by the KT-1000 or KT-2000 has also been used in the literature as a criterion to differentiate between the presence or absence of displaced fractures. For example, the Gopinatth [18] found that non-operative treatment of higher PTT displacement > 4 mm in the tibia resulted in a lower rate of healing of the avulsion fracture block. However, the author believes that the measurement value of KT-1000 or KT-2000 does not reflect the real situation of the patient in the non-anesthetized state due to the patient’s pain leading to tension in the quadriceps muscles, etc., and the value may be underestimated. Previous literature had similarly shown significant differences between the KT-1000, electromagnetic measurement system (EMS), and knee measurements when patients were awake and under general anesthesia [19]. Therefore, an avulsion fracture block displaced by a distance of ≤ 3 mm was defined as a non-displaced fracture in this study based on reference to previous experience.

Audige [20] suggested that a new classification should undergo a three-step validation process.(1) definition of a classification system by experts; (2) multicenter concordance assessment by a representative group of users of future classifications; (3) prospective clinical studies to assess their clinical usefulness. Our study was validated by expert joint surgeons from 9 centers and assessed for multicenter concordance, including trauma surgeons as they are also potential users of the classification. The new classification successfully passed Phase I (development) and Phase II (multi-center protocol studies). Our classification will be ready to enter the clinical validation phase after modification based on the results of the Phase II study. In the future, the prospective clinical cohort study will be further improved to assess the accuracy and usefulness of the classification.

A prerequisite for a good prognosis is the selection of an appropriate surgical procedure for the different types of PCL avulsion fractures. Both arthroscopic suture fixation and the open posterior approach for PCL avulsion fractures were currently accepted as the mainstream surgical methods. Based on the size of the avulsed fragment block, Kim [4] proposed the fixation of comminuted fragments or fragments < 10 mm with sutures, fragments > 10 mm with K wires, and fragments > 20 mm with screws. Various materials such as steel plates [21] and suture anchors [22] have also been used for the fixation of avulsed fragments depending on the size and comminution. According to previous literature and clinical experience, it is suggested that for type Ia/IIa, conservative treatment or arthroscopic ligature repositioning with internal fixation may be considered with a view to early recovery. For type Ib/Ic/IIb/IIc, arthroscopically assisted reduction and internal fixation or open incision fixation of avulsed fracture blocks with hollow screws, absorbable screws, or threaded anchors can achieve satisfactory results. Arthroscopic assisted reduction and internal fixation or arthroscopic PCL reconstruction, depending on the injury, may be considered for comminuted type Ic/IIc fractures. For type IId, arthroscopically assisted one-stage tibial plateau reduction and fixation with the PCL avulsion fracture block repair is recommended.

Limitations

This study encountered some limitations to this study. Firstly, this is a retrospective study, and images were collected retrospectively from various institutions, which may be subject to selection bias. However, the large amount of data in this study can compensate for this shortcoming. Secondly, although we have proposed treatment recommendations for each subtypes, there was currently a lack of prospective outcome data to validate these recommendations, and prospective cohort studies should be conducted in the future to further explore the optimal treatment for each classification to study its safety and feasibility.

To our knowledge, the present multicenter study is the first 3D CT-based classification system for PCLAF proposed in the existing literature. The classification comprehensively considers the factors associated with avulsion fractures, while this new classification shows better inter- and intra-observer reliability than the Meyers-McKeever classification. Among them, type IId can complement the Schatzker classification, suggesting that traumatologists manage tibial plateau fractures with simultaneous consideration of the PCLAF and restoration of PCL stability. Due to the growing popularity of minimally invasive surgery, physicians are increasingly favoring arthroscopic treatment of PCLAF. This approach is less invasive and can effectively address additional problems such as meniscal injuries. The new classification has been proposed to help assess the fracture type for preoperative planning and to support the development of arthroscopic treatment of PCLAF. In conclusion, the proposed classification facilitates clinical diagnosis, guides treatment, statistical analysis, and academic communication.

Conclusion

The CT-based 3D Dong classification is the first proposed 3D CT classification of posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fractures, which helps analyze the type of avulsion fracture and whether it is combined with a tibial plateau fracture from a 3D level. This classification shows higher inter- and intra-observer reliability than the Meyers - McKeever classification and can be used as a complement to the Meyers - McKeever and Schatzker classifications.
