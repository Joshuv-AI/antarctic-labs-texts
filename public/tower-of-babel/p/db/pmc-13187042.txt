Knowledge, attitudes, and practices of male barbers on hepatitis B and C transmission in Herat City, Afghanistan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mohammad Masudi, Ali Reza Zahed, Ali Rahimi, Abdul Subhan Seddiqi, Ali Agha Arefi, Mohammad Khalil Asghari, Abbas Ali Moneer, Enayatollah Ejaz, Nasar Ahmad Shayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to assess male barbers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) in Herat City, Afghanistan, regarding HBV/HCV transmission and prevention. A cross-sectional survey using a structured, localized KAP questionnaire based on validated tools was conducted from April to June 2025, involving 283 practicing male barbers in Herat. Descriptive statistics summarized binary scores, while associations between KAP and sociodemographic factors were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, and logistic regression has also been applied (p < 0.05). Among 283 male barbers, good knowledge was higher among those aged 31–50 years (73.7%) compared with those aged 21–30 years (58.5%) and 18–20 years (48.5%) (p = 0.008), and among those with > 8 years of work experience (70.5%) compared with those with ≤ 8 years (45.8%) (p < 0.001). Good preventive practice was higher among barbers who had formal training (68.8%) compared with those without training (48.0%) (p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, barbers with > 8 years of work experience had higher odds of good knowledge than those with ≤ 8 years (AOR = 2.74; 95% CI 1.40–5.35) (p = 0.003). For attitude, barbers with fair economic status had lower odds of a positive attitude compared with those with good economic status (AOR = 0.25; 95% CI 0.06–0.97) (p = 0.046), while those working > 12 h per day had higher odds of a positive attitude than those working ≤ 12 h (AOR = 1.98; 95% CI 1.11–3.51) (p = 0.020). For practice, barbers aged 21–30 years had lower odds of good practice than those aged 18–20 years (AOR = 0.32; 95% CI 0.16–0.66) (p = 0.002), barbers serving > 5 customers/day had lower odds of good practice than those serving ≤ 5 customers/day (AOR = 0.50; 95% CI 0.29–0.88) (p = 0.016), and barbers without formal training had lower odds of good practice than those with training (AOR = 0.50; 95% CI 0.29–0.89) (p = 0.017). Male barbers in Herat City demonstrate commendable hygiene practices but exhibit significant gaps in their knowledge and attitudes regarding HBV/HCV. These findings highlight the need for targeted education and vaccination programs, integrated into barber training, to improve preventive attitudes and reduce the risk of viral transmission. Introduction

Viral hepatitis remains a major global public health concern, with hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) responsible for substantial morbidity and mortality worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), HBV and HCV together account for an estimated 1.3 million deaths annually, primarily due to chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma1. As of 2022, approximately 254 million people were living with chronic HBV infection and 50 million with chronic HCV infection globally. Despite the availability of effective prevention and treatment strategies, diagnosis and treatment coverage remain low, particularly in low- and middle-income countries2,3.

In Afghanistan, HBV and HCV constitute an underrecognized public health problem. Available studies estimate the prevalence of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) to range from 1.9 to 6.3%, while HCV prevalence varies between 0.13 and 6%, depending on population groups4–6. Higher prevalence has been documented among high-risk populations, such as injection drug users, where HCV prevalence reached 36% in Herat7. Although HBV vaccination coverage among healthcare workers has improved, national immunization coverage remains suboptimal, reflecting persistent systemic challenges in hepatitis prevention and control8,9.

Barbershops represent a potential but often overlooked route for HBV/HCV transmission, particularly in settings where shared or improperly sterilized sharp instruments are commonly used. Barbers routinely use razors, blades, and scissors on multiple clients, and inadequate infection control practices—such as blade reuse or insufficient disinfection—can facilitate blood-borne virus transmission10. Studies from South Asia, the Middle East, and Africa consistently demonstrate that limited knowledge and unsafe practices among barbers contribute to the community spread of hepatitis viruses11.

In Afghanistan, traditional barbershops are widely used, especially by men, and services often include shaving with straight razors. Cultural practices, informal work settings, and limited regulatory oversight may further increase the risk of HBV/HCV transmission in these environments12. Evidence from neighboring and comparable countries has shown substantial gaps in barbers’ knowledge, low HBV vaccination coverage, and poor adherence to infection prevention practices10,13–17.

Educational level and professional training have been repeatedly associated with improved knowledge and safer practices, and intervention studies indicate that targeted education can significantly improve barbers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) related to hepatitis prevention18–20.

Despite this growing body of international evidence, no published studies have assessed the KAP of barbers regarding HBV and HCV transmission in Afghanistan, particularly in Herat City. Given the widespread use of traditional barbershops and the country’s fragile healthcare system, understanding barbers’ role in potential hepatitis transmission is essential. Although the Ministry of Public Health has initiated the National Hepatitis Control Program to address hepatitis prevention and awareness, implementation in informal sectors such as barbershops remains limited12.

Therefore, this study aims to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of male barbers in Herat City, Afghanistan, regarding the transmission routes of HBV and HCV. The findings are intended to fill a critical data gap and inform targeted public health interventions and regulatory strategies to reduce hepatitis transmission in the community.

Methods

Study setting and design

This cross-sectional study was conducted from April 2025 to Jun 2025 in Herat City, Afghanistan, to assess the KAP of male barbers regarding the transmission of HBV and HCV. Data collection was carried out through face-to-face interviews with barbers at their workplaces. The study design was chosen to provide a snapshot of current awareness, attitude and practices among barbers, a high-risk occupational group in terms of exposure to bloodborne pathogens.

Study population and sampling

The study population consisted of practicing male barbers from various districts across Herat City. A convenience sampling method was used, with eligible participants approached directly in their barbershops and invited to participate. All participants were aged 18 or older and actively engaged in barbering. Barbers who declined participation or were unavailable during data collection were excluded.

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula for large populations, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and a margin of error (ME) of 6%. Using a conservative estimate of 50% (p = 0.5) for maximum variability, the required sample size was initially 267 barbers. To account for potential non-responses and refusals, a 5% buffer was added to ensure the study retained statistical power despite some barbers being unavailable or unwilling to participate. Consequently, the final sample size was adjusted to 283 barbers.

A total of 283 barbers were successfully enrolled, representing a wide range of sociodemographic characteristics, including age, marital status, education, income, daily customer volume, working hours, years of experience, formal barber training, and residential area. To ensure a diverse sample, barbershops were selected from all 16 districts of Herat City, with at least 15 barbers from each district. The selection process included both urban and suburban areas, and participation was voluntary. If a barber declined to participate, no further attempts were made to recruit from that shop, and a new shop in the same district was approached.

Study instrument

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire adapted from previously validated KAP surveys on HBV and HCV14, with modifications for the Afghan context. It was first translated from English into Persian (Dari) and then back-translated to ensure linguistic and conceptual accuracy. This pre-final Dari version was reviewed by ten professors for clarity, cultural appropriateness, and content validity. The content validity was assessed using the Content Validity Index (CVI), which resulted in a value of 0.85, indicating good content validity. Additionally, the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was calculated, with a value of 0.91, confirming the relevance of the items. Furthermore, two barbers provided feedback on the ease of understanding and appropriateness of the questionnaire for the target population.

Prior to the main study, a pilot study was conducted with 30 barbers who met the main study’s inclusion criteria but were not included in the final sample. The purpose of the pilot was to assess the acceptability of the questionnaire, the clarity of the items, and the time required for completion, and to establish the internal consistency (reliability) of the translated instrument. The questionnaire was well-accepted by all pilot participants, with an average completion time of 15 min. The internal consistency of the knowledge, attitude, and practice sections was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding coefficients of 0.82, 0.75, and 0.78, respectively, indicating good internal reliability.

The study instrument consisted of four sections: sociodemographic information, knowledge, attitudes, and practices.

The knowledge section contained 17 questions assessing participants’ understanding of HBV and HCV transmission, prevention, treatment, and health consequences. Items covered correct knowledge of transmission routes through blood transfusion, sharing needles, contaminated surgical equipment, sharp tools, piercings, tattoos, sexual contact, intravenous drug use, and dental procedures. It also included questions addressing misconceptions, such as water and food transmission, and assessed awareness regarding the availability of HBV vaccination, the absence of HCV vaccination, the curability of these infections, and their association with liver cirrhosis.

The attitude section included 8 questions exploring participants’ willingness to undergo testing, their perception of the importance of HBV vaccination, and their recognition of specific high-risk groups such as intravenous drug users. Further items assessed attitudes towards promoting safe blood transfusions and awareness of risks linked to unsterilized surgical tools, careless handling of sharp instruments, body piercing, and tattooing.

The practices section comprised 6 questions evaluating self-reported preventive behaviors, such as handwashing with soap before attending clients, disinfection of grooming tools after each use, washing tools with hot water after shaving, using separate razor blades for each client, applying disinfectants to cuts in the presence of clients, and proper disposal of used blades.

Scoring system

For each of the knowledge, attitude, and practice sections, responses were coded dichotomously, with “Yes” answers scored as 1 and “No” answers scored as 0. The total knowledge score therefore ranged from 0 to 17, the attitude score from 0 to 8, and the practice score from 0 to 6. To classify levels of KAP, the total scores for each domain were dichotomized into “High” and “Low” categories based on the median value, as applied in similar studies. Participants scoring at or above the median were classified as having high knowledge, attitude, or practice, while those scoring below the median were classified as low.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected through structured, face-to-face interviews conducted by trained research assistants who explained the study purpose and obtained written consent from participants prior to enrollment. Questionnaires were reviewed daily for completeness, and responses were entered into IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize participant characteristics and responses to KAP items.

Inferential analysis was conducted to assess the relationships between sociodemographic factors and KAP (Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices) scores. In bivariate analysis, Pearson’s chi-square test (χ2) was used for categorical comparisons, with Fisher’s exact test applied when expected cell counts were below five. In addition, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between knowledge, attitude, and practice scores. Prior to multivariable analysis, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF), and all VIF values were below 2, indicating no significant multicollinearity. Variables were then included in the binary logistic regression model using the enter method. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of Jami University (J.2025.1.27.4) and the local health authorities in Herat City. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards set by the institutional and national research committees, as well as the principles outlined in the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participation in the study was voluntary, and the confidentiality of participants’ responses was ensured throughout the research process.

Results

A total of 283 male barbers participated in this study. The majority (43.5%) were aged 21–30 years, followed by 36.4% aged 18–20 years and 20.1% aged 31–50 years. Slightly more than half were married (50.9%), while 49.1% were single. Regarding education, 49.8% had completed primary school, 29.7% high school, 13.8% were illiterate, and only 6.7% had attained a university education. In terms of economic status, most participants (54.8%) reported fair economic conditions, 41.7% reported being poor, and 3.5% reported good economic conditions. Over half (56.9%) earned ≤ 8000 Afghani per month. A total of 53.4% served ≤ 5 customers daily, while 46.6% attended to more than five customers. The majority (73.5%) worked ≤ 12 h per day, and 50.9% had ≤ 8 years of work experience. Only 39.6% had received formal barber training. Participants were distributed across 15 city areas, with the highest proportion in Area 1 (8.8%) and the lowest in Areas 8 and 15 (6.0% each) (Table 1).

Knowledge levels varied widely across different transmission and prevention aspects (Fig. 1). The highest proportion of correct responses was for “HBV/can be transmitted through blood transfusion” (84.1%) and “sharing needles” (79.4%). Awareness was also high for transmission via contaminated surgical equipment (77.4%) and sexual contact (64.0%). In contrast, only 8.1% correctly identified that these infections have significant health impacts, 31.4% knew that HBV/HCV cannot be easily treated and cured, and 20.1% recognized that both viruses have similar transmission methods.

Attitude scores were generally low (Fig. 2). Less than half (48.8%) were willing to get tested for HBV/HCV, and only 13.8% believed that hepatitis B vaccination is important. Very few recognized that intravenous drug users are at higher risk (2.8%) or encouraged safe blood transfusions (4.6%). On the other hand, 68.9% acknowledged that careless handling of sharp objects increases the risk of transmission, while less than 20% recognized the risks associated with unsterilized piercings or untrained tattooing.

Overall, self-reported preventive practices were considerably better than knowledge and attitudes (Fig. 3). Almost all barbers reported using separate razor blades for each client (99.3%) and disposing of used blades properly (99.3%). Similarly, 97.9% applied disinfectants to cuts in front of clients, 82.0% disinfected grooming tools after each use, and 78.8% washed their hands with soap before attending to clients. However, only 67.6% reported washing tools with hot water after shaving.

Bivariate analysis revealed several significant associations (Table 3). Participants aged 31–50 years demonstrated a higher proportion of good knowledge (73.7%) compared to those aged 21–30 years (58.5%) and those aged 18–20 years (48.5%) (p = 0.008), and also had the highest practice scores (68.4%) compared to 59.2% in the 18–20 year group and 48.0% in the 21–30 year group (p = 0.027). Married participants showed better knowledge (65.3%) compared to single participants (50.4%) (p = 0.011), although attitude and practice scores did not differ significantly. Higher income was associated with better knowledge, as those earning more than 8000 Afghani per month scored 66.4% compared to 51.6% among those earning ≤ 8,000 Afghani (p = 0.012). Barbers serving more than five customers daily had better knowledge (65.2%) compared to those serving ≤ 5 customers (51.7%) (p = 0.022), although practice scores were slightly higher among those serving fewer customers (60.9% compared to 50.8%), a difference that was not statistically significant (p = 0.085). Greater work experience was strongly associated with higher knowledge, with barbers having more than eight years of experience scoring 70.5% compared to 45.8% among those with ≤ 8 years (p < 0.001). Formal barber training was significantly associated with better practice scores, as trained barbers achieved 68.8% compared to 48.0% in untrained barbers (p < 0.001). No statistically significant associations were found between education level, economic status, or working hours per day and KAP scores, except for a borderline association between working more than 12 h per day and higher attitude scores (44.0% compared to 31.7%; p = 0.056).

Discussion

This cross-sectional survey provides the first comprehensive assessment of male barbers’ KAP regarding HBV and HCV in Herat City. The findings reveal a pronounced discordance between KAP domains: although reported hygiene practices were exceptionally high, knowledge levels and preventive attitudes were comparatively low, and correlations between the three domains were weak. This pattern warrants cautious interpretation, particularly given the reliance on self-reported data.

Most barbers demonstrated awareness of major HBV and HCV transmission routes, including blood transfusion (84.1%), shared needles (79.4%), contaminated surgical instruments (77.4%), and sexual contact (64.0%). These levels are comparable to findings from Pakistan and Bangladesh, where awareness of bloodborne transmission among barbers ranges between 70 and 90%11,14. However, this surface-level awareness did not translate into deeper understanding. Only 8.1% of respondents recognized the long-term health consequences of HBV and HCV, and fewer than one-third (31.4%) were aware that these infections are not easily curable. Similar misconceptions have been reported among barbers in rural Pakistan21, indicating persistent gaps in conceptual understanding across comparable settings.

Notably, only 20.1% of participants correctly identified that HBV and HCV share similar modes of transmission, a finding consistent with the low awareness (< 25%) reported in Yemen15. This misunderstanding may lead to selective risk perception, whereby barbers underestimate the importance of preventive measures against one virus relative to the other, thereby undermining comprehensive infection control.

Preventive attitudes were particularly weak. Fewer than one in seven barbers (13.8%) valued HBV vaccination, and willingness to undergo HBV or HCV testing was moderate (48.8%), lower than the 62% reported in Nigeria20. Additionally, only 13.8% of participants valued HBV vaccination, compared with just 2.7% actual vaccination coverage in Indian barbers13. Awareness of high-risk groups was alarmingly low; only 2.8% identified intravenous drug users as being at increased risk, compared with 35% in Egypt17. Similarly, endorsement of safe blood transfusion practices was negligible (4.6%). These findings suggest that while barbers may recognize specific transmission mechanisms, they lack a broader preventive framework linking risk perception, vaccination, and testing.

In contrast, reported hygiene practices were remarkably high. Nearly all barbers (99.3%) reported using separate razor blades and disposing of them appropriately, exceeding rates reported in Bangladesh (92.1%)14. Disinfecting cuts in front of clients (97.9%), disinfecting tools between clients (82.0%), and handwashing before service (78.8%) were also substantially higher than rates reported in Yemen and Bangladesh15. However, cleaning instruments with hot water (67.6%) remained lower than the 96% achieved in a post-intervention study in Pakistan22.

While these practice scores are commendable, the magnitude and consistency of self-reported compliance strongly suggest the presence of social desirability bias. No direct observational validation was conducted, which limits the ability to verify whether reported practices reflect routine behavior. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted as perceived or claimed practices rather than objectively observed behaviors. Similar discrepancies between high reported practice and low knowledge have been documented in other low- and middle-income settings, where hygienic behaviors are often shaped by customer expectations, cultural norms emphasizing cleanliness, or fear of reputational harm rather than by biomedical understanding of disease transmission9. This distinction is critical, as practices driven by social norms may deteriorate over time in the absence of sustained external reinforcement.

Sociodemographic and occupational factors influenced KAP outcomes. Older barbers (31–50 years) demonstrated higher knowledge and practice levels than younger counterparts, consistent with findings from Iran and Pakistan, where professional experience is associated with safer behaviors23,24. Higher income was associated with better knowledge, mirroring observations from Ghana and Bangladesh16. Barbers serving more than five clients per day showed higher knowledge levels but slightly poorer practice, possibly reflecting time constraints that compromise adherence to hygiene protocols, as previously reported in Pakistan10. Greater professional experience (> 8 years) was strongly associated with higher knowledge, reinforcing evidence from Ghana and Pakistan that cumulative exposure and peer learning play a central role in knowledge acquisition16,21. Formal training had the strongest association with practice, consistent with post-intervention improvements reported in Egypt18.

The multivariable logistic regression showed that work experience was the only significant predictor of knowledge. Barbers with more than eight years of work experience had nearly three times higher odds of good knowledge compared with those with eight years or less (AOR = 2.74, 95% CI 1.40–5.35, p = 0.003). Longer experience likely provides repeated exposure to customers and professional networks, increasing opportunities to learn about blood-borne infections. Similar associations have been reported in Ghana, where a heavy workload (> 20 customers per day) and at least four years of work experience significantly increased the odds of good knowledge about viral hepatitis (AORs 3.93 and 1.65, respectively)25. These findings suggest that hands-on exposure and cumulative professional experience may be more influential than formal education in shaping barbers’ knowledge.

Economic status and working hours were associated with attitude. Barbers with fair economic status had lower odds of a positive attitude compared with those reporting good economic status (AOR = 0.25, 95% CI 0.06–0.97, p = 0.046). Conversely, those working more than 12 h per day were twice as likely to express a positive attitude (AOR = 1.98, 95% CI 1.11–3.51, p = 0.020). A plausible explanation is that barbers with limited income may prioritize earning over health‐related behaviors, whereas those working long hours accumulate more clientele and feedback that reinforce the importance of infection prevention. Comparable evidence from Ethiopia shows that working fewer than eight hours per day is associated with unsafe practices (AOR 0.27, 95% CI 0.15–0.50)26.

Practice was influenced by age, daily workload and formal training. Compared with the youngest group (18–20 years), participants aged 21–30 years had about one-third the odds of good practice (AOR = 0.32, 95% CI 0.16–0.66, p = 0.002), whereas no significant difference was observed for the oldest group. Serving more than five customers per day reduced the likelihood of good practice by half (AOR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.29–0.88, p = 0.016), supporting the idea that time pressure diminishes adherence to hygiene protocols. Formal training proved highly beneficial: barbers without training had half the odds of good practice compared with those who received formal training (AOR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.29–0.89, p = 0.017). This is consistent with mixed-methods research from Ghana where tertiary education, heavy workload and at least four years of experience were associated with higher knowledge and beauticians (who are often formally trained) were over eleven times more likely to adhere to safe practices (AOR = 11.2, 95% CI 3.46–36.3)25. Intervention studies have shown that targeted training programs dramatically improve KAP scores among barbers: after educational guidelines in Egypt, the proportion with satisfactory practice increased from 2.7 to 94.6%, and positive attitudes from 4.8 to 97.8%27.

Notably, no other sociodemographic variables (age beyond 30 years, education, marital status or income) remained significant predictors in the multivariable model. This finding diverges from studies where higher education increased the odds of good knowledge and safe practices25,28. The absence of association in our sample may reflect the predominance of primary-school-educated participants and limited variation in educational attainment.

The exceptionally high levels of self-reported hygiene practices (e.g., separate blade use, disinfection and handwashing exceeding 80%) raise concern about social desirability bias. Similar cross-sectional surveys have acknowledged that barbers might over-report good practices to meet perceived expectations14. In our study, we relied solely on self-reports without direct observation of daily practices; therefore, the reported compliance may not reflect actual behaviors. Other researchers have used observational checklists to validate self-reported practices. For example, a mixed-methods study in Ghana employed covert observations by research assistants from outside the study communities to minimize the Hawthorne effect; nonetheless, they cautioned that participants may still modify their behavior under observation and recommended risk-assessment audits. A study similarly collected both questionnaire and observational data and noted that self-reported compliance might be subject to social desirability bias29. These precedents underscore the importance of supplementing surveys with unobtrusive observations to accurately assess hygiene behaviors.

Policy significance

The observed discrepancy between high self-reported hygiene practices and limited knowledge and attitudes suggests that current infection-control behaviors among barbers may be sustained by routine, social norms, or client expectations rather than by a solid understanding of disease transmission. Without reinforcing the underlying rationale for these practices, adherence may diminish over time. Integrating barbers into Afghanistan’s national hepatitis HBV/HCV control strategy—through mandatory health education, vaccination campaigns, and public awareness initiatives—would address both behavioral and cognitive gaps. Accordingly, interventions should extend beyond technical hygiene measures and explicitly target attitudinal and knowledge-based barriers through culturally adapted, compulsory health and safety training programs. To reduce social desirability bias, future monitoring efforts should incorporate observational audits and anonymous self-administered assessments to more accurately capture actual practices.

From a policy standpoint, the formal inclusion of barbers within Afghanistan’s national HBV/HCV control framework is warranted. Afghanistan has an intermediate HBV prevalence, estimated to range from 1.9 to 6.3%, and an HCV prevalence of approximately 0.7–4% in the general population4,30. Given these epidemiological patterns, incorporating barbers into adult vaccination campaigns could yield measurable population-level benefits. Enhancing hygiene knowledge and infection-control practices in high-contact occupations, such as barbershops, is therefore critical for improving public health outcomes and reducing the transmission of hepatitis and other infectious diseases31.

Although Afghanistan’s national immunization program includes hepatitis B vaccination for children, adult coverage—particularly among high-risk, high-contact occupational groups—remains limited32. Expanding vaccination initiatives to include adult service-sector workers, including barbers, would directly mitigate occupational transmission risks. Moreover, introducing mandatory barber certification linked to compliance with infection-control standards, together with targeted awareness campaigns and subsidized vaccination programs, would strengthen the country’s hepatitis prevention infrastructure5. Evidence of improved hygiene knowledge among health sciences students in Herat further illustrates the potential effectiveness of structured educational interventions in improving health outcomes across other professional sectors.

Additionally, several actionable policy implications emerge from these findings. First, barbers with limited experience and those lacking formal training should be prioritized for tailored health education interventions, as evidence from intervention studies demonstrates that targeted training can substantially improve both knowledge and hygienic practices27. Second, economic constraints must be addressed, as the findings indicate that barbers with lower socioeconomic status are less likely to adopt positive attitudes toward infection control. Subsidized or free vaccination and training programs, alongside financial support for acquiring sterilization equipment, could help reduce these inequities. Third, strengthened regulatory frameworks are necessary. A mixed-methods study from Ghana identified inadequate licensing, monitoring, and structured training and called for more rigorous policies and routine inspections25. In many high-income settings, licensing and infection-control regulations for barbershops have been implemented to reduce blood-borne pathogen transmission. Similar regulatory approaches could be adapted in Afghanistan by local health authorities, including mandatory sterilization requirements and periodic risk-based inspections.

Finally, the weak correlations observed between knowledge, attitudes, and practices indicate that behavioral change is unlikely to be achieved through information dissemination alone. Effective interventions should combine educational content with practical demonstrations, mentorship, and ongoing reinforcement, potentially supported by client feedback mechanisms. Peer-learning and experience-sharing forums may also facilitate the transfer of tacit knowledge from experienced barbers to novice practitioners. Future research should integrate observational assessments and qualitative methods to better understand the reasons for discordance between reported practices and knowledge levels, as well as the cultural norms influencing hygiene behaviors. Incorporating observational components would reduce reliance on self-reported data and provide a more accurate assessment of infection-control practices in barbershops29.

Strength and limitations

The study provides valuable insights into the KAP of male barbers in Herat City, Afghanistan, which is crucial for understanding the role of barbershops in the transmission of HBV/HCV. A notable strength of the study is its clear and comprehensive assessment of barbers’ hygiene practices, which were found to be generally commendable, with nearly all barbers following basic preventive measures like using separate razor blades and disinfecting tools. These findings suggest that barbers are willing to take steps to reduce the risk of transmission, likely influenced by professional standards or customer expectations. Additionally, the study offers useful demographic data that help identify specific groups of barbers who might benefit most from targeted interventions, such as those with less experience or formal training.

However, the study has several limitations. A major limitation is the reliance on self-reported data, which may be subject to social desirability bias, potentially leading to overreporting of positive hygiene practices. The actual adherence to these practices could be lower than reported. Another limitation is the convenience sampling method, which could introduce selection bias, as barbers who declined participation were not replaced, possibly skewing the sample towards those more willing or available to participate. Furthermore, the study’s findings are geographically limited to Herat City, and may not be representative of barbers’ practices in other regions of Afghanistan or in countries with different socio-cultural contexts.

Conclusion

This study highlights significant gaps in knowledge and attitudes towards hepatitis prevention among male barbers in Herat City, despite strong preventive practices. These knowledge and attitude gaps pose long-term risks to infection control and public health. To address these issues, it is crucial to implement structured training programs, integrate hepatitis awareness into public health initiatives, and ensure better access to hepatitis testing and vaccination. These actions will not only reduce transmission risks but also improve overall infection prevention in the community. Future research should focus on evaluating the impact of these interventions, and policy reforms should prioritize hepatitis education and preventive measures for at-risk populations like barbers.
