A technology-based intervention to reduce alcohol use after metabolic and bariatric surgery: feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary outcomes
Authors: Lisa R. Miller-Matero, Celeste Pappas, Brittany Christopher, Roman Grossi, Alyssa Vanderziel, Nancy P. Barnett, Roland S. Moore, Aaron Hamann, Arthur M. Carlin, Oliver A. Varban, Jordan M. Braciszewski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Patients who undergo metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) are at increased risk for an alcohol use disorder. A technology-based intervention, rooted in motivational interviewing, could broadly reach patients after MBS and has the potential to reduce alcohol use.Objective:Examine the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary outcomes of a technology-based intervention to reduce alcohol use delivered after MBS.Setting:Health system.Methods:Participants (N = 60) who were 3–18 months post-MBS were randomized to the intervention or treatment-as-usual control group. The tailored intervention consisted of 2 (15-minute) sessions of interactive web-based content followed by 3-months of daily text messaging. Participants completed baseline and a postintervention assessment (91.7% retention).Results:Participants were primarily female (90%), White (55.0%) or Black (43.3%), with a mean age of 44.6 years (SD = 10.4). Of those randomized to the intervention (n = 24), 83.3% (n = 20) began the intervention and 95% (n = 19) completed it. The majority of participants rated all intervention components positively and 100% agreed that other patients would use the intervention. The intervention group reported a significant increase in level of motivation to avoid alcohol use from baseline to postintervention (P = .02), whereas the control group did not show a significant change (P = .73). At the postintervention, the intervention group had significantly fewer participants endorsing alcohol use than the control group (43.5% versus 75%; X2 = 5.63, P = .02).Conclusion:A technology-based intervention delivered after MBS was feasible, acceptable, and showed promising preliminary outcomes for increasing motivation to avoid alcohol use as well as reducing alcohol use.

Metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) is the most effective treatment for individuals with class III obesity leading to significant weight loss with improvement/remission of medical comorbidities [1–3]. Despite the many benefits of MBS, patients who undergo MBS are at risk for a postoperative alcohol use disorder (AUD). Prior to MBS, less than 8% of patients have an AUD [4]. After MBS, AUD rates begin to increase around 2 years postoperatively with 10–12% of individuals having an AUD; approximately 20% may have an AUD after 5 years [4–9]. Most cases of AUD after MBS are thought to be new onset [4].

Because patients are at high risk for AUD after bariatric surgery, national guidelines recommend that patients be carefully assessed and managed prior to surgery if they are identified as high risk for alcohol problems [10]. In response, many health care providers instruct patients to abstain from alcohol or recommend alcohol use in very low amounts [10,11]. Yet, despite recalling receiving education about the risks of postoperative alcohol use, the majority of patients consume alcohol after MBS [12,13]. Factors associated with increased risk include younger age, male sex, history of pre-surgical alcohol use, and presurgical disordered eating behaviors [14,15]. However, because predictors are nonspecific and apply to many of the patients seeking MBS, it is challenging to identify individuals at high risk. In sum, it is common for patients to consume alcohol after MBS, yet we are unable to identify who will develop an alcohol-related problem. As such, it may be useful to target all patients who undergo MBS to reduce postoperative alcohol use, in order to prevent development of future AUDs.

A technology-based intervention has the potential to broadly reach patients after MBS to reduce their alcohol use. The intervention may be particularly well-suited after MBS, as these patients may not be motivated to attend therapy sessions for alcohol use, especially if they do not believe they have a problem with alcohol [16]. Moreover, content can be tailored for after MBS, accessed at any time of the day, and delivered briefly, all of which could increase engagement among those without an identified problem and be used as a preventive measure.

The present research initially involved patients as stakeholders in the development of a technology-based intervention to reduce alcohol use after MBS[17]. Patients strongly endorsed the use of a technology-based approach, appreciated how this approach could be tailored to the individual’s readiness to change alcohol use, and provided feedback regarding the duration of the proposed intervention[17]. The objective of this investigation was to examine the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary outcomes of this technology-based intervention to reduce alcohol use delivered after MBS through a pilot randomized controlled trial.

Methods

Participants and procedures

This study was approved by the health system’s Institutional Review Board and was preregistered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04788316). Participants who had undergone MBS at a single health care system 3–18 months prior were invited by email to complete a web-based eligibility survey. Patients tend to re-initiate alcohol use between 6–18 months following MBS and alcohol misuse and AUD appear to rise between 1 and 2 years postoperatively [4,6,12,13]. The goal of this intervention is to prevent re-initiation of postoperative alcohol use as well as prevent the escalation of alcohol use and development of an AUD. Thus, the 3–18 month timeframe for the intervention was selected so that individuals would be targeted before or at the time they may reinitiate alcohol use as well as before alcohol misuse or AUD is expected to develop. The eligibility survey asked patients if they consumed alcohol prior to MBS, if they had consumed any alcohol since having MBS, and whether or not they had internet access and a cell phone with text messaging capabilities. Participants were excluded if they had not ever consumed alcohol prior to MBS or if they did not have access to internet or text messaging. Participants were provided with information about the study verbally and provided informed consent verbally and documented their consent electronically. After consenting to participate and completing the baseline assessment, participants were randomized to the intervention (described below) or a treatment-as-usual control group. Urn randomization was used, stratifying by sex and time since MBS (i.e., less than 1 year versus 1 year or more). Participants again completed questionnaires 3-months after baseline (i.e., immediately after the text messaging phase was complete). All assessments were completed through Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), which is a web-based, secure, and HIPAA compliant program.

Measures

Feasibility

To determine feasibility of the intervention, the proportion of those who began and completed the intervention was calculated. To determine feasibility of the study protocol, the proportion of those who completed the postintervention assessment was calculated as well as the number of weekly text messages that were responded to (out of the total numbers that were sent). In the intervention development, an appropriate benchmark to determine feasibility is 75% adherence [18,19].

Acceptability

Participants who completed the intervention responded to items on the Usability and Satisfaction Assessment to measure acceptability of the intervention. This measure was adapted from prior work and captures quantitative data on perceived usability, overall satisfaction, helpfulness, and content relevance of a technology-based intervention [20,21]. Each item is on a five-point Likert scale.

Motivation and alcohol use outcomes

At baseline and the postintervention assessment (3-months after baseline), participants responded to the following question: “On a scale from 0 (not at all important) to 10 (very important), how important is it to you to avoid any alcohol use?” Using importance rulers is common in motivational interviewing, and using a single item importance ruler has been validated as a proxy for readiness to change drinking behavior [22]. We also measured past 30-day alcohol use similar to other studies, in which items were adapted from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System Questionnaire [23]. Quantity-frequency measures are the most common method of alcohol assessment and are less costly and time intensive, while also being reliable and valid [24,25]. Participants reported the number of days they consumed any alcohol in the past 30 days (0–30), which was dichotomized into whether or not they had consumed alcohol at each assessment. Participants were also shown a picture and description of a standard drink and reported the average number of standard drinks consumed per drinking day in the past 30 days. Because studies have not yet identified a level of drinking associated with low risk of alcohol misuse, our primary outcome was whether or not participants were drinking. Secondary outcomes were drinking frequency and average number of drinks per drinking day.

Intervention

Computerized brief intervention (CBI)

The intervention began with 2 (15-minute) sessions of interactive web-based content that was grounded in motivational interviewing, which were delivered 4 days apart [26,27]. The first web-based session focused on delivering psychoeducation and building motivation. Participants first reported their level of importance to abstain from alcohol use after MBS on a scale of 0 (not at all important) to 10 (very important). They then were shown a video vignette (with a character that was tailored to their reported gender and race) which delivered information regarding the increased risk of alcohol misuse and AUD after MBS. Content after the video was then tailored based on their importance rating. For example, participants characterized as “not important” or “somewhat important” (scores of 0–7) received a section on identifying ways to know when it is time to change. Participants who endorsed a high level of importance (scores of 8–10) were asked about reasons for change, how they might change, and who might help them. Statements that participants provided were selected and subsequently used in the text messaging component. The second web-based session focused on developing adaptive coping skills. The same character from session 1 was presented again in a video in which the character used several different coping skills to achieve both weight loss and reductions in alcohol use. The participant was then asked to identify potential risky situations for using alcohol and then was guided through options to select coping strategies. This content was also presented with following the spirit of Motivational Interviewing. For example, the narrator acknowledged that not everyone wants to change or believes change is necessary. Strategies could be selected that are useful for other behavior change (i.e., stress, maladaptive eating) should the participant have goals other than alcohol use.

After finishing both web-based sessions, participants were asked to provide another rating on their level of importance to abstain from alcohol use after MBS on a scale of 0–10. This final rating impacted the tailoring of the text messaging component of the intervention.

Text messaging

After completing the web-based sessions, participants received 3-months of daily text messaging based in Motivational Interviewing and adapted to each participant’s rating on the importance to avoid alcohol use (scale of 0–10). The initial week of text messages were based on the final rating provided at the second web-based session, and then participants were asked to provide a new rating each week, which could subsequently change the content that was delivered. Text messages were alcohol-related, related to lifestyle changes after MBS, or could apply to both. Following the principles of Motivational Interviewing, those with a low rating for importance in avoiding alcohol use received content that helped to develop a discrepancy between behavior and goals. Participants with a moderate importance rating received content more motivational in nature. Individuals with a high rating received messages that included advice and tips. On a weekly basis, participants also reported the number of days they used alcohol over the previous week through text messaging.

Analyses

SPSS version 25 (IBM) was used to conduct analyses. Descriptives were run for participant characteristics, feasibility, and acceptability items. We used an intention to treat approach with all participants included in outcome analyses regardless of their treatment completion status. However, we did not carry forward values for those who did not complete the post-assessment. This was chosen because alcohol use after MBS can begin (and escalate) as time elapses from MBS, and we did not want to underestimate drinking behavior. ANCOVAs were conducted to explore differences between the intervention and control groups at postintervention in ratings on the importance to avoid alcohol use, frequency of alcohol use, and average number of standard drinks, controlling for baseline values. Paired-samples t-tests were conducted within the intervention and control groups to examine change in ratings on the importance to avoid alcohol use from baseline to postintervention Chi-square analyses were conducted to examine differences between the intervention and control groups for alcohol use at baseline and postintervention.

Results

The CONSORT diagram for participants who enrolled in this study is presented in Figure 1. There were 790 patients who were contacted, 247 who completed the eligibility survey, and 208 who screened as eligible. There were 192 eligible patients who were outreached to for enrollment, and 60 who consented and enrolled (31.3% enrollment rate). Participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. Participants (N = 60) were primarily female (90%), White (55.0%) or Black (43.3%), with a mean age of approximately 45 years, which is representative of the individuals who undergo MBS at this institution. Of the 60 participants, 38 (63.3%) had reinitiated alcohol use after MBS, which was reported at the baseline assessment. There were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups for age (t = −1.36, P = .18), sex (X2 = 1.51, P = .22), race (X2 = .24, P = .63), or baseline drinking status (X2 = .43, P = .51).

Regarding feasibility, of those randomized to the intervention (n = 24), 83.3% (n = 20) began the intervention and 95% of those (n = 19) completed it. Among both the intervention and control groups, there were 55 participants (91.7%) who completed the postintervention assessment. There was a total of 490 weekly text message questions sent to the intervention participants, of which 479 were responded to (97.8% response rate). The majority of individuals rated the intervention as acceptable (Table 2). Specifically, most participants responded positively to all components of the intervention (including the CBI sessions and text messaging). Notably, 100% of participants thought that other patients would use the intervention.

Outcomes examined included motivation to avoid alcohol use and self-reported alcohol use. Controlling for baseline ratings, there were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups for their ratings of the importance of avoiding alcohol use after MBS at the postintervention (F = 3.02, P = .09). However, within-group analyses revealed that the intervention group reported a significant increase in the level of importance to avoid alcohol use from baseline to postintervention (7.04 versus 7.91, t = −2.51, P = .02) whereas the control group did not show a significant change (5.63 versus 5.78; t = −.35, P = .73). Among those with postintervention data, at baseline, there was not a significant difference between the intervention and control groups for the number of participants who were drinking versus not drinking (60.9% versus 65.6%, respectively; X2 = .13, P = .72). At postintervention, the intervention group had fewer participants endorse alcohol use compared to the control group (43.5% versus75%; X2 = 5.63, P = .02). Among those who denied postoperative alcohol use on the baseline assessment, no participants in the intervention reported alcohol use at postintervention whereas 4 participants (36.4%) in the control group reported alcohol use. Among those who reported postoperative alcohol use on the baseline assessment, 4 intervention group participants (28.6%) reported no alcohol use at postintervention whereas only 1 control group participant (4.8%) reported no alcohol use at postintervention. Controlling for baseline ratings, there were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups for frequency of alcohol use at postintervention (F = 3.81, P = .09); however, those in the intervention group reported fewer drinks per drinking day at postintervention compared to the control group (F = 6.14, P = .02).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to examine the feasibility and acceptability of a technology-based intervention to reduce alcohol use after MBS and to also explore preliminary outcomes of the intervention. Results from this study demonstrated that it was feasible to recruit patients for this intervention and deliver the intervention after MBS. The benchmark for enrollment (60 participants) was met and the sample characteristics were representative of the patients who undergo MBS at the recruitment site. More than 80% of participants initiated the intervention and of those who initiated, over 90% completed the intervention, and retention at the postintervention assessment was high (91.7%). The retention rate in this study exceeds the 80% retention rate found in other pilot studies [18]. This suggests that intervention delivery methods of web-based and text messaging platforms are successful at engaging individuals who underwent MBS. Moreover, the intervention was considered highly acceptable across all domains by most participants. The CBI sessions had higher overall ratings than the text messaging. This suggests participants prefer engaging in interventions through web-based platforms compared to text messaging. Alternatively, the slightly lower ratings for text messaging may have been due to the text messaging content itself. The goal was to provide a variety of text messages over a longer duration than the CBI, and not all content may have been relevant or useful to all participants. However, most participants rated the text messages positively. Additionally, we had approximately 98% engagement with our weekly text messages, suggesting that participants continued to read these over the 3-month period, and most suggested that a 6-month duration of text messages would be appropriate. The variety of text messages, including MBS-specific messages, may have assisted with this high level of engagement. Moreover, the primary goal of the CBI sessions and text messaging was to provide adaptive coping strategies, and no participants rated the strategies negatively. Finally, 100% of participants thought that patients would use this intervention postoperatively. Taken together, these findings support that this intervention was highly feasible and acceptable, especially when compared to other pilot studies and benchmarks set for successful intervention development [18,19].

Because this was a pilot randomized controlled trial, the main focus was on the feasibility and acceptability of the intervention; however, a second goal of this pilot study was to examine the potential impact on postintervention outcomes. Preliminary results suggested that the intervention could prevent re-initiation of alcohol use among those who had not yet consumed alcohol postoperatively, as there were no patients in the intervention group who denied alcohol use at baseline that then went on and reported alcohol use at the postintervention assessment. Findings also suggested that the intervention may reduce alcohol use. Among those who had engaged in post-operative alcohol use prior to the intervention, more of these participants denied alcohol use at the postintervention assessment in the intervention group compared to the control group. In addition, the intervention group consumed fewer drinks per sitting at postintervention compared to the control group. There was not a significant difference between the intervention and control groups for frequency of drinking; however, this could be due a lack of power with the small size. Though abstinence from alcohol may be best after surgery since there is no known “safe” amount to drink, prior work has suggested that abstinence may not be realistic for many patients who undergo MBS [28]. Preliminary findings suggest that this intervention could be useful at preventing re-initiation of alcohol use, as well as reducing alcohol use among those who have engaged in postoperative alcohol use. Both prevention of re-initiation and reduction of alcohol use could lead to decreased risk for development of an AUD, though more work is needed to determine the efficacy of the intervention. In addition, because the goal of this intervention is to prevent the development of an AUD, individuals who have already developed an AUD would likely need a more intensive intervention.

There are several limitations to note. First, after using urn randomization, groups were unbalanced with fewer participants in the intervention group. In addition, since this was a pilot study with a small sample size, it was not adequately powered to detect differences in outcomes. Future work with a larger sample size is needed to determine intervention efficacy. Further, there may have been participation and response bias. Individuals electing to participate may have been more likely to have concern regarding postoperative alcohol use, leading to their participation, and may not be representative of all patients who undergo MBS. Participants also self-reported alcohol use and may not have accurately reported their use. Future work should consider using objective measurement of alcohol use (i.e., phosphatidylethanol testing), which may be superior to self-reported use [29].

A technology-based intervention delivered after MBS was feasible, acceptable, and showed promising preliminary outcomes for reducing alcohol use. The next step in this line of work is to conduct a fully powered randomized controlled trial to determine efficacy of the intervention in reducing alcohol use following MBS. It it also important to determine whether this intervention is successful at preventing re-initiation of postoperative alcohol use, reducing alcohol use among those who have already re-initiated, or both. This would inform the timing of when this intervention could be delivered and who might benefit most from this type of intervention. Long-term follow-up is also needed to determine whether there are lasting effects of this intervention and whether it has the potential to prevent the development of an AUD.
