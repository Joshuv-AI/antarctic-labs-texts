Introduction

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) fragmentomics—the systematic analysis of DNA fragment characteristics including size distribution, end motifs, and jagged end profiles—has emerged as a transformative approach in liquid biopsy research [1, 2]. The characteristic of fragment size, 166 bp peak dominant in plasma cfDNA, are associated with nucleosomes and liker DNA [3]. Four-mer end motifs profile is association with pathophysiologic conditions such as pregnancy, transplantation, and cancer [4]. Crucially, jagged end formation (single-stranded protruding ends) is modulated by both nuclease concentration gradients across biofluids and the mode of cellular demise: Apoptosis typically produces cleaner double-strand breaks via caspase-activated Deoxyribonuclease(DNase), whereas necrosis releases DNA with complex terminal modifications due to uncontrolled protease activity [3, 5, 6]. These fragmentomic characteristics—including cleavage patterns relative to nucleosomes, fragment length size distributions, and strand end profiles—collectively serve as molecular fingerprints, encoded by both the tissue origin of cfDNA, the enzyme activity in body fluids and underlying pathophysiological states [7].

While blood-derived cfDNA fragmentomics has been extensively characterized in prenatal screening and oncology [8–10], fluid-specific fragmentation patterns are increasingly recognized. For example, urine cfDNA exhibits high fragmentation, shorter fragment peaks, and altered jagged end periodicity compared to plasma [11]. These observations support the notion that distinct biofluids harbor unique cfDNA fragmentation profiles, shaped by tissue origins and local enzymatic activity.

Despite these advancements, seminal plasma (SP) cfDNA remains poorly understudied. SP is a complex mixture of secretions from testicular, epididymal, prostatic, and seminal vesicle sources, offering a unique window into male reproductive health [12]. The dynamic nature of spermatogenesis—including germ cell proliferation, meiosis, and apoptosis—implies that cfDNA derived from SP may exhibit unique fragmentomic features. Previous studies have investigated cfDNA concentration in SP and its potential relationship to sperm quality [13], but a systematic analysis of fragmentomic signatures and disease-specific alterations in SP cfDNA has not been conducted. This knowledge gap is particularly critical for two prevalent infertility etiologies: nonobstructive azoospermia (NOA), characterized by complete spermatogenic failure, and varicocele (VC), where venous reflux induces testicular microenvironmental alterations. Current diagnostic tools—such as invasive testicular biopsies for NOA [14] and subjective physical examinations for VC [15]—underscore the urgent need for accurate, noninvasive biomarkers.

In this article, we used an integrative multi-omics framework combining whole-genome sequencing, jagged end sequencing (Jag-Seq) with non-CG(CH) methylation analysis, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC)-based statistical modeling. This framework revealed both fluid-specific and disease-specific cfDNA fragmentation signatures in SP, offering novel insights for the development of diagnostic strategies targeting male infertility.

Methods

Human sample collection and processing

The study included patients (diagnoses of VC [n = 14] and NOA [n = 19]), along with age-matched healthy controls provided SP (n = 18), and additional age-matched healthy controls provided plasma from a separate cohort (n = 20). Following 3–5 days of sexual abstinence, participants collected seminal samples via masturbation into sterile wide-mouthed containers. Semen samples were immediately transported to the laboratory. Samples that did not liquefy within 30 min at room temperature were excluded from the study, as incomplete liquefaction can affect cfDNA extraction efficiency and may reflect underlying prostatic abnormalities. All participants were Asian. Comprehensive demographic characteristics and clinical parameters are detailed in Supplemental Table. Study design and analytical workflow

Firstly, we performed fragmentomic comparisons of seminal plasma cfDNA (n = 18) and blood plasma cfDNA (n = 20) using WGS and Jag-Seq, analyzing fragment size distribution, end motifs heat map, preferred end motif, jagged end length, methylation level, and the relationship of cfDNA size and jagged end length. Then we performed a translational application varicocele (n = 14) and nonobstructive azoospermia (n = 19) to develop a diagnostic model.

Sample collection and processing

Following 3–5 days of sexual abstinence, participants collected seminal samples via masturbation into sterile wide-mouthed containers. Semen samples were immediately transported to the laboratory for centrifugation at 4000×g (10 min, room temperature). The obtained SP was aliquoted and stored at – 80 °C until analysis.

Plasma from the blood samples was collected through centrifugation at 1600×g (10 min, 4 °C). The obtained plasma supernatant was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

cfDNA extraction and quantification

cfDNA was extracted from approximately 0.4 mL of SP or plasma using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen). All cfDNA samples underwent dual quality assessment: purity analysis via NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and precise quantification using Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The specific sequencing protocol are shown in Methods of Additional file.

The MDS theoretically ranges from 0 to 1, with a value of 1 indicating a perfectly uniform distribution. Lower MDS values reflect increased skewness in motif distribution. For example, if one motif accounts for 99% of the total, the MDS would approach 0, indicating highly uneven motif usage.

Jagged index based on the filling of methylated cytosines

This method assesses cfDNA end jaggedness by leveraging methylated cytosine incorporation during end repair. For cfDNA molecules with 5′ or 3′ overhangs, DNA polymerase extends jagged end using methylated dCTP alongside standard nucleotides. Bisulfite treatment selectively converts unmethylated cytosines to uracils, preserving methylated cytosines in both strands. Methylation densities are calculated for Read1 (original strand, M1) and Read2 (synthesized strand, M2 ≈ 100%), with the jaggedness index based on methylation (JI-M) defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$JI-M=\frac{M2-M1}{M2}\times 100\%$$\end{document}JI-M=M2-M1M2×100%where M1 represents the methylation densities of read1 and M2 represents the methylation density of read2.

Statistical analysis

Clinical characteristics of the study were described as median and range for continuous variables. Differences between groups were analyzed by Mann–Whitney U-tests for continuous (not normally distributed) variables. Statistical analyses and figure plot were performed using Python; all statistical tests were two-sided, and P values smaller than 0.05 were defined as significant. There was no adjustment of the significance level for multiple testing.

Discussion

This study establishes the first multi-dimensional atlas of SP cfDNA architecture, revealing four cardinal distinctions through integrated WGS and Jag-seq fragmentomic analysis. SP cfDNA exhibits bimodal size distribution with broader peak at 151 bp and 110 bp sub-peak, contrasting with plasma cfDNA 166 bp sharp peak pattern. The frequency of sub-nucleosome length (< 150 bp) is higher in SP, suggesting testicular-specific fragmentation processes. Hierarchical clustering of 256 4-mer motifs demonstrated fluid-specific grouping, and the second base position of cfDNA preferred C in plasma, whereas SP cfDNA retains A-preferred bases at positions − 2 to − 4. SP cfDNA displayed enhanced 10-bp periodicity, longer jagged ends, and higher JI-M. These fragmentation features were consistent with distinct nuclease activity and chromatin remodeling mechanisms in the male reproductive system, as supported by previous reports of elevated nuclease levels in semen and our ELISA findings. However, additional functional studies are needed to validate the causal mechanisms behind these observations. In this study, we investigated that difference of two biofluid cfDNA fragmentomics. On the other hand, the disease-specific SP cfDNA fragmentomics were present in patients with VC and NOA. This finding may have physiological relevance, reflecting fluid-specific cfDNA release patterns, and pathophysiological importance, providing insights into reproductive health and the potential development of noninvasive biomarkers. Nonetheless, further validation in larger cohorts and functional studies are required to confirm their clinical utility.

Previous studies on plasma cfDNA have primarily reported unimodal distribution, typically with fragments around 166 bp, corresponding to nucleosome-protected DNA during apoptosis in somatic cells [2]. The observed abundance of short fragments and bimodal distribution (110 bp and 151 bp peaks) in SP cfDNA may be associated with unique biological processes in the male reproductive system, including spermatogenesis, apoptosis, DNA histone-to-protamine replacement during sperm nuclear condensation, and activity of DNase. The activity of DNase Ⅰ family nucleases in body fluids were different, which are known to be active during cell death and tissue turnover, could be involved in generating these shorter fragments [5] [16, 17]. The activity of DNase Ⅰ has been reported to be substantially higher in semen (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$25\pm 4.8\times {10}^{6}$$\end{document}25±4.8×106 units/g protein) compared to serum (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4.6\pm 1.9\times {10}^{6}$$\end{document}4.6±1.9×106 units/g protein), which may explain the enhanced DNA cleavage and distinct cfDNA fragmentation profiles observed in SP [18].

The cfDNA end motif analysis revealed notable differences between SP and blood plasma, suggesting fluid-specific nuclease activity and distinct degradation mechanisms [1]. The positional nucleotide bases at motif sites—a predominance at positions − 2 ~ − 4 in SP versus C/T in plasma cfDNA. The unique preferred end motifs possibly reflected the activity of reproductive system-associated nucleases, supported by the higher concentration of DNase Ⅰ in SP [18]. Such motifs may originate from apoptosis or other programmed cell death processes during spermatogenesis, where endonucleases cleave DNA at precise sites [19]. While in contrast, blood plasma cfDNA typically displays end motifs indicative of general cellular turnover, reflecting more ubiquitous degradation processes across various tissues. MDS elevated in SP, which constitutes one of the features of the subsequent disease diagnosis, and correlates with the concentration of SP DNase Ⅰ, reflecting “systemic” nuclease perturbation.

Compared with traditional diagnostic methods, such as semen analysis, imaging, or biopsy, cfDNA-based analysis offers a minimally invasive, reproducible, and molecular-level approach to evaluating male reproductive health [20]. VC is associated with venous reflux-induced oxidative stress and local hypoxia, whereas NOA is characterized by complete spermatogenic failure due to germ cell apoptosis [14, 15]. These disease-specific states were reflected in SP cfDNA profiles. VC cfDNA showed C-preferred and NOA showed T-preferred at position − 1. NOA cfDNA showed enhanced 10-bp periodicity, and this periodicity likely reflects increased accessibility of cfDNA to nucleosomal cleavage, which may result from disrupted chromatin organization and aberrant apoptotic processing of germ cells during failed spermatogenesis [11]. The global hypermethylation at CH sites was significantly higher in NOA, and jagged end length were difference in disease, potentially linked to disrupted nucleosome protection and altered DNA–protein interactions resulting from epigenetic dysregulation. In this study, ROC analysis using cfDNA features achieved diagnostic accuracies of 83% for VC and 87% for NOA. These findings highlight the promise of cfDNA fragmentomics as a biomarker platform for subclassifying male infertility with high specificity.

This study, while providing valuable insights into the structural characteristics of SP cfDNA, does have several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Validation in larger and more diverse cohorts is necessary to confirm the reproducibility and robustness of the observed cfDNA fragmentation patterns. Additionally, technical limitations such as variations in cfDNA extraction and quantification methods could affect the reproducibility of the results. Future studies should also explore cfDNA fragmentomics in other male reproductive conditions to broaden clinical applicability.

In conclusion, this work presents novel insights into fluid- and disease-specific cfDNA fragmentation features, contributing to a deeper understanding of its biological and pathological roles. Beyond its diagnostic utility, the disease-specific fragmentomics of cfDNA presents a promising noninvasive frontier for monitoring reproductive health conditions and evaluating the effectiveness of various interventions, including vasectomy, fertility treatments, and oncological therapies relevant to the reproductive system.
