Electrochemical and dielectric properties of cobalt doped SnS on cotton based electrodes for potential use in supercapacitors
Authors: Mohd Arif Dar, Safwat A Mahmoud, S R Majid, M Satgunam, L Guganathan, P Arularasan, M Karunakaran, S Sheik Fareed, Hilal Ahmad Rather, Lamiaa Galal Amin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerein, we synthesize Co-doped SnS nanoparticles (NPs) using a simple solvothermal method. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra determine the orthorhombic structure of the Co-doped SnS NPs. The Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirm the rod-like structure of the Co-doped SnS NPs. The surface area of 31, 225, 256 and 383 (m2/g) of Co-doped SnS NPs determines their tendency for electrical and supercapacitive properties. The deconvolution curves show the binding energy peaks of Sn, S and Co, confirming the doping of Co ions in the lattice of SnS compound. The 20%Co:SnS electrode retains 94% of its capacitance with a coulombic efficiency of 78% even after 3000 cycles of GCD curves. The SnS XRD peaks observed even after the cyclic stability test of 20%Co:SnS electrode determine the stability of the electrodes. The conductivity values of the AN1, AN2, AN3 and AN4 cotton electrodes are 9.6 × 10−08, 3.5 × 10−07, 5.8 × 10−07 and 1.2 × 10−06, providing their tendency for future material in dielectric and microelectronic device applications.Keywords: Electrochemical, Electrical, Capacitive retention, Coulombic efficiencySubject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The rapid increase in global energy consumption, together with the transition toward renewable resources, has intensified the demand for efficient and reliable energy storage systems. Among various technologies, supercapacitors have emerged as highly promising due to their outstanding power density, ultrafast charge–discharge response, and prolonged cycling stability, which render them particularly suitable for applications requiring instantaneous energy delivery such as electric mobility, smart grids, and portable electronics1–5. Despite these advantages, their relatively low energy density compared to conventional batteries remains a critical limitation, necessitating the development of innovative electrode materials that can bridge this gap and extend device functionality. The electrode structure and composition dictate capacitance, durability, and rate characteristics of the material. For this purpose, significant research efforts are directed toward the design of materials that simultaneously exhibit superior electrochemical performance, sustainability, and scalability for practical deployment6–15. Furthermore, the growing need for environmentally benign and cost-effective fabrication strategies underscores the importance of developing electrode systems that can balance high efficiency with large-scale manufacturability, thereby accelerating the integration of supercapacitors into next-generation energy technologies.

Beyond their established role in rapid energy delivery, supercapacitors are also valued for their safety, wide operating temperature range, and superior power handling compared to batteries. Their ability to withstand millions of cycles without significant degradation makes them indispensable for applications where long-term reliability is crucial. Depending on the charge storage mechanism, they are broadly categorized into electrochemical double-layer capacitors (EDLCs), which rely on non-faradaic ion adsorption at the electrode–electrolyte interface, and pseudocapacitors, which utilize fast and reversible redox reactions to achieve higher capacitance16–19. A hybrid configuration that combines both mechanisms has also gained momentum, aiming to achieve a balance between power and energy density. This classification highlights the central importance of electrode materials, as their surface area, conductivity and structural stability determine the efficiency of charge storage and transfer processes. Consequently, tailoring material properties at the nanoscale, incorporating heteroatom doping and engineering hierarchical architectures are being actively explored to overcome existing limitations and advance supercapacitor technology for emerging energy-intensive applications20–22.

Also, dielectric properties are increasingly recognized as a decisive factor governing the electrochemical performance of supercapacitor electrodes, as they directly influence charge storage capacity, charge–discharge kinetics and coulombic efficiency23–25. In EDLCs, energy storage occurs through the formation of electric double layers at the electrode–electrolyte interface, where a high dielectric constant substantially enhances interfacial charge accumulation, thereby improving the attainable energy density26,27. Equally important is the dielectric strength, which reflects the resistance of a material to electrical breakdown, thereby, electrodes with high dielectric strength enable ultrafast cycling stability and efficient energy delivery, a requirement in advanced technologies such as electric transportation and smart grids. In hybrid supercapacitors that integrate double-layer and faradaic storage, fine-tuning dielectric characteristics becomes even more critical, as these parameters govern the kinetics and stability of charge transfer across the electrode–electrolyte boundary28–30. Thus, rational optimization of dielectric behavior in electrode materials is indispensable for enhancing energy density, accelerating charge–discharge processes and ensuring thermal robustness, these are key attributes required for the large-scale deployment of supercapacitors in high-performance and energy-intensive systems31,32.

Parallel to dielectric optimization, material selection plays a pivotal role in defining the overall storage capability of supercapacitors. Conventional systems have employed transition metal oxides, conductive polymers, and carbon-based nanostructures such as graphene, owing to their large surface area, electrical conductivity and tunable surface chemistry, which collectively contribute to enhanced charge accumulation and high energy density33–36. More recently, metal chalcogenides have attracted considerable attention due to their diverse structural phases, rich redox activity, and favorable electrical properties, with binary compounds such as SnS, NiS, MnS, PbS and SnSe emerging as promising candidates37–39. Despite their potential, pristine chalcogenide electrodes often suffer from limited specific capacitance and inferior cycling stability compared to oxide-based counterparts. To overcome these challenges, cationic doping strategies involving transition metals such as Co, Ni, Mn, Fe, Cu, and Zn have been employed to tailor the physicochemical properties and electrochemical response40. Among these, cobalt incorporation has proven particularly effective, as Co ions introduce additional redox-active sites, enhance electronic conductivity, and increase the electrochemically accessible surface area, thereby promoting rapid charge transport and higher energy storage capability. Such modifications significantly elevate the specific capacitance and energy density, underscoring the promise of doped metal chalcogenides as next-generation electrode materials for high-performance supercapacitors40–46.

Here, in this study, Co-doped SnS NPs are synthesized through the hydrothermal method. The synthesized NPs are characterized through the different characterization techniques such as Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) studies. Finally, the electrodes of the Co-doped SnS NPs are prepared using Ni-foam, graphite substrate and white cotton pads. The Ni-foam electrode was made in the dimension (2 × 1 cm2), graphite and cotton pads were cut into coin cell size. The slurry of Co-doped SnS NPs formed was pasted on Ni-foam, graphite substrate and cotton using the doctor blade method. The electrodes were then analyzed using cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charging/discharging (GCD), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests. The Co-doped SnS cotton electrodes were further used for electrical studies. Lastly, XRD, FTIR, and Raman studies were conducted after the cyclic stability test of the Co-doped SnS graphite electrode to assess the electrode’s stability.

Experimental

The solvothermal method was used to synthesize nanocrystals of Sn1− xCoxS, where x is 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, and 0.20. The investigation utilized multiple types of raw materials of analytical grade without any additional purification. The tin chloride (SnCl2·2H2O), cobalt chloride (CoCl2·4H2O), and Thiourea (SC(NH2)2) purchased from Sigma Aldrich (99.9% analytical grade) were weighed based on the mmol ratio and chemical formula of the metal cation in Sn1− xCoxS. The heterogeneous components were dissolved in 5 mL of acetic acid and 50 ml of tartaric acid. The mixture was then agitated for approximately 30 min until a uniform solution was obtained. Ultimately, the solution was placed within a 50 ml stainless steel autoclave and subjected to a temperature of around 180 °C for 18 h in an electric oven. The resultant precipitates were repeatedly rinsed with deionized water and acetone to eliminate contaminants. The final product was dehydrated in an electric oven at a temperature of 60 °C for 12 h. The produced NPs of Sn1− xCoxS (x = 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, and 0.20) were utilized for various characterization experiments. The NPs were designated as 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co:SnS NPs, respectively. Figure 1 displays the schematic image of the formation of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co:SnS NPs. The slurry for the formation of the electrode was prepared by using the active material (5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co: SnS NPs, separately), acetylene black, and PVDF in the ratio (85:10:05), as shown in Fig. 2. The substrates used are Ni foam (2 × 1 cm2), graphite, and cotton in the form of coin cells (Fig. 2). The specific capacitance (Cp) values are calculated using the formulas for CV and GCD studies, respectively46,47.

Results and Discussion

Structural and morphological studies

Figure 3a shows the XRD spectra of 5%, 10%, 15% and 20%Co:SnS NPs which exhibited diffraction peaks at 20.07o, 26.07º, 27.48o, 30.49o, 31.55o, 32.0º, 39.09o, 44.77o, 45.61o, 48.58o, 51.33o, 54.32o and 66.81o, corresponding to (110), (120), (021), (101), (111), (040), (131), (141), (150), (211), (151), (061) and (080) hkl planes, respectively. The peak positions of the 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co:SnS NPs matched with JCPDS card No. 14–0620, having the orthorhombic structure of space group Pnma-62. Furthermore, the XRD spectra of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co:SnS NPs have not shown any additional crystal phases such as SnS2, S, and Sn2S3. Also, the intensity of high peaks decreases continuously from 5% to 15%Co:SnS NPs with increasing Co dopants (Fig. 3b). The decrease of the high-intensity peak occurs due to the difference in the ionic radius between Sn2+ (1.22 Aº) and Co2+ (0.65 Aº) ions48,49. This observation provides strong evidence that Co2+ ions are present in the lattice positions of SnS. Further, it is observed that when the doping concentration reaches up to 20%Co:SnS, the high-intensity peak increases, indicating the interstitial and substitutional doping of Co2+ ions in the SnS lattice sites. The pronounced increase in the (040) peak intensity at 20%Co doping can be primarily attributed to the preferred orientation of the rod-like crystallites and dopant-induced recrystallization, which enhances crystallinity and reduces lattice strain49. The decrease in the intensity of most peaks from 5% to 20%Co:SnS NPs has changed their morphology from rod-like to spherical nanospheres. The calculated average crystallite size for 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co-doped SnS nanoparticles was found to be 33, 30, 33, and 34 nm, respectively, with corresponding microstrain values of 1.1 × 10−3, 1.4 × 10−3, 1.4 × 10−3 and 1.3 × 10−3 and dislocation density 1.3 × 10−3, 2.0 × 10−3, 2.3 × 10−3 and 2.0 × 10−3, respectively. The marginal variations in crystallite size and microstrain suggest that the incorporation of Co ions into the SnS lattice does not cause significant distortion or phase transformation. This behavior can be attributed to the comparable ionic radii of Co²⁺ and Sn²⁺, which facilitates substitution without inducing major lattice strain. The relatively stable dislocation density further confirms that the introduction of dopant ions does not substantially disrupt the crystalline framework. The consistency of the XRD peak profiles across all compositions reinforces this observation, reflecting a robust crystal structure with only minor local perturbations induced by Co incorporation. Thus, the near invariance of the average structural parameters demonstrates that the Co dopant is effectively accommodated within the SnS lattice, preserving crystallinity while introducing only minimal strain and defect density. This structural stability is advantageous for maintaining the integrity of the material while tuning its physical and electrochemical properties.

The purity and bonding of elements in 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co:SnS NPs were classified using FTIR spectroscopy. Figure 4 shows the FTIR spectrum of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co:SnS NPs recorded in the wavelength range between 400 and 4000 cm−1. The FTIR spectrum exhibited several absorption bands at 566, 570, 608, 669, 671,693, 1113, 1384, 1632, 2852, 2921 and 3443 (cm−1) for the 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co:SnS NPs. The band observed at 3443 cm−1 belongs to the hydroxyl groups related to O-H stretching vibrations51. The band observed at 1632 cm−1 belongs to hydroxyl groups of O-H bending vibrational modes52. The bands observed at 2852 and 2921 (cm−1) are related to the C-H stretching vibrations52. The band pattern observed at 1113 and 1384 (cm−1) belongs to C-O and C=O functional groups53. The characteristic band observed at 608 cm−1 belongs to the vibrational modes of antisymmetric Sn-S stretching vibrations54,55. This band moves towards a higher wavelength with doping of Co ions and appears at 669, 671, and 693 (cm−1) for 10%, 15%, and 20%C:SnS NPs. Further, the intensity of this band decreases with Co ion concentration from 5% to 20%C:SnS NPs coincides with XRD results. Also, two more small peaks are seen at 566 and 570 (cm−1) for the 10% and 15%Co:SnS NPs belong to the doping of metal Co ions.

Figure 6(a–l) displays the SEM images of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co:SnS NPs. At lower doping concentration (5%), the nanoparticles exhibit a pronounced rod-like morphology [Figure 6(a–c)]. However, with increasing Co content, a progressive morphological transformation occurs, in which the rods evolve into nearly spherical nanoparticles. This transition can be attributed to the influence of Co incorporation on the lattice dynamics and surface energy. The reduction in XRD peak intensity and the shift in the FTIR Sn–S vibrational band suggest that Co dopants induce subtle lattice distortions, which in turn alter the preferred growth directions. As a result, the anisotropic rod-like growth is suppressed, favoring isotropic nucleation and the formation of spherical particles. Moreover, the diameter of the spherical structures increases steadily beyond 10%Co doping [Figure 6(d–l)], reflecting dopant-driven modifications in crystallization and coalescence processes.To corroborate these findings, TEM analysis of 5%Co:SnS nanoparticles was performed. The TEM images [Figure 7(a, b)] clearly confirm the rod-like morphology, consistent with the SEM observations. HRTEM images [Figure 7(c–f)] provide further insights: the presence of fine spherical contrast spots within the rods indicates localized incorporation of Co dopants, generating minor inhomogeneities within the lattice. The lattice fringe image [Figure 7(e)] shows the (040) crystallographic plane with well-resolved spacing, while the SAED pattern [Figure 7(f)] exhibits sharp diffraction rings, confirming the crystalline nature of the doped nanoparticles.

SEM images (a-c) 5%, (d-f) 10%, (g-i) 15% and (j-l) 20%Co:SnS NPs, respectively.

(a, b) TEM images (c-e) HRTEM images (f) SAED pattern of the 5%Co:SnS NPs.

Thus, the introduction of Co not only modifies the crystallographic features but also governs the morphological transition from rods to spheres. This transformation is likely driven by surface energy minimization and strain relaxation, as spherical particles present lower surface energy and reduced lattice strain compared to anisotropic rods at higher doping levels. Importantly, such morphological evolution enhances the electrochemical properties of the Co:SnS system, since spherical nanostructures offer a larger electrochemically active surface area and improved ion accessibility compared to rod-like structures.

Figure 8(a) presents the XPS survey spectrum of the 5%Co:SnS NPs, confirming the presence of Sn, S, and Co elements in the sample. The high-resolution spectrum of Sn 3 d [Figure 8(b)] displays two distinct peaks at binding energies between 485 and 500 eV, corresponding to Sn 3d5/2 and Sn 3d3/2, which are characteristic of Sn species47. The S 2p spectrum [Figure 8(c)] exhibits peaks in the range of 155–175 eV, consistent with the binding energies of S atoms in the SnS lattice6. The Co 2p spectrum [Figure 8(d)] reveals well-defined peaks in the range of 770–810 eV, which can be assigned to the Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2 states20. The successful detection of Co 2p features, along with the characteristic Sn and S peaks, confirms the incorporation of Co ions into the SnS lattice without the formation of secondary impurity phases.

Electrochemical studies

The electrochemical studies of the 5%, 10%, 15% and 20%Co:SnS electrodes were checked out in a three and two-electrode system. Figure 9(a-d) shows the CV of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co:SnS electrodes in a three electrode system. All the electrodes show close to rectangular CV profiles, typical for EDLC behavior, with no visible faradaic redox peaks. The lack of redox features demonstrates that the charge storage mechanism is primarily controlled by non-faradaic electrostatic adsorption of ions at the electrode/electrolyte interface, instead of pseudocapacitive or battery-type reactions. The retention of this EDLC-type behavior at all doping levels indicates that the introduction of Co into the SnS lattice does not bring about substantial surface redox activity but rather preserves structural integrity and rapid ion adsorption/desorption kinetics. Significantly, the progressive development of the CV region with growing Co content indicates improved capacitive characteristics, which can be explained by the change in morphology (rod-like to spherical) and related gain in available electroactive surface area. The close-to-ideal rectangular characteristics of the curves, even under more elevated scan rates, also emphasize the good reversibility and fast charge transfer of the Co:SnS electrodes, evidencing their applicability for high-rate energy storage. Figure 9(e) shows the GCD curves of pure, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co:SnS electrodes at the current density of 1 A/g in a three electrode system. The values of specific capacitance derived from the GCD plots of pure, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co:SnS electrodes are 8, 11, 21, 41, and 115 F g⁻¹, respectively (Fig. 9f). This stepwise increase unequivocally illustrates that incorporation of Co greatly increases the charge storage ability of SnS, where greater doping concentrations offer greater electroactive sites, better surface area, and quicker ion mobility, which in turn increases the overall capacitance performance.

Figure 15 (a) shows the Nyquist plots of pure, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%Co:SnS electrodes along with the equivalent circuit model employed for fitting. The spectra show a semicircle arc in the high-frequency region followed by a linear Warburg tail at low frequencies, characteristic of combined charge-transfer resistance and ion diffusion mechanisms, respectively. Here, it is seen that 20%, 15%, 10% and 5% doped samples of SnS have smaller semicircle diameters than pristine SnS, verifying a significant reduction of charge-transfer resistance by doping. Among all the compositions, the 20%Co:SnS electrode shows the lowest, implying augmented back electronic conductivity and more rapid interfacial charge transport. This enhancement is ascribable to the synergistic action of Co incorporation, which causes defect states, maximizes the lattice structure and facilitates efficient accessibility of the electrolyte ions. The approximately vertical slope in the low-frequency region for the 20%Co:SnS electrode still highlights superior capacitive behavior with lower diffusion resistance, consistent with the ideal EDLC-type response. Figure 15 (b) presents the Bode phase angle plots of the pure, 5%, 10%, 15% and 20%Co:SnS electrodes. The maximum phase angle for the pure SnS electrode is near 50°, whereas the phase angles of the Co-doped samples are lower, with 20%Co:SnS retaining a consistent response at a wide range of frequencies. The decreased phase angle at higher Co concentrations suggests fast ion dynamics and better capacitive kinetics with faster relaxation times and greater charge storage reversibility. This agrees with the Nyquist analysis, which supports the fact that Co doping efficiently accelerates electron and ion transport in the electrode–electrolyte system.

(a) Nyquist plot (b) Bode plot of the pure, 5%, 10%, 15% and 20%Co:SnS electrodes, respectively.

In general, the EIS outcomes reveal that Co doping considerably reduces charge-transfer resistance and aligns ion diffusion channels to the best extent, with 20%Co:SnS possessing the most effective kinetics. These observations are very clear-cut evidence that controlled doping may be a facile method to modify interfacial charge transfer and enhance the capacitive performances of SnS-based supercapacitor electrodes.

At lower frequencies, these mechanisms have sufficient time to align with the alternating electric field, leading to an enhanced dielectric response. The interfacial polarization, also known as Maxwell-Wagner polarization, is particularly significant in composite materials like Co-doped SnS, where distinct interfaces between the doped and undoped regions contribute to charge accumulation and enhanced polarization62. As the frequency increases beyond this range (up to log 5.12 Hz or approximately 130,000 Hz), the ability of these polarization mechanisms to keep pace with the rapidly alternating electric field diminishes. The heavier and slower polarization components, such as interfacial and ionic polarization, cannot respond as quickly, resulting in a decrease in ε. Eventually, at even higher frequencies, only the fastest electronic polarization remains active, and the dielectric constant reaches a steady state or saturation. This behavior reflects the frequency-dependent dielectric response intrinsic to AN1, AN2, AN3 and AN4 electrodes, demonstrating how the material’s microstructure and dopant distribution influence its overall polarization characteristics and electrical performance62. Conclusion

In this research, Co-doped SnS NPs were successfully prepared by a simple solvothermal method, producing nanorods and nanospheres with orthorhombic crystal phases. The structural and morphological characterization (XRD, SEM, TEM) affirmed the uniform substitution of Co into the SnS lattice, whereas XPS deconvolution confirmed the existence of Sn, S, and Co binding states. The progressive enhancement in surface area from 31 to 383 m²/g upon increasing Co doping, as confirmed by BET studies, emphasizes the importance of dopants in maximizing electrochemical activity. The 20%Co:SnS electrode exhibited outstanding capacitive performance, maintaining 94% capacitance with 78% coulombic efficiency after 3000 cycles, thus expressing its long-term durability and stability. Furthermore, the enhanced electrical conductivity of the Co-doped SnS NPs at high Co content further supports the multifunctional applications of these nanostructures. Overall, these findings make Co-doped SnS NPs efficient candidates to be used as next-generation supercapacitors, dielectric devices, and microelectronic applications, and this provides a solid basis for their translation into useful energy storage devices.
