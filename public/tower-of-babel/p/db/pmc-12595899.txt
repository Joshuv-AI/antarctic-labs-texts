Clinical features and prognostic factors of ovarian cancer brain metastases from ovarian cancer: a retrospective multicenter study of 129 cases

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOvarian cancer brain metastases (OCBM) are rare and have poor prognosis, with limited clinical management guidelines. This study aimed to identify prognostic factors and optimal treatment approaches for patients with OCBM.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective multicenter analysis of patients with OCBM from 12 hospitals in China from May 2010 to May 2022. The primary outcomes were overall survival (OS) and brain metastasis–specific survival (BMSS). Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses were used to assess treatment outcomes and identify prognostic risk factors.ResultsIn total, 129 patients with OCBM were included. The median interval from ovarian cancer diagnosis to brain metastasis (BM) was 25.74 (range: 0–103.1) months. Headache attributed to BM was the most common presenting symptom, reported in 64 (49.6%) patients, followed by paralysis in 23 (17.8%) patients. The cerebellum and brainstem were the most frequent metastatic sites (36 patients, 27.9%), followed by the frontal lobe (27 patients, 20.9%). In BMSS analysis, multiple BM lesions (hazard ratio [HR]: 2.060; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.308–3.244; P = 0.002), headache attributed to BM (HR: 1.765; 95% CI: 1.049–2.968; P = 0.032), and relapse lines before BM diagnosis (HR: 2.060; 95% CI: 1.308–3.244; P = 0.002) were independent predictors of worse BMSS. Subgroup Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients with a single BM lesion achieved significantly better outcomes with stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) compared with those having multiple lesions.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that patients with a single BM lesion may benefit from SRS, whereas those with multiple lesions require more individualized treatment strategies.

Background

Brain metastases (BM) occur in approximately 2.5%–6.0% of patients with ovarian cancer (OC), with a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of only 29.2% [1–4]. Because BMs in OC (OCBM) are rare, treatment strategies are often adapted from approaches used for BM in other cancers or guided by clinical experience.

Current guidelines recommend craniotomy, radiotherapy, and molecularly targeted therapies [5]. However, the absence of standardized treatment protocols poses major challenges in patient management. Further complicating care, many clinical trials exclude patients with BM, thereby simplifying research protocols but neglecting the complexities of OCBM treatment [6]. In contrast, precision treatment strategies for BM in more common cancers, such as lung or breast cancer, have significantly improved survival outcomes [7, 8]. These approaches may provide valuable insights for OCBM management and warrant further exploration [9]. Consequently, effective OCBM treatment requires a comprehensive, multidimensional approach balancing survival outcomes and quality of life [10, 11].

To address these challenges, we conducted a retrospective, pooled, multicenter study. The goal was to evaluate current management practices and examine the distinctive clinical features of OCBM, aiming to enhance care quality and bridge existing gaps for these patients.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective, hospital-based, multicenter study included patients diagnosed with OC and BM between May 2010 and May 2022 across 12 institutions in China. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years at diagnosis; (2) confirmed primary malignant tumor originating in the ovary, fallopian tube, or primary peritoneal epithelium; (3) clearly documented BM; (4) available clinical data; and (5) minimum follow-up period of 12 months. Exclusion criteria were (1) incomplete information on BM and (2) unspecified survival time (Supplemental Fig. S1).

Outcome assessment

Demographic, clinical, treatments, and outcome data were collected from electronic medical records at participating institutions (Table 1 and Supplemental Table S1). Clinicopathological variables included age at OC diagnosis, FIGO stage (2018), histological subtype of the primary tumor, antigen 125 (CA-125) level at BM diagnosis, details of primary surgery, and response to platinum-based therapy. BM-related factors included relapse lines prior to BM, size, location, presenting symptoms, time from OC diagnosis to BM onset, and BM treatments (Table 1). Because only 10.8% of patients were diagnosed at FIGO stage I and II, survival analyses were restricted to those with advanced disease.

The primary outcomes were OS, defined as time from OC diagnosis to death from any cause; BM-specific survival (BMSS), defined as time from BM diagnosis to death from any cause; and progression-free survival (PFS), defined as time from initiation of treatment till disease progression or death whichever occurs first. Headache attributed to BM was defined as headache directly caused by intracranial neoplastic lesions, following the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3), criteria for headache attributed to intracranial malignancy [5, 12]. The interval to BM (IBM) was defined as time from OC diagnosis to BM occurrence, and the platinum-free interval (PFI) was defined as the time from completion of first-line platinum-based chemotherapy to first recurrence or progression. The platinum-free interval was categorized as platinum-sensitive (≥ 6 months), platinum-resistant (3–6 months), or platinum-refractory (< 3 months).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize clinicopathological characteristics and survival outcomes. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and continuous variables as medians with ranges. Time-to-event outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and log-rank tests were employed to determine survival differences. The Cox proportional-hazards model was applied for univariable and multivariable analyses, with factors significant or clinically relevant included in multivariable Cox regression analysis. Hazard ratios (HRs) are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was defined as P ≤ 0.05, with all tests being two-sided. Analyses were performed using R or IBM SPSS Statistics (v26.0).

Results

Patient characteristics

The baseline characteristics of 129 patients with OCBM from 12 Chinese institutions are shown in Table 1 and Supplemental Table S1. In the cohort, 32 patients (24.8%) reported a family history of cancer and 38 (29.5%) underwent genetic testing, which identified BRCA pathogenic mutations in 15.8% of cases. Maintenance therapy was administered to 37 patients (28.7%), with 18 (14.0%) receiving poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitor (PARPi) therapy.

By the end of the study period, 81 patients (62.8%) had died. The median interval from primary OC diagnosis to BM development was 25.74 (range: 0–103.1) months, and the median survival time from BM diagnosis to death was 19.53 (range: 0.39–179.3) months. Single BM lesions occurred in 56 patients (43.4%), whereas 71 (55.0%) presented with multiple lesions. The median largest diameter of BM was 2.7 (range: 0.40–5.30) cm. With respect to the main locations of BM, the cerebellum and brainstem were the most common sites (36 patients, 27.9%), followed by diffuse intracranial lesions (31 patients, 24.0%) and the frontal lobe (27 patients, 20.9%). Concurrent peritoneal or intra-abdominal disease was detected in 35 patients, with the liver being the most common extracranial metastatic site. Headache attributed to BM was the most frequent BM-related symptom, reported in 49.6% of cases. Paralysis occurred in 17.8% of patients, and 5.4% were asymptomatic at BM diagnosis (Table 1).

Survival analysis

BMs were present at the time of primary OC diagnosis in 6 patients (4.7%); they were detected at first relapse in 74 patients (57.4%), at second relapse in 25 patients (19.4%), and at third or later relapse in 24 patients (18.6%) (Table 1).

Multivariable Cox regression analysis for OS revealed serum CA125 levels exceeding 100 U/mL at BM diagnosis (HR = 1.785; 95% CI: 1.038–3.069; P = 0.036), multiple BM lesions (HR = 2.950; 95% CI: 1.631–5.336; P < 0.001), and headache attributed to BM (HR = 3.118; 95% CI: 1.631–5.949; P = 0.001) as independent predictors of poor prognosis. Conversely, BM diagnosed during second-line or later relapse was associated with better OS compared with diagnosis at baseline or first-line relapse (HR = 0.400; 95% CI: 0.225–0.714; P = 0.002; Fig. 1A). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest multicenter study on OCBM, including 129 patients with single or multiple BM lesions across 12 Chinese institutions. The relatively large sample size and number of events enabled a comprehensive evaluation of clinical features and prognostic factors affecting survival outcomes.

Advanced FIGO stage has been linked to a higher risk of BM. One study found that patients with stage III or IV disease develop BM five times faster than those with stage I or II disease [13]. This trend was also observed in our cohort, where only 10.8% of patients were diagnosed at an early stage (I–II). However, we did not find a significant association between FIGO stage and the time of BM development. The median interval from initial OC diagnosis to BM occurrence was 25.7 (range: 0–103.1) months, slightly shorter than the 28.8 (range: 2.6–133.1) months reported in a German cohort in 2010 [14]. A plausible explanation for this delay is that routine brain imaging is often omitted during metastatic work-up, particularly in asymptomatic patients. Consequently, brain lesions may remain undetected until late in the disease course, underscoring the need for more intensive surveillance and earlier intervention in high-risk patients [15].

At BM diagnosis, the median serum CA125 level was 82.15 U/mL. Lower CA125 levels were associated with prolonged survival, consistent with the finding of Divine et al. [16]. Headache attributed to BM was another independent predictor of poor prognosis (HR = 3.118). Although prior studies have reported improved survival among BRCA-mutated patients after BM diagnosis [17, 18], the small number of patients tested for BRCA in our cohort restricted definitive conclusions.

The overall prognosis for OCBM remains poor, with prior studies reporting median survival ranging from 1 to 9 months. In our cohort, the median interval to BM was 25.74 months, notably longer than earlier reports and possibly reflecting advances in OC therapy, especially the PARPi maintenance therapy. The number of brain lesions was a significant prognostic factor. Patients with multiple lesions exhibited worse outcomes (HR = 2.950), consistent with the findings of LeRoux et al. [13]., who also observed a negative survival impact in cases of multiple BM lesions. Kaminsky-Forrett et al. [15]. found that 54% of patients with OCBM had multiple lesions, aligning with our findings (55%), although they did not report a significant association with OS. Extraperitoneal metastasis were present in 51.9% of patients at BM diagnosis. Although not significantly associated with OS in univariate analysis, bone metastases were negatively associated with BMSS (P = 0.039).

More than half of our patients were diagnosed with BM at baseline or during first-line relapse, both of which were associated with worse survival outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of early OCBM detection to improve prognosis. Advances in neuroimaging, particularly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT), have improved BM detection [19]. In our cohort, MRI and PET-CT identified BM in 63.6% and 17.1% of cases, respectively. The high frequency of asymptomatic BM emphasizes the need to better understand clinical risk factors and to establish proactive, evidence-based screening and treatment strategies. Despite the rising incidence of BM in recurrent OC, routine brain imaging is not currently standard practice. Instead, imaging is usually performed only after central nervous system (CNS) symptoms appear, often delaying diagnosis until advanced stages when treatment options are limited.

Treatment options for BM include craniotomy, radiotherapy, and systemic therapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted agents. Craniotomy followed by radiotherapy has been shown to extend survival, particularly in patients with lesions larger than 2 cm, by approximately 5–12 months [20–23]. For resectable BM, SRS offers notable advantages, lowering one-year local recurrence rates from 57% to 28% in patients with one to three resected metastases [24]. SRS is also associated with a lower risk of cognitive impairment compared with WBRT [25]. Although WBRT has long been a primary treatment in BM management, it confers no survival benefit and is frequently linked to cognitive decline, especially in patients with multiple BM lesions who are ineligible for craniotomy or SRS [26–28]. The reduced efficacy of SRS in patients with multiple BMs likely reflects challenges in treating larger or more numerous lesions, higher radiation-induced toxicity, and failure to control systemic disease progress [29]. Several randomized trials have shown that in patients with one to four BMs and good performance status (ECOG 0–2 or KPS ≥ 70), SRS provides superior intracranial control compared with WBRT, especially when WBRT is administered without hippocampal avoidance or memantine. However, neither modality improves OS. Combining WBRT with SRS increases cognitive deterioration risk without improving survival outcomes, making SRS alone the preferred option for lesions smaller than 4 cm. For patients with one to two resected metastases, cavity-directed postoperative SRS is recommended when technically feasible. SRS efficacy declines with increasing lesion count due to technical and toxicity limits. These findings, consistent across cancer types, support SRS as the preferred therapy for patients with limited BMs, particularly when preservation of cognitive function is critical [5, 29]. In our study, patients with single BM lesions achieved superior local control with SRS compared with craniotomy, WBRT, or chemotherapy. Among patients with multiple lesions, SRS showed a trend toward improved survival relative to WBRT and craniotomy, although without statistical significance. In such cases, alternative approaches, including WBRT, systemic therapy, or multimodal regimens, may be required to optimize outcomes. Larger retrospective or prospective studies are needed to clarify whether SRS improves prognosis in patients with multiple BM lesions.

The long-term effects of newer CNS-active systemic agents on function and quality of life, as monotherapies or combined with radiotherapy or craniotomy, remain largely unexplored. Conventional chemotherapy often fails to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB). However, innovative strategies such as nanoparticle-based delivery, focused ultrasound with microbubbles, and viral vectors are being developed to overcome this challenge [30, 31]. Radiotherapy, including SRS and WBRT, may also act as an immunological adjuvant by promoting the release of tumor antigens from necrotic cells, thereby enhancing immune responses when combined with checkpoint inhibitors [32, 33]. This has led to studies on the abscopal effect, in which SRS may trigger immune responses against nonirradiated tumors. The synergistic potential of radiotherapy and chemotherapy remains a key research area, with over 200 clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. In glioma models, microbeam radiotherapy increases tumor vascular permeability [34], while in glioblastoma models, low-dose radiotherapy enhances the uptake of lipid nanoparticles in brain tumors, slowing tumor progression [35, 36]. Radiotherapy has also been linked to greater cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) penetration of certain anticancer drugs [37], facilitated by acute and chronic BBB changes. Acute effects, occurring within 24 h of cranial radiotherapy, include endothelial cell death and heightened neuroinflammation, which may be mitigated by anti-TNF-α agents [38]. Chronic effects, such as reduced microvascular cell density and impaired tight-junction protein expression, indicate lasting changes in BBB permeability [39]. Elevated CSF levels of chemotherapy agents following radiotherapy in patients with BM further support these findings [40].

The present study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and reliance on incomplete or inconsistently documented medical records may introduce selection and reporting bias as well as unmeasured confounding. Second, survival analysis mainly focused on advanced-stage patients, as few early-stage cases were available. This may bias survival estimates by excluding individuals with early mortality or rapid progression. Third, data on targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors were not collected as these agents were not approved for OC in China during most of the study period. Future studies should include larger early-stage cohorts and integrate newer therapeutic modalities to improve generalizability and overcome these limitations.

Conclusions

This study identified several prognostic risk factors for OCBM, notably headache attributed to BM and the number of relapse lines before BM diagnosis, both significantly associated with OS and BMSS in advanced cases. Our survival analysis suggests that treatment for OCBM should account for the number of intracranial lesions. For patients with a single lesion, SRS should be prioritized, whereas for patients with multiple lesions, treatment selection, potentially including radiotherapy or chemotherapy, should be tailored to the patient’s overall clinical status.
