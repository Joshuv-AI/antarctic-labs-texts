Patient Journey After Family Screening for a Rare Genetic Arrhythmia

History of Presentation

An 11-year-old girl was referred to a tertiary cardiology hospital for family screening after the identification of a pathogenic variant in the KCNJ2 gene (sequence reference NM_000891.3, chromosomal coordinate chr17-70175938G>T [on GRCh38], c.DNA variant c.899G>T, protein variant p.Gly300Val) in her father, who had a history of syncope since childhood and ventricular arrhythmia of previously undetermined etiology, with good clinical control under beta-blocker use.

After her father's test results became available, the patient was called in for genetic testing. She was asymptomatic from a cardiovascular point of view, denying syncope, palpitations, chest pain, dyspnea, or edema. On initial physical examination, she had an elevated heart rate, without other relevant hemodynamic changes.

Past Medical History

The patient had no known heart disease. Her mother reported an isolated episode of generalized and afebrile tonic-clonic seizures at 14 months of age, in addition to mild learning difficulties throughout her school years. When asked about symptoms during exertion, she reported recurrent episodes of weakness in the lower limbs and myalgia after intense physical activity, sometimes with interruption of gait, suggestive of periodic paralysis.

On physical examination, relevant phenotypic features were observed, such as clinodactyly in the fourth and fifth fingers of both hands and in the fourth and fifth toes of both feet (Figures 1A and 1B, blue arrows) and partial syndactyly of the third and fourth toes of both feet (Figure 1B, red arrows). There was no previous medication use or exposure to digitalis, potassium levels (4.2 mmol/L) were within the normal range, and the thyroid-stimulating hormone level (1.08 μIU/mL) was normal.

(A) Clinodactyly (blue arrows) and (B) partial syndactyly (red arrows).

The electrocardiogram (ECG) performed on admission demonstrated regular tachycardia with a wide QRS complex with beat-to-beat alternation of the electrical axis in the frontal plane, more evident in the inferior leads (II, III, and aVF), alternating positive and negative polarity of the QRS (Figure 2). This pattern is characteristic of bidirectional ventricular tachycardia, a rare ECG finding strongly associated with hereditary channelopathies, particularly Andersen-Tawil syndrome, and should always motivate targeted clinical and genetic investigation.1

Bidirectional Ventricular Tachycardia on 12-Lead Electrocardiogram

Differential Diagnosis

The presence of bidirectional ventricular tachycardia on the ECG directed the differential diagnosis to a restricted group of specific clinical conditions. The main hypotheses considered catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, typically triggered by adrenergic stress; digitalis poisoning, ruled out given lack of exposure; acute myocardial ischemia, rarely associated with this ECG pattern; and Andersen-Tawil syndrome, a hereditary channelopathy characterized by ventricular ectopia, bidirectional ventricular tachycardia, periodic paralysis, and skeletal dysmorphisms.1, 2, 3

Investigations

The initial 12-lead ECG showed regular tachycardia with a wide QRS complex with beat-to-beat alternation of the electrical axis in the frontal plane, more evident in the inferior leads, a characteristic pattern of bidirectional ventricular tachycardia. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed incipient dilation of the left ventricle, without significant systolic dysfunction. Ambulatory 24-hour Holter monitoring demonstrated a high burden of ventricular ectopy, reinforcing the risk of progression to arrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy.1,3

Genetic testing confirmed the pathogenic variant previously identified in the patient's father, establishing the definitive molecular diagnosis and confirming the phenotype-based clinical suspicion.

Management (Medical/Interventions)

Treatment with a beta-blocker (metoprolol succinate 50 mg/d) was started, with clinical intolerance related to excessive drowsiness. It was replaced with progressive doses of propranolol reaching up to 160 mg/d, with adverse effects persisting, requiring a dose-progressive reduction to 80 mg/d, with good tolerance. Given the high arrhythmic burden, and in the absence of flecainide availability, propafenone was initiated at a dose of 300 mg/d, with good clinical tolerability. Serial Holter monitoring demonstrated a progressive reduction in ventricular arrhythmia burden from 59% to 43% with low-dose beta-blocker and, subsequently, to 39% after the initiation of propafenone, with complete suppression of bidirectional ventricular tachycardia.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After therapeutic adjustment, the patient remained clinically stable and asymptomatic. The follow-up ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm with isolated ventricular extrasystoles (Figure 3). With the reduction of the arrhythmic load, it was possible to calculate the QT interval which, corrected by Bazett's formula, measured 438 ms, an interval considered normal. Echocardiology performed 6 months after the introduction of combined antiarrhythmic therapy showed a reduction in the end-diastolic diameter of the indexed left ventricle, suggesting reverse ventricular remodeling after reducing the arrhythmic load.

Reducing the arrhythmic load allowed treadmill testing, which demonstrated complete suppression of ventricular arrhythmia on both resting ECG (Figure 3) and ECG during exercise and recovery. These findings supported Andersen-Tawil syndrome–related bidirectional ventricular tachycardia and argued against catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, in which exertion typically increases arrhythmic activity, thereby supporting safe exercise prescription.4,5

Discussion

Andersen-Tawil syndrome is a rare autosomal dominant channelopathy, most commonly caused by loss-of-function variants in the KCNJ2 gene, which encodes the inward rectifying potassium channel Kir2.1. Dysfunction of the IK1 current impairs the stabilization of the resting membrane potential and terminal repolarization, predisposing affected individuals to ventricular ectopy and characteristic arrhythmic patterns. Given its hereditary nature, recognition of the clinical and ECG phenotype is essential, as family screening and cascade genetic testing play a crucial role in identifying asymptomatic carriers and enabling early diagnosis and treatment.1, 2, 3

Bidirectional ventricular tachycardia represents a striking ECG manifestation of this syndrome and constitutes a diagnostic clue of high value, although it is not pathognomonic. Its recognition on ECG, especially in asymptomatic pediatric patients identified by family screening, is essential to direct genetic investigation, early management, and prevention of arrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy and sudden death, as well as the safe prescription of exercise.3, 4, 5

Class Ic antiarrhythmic drugs, such as flecainide and propafenone, act primarily through potent blockade of fast sodium channels, reducing conduction velocity and suppressing ventricular ectopic activity. Flecainide has been mostly studied in hereditary arrhythmia syndromes, particularly catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia and Andersen-Tawil syndrome, where it has been shown to reduce arrhythmic burden by inhibiting sodium channels.4,6,7

In contrast, propafenone has a similar sodium-channel blocking effect but also exhibits mild beta-adrenergic blocking properties, which may contribute to additional antiarrhythmic effects, as well as the beta-blocker class medication used initially and later in association with propafenone. Although flecainide is generally considered the preferred class Ic agent in hereditary arrhythmia syndromes, according to current guidelines, propafenone may represent a reasonable therapeutic alternative in situations where flecainide is not available, particularly in patients without structural heart disease and with a high burden of ventricular ectopy.4,7,8

In the present case, the patient showed a favorable clinical response after the introduction of propafenone, with a significant reduction in ventricular arrhythmic load and complete suppression of bidirectional ventricular tachycardia during the treadmill stress test. Effective arrhythmia control was also associated with reverse ventricular remodeling, suggesting recovery from tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy. These findings indicate that propafenone may contribute to arrhythmia control in selected patients with Andersen-Tawil syndrome, particularly in contexts where other class Ic agents, such as flecainide, are not available. Although current evidence supporting the use of propafenone in this condition is still limited, the favorable response observed in our patient raises the possibility that sodium-channel blockade with propafenone may reduce ventricular ectopy and improve electrical stability.

Conclusions

This case highlights the central role of ECG and cascade genetic testing in the diagnosis of rare inherited cardiovascular diseases, emphasizing the importance of recognizing bidirectional ventricular tachycardia as a key ECG marker of Andersen-Tawil syndrome. From a therapeutic perspective, the combination of propafenone and a beta-blocker was associated with a significant reduction in arrhythmic burden and reversal of tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy in a setting where flecainide, the usual first-line therapy, was not available.
