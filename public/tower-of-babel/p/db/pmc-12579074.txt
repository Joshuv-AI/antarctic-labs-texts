Targeting IL-6 Receptor Signaling with Satralizumab in Thyroid Eye Disease: Design of the Phase 3 SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 Trials
Authors: Daniel Ezra, Atif Collins, Zdenka Haskova, Thomas Kuenzel, Hiroaki Ida, Miriam Triyatni, Christopher Brittain, Oluwatobi Idowu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThyroid eye disease (TED) is a rare, autoimmune, orbital inflammatory disorder that is disfiguring, debilitating, and potentially sight-threatening. There is an unmet need for a fast-acting, durable, systemic disease-modifying therapy in active TED, for which current options are associated with relapses and side effects, and for chronic inactive TED, which is largely managed with surgery. Satralizumab is a recombinant humanized anti–interleukin-6 receptor (IL-6R) monoclonal antibody that prevents IL-6 from binding to its receptor, thereby reducing proinflammatory and profibrotic signaling. The SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 trials evaluated the efficacy, safety, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics of satralizumab in participants with active, moderate-to-severe, and chronic inactive TED.MethodsSatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 were two identically designed, 72-week, double-masked, placebo-controlled, multicenter, two-stage randomization, phase 3 trials in adults with active, moderate‑to‑severe TED or chronic inactive TED. Participants were randomized 1:1 to receive satralizumab or matching placebo. Based on the proptosis response assessed at week 24, nonresponders received satralizumab every 4 weeks (Q4W), while responders were rerandomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive satralizumab or placebo Q4W through week 44. The primary end point was the proportion of participants with active, moderate-to-severe TED who achieved ≥ 2-mm reduction in proptosis in the study eye from baseline at week 24.ResultsSatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 enrolled 131 and 127 participants, respectively.ConclusionsThe SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 trials investigated IL-6R inhibition via satralizumab in TED. Satralizumab offers a potential disease-modifying treatment option for TED while minimizing safety risks associated with current treatments.Trial registration: SatraGO-1 (NCT05987423) and SatraGO-2 (NCT06106828).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-025-01255-3.

Introduction

Thyroid eye disease (TED) is a rare, autoimmune, orbital inflammatory disorder that can be disfiguring, debilitating, and potentially sight-threatening [1–3]. The clinical presentation of TED is highly variable and ranges from mild disease that may stabilize without intervention, to severe, sight-threatening cases affecting 3–7% of patients [4, 5]. The disease is characterized by enlargement of the extraocular muscles and increased orbital fat and connective tissue volume. Moderate-to-severe TED typically presents with eyelid retraction, proptosis, soft tissue swelling, and/or diplopia [6], which can significantly impair a patient’s quality of life. Approximately 90% of individuals with TED exhibit biochemical evidence of hyperthyroidism, most commonly due to Graves’ disease. However, TED can also occur in individuals with hypothyroidism or euthyroidism [2, 3]. Recent analysis of the United States (US) Intelligent Research in Sight (IRIS®) registry estimated TED prevalence at 0.09% [7], with a three-fold higher prevalence in female patients compared with male patients, consistent with other real-world evidence [7, 8].

TED is hypothesized to be mediated by activation of orbital fibroblasts through autoantibodies targeting thyroid antigens and insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor [1]. This process leads to infiltration of orbital tissues by mononuclear cells, including CD8+ T lymphocytes, B cells, and macrophages, which results in increased levels of inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-6, IL-2, interferon-gamma, and tumor necrosis factor. The disease course typically consists of a progressive (active) inflammatory phase, characterized by rapidly worsening symptoms and signs, including the emergence of fibrosis, followed by a stable (inactive) postinflammatory phase that does not fully return to the premorbid state [2].

Management of TED is guided by disease activity, severity, clinical presentation, duration, and its impact on patients’ quality of life. Current treatment options for active, moderate-to-severe TED include intravenous pulses of corticosteroids or teprotumumab, a monoclonal antibody (mAb) that inhibits the insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor; however, both are associated with significant disease relapse rates and serious side effects [9–11]. Specifically, corticosteroids and teprotumumab are associated with relapse rates of 10–20% and 37%, respectively [12, 13]. Furthermore, postmarketing safety data from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Adverse Events Reporting System identified ear and labyrinth disorders (including deafness), female reproductive system and breast disorders, and metabolic disorders (including hyperglycemia and type 2 diabetes) as the most common adverse events (AEs) reported with the use of teprotumumab [14]. Chronic inactive TED is primarily managed with reconstructive surgery. Although teprotumumab is also approved for long-standing TED in patients with low Clinical Activity Score (CAS) in the USA, its efficacy has not been consistent, and its availability outside the USA is limited [9, 10].

There is an unmet need for a fast-acting, durable, systemic disease-modifying therapy that targets key pathways in TED pathogenesis while minimizing AEs. Molecular and clinical evidence suggest that IL-6 and its receptor (IL-6R) play a key role in the pathogenesis of TED, including orbital fibrosis, which is a characteristic feature of chronic disease [15, 16]. Elevated IL-6 expression correlates with orbital tissue expansion and drives fibrosis development [17, 18]. Clinical studies show that IL-6R inhibition significantly reduces disease activity (as measured by CAS) and improves proptosis in corticosteroid-resistant TED compared with placebo [19]. These findings highlight the potential of IL-6R inhibition as a targeted therapeutic approach for TED.

Satralizumab is a recombinant humanized anti–IL-6R mAb that prevents IL-6 from binding to its receptor, thereby reducing proinflammatory and profibrotic signaling. Satralizumab was engineered using recycling antibody technology to extend plasma half-life, enabling prolonged therapeutic antibody circulation compared with conventional antibody therapies [20]. Satralizumab has been approved for the treatment of neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) in multiple countries worldwide, including the USA, since 2020, and has proven efficacy and an established safety profile [21].

SatraGO-1 (NCT05987423) and SatraGO-2 (NCT06106828) were two identically designed phase 3 trials that evaluated the efficacy, safety, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics of satralizumab in participants with active, moderate-to-severe, and chronic inactive TED. Herein, we describe the study design for SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2.

Methods

Trial Overview

SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 were two identically designed 72-week, double-masked, placebo-controlled, multicenter, two-stage randomization trials investigating satralizumab in participants with TED (Fig. 1). These global trials were carried out across approximately 140 study sites in 19 countries. SatraGO-1 enrolled participants from Argentina, Australia, Austria, Germany, Hong Kong, Hungary, Italy, Japan, Singapore, and the USA, while SatraGO-2 recruited participants from Canada, China, France, Israel, Poland, Portugal, South Korea, Spain, the UK, and the USA. Input from health authorities, academic experts, and patient advocacy groups was obtained on the design of the studies. The trials adhered to the ethical guidelines set out in the Declaration of Helsinki and by the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences and complied with the International Council for Harmonisation E6 guideline for Good Clinical Practice and applicable laws and regulations. Institutional review board (IRB) approval was received for this study protocol, please see the supplementary material for a full list. Written informed consent was obtained before participants enrolled in these trials. Participants and Eligibility Criteria

A total of 258 eligible participants (SatraGO-1: 131; SatraGO-2: 127) aged ≥ 18 years (or ≥ 19 years in Korea) were enrolled. In each study, approximately 75% of the participants had active, moderate‑to‑severe TED, while 25% of the participants had chronic inactive TED. Key ocular inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Table 1. One eye was designated as the study eye at baseline (day 1). If both eyes met eligibility criteria, the eye exhibiting greater severity was selected. A TED diagnosis for study entry required proptosis ≥ 3 mm above normal for participant’s race and sex [22], and at least one of eyelid retraction ≥ 2 mm, moderate or severe soft tissue involvement (swelling/redness of the eyes as defined in the European Group on Graves’ Orbitopathy [EUGOGO] guidelines [22]), and/or inconstant or constant diplopia. For active, moderate‑to‑severe TED, participants with CAS ≥ 3 (on the 7‑item scale) during screening and at day 1, and onset of active symptoms ≤ 12 months before day 1 in the study eye were included. For chronic inactive TED, participants with CAS < 3 (on the 7‑item scale) in both eyes for ≥ 6 months before day 1, and initial diagnosis > 12 months but < 10 years before screening were included. Furthermore, all participants were required to be euthyroid, with the underlying disease under control, or have mild hypo- or hyperthyroidism (defined as free triiodothyronine and free thyroxine < 50% Participants were excluded from trial entry if they experienced a decrease in CAS of ≥ 2, or a decrease in proptosis of ≥ 2 mm in the study eye between screening and baseline. Participants with active TED were ineligible if they had any prior corticosteroid use within 1 month of screening (< 7 days for corticosteroid eye drops), any previous use of systemic, nonbiologic, nonsteroidal immunosuppressive agents for the treatment of TED, or any previous treatment with systemically administered immunomodulatory biological agents. In addition, participants with inactive TED were ineligible if they had used any corticosteroids within 3 weeks (< 28 days for corticosteroid eyedrops) of screening, or systemic, nonbiologic, nonsteroidal immunosuppressive agents for TED within 3 months of screening, or any systemically administered immunomodulatory biological agent for TED within 6 months of screening. These differing washout periods take into consideration any potential confounding effects based on each agent’s pharmacological properties. For example, there is a shorter washout period for corticosteroids owing to their relatively short systemic half-life and reversible immunosuppressive effects; the longer or indefinite washout periods for nonbiologic, nonsteroidal immunosuppressive agents and immunomodulatory biological agents reflect the longer systemic half-life and unpredictable immunomodulatory effects of these agents.

Treatment Protocol

The trials included two placebo-controlled treatment periods: part I and part II. At baseline, participants were randomized (1:1) to receive either satralizumab or matching placebo by subcutaneous injection. Randomization was stratified by current smoking status (current smoker versus former smoker versus never smoked) and TED disease activity status (active versus inactive) to minimize imbalances of these potentially confounding variables across the study arms. During part I, participants received satralizumab loading doses (administered to achieve the desired therapeutic concentration in a short timeframe before initiating maintenance dosing regimen) according to body weight or placebo on day 1, week 2, and week 4, followed by maintenance doses every 4 weeks (Q4W) up to week 20. The Q4W maintenance dosing regimen was selected based on a previous pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic study that demonstrated satralizumab provides significant inhibition of IL-6R signaling for 4 weeks [23].

During part II, participants received treatment assignments on the basis of proptosis response at week 24. Proptosis responders (defined as ≥ 2-mm improvement from baseline, and no corresponding deterioration [≥ 2-mm increase] in the fellow eye) at week 24 were rerandomized (1:1), according to the original randomization stratification factors measured at baseline (current smoking status and TED disease activity status) to another cycle of satralizumab or placebo Q4W through week 44. The rationale for this approach was to determine whether satralizumab treatment is beneficial after improvement (regardless of initial group assignment). Proptosis nonresponders at week 24 (who received satralizumab or placebo during part I) received satralizumab Q4W through week 44 to understand the magnitude of treatment effect in nonresponding participants where there may have been a 6-month delay in initiating satralizumab. Participants who received placebo during part I and commenced satralizumab in part II received a loading dose of satralizumab at week 26. Participants who received satralizumab in part I and continued to receive satralizumab in part II did not receive a loading dose and instead received placebo at week 26. During part II, participants and trial personnel remained masked to the treatment received during part I. The final in-person visit of the study was at week 48. During the 6-month follow-up period (weeks 48–72), AEs, concomitant treatments, and any treatments or surgery received for TED were monitored via a telephone call every 8 weeks.

All participants were allowed to receive rescue intervention per investigator’s discretion from week 4 through to the end of the trials on the basis of protocol-defined criteria, including diagnosis of sight-threatening disease for which prompt intervention is indicated. The choice of rescue intervention was at the discretion of the investigators according to their best medical judgment and included systemic, local, or topical pharmacological approaches, radiotherapy, and/or surgical management. Participants receiving rescue intervention discontinued the study drug but were encouraged to continue study participation and complete all remaining study visit assessments.

Clinical Assessments

Proptosis assessments were completed in the study eye first, followed by the fellow eye. To ensure consistency of measurement, all proptosis assessments were completed by the same observer for each participant at each site using a sponsor-provided Hertel exophthalmometer. Proptosis was measured during screening and at baseline, and then at 4-week intervals until week 48.

Standard ocular assessments were completed for both eyes. Assessments consisted of eyelid measurements, including margin reflex distance (1 and 2), vertical interpalpebral fissure distance and lagophthalmos, pupillary examination, slit-lamp examination, visual acuity, intraocular pressure, CAS, corneal staining (using the Oxford Grading Chart), diplopia, motility restriction, clinical measures of severity (using the EUGOGO guidelines [24]), and indirect ophthalmoscopy.

Diplopia grade was assessed using the Gorman subjective diplopia score, which assigns scores on the basis of severity: no diplopia (absent, scored 0), diplopia in the primary position of gaze when the participant is tired or awakening (intermittent, scored as 1), diplopia at extremes of gaze (inconstant, scored as 2), and continuous diplopia in the primary or reading position (constant, scored as 3) [25].

Motility restriction was assessed by estimating the degrees of restriction in eye movements, with monocular excursions in horizontal and vertical directions of gaze recorded using the light reflex test, which has demonstrated reliability versus other clinical techniques [26].

The 7-item EUGOGO-amended CAS was used to assess disease activity and pain (Table 3). For consistency, the same observer completed each CAS evaluation for the duration of the trials. Patient-Reported Outcome Assessments

Health-related quality of life was assessed using the Graves’ Ophthalmology Quality of Life (GO-QoL) questionnaire at baseline and at weeks 12, 24, 36, and 48. The GO-QoL is a TED-specific, 16-item, self-administered questionnaire designed to assess the participants’ perceived effects of TED on visual functioning and appearance [27]. Corneal and ocular surface symptoms were assessed using the Ocular Surface Disease Index [28] at baseline and at weeks 12, 24, 36, and 48.

Safety Assessments

Safety outcomes included the incidence, seriousness, and severity of AEs. All AEs that occurred from the start of treatment through to the last visit (week 72) were recorded. The trial investigator recorded all AEs in the electronic case report form. An independent data monitoring committee oversaw both trials and monitored participant safety on an ongoing basis. Safety was assessed through summaries of exposure to treatment, AEs, changes in laboratory test results, and changes in vital signs and electrocardiograms. All AEs were mapped according to Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities terms, while AE severity was graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Participants were closely monitored for the development of signs and symptoms of infection, including tuberculosis and hepatitis B. Participants were also monitored for elevated transaminases, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia throughout the study.

Pharmacokinetic and Pharmacodynamic Assessments

Serum samples were collected for measurement of satralizumab pharmacokinetic parameters and for determination of pharmacodynamic biomarkers, including IL-6 and soluble IL-6R. In addition, serum samples were screened for antibodies to satralizumab, and the titer of confirmed positive samples will be reported.

Statistical Approaches

The trials aimed to enroll a total sample size of approximately 60 participants per arm for each study. This provided > 90% power to demonstrate the superiority of satralizumab versus placebo for the percentage of participants with active TED who had a reduction in proptosis of ≥ 2 mm at week 24 in the full analysis and under the following assumptions: detect a 40 percentage-point difference between the satralizumab arm and the placebo arm in the percentage of participants who had a reduction in proptosis of ≥ 2 mm at week 24, using Fisher’s exact test for two proportions, 5% two-sided type I error rate, and 10% dropout rate. The primary analysis was performed when all participants completed the week 24 assessment in part I. The primary end point in part I was evaluated as the difference in the proportions of proptosis responders between the satralizumab and placebo arms at week 24 in participants with active TED, estimated as a weighted mean of stratum specific risk differences using Mantel–Haenszel stratum weights. Smoking status was used as the only stratum. Missing proptosis values in the study eye and the fellow eye, respectively, at all follow-up visits until week 24 were multiply imputed before the derivation of the proptosis response through sequential (monotone) imputation. Early discontinuations of study treatment owing to AEs or lack of efficacy, use of any prohibited systemic treatment or prohibited therapy, administration of rescue treatment, or orbital or ocular surgery for TED were handled as intercurrent events.

A fixed‑sequencing test approach was used to control the overall type I error rate for the hypothesis testing of the primary and key secondary end points in part I. Hypothesis testing in part II will be completed only if both trials are positive with respect to the primary end point in part II. Another fixed sequencing test approach on the pooled study population will be used for all key secondary end points in part II. The alpha control approach by Bretz and Xi will be used to control the overall type I error in parts I and II (submission wise error rate) [29, 30].

Trial Status

SatraGO-1 started recruitment on 26 October 2023, while SatraGO-2 started recruitment on 15 November 2023. The anticipated trial completion dates are June 2026 for SatraGO-1 and May 2026 for SatraGO-2.

Discussion

Satralizumab is a novel disease-modifying agent that inhibits IL-6 signaling pathways and is approved for the treatment of NMOSD, and under investigation for the treatment of TED. SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 are two global, simultaneous, identically designed, double-masked, placebo-controlled, two-stage randomization trials designed to investigate satralizumab in participants with TED. The trial findings will provide information on the efficacy, safety, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics of satralizumab in active, moderate-to-severe, and chronic inactive TED.

Globally, systemic corticosteroids are used as an off-label treatment option in TED, and remain the mainstay of management for active moderate-to-severe TED because of their rapid antiinflammatory and immunosuppressive effects in some patients at high doses [24, 31]. However, the treatment response to corticosteroids is not always consistent, and a proportion of patients experience disease progression or do not respond to corticosteroid therapy at all, requiring one or more surgical interventions [24]. Teprotumumab is an approved treatment option for TED in the USA and a few other countries [32], but its use is limited by high relapse rates [10, 33, 34], a significant safety profile (including hearing impairment [10%], hyperglycemia [10%], menstrual disorders [23%], risk of fetal harm, and exacerbation of inflammatory bowel disease) [11, 14, 35, 36], and the burden of care coordination with intravenous infusion centers. Although surgical intervention remains the mainstay of treating patients with chronic inactive TED, it often represents challenges such as the need for multiple procedures, prolonged recovery, or suboptimal aesthetic and functional outcomes [24]. Given these limitations, there is an unmet need for a disease-modifying agent for the treatment of TED, especially one that works quickly, is durable, and prevents downstream fibrotic sequelae with minimal side effects. For patients with chronic inactive TED, where surgical interventions are often the only recourse but carry the inherent limitations described above, a disease-modifying pharmacological approach with an IL-6 pathway inhibitor could represent a significant breakthrough.

The role of IL-6 in the pathogenesis of TED is well-documented [17, 18, 37], and inhibition of the IL-6 pathway has shown promise in TED. A randomized, double‑masked, placebo‑controlled trial that investigated the efficacy of an anti–IL-6R mAb (tocilizumab, off-label use) in patients with active, moderate-to-severe, corticosteroid-resistant TED demonstrated a reduction in proptosis at week 16 in 93% of patients compared with 47% on placebo [19]. Efficacy was also demonstrated with a change in CAS of ≥ 2 points from baseline at week 16, which was achieved in 93% of patients receiving tocilizumab versus 59% receiving placebo [19]. These compelling results with tocilizumab, an anti–IL-6R mAb, provide strong mechanistic and clinical proof of concept for IL-6 pathway inhibition in TED. Consequently, given its similar mechanism of action as an IL-6 pathway blocker, satralizumab is hypothesized to similarly improve proptosis and CAS signs and symptoms in patients with TED by inhibiting the inflammatory cascade and mitigating adipogenesis and extracellular matrix formation.

The design of these trials allowed for the thorough evaluation of the efficacy, safety, and durability of satralizumab. The selection of week 24 efficacy data as the primary end point of SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 was based on existing satralizumab pharmacokinetics data [23] and previous data for time-to-effect of tocilizumab in TED [19]. In addition, the design of part II of these trials, i.e., the rerandomization of participants with a proptosis response, will assist with the understanding of whether continued treatment (post week 24) provides additional benefits. Specifically, the rerandomization design will enable investigation into whether continued satralizumab treatment offers additional benefits, even in participants who achieved proptosis response on placebo during part I (i.e., “spontaneous placebo responders”). This unique rerandomization aspect of the trial design will help differentiate the effects of satralizumab from natural disease remission or placebo response and will address the need for continued treatment with satralizumab after the initial response has been achieved. Part II of the trials investigates and will provide answers on the maintenance of efficacy of satralizumab and the rate of, and time to, potential TED relapses. Specifically, this study design allows for the time course of potential relapse to be evaluated from week 24 to week 48 for proptosis responders to satralizumab in part I who are rerandomized to receive placebo in part II. The identical design of SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 ensures the reproducibility and robustness of the clinical trial data, and by conducting two adequately powered and controlled studies in parallel, it strengthens the reliability of the findings to provide substantial evidence for efficacy and safety of satralizumab in TED. Based on use in the existing indication of NMOSD, satralizumab has a well-established safety profile. The main risks observed are rash (17%), arthralgia (17%), elevated liver enzymes, and a decrease in neutrophil count [21, 38].

Limitations of SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 included the absence of a comparator drug and controlled enrollment criteria. Notably, the FDA has not established a noninferiority margin for the treatment of TED, which complicates comparison with the only therapy approved in the USA at the time of study initiation. Systemic corticosteroids and teprotumumab were deemed unsuitable as comparators owing to various challenges, such as inconsistent treatment response, potential contraindications, and a lack of global approval. Furthermore, teprotumumab and corticosteroids have different routes of delivery compared with subcutaneous satralizumab. Owing to these limitations and the potential for serious AEs, SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 used a placebo instead of a comparator drug. Furthermore, the SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 trials did not allow for the enrollment of participants with active, moderate-to-severe TED who were previously treated with other immunotherapies/biologics, participants with active TED for longer than 12 months, or for participants with progressive, treatable, low-CAS TED, which is common in Asian populations [39], which may limit the generalizability of the findings from SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 to these populations. Finally, in the SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 trials, only 25% of participants in each study had inactive TED, which may again limit the generalizability of the findings to patients with inactive TED.

Regardless, SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 were designed to generate robust, reliable evidence for the efficacy and safety of satralizumab in TED. Beyond demonstrating efficacy and safety, the findings from SatraGO-1 and SatraGO-2 may redefine the treatment paradigm for TED and could improve long-term patient outcomes and quality of life.
