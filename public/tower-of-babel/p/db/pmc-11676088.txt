Spectrum of Renal Tumors Other Than Renal Cell Carcinoma with Emphasis on Cytomorphological, Immunohistochemical, and Cytogenetic Study
Authors: Soumya Alashetty, Priya Dharmalingam, Sindhu Ramamurthy, Bidadi Lingappa Kavitha, Siddappa Shanthala, Rajasab Subhan Ali
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The role of fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) in the diagnosis of renal malignancies is established and has been getting more precise and important over a period of time. Knowledge of the pathology of uncommon renal neoplasms along with radiological and clinical correlations often aids in correct diagnosis.Aims:The present study aims to describe the cytomorphological and immunohistochemical findings in the varied spectrum of renal tumors, other than renal cell carcinomas (RCC).Materials and Methods:Data of 238 cases of ultrasound-guided renal FNAC performed in our tertiary cancer institute over 4 years were collected from the department registry. All nondiagnostic cases and cases diagnosed as RCC were excluded from the study, so 57 cases of renal tumors were reviewed along with the cell blocks and ancillary studies.Results:Out of the 57 cases, 35 cases were primary renal neoplasms, which included renal oncocytoma (3.5%), angiomyolipoma (3.5%), rhabdoid tumor (1.8%), Wilms tumor (28.0%), Ewing sarcoma (3.5%), urothelial carcinoma (8.8%), and small round cell tumor unclassified (12.3%). Twenty two cases were metastatic tumors, which included hematolymphoid neoplasm (14%) and metastatic carcinomas (24.6%) from various other primary carcinomas.Conclusion:Our study shows that renal FNAC is safe and fairly accurate in diagnosing a wide spectrum of renal tumors and has high diagnostic accuracy, when performed along with cell block and immunohistochemistry. Awareness of the pathology of uncommon renal tumors along with relevant clinical history and radiological findings may aid in identifying the type of tumor for further appropriate management.

INTRODUCTION

Renal fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) when performed in appropriate clinical settings has been an excellent diagnostic tool.[1,2,3] Few established indications are suspected metastasis, hematological neoplasms, patients with renal cell carcinoma (RCC) who are poor surgical candidates, or patients with unresectable tumors or wherein conservative treatment such as ablation is planned and very small lesions wherein differentiating between the benign and reactive process becomes critical by applying radiology as well as in radiologically indeterminate lesions.[2,3,4]

Very few studies are performed in evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of FNAC in renal lesions. To our knowledge, this is the first study that has been performed for evaluating the role and diagnostic accuracy of fine needle aspiration and ancillary techniques in renal lesions other than renal cell RCC. Studies performed for evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of FNAC in renal masses as a whole have found that the major discordance was attributed to inadequate sampling and lack of awareness of rarer and newer entities.[5] Hence, in our study, we emphasize on the spectrum of renal tumors, diagnosed on FNAC, and the importance of ancillary techniques in diagnosing them.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data on all ultrasound (USG)-guided renal FNAC procedures that were done in our institute over 4 years were collected retrospectively from the department registry. All the nondiagnostic cases due to unsatisfactory samples and cases diagnosed as RCC were excluded from the study. As it was a retrospective study and the identity of the patients was not revealed in the study, ethics committee approval was not taken. Clinical and radiological correlations were performed wherever necessary. A histopathological correlation was performed in few cases where available.

Under USG guidance, the FNAC material was obtained using a 21–24 gage needle with multiple passes. For all the cases, both dry and wet fixed smears were prepared. Air-dried smears were stained by May Grunwald Giemsa stain, and alcohol-fixed smears were stained by Papanicolaou stain. Cell blocks was prepared with the remaining material after fixing in formalin using the agar method, and cell blocks were sectioned, and slides were stained by hematoxylin and eosin. IHC analysis was performed on cell blocks using the HRP method using 3,3’ diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride as chromogen. The FNAC material was sent for cytogenetic study in few cases. The cytogenetic study was performed by subjecting FNAC samples to 24–48 h unstimulated culture in RPMI 1640 media with fetal bovine serum supplementation and harvested. Slides were prepared, and the GTG banding procedure was performed as per the standard protocol, and analyses of 15–20 metaphases were done. Results were reported using GenASis software (Applied Spectral Imaging Limited, Yokneam, Israel). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (IBM Corp, New York, USA) for calculating the diagnostic accuracy.

RESULTS

A total of 238 renal FNAC procedures were carried out under USG guidance. Out of 238 cases, 136 cases (57.1%) were found to be RCC and 45 cases (18.9%) were nondiagnostic because of aspiration failure and insufficient material, so these cases were excluded from the study. Hence, our study population included the remaining 57 cases. All these cases were reviewed along with the cell blocks and ancillary studies wherever available. Among the 57 cases, there were 33 (57.9%) males and 24 (42.1%) females. The age distribution was as follows: <10 years (27 cases), 10–19 years (two cases), 20–29 years (four cases), 30–39 years (two cases), 40–49 years (two cases), 50–59 years (7 cases), and >60 years (13 cases), with the majority of the cases being in two extreme age groups.

The distribution of cases is as follows: 35 cases of primary renal tumors consisting of renal oncocytoma (3.5%), angiomyolipoma (3.5%), rhabdoid tumor (1.8%), Wilms tumor (28.0%), Ewing sarcoma (3.5%), urothelial carcinoma (8.8%), and small round cell tumors (SRCT) unclassified (12.3%) among the primary renal tumors. Twenty two cases were metastatic tumors which included hematolymphoid neoplasm (14.0%) and metastatic carcinomas (24.6%) from serous carcinoma ovary, papillary thyroid carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma of lung, and gastrointestinal adenocarcinoma. In our study, the most common renal tumor was Wilms tumor in children and metastatic carcinomas in adults [Table 1].

Metastatic tumors

There were 14 cases of renal metastatic tumors. The age of the patient ranged from 15 to 83 years with eight males and six females. All cases had presented with unilateral renal involvement. Two of 14 cases in addition to renal metastasis had liver metastasis (one case) and lung metastasis (one case). Twelve cases had a previously known history of malignancy, which included neuroendocrine carcinoma lung (one case), squamous cell carcinoma lung (three cases), mucinous adenocarcinoma colon (one case), serous carcinoma ovary (three cases), breast carcinoma (two cases), mesenchymal chondrosarcoma of iliac bone (one case), and papillary carcinoma of thyroid (one case), and hence diagnosis was made by FNAC smears and cell blocks and correlated with histopathological report of their primary tumor. Most of the cases showed renal metastasis/infiltration within 1 year of their diagnosis. In two cases wherein the primary diagnosis was not known, one case was diagnosed as metastatic lung adenocarcinoma with positivity for TTF1, CK, and CK7, and the other was metastatic lung neuroendocrine carcinoma positive for CK, CD56, synaptophysin, chromogranin, and TTF1. In both the cases, renal metastasis was diagnosed by FNAC and IHC study on the cell block, even before the diagnosis of primary carcinoma.

Hematolymphoid neoplasm

Our study included eight cases of hematolymphoid neoplasm, of which five were male and three were female. The minimum age of presentation was 1.5 years, and the maximum age was 88 years. Six cases presented with bilateral and multiple renal lesions, and two cases presented with unilateral multifocal renal lesions. Seven of eight cases of lymphoma including four cases in adults and three cases in children were already a known case of lymphoma with renal secondary infiltration. Of these seven cases, four cases were lymphoblastic lymphoma, two cases were diffuse large B cell lymphoma, and one case of extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of the breast. There was one case of primary renal lymphoma diagnosed in adult patients. FNA smears showed sheets of medium-to-large sized atypical lymphoid cells associated with lymphoglandular bodies. A probable diagnosis of non-Hodgkin lymphoma was made by FNAC, and biopsy was suggested. On HPE, it was diagnosed as diffuse large B cell lymphoma-germinal center subtype.

Urothelial carcinoma

In our study, five cases were diagnosed as urothelial carcinoma. All cases were in the age group of 50–65 years with three males and two females. All the cases had unilateral renal involvement. FNAC smears were cellular composed of small clusters, sheets, papillae, and isolated cells. The cell morphology varied from low-grade tumors with cells having moderate-to-abundant cytoplasm and cells having characteristic elongated cytoplasmic tail Figure 1 to high-grade tumors having clusters of cells with scanty cytoplasm and hyperchromatic nucleus with marked pleomorphism. Out of the five cases, three cases had cell block, and IHC was done in two cases and was positive for CK, CK7, and GATA3. A histopathological correlation was available in other two cases and were found to have synchronous urothelial carcinoma in bladder biopsy.

Oncocytoma

Our study included two cases of oncocytoma, which was diagnosed in 68- and 45-year-old male patients. Both the cases had unilateral renal lesion. One of them was a known case of carcinoma penis on follow-up. Radiological imaging had suggested the diagnosis due to the presence of stellate scar visualized on USG. FNAC smears in both cases showed cohesive tissue fragments with few dispersed cells. Cells were round with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and eccentric placed nucleus, and few scattered bizarre large hyperchromatic cells were observed in one case. Cell block and IHC were performed in both the cases, and tumor cells were positive for CK, PAX8, and CD117 Figure 2 and negative for CK7, AMACR, and carbonic anhydrase. Histopathological correlation of resection specimens in both the cases was consistent with cytological diagnosis.

Angiomyolipoma

Diagnosis favoring angiomyolipoma was made in two cases. Both of them were female patients aged 29 and 50 years. One case presented as a unilateral lesion, and the other case was of multifocal lesions involving a single kidney. A diagnosis of angiomyolipoma was suspected in one case on imaging findings. FNAC smears were less cellular with few cohesive clusters of bland-looking spindle cells with few sheets of adipocytes, so a spindle cell lesion with a possibility of angiomyolipoma was made. Cell block was available in both the cases; however, IHC was possible in only one case. The cells were diffuse positivity for SMA and focally for HMB 45 and were negative for S100, CK, CD34, and desmin. Hence, a diagnosis favoring angiomyolipoma was made, following which resection was performed and was confirmed to be the same in HPE, whereas in the other case with multifocal lesions, a possibility of angiomyolipoma was made in FNAC. IHC was not done due to insufficient material on the cell block and also later, the patient was lost to follow-up.

Rhabdoid tumor

Our study included one case of renal rhabdoid tumor, which was diagnosed in 1-year-old male child who had presented with large unilateral renal lesions. FNAC smears were cellular with clusters and single scattered cells with rhabdoid morphology, and a diagnosis of malignant tumor with round cell morphology was made. Cell block and IHC was done. IHC included a panel of round cell markers. Tumor cells showed CK and EMA patchy positivity, with loss of INI1 in the tumor cells and negative for CD99, desmin, WT1, synaptophysin, FLI1, BCL2, and myogenin. Hence, a diagnosis of malignant rhabdoid tumor was made. Cytogenetic study done from the FNA material showed a malignant clone with complex karyotype with monosomy of 22 chromosomes.

Wilms tumor

A total of 16 cases were diagnosed with Wilms tumor. The average age group was 2–3 years with the minimum and maximum age at diagnosis being 6 months and 12 years, respectively. Of the 16 cases, there were eight females and eight males. All the cases had unilateral renal involvement, of which one case was also found to have agenesis of the other kidney. A diagnosis of Wilms tumor was made with FNAC smears, alone in eight cases. Seven out of eight cases had triphasic morphology composed of blastemal, epithelial, and stromal component, and the other case was having a biphasic pattern with blastemal and stromal components; hence, a possible diagnosis of Wilms tumor was made correlating with clinical and radiological features. In the rest of eight cases, IHC was performed, of which seven cases had blastemal predominant and one case had biphasic morphology. IHC markers positive were PAX8, CK, and WT1, which was positive in epithelial components [Figure 3] and PAX8 and WT1 focally in few cases in the blastemal component. Other markers for SRCT which included CD 99, synaptophysin, desmin, and LCA were all negative. All the cases had HPE correlation in the resection specimen after chemotherapy and were consistent with cytological diagnosis. The cytogenetics study was performed in seven cases, which showed a normal karyotype in four cases and hyper-diploidy was noted in three cases.

Ewings sarcoma

Ewing sarcoma of the kidney was diagnosed in two cases. Both the cases were female patients aged 2 and 27 years, presenting with unilateral renal mass. FNAC smears were cellular with cells arranged in sheets and rosette. Cells were small round with scanty cytoplasm with fine chromatin, and few cells had cytoplasmic vacuolation and was diagnosed as SRCT by FNAC and was confirmed as Ewing sarcoma by IHC on cell block. Both the cases showed diffuse positivity for CD 99 and NKX 2.2, and in one case, CK and synaptophysin were focally positive [Figure 4]. Both the cases underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy following which resection was performed, and the diagnosis was confirmed with good chemotherapy response. Cytogenetic study was available in one case which showed t (11;22) (q24;12) consistent with Ewing sarcoma.

Small round cell tumor unclassified

SRCT unclassified included seven cases in which only diagnosis of SRCT was made on FNAC smears; however, IHC study on cell block was inconclusive. Six cases were in the pediatric age group, with five males and one female, and one case was diagnosed in an adult male. FNAC and cell block in all cases showed clusters and sheets of small round cells with scanty cytoplasm and high nuclear cytoplasmic ratio. This group of SRCT unclassified constituted the major diagnostic difficulty in our study as they were not further classified even after IHC. Our IHC panel for all round cell tumors included CD99, CD 56, CK, LCA, desmin, NKX 2.2, and WT1. In four of the seven cases, a correct cell typing was not possible, and correct diagnosis could not be made. But, on correlating with histopathology, they were found to be of clear cell sarcoma of kidney in two cases, one case each of blastemal predominant Wilms tumor and Ewing sarcoma. One case of clear cell sarcoma kidney, 2 years after surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy, had presented with recurrence in the anterior abdominal wall. FNAC was performed from the abdominal wall mass showed SRCT morphology and was diagnosed as metastatic lesions. In three cases, IHC was not feasible due to scanty tissue on cell block, and cases were lost to follow-up.

DISCUSSION

Renal FNAC procedures are important particularly in lesions other than RCC, because most of the time they are misdiagnosed by radiology. It is of utmost importance to not miss these lesions and correctly diagnose them in order to provide appropriate treatment because many lesions such as lymphomas and metastasis may not require resection. Tumors such as Ewing sarcoma and Wilms tumor may require chemotherapy before resection. Hence, over-treatment by surgery can be avoided by accurate diagnosis by FNAC preoperatively.

In our study, the most common diagnosis in the adults was metastatic carcinomas, which was similar to a study done by Lau et al.,[5] who evaluated 247 cases of renal masses and found that metastatic carcinomas and hematolymphoid neoplasms were the commonest diagnosis after RCC in their study. Even in our study, hematolymphoid neoplasms were next common tumors after metastatic carcinomas in adults. All cases of metastatic carcinoma presented with unilateral renal involvement and had renal metastasis within 1 year of diagnosis of primary malignancy. Metastasis from lung carcinoma was the most common primary malignancy, which included three cases of squamous cell carcinoma, two cases of neuroendocrine carcinoma, and one case of adenocarcinoma, which is comparable to a study performed by Giashuddin et al.,[6] who reported 11 cases of renal metastasis, with lung carcinoma being the most common primary carcinoma, followed by lymphoma. An accurate diagnosis of renal metastasis can be made with cytomorphological and ancillary studies on cell block and also correlation of histopathological slides, in cases with a known history of malignancy.

Hematolymphoid neoplasms accounted for 14% in our study, with seven cases of secondary lymphoma and one case of primary lymphoma. Truong et al.,[7] identified 10 cases of secondary renal lymphoma and eight cases of primary renal lymphoma, which is similar to our study. Six cases in our study had multiple bilateral renal lesions, which was similar to a study by Morel et al.,[8] as they identified multiple nodules as the most common type of renal localization (33%) in a series of 48 renal lymphomas. In a study by Dimopoulos et al.[9] and Gattuso et al.,[10] renal lymphomas accounted for 3% and 29%, respectively.

Renal masses in a patient with systemic lymphoma may represent lesions other than lymphoma, such as infarct, infection, abscess, pyelonephritis, hydronephrosis, or even a RCC.[11] FNAC is the best method to document renal lymphoma unequivocally in this clinical setting and avoid unnecessary radical surgery.

Benign renal tumors in our study included renal oncocytoma and angiomyolipoma. Oncocytoma accounted for 2.5% and was diagnosed in male patients, comparable to that reported in literature.[12] The diagnosis was rendered with the aid of IHC finding as the tumor cells are usually negative for CK7 and positive for CD117 and also with the correlation of cytomorphological and radiological findings. Angiomyolipoma also accounted for 2.5% in our study. They are usually picked up by radiology, except in certain cases, where it is critical to diagnose when the fat content of the lesion is poor.[13,14,15] Even cytological diagnosis is usually not straightforward when adipose tissue is sparse or absent, and rendering diagnosis is often difficult based only on few clusters of spindled or epithelioid cells, and most often these are mistaken as RCC and less often as other sarcomas. IHC study helps confirm the diagnosis.

Urothelial carcinoma accounted for 8.8% in our study. Two cases in our study had synchronous urothelial carcinoma of the kidney and bladder, whereas in the study by Cosentino et al.,[16] the incidence of concomitant urothelial carcinoma of the bladder at the time of diagnosis of primary upper urinary tract urothelial carcinoma was 17%. In a study by Van Doeveren et al.,[17] concomitant urothelial carcinomas were found to be clonally related.

In the present study, Wilms tumor was the most common pediatric renal neoplasm accounting for 28%. All the cases presented with unilateral involvement and had favorable histology. We found that triphasic and blastemal predominant cytomorphology were the most common in our study, which is comparable to a study by Gow and Murphy,[18] wherein triphasic was more common followed by blastemal predominant cytomorphology. In our study, an abnormal karyotype, identified in the three cases, was more likely to reflect gain rather than loss of the chromosomal material, similar to that reported by Slater and Mannens.[19]

Our study included one case of rhabdoid tumor. The classical rhabdoid morphology of cells and immunostaining for CK and loss of INI1 in tumor cells as well as negative staining for other IHC markers of small round cell panel confirmed the diagnosis. Even with the presence of tumor cells with rhabdoid morphology, ancillary studies such as IHC, cytogenetics, and molecular studies are necessary to confirm the diagnosis of rhabdoid tumor, as tumor misclassification has been reported in the studies by Goregaonkar et al. and Thomson et al.,[20,21] and many other tumors with dedifferentiation to the rhabdoid phenotype can mimic rhabdoid tumor.

In our study, Ewings sarcoma accounted for 3.5%, one case was diagnosed in child, and other in young adult. Primary presentation in the kidney represents a rare disease entity, with approximately 100 prior cases in the literature, and predominantly affects young adults.[22] However, Bradford et al.[23] have reported seven pediatric and one adult cases of primary renal Ewing sarcoma. The clinical presentation and imaging characteristics of ES are nonspecific, and moreover confusion with other more common primary SRCT of childhood, such as Wilms tumor or rhabdoid tumor of the kidney, is not infrequent on histological examination. Hence, the diagnosis of Ewings sarcoma is based on combination of IHC, cytogenetic analysis, and FISH study to demonstrate multiple diagnostic translocations, with the most common being (11; 22) (q24; q12) translocation, resulting in the EWSR-FLI-1 fusion gene. In our study, diagnosis was made with SRCT morphology and immunostaining for CD99 and NKX2.2 in all three cases and also in one of the cases t (11;22) (q24; q12) was seen in the cytogenetic study.

The differential diagnoses of primary renal neoplasms in children and young adults are broad, including Wilms tumor, Ewings sarcoma, rhabdoid tumor, and clear cell sarcoma, however when considering small round cell tumor.[24] Small round cell tumor unclassified accounted for seven cases. Due to their undifferentiated or primitive characters, definitive diagnosis was not possible on smears. IHC panel testing for SRCT which included CK, desmin, synaptophysin, CD99, and LCA was done in four cases; however, IHC was inconclusive, and definite diagnosis was not possible. Two cases of clear cell sarcoma kidney were falsely diagnosed as SRCT unclassified as they were not picked up even on FNAC smears, which was similar to a study done by Sharifah NA,[25] in which two cases of clear cell sarcoma of kidney were missed primarily, but both presented with recurrence and were picked later by FNAC. Diagnostic accuracy for FNAC in our study was 88.9% and increased to 93.5% with use of cell blocks. Although cell blocks prepared from FNAC samples provide limited information regarding tumor architecture, they are comparable to biopsy when ancillary studies are done on cell block. FNAC samples also provide adequate genetic material for molecular testing. Veltri et al.[26] reported an accuracy of 76.2% for FNAC and 93.2% for core biopsy with improvement of 22.5% accuracy on cell block. A study done by Scanga and Maygarden[27] supports a greater utility of core biopsy over FNAC in the evaluation of renal masses with indeterminate or suspicious radiology features. A recent study by Barwari et al.[28] directly compared FNAC and core biopsy and found that combined FNAC and core biopsy increased the accuracy by 3.5% to 14%. The majority of literature evaluates the diagnostic accuracy of FNAC and core biopsy for all the renal lesions. To our knowledge, this is the first study also evaluating some uncommon renal tumors. To conclude, renal FNAC when done with cellblock and IHC has high diagnostic accuracy and may be used as adjunct to biopsy. Preoperative diagnosis of these uncommon renal lesions will definitely help the clinician in appropriate management, and overtreatment in the form of radical surgeries can be avoided.
