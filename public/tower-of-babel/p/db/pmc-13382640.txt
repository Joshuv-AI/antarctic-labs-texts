Evaluation of Xanthoceras sorbifolium meal as a novel feed ingredient for lambs: effects on growth performance, meat quality, and flavor
Authors: Yannan Ma, Yongxiao Ma, Guozhao Sheng, Zhixian Liu, Lei Yang, Jiao Luo, Lei Zheng, Chanchan Pan, Xiaoyan Ma, Qianlong Liu, Yingjin Li, Hanqing Feng, Jianping Wu, Fuliang Cao, Ji Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study tested whether Xanthoceras sorbifolium meal (XSM) could replace soybean meal (SBM) in finishing lamb diets at 0%, 50%, 75%, and 100%. Forty male lambs were fed for 112 days. At 50% replacement, growth performance and carcass traits were not affected, but full replacement lowered feed efficiency. Across all XSM groups, press loss decreased and muscle antioxidant capacity improved, with higher catalase, glutathione, glutathione peroxidase, and total antioxidant capacity and lower malondialdehyde. XSM also increased n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) and essential amino acid (EAA) levels in the longissimus thoracis. Gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC–IMS) volatile profiling showed that the 50% group had the most distinctive aroma, with higher levels of 1-octen-3-one, (E)-2-octenal, and benzeneacetaldehyde but lower off-flavor precursors. Correlation analysis linked these volatile shifts mainly to n-6 C20 PUFA. We conclude that 50% XSM substitution best balances production, meat quality, and flavor.

Introduction

Demand for sheep meat is projected to rise over the coming decade (OECD & FAO, 2025). Lamb is valued in part for its nutritional value, as it provides high-quality protein, a favorable balance of mono- and polyunsaturated fatty acids, and micronutrients such as iron, zinc, and B vitamins (Ding et al., 2024; Schmid et al., 2006). Consumer acceptance, however, depends not only on nutritional composition but also on flavor. Flavor is closely linked to the intramuscular lipid fraction. Its fatty acid composition and oxidative stability influence the volatile aldehydes and ketones produced during cooking, while lipid-derived carbonyls can react with free amino acids through Maillard reactions during heating. Because several of these precursors are influenced by dietary lipids, the lipid and bioactive composition of the diet may, in turn, affect the volatile profile of meat. In ruminant production, diet formulation is also constrained by the limited range of protein ingredients available for concentrates. Soybean meal (SBM) remains one of the most widely used protein ingredients, yet much of it is imported, leaving feed costs exposed to exchange-rate movements, supply disruptions, and geopolitical risk (Miranda et al., 2019). Locally available protein sources of good quality could reduce this dependence and may also help maintain or improve meat quality.

Xanthoceras sorbifolium Bunge is one such option that has received relatively little attention in ruminant nutrition. This deciduous tree grows across northern and western China on arid hillsides, alkaline flats, and sandy ground, and therefore does not compete directly with food crops for arable land (Chen, Zhang, Qi, et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2020). Its oil-rich seeds are increasingly cold-pressed, and the resulting defatted meal, Xanthoceras sorbifolium meal (XSM), is produced in growing quantities but remains underutilized. Reported crude protein contents mostly range from 26% to 35%, with occasional values above 37%, and its amino acid profile resembles that of SBM (Liang et al., 2021; Venegas-Calerón et al., 2017). These characteristics suggest that XSM could serve as a candidate protein ingredient for ruminant feed, although direct feeding evidence remains limited. Beyond its basic nutrient value, the fruit and its by-products contain biologically active secondary metabolites, including triterpenoid saponins, flavonoids, and polyphenols. Several of these compounds show antioxidant activity and have been linked to lipid metabolism (Cui et al., 2024; Zang et al., 2021). Their effects in ruminants appear to be dose-dependent. Low to moderate levels may improve fermentation efficiency or reduce methane emissions through effects on the rumen microbial community and redox status (Goel & Makkar, 2012; Ku-Vera et al., 2020). In contrast, higher intakes of saponins and tannins may reduce palatability, feed intake, and digestibility, ultimately impairing growth (Patra & Saxena, 2010). Given this combination of potential benefits and risks, XSM cannot be assessed on protein content alone; its effects on animal performance, nutrient use, and meat quality must be considered together.

These compounds may also be relevant to meat flavor. Dietary lipids and bioactive compounds can alter ruminal biohydrogenation and the microbial community, thereby influencing the amount and composition of polyunsaturated fatty acids deposited in muscle. Some of these compounds may also help maintain muscle antioxidant status (Priolo et al., 2009; Vasta et al., 2019). Lipid oxidation is closely related to the formation of volatile precursors of meat flavor (Elmore et al., 2005). Dietary fatty acid composition also modulates the oxidative stability and flavor of lamb (Chikwanha et al., 2018; Fu et al., 2022). Therefore, replacing a protein source with one that differs in lipid and bioactive composition could influence flavor-related volatiles. Even so, woody oilseed by-products such as XSM have rarely been used in ruminant diets. To our knowledge, no feeding trial has systematically examined how graded replacement of SBM with XSM affects growth performance, carcass traits, meat quality, muscle antioxidant status, and volatile flavor formation in finishing lambs. Two practical uncertainties therefore remain: how much SBM can be replaced before anti-nutritional effects outweigh functional benefits, and whether a substitution level that maintains performance also favors desirable flavor compounds.

We therefore fed finishing lambs four isonitrogenous and isoenergetic diets in which XSM replaced 0%, 50%, 75%, or 100% of SBM over the full fattening period. We evaluated growth performance, carcass traits, meat physicochemical properties, fatty acid and amino acid composition, muscle antioxidant status, and aroma-related volatiles profiled by gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC–IMS). We also examined whether the observed volatile changes corresponded to particular fatty acid precursors. By integrating production, carcass, antioxidant, fatty acid, amino acid, and GC–IMS volatile data in a single feeding trial, this study aimed to identify a replacement level that preserves animal performance while improving meat quality and flavor-related traits. A second, more exploratory aim was to determine whether, and how, a change in dietary protein source is reflected in lamb flavor.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental design

All animal procedures complied with applicable Chinese laws and institutional guidelines, including the relevant provisions of Laboratory animal—Guideline for ethical review of animal welfare (GB/T 35892-2018). These procedures were consistent with internationally recognized principles for animal care and use. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of the College of Life Sciences, Northwest Normal University (Approval No. NWNU-CLS-2024-001). The experiment was conducted at Qianlong Planting and Breeding Farmers' Professional Cooperative, Jingyuan County, Baiyin City, Gansu Province, China (36°26′N, 104°42′E).

Forty healthy male lambs (3 months old, initial body weight 20.78 ± 2.17 kg) were selected for the experiment. Their genetic background and immunization history were consistent. Using a stratified randomization scheme with initial body weight as the blocking factor, the 40 lambs were distributed among four dietary treatments, with 10 head allocated to each treatment. SBM in the diet was replaced with XSM at four replacement levels of 0%, 50%, 75%, and 100%. The corresponding treatment groups were designated as CON, XSM50, XSM75, and XSM100. The XSM used in the experiment was provided by Baiyin Jinhongyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and the experimental diets were produced by Jingtai Dehua Feed Co., Ltd. Based on the nutrient requirements for sheep recommended by the National Research Council (NRC, 2007), diets were balanced to supply equivalent metabolizable energy and crude protein across all four formulations. The diet formulas and nutrient levels are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The nutrient profile of XSM itself is shown in Supplementary Table S2. The whole trial lasted 126 days. The first 14 days were for adaptation, and the remaining 112 days were the formal feeding period. Feed was offered at 08:00 and 18:00 each day, and clean water was always available.

Growth performance

Each lamb was weighed before morning feeding on day 0, which gave the initial body weight (IBW). The same procedure was repeated on day 112 to determine the final body weight (FBW). Feed offered and refusals were recorded daily. Average daily feed intake (ADFI), average daily gain (ADG), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated using the following equations:(1)ADG=FBW−IBW112d(2)ADFI=Feed offered−Feed refusals112d(3)FCR=ADFIADG

Slaughter procedure and carcass characteristics

At the end of the feeding trial, all lambs were fasted for 12 h before slaughter, with free access to water. All 10 lambs from each treatment group were used for slaughter measurements and sample collection. The lambs were transported by a medium-sized truck from the cooperative to the slaughterhouse of Gansu Senyang Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Technology Development Co., Ltd. (36°34′N, 104°43′E). The transport distance was approximately 20 km, and the transport time was approximately 25 min.

Slaughter was performed by trained personnel in a qualified commercial slaughterhouse according to the standard commercial halal slaughter procedure routinely used in the local lamb production system. This procedure was selected because it represents the actual commercial slaughtering and processing conditions in the region where the experimental animals were raised and samples were collected. Therefore, the meat samples obtained in this study could more accurately reflect the production conditions of the local food supply chain. The same transport and slaughter procedures were applied to all treatment groups to ensure consistency among treatments.

During slaughter, a trained operator who met the halal slaughter requirements used a sharp knife to rapidly perform a ventral neck cut. This cut severed the major cervical blood vessels, including the carotid arteries and jugular veins, as well as the trachea and esophagus, to achieve rapid exsanguination. In accordance with the local standard commercial halal slaughter procedure used by the slaughterhouse, electrical stunning was not applied before neck cutting. To minimize unnecessary stress and distress, slaughter was performed by trained personnel according to the standard operating procedures of the commercial slaughterhouse. The process was controlled by using a sharp knife for rapid cutting, ensuring prompt severance of the major cervical blood vessels, and allowing sufficient exsanguination. Slaughter was completed following sufficient exsanguination, and subsequent carcass processing was performed only after death had been confirmed. All slaughter and sample collection procedures complied with the animal ethics approval described in Section 2.1.

Blood, skin, head, distal limbs, and viscera were removed, while kidneys and perirenal fat were retained. The hot carcass was weighed immediately, and the dressing percentage was calculated as:(4)Dressing percentage=Hotcarcass weightPre−slaughter live weight×100

Backfat thickness was measured at the dorsal midline over the 12th–13th intercostal space using a Vernier caliper. Three readings were taken per lamb and averaged. The GR value (soft tissue depth) was measured at the same rib position, but 11 cm away from the midline. Again, three measurements were taken and averaged. For eye muscle area, the longissimus thoracis (LT) cross-section at the 12th–13th rib was traced onto clear film; the tracing was placed on grid paper, and the grids were counted to estimate the area.

Sample collection

Samples of LT were collected from the right side of each carcass for determination of pH, drip loss, cooking loss, meat color, and shear force. Approximately 150 g of LT was excised at the 12th rib, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for proximate composition, fatty acid, amino acid, and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) analyses.

Meat quality analysis

pH and meat color

Muscle pH was recorded at two postmortem time-points (45 min, 24 h) by driving a temperature-compensated spear-tip electrode (Testo 205; Testo SE, Titisee-Neustadt, Germany) ∼20 mm into the LT cross-section. Before each measurement session, the pH meter was calibrated with pH 4.01 and 7.01 buffers. Three pH readings were taken per sample and averaged. For color measurement, meat samples were allowed to bloom for 45 min at room temperature. CIE L* (lightness), a* (redness), and b* (yellowness) values were then measured at three different surface locations on each sample. Measurements were performed using a Minolta CR-10 colorimeter equipped with a D65 illuminant, 10° observer angle, and 8-mm aperture. The mean value of the three readings was used for analysis.

Water-holding capacity and shear Force

LT samples were cut into pieces of approximately 3 × 2 × 1 cm and weighed (W1). The pieces were hung inside drip-loss tubes and kept at 4 °C for 24 h. The sample surfaces were then blotted dry and weighed again (W2), following Li et al. (2018). Drip loss was then calculated:(5)Drip loss=100×W1−W2W1

Another set of LT samples (approximately 3 × 2 × 1 cm) was weighed (W1) and placed between 18 layers of filter paper. A 35 kg load was applied for 5 min using a Tena10 Meat-1 press (China). After pressing, the samples were weighed again (W2). Press loss was obtained by:(6)Press loss=100×W1−W2W1

Approximately 20 g of LT was trimmed free of connective tissue and visible fat and weighed to 0.0001 g (W1). The sample was sealed in a vacuum bag and placed in a water bath at 80 °C. When the core temperature reached 70 °C, the sample was removed and then cooled at 4 °C for 2 h. Surface moisture was blotted off before the final weighing (W2), as described by Su et al. (2022). Cooking loss was:(7)Cooking loss=100×W1−W2W1

From the same cooked sample, a meat strip (1 cm × 1 cm × 0.2 cm) was cut along the muscle fiber direction (Li et al., 2018). Shear force was measured using an electronic tenderness tester (C-LM3B, China). Each sample was measured three times, and the average value was used.

Proximate composition

At 48 h postmortem, the contents of moisture (AOAC 925.04), ether extract (EE; AOAC 935.38), ash (AOAC 938.08), and crude protein (CP; AOAC 981.10) were analyzed following the methods described by AOAC (2005).

Muscle antioxidant indices

The concentrations of malondialdehyde (MDA), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione (GSH), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and catalase (CAT) in LT muscle were measured using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Fatty acid composition of LT muscle

Lipids were extracted from approximately 2 g of LT by acid hydrolysis and subsequently converted into fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs). The FAMEs were analyzed using a Shimadzu GC-2010 Plus gas chromatograph equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID). Separation was achieved on an SP-2560 capillary column (100 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.20 μm film thickness). Nitrogen was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 0.9 mL min−1. Samples were injected in split mode (split ratio 10:1) with an injection volume of 1.0 μL. The initial temperature was 110 °C, increased to 190 °C at 5 °C min−1 and held for 25 min, then ramped to 230 °C at 1.5 °C min−1 and held for 12 min.

Amino acid composition of LT muscle

For amino acid analysis, about 100 mg of sample was mixed with 10 mL of 6 mol/L HCl in a glass tube. The tube was flushed with nitrogen, sealed, and heated at 110 °C for 22 h. After cooling, the hydrolysate was filtered into a volumetric flask. The tube was rinsed several times with deionized water, and the rinses were combined in the flask before making up to volume. An aliquot of 1.0 mL of the solution was evaporated to dryness under nitrogen at 40 °C and re-dissolved in sodium citrate buffer. After filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane, the sample was injected into a Hitachi LA8080 amino acid analyzer. Amino acids were separated by cation-exchange chromatography and detected using ninhydrin.

GC–IMS analysis of LT muscle

VOCs were analyzed using a GC–IMS system with slight modifications to the method described by H. Li et al. (2024). The system consisted of a Shimadzu GC-2010 Pro gas chromatograph (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) equipped with an HS-10 headspace autosampler, coupled to an independent ion mobility spectrometry detector (G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany). Briefly, 2 g of sample was introduced into a 20 mL headspace vial and incubated at 65 °C for 20 min under agitation. The temperatures of the sampling loop and transfer line were maintained at 85 °C and 95 °C, respectively. Headspace gas was injected onto a nonpolar SH-5 capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm; Shimadzu) using N2 (99.999%) as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.00 mL min−1. The oven temperature program was as follows: 30 °C for 5 min; increased from 30 °C to 40 °C at 5 °C min−1 and held for 5 min; then ramped from 40 °C to 130 °C at 5 °C min−1 and maintained for 2 min. The GC effluent was subsequently directed into the IMS drift tube through a heated transfer line. The drift tube temperature was set at 45 °C, with N2 employed as the drift gas at 150 mL min−1. VOCs were identified based on retention indices (C4–C9 n-ketones) and drift times using the GC–IMS database. Data acquisition and visualization were conducted with VOCal, supported by the built-in National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) and IMS databases. Spectral variations were evaluated using the Reporter plug-in (3D spectra, 2D topographic and differential plots), and VOC fingerprints were generated with the Gallery Plot plug-in for semi-quantitative comparison. All results are presented as the mean of 10 biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

The GC–IMS data (peak intensity values for both monomers and dimers) were uploaded to MetaboAnalyst for multivariate analysis. All other data, including growth, carcass, meat quality, fatty acid, and amino acid results, were compiled in Microsoft Excel. Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS 27.0 (IBM, USA). One-way ANOVA was conducted with XSM substitution level as the fixed factor. Each lamb was treated as one experimental unit. When ANOVA indicated a significant effect, treatment means were compared using Tukey's test. Values are presented as means with pooled SEM, and P < 0.05 was considered significant. Spearman correlation analysis was performed using the pandas and scipy libraries in Python 3.12.6, and triangular bubble plots were generated using matplotlib and seaborn. All other figures were produced using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Results and discussion

Growth performance and carcass traits

At the 50% substitution level, FBW, ADG, carcass weight, and FCR did not differ significantly from CON. The XSM75 group generally showed intermediate values between CON and XSM100. FBW, FCR, and carcass weight did not differ significantly among these groups. In contrast, ADG in the XSM75 group was lower than that in CON (P < 0.05) but remained comparable to XSM50 and XSM100. At full replacement, lambs had lower FBW, ADG, and carcass weight than CON, with a higher FCR, indicating reduced feed-utilization efficiency.

No significant differences in dressing percentage or other carcass characteristics were detected among the groups, suggesting that the reduction in carcass weight was mainly attributable to the lower pre-slaughter live weight (Table 1). The average daily feed intake (ADFI) was similar among groups (P = 0.610), indicating that the decline in growth performance in the XSM100 group was not caused by reduced feed intake but rather by impaired nutrient utilization. Secondary metabolites in XSM, such as triterpenoid saponins, tannins, and polyphenols, may have contributed to this effect. Although saponins have been reported to cause bitterness and reduce feed palatability in some species, no significant differences in ADFI were observed among treatments in the present study, suggesting that palatability was not a limiting factor (Kholif, 2023). Tannins tend to bind to proteins at high concentrations, thereby reducing protein degradation in the rumen and potentially decreasing fiber digestibility and microbial protein synthesis (Jayanegara et al., 2015; Patra & Saxena, 2011). A meta-analysis by Yanza et al. (2024) reported that low-to-moderate intakes of plant secondary metabolites do not impair, and may improve, productive traits in ruminants, whereas higher intakes tend to depress feed efficiency and growth. The increased feed conversion ratio and reduced average daily gain observed in the XSM100 group are consistent with this dose–response pattern. This trend suggests that the tolerance threshold of lambs to replacing SBM with XSM may lie between the 75% and 100% substitution levels. However, because the contents of saponins and tannins in the experimental diets were not quantified, the actual load of anti-nutritional factors at each substitution level remains unclear. Future studies should quantify saponin and tannin concentrations in XSM and combine these data with measurements of apparent digestibility and rumen fermentation parameters to better define the safe substitution threshold. In addition, processing techniques such as fermentation or heat treatment may reduce the anti-nutritional factor content of XSM, thereby increasing its safe upper inclusion level in the diet (Álvarez et al., 2025; Das et al., 2022).

Meat quality

Table 2 presents the effects of different levels of XSM substitution on the meat quality and nutritional composition of the LT muscle in finishing lambs. No significant differences were detected among the groups in pH values measured at 45 min postmortem (pH45min) or 24 h postmortem (pH24h), and all measurements fell within the normal physiological range. Similarly, meat color parameters (L*, a*, b*), drip loss, cooking loss, and shear force were not significantly affected by dietary treatments. The contents of conventional nutrients (moisture, ash, crude protein, and crude fat) also remained stable among the groups. Compared with the control group, press loss in all XSM substitution groups was significantly reduced (P < 0.05). Although crude fat content did not differ significantly, it showed a decreasing trend with increasing XSM substitution level, with the XSM100 group showing the lowest intramuscular fat content. The unchanged pH values and color parameters suggest that XSM did not significantly alter postmortem glycolysis or pigment stability (Mancini & Hunt, 2005). Similar stability of pH, color and water-related traits has been reported in lambs fed partial substitutions of rapeseed meal (Samir et al., 2024) or flaxseed meal (Zhang, Guo, et al., 2025), indicating that the basic physicochemical properties of LT muscle are not strongly affected by moderate changes in dietary protein source. While drip loss, cooking loss, and shear force showed no significant changes, the decrease in press loss indicates an enhancement in muscle water-holding capacity when subjected to compression (Huff-Lonergan & Lonergan, 2005; Hughes et al., 2014). Because pH was similar across treatments, the lower press loss may reflect reduced oxidative damage to muscle proteins mediated by XSM bioactives, such as saponins and polyphenols. This interpretation is consistent with reports that antioxidant strategies and bioactive feed by-products can improve water-holding capacity and oxidative stability in small ruminants (Jacondino et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2025). The combination of reduced press loss and a numerical decrease in intramuscular fat, with tenderness unaffected, is also consistent with observations in Hu sheep fed flaxseed meal, where leaner meat was obtained without compromising tenderness (Fan et al., 2019). Collectively, these results indicate that XSM is a feasible partial substitute for SBM, maintaining key meat quality traits and potentially improving certain aspects of water-holding capacity.

Overall, XSM enhanced muscle antioxidant capacity, as CAT activity, GSH-Px activity, and GSH content increased, whereas MDA, a major end-product of lipid peroxidation, decreased. The antioxidant response appeared to involve the GSH–GSH-Px system. GSH can directly scavenge reactive oxygen species and provides a substrate for GSH-Px to reduce lipid hydroperoxides. Thus, concurrent increases in GSH content and GSH-Px activity may interrupt chain reactions and inhibit MDA formation (Domínguez et al., 2019; Ponnampalam et al., 2022). The parallel elevation in CAT activity further supports a broad upregulation of enzymatic antioxidant defenses. In contrast, the lack of significant change in SOD activity suggests that XSM bioactives may preferentially target downstream peroxide-scavenging enzymes rather than superoxide dismutation, or that basal SOD activity was already sufficient under the present experimental conditions. Similar selective modulation of individual antioxidant enzymes has been reported in livestock fed polyphenol- or saponin-rich ingredients (Mei et al., 2024). The observed pattern—concurrent increases in GSH and GSH-Px together with decreased MDA—is consistent with activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2)–antioxidant response element (ARE) signaling pathway, a response previously reported for plant-derived polyphenols and saponins (J. Zhang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2022). However, Nrf2 and its downstream targets were not measured here, so this hypothesis requires validation in future work using transcriptomic or proteomic approaches.

Lipid oxidation is a major driver of meat discoloration, off-flavor formation, and shortened shelf life (Estévez, 2021). MDA is a principal secondary product generated during lipid peroxidation and is widely recognized as a biomarker of oxidative damage; its accumulation during postmortem storage is closely associated with rancid aroma formation and surface discoloration (Bottegal et al., 2023; Ponnampalam et al., 2022). Previous studies have shown that feeding polyphenol-rich by-products can strengthen endogenous antioxidant defenses, thereby maintaining thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) and metmyoglobin at acceptable levels under refrigerated storage. For example, Menci et al. (2023) reported that incorporating hazelnut skin into the diet increased tocopherol concentrations in muscle and significantly reduced lipid oxidation in lamb meat during 7 days of refrigerated storage. Similarly, Álvarez-Rodríguez et al. (2022) reported that high dietary levels of proanthocyanidins and other phenolic compounds can suppress lipid oxidation in muscle tissue and may contribute to improved shelf life of lamb meat. It should be noted that MDA was measured only at the time of slaughter in the present study, and oxidative changes during postmortem refrigerated storage were not evaluated. Therefore, the potential shelf-life extension remains a hypothesis that warrants confirmation by monitoring lipid oxidation markers over a defined storage period in future studies.

At the individual fatty acid level, SFA variation was driven by coordinated changes in C14:0, C15:0, C16:0, and C17:0 (all P ≤ 0.001), whereas C18:0 was unaffected (P = 0.474). The higher C15:0 and C17:0 in XSM75 likely reflected altered contributions from rumen microbe-derived odd-chain fatty acids (Kupczyński et al., 2024). C20:1, C22:1n-9, and C24:1 were absent in CON but detected in all XSM groups (all P < 0.001), consistent with the very long-chain MUFA-rich oil retained in XSM (Yu et al., 2017). Within n-6 PUFA, C20:3n-6 reached its lowest level in XSM75; C20:4n-6 was also lowest in XSM75 but highest in XSM100 (both P < 0.001), suggesting redistribution of tissue elongation/desaturation metabolism that warrants confirmation via Δ-5/Δ-6 desaturase activity indices. Ruminal biohydrogenation (BH) is the most plausible pathway underlying this redistribution, because BH governs the conversion trajectory of dietary C18 fatty acids before tissue deposition (Toral et al., 2024). XSM-derived polyphenols and tannins may perturb rumen microbiota and alter BH efficiency (Frutos et al., 2020; Vasta et al., 2019), but because trans-18:1 and conjugated linoleic acid (CLA) isomers were not quantified, the specific BH steps affected cannot be resolved.

An elevated proportion of unsaturated fatty acids generally increases the vulnerability of lipids to oxidative stress (Chikwanha et al., 2018). In the present study, both the polyunsaturated fatty acid content and the peroxidizability index (PI) were highest in the XSM100 group (P < 0.001), indicating greater oxidative susceptibility. However, antioxidant measurements (see Section 3.3) showed that the malondialdehyde concentration in lamb meat was numerically lower in all XSM-fed groups, reaching significance at full substitution (XSM100). This suggests that polyphenolic and saponin constituents of XSM may have offset the higher peroxidation potential associated with PUFA enrichment, although a direct causal link was not tested in this study. This protective effect is consistent with findings reported for other unconventional feed ingredients rich in polyphenols, such as grape pomace (Chikwanha et al., 2018) and Silybum marianum seed cake (Rayni et al., 2023). Health-related lipid indices showed a similar pattern. The XSM75 group had the highest atherogenic index (AI) and thrombogenic index (TI) (P < 0.001 and P = 0.030) and the lowest ratio of hypocholesterolemic to hypercholesterolemic fatty acids (h/H) (P = 0.004), and therefore showed the least favorable cardiovascular risk profile among the groups. The maximum concentration of erucic acid (C22:1n-9) was 5.73 mg/100 g. Based on a daily intake of 250 g of lamb meat, the estimated intake would be approximately 14.33 mg, which is far below the tolerable daily intake of 7 mg/kg body weight (equivalent to 490 mg/day for a 70 kg adult) established by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) Panel on Contaminants in the Food Chain (CONTAM) (2016). The content of nervonic acid (C24:1) was extremely low and may represent a characteristic lipid marker of XSM-derived oils (Yu et al., 2017).

Overall, among the substitution levels evaluated in this experiment, the XSM50 group exhibited the most balanced fatty acid composition in lamb meat. Relative to the control group, this treatment resulted in a moderate increase in polyunsaturated fatty acids while maintaining a favorable h/H ratio (i.e., the ratio of hypocholesterolemic to hypercholesterolemic fatty acids) and keeping oleic acid content at a level comparable to that of the CON. This improvement did not coincide with the higher SFA content and poorer AI and TI observed in XSM75. It also avoided the lower oleic acid content and greater oxidative susceptibility observed in XSM100.

Among the three substitution levels, XSM50 gave the most favorable compromise between increased PUFA content and limited oxidative susceptibility. Although the XSM100 group exhibited the highest polyunsaturated-to-saturated fatty acid ratio, it also showed a significant decrease in monounsaturated fatty acids and an increase in theoretical oxidative susceptibility. More importantly, when the XSM substitution level exceeded 75%, it was associated with decreased growth performance and feed efficiency (Section 3.1). This reduction in production performance offsets the minor improvement in fatty acid composition resulting from complete substitution. Therefore, considering both meat lipid quality and sustainable production performance, a 50% XSM substitution level is recommended to optimize the fatty acid composition of lamb LT muscle.

The rise in EAA was mainly driven by a significant increase in histidine (His) in all XSM treatment groups (P < 0.001). This gain largely offset the declines in valine (a 26% reduction from CON to XSM100; P < 0.001), leucine (P = 0.029), and lysine (P = 0.017), whereas isoleucine and methionine showed no significant changes. Among NEAA, arginine (Arg) exhibited the largest decrease (−34%; P < 0.001), while alanine (Ala) was the only amino acid that significantly increased (P < 0.001). Analysis of taste-related amino acids showed that with increasing XSM inclusion levels, the content of umami amino acids (aspartic acid + glutamic acid) decreased (P = 0.010), whereas total sweet amino acids and bitter amino acids did not change significantly (P = 0.736 and P = 0.155). These results suggest that XSM mainly altered the relative composition of taste-related amino acids rather than their overall content.

The most prominent change was the substantial increase in histidine content. Due to the use of the acid hydrolysis method, the measured histidine covers the portion released from histidine-containing dipeptides (mainly carnosine and anserine), so this increase may reflect higher dietary supply of this amino acid, increased dipeptide deposition in the muscle, or altered histidine metabolism. These dipeptides have biological activities of scavenging free radicals and chelating metal ions (R. Liu et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2022), and thus the increase in histidine content is consistent with the observed improvement in antioxidant status in Section 3.3. From a flavor-chemistry perspective, the altered amino acid pool may reconfigure taste and aroma precursors. Lower Glu, Asp, Thr, and Ser may reduce umami and sweet precursors, whereas the substantial Ala increase may partly compensate through its contribution to sweetness and mild umami perception (Xu et al., 2021). Free branched-chain and aromatic amino acids are key precursors of Strecker aldehydes, and Lys and Pro contribute to pyrazines and other heterocyclic aromas (Khan et al., 2015); thus the reduced Val and Leu could theoretically affect volatile generation. However, it should be noted that the present study measured total (hydrolyzed) amino acid composition rather than free amino acids, and moderate compositional shifts in hydrolyzed amino acids do not necessarily translate into changes in sensory outcomes. Replacing SBM with rapeseed meal and faba beans altered the free amino acid profile in pigs without reducing acceptability (Grabež et al., 2020).

Among the three substitution levels, XSM50 offered the most balanced amino acid profile. XSM50 achieved full His enrichment, with approximately 2.02 g/100 g compared with 0.99 g/100 g in CON, and therefore retained the associated antioxidant benefit. At this level, reductions in key EAAs were less severe than at higher replacement levels (Val: 1.03 in XSM50 vs 0.98 in XSM100; Lys: 1.81 vs 1.80), and the decline in umami amino acids was also more moderate (4.96 vs 4.81 g/100 g in XSM100). Histidine content was nearly identical among the three XSM treatment groups (2.02, 1.99, and 1.97 g/100 g for the XSM50, XSM75, and XSM100 groups, respectively), indicating that the enrichment effect reached saturation at the 50% substitution level. Further increases in the substitution level did not result in additional histidine accumulation. Considering the amino acid composition, antioxidant properties (Section 3.3), fatty acid profile characteristics (Section 3.4), and the decline in production performance observed at higher substitution levels (Section 3.1), a 50% XSM substitution level appears to provide the most balanced amino acid composition for improving the quality of lamb meat.

Volatile compounds in LT muscle

GC–IMS imaging and qualitative identification of volatile compounds

GC–IMS was used to analyze the volatile compounds in LT muscle from the four dietary groups. All spectra were normalized to the reactant ion peak (RIP) to allow direct comparison. The complete GC–IMS output is presented as three-dimensional topographic spectra in Fig. 2A. GC–IMS resolves analytes in two dimensions, namely gas-chromatographic retention time and ion-mobility drift time. Together with the two-dimensional plots (Fig. 2B) and difference plots (Fig. 2C), these topographic spectra constitute the full chromatographic and spectrometric record of the volatile profile. Using the GC–IMS data, a two-dimensional topographic map of the volatile compounds (Fig. 2B) was constructed, along with a corresponding difference comparison plot (Fig. 2C). In the two-dimensional plot, the XSM50 and XSM75 groups exhibited denser and stronger signals in the medium- to high-retention-time regions compared with the control group (CON). These signals were mainly associated with alcohols, ketones, and esters (Fig. 2A,B). Using CON as the reference, the difference plot showed intensified XSM50 signals across several retention-time regions, shown in warm colors. This pattern indicates a higher overall response intensity of volatile compounds relative to CON. The XSM75 group also showed moderate enhancement, whereas the XSM100 group exhibited relatively smaller overall changes, with some aldehyde signals decreasing. A similar diet-induced remodeling of volatile fingerprints has also been reported in Angus beef supplemented with Mongolian onion (W. Liu et al., 2024).

Analysis using GC–IMS identified a total of 62 signal peaks, corresponding to 51 distinct volatile compounds. These included 17 aldehydes (33.33%), 11 ketones (21.57%), 10 alcohols (19.61%), 8 esters (15.69%), 2 acids (3.92%), 1 furan (1.96%), and 2 nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds (3.92%). Detailed information on these volatile compounds is provided in Table 5. It should be noted that some compounds simultaneously exhibited ionization signals of both monomers (M) and dimers (D), thereby forming multiple response peaks, including 1-hexanal, n-pentanal, heptanal, octanal, n-nonanal, (E)-2-octenal, 1-butanol, 1-pentanol, 2-butanone, 2-heptanone, and ethyl hexanoate. This phenomenon may be attributed to the high proton affinity of these compounds or to the formation of stable dimer ions in the drift tube under atmospheric pressure conditions (Jia et al., 2024; The most common volatiles were aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones. F. Wang et al. (2024) reported a similar finding using GC–IMS to study lamb volatiles from Chinese local breeds. These compounds mainly come from two sources: the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids, and the Strecker degradation of amino acids (Domínguez et al., 2019; H. Li et al., 2024).

Several volatile compounds exhibiting strong signal intensities were consistently detected across all treatment groups. Among aldehydes, 1-hexanal had the highest relative signal intensity, followed by propanal, n-pentanal, and heptanal. 1-Hexanal and n-nonanal are commonly considered reliable markers of lipid oxidation in meat and primarily arise from the oxidative degradation of n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids (Chen, Zhang, Feng, et al., 2024; Domínguez et al., 2019). Among ketones, 2-propanone (acetone) showed the highest signal intensity, followed by 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one and 2-heptanone. Alcohols mainly included 1-pentanol and 1-butanol. The esters mainly included methyl acetate, ethyl hexanoate, and ethyl formate. In addition, 2-pentylfuran was the main furan compound detected, and two nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds, 2,4,6-trimethylpyridine and 2-acetyl-3-methylpyrazine, were also identified. These compounds may originate from Maillard reactions between reducing sugars and amino acids at high temperatures during headspace sampling (Sohail et al., 2022).

Fingerprint map and heatmap analysis

GC–IMS fingerprint analysis (Fig. 2D) indicated that the volatile substance profiles of the LT muscle were significantly influenced by the level of XSM substitution, but not in a simple dose-dependent manner. The heatmap visualization (Fig. 2E) showed good intra-group repeatability and obvious inter-group differences. In the Z-score heatmap, the XSM50 column block displayed the most extensive and intense red coloration across nearly all compound categories, indicating that the overall volatile signal intensity in this group was markedly above the global mean. In contrast, the XSM100 column block showed predominantly blue shading, suggesting an overall lower relative volatile signal compared with the global mean. The CON and XSM75 groups displayed intermediate patterns, and CON shared a more comparable low-intensity tendency with XSM100 than with XSM50. Accordingly, XSM50 formed the most visually distinctive block in the heatmap, clearly separated from the other three groups, consistent with the feasibility of discriminating meat samples across dietary treatments (Jia et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021).

Semi-quantitative data indicated a non-monotonic response for most volatile compounds, with 47 of the 62 signal peaks reaching their highest intensity in XSM50 (Table 5). This trend was most evident for aldehyde peaks, including 1-hexanal, heptanal, octanal, n-nonanal, n-pentanal, butanal, and the unsaturated compounds (E)-2-heptenal, (E)-2-octenal, and (E,E)-2,4-heptadienal. These compounds are among the main lipid-oxidation volatiles in meat and are useful indicators of the overall volatile profile (Domínguez et al., 2019; H. Li et al., 2024). Similar trends were also observed for several ketones (2-heptanone, 2-hexanone, 3-hexanone, 1-octen-3-one, and 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one), some alcohols (1-pentanol, 1-butanol, and (E)-2-hexen-1-ol), as well as 2-pentylfuran and ethyl hexanoate, which are related to the dietary background of mutton (Karabagias, 2018). Several compounds deviated from the general trend: 2-propanone and methyl acetate showed their lowest concentrations at XSM75 and their highest at XSM100, whereas benzaldehyde, propanal, and 2-butanone reached maximum levels at XSM75. Acetic acid was lower in all XSM groups than in CON. The lowest response was observed in XSM75, followed by a partial rebound in XSM100. In contrast, 2-methylbutanal decreased progressively as the XSM substitution level increased. The pronounced clustering of signals at this substitution level and the increase in intensity suggest the existence of a threshold response to changes in the diet composition. The mechanism may be attributed to the optimal balance achieved between the lipid substrate provided by XSM and ruminal biohydrogenation capacity, thereby regulating the supply of polyunsaturated fatty acid precursors for the production of oxidative volatile compounds. The signal pattern in the XSM100 group returned to a level close to that of the control group, suggesting the existence of some compensatory regulation or saturation mechanism, which is worthy of further exploration.

Multivariate analysis of LT volatiles

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed significant differences in the volatile compound profiles of the LT muscle among the four dietary treatment groups (Fig. 2F). The first principal component (PC1) accounted for 63.3% of the total variance, while the second principal component (PC2) explained 14.0%, yielding a cumulative contribution of 77.3%. This indicates that the model effectively captured the primary sources of variation among the samples. In the PCA score plot, samples from the XSM100 group were located relatively close to those of the control group (CON), suggesting that the volatile profile under complete substitution tended to resemble that of the CON. In contrast, XSM50 showed the most pronounced separation from the other three groups. This indicates that 50% substitution substantially changed volatile formation in LT muscle, producing a fingerprint distinct from those of CON and XSM100.

Because XSM50 showed the greatest difference from CON, an orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was further conducted to compare LT samples from CON and XSM50 (Fig. 2G). This OPLS-DA model explained 69.6% of the predictive variance and showed clear separation between the two groups, with excellent goodness-of-fit and predictive ability (R2Y = 0.983, Q2 = 0.961). A permutation test (n = 1000; Fig. 2H) was conducted to validate the supervised model and confirmed that overfitting did not occur, as the R2Y and Q2 values obtained from all permuted models were lower than those of the original model, and the empirical p-value was <0.05 (0/1000). Accordingly, the OPLS-DA model was applied as an exploratory approach, and variables with a variable importance in projection (VIP) value ≥1.2 and P < 0.05 were considered discriminant volatile compounds.

Selection of characteristic volatile compounds in LT

As illustrated in Fig. 2I, OPLS-DA identified nine characteristic volatile compounds using VIP ≥ 1.2 and P < 0.05 as selection criteria. These compounds contributed most strongly to the differentiation between CON and XSM50: acetic acid, ethyl formate, propanal, 2-propanone, 1-octen-3-one, (E)-2-octenal (D), methyl acetate, (E)-2-hexen-1-ol, and benzeneacetaldehyde. Of these, five compounds (propanal, 1-octen-3-one, (E)-2-octenal, (E)-2-hexen-1-ol, and benzeneacetaldehyde) showed increased signal intensities in XSM50 relative to CON, whereas the remaining four (acetic acid, ethyl formate, 2-propanone, and methyl acetate) were reduced.

From the perspective of meat aroma chemistry, the volatile compounds that increased in the XSM50 group are generally regarded as important contributors to desirable meaty aroma. 1-Octen-3-one has a very low odor threshold, in the parts-per-billion range and contributes the characteristic mushroom-like top-note of cooked sheep meat. It is generated mainly through 12-lipoxygenase-mediated cleavage of C18:2n-6c and C20:4n-6 hydroperoxides (Li et al., 2023; Sohail et al., 2022). (E)-2-Octenal likewise contributes fatty and roasted-meat-like notes; its low odor threshold gives it a critical role in shaping the overall aroma profile (Chen, Zhang, Feng, et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023). Benzeneacetaldehyde is a Strecker aldehyde derived from the degradation of phenylalanine, characterized by floral and honey-like odor notes that may enhance aroma complexity (Bleicher et al., 2022; Chen, Zhang, Feng, et al., 2024). Propanal was also elevated in XSM50. This short-chain aldehyde can originate from oxidation of both n-3 and n-6 PUFA. Because total n-3 PUFA content (C18:3n-3) did not differ significantly among groups, its increase may reflect enhanced lipid oxidation at this substitution level. The unsaturated alcohol (E)-2-hexen-1-ol, which is associated with green and fresh odor notes, also increased moderately. In the present dataset, however, the GC–IMS signal of 1-octen-3-one correlated most strongly with C24:1 rather than with its canonical C18:2n-6c/C20:4n-6 precursors (Section 3.6.5), indicating that the semi-quantitative associations reported here reflect treatment-level co-variation rather than direct precursor–product relationships.

The reduction in the signal intensity of acetic acid in the XSM50 group may have a positive impact on the flavor quality of the meat. Previous studies have shown that acetic acid is usually associated with unpleasant flavor characteristics such as pungent, sour, and vinegar-like notes (Watkins et al., 2013). At the same time, the content of compounds such as acetic acid, methyl acetate, and 2-propanone, which are related to the fermentation process of carbohydrates, has also decreased in this group. This suggests that the generation of volatile compounds may be to some extent shifted from the carbohydrate fermentation pathway to the lipid oxidation pathway (Wang et al., 2022).

Overall, the volatile profile of XSM50 was distinguished by elevated signal intensities of lipid-derived aroma-active compounds, while potential off-flavor precursors associated with fermentation were comparatively diminished. Because odor activity value (OAV) calculations and sensory evaluation were not performed, the present GC–IMS data describe relative changes in the volatile profile rather than perceived flavor differences. Nevertheless, the concurrent increases in multiple low-threshold odorants (e.g., 1-octen-3-one, (E)-2-octenal, and benzeneacetaldehyde), together with the reduction in acidic and fermentation-derived compounds, suggest that 50% XSM substitution may favorably modulate the volatile compound composition of lamb LT muscle.

Correlation analysis of characteristic volatile compounds with precursors

Spearman rank correlations were calculated between nine characteristic volatile compounds and their putative precursors, including 17 amino acids and 14 fatty acids. The analysis used LT muscle samples from lambs fed the four XSM replacement levels (CON, XSM50, XSM75, and XSM100; n = 10 per group; total n = 40) to place the volatile profile in a metabolic context (Fig. 3). Out of a total of 279 correlation comparisons, 70 pairs reached nominal statistical significance (P < 0.05). However, after applying false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, only 10 volatile–precursor pairs remained statistically significant (q < 0.05), indicating that strong correlations were confined to a limited subset of precursor compounds. It should be noted that these correlations represent observational associations and do not imply causal relationships. Fatty acid analysis (Fig. 3A) showed that the strongest FDR-adjusted correlations were associated with n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids, particularly C20:4n-6 (arachidonic acid) and C20:3n-6. 2-Propanone showed the strongest correlation with C20:4n-6 (ρ = 0.69, q < 0.001), followed by methyl acetate (ρ = 0.66, q < 0.001), ethyl formate (ρ = 0.62, q = 0.001), and acetic acid (ρ = 0.61, q = 0.001). All of these compounds were also positively correlated with C20:3n-6. This pattern is consistent with previous evidence showing that variation in n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids can influence volatile profiles in mutton by promoting lipid oxidation (Li et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024).

Acetic acid exhibits a bidirectional correlation pattern: it is positively correlated with C20:3n-6 and C20:4n-6, while negatively correlated with the long-chain monounsaturated fatty acids C20:1 (ρ = −0.63, q < 0.001) and C22:1n-9 (ρ = −0.60, q < 0.001), reflecting its association with changes in the PUFA/MUFA ratio in muscle lipids. Propanal is negatively correlated with C20:4n-6 (ρ = −0.62, q = 0.001), which is consistent with previous studies showing different temporal dynamics of aldehyde derivatives during lipid peroxidation (Bleicher et al., 2022). 1-Octen-3-one, a key compound responsible for mushroom-like aroma, is significantly positively correlated with C24:1 (ρ = 0.46, q = 0.027). This result is consistent with GC–IMS findings indicating that long-chain unsaturated fatty acids are associated with the formation of this characteristic ketone compound (Wang et al., 2024).

After FDR correction, significant associations involving amino acids were fewer and mainly concentrated on histidine, arginine, and glycine (Fig. 3B). Among these, ethyl formate showed the strongest association with histidine (ρ = −0.59, q = 0.008). Acetic acid was positively correlated with arginine (ρ = 0.52, q = 0.030) but negatively correlated with histidine (ρ = −0.51, q = 0.030); (E)-2-octenal and 1-octen-3-one exhibited a similar opposing pattern. These correlations may reflect coordinated metabolic changes driven by protein hydrolysis, redox status, and interactions with lipid-derived carbonyl compounds rather than direct precursor–product relationships (Bleicher et al., 2022). Glycine was positively correlated with acetic acid (ρ = 0.51) and ethyl formate (ρ = 0.49), which is consistent with previous reports describing coordinated changes between small neutral amino acids and volatile compounds in lamb meat (Chen, Zhang, Feng, et al., 2024). The weak negative correlation between benzeneacetaldehyde and phenylalanine (ρ = −0.33, P = 0.036) did not remain significant after FDR correction and therefore should be regarded as an exploratory observation (Bleicher et al., 2022). Notably, ethyl formate exhibited a distinctive association pattern: it was negatively correlated with C20:1 and inversely associated with histidine. This feature suggests that ethyl formate may serve as a potential marker reflecting the degree of muscle lipid unsaturation and amino acid metabolic status (Chen, Zhang, Feng, et al., 2024).

Variation in n-6 C20 polyunsaturated fatty acids was associated with the main pattern of volatile differences among dietary groups, particularly selected ketones, acids, and esters. Histidine, arginine, and glycine appeared to play secondary roles in these associations. These patterns may partly reflect the effect of dietary XSM on muscle fatty acid and amino acid composition, suggesting that XSM may shape the volatile profile of lamb meat through coordinated changes in these precursors.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Xanthoceras sorbifolium meal (XSM) can replace up to 50% of soybean meal (SBM) in finishing lamb diets without compromising growth performance or carcass traits, whereas higher substitution levels reduced feed efficiency. At this level, XSM improved muscle water-holding capacity, lowered lipid peroxidation, and enriched n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids and histidine in the longissimus thoracis. This level also produced the most distinctive volatile fingerprint, with a higher abundance of aroma-active compounds and a lower abundance of off-flavor-related compounds; these changes were most closely associated with n-6 C20 PUFA and, secondarily, with histidine and arginine. Although full SBM replacement gave the lowest malondialdehyde concentration, it impaired growth performance and feed efficiency, supporting 50% substitution as the level that best balanced production stability with meat-quality improvement. In summary, moderate XSM substitution can influence lipid oxidation, amino acid composition, and volatile formation in lamb meat. Future studies integrating anti-nutritional factor analysis, storage-stability and odor activity value assessments, and sensory validation are needed to confirm its practical value.
