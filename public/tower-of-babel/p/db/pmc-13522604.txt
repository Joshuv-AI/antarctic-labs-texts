Impact of butanol-sunflower oil biodiesel–diesel blends on engine performance, combustion, and after-treatment emissions (DOC + SCR)
Authors: Şükrü Ayhan Baydır
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effects of n-butanol–sunflower biodiesel–diesel ternary blends on the combustion, performance, and emission characteristics of a single-cylinder compression ignition (CI) engine equipped with an integrated diesel oxidation catalyst and selective catalytic reduction (DOC + SCR) aftertreatment system. Eight fuel formulations were evaluated at 1800 rpm under four engine loads (10.98, 16.48, 21.97, and 27.46 Nm).: neat diesel (D100); binary n-butanol–diesel blends containing 5, 10, and 15 vol% butanol (D95BUT5, D90BUT10, and D85BUT15); a sunflower biodiesel–diesel blend containing 30 vol% biodiesel (B30D70); and ternary blends combining B30 with 5, 10, and 15 vol% butanol (B30D65BUT5, B30D60BUT10, and B30D55BUT15). Combustion parameters were derived from 50-cycle ensemble-averaged pressure data, while emissions were quantified upstream (P1) and downstream (P3) of the aftertreatment system. Results showed that D90BUT10 achieved the lowest specific fuel consumption (241.01 g/kWh) and highest brake thermal efficiency (33.97%) at 21.97 Nm, corresponding to a 4.4 percentage point improvement over D100 at the same condition. D85BUT15 yielded the lowest HC emissions, reaching 54.77 ppm at 10.98 Nm, whereas B30D55BUT15 produced the lowest CO emissions (0.032 vol%) under low-load conditions. However, B30-based blends generated the highest NOx concentrations, with a peak value of 2109 ppm at 16.48 Nm. Following DOC + SCR treatment, CO emissions were completely eliminated for all oxygenated blends, HC emissions decreased by 10–35% depending on load and fuel composition, and NOx conversion efficiency ranged between 40 and 68%, with the highest conversion observed at 27.46 Nm. An anomalous deterioration in combustion and performance was identified for B30D60BUT10 at intermediate loads, attributed to the combined effects of biodiesel-induced atomization limitations and butanol-related ignition delay. Overall, the study provides a systematic characterization of sunflower biodiesel–n-butanol ternary blend interactions within an integrated DOC + SCR framework and highlights the non-linear coupling between oxygenated fuel composition and aftertreatment performance.

Introduction

Escalating global energy demand and increasingly stringent emission regulations have intensified research into alternative fuels for compression ignition (CI) engines. Beyond resource constraints, the vulnerability of critical energy supply routes to geopolitical disruptions has introduced significant volatility in fuel and urea markets, emphasizing the need for localized and resilient energy solutions1. In this regard, leveraging Turkey’s capacity for sunflower oil methyl ester (SOME) production represents a promising pathway to enhance energy security while maintaining effective emission control strategies.

Among renewable fuel options, biodiesel and higher alcohols such as n-butanol have emerged as extensively investigated candidates for CI engine applications2. While each fuel exhibits distinct advantages when blended individually with diesel, their binary formulations introduce specific physicochemical limitations that necessitate the transition to ternary mixtures.

Sunflower oil methyl ester (SOME) is an attractive feedstock due to its generally compatible density, viscosity, and cetane properties. Its oxygen content promotes more complete combustion, reducing CO and HC emissions. However, higher SOME ratios often lead to moderate torque reductions (6–18%) and inferior cold-flow properties. Furthermore, fuel-bound oxygen elevates local flame temperatures, accelerating thermal NOx formation via the Zeldovich mechanism (N2 + O → NO + N). Simultaneously, the higher kinematic viscosity of SOME imposes viscosity-driven atomization constraints, producing a coarser droplet population with an increased Sauter mean diameter (SMD). This extends combustion duration and promotes PM formation via a nucleation–surface growth–agglomeration pathway, particularly in locally fuel-rich zones1–3.

n-Butanol offers a higher energy density (33.1 MJ/kg), lower hygroscopicity, and complete miscibility without co-solvents compared to lower alcohols such as ethanol. It provides improved ignition quality control, higher thermodynamic stability, and reduced volatility-related handling issues. Its high latent heat of vaporization (582 kJ/kg) induces significant evaporative charge cooling, reducing peak temperatures below the Zeldovich threshold. Critically, n-butanol acts as an effective soot suppressor by improving spray atomization and reducing the carbonaceous load entering downstream aftertreatment, thereby mitigating DOC fouling and SCR catalyst deactivation3. Nevertheless, binary butanol blends are constrained by ignition quality deficits and lower calorific values.

In the ternary biodiesel–n-butanol–diesel system, these limitations are mutually compensated through synergistic interactions: (i) SOME’s high cetane number counteracts butanol’s ignition penalty; (ii) butanol’s charge-cooling offsets biodiesel’s NOx increase; and (iii) butanol’s lower viscosity improves atomization, partially compensating for the SOME penalty3–5. These interactions are inherently non-linear and produce complex synergistic effects on combustion phasing and emission formation that cannot be predicted from individual fuel properties alone.

Beyond engine-out emissions, compliance with Euro VI requires integrated aftertreatment. The Langmuir–Hinshelwood surface oxidation mechanism governs CO and HC conversion within the DOC, which also partially converts NO to NO2, aiming for an optimal NO2/NOx ratio (~ 0.5) to trigger the kinetically dominant fast-SCR reaction pathway. Additionally, NO2 generated within the DOC can enhance downstream soot oxidation through NO2-assisted carbon oxidation pathways, thereby influencing particulate removal efficiency and reducing the carbonaceous load entering the SCR system. The efficiency of the SCR system is strongly governed by the catalyst temperature window (typically 200–450 °C) and by transient ammonia storage-desorption dynamics, which together determine NOx conversion stability under varying engine load conditions. Furthermore, the exothermic oxidation of HC and CO promotes a critical temperature rise from the DOC inlet (T1) to the SCR inlet (T2), maintaining the aftertreatment system above the activation threshold, especially under low-load conditions. In alignment with recent advances that incorporate such evaluations within broader sustainability metrics including exergy, thermoeconomic, and environmental analyses4–6, the current research integrates combustion and aftertreatment characterization to evaluate the energy efficiency and emission control trade-offs inherent to oxygenated multi-component fuel systems.

This objective is pursued through a comprehensive systematic investigation that simultaneously quantifies emission transformations at three discrete positions, namely upstream of the DOC (P1), between the DOC and SCR (P2), and downstream of the SCR (P3), across a load-resolved matrix. This approach enables detailed evaluation of the non-linear interaction and load-dependent responses of sunflower biodiesel–n-butanol ternary blends within an integrated DOC + SCR framework. Previous studies on biodiesel-fueled DOC + SCR systems have demonstrated the effectiveness of integrated aftertreatment configurations for reducing regulated emissions under CI engine operating conditions7.

Accordingly, the present study experimentally investigates the combustion, performance, and exhaust emissions of eight fuel formulations: neat diesel, three n-butanol–diesel blends (5, 10, and 15 vol% butanol), one sunflower biodiesel–diesel blend (30 vol% biodiesel), and three ternary blends (B30 combined with 5, 10, and 15 vol% butanol). Experiments were conducted at 1800 rpm under four engine loads using a single-cylinder direct-injection CI engine equipped with a custom-designed DOC + SCR system. The specific objectives are to: (i) analyze in-cylinder combustion parameters including peak pressure, heat release rate, ID, combustion duration, CA10, CA50, and ringing intensity; (ii) evaluate engine performance (BSFC and BTE); (iii) quantify engine-out emissions (CO, CO2, HC, and NOx); and (iv) assess the conversion efficiency of the DOC + SCR system at three aforementioned measurement positions. The study additionally identifies a distinct load-dependent combustion and emission response for the B30D60BUT10 ternary blend, highlighting the non-linear interaction behavior that can emerge in oxygenated multi-component fuel systems. This multi-stage characterization provides new insight into the interaction between oxygenated ternary fuels, DOC exothermic behavior, and SCR conversion performance in agricultural CI engine platforms.

Experimental setup and procedures

Test engine and dynamometer

All experiments were conducted at the Engine Testing Laboratory of Afyon Kocatepe University. The test engine was a single-cylinder, four-stroke, direct-injection, water-cooled compression ignition engine (Antor AD510 BS). The Antor AD510 BS engine (Lombardini 3LD 510 licensed production), was specifically selected for this study because it is a widely utilized single-cylinder power unit in the Turkish agricultural sector, commonly integrated into power tillers (hoeing machines), water pumps, portable generators, and small-scale irrigation systems. A closed-loop coolant recirculation circuit, regulated by a water temperature sensor, maintained engine thermal stability throughout the tests. Engine loading was applied through a direct-current regenerative dynamometer with a rated power absorption capacity of 15 kW. Braking torque was quantified by an STCS S-type load cell (50 kg capacity, C3 accuracy class, 0.003% linearity error) mounted to the dynamometer housing. Fuel consumption was determined gravimetrically using a high-precision electronic balance (30 kg capacity, 1 g resolution) in conjunction with a digital stopwatch.

Combustion measurement system

In-cylinder pressure was acquired with an AVL QC34C piezoelectric pressure transducer (operating range: 0–250 bar; linearity: ± 0.2%) flush-mounted in the cylinder head. The charge signal from the sensor was conditioned by a Kistler 5018A charge amplifier, whose output voltage was sampled in synchrony with crank angle. Crank angle resolution was provided by an Opkon PRI 50AR8 optical encoder (360 pulses per revolution) coupled to the crankshaft. Both the amplified pressure signal and the encoder pulse train were digitized by a National Instruments USB-6351 data acquisition card and transferred to a PC. To minimize cycle-to-cycle variability effects, pressure traces over 50 consecutive engine cycles were ensemble-averaged for each test condition, and the mean cycle was used for all combustion analyses.

The CA10, CA50, and CA90 parameters were calculated from the CHR curve. The start of combustion (SOC) is defined as the crank angle at which the HRR transitions from negative to positive values, marking the point where exothermic reactions overcome the evaporative cooling effect of the injected fuel9,10,12. CA10, defined as the crank angle at which 10% of the total heat has been released, is widely adopted as a practical proxy for the effective start of combustion, given the difficulty of precisely identifying the HRR sign-change point under experimental conditions13–15. To avoid conceptual ambiguity, a clear distinction is maintained between SOC and CA10: SOC represents the physical onset of exothermic reactions, whereas CA10 denotes the crank angle at which combustion becomes thermodynamically established; the interval between them is referred to as the early flame development phase9,12. CA50 represents the crank angle at which 50% of the total heat has been released and serves as the primary indicator of combustion phasing16. CA90, denoting the crank angle at which 90% of the total heat has been released, is used to represent the end of combustion (EOC)14,15. Combustion duration (CD) is defined as the crank angle interval from CA10 to CA9017.

Exhaust gas aftertreatment system

DOC + SCR system configuration

Although the Antor AD510 BS engine is not equipped with emission aftertreatment technologies in its standard commercial configuration, a dedicated two-stage DOC + SCR system was custom-designed and fabricated specifically for this engine as part of an extensive experimental development program20,21. Conventional ceramic honeycomb DOC and vanadium-based (V2O5-WO3/TiO2) SCR catalyst substrates were acquired and cut to a precise volume of 400 cm3 each to match the 510 cm3 engine displacement20. The fabrication involved a six-section exhaust assembly in which the catalysts were welded into the line using conical reducers to optimize flow transition, alongside dedicated mounting ports for the DEF injector, a static mixer, and dual NOx sensors20.

The DOC employs a platinum-group metal catalyst deposited on a ceramic honeycomb monolith; it oxidizes CO and unburned hydrocarbons (HC) and partially converts NO to NO2, thereby enhancing the fast-SCR reaction (R1) kinetics in the downstream unit. The SCR catalyst is a vanadium-based (V2O5-WO3/TiO2) honeycomb monolith with a cell density of 400 cpsi. A 32.5% aqueous urea solution (diesel exhaust fluid, DEF) served as the reductant and was delivered upstream of the SCR brick by a Bosch 2nd Generation (deNOx 2.2) dosing injector. A static mixer installed immediately downstream of the DEF injection point ensured homogeneous ammonia distribution across the catalyst face22. Exhaust gas temperature (EGT) was recorded at three discrete locations by NiCr-Ni (K-type) thermocouples: T1 at the DOC inlet, T2 between the DOC outlet and the SCR inlet, and T3 at the SCR outlet.

Urea dosing and control strategy

The DEF dosing pump, SCR control unit, and injector were operated via a purpose-built MATLAB-based supervisory interface, enabling real-time monitoring and adjustment of the urea injection parameters independently of the engine management system21. The theoretical dosing requirement was established from the stoichiometry of the fast-SCR reaction pathway (Equation R1), which represents the most kinetically efficient NOx reduction route under typical exhaust operating temperatures23:R1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$NO+N{O}_{2}+2N{H}_{3}\to 2{N}_{2}+3{H}_{2}O$$\end{document}

Based on the respective molecular weights (NO = 30, NO2 = 46, NH3 = 17 g/mol), the reduction of 76 g of NOx requires 34 g of NH3, yielding a theoretical demand of 0.448 g NH3 per gram of NOx20. Since one mole of urea (CO(NH2)2) yields two moles of NH3 via successive thermolysis and hydrolysis reactions, 0.789 g of pure urea is required per gram of NOx23. Adjusting for the 32.5% standard DEF concentration gives a theoretical dosing factor of 2.43 g DEF per gram of NOx20,23. The instantaneous mass flow rate of NOx (g/h) was calculated by integrating the measured NOx concentrations (ppm) with the exhaust mass flow rate, which was derived from the measured fuel consumption and the excess air ratio (λ) data. Although the actual NO2/ NOx ratio at the SCR inlet was not measured in this study, the fast-SCR pathway was adopted as the design basis, as it represents the kinetically dominant reduction route under the exhaust temperatures achieved (T2 > 285 °C throughout the test matrix), and the NH3/NOx molar ratio of 1.0 derived from its stoichiometry is equally valid for the standard SCR pathway23. The DEF injection rate was pre-characterized from injector calibration curves established prior to the main experiments using a precision balance (SF-400 C, 0.01 g resolution), following the procedure described in20. The pre-characterized flow rate confirmed that the selected injection setting delivered an NH3/NOx molar ratio of approximately 1.0 under the nominal operating conditions of the test, consistent with the theoretical dosing requirement20,21. At each engine load condition, post-SCR exhaust emissions were recorded in three consecutive measurement phases: a pre-injection phase without DEF, an active DEF injection phase, and a post-injection recovery phase without DEF. The reported post-SCR emission values (P3) represent the arithmetic mean of all three phases. The close agreement observed among the three phase measurements is attributable to the ammonia storage capacity of the vanadium-based SCR catalyst, which sustains NOx reduction activity beyond the active injection interval through adsorbed NH3 surface coverage, thereby confirming stable catalyst operation and validating the representativeness of the averaged post-SCR values21,23.

Uncertainty analysis

To assess the statistical reliability of the experimental measurements obtained in this study, a comprehensive uncertainty analysis was conducted in accordance with the error propagation framework established by Holman24 and Kline and McClintock25. Test fuels

Biodiesel production

Sunflower oil biodiesel was produced via alkaline transesterification. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 0.5 wt.% relative to oil mass) was dissolved in anhydrous methanol (20 wt.% relative to oil mass) to prepare the catalyst solution. Prior to the reaction, the sunflower oil feedstock was heated to 120 °C to reduce residual water content and then allowed to cool to approximately 60 °C. The oil was subsequently combined with the catalyst-methanol mixture and stirred continuously with a magnetic stirrer at 60 °C for 90–120 min to complete the transesterification. Following phase separation, the lower glycerol layer was withdrawn, and the crude biodiesel phase was washed with warm distilled water until a neutral pH was attained and then dried under mild vacuum to remove residual moisture.

Fuel blending and properties

Eight fuel blends were prepared volumetrically for the engine tests: neat diesel (D100); three butanol-diesel binary blends (D95BUT5, D90BUT10, and D85BUT15, containing 5, 10, and 15 vol% n-butanol, respectively); one biodiesel-diesel binary blend (B30, containing 30 vol% sunflower oil biodiesel); and three biodiesel-butanol-diesel ternary blends (B30D65BUT5, B30D60BUT10, and B30D55BUT15, each containing 30 vol% biodiesel combined with 5, 10, and 15 vol% n-butanol, with the remainder being diesel).

In this study, a 30 vol% sunflower biodiesel blend (B30) was adopted as the constant base fuel for all ternary formulations, based on converging evidence from the existing literature. Previous experimental and simulation studies have consistently shown that increasing the biodiesel fraction systematically reduces effective power and torque while raising BSFC, with B30 representing a practical upper limit before these performance penalties become prohibitive for unmodified agricultural engines27–29. Several engine studies further indicate that blend ratios above 30 vol% may induce combustion instability or hardware incompatibility in standard injection systems, confirming that higher fractions are not suitable as a baseline without engine modifications29. From a fuel-property perspective, the progressive increase in kinematic viscosity and surface tension with biodiesel content degrades atomization quality and increases injection system stress; experimental and numerical analyses identify viscosity thresholds above which feed-system issues become likely unless preheating or hardware changes are introduced30–32. Studies on comparable single-cylinder platforms report that exceeding the 30 vol% biodiesel threshold typically results in torque losses of 7 to 18% and measurable deterioration in spray characteristics, with B100 producing reductions of up to 7.06% in torque under full-load conditions27,29. At the same time, B30 provides a sufficiently oxygenated exhaust environment to challenge the DOC + SCR assembly under elevated engine-out NOx loading, and previous aftertreatment studies demonstrate that moderate-to-high biodiesel fractions can enhance DOC oxidation activity and SCR NOx reduction performance33,34. Consequently, B30 was selected as a literature-consistent upper-limit blend that maximizes the renewable fuel share and exhaust oxygenation while preserving acceptable torque capability, injection quality, and durability margins for extended DOC + SCR characterization on an unmodified single-cylinder platform.

Experimental procedure

Before each test sequence, the engine was operated on neat diesel under no-load conditions for approximately 25 min in order to raise the coolant temperature to 85 °C and to stabilize the lubricating oil temperature as well as the exhaust backpressure within the aftertreatment system. All experiments were performed at a constant engine speed of 1800 rpm, which corresponds to the rated maximum torque point of the Antor AD510 BS engine7. This specific speed was selected primarily because 1800 rpm represents the most prevalent and critical operating condition for this engine model in real-world agricultural applications, including power tillers, water pumps, and small-scale irrigation systems that are widely utilized across the Turkish agricultural sector7. By performing the evaluation at this speed, the study ensures that the synergy between the ternary fuel blends and the DOC + SCR system is characterized under thermodynamically representative conditions that align with the engine’s peak mechanical loading and typical field operations.

The experiments were conducted under four progressive load conditions of 10.98, 16.48, 21.97, and 27.46 Nm. The maximum load was intentionally limited to 27.46 Nm, which is below the rated maximum torque of 32.8 Nm, to provide a safety margin considering the lower flash points and higher volatility of butanol-containing blends and to ensure engine durability throughout the extended test campaign. Each fuel was tested, and prior to each measurement, the fuel system was thoroughly purged and the engine was allowed to reach steady-state thermal conditions.

At each load point, emissions data were recorded simultaneously at P1 and P3 after allowing 30 s of steady-state operation following stabilization. Figure 2 illustrates the real-time output of the time-resolved measurement profile for one experimental repetition of the B30D60BUT10 blend at 10.98 Nm. The profile shows the engine-out NOx concentration measured at P1 (approximately 1800 ppm), followed by the post-SCR concentration measured at P3 (approximately 950 ppm) after the exhaust probe was relocated to the SCR outlet. Furthermore, the graphs in Fig. 2 depict the simultaneous time-dependent variations of fuel mass, T1, T2, and T3 exhaust gas temperatures (EGT), and engine load (in kg), with the data updated every second. The stability of these thermal profiles throughout the measurement window confirms steady-state operation during data acquisition.Fig. 2Real-time variation of NOx emissions, exhaust temperatures, fuel mass, and engine load during sampling probe displacement.

Results and discussion

Combustion and performance analyses

In-cylinder pressure and heat release rate (HRR)

Figure 4 presents the variation of in-cylinder pressure and HRR curves of the test fuels under different engine load conditions. Across all loads, both in-cylinder pressure and HRR increased markedly with increasing engine load. While peak cylinder pressure values ranged between approximately 64–66 bar at 10.98 Nm, they increased to 71–75 bar at 27.46 Nm. This behavior is mechanistically governed by the greater total fuel energy input at elevated loads: a larger injected fuel mass releases more chemical energy per cycle, elevating both the adiabatic flame temperature and the mean in-cylinder pressure during the expansion stroke9,10,39.Fig. 4The influences of test fuels on cylinder pressure and HRR.

The influences of test fuels on cylinder pressure and HRR.

At the lowest load condition (10.98 Nm), neat diesel (D100) exhibited the highest peak cylinder pressure at 66.31 bar. Among the butanol-diesel blends, D95BUT5 (66.23 bar) and D85BUT15 (66.03 bar) produced values close to D100, whereas increasing butanol content showed a slight tendency to reduce peak pressure. This attenuation can be attributed to the lower calorific value of n-butanol (33.1 MJ/kg vs. 43.4 MJ/kg for diesel), which reduces the total heat input per cycle despite the improved premixed combustion quality. Blends containing B30 generally exhibited lower peak pressure values than D100 across all load conditions. This behavior originates from two compounding physical effects: first, the higher kinematic viscosity of SOME impairs fuel atomization, producing larger droplet sizes and slower evaporation rates that limit the fuel mass participating in the premixed phase; second, the lower net heating value of biodiesel directly reduces the peak pressure rise per unit of injected fuel mass29,40,41.

At 16.48 Nm, D100 (71.95 bar) continued to produce the highest cylinder pressure. At 21.97 Nm, the D90BUT10 blend (71.80 bar) reached a peak pressure nearly identical to D100 (71.76 bar), demonstrating that a moderate butanol fraction of 10 vol% represents a thermodynamic optimum at medium-to-high loads. The underlying mechanism involves the high latent heat of vaporization of n-butanol (582 kJ/kg vs. 232 kJ/kg for diesel), which promotes charge cooling during the intake and compression strokes, increases the effective air-to-fuel ratio in the premixed zone, and extends the ignition delay, collectively increasing the fuel mass burned in the premixed phase and producing a sharper, more energetic heat release event13,42,43. Under the highest load condition (27.46 Nm), D85BUT15 produced the highest peak cylinder pressure at 75.04 bar, followed by B30D70 (74.90 bar) and D90BUT10 (74.48 bar). At this load, the elevated in-cylinder temperature sufficiently overcomes the evaporative penalty of both butanol and biodiesel, thereby allowing their fuel-bound oxygen to promote more complete oxidation and sustain a high peak pressure despite the lower calorific values of the blends42,43.

A notable anomaly is observed for the B30D60BUT10 blend, which exhibited significantly lower peak pressures at 16.48 Nm (67.20 bar) and 21.97 Nm (63.66 bar). This behavior reflects a critical synergistic interaction between the physical and chemical properties of the two alternative fuel components. The high viscosity of B30 impairs spray penetration and droplet breakup, while the low cetane number of n-butanol (CN ≈ 17) simultaneously extends the chemical ignition delay. Under medium-load conditions, the in-cylinder temperature at the end of compression is insufficient to fully compensate for these compounding limitations: the thermal environment neither provides adequate energy for rapid droplet evaporation nor sufficient activation energy for butanol autoignition within the available time window before TDC. Consequently, a disproportionately large fraction of the fuel–air mixture burns in the post-TDC diffusion-controlled phase, producing a lower and broader pressure trace. At low load, reduced fuel quantity masks this interaction, while at high load, elevated temperatures and pressures restore adequate atomization and ignition kinetics. This load-specific vulnerability of the B30D60BUT10 blend confirms that the interplay between viscosity-driven atomization limitations and cetane-driven ignition chemistry is non-linear and depends critically on the thermodynamic state of the cylinder charge40,41,44.

An evaluation of the HRR curves reveals that butanol-blended diesel fuels enhance the premixed combustion phase, particularly under medium and high load conditions13. This enhancement originates from the dual physicochemical role of n-butanol: its lower boiling point (117.7 °C vs. ~ 180–360 °C for diesel) accelerates fuel vaporization and promotes a more homogeneous charge distribution during the ignition delay period, while its molecular oxygen content (21.6 wt.%) increases the local equivalence ratio toward unity in the premixed zone, thereby accelerating the elementary reaction rates during the initial combustion phase45. At elevated butanol concentrations (15 vol%), these effects produce a discernible double-peak pattern in the HRR diagram: the first peak, occurring before TDC, reflects the rapid oxidation of the well-mixed premixed charge; the second peak corresponds to the diffusion-controlled combustion of fuel injected after the onset of heat release45. At 10.98 Nm, HRR peaks occurred before TDC, approximately between − 6 and − 7 °CA. Among the tested fuels, D95BUT5 exhibited the highest HRR peak at 33.57 J/°CA, whereas B30-based blends remained at relatively lower levels of 29–30 J/°CA13,39.

At 16.48 Nm, D85BUT15 (37.49 J/°CA) and D90BUT10 (37.19 J/°CA) exceeded the HRR value of D100 (36.78 J/°CA), confirming that the charge-cooling and homogenization effects of butanol become increasingly effective as the in-cylinder temperature rises with load, thereby providing a stronger thermal driving force for premixed combustion. Notably, the leading fuel at this load shifted from D95BUT5 (which led at 10.98 Nm) to D85BUT15 and D90BUT10, reflecting the load-dependent balance between two competing effects: the charge-cooling benefit of higher butanol concentrations, which intensifies the premixed combustion peak, and the calorific value penalty, which limits total heat release per cycle. At medium loads, the 10–15 vol% butanol range therefore represents the optimal blending window, maximizing the premixed fuel fraction without incurring an excessive reduction in total energy input13,39.

Under higher load conditions (21.97 and 27.46 Nm), D90BUT10 achieved the highest HRR peak values among all fuel-load combinations, reaching 38.99 and 39.08 J/°CA, respectively. The superiority of D90BUT10 over D85BUT15 at these loads suggests that 10 vol% butanol represents the optimum blending ratio under the tested conditions: beyond this concentration, the calorific value deficit and the increasingly oxygen-diluted diffusion flame begin to suppress the total heat release rate, partially offsetting the premixed combustion gains13,42. In contrast, B30D60BUT10 exhibited the lowest HRR value at 21.97 Nm (27.00 J/°CA), consistent with the anomaly described above for cylinder pressure.

Although B30-based blends generally produced lower HRR values compared to D100 and butanol-diesel blends across all load conditions, the B30D55BUT15 blend showed relatively improved HRR at high load, reaching 35.49 J/°CA at 27.46 Nm. This partial recovery suggests that the elevated butanol fraction (15 vol%) provides sufficient charge-cooling and oxygen enrichment to partially offset the viscosity-induced atomization penalty of the B30 component at high in-cylinder temperatures, where improved spray breakup narrows the performance gap between biodiesel-containing and pure butanol-diesel blends13,39,42.

Ignition delay (ID) and combustion duration (CD)

An analysis of the ID results presented in Fig. 5a shows that, for all fuels, ID generally exhibits a slight increase with increasing engine load, although this trend tends to stabilize at higher loads. This behavior reflects the competing thermochemical effects governing autoignition: on one hand, the elevated end-of-compression temperature and pressure at higher loads accelerate pre-ignition reaction kinetics in accordance with Arrhenius-type rate expressions, reducing the chemical component of the ignition delay; on the other hand, the greater injected fuel mass increases the total evaporative heat demand and extends droplet lifetime within the spray, thereby prolonging the physical component of the ignition delay associated with fuel–air mixing. The near-stabilization of ID at high loads indicates that the thermodynamic acceleration of chemical kinetics progressively dominates over the physical evaporation delay as in-cylinder conditions intensify46.Fig. 5Variations of (a) ID and (b) CD with test fuels under different engine loads.

Variations of (a) ID and (b) CD with test fuels under different engine loads.

From a fuel-type perspective, butanol-blended diesel fuels, particularly D90BUT10 and D85BUT15, exhibited longer ID durations than D100, reaching approximately 14.0°CA at 16.48 Nm. This extension is primarily attributable to the low cetane number of n-butanol (CN ≈ 17 vs. CN ≈ 51 for diesel), which reflects its inherently slower pre-ignition chemistry. The cetane number governs the ease with which low-temperature oxidation reactions, specifically the cool-flame reactions associated with alkylperoxy radical isomerization pathways, proceed prior to the main ignition event. In n-butanol, the absence of low-temperature reactivity pathways characteristic of long-chain n-alkanes means that the chemical ignition delay is substantially extended, requiring higher activation energies before the transition to hot ignition occurs9,46. In addition to this chemical mechanism, the high latent heat of vaporization of n-butanol (582 kJ/kg vs. 232 kJ/kg for diesel) absorbs thermal energy from the surrounding charge during evaporation, thereby locally reducing the gas temperature in the spray zone and imposing a supplementary thermal component on the ignition delay. Taken together, these two effects, namely the suppression of cool-flame pre-ignition chemistry and the evaporative charge cooling, provide additional time for fuel–air mixture formation prior to TDC and directly contribute to the higher premixed HRR peaks observed in Fig. 446.

In contrast, the B30D70 blend exhibited the shortest ID value among all test fuels, reaching 11.0°CA at 10.98 Nm. This behavior is mechanistically linked to the molecular structure of SOME: the ester functional group (–COOCH3) incorporated within the fatty acid chains provides fuel-bound oxygen that promotes the formation of reactive OH radicals during the pre-ignition phase, lowering the effective activation energy for ignition and advancing the onset of exothermic reactions40,46. Furthermore, the higher cetane number of SOME (CN ≈ 52–56) reflects its greater susceptibility to low-temperature oxidation pathways, facilitating earlier cool-flame activity and shortening the overall ignition delay. For ternary B30-based blends containing butanol, the cetane-enhancing effect of biodiesel and the cetane-reducing effect of butanol exert opposing influences on the pre-ignition chemistry, yielding intermediate ID values that shift progressively with butanol fraction. However, the cetane contribution of SOME is insufficient to fully counteract the chemical ignition delay penalty of butanol, resulting in a net ID extension relative to B30D70 across all load conditions40,46.

An examination of the CD results presented in Fig. 5b indicates that CD increases consistently with increasing engine load for all fuels, ranging from 55–59°CA at 10.98 Nm to 65–72°CA at 27.46 Nm13. This trend is governed by the increasing fuel mass injected at higher loads, which not only raises the total chemical energy that must be oxidized per cycle but also sustains a larger diffusion-controlled combustion zone in the post-TDC period, where combustion rate is limited by the local mixing rate between fuel vapor and oxidizer rather than by chemical kinetics13.

In terms of fuel type, D100 produced the shortest or near-shortest CD values under all load conditions, whereas B30-based blends generally exhibited longer combustion durations13. This behavior is mechanistically rooted in the higher kinematic viscosity and density of SOME relative to diesel: increased viscosity impairs spray atomization by reducing the aerodynamic breakup forces acting on fuel ligaments, resulting in a larger Sauter mean diameter (SMD) of the droplet population. Larger droplets require a proportionally longer evaporation time before sufficient vapor concentration builds up for sustained combustion, thereby extending the diffusion-controlled tail of the heat release event and widening the overall CD13,47.

A particularly notable finding is that the B30D60BUT10 blend yielded the longest CD value among all test fuels at 21.97 Nm, reaching 68°CA. As discussed in “In-cylinder pressure and heat release rate (HRR)” Section, this anomaly reflects the adverse synergistic interaction between the viscosity-driven atomization limitations of B30 and the cetane-number-driven ignition delay of butanol under medium-load thermodynamic conditions, which collectively shift a disproportionately large fraction of the total heat release into the late diffusion-controlled combustion phase, broadening the CD beyond that of either component blended individually13.

For butanol-blended diesel fuels, a different trend was observed at high load. At 27.46 Nm, D85BUT15 and D95BUT5 produced CD values of 70 and 71°CA, respectively, compared to 65°CA for D100. This prolongation reflects the slower oxidation kinetics of the n-butanol molecule in the diffusion combustion phase: the C4 alcohol chain, while reactive in the premixed zone due to its oxygen content, exhibits a lower adiabatic flame temperature and a reduced laminar burning velocity compared to the long-chain alkanes dominant in diesel, resulting in a slower burnout of the diffusion-controlled fuel fraction at the periphery of the spray13,42. Importantly, however, this extended late-combustion phase does not translate into reduced efficiency at medium loads, because, as demonstrated in Fig. 4, the stronger premixed combustion peak generated by the extended ID of butanol blends releases a greater proportion of the total energy before TDC, partially compensating for the slower diffusion-phase burnout13,39.

Combustion phasing (CA10, CA50)

Figure 6a presents the variation of CA10 across test fuels and load conditions. With increasing engine load, the CA10 position shifts progressively closer to TDC for all fuels, with values decreasing from 7–8°CA before TDC at 10.98 Nm to 4–6°CA before TDC at 27.46 Nm. Although this trend may appear counterintuitive given that greater fuel mass is associated with longer ID, it reflects a fundamental shift in the balance between physical and chemical ignition delay components at elevated loads. The substantially higher end-of-compression temperature and pressure at increased loads accelerate the Arrhenius-governed pre-ignition reaction rates to a degree that more than compensates for the additional evaporative demand of the larger fuel quantity, resulting in a net advancement of the autoignition event and consequently an earlier CA109,40. This finding is fully consistent with the ID convergence behavior discussed in “Ignition delay (ID) and combustion duration (CD)” Section, wherein the chemical kinetic acceleration at high loads progressively dominates over the physical mixing delay.Fig. 6Variations of (a) CA10 and (b) CA50 with test fuels under different engine loads.

Variations of (a) CA10 and (b) CA50 with test fuels under different engine loads.

In terms of fuel type, B30D70 and other B30-based blends exhibited earlier or comparable CA10 values relative to D100 under low and medium load conditions. This advancement is mechanistically driven by the molecular oxygen embedded within the ester chains of SOME, which promotes local OH radical production during the pre-ignition phase, lowering the effective activation barrier for cool-flame initiation and advancing the transition to the main combustion event. Additionally, the higher cetane number of SOME facilitates alkylperoxy radical isomerization at lower temperatures, further contributing to an earlier CA10 under conditions where in-cylinder temperatures are still moderate16,29.

In contrast, butanol-blended diesel fuels (D95BUT5, D90BUT10, D85BUT15) showed CA10 values delayed by approximately 1–2°CA compared to D100, particularly at higher loads. This retardation reflects the suppression of low-temperature pre-ignition chemistry by n-butanol: the β-scission decomposition pathways dominant in butanol oxidation generate primarily C2-C3 radical fragments rather than the reactive peroxy intermediates responsible for cool-flame activity in n-alkanes, thereby delaying the energy release onset. Furthermore, the charge-cooling effect of butanol’s high latent heat of vaporization reduces the local gas temperature in the spray zone during the ignition delay period, adding a thermal component to the retardation that becomes more pronounced as butanol concentration increases13,40,48. The delayed CA10 of butanol blends is thus mechanistically linked to the extended ID reported in “Ignition delay (ID) and combustion duration (CD)” Section and directly explains the enhanced premixed HRR peaks observed in Fig. 4.

As shown in Fig. 6b, CA50 shifts progressively toward later crank angles with increasing engine load for all fuels, increasing from 6 to 8°CA after TDC at 10.98 Nm to 15–16°CA after TDC at 27.46 Nm. This retardation of CA50 with load is governed by the increasing dominance of diffusion-controlled combustion at higher fuel injection quantities: as the injected fuel mass grows, a larger fraction of the total charge must be oxidized through the slower mixing-limited diffusion pathway, which extends the crank angle interval required to reach the 50% mass fraction burned milestone and shifts CA50 progressively away from TDC13,48.

At the lowest load condition, D100, D90BUT10, and D85BUT15 exhibited the earliest CA50 values at 6°CA after TDC, whereas all B30-based blends showed delayed combustion phases at approximately 8°CA. This 2°CA retardation of B30-containing blends reflects the spray quality penalty imposed by the higher kinematic viscosity of SOME: impaired atomization produces a coarser droplet population with a larger Sauter mean diameter, which slows the rate of fuel vapor generation in the diffusion zone and delays the consumption of the second half of the fuel mass, ultimately retarding CA50. The thermodynamic consequence of this delayed CA50 is a reduction in the effective expansion ratio during the power stroke, which contributes to the lower thermal efficiency of B30-based blends at low load conditions40.

With increasing load, the fuel-type divergence in CA50 became more pronounced before subsequently narrowing. At 16.48 Nm, B30D65BUT5 and B30D60BUT10 exhibited the most delayed CA50 values at 13°CA after TDC, compared to 9°CA for D100. This amplified divergence arises from the compounding of two load-sensitive effects: the viscosity-driven atomization penalty of B30 intensifies as the injected fuel mass increases, producing a larger absolute quantity of coarse droplets that must evaporate through diffusion, while the simultaneously extended ID of the butanol component shifts a greater proportion of the heat release to the post-TDC period. The pronounced delay of B30D60BUT10 at this load is fully consistent with the anomalous pressure and HRR behavior identified in “In-cylinder pressure and heat release rate (HRR)” Section, confirming that medium-load conditions represent the most unfavorable operating window for this blend ratio40,49.

At higher load conditions (21.97 and 27.46 Nm), CA50 values converged strongly across all fuels. This thermodynamic convergence occurs because the elevated in-cylinder temperature and pressure at high loads accelerate both droplet evaporation and diffusion flame oxidation rates to the point where fuel-specific differences in viscosity, cetane number, and latent heat become relatively insignificant compared to the overwhelming influence of the thermodynamic state of the cylinder charge. In essence, the high-load thermal environment enforces a common combustion phasing on all fuels regardless of their individual physicochemical properties, explaining the collapse of inter-fuel CA50 differences observed experimentally13,48.

Regarding the ignition delay phase, B30D70 exhibits the longest ID at the lowest load (13.0°CA at 10.98 Nm), reflecting the compounding effects of the higher kinematic viscosity of SOME, which delays physical fuel evaporation, and its paradoxically higher cetane number, which partially counters this effect but is insufficient to overcome the viscosity penalty at low in-cylinder temperatures. Butanol–diesel blends (D95BUT5–D85BUT15) yield slightly shorter IDs of 11.0°CA at the same condition, consistent with the cetane-number and latent-heat mechanisms established in “Ignition delay (ID) and combustion duration (CD)” Section. As engine load increases, ID converges across all fuels to a narrow range of 10.0–11.0°CA at 27.46 Nm, reflecting the progressive dominance of Arrhenius-governed pre-ignition kinetics over fuel-specific physical evaporation delays as end-of-compression temperatures and pressures intensify41,48.

The CA10 → CA50 phase widens moderately with increasing load for all fuels, ranging from 13–16°CA at 10.98 Nm to 20–22°CA at 27.46 Nm, with B30-based blends consistently occupying the upper bound of this range due to their coarser droplet population and slower fuel vapor generation rate, as discussed in “Combustion phasing (CA10, CA50)” Section. The late burnout phase (CA50 → CA90) constitutes the dominant portion of the total combustion event, spanning 42–44°CA across all fuels at low load. At higher loads, this phase extends notably for oxygenated blends, reaching 48–51°CA for butanol- and biodiesel-containing fuels compared to 45°CA for D100. This extension reflects two compounding mechanisms: the slower C4-chain oxidation kinetics of n-butanol in the diffusion combustion phase, which reduces the laminar burning velocity of the peripheral flame zone, and the increasing dominance of mixing-limited diffusion combustion at higher fuel injection quantities, which spreads the tail of the heat release event over a progressively wider crank angle interval41,48.

Consequently, total CD ranges from 55–59°CA at 10.98 Nm to 65–72°CA at 27.46 Nm. D100 exhibits the shortest CD across all loads (65°CA at 27.46 Nm), while B30D70 and B30D65BUT5 yield the longest (72°CA), consistent with the CA50 and CD analyses reported in “Ignition delay (ID) and combustion duration (CD)” and “Combustion phasing (CA10, CA50)” Section40,41,48. The phasing diagram thus confirms that fuel composition exerts its most pronounced influence on the late burnout phase and total combustion duration at higher loads, where the viscosity-driven atomization penalty of biodiesel and the slower diffusion-phase oxidation kinetics of butanol collectively extend CA50 → CA90, while the ignition delay phase converges as elevated in-cylinder thermodynamic conditions become the overriding governing factor41,48.

Combustion stability (MPRR and RI)

An examination of the maximum pressure rise rate (MPRR) results presented in Fig. 8a reveals that the overall trend exhibits a complex dependence on both engine load and fuel type. MPRR is mathematically defined as the maximum of dP/dθ over the combustion cycle and is therefore directly governed by the rate at which chemical energy is released in the premixed combustion phase: a larger premixed fuel mass burning over a shorter crank angle interval produces a steeper pressure gradient and a higher MPRR. Accordingly, MPRR tends to increase with engine load as the total fuel mass and the associated premixed fraction both grow, subject to the fuel-specific ignition characteristics16Fig. 8Variations of (a) MPRR and (b) RI with test fuels under different engine loads.

Variations of (a) MPRR and (b) RI with test fuels under different engine loads.

Under low and medium load conditions (10.98 and 16.48 Nm), D100 and butanol-blended diesel fuels produced higher MPRR values than B30-based blends. In particular, D90BUT10 and D100 reached the highest values at 16.48 Nm, with 8.41 and 9.09 bar/°CA, respectively. This behavior is mechanistically consistent with the longer ignition delays of butanol-diesel blends established in “Ignition delay (ID) and combustion duration (CD)” Section: the extended ID allows a greater mass of fuel to accumulate in the vapor phase prior to autoignition, so that when combustion initiates, a larger premixed charge burns simultaneously and generates a steeper dP/dθ. The positive correlation between ID and MPRR observed here is also supported by the elevated HRR peaks documented in Fig. 416,40.

At high load (27.46 Nm), this trend became more pronounced, with D85BUT15 (9.02 bar/°CA) and D90BUT10 (8.94 bar/°CA) exhibiting the highest MPRR values. As the butanol fraction increases, both the cetane-number-driven extension of chemical ignition delay and the latent-heat-driven evaporative charge cooling act cumulatively to increase the premixed fuel accumulation period, amplifying the pressure rise gradient upon ignition. While elevated MPRR values indicate more intense and efficient premixed combustion, values exceeding approximately 10 bar/°CA are generally associated with increased structural loading on engine components and heightened knock risk; the fact that all tested blends remained below this threshold confirms the mechanical safety of the evaluated fuel formulations13,40.

In contrast, B30-based blends consistently showed lower MPRR values across all load conditions. The higher kinematic viscosity of SOME impairs spray atomization and reduces the rate of fuel evaporation, thereby limiting the premixed fuel fraction and producing a more gradual pressure rise characteristic of diffusion-dominated combustion. The lower net calorific value of biodiesel further constrains the total energy release per unit crank angle, collectively resulting in a more controlled and mechanically benign pressure rise profile40,50.

A particularly notable observation is that the B30D60BUT10 blend produced the lowest MPRR value among all test fuels at 21.97 Nm, with 5.92 bar/°CA. This result is the direct mechanistic consequence of the anomaly described in “In-cylinder pressure and heat release rate (HRR)” and “Ignition delay (ID) and combustion duration (CD)” Sections: under medium-load conditions, the compounding of biodiesel viscosity and butanol cetane-number effects shifts combustion disproportionately toward the post-TDC diffusion-controlled phase, where the fuel burns at a slower rate governed by turbulent mixing rather than by chemical kinetics. The resulting heat release is spread over a wider crank angle interval, flattening the dP/dθ profile and yielding an anomalously low MPRR that reflects degraded rather than controlled combustion40.

D100 and butanol-blended diesel fuels exhibited higher RI values than B30-based blends under low and medium load conditions, with D100 producing the highest RI of 7.64 MW/m2 at 10.98 Nm. The elevated RI of butanol blends at medium-to-high loads reflects both their higher MPRR and their higher Tmax, the latter arising from the more intense premixed combustion phase generated by the extended ID16.

At higher load conditions (21.97 and 27.46 Nm), D85BUT15 emerged as the fuel with the highest RI, reaching 8.36 and 8.69 MW/m2, respectively. This outcome reflects the compounding of three load-sensitive effects: the increasingly extended ID of D85BUT15 produces greater premixed fuel accumulation and a higher MPRR; the stronger premixed combustion raises Tmax, which amplifies RI through the temperature-dependent term in the RI formula; and the elevated in-cylinder pressure at high loads further modulates the RI through its Pmax term, albeit in the denominator13,42.

B30-based blends generally exhibited lower RI values across all conditions, consistent with their lower MPRR and the moderate peak temperatures associated with diffusion-dominated combustion. The reduced knocking tendency of biodiesel blends is further supported by the higher cetane number of SOME, which promotes earlier and more gradual heat release, distributing the energy input over a wider crank angle window and limiting the instantaneous pressure oscillation amplitude27.

However, a notable exception was observed at 16.48 Nm, where the B30D65BUT5 blend produced an unexpectedly high RI value of 10.06 MW/m2, exceeding all other fuels at this load including D100. This anomaly is mechanistically attributed to a specific thermodynamic interaction between the 5 vol% butanol fraction and the B30 biodiesel base at this intermediate load condition: at 16.48 Nm, the in-cylinder temperature is sufficient to activate the charge-cooling and evaporation-enhancement effects of butanol without yet providing the high-temperature thermal environment that would moderate the pressure oscillation amplitude at full load. The result is a localized intensification of the premixed combustion event, potentially associated with micro-explosion phenomena at the droplet scale where butanol and biodiesel components evaporate at different rates, generating abrupt local pressure gradients that manifest as elevated RI40,49.

Engine performance (BSFC, BTE)

An examination of Fig. 9a, which presents the brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC) results, shows that BSFC values for all fuels decrease consistently as engine load increases from 10.98 Nm to 21.97 Nm, after which a slight increase is observed at 27.46 Nm10,46. The decreasing BSFC trend at low-to-medium loads reflects the improving combustion efficiency associated with higher in-cylinder temperatures and pressures: as load increases, the thermodynamic conditions accelerate fuel oxidation kinetics, reduce the fraction of fuel energy lost to incomplete combustion, and improve the indicated thermal work per cycle. At maximum load, however, the slight BSFC increase is attributable to the combined effects of extended combustion duration beyond TDC, increased friction and pumping losses at higher cylinder pressures, and the enhanced convective heat transfer to the cylinder walls driven by elevated in-cylinder gas temperatures, which collectively reduce the fraction of released chemical energy converted to useful brake work9.Fig. 9Effects of test fuels on (a) BSFC and (b) BTE under different engine loads.

Effects of test fuels on (a) BSFC and (b) BTE under different engine loads.

From a fuel-type perspective, the D90BUT10 blend achieved the lowest BSFC value among all fuels at 21.97 Nm, with 241.01 g/kWh, followed by D85BUT15 (254.59 g/kWh) and D95BUT5 (259.83 g/kWh). This result is mechanistically explained by the dual role of n-butanol at moderate blending ratios: its lower boiling point and surface tension improve spray atomization and droplet breakup relative to neat diesel, producing a finer droplet population that evaporates more rapidly and generates a more homogeneous air–fuel mixture; simultaneously, its molecular oxygen content enhances local oxidation rates in the premixed zone, reducing the quantity of partially oxidized species that would otherwise represent thermodynamic losses. Together, these effects increase the fraction of fuel chemical energy converted to useful brake work per unit mass of fuel consumed, yielding a net reduction in BSFC despite the lower calorific value of butanol13,40.

On the other hand, B30-based blends exhibited higher BSFC values than D100 under all load conditions. This behavior is governed by two compounding mechanisms: first, the lower net calorific value of SOME (approximately 37.5 MJ/kg vs. 43.4 MJ/kg for diesel) requires a proportionally greater fuel mass to deliver an equivalent energy input per cycle; second, the higher kinematic viscosity of B30 blends impairs spray atomization, increases droplet size, and slows fuel evaporation, collectively reducing combustion efficiency and further increasing the mass of fuel required to sustain the target brake output. In particular, the B30D65BUT5 blend reached the highest BSFC value of 307.81 g/kWh at 10.98 Nm, where the adverse viscosity effects are most pronounced due to the lower in-cylinder temperatures prevailing at minimal load13,27.

The BTE results presented in Fig. 9b exhibit a trend fully consistent with the BSFC findings, as BTE is the thermodynamic inverse of BSFC when normalized by fuel heating value. For all fuels, BTE increases with engine load up to 21.97 Nm and then decreases slightly at 27.46 Nm, reflecting the same balance between improving combustion efficiency at moderate loads and increasing thermal and mechanical losses at maximum load16,46.

The most notable result is that D90BUT10 achieved the highest BTE of the entire test matrix at 21.97 Nm, reaching 33.97%, which is 4.4 percentage points higher than D100 (29.53%). This performance advantage originates from the thermodynamic synergy between extended ignition delay and improved premixed combustion phasing: the longer ID of D90BUT10, driven by the cetane-number and latent-heat effects of butanol described in “Ignition delay (ID) and combustion duration (CD)” Section, promotes a more homogeneous charge distribution before ignition, concentrating a greater proportion of the heat release near TDC where the in-cylinder volume is minimum and the thermodynamic work conversion efficiency is maximum. The D85BUT15 blend also achieved a high BTE of 32.49% at the same load, confirming that the 10–15 vol% butanol range consistently maximizes thermal efficiency under medium-to-high load conditions in this engine13,42.

In contrast, B30-based blends generally produced BTE values comparable to or slightly below D100. Notably, B30D55BUT15 achieved the highest BTE among B30 blends at 10.98 Nm with 29.66%, confirming that butanol addition partially mitigates the adverse effects of biodiesel by improving spray quality and advancing the premixed combustion fraction. However, B30D65BUT5 and B30D70 exhibited lower BTE than D100 at high load conditions, indicating that the viscosity-driven combustion quality penalty of high biodiesel fractions progressively outweighs the oxygen-content benefit as the thermodynamic demand on the fuel injection system intensifies13,40.

Emissions analyses

Carbon monoxide (CO) emissions

The effects of the test fuels and the DOC + SCR system on CO emissions are presented in Fig. 10. An examination of the pre-DOC + SCR (P1) results shows that CO emissions increase consistently with rising engine load for all test fuels, reaching a pronounced peak at 27.46 Nm, where D100 recorded 1.092 vol%29,42. This behavior is governed by the progressive deterioration of local air–fuel stoichiometry at elevated loads: the greater injected fuel mass creates locally fuel-rich regions within the spray where the available oxygen is consumed by the initial oxidation steps, leaving insufficient O2 for the complete oxidation of carbon intermediates through the CO → CO2 pathway. CO formation is thermodynamically favored over CO2 production in oxygen-deficient zones because the reaction C + ½O2 → CO is kinetically faster than the subsequent oxidation step CO + ½O2 → CO2, which requires a higher activation energy and is strongly inhibited by the low local oxygen partial pressure51.Fig. 10Combined effects of test fuels and engine load on CO emissions (a) before DOC + SCR (P1) and (b) after DOC + SCR (P3).

Combined effects of test fuels and engine load on CO emissions (a) before DOC + SCR (P1) and (b) after DOC + SCR (P3).

From a fuel-type perspective, blends containing biodiesel and butanol generally produced lower CO emissions than D100, owing to their higher inherent oxygen content and lower carbon-to-hydrogen (C/H) ratio29,42. The fuel-bound oxygen in both SOME and n-butanol serves as an in-situ oxidant within the diffusion flame: oxygen atoms covalently bonded to the fuel molecule are released during the initial pyrolysis and decomposition reactions, creating a localized oxygen surplus in the immediate vicinity of the burning droplet that promotes the conversion of CO intermediates to CO2 before they can escape the primary reaction zone. This mechanism is particularly effective for the B30D55BUT15 blend, which yielded the lowest CO values at both 10.98 Nm (0.032 vol%) and 21.97 Nm (0.107 vol%), reflecting the additive contributions of biodiesel oxygen (approximately 11 wt.%) and butanol oxygen (21.6 wt.%) to the in-cylinder oxidation environment49,52.

As shown in Fig. 10b, the post-DOC + SCR (P3) results demonstrate a dramatic reduction in CO emissions following aftertreatment. For all oxygenated blends, CO emissions decreased to zero, reflecting the highly effective catalytic oxidation of CO within the DOC substrate. The DOC facilitates the heterogeneous surface reaction CO + ½O2 → CO2 over the platinum-group metal catalyst, a process that proceeds at high efficiency once the catalyst surface temperature exceeds the CO light-off threshold of approximately 150–200 °C53. Since the exhaust gas temperature at the DOC inlet (T1) consistently exceeded 267 °C across all test conditions, the catalyst operated well above its light-off temperature, ensuring near-complete CO conversion for all oxygenated fuel blends whose engine-out CO concentrations were within the catalyst’s oxidative capacity53.

For D100, a small residual CO value of 0.015 vol% was measured at 27.46 Nm, corresponding to a DOC conversion efficiency of approximately 98.6%. This residual CO is attributable to the mass transfer limitation of the catalytic reaction at high exhaust flow rates: under maximum load conditions, the increased volumetric flow of exhaust gas reduces the residence time of CO molecules within the catalyst channels, limiting the contact time available for the heterogeneous oxidation reaction and allowing a small fraction of CO to pass through unconverted despite the favorable operating temperature53.

Combined effects of test fuels and engine load on CO2 emissions (a) before DOC + SCR (P1) and (b) after DOC + SCR (P3).

D100 produced comparatively lower CO2 emissions at low and medium load conditions relative to B30-based blends. This behavior is mechanistically linked to the lower oxygen content of neat diesel: in oxygen-deficient combustion zones, a portion of the carbon that would otherwise be fully oxidized to CO2 is instead converted to CO or remains as unburned hydrocarbons, as discussed in “Carbon monoxide (CO) emissions” Section. Consequently, the incomplete carbon oxidation pathway diverts carbon away from CO2 production, yielding lower measured CO2 concentrations41,51. In contrast, B30-based blends promoted more complete carbon oxidation due to the fuel-bound oxygen of SOME, which sustains CO → CO2 conversion even in locally fuel-rich zones, resulting in higher CO2 emissions at low and medium loads. At the highest load (27.46 Nm), the lowest CO2 concentration after DOC + SCR was recorded for B30D60BUT10 at 3.844 vol%, followed by B30D65BUT5 (4.234 vol%) and D95BUT5 (4.749 vol%), a ranking consistent with the relative DEF injection rates at this load condition. Unlike CO, which is catalytically oxidized to near-zero levels within the DOC, CO2 cannot be reduced to zero by any mechanism operating within the aftertreatment system, as it represents the fully oxidized terminal state of all carbon-containing species in the exhaust.

Hydrocarbon (HC) emissions

The effects of the test fuels and the DOC + SCR system on hydrocarbon (HC) emissions are presented in Fig. 12. An evaluation of the pre-DOC + SCR (P1) results in Fig. 12a shows that D100 produced the highest HC emissions under all load conditions, with values increasing from 151.44 ppm to 215.78 ppm as engine load increased. HC emissions in diesel engines originate from multiple physicochemical sources: the quench layer adjacent to the cold cylinder wall, where the flame is extinguished before complete oxidation; the crevice volumes between the piston rings and cylinder liner, where the fuel–air mixture is trapped and cannot sustain combustion; and locally over-lean or over-rich zones within the spray that either fall outside the flammability limits or suffer from insufficient oxygen for complete oxidation. The increase in HC with load for D100 reflects the growing contribution of fuel-rich spray regions under higher injection quantities, where the low inherent oxygen content of diesel is insufficient to sustain complete carbon chain oxidation9,29,35.Fig. 12Combined effects of test fuels and engine load on HC emissions (a) before DOC + SCR (P1) and (b) after DOC + SCR (P3).

Combined effects of test fuels and engine load on HC emissions (a) before DOC + SCR (P1) and (b) after DOC + SCR (P3).

Butanol-blended diesel fuels exhibited a pronounced reduction in HC emissions, with D85BUT15 yielding the lowest HC levels across all load conditions and reaching a minimum of 54.77 ppm at 10.98 Nm. This reduction is mechanistically driven by two synergistic properties of n-butanol: first, its lower kinematic viscosity and surface tension relative to diesel improve spray atomization by reducing the energy barrier to droplet breakup, producing a finer droplet population with a smaller Sauter mean diameter that evaporates more rapidly and reduces the likelihood of wall wetting and crevice trapping; second, the molecular oxygen content of n-butanol (21.6 wt.%) provides an in-situ oxidant that sustains the oxidation of hydrocarbon radicals within the diffusion flame, preventing their escape from the primary reaction zone as unburned species. Together, these mechanisms reduce HC emissions proportionally with butanol concentration, explaining the monotonic decrease observed from D95BUT5 to D85BUT1516,39,54.

B30-based blends, although producing lower HC emissions than D100, showed higher values compared to butanol-diesel blends across all load conditions. This behavior is rooted in the molecular structure of SOME: the long-chain fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) molecules (C16-C18) have inherently slower oxidation kinetics compared to the shorter-chain alkanes dominant in diesel, requiring higher activation energies and longer residence times for complete combustion. Furthermore, the higher kinematic viscosity of SOME impairs spray atomization and increases the probability of droplet impingement on the cylinder wall, where fuel deposits form a liquid film that burns incompletely during the expansion stroke, contributing disproportionately to HC emissions43,51.

As illustrated in Fig. 12b, the post-DOC + SCR (P3) results indicate that the system reduced HC emissions by approximately 10–35% across all fuel and load combinations23,55. This conversion efficiency is substantially lower than that achieved for CO (approximately 98.6%), reflecting the fundamental difference in catalytic oxidation kinetics between CO and unburned hydrocarbons. While CO oxidation over platinum-group metal catalysts proceeds through a simple Langmuir–Hinshelwood surface mechanism with a relatively low activation energy and a light-off temperature of approximately 150–200 °C, the catalytic oxidation of long-chain hydrocarbon molecules requires a significantly higher surface temperature to overcome their greater activation energy barrier, with HC light-off typically occurring above 250–300 °C depending on the molecular weight and structure of the hydrocarbon species. Since the T1 values at low loads (267–294 °C) approach but do not substantially exceed the HC light-off threshold, the DOC operates in a thermally marginal regime for HC oxidation at these conditions, explaining the lower and more variable conversion efficiencies relative to CO53. At the highest load condition (27.46 Nm), the lowest HC after DOC + SCR was recorded for D85BUT15 at 62.58 ppm, followed by D90BUT10 at 69.00 ppm, consistent with their lower engine-out HC concentrations entering the DOC.

While the measured HC conversion efficiency of 10–35% highlights the relative resistance of long-chain hydrocarbon molecules to catalytic oxidation within the tested operating range, this does not indicate that ternary blends are unsuitable for integrated aftertreatment systems9,53. Although partially converted high-molecular-weight hydrocarbons from sunflower biodiesel could theoretically deposit on the DOC and SCR catalyst surfaces through coking and pore-plugging mechanisms, potentially reducing the active catalyst surface area and inducing long-term deactivation, the presence of n-butanol in the ternary blend serves as a critical mitigating factor23,53. n-Butanol reduces the total carbonaceous load entering the DOC by acting as a soot suppressor through two complementary pathways: its lower viscosity and higher vapor pressure promote more complete in-cylinder combustion, reducing the mass of unburned hydrocarbon species reaching the catalyst; and its molecular oxygen content lowers the C/H ratio of the combustion intermediates, shifting the product distribution away from high-molecular-weight polycyclic species toward lighter, more readily oxidized fragments13,39. Furthermore, the combined fuel-bound oxygen of the biodiesel-butanol-diesel triad ensures that engine-out HC concentrations are already substantially lower than those of neat diesel, reducing the thermal and chemical stress imposed on the downstream catalysts40,52. To comprehensively address long-term durability concerns, future research involving dedicated endurance tests and post-test catalyst characterization is recommended to quantify the deactivation rate of vanadium-based SCR systems under sustained operation with oxygenated ternary fuel blends23,55.

Nitrogen oxides (NOx) emissions

In contrast, butanol-blended diesel fuels (D90BUT10 and D85BUT15) exhibited lower NOx emissions compared to D100 across most load conditions. A crucial mechanism governing SCR performance is the NO2/NOx ratio at the SCR inlet, which determines the relative contributions of the standard SCR (4NO + 4NH3 + O2 → 4N2 + 6H2O) and the fast-SCR (NO + NO2 + 2NH3 → 2N2 + 3H2O) reaction pathways. The fast-SCR reaction proceeds at a substantially higher rate than the standard pathway because NO2 is a stronger oxidant than O2 for the reduction of NH3, and the reaction is kinetically favorable at the operating temperatures of vanadium-based catalysts (250–450 °C). The upstream DOC partially oxidizes NO to NO2 over its platinum-group metal surface, shifting the NO2/NOx ratio toward the stoichiometric optimum of 0.5 required for maximal fast-SCR activity9,23. Although the emission analyzer employed in this study measures total NOx without speciation, the superior conversion efficiencies recorded for oxygenated ternary blends suggest that their higher engine-out HC and CO concentrations provide additional reducing species within the DOC that promote the selective oxidation of NO to NO2, thereby enhancing the NO2/NOx ratio entering the SCR7,53.

Unlike the near-complete CO conversion observed in “Carbon monoxide (CO) emissions” Section, zero NOx emission levels could not be achieved. This limitation reflects the inherent sensitivity of SCR performance to the precise stoichiometric balance between injected urea and instantaneous NOx load, the minimum catalyst temperature required for efficient urea hydrolysis, and the residence time available for the heterogeneous reduction reactions within the catalyst bed. At low load conditions, where exhaust temperatures are closer to the SCR light-off threshold, these constraints become more binding and limit the achievable conversion efficiency23,55.

In contrast, the post-DOC + SCR (P3) O2 concentrations were consistently and substantially higher than the corresponding P1 values across all fuel and load combinations, ranging from 16.23 to 17.08 vol% at 10.98 Nm and 12.65–15.16 vol% at 27.46 Nm. This counterintuitive increase does not reflect a generation of oxygen within the aftertreatment system, as the DOC reactions (CO + ½O2 → CO2 and HC + O2 → CO2 + H2O) actually consume oxygen. Rather, the elevated P3 O2 concentrations arise from two compounding measurement effects: first, the emission analyzer operates on a dry-gas basis, removing water vapor before quantification; since DEF injection introduces a substantial water vapor load into the exhaust stream, its removal before measurement concentrates all remaining dry-gas species, including O2, proportionally increasing their apparent vol% values; second, the SCR reactions convert NOx molecules (which constituted a measurable fraction of the total exhaust gas at P1) to N2 and H2O, reducing the total mole count of non-O2 species in the dry gas matrix and thereby increasing the relative O2 fraction23,56.

An evaluation of Fig. 14b, which presents the excess air ratio (λ) results, shows that the pre-DOC + SCR (P1) values decrease consistently with increasing engine load. At 10.98 Nm, λ values ranged between 3.04 and 3.25 for all fuels, indicating a highly lean air–fuel mixture, whereas at 27.46 Nm, these values decreased to 1.15–1.22, approaching stoichiometric conditions. This behavior reflects the fundamental load control mechanism of unthrottled diesel engines: since intake air quantity is not throttled, increasing the injected fuel mass at higher loads progressively reduces the global excess air ratio while the total air mass per cycle remains approximately constant9,10. The measured λ values at maximum load (1.15–1.22) are consistent with the smoke-limited operating boundary of direct-injection diesel engines, below which incomplete combustion of the diffusion-controlled fuel fraction generates visible particulate emissions9.

Among the tested fuels, D90BUT10 produced the leanest mixture at 10.98 Nm with λ = 3.25, reflecting its superior combustion efficiency and more complete oxygen utilization per unit fuel mass, which leaves a greater residual O2 fraction in the exhaust. In contrast, B30D70 yielded the lowest λ at 21.97 Nm with λ = 1.57, indicating a relatively richer effective mixture attributable to the higher fuel-to-air mass ratio required to compensate for the lower calorific value of the B30 blend and maintain equivalent brake torque output13,46.

The post-DOC + SCR (P3) λ values were consistently higher than the corresponding P1 values under all conditions, with the highest value observed for B30D65BUT5 at 10.98 Nm, reaching λ = 6.03. It is important to note that the P3 λ values do not represent the true combustion air–fuel ratio, as λ at the SCR outlet is influenced by the same DEF-induced dilution and dry-basis measurement effects described for O2 above, rather than by the in-cylinder combustion stoichiometry. Nonetheless, the elevated P3 λ values qualitatively confirm that the aftertreatment system maintains a strongly oxidizing atmosphere in the post-treatment exhaust stream, which is a prerequisite for efficient DOC operation and is consistent with the near-complete CO conversion reported in “Carbon monoxide (CO) emissions” Section56,57.

An examination of the P1 region reveals a general inverse relationship between BSFC and NOx emissions: fuels with lower specific fuel consumption tend to exhibit higher NOx concentrations. This trade-off reflects the fundamental thermodynamic coupling between combustion efficiency and thermal NOx formation: improved combustion quality increases the mean in-cylinder gas temperature and the residence time of combustion gases above the Zeldovich threshold (~ 1800 K), thereby simultaneously reducing fuel consumption and accelerating thermal NOx production. This behavior is well-established in the literature for oxygenated fuel blends and represents an inherent constraint of conventional diffusion-flame diesel combustion13,29.

B30-based blends exhibited a characteristic clustering in the upper-right region of the P1 diagram, combining high NOx emissions (1724–2109 ppm) with relatively elevated BSFC values (282–315 g/kWh). This apparently paradoxical combination, in which both high NOx and high fuel consumption occur simultaneously, is mechanistically explained by the dual penalty of biodiesel: the fuel-bound oxygen of SOME elevates local flame temperatures and promotes Zeldovich NOx formation, while the lower net calorific value and higher viscosity of B30 blends simultaneously increase the fuel mass required to sustain the target brake torque, raising BSFC. Consequently, B30-based blends occupy a thermodynamically unfavorable position that combines the NOx penalty of high-oxygen combustion with the efficiency penalty of impaired atomization13,40.

In contrast, butanol-blended diesel fuels clustered in the lower-left region, yielding both reduced BSFC (241–296 g/kWh) and lower NOx emissions (1322–1743 ppm), consistent with the findings reported in “Engine performance (BSFC, BTE) and “Nitrogen oxides (NOx) emissions” Sections. This favorable positioning reflects the charge-cooling effect of butanol’s high latent heat of vaporization, which simultaneously suppresses peak flame temperatures (reducing NOx) and enhances premixed combustion efficiency (reducing BSFC)13,39.

Following DOC + SCR application (P3 region), NOx emissions for all fuels decreased substantially to the range of 569–1022 ppm, while BSFC remained unchanged, as expected given that the aftertreatment system operates exclusively on the exhaust stream without influencing the in-cylinder combustion process. The overall structure of the BSFC-NOx trade-off was therefore preserved in the P3 region, with B30-based blends retaining their relatively higher post-treatment NOx levels compared to butanol-diesel blends. This confirms that the fuel-composition-dependent NOx formation advantage of butanol blends is maintained through the full aftertreatment chain13,53.

Figure 15b presents the T2-NOx relationship across both the P1 (upper) and P3 (lower) regions, using T2 as the reference temperature since it reflects the thermal state of the exhaust stream entering the SCR catalyst after DOC oxidation. An analysis of the P1 region shows that NOx emissions generally increase with rising T2, consistent with the Arrhenius-governed exponential temperature dependence of the Zeldovich mechanism. Within the T2 range of 285–365 °C, NOx values varied between 1322 and 2109 ppm, with B30-based blends clustering at higher T2 values due to the more intense exothermic oxidation reactions within the DOC that elevate T2 beyond T1, as discussed in “Exhaust gas temperature (EGT)” Section29,53.

However, at the highest load condition (27.46 Nm), where T2 temperatures exceeded 400 °C, a relative decrease in engine-out NOx was observed despite the elevated temperature. This apparent reversal of the T2-NOx trend is not attributable to aftertreatment effects, as these are P1 measurements taken upstream of the DOC. Rather, it reflects the richer air–fuel mixture prevailing at maximum fuel injection rates, where the local oxygen partial pressure in the spray zone is insufficient to sustain the bimolecular Zeldovich reactions (N2 + O → NO + N; O2 + N → NO + O) at their maximum rate despite the elevated temperature, as both high temperature and adequate oxygen availability are required simultaneously for significant thermal NOx formation9,57.

In the P3 region, following DOC + SCR application, NOx emissions for all fuels decreased to the range of 569–1022 ppm. The persistence of the T2-NOx positive correlation in the P3 region confirms the strong dependence of SCR conversion efficiency on exhaust gas temperature: higher T2 values accelerate urea thermolysis and hydrolysis kinetics within the SCR bed, increase the surface reaction rates of the fast-SCR and standard SCR pathways on the vanadium-based catalyst, and shift the equilibrium of the NH3 adsorption–desorption balance toward higher surface coverage, collectively enhancing NOx reduction efficiency at elevated temperatures23,56.

Exhaust gas temperature (EGT)

Exhaust gas temperature (EGT) measurements were conducted at three locations along the aftertreatment circuit: upstream of the DOC (T1), between the DOC outlet and the SCR inlet (T2), and downstream of the SCR (T3). The results are presented in Fig. 16.Fig. 16EGT values versus engine load for (a) T1 (before DOC) and (b) T2 (between DOC and SCR) and T3 (after SCR) with test fuels.

EGT values versus engine load for (a) T1 (before DOC) and (b) T2 (between DOC and SCR) and T3 (after SCR) with test fuels.

An analysis of T1 values reveals a consistent increase in exhaust temperature with increasing engine load for all fuels, with values ranging from 267–294 °C at 10.98 Nm to 413–435 °C at 27.46 Nm9,29. This rise reflects the direct thermodynamic relationship between fuel mass flow rate and total heat release per cycle: at higher loads, the greater quantity of fuel burned per cycle increases both the peak combustion temperature and the sensible enthalpy of the exhaust gas leaving the cylinder, elevating T1 in proportion to the total energy input9.

In terms of fuel type, butanol-blended diesel fuels produced T1 values broadly comparable to D100 under low and medium load conditions, consistent with their similar total heat release profiles. At higher loads, D85BUT15 produced notably elevated T1 values, reaching 358 °C at 21.97 Nm and 426 °C at 27.46 Nm. This behavior is mechanistically linked to its extended combustion duration and retarded CA50, as established in “Ignition delay (ID) and combustion duration (CD)” and “Combustion phasing (CA10, CA50)” Section: the prolonged late-phase heat release of D85BUT15 sustains elevated gas temperatures through a greater portion of the expansion stroke, increasing the thermal energy content of the exhaust gas at the cylinder exit48. B30-based blends generally exhibited slightly lower T1 values than D100 at low loads, a consequence of their diffusion-dominated combustion profile which distributes heat release over a wider crank angle interval and reduces the peak exhaust enthalpy flux at minimal load conditions40.

T2 values measured downstream of the DOC were consistently higher than the corresponding T1 values across all fuel and load combinations, with the temperature rise being more pronounced for biodiesel-containing blends. This phenomenon is governed by the exothermic heterogeneous oxidation reactions occurring on the platinum-group metal surface of the DOC: CO and unburned HC present in the exhaust stream are catalytically oxidized to CO2 and H2O, releasing thermal energy that elevates the bulk gas temperature between T1 and T27,53. The intensified T1 → T2 temperature rise observed for B30-based blends is mechanistically attributable to the higher engine-out HC and CO concentrations documented in “Carbon monoxide (CO) emissions” and “Hydrocarbon (HC) emissions”Sections: the greater quantity of incompletely oxidized species entering the DOC provides a larger exothermic heat source within the catalyst substrate, proportionally elevating T2 relative to T1. The higher kinematic viscosity of SOME impairs in-cylinder fuel atomization, producing larger droplet sizes and a greater mass of unburned HC escaping the primary combustion zone, thereby sustaining the elevated DOC exotherm characteristic of biodiesel-containing blends53,54. Importantly, this active heat generation within the DOC ensures that T2 remains well above the minimum effective operating temperature of 285 °C required for efficient urea thermolysis and NOx reduction in the downstream SCR system under all tested conditions23,53.

T3 values measured at the SCR outlet ranged between 141–195 °C, representing a substantial temperature drop of 230–260 °C relative to T2 under high-load conditions. This marked decrease confirms the active thermochemical operation of the SCR system and is attributable to two compounding endothermic processes: first, the thermolysis of urea (CO(NH2)2 → NH3 + HNCO, ΔH > 0) and the subsequent hydrolysis of isocyanic acid (HNCO + H2O → NH3 + CO2, ΔH > 0) absorb thermal energy from the exhaust gas stream; second, the heterogeneous SCR reduction reactions on the vanadium-based catalyst surface consume additional enthalpy as the reactive N–H bonds in ammonia are utilized to reduce NOx to N223. The consistently low T3 values relative to T2 thus serve as indirect confirmation that the SCR system was thermally active and chemically reducing NOx throughout all test conditions, further supported by the NOx conversion efficiencies of 40–68% reported in “Nitrogen oxides (NOx) emissions” Section.

Limitations and future work

Although this study provides a systematic and comprehensive evaluation of combustion characteristics, engine performance, gaseous emissions, and DOC + SCR aftertreatment conversion efficiencies across eight fuel formulations and four engine load conditions, several limitations are acknowledged that define the scope of the present work and motivate future research directions.

The most significant experimental constraint is the absence of direct particulate matter (PM) and smoke opacity measurements, as the current test bench was not equipped with a dedicated PM measurement system such as a smoke meter, filter smoke number analyzer, or particle size spectrometer. Since PM and NOx emissions pose synergistic environmental and public health risks, being directly linked to cardiopulmonary diseases, respiratory tract infections, and adverse neurological effects, their simultaneous quantification alongside gaseous emissions is essential for a holistic assessment of ternary oxygenated blends in DOC + SCR-equipped engines9,10. Although n-butanol is theoretically established as an effective soot suppressor through its charge-cooling, viscosity-reduction, and oxygen-enrichment effects, all of which were discussed in the context of HC emissions in “Hydrocarbon (HC) emissions” Section, direct experimental verification of PM reduction under the tested fuel-load matrix remains a primary objective for future research23,40.

A second limitation concerns the speciation of NOx. The emission analyzer employed in this study measured total NOx (NO + NO2) without providing individual species concentrations. The NO2/NOx ratio at the DOC outlet (T2 position, SCR inlet) is a critical parameter governing fast-SCR reaction kinetics, as the optimal stoichiometric condition for maximum NOx conversion requires NO2/NOx ≈ 0.5, as discussed in “Nitrogen oxides (NOx) emissions” Section. Consequently, while the superior NOx conversion efficiencies observed for oxygenated ternary blends were attributed to a favorable shift in this ratio, direct experimental verification was not possible within the current instrumentation setup. Future studies should incorporate NO/ NO2 speciation measurement capability to quantify the fuel-composition-dependent influence on DOC oxidation selectivity and fast-SCR pathway utilization23,57.

Third, while the exhaust mass flow rate was reliably derived from fuel consumption and lambda (λ) data for urea dosing control, the absence of direct, high-frequency intake air and exhaust flow measurements represents a technical refinement opportunity. Incorporating real-time mass flow sensors in future iterations could further enhance the transient response of the closed-loop dosing system and enable a more granular analysis of space velocity effects within the catalyst substrate. Such high-fidelity measurements would complement the current steady-state findings and support the development of even more precise aftertreatment control algorithms for variable-speed applications.

Fourth, the potential for long-term catalyst deactivation through surface coking and pore-plugging by high-molecular-weight hydrocarbon species from sunflower oil biodiesel was identified as a mechanistic concern in “Hydrocarbon (HC) emissions” Section. While the short-duration experiments conducted in this study did not reveal any measurable degradation in DOC or SCR conversion efficiency, the cumulative effect of sustained high-HC loading on vanadium-based catalyst activity under real-world operating conditions remains uncharacterized. Dedicated endurance testing and post-test catalyst characterization, including BET surface area analysis and X-ray diffraction, are therefore recommended to quantify the deactivation rate and establish operational service intervals for DOC + SCR systems operating on biodiesel-butanol-diesel ternary blends23,55.

Finally, all experiments were conducted at a constant engine speed of 1800 rpm, which was selected as the rated speed of the Antor AD510 BS test engine and represents a typical operating condition for stationary agricultural and industrial diesel applications. While this approach enables controlled and reproducible load-sweep characterization, it does not capture the speed-dependent variations in exhaust temperature, flow rate, and emissions speciation that occur across the full engine operating map. Future research will therefore focus on extending the evaluation across a complete speed-load matrix under both steady-state and transient operating conditions to comprehensively characterize the synergistic impact of high-alcohol ternary blends and integrated DOC + SCR aftertreatment systems across the full operational envelope7,56.
