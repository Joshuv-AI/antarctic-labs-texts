Temocillin: a therapeutic alternative in bone and joint infections due to third generation cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacterales?
Authors: Louise Piquart, Sylvain Goutelle, Mathieu Maillard, Sandrine Boisset, Anne Carricajo, Anne Tristan, Anne-Gaëlle Ranc, Frédéric Laurent, Céline Dupieux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesBone and joint infections (BJIs) due to third-generation cephalosporin-resistant (3GC-R) Enterobacterales are particularly challenging-to-treat infections, and their treatment often relies on carbapenems. Due to its stability towards various beta-lactamases, as well as its limited spectrum of activity, temocillin could be a promising alternative. This study evaluated the in vitro activity of temocillin against a collection of 3GC-R Enterobacterales isolates from BJIs and modelled the probability of PK/PD target attainment according to the dosage regimen used.MethodsMICs of temocillin and seven comparators (ertapenem, imipenem, meropenem, levofloxacin, delafloxacin, doxycycline and tigecycline) were determined using gradient strips for 104 3GC-R Enterobacterales isolates from BJIs in three French hospitals. Isolates were characterized about their mechanism of resistance to 3GC (ESBL or hyperproduced cephalosporinase). PK/PD simulations were performed with six dosage regimens and different stages of renal function in order to calculate PTA according to temocillin MIC.ResultsTemocillin showed a susceptibility rate of 92.3% (96/104) in 3GC-R isolates from BJIs: 95.3% (61/64) in ESBL producers and 87.5% (35/40) in AmpC hyperproducers. Temocillin was the only alternative to carbapenems for 25% (26/104) of isolates. PTA values increased with declining renal function. In normorenal patients, none of the tested regimen achieved adequate PTA up to the Enterobacterales breakpoint (16 mg/L).ConclusionsThis study demonstrated the high in vitro efficacy of temocillin against 3GC-R Enterobacterales from BJIs, regardless of the underlying resistance mechanism. However, dosage adjustments based on the strain’s MIC and the patient's renal function are crucial for optimal efficacy.

Introduction

Bone and joint infections (BJIs) are a major cause of recurrent and prolonged hospitalizations due to frequent relapses and therapeutic failures. According to retrospective studies conducted in French BJI reference centres, Enterobacterales account for 12.5% to 17.0% of all microorganisms involved in BJIs, with approximately 11.4% of the isolates resistant to cefotaxime, including 7.8% producing extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs).1,2 The treatment of BJIs caused by third-generation cephalosporin-resistant (3GC-R) Enterobacterales is particularly challenging, as third-generation cephalosporins (3GC) and fluoroquinolones are the recommended first-line antimicrobials for BJIs caused by Enterobacterales. However, 3GC-R Enterobacterales often exhibit co-resistance to fluoroquinolones, further limiting treatment options. Therefore, carbapenems remain one of the last therapeutic options for these infections.

While effective, carbapenems present major drawbacks. They have a strong ecological impact on the gut microbiota and their ability to induce dysbiosis varies according to their specific characteristics. These effects are well-documented and are attributed to their broad-spectrum activity, potent anti-anaerobic effect, pharmacokinetic properties (e.g. biliary excretion), and pharmacodynamic factors (e.g. prolonged treatment duration, high doses, and sub-inhibitory concentrations).3,4 Expert opinion identifies carbapenems, particularly imipenem and meropenem, as among the beta-lactam antibiotics with the greatest impact on microbiota diversity.5,6 These changes in microbiota result in reduced diversity, promoting the onset of infections due to the dominance and proliferation of pathogenic bacteria and the selection of resistant strains. Notably, carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPEs) have emerged worldwide over the past decade, with their prevalence increasing in most countries.7 Therefore, to limit this ecological impact and the spread of carbapenemases, reducing the use of carbapenems is crucial to preserve the efficacy of these last-resort antibiotics. The evaluation and use of alternatives to carbapenems could be an effective strategy in sparing these molecules.

Due to its activity against Ambler class A (ESBLs) and class C (hyperproduced cephalosporinases—AmpC) beta-lactamases, as well as its limited spectrum (lack of activity against Gram-positive microorganisms, anaerobes and Pseudomonas aeruginosa), temocillin could be a promising alternative to carbapenems,8,9 although its indications are currently limited (urinary tract infections including pyelonephritis, lower respiratory tract infections, skin and soft tissue infections and bacteraemia). The use of temocillin in these indications is well documented, but very few data are available regarding its potential use in the treatment of BJIs caused by 3GC-R Enterobacterales, for which carbapenems are usually used as first line therapy. Given this lack of data and the known impact of carbapenem use on the emergence of CPEs, it would be interesting to investigate whether temocillin could be an effective alternative to carbapenems in such infections.

The aims of this study were: (a) to evaluate the in vitro activity of temocillin and comparator antibiotics as alternatives to carbapenems against a large collection of Enterobacterales isolates originating from BJIs and resistant to at least one 3GC (excluding CPEs), and (b) to conduct pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) simulations to predict temocillin exposure and target attainment in bone tissue based on various dosage regimens.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolates

We conducted a multicentre retrospective study over a 7-year period (2017–2023). Enterobacterales isolates originating from BJIs (both mono- and polymicrobial) were collected from three different hospital laboratories: Hospices Civils de Lyon (n = 94), Saint-Etienne University Hospital (n = 24), and Grenoble University Hospital (n = 20). A total of 104 isolates were included based on their resistance to at least one 3GC (ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, or ceftazidime), as determined by automated antimicrobial susceptibility testing, excluding CPEs. The presence of carbapenemase was suspected based on resistance to at least one carbapenem and confirmed using the O.K.N.V.I. Resist-5 immunochromatographic test (Coris, Gembloux, Belgium) and/or the Rapidec Carba NP colorimetric test (bioMérieux, La-Balme-les-Grottes, France). In addition, a control group of 26 3GC-susceptible isolates was selected for comparison. Frozen strains were subcultured on Columbia agar supplemented with 5% sheep blood (bioMérieux). Species identification was confirmed using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry (Vitek® MS, bioMérieux).

Temocillin susceptibility

The MIC of temocillin and seven other antibiotics (ertapenem, imipenem, meropenem, levofloxacin, delafloxacin, doxycycline and tigecycline) was determined using Etest® gradient strips (bioMérieux) on Mueller-Hinton agar (Bio-Rad), according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Antimicrobial susceptibility was interpreted according to the 2024 EUCAST guidelines. Of note, the current EUCAST clinical breakpoint for temocillin and Enterobacterales is 16 mg/L: isolates are susceptible at increased exposure (I) if their MIC is ≤ 16 mg/L and resistant to temocillin if their MIC is > 16 mg/L. Each batch of agar plates, strips, and discs used for MIC measurements was controlled using a reference strain (ESBL-negative Escherichia coli ATCC 25922).

The mechanism of resistance to 3GC (ESBL or hyperproduced AmpC) was determined using a synergy test. This test was performed on Mueller-Hinton agar supplemented with cloxacillin (Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France) using cefotaxime, cefepime, aztreonam, ceftazidime and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid discs (SirScan Discs®, i2a, Montpellier, France), incubated at 35°C for 24 hours.

Pharmacokinetic study

PK/PD simulations were performed with the Pmetrics package for R (version 1.5.2),10 using the model published by Wijnant et al.11 This model, developed in 22 non-critically ill patients with complicated urinary tract infections, is a one-compartment model that incorporates the influence of glomerular filtration rate (GFR) on temocillin CL. Other available population PK models were considered less relevant for BJIs, because they were developed in critically ill patients.12–14

Six dosage regimens were simulated: 2 g every 12 hours (q12h, 30-min infusion), 2 g every 8 hours (q8h, 30-min infusion), 2 g every 12 hours (q12h, 4-hour infusion), 2 g every 8 hours (q8h, 4-hour infusion), 4 g every 24 hours (q24h, continuous infusion) and 6 g every 24 hours (q24h, continuous infusion). Different stages of renal function were considered: normal (90 < GFR ≤ 120 mL/min/1.73 m2), mild renal impairment (60 < GFR ≤ 89 mL/min/1.73 m2), moderate renal impairment (30 < GFR ≤ 59 mL/min/1.73 m2), and severe renal impairment (10 < GFR ≤ 29 mL/min/1.73 m2). PTAs were calculated for each renal stage.

The temocillin PK profile was simulated for 1000 subjects at steady-state for all dosages and renal function by sampling the log-normal distribution of temocillin CL and volume of distribution (V) described by Wijnant et al. They reported low inter-individual variability of CL and V (coefficient of variation of 7% and 8%, respectively), which can be explained by the small number of participants. Here, we consider an inflated variance with a coefficient of variation of 50% for both parameters, in line with the high variability reported in other populations.14

No data have been reported on the diffusion of temocillin into bone and joints. Data from other penicillins suggest a low bone to plasma concentration ratio (B/P), ranging from 0.07 to 0.5, with a median of 0.17.15 Because of this uncertainty, three B/P ratios were considered: a low (0.1), intermediate (0.2), and high (0.4) ratio. These ratios were denoted as the active fraction (fa) in bone tissue. As with other beta-lactams, the PK/PD target for temocillin is the time during which the free, active concentration is above the MIC (fT > MIC). The usual target is 35% fT > MIC for temocillin.12 We used the same target, but with the active fraction in bone instead of the unbound fraction, so the target was 35% faT > MIC.

PTA values were computed for all combinations of dosage, renal function, active fraction, and for the MIC range observed in our strain collection (1 to 32 mg/L). A PTA > 90% was considered acceptable. The PTA weighted by MIC frequency, also known as the cumulative fraction of response (CFR), was also calculated to reflect the overall a priori efficacy of the antibiotics, considering both PK variability and MIC distribution observed in our collection.

Statistical analysis

MIC distributions were described by determining MIC50 and MIC90. When relevant, susceptibility rates and MIC distributions were compared using Fisher’s exact test and Chi-squared test, respectively, with Prism software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). The significance threshold was set at 0.05.

Results

Temocillin activity in third-generation cephalosporin susceptible population.

Among the 3GC-susceptible population, Enterobacter cloacae complex was the most prevalent species accounting for 38.5% of the isolates (10/26), followed by Escherichia coli 11.5% (3/26), Klebsiella pneumoniae 11.5% (3/26), and other species 38.5% (10/26). All isolates were susceptible at increased exposure to temocillin, with MICs ranging from 1 to 8 mg/L. Resistance rate among the other tested antibiotics was 26.9% (7/26) for delafloxacin, 7.7% (2/26) for levofloxacin, and 42.3% (11/26) for tigecycline.

Temocillin activity in third-generation cephalosporin resistant Enterobacterales isolates.

Of the 104 3GC-resistant Enterobacterales isolates included, E. cloacae complex was the most prevalent species with 38.5% of the isolates (40/104), followed by E. coli 19.2% (20/104), K. pneumoniae 19.2% (20/104), Morganella morganii 6.7% (7/104) and other species 16.3% (17/104). Resistance to 3GC was linked to ESBL in 61.5% of the isolates (64/104) and to hyperproducing AmpC in 38.5% (40/104).

Among the 104 3GC-R isolates, temocillin showed a susceptibility rate of 92.3% (96/104), with MIC ranging from 1 to 32 mg/L (Table 1). Among ESBL-producing isolates, 95.3% (61/64) were susceptible at increased exposure, compared to 87.5% (35/40) of AmpC-hyperproducing isolates (P = 0.26). At the species level, susceptibility rates were 92.5% for E. cloacae (37/40), 100% for E. coli (20/20) and 95.0% for K. pneumoniae (19/20). MIC50 and MIC90 for 3GC-R isolates were 4 mg/L and 16 mg/L, respectively. Among ESBL population, MIC50 and MIC90 were 4 mg/L and 16 mg/L, while among AmpC-hyperproducing isolates, MIC50 and MIC90 were 8 mg/L and 32 mg/L, respectively. No significant differences were observed in MIC distributions between ESBL- and AmpC-producing populations (P = 0.36).

Assuming a fa of 20%, in patients with normal renal function (GFR 90–120 mL/min), none of the tested regimen achieved acceptable PTA up to the Enterobacterales breakpoint of 16 mg/L. PTA > 90% was achieved up to MIC = 8 mg/L, only with the 6 g/day regimens. CFR values were < 90%, except if assuming fa = 40%. PTA results in patients with mild and moderate renal impairments (GFR 30–89 mL/min) were quite similar. For fa = 20%, PTA values remained insufficient for MIC = 16 mg/L, but were acceptable for MIC ≤ 8 mg/L with 6 g/day regimens, as well as prolonged infusion of 2 g every 12 hours. Discussion

This study evaluated the in vitro activity of temocillin against a large collection of Enterobacterales strains isolated from BJIs in three French reference centres, including both third-generation cephalosporin-susceptible (n = 26) and third-generation cephalosporin-resistant isolates (n = 104). In addition, the study modelled the probability of PK/PD target attainment in BJIs according to the dosage regimen used. The findings demonstrated (a) the high in vitro efficacy of temocillin against BJI isolates resistant to third-generation cephalosporins, regardless of the underlying resistance mechanism, (b) the overall high PTA for MIC values ≤ 8 mg/L, and (c) the need to adjust dosage according to the strain’s MIC and the patient's renal function.

Temocillin, a 6-α-methoxy derivative of ticarcillin, is usually recommended for a limited range of indications, including bacteriemia, urinary tract infections, skin and soft tissue infections, and lower respiratory tract infections in which Enterobacterales are suspected or confirmed as the causative pathogens. Despite its narrow spectrum of activity, temocillin has previously demonstrated good activity against ESBL, AmpC, and KPC-producing Enterobacterales.16 The present study is the first to investigate temocillin activity against such a large collection of BJI-associated strains, and to combine these results with modelling of the probability of therapeutic success in BJI based on the MIC distribution and the dosage used. We confirmed the high activity of temocillin against 3GC-R strains from BJIs, including both ESBL-producing and AmpC hyperproducing strains. All isolates included in this study were collected over seven years from three French centres located within the same region; therefore, the epidemiology and MIC distribution may be subject to regional and temporal bias. Nevertheless, the MIC50 and MIC90 values observed are consistent with previous studies on temocillin susceptibility, although those studies did not include BJI isolates.17–20 Assuming an active fraction of 0.2 in bone tissue, our results indicate that for MIC values ≤ 4 mg/L, all dosage regimens achieve the PK/PD target; for a MIC = 8 mg/L, a dosage regimen with 6 g/24 h (corresponding to ‘increased exposure’) is required to ensure acceptable PTA. However, for a MIC value of 16 mg/L, even high dosages (2 g q8h) were associated with low PTA in our PK/PD modelization, except in patients with severe renal impairment (of note, the dosages used for patients with severe renal insufficiency are generally 1 or 2 g/24 h). Those results suggest the potential value of individualizing temocillin dosage according to both renal function and measured MIC, and to consider another option in case of MIC ≥ 16 mg/L.

Temocillin could therefore be an interesting alternative to carbapenems for several reasons. Temocillin is stable towards AmpC enzymes and show a low propensity to select derepressed mutants, unlike certain beta-lactams that are strong inducers.21 Temocillin has a low impact on the gut microbiota, as shown in comparison to cefotaxime in the treatment of febrile urinary tract infections.22 Studies in healthy volunteers have shown no significant effect on total anaerobe counts, a decrease in Enterobacterales counts without selection of temocillin-resistant strains, and an increase in enterococci.23 Finally, clinical use has also shown a lower incidence of Clostridioides difficile infections compared to piperacillin-tazobactam.24 These advantages make temocillin a promising carbapenem-sparing antibiotic for the treatment of BJIs caused by 3GC-R Enterobacterales, with a narrower spectrum. Furthermore, temocillin is well-tolerated, with rare reports of adverse effects,9 which is important in the management of BJIs that typically require prolonged antimicrobial treatment.

We acknowledged several limitations in this work, and further studies are necessary to define the optimal conditions for the use of temocillin in BJIs. First, to date, there are no data on the pharmacokinetics of temocillin in joint fluid or bone tissue, which led us to assume an active fraction similar to that of other beta-lactams.25 Therefore, to confirm the potential use of temocillin in BJIs, studies are needed to determine its penetration into bone tissue and to refine the probability of target attainment with the dosages used in clinical practice. For infections other than BJIs, pharmacokinetic studies suggest that a target of ft > MIC = 40–50% correlates with efficacy and antibacterial activity. Monte Carlo simulations have shown that dosages of 2 g every 8 to 12 hours achieve > 95% probability of reaching this target for microorganisms with MICs ≤8 mg/L.23 These dosages are consistent with those generally used in previous clinical studies26 and with those used in our PK/PD simulations. Secondly, given the limited number of strains collected in this study, the MIC distribution is uncertain, as is the CFR. The third limitation is the limited clinical data available on the use of temocillin in BJIs and, in the available cases, the lack of specification of the isolates’ MIC values and the dosing regimens received by patients. This lack of detailed information impedes the formulation of precise guidelines for its clinical application and dosing strategies. Indeed, reports on temocillin use in BJIs are scarce. Very few case reports were published: a cervical spine infection with epidural abscess, caused by ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae, successfully treated with 3 weeks of imipenem, followed by 7 weeks of intravenous temocillin due to imipenem-induced neutropenia,27 or a case of Burkholderia cepacia cervical osteomyelitis successfully treated with temocillin.28 Three retrospective cohorts investigating the efficacy of temocillin against multidrug-resistant (MDR) Enterobacterales from diverse types of infections included BJI cases; one study reported clinical success in 5 of 6 cases,17 the second reported a failure for the only patient with BJI included,26 and the third reported clinical success in the 7 cases included.29 Interestingly, a recent study described the real-life use of temocillin in a French reference centre for BJIs: clinical cure was observed in 8 of 12 cases, with a median daily dose of 6 g of temocillin.30

In conclusion, this study highlights the high in vitro activity of temocillin against 3GC-resistant Enterobacterales isolated from BJIs, and the need to consider both the strain’s measured MIC for temocillin and renal function to determine the dosage regimen. In the absence of a measured MIC available, a high dosage of 6 g/24 h should be used to optimize PK/PD parameters, especially in patients with normal renal function. Clinical data on the efficacy of temocillin in BJIs are limited, but these microbiological results position temocillin as a promising alternative to carbapenems for this indication. However, further research, including in vivo models, clinical studies, and studies on bone tissue diffusion of temocillin is essential to confirm its role in these difficult-to-treat infections.
