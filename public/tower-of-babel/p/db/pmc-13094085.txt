Dual roles of GDNF in enteric glial cell plasticity: direct transdifferentiation via the CaMKII/NeuroD1 pathway and cooperative regulation in a neural stem cell-inducing medium
Authors: Wanying Jia, Hanlei Yan, Jingjing Huang, Wei Liu, Zihao Fu, Donghao Tian, Wenyao Xu, Xinlin Chen, Ya Gao, Hui Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHirschsprung disease (HSCR) is a congenital condition featuring aganglionosis in the distal colon, causing functional obstruction. While EGF and bFGF are well-characterized neurogenic factors, the precise mechanistic role of GDNF in modulating enteric glial cell plasticity remains incompletely understood.MethodsEGCs were identified via proteomic profiling and immunofluorescence in Ednrb⁻/⁻ mice modeling HSCR. EGC/PK060399egfr and primary EGCs were induced with neural stem cell–inducing medium (NSC-Med). Morphological changes, EdU assay, immunofluorescence, RT‒qPCR, and Western blotting were employed to assess the expression of stemness- and neuron-associated markers. Metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses were performed to evaluate metabolic remodeling and signaling pathways.ResultsFollowing treatment with NSC-Med, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that neurospheres expressed high proportions of Nestin-positive (97.09%), Sox2-positive (50.11%), and p75NTR-positive (77.87%) cells. Metabolomic profiling revealed a significant enhancement of the Warburg effect in the NSC-Med group. Western blot analysis further revealed elevated expression of PKM2, along with significant increases in both extracellular and intracellular lactate levels following NSC-Med treatment. NSC-Med treatment significantly enhanced proliferation, as demonstrated by a 2.3-fold increase in EdU incorporation (P < 0.05). Transcriptomic analysis revealed the activation of the calcium signaling pathway in the GDNF group. Western blotting revealed a significant increase in CaMKII phosphorylation, and treatment with the calcium chelator BAPTA-AM attenuated GDNF-induced NeuroD1 upregulation.ConclusionNSC-Med promotes stem cell-associated features and gene expression in enteric glial cells. GDNF—a key component of NSC-Med—activates a neurogenic cascade via the calcium signaling pathway (CaMKII-NeuroD1 axis), which offers a potential targeted molecular strategy for HSCR therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-04966-x.

Introduction

The enteric nervous system (ENS) serves as a pivotal regulatory network that governs essential gastrointestinal (GI) functions, including motility, secretion, nutrient absorption, and mucosal immunity [1, 2]. Disruptions in ENS development can precipitate a spectrum of GI disorders [3, 4], among which Hirschsprung disease (HSCR) represents a paradigmatic neurocristopathy. HSCR is pathologically defined by the congenital absence of enteric ganglion cells in the distal colon, resulting in functional obstruction and clinically presenting as refractory constipation, abdominal distension, and, in severe cases, life-threatening enterocolitis [5, 6].

To address these issues, we employed a combination of in vivo and in vitro models alongside multi-omics approaches. The endothelin receptor B knockout (Ednrb−/−) mouse has emerged as a principal experimental model, as it faithfully reproduces the hallmark pathological features of human HSCR, most notably distal colonic aganglionosis caused by impaired ENS development. This well-characterized model system provides an invaluable tool for elucidating the disease pathogenesis and evaluating potential therapeutic interventions [7, 8].

In recent years, cell-based therapies have emerged as promising strategies to correct the pathological deficits in HSCR, attracting significant research interest [9–12]. Studies have demonstrated that transplanted stem cells can migrate directionally to aganglionic colon regions, differentiate into functional enteric neurons, and integrate with the host ENS to restore neural circuitry and motility [13–16]. These findings highlight the potential of regenerative approaches to overcome the limitations of conventional surgical interventions for HSCR.

Enteric glial cells (EGCs), essential regulators of the ENS, exhibit dual functionality in neural–immune crosstalk [17–20] and lineage plasticity, such as dedifferentiation into neural stem cells (NSCs) or functional neurons under specific stimuli [21–23]. Glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), a master regulator of neural regeneration, orchestrates fundamental NSC dynamics, including proliferation, migration, and differentiation, through activation of the RET/GFRα1 receptor signaling pathway [24–28]. Beyond its survival-promoting role, GDNF may act as a reprogramming factor to reverse the fate of terminally differentiated EGCs [29].

Our preliminary observations indicated that NSC-inducing medium (NSC-Med), composed of EGF, bFGF, and GDNF, was associated with enhanced stem cell-related features and gene expression in EGCs. While the roles of EGF and bFGF in neural stem cell maintenance have been extensively characterized [30], the specific contribution of GDNF within NSC-Med, as well as its potential effect when applied alone, remains insufficiently defined.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

Ednrb+/− transgenic mice (B6;129-Ednrbtm1Ywa/J) were kindly donated by Jiexiong Feng’s laboratory at Huazhong University of Science and Technology. Mice were housed under standard conditions (22–24℃, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water in the Laboratory Animal Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University. Ednrb−/− mice with white fur were used as the experimental group, and the control group was selected from the same litter of same-aged Ednrb+/+ gray-furred mice. Euthanasia was conducted via an overdose of isoflurane (5%) followed by cervical dislocation. The procedure was performed by trained personnel to ensure minimal distress. The experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Ethics Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Approval No. XJTUAE2023-2153). The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Cell culture

The rat EGC line EGC/PK060399egfr, derived from the jejunum region of Rattus norvegicus (ATCC, CRL-2690), which exhibits morphological and functional characteristics comparable to those of primary EGCs [31], was maintained in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cellmax) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin(NCM biotech) at 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL, 37℃ and 5% CO₂.

Primary EGCs were isolated from fetal rats. Pregnant rats were euthanized and fetal intestines were dissected and rinsed in ice-cold PBS (Servicebio). The tissues were minced, centrifuged (800 rpm, 3 min, 4℃), and digested with 0.25% trypsin (Gibco), 0.5% DNase I (BioFroxx), and 0.5% collagenase IV (BioFroxx) at 37℃ for 20 min [32]. The digestion was stopped with complete medium, and the mixture was filtered (300-mesh), then cultured for 1 week in serum-free DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco) containing bFGF (Gibco, 10 ng/mL), EGF (Gibco, 10 ng/mL), B27(Gibco), N2(Gibco), and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (NCM biotech) to remove myocytes [33, 34]. The cells were then maintained in the same glial medium as the cell line, with the medium changed every 2 days. Adherent myocytes were removed via brief trypsinization. Third-passage cells were used for experiments.

NSC-Med

Neural stem cell-inducing medium (NSC-Med) was prepared according to the literature method [35]: DMEM/F12 (Gibco) with B27 (Gibco), N2 (Gibco), GDNF (Gibco, 50 ng/mL), bFGF (Gibco, 20 ng/mL), EGF (Gibco, 20 ng/mL) and penicillin/streptomycin (NCM biotech). P1 and P2 representing cells cultured in NSC-Med for 3 days (initial reprogramming phase) and 7 days (advanced reprogramming stage), respectively.

Morphological observation

The cells were cultured in standard glial medium until they reached the appropriate density and then treated with NSC-Med for 3 days (P1) or 7 days (P2). Morphological changes were observed under a light microscope. The diameter and roundness of the neurospheres were measured to evaluate their morphological characteristics.

Immunofluorescence staining

The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, and sequentially incubated with goat serum, primary antibodies (4℃, overnight), and fluorescent secondary antibodies (2 h, room temperature). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Bioworld Technology), and images were acquired via a fluorescence microscope. The following markers were examined: S100β (66616-1-lg, Proteintech), Nestin (19483-1-AP, Proteintech), Sox2 (66411-1-lg, Proteintech), P75NTR (ab52987, Abcam), HuD (67835-1-lg, Proteintech), GFAP(GTX108711, GeneTex), Phox2b(No.F2798, Selleck), NeuN(ab177487, Abcam), and Tuj1 (GT11710, GeneTex).

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted via the Rapid RNA Extraction Kit (Mei5bio), and cDNA was synthesized via the Reverse Transcription Kit (Mei5bio). Quantitative PCR was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX Connect System with 2×SYBR qPCR Mix (ZOMANBIO). The primer sequences are listed in Table 1. Relative mRNA expression was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCT method, with β-actin serving as the internal reference. The data were obtained from at least three independent experiments.

Western blot (WB) assay

Total protein was extracted with RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors, and protein concentrations were determined via the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE, transferred onto membranes under cooling conditions, and blocked with 5% milk. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4℃ overnight, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized via chemiluminescence. Each experiment was repeated at least three times. The following antibodies were used: Sox2 (66411-1-lg, Proteintech), P75NTR (ab52987, Abcam), Vimentin (10366-1-AP, Proteintech),α-Tubulin (66031-1-lg, Proteintech),β-actin (66009-1-lg, Proteintech), PKM2(YM8043, Immunoway), PHOX2B(No.F2798, Selleck), phospho-Thr286-CaMKⅡα/δ(YP0042, Immunoway), CaMKⅡα/δ(YT0623, Immunoway), phosphor-Ser274-Neuro D (YP0473, Immunoway), Neuro D (YT3061, Immunoway) and Tuj1 (GT11710, GeneTex).

Lactate measurement

According to the instructions of the kit (S0208S, Beyotime), a standard calibration curve was first established via serial dilutions of lactate standards. For intracellular lactate measurement, the cells were harvested and lysed, followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant. For extracellular lactate, culture supernatants were collected directly and clarified by centrifugation. The samples were then subjected to microspectrophotometric detection, and the lactate concentrations were calculated on the basis of the standard curve.

EdU immunofluorescence staining

Cell proliferation was assessed via the incorporation of EdU. The cells were incubated with EdU staining solution (Elabscience) at a final concentration of 10 µM for 2 h at 37℃. After labeling, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Permeabilization was performed with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min, followed by fluorescent labeling using the Click-iT reaction mixture according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were captured via a fluorescence microscope.

Cell viability

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8).Primary EGCs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. At the end of treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well containing 100 µL of medium, followed by incubation at 37℃ for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the control group.

Primary EGCs were first expanded in the standard EGC culture medium containing 10% FBS until reaching the desired confluence. For the assay, cells in the control (CTRL) group were maintained in high-glucose DMEM to provide basal nutritional support, whereas cells in the experimental group were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with GDNF at the indicated final concentration. Both groups were treated for the same duration before CCK-8 measurements. The subsequent experimental grouping followed the same conditions.

Metabolomic analysis

UPLC-QTOF-MS technology was used to detect metabolites, SIMCA-P 14.1 was used to construct a PLS-DA model, differentially abundant metabolites with FC ≥ 1.5 or FC ≤ 0.67 and P < 0.05 were screened, and volcano diagrams were plotted. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database was used to annotate the pathways, and KEGG bar pathway diagrams and bubble diagrams were plotted.

Proteomic analysis

Distal colon tissues from wild-type and Ednrb⁻/⁻ mice were excised, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subjected to proteomic analysis. Intestinal proteins were extracted, quantified, reduced, alkylated, digested with trypsin, and labeled with TMTsixplex™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Labeled peptides were fractionated by high-pH reversed-phase chromatography and analyzed by LC–MS/MS on an Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The raw data were processed with Proteome Discoverer against the UniProt mouse reference proteome, and the differentially expressed proteins were identified.

Transcriptome sequencing

Primary EGCs were treated with GDNF (50 ng/mL) for 24 h, and total RNA was extracted via TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). The RNA concentration was assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. mRNA was enriched via oligo(dT) magnetic beads, fragmented, and reverse-transcribed into cDNA, followed by end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. The libraries were sequenced on the Illumina platform to generate paired-end reads. The raw data were processed for quality control, and the clean reads were aligned to the reference genome via HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM values, and differential expression analysis was conducted with edgeR.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.5 was used to conduct the data analysis. Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR). The Shapiro‒Wilk test was used to access the normality. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Proteomic and histological characterization of EGCs in HSCR model mice

To assess the characteristics of EGCs in the enteric segments of HSCR model mice (Ednrb−/−), whole colon tissues were extracted for proteomic analysis. Proteomic profiling revealed distinct molecular signatures between colon tissues from Ednrb−/− mice and wild-type (WT) controls. Principal component analysis (PCA) clearly revealed differences in the principal components between the two groups, indicating substantial differences in global protein expression patterns (Fig. 1A). Differential protein analysis revealed significant changes (P < 0.05, |log2FC| > 0.585), as visualized in the volcano plot (Fig. 1B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis highlighted several pathways related to neuronal function. Notably, neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions and serotonergic synapses were markedly downregulated in the HSCR model (Fig. 1C). Heatmap analysis of neuron-associated markers revealed pronounced downregulation in HSCR tissues. Although glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and S100β were not detected in the proteomic dataset, S100A6 and S100A10 were selected as surrogate glial markers. Among these proteins, S100A6 showed a moderate decrease in abundance but remained the most abundant marker detected, whereas S100A10 was not significantly different between the groups (Fig. 1D).

Immunofluorescence analysis further validated the proteomic findings. Double-label staining for the neuronal marker HuD (red) and the glial marker GFAP (green) was performed on stenotic colon segments from Ednrb−/− mice and the corresponding colons from WT controls (Fig. 1E). In Ednrb−/− mice, GFAP-positive cells were observed exclusively in regions containing HuD-positive cells. These results indicate the presence of a small population of EGCs within the transitional regions of HSCR mice.

NSC-Med intervention induces a neural stem cell-like phenotype in EGCs

To evaluate the biological effects of neural stem cell-inducing medium (NSC-Med), consisting of EGF, bFGF, and GDNF, EGC cell lines were cultured in complete NSC-Med or NSC-Med lacking either EGF/bFGF or GDNF. As shown in Fig. 2A, complete NSC-Med significantly increased the expression of stem cell-associated markers Vim (Ctrl: 1.00 ± 0.06 vs. NSC-Med-(EGF/bFGF): 3.84 ± 0.16 vs. NSC-Med(-GDNF): 1.92 ± 0.12 vs. NSC-Med: 4.28 ± 0.21) and Sox2 (Ctrl: 1.01 ± 0.17 vs. NSC-Med-(EGF/bFGF): 1.36 ± 0.01 vs. NSC-Med(-GDNF): 1.38 ± 0.12 vs. NSC-Med: 1.64 ± 0.07) compared with the control group. EGC cell lines were continuously cultured in NSC-Med, and the morphological and phenotypic characteristics of first- (P1) and second-passage (P2) cells were systematically evaluated. Light microscopy revealed that NSC-Med–treated EGCs exhibited marked morphological changes, transitioning from the original adherent monolayer growth pattern to suspended neurosphere-like structures (Fig. 2B). Although the average diameter and percentage of neurospheres did not significantly differ between P1 and P2 (P1: 49.05 ± 22.09 μm vs. P2: 52.28 ± 20.23 μm, P = 0.58; 0–50 μm: P1: 57.90% vs. P2: 54.55%, 50–100 μm: P1: 36.84% vs. P2: 42.42%, 100–150 μm: P1: 5.26% vs. P2: 3.03% ), circularity was notably increased in P2 spheroids (P1: 0.14 ± 0.12 vs. P2: 0.86 ± 0.06, P<0.0001, Fig. 2C). To characterize these structures further, immunofluorescence staining revealed elevated expression of the stem cell-associated markers Nestin, Sox2, and P75NTR in the NSC-Med group, whereas the CTRL group maintained high expression of the glial marker S100β (Fig. 2D). These findings indicate that NSC-Med treatment enhanced stem cell-associated gene expression in EGCs.

NSC-Med intervention induces phenotypic reprogramming of EGCs

To further evaluate the effects of NSC-Med on the phenotypic reprogramming of EGCs, we systematically assessed the expression of glial and neural stem cell markers at both the transcriptional and protein levels. Quantitative RT–qPCR analysis revealed that the expression of the glial marker Gfap was significantly downregulated following NSC-Med intervention (CTRL: 1.00 ± 0.11 vs. P1: 0.89 ± 0.08 vs. P2: 0.57 ± 0.09, P<0.01). In contrast, stem cell-associated markers including Sox2(CTRL: 1.00 ± 0.04 vs. P1: 5.65 ± 0.12 vs. P2: 3.50 ± 1.81, P<0.01), P75NTR (CTRL: 1.01 ± 0.19 vs. P1: 0.76 ± 0.03 vs. P2: 1.63 ± 0.31, P<0.01), and Vim(CTRL: 1.00 ± 0.08 vs. P1: 1.39 ± 0.06 vs. P2: 4.70 ± 0.40, P<0.0001) were significantly upregulated (Fig. 3A). Western blot experiments further confirmed these findings, showing that the protein levels of Sox2, P75NTR and vimentin were significantly elevated after NSC-Med intervention (Fig. 3B-C). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the induction of an NSC-like phenotype in EGCs: the percentage of Sox2-positive cells in neurospheres was 50.11% ± 18.62%, the percentage of Nestin-positive cells was as high as 97.09% ± 2.06%, and the percentage of P75NTR-positive cells was 77.87% ± 8.69% (Fig. 3D). Collectively, these results reveal that NSC-Med treatment reprograms EGCs toward a neural stem cell-like state.

NSC-Med intervention regulates the Warburg effect through metabolic reprogramming

Metabolomic analysis revealed that the metabolic profiles of the NSC-Med–treated group were significantly different from those of the control group. The R² and Q² values of the PLS-DA model were close to 1, and Q²>0.5 (Fig. 4A). Volcano plot analysis revealed significantly upregulated metabolites related to lipid biosynthesis and energy metabolism (red dots), whereas metabolites involved in amino acid and nucleotide metabolism were significantly downregulated (blue dots) (P < 0.05, |log2FC| > 0.585; Fig. 4B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that lipid metabolism pathways were markedly activated, whereas amino acid metabolism and mitochondrial metabolic pathways were significantly suppressed (Fig. 4C and E). Disease enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed metabolites revealed associations with Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and colorectal disorders (Fig. 4D). KEGG analysis of secondary metabolites further suggested that NSC-Med intervention reprograms metabolism by modulating the Warburg effect and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity (Fig. 4F). To validate these findings, we quantified lactate levels in primary EGCs. Both extracellular (CTRL: 100.00 ± 1.39 vs. P2: 106.30 ± 1.33, P<0.01) and intracellular lactate levels (CTRL: 100.00 ± 5.48 vs. P2: 190.50 ± 27.89, P<0.01) were significantly elevated in the NSC-Med–treated group compared with the control group (Fig. 4G). Additionally, Western blot analysis revealed that the expression of PKM2, a key enzyme in glycolysis, was markedly upregulated following NSC-Med intervention (CTRL: 1.00 ± 0.16 vs. P1: 3.50 ± 0.06 vs. P2: 3.75 ± 0.20, P<0.0001, Fig. 4H). These results indicate that NSC-Med promotes a metabolic shift toward enhanced glycolysis in EGCs.

NSC-Med enhances proliferative and neurogenic competence in EGC-derived cells

On the basis of the above findings, we hypothesized that EGCs may acquire neural stem cell-like properties following NSC-Med treatment. To test this hypothesis, we performed immunofluorescence staining in primary EGCs, which detected that the expression of the stem cell-associated markers p75NTR and SOX2 was markedly enhanced after culture in NSC-Med (Fig. 5A). We evaluated the effects of NSC-Med on the proliferation of EGC-derived cells. EdU immunofluorescence staining was performed in both the CTRL and P2 groups. Compared with the control, NSC-Med significantly increased cell proliferation, with a 2.3-fold increase (CTRL: 13.13 ± 2.69% vs. P2: 30.84 ± 12.18%, P < 0.05, Fig. 5B-C).

We next examined whether these cells exhibited enhanced neurogenic competence, one of the defining functional characteristics of neural stem cells, as indicated by the expression of the neuronal lineage-associated marker Phox2b. RT–qPCR demonstrated significant upregulation of Phox2b expression in NSC-Med–treated cells compared with the control group (CTRL: 1.00 ± 0.09 vs. P1: 1.69 ± 0.03 vs. P2: 1.20 ± 0.06, P < 0.05; Fig. 5D). This transcriptional induction was corroborated at the protein level by western blotting (Fig. 5E). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining showed a significant increase in the percentage of Phox2b-positive cells in the P2 group compared with the control group (CTRL: 14.49 ± 9.81% vs. P2: 39.44 ± 12.07%, P < 0.05, Fig. 5F), suggesting that NSC-Med treatment enhances the neurogenic competence of EGC-derived cells.

GDNF promotes the neuronal transdifferentiation of primary EGCs

To confirm the purity of the primary EGC culture, we performed immunofluorescence staining for the glial marker S100β and the stem cell-associated marker Sox2. As shown in Fig. 6A, more than 90% of the cultured cells were positive for S100β(92.15% ± 1.88%), while Sox2 expression was nearly absent (1.20% ± 0.27%). Under NSC-Med conditions containing EGF, FGF, and GDNF, EGCs exhibited upregulation of stem cell-associated genes. To dissect the individual contribution of each factor, we removed the canonical culture components EGF and bFGF, and applied GDNF as a single-factor intervention. CCK-8 assays revealed that 50 ng/mL GDNF significantly increased the viability of primary EGCs, which was identified as the optimal concentration (CTRL: 100.00 ± 9.47% vs. 25 ng/mL: 152.30 ± 20.15% vs. 50 ng/mL: 215.60 ± 32.27% vs. 100 ng/mL: 162.30 ± 45.05%, P < 0.001, Fig. 6B). RT–qPCR revealed that treatment with 50 ng/mL GDNF for 24 h significantly decreased Gfap expression(CTRL: 1.00 ± 0.01 vs. GDNF: 0.67 ± 0.09, P < 0.001) but increased Phox2b expression (CTRL: 1.00 ± 0.10 vs. GDNF: 2.27 ± 0.20, P < 0.0001,Fig. 6C). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining revealed that 72 h of GDNF treatment promoted neuronal transdifferentiation, as indicated by elevated expression of the neuronal markers NeuN (8.67 ± 5.40%) and Tuj1 (37.74 ± 12.60%, Fig. 6D-E). These results indicate that GDNF effectively activates neuronal gene transcription in primary EGCs, driving their transdifferentiation toward a neuronal fate.

GDNF activates calcium signaling and neuronal gene transcription in primary EGCs

After 24 h of GDNF stimulation in primary EGCs, transcriptomic profiling revealed that stem cell markers were not further upregulated, whereas neuronal structural and maturation-associated markers were significantly increased (|log2FC| > 2, P < 0.05, Fig. 7A). GO enrichment analysis highlighted terms such as nervous system development and neuronal regeneration, reflecting the differential expression of relevant genes (Fig. 7B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed several signaling cascades relevant to neural fate determination, including the calcium signaling pathway (P = 0.02) and neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction (P = 0.05, Fig. 7C). To further validate calcium signaling activation, western blotting was performed. While the total protein level of CaMKII did not significantly change (1.00 ± 0.25 vs. 1.11 ± 0.42), its phosphorylation markedly increased (1.00 ± 0.20 vs. 1.63 ± 0.43, P < 0.05, Fig. 7D-E).Similarly, RT–qPCR confirmed the transcriptional upregulation of the upstream signaling component Plcd3 and Slc8a3(1.00 ± 0.05 vs. 1.48 ± 0.07, 1.00 ± 0.06 vs. 1.50 ± 0.25) and the downstream transcription factor NeuroD1 (1.02 ± 0.21 vs. 1.45 ± 0.20, P < 0.05, Fig. 7F). To investigate whether GDNF regulates the expression of NeuroD1 through calcium signaling pathway, cells were treated with BAPTA-AM, a cell-permeable Ca²⁺ chelator that binds free Ca²⁺ and suppresses intracellular calcium signaling. Western blot analysis indicated that the levels of phosphorylated NeuroD1, total NeuroD1 and Tuj1were downregulated in GDNF + BAPTA group compared with the GDNF group (CTRL: 1.00 ± 0.17 vs. GDNF: 1.44 ± 0.20 vs. GDNF + BAPTA: 1.02 ± 0.25; CTRL: 1.00 ± 0.10 vs. GDNF: 1.52 ± 0.15 vs. GDNF + BAPTA: 1.25 ± 0.14; CTRL: 1.00 ± 0.19 vs. GDNF: 1.58 ± 0.08 vs. GDNF + BAPTA: 1.13 ± 0.23; Fig. 7G-H). Collectively, these findings indicate that GDNF stimulation promotes the transition of EGCs into an intermediate transdifferentiation state via the CaMKII/NeuroD1 pathway.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that NSC-Med, with GDNF as a critical component, promotes the acquisition of stem cell-like features in EGCs, accompanied by coordinated phenotypic and metabolic remodeling. Moreover, we identified the CaMKII/NeuroD1 axis as a novel signaling pathway mediating GDNF’s effects.

In this study, we integrated metabolomics with functional validation and revealed that NSC-Med reprogramming drives EGCs into a distinct anabolic–catabolic metabolic state, characterized by enhanced lipid degradation, preferential glycolysis, and redistribution of amino acid pools. These shifts represent adaptive strategies that sustain stemness, prime cells for differentiation, and enable responses to microenvironmental fluctuations. In contrast to glial cells, which primarily maintain homeostasis, stem cells demand dynamic and energy-intensive metabolic programs to support self-renewal and lineage commitment [36, 37]. Metabolomic profiling further revealed the suppression of mitochondrial function in NSC-Med–treated cells, suggesting reliance on glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation, which is consistent with the Warburg effect, where aerobic glycolysis rapidly generates ATP while sustaining biosynthesis and redox balance [38]. The resulting glycolytic flux enhances Yap/Tead signaling, thereby activating lineage-specific transcription factors in enteric neural crest cells [39, 40]. Consistent with reports by Mingyue Xu et al. [41–43]., our findings support aerobic glycolysis as a central characteristic for maintaining neural stem cell properties.

Our data not only demonstrate a shift toward glycolytic metabolism but also reveal the activation of calcium signaling. This interplay between metabolism and signaling is intriguing, as multi-omics data in this study reveal that GDNF-induced reprogramming is underpinned by a coordinated metabolic and signaling rewiring. The observed Warburg effect (Fig. 4) actively creates a permissive metabolic state for dedifferentiation by providing rapid ATP and biosynthetic precursors, while spatiotemporal Ca²⁺ oscillations (Fig. 7) function as precise regulatory signals that (1) modulate neurogenic transcription factors (CREB, NeuroD) [44–46], (2) recruit chromatin-remodeling complexes [47, 48], and (3) coordinate downstream genetic programs. Transcriptomic analysis confirmed the early activation of Ca²⁺ signaling post-GDNF treatment, revealing its role as a central integrator linking metabolic adaptation to transcriptional reprogramming. Critically, these parallel pathways converge on NeuroD1, demonstrating a unified mechanism where cellular energetics and signaling cascades cooperatively license phenotypic plasticity in the ENS.

As a key regulator of neural regeneration, GDNF has dual effects: in the CNS, it promotes neural stem cell differentiation and axonal remyelination via extracellular matrix remodeling, which significantly improving motor function after spinal cord injury [27, 49]; in the ENS, it enhances the proliferation, migration, and neuronal differentiation of enteric neural stem cells [35, 50, 51]. Traditionally, EGCs have been regarded as supportive cells lacking dedifferentiation potential [52]. However, recent studies have shown that EGCs with partial neuronal transcriptional features possess transdifferentiation capacity in chemical injury models [53–56]. Although glia-to-neuron conversion can also be achieved by modulating the NeuroD1/ASCL1/SOX2 network [57–60], its low efficiency limits its clinical application. A few days ago, Jessica L. Mueller and colleagues demonstrated that GDNF can act through the receptor NCAM1 in the absence of RET signaling, thereby partially restoring the neurogenic potential of EGCs in aganglionic segments [61]. This finding is consistent with our results, as both studies indicate that GDNF can redirect EGCs away from a glial fate toward neuronal differentiation. Our study further extends this observation by elucidating the downstream signaling and transcriptional regulatory mechanisms involved in this process. Our results demonstrate that when GDNF is combined with EGF/bFGF, it drives EGCs to acquire neural stem cell-like properties, including the upregulation of pluripotency genes, as well as enhanced proliferative and neurogenic potential. In contrast, GDNF stimulation promotes the transition of EGCs into an intermediate transdifferentiation state via the CaMKII/NeuroD1 pathway.

In this study, we established a GDNF-mediated, integration-free reprogramming system with three major advantages: (1) significantly improved reprogramming efficiency, (2) avoidance of tumorigenic risks by eliminating exogenous gene integration, and (3) the development of a novel “cell-free transplantation” strategy with superior microenvironmental adaptability and functional integration potential [16, 62, 63]. Based on these findings, future research should focus on (1) developing GDNF-releasing biomaterials to enhance the survival and functional integration of transplanted cells; (2) screening small-molecule compounds targeting metabolic pathways to further improve the efficiency of EGC dedifferentiation; and (3) establishing a patient-derived EGC cell bank to promote the clinical application of personalized therapeutic strategies. Pursuing these directions will accelerate the clinical translation of HSCR cell therapy and provide new hope for patients.

This study has several limitations that warrant further investigation. First, our findings are based on in vitro cell experiments, and the reparative effects of GDNF on the ENS need validation in HSCR animal models. Second, the downstream calcium signaling pathways regulated by GDNF remain incompletely defined, and their precise molecular mechanisms require further study. Third, EGCs are likely heterogeneous, and only specific subpopulations may respond to GDNF; identifying these key subgroups will be essential. Moreover, postnatal enteric Schwann cells, which outnumber EGCs, have been reported as potential sources for neuronal replenishment [64–66]. However, direct comparisons of dedifferentiation efficiency between Schwann cells and EGCs are lacking, and such studies could provide valuable insights for cell replacement therapy in HSCR.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study extends the role of GDNF from a survival factor to a potent reprogramming factor. Specifically, we propose that GDNF has dual roles: direct transdifferentiation of enteric glial cells via the CaMKII/NeuroD1 pathway, and cooperative regulation in a neural stem cell-inducing medium.
