Life expectancy estimation in older men using the prostate cancer comorbidity index in VA & SEER-Medicare cohorts
Authors: John Heard, Michael Luu, I-Chun Thomas, John Leppert, Timothy Daskivich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuidelines endorse specific life expectancy (LE) cutoffs for triage of definitive local treatment of prostate cancer, but the lack of validated, prostate cancer-specific LE prediction tools limits incorporation of LE in management decisions.ObjectiveWe sought to provide long-term LE predictions in older men using the Prostate Cancer Comorbidity Index (PCCI), a validated tool for prediction of other-cause mortality based on age and presence and severity of major comorbidities, in nationally representative cohorts of US prostate cancer patients.Design, Setting, SubjectsWe performed an observational study of 916,890 men in the SEER-Medicare database and 243,928 men in the VA diagnosed with clinically localized prostate cancer between 2000 and 2019.MethodsPCCI scores were calculated using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes. Kaplan Meier and multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis were used to measure overall survival by age-adjusted PCCI score groups.ResultsMedian follow up was 11 years in both cohorts. In SEER-Medicare, men with PCCI scores of 1–2, 3–4, 5–6, 7–9, and 10+ had median estimated LE (95%CI) of 16.6 (16.5–16.7), 12.2 (12.1–12.2), 10.7 (10.6–10.7), 9.3 (9.2–9.3), and 5.4 (5.3–5.4) years, respectively. Introduction

Life expectancy (LE) estimation is vital for guidelines-concordant management of men with localized prostate cancer (PC). Men with lower LE are less likely to live long enough to accrue a survival benefit from aggressive treatment [1, 2], incur worse treatment-related morbidity [3], and have lower treatment effectiveness [4]. As a result, NCCN guidelines recommend consideration of conservative management of men with less than 15, 10, 10, and 5 years for men with low-, favourable intermediate-, unfavourable intermediate-, and high-risk disease, respectively. EAU and AUA guidelines similarly endorse conservative management for men with limited longevity based on tumour risk [5, 6]. Despite the availability of clear guidelines and LE nomograms, overtreatment has persisted in the active surveillance era, with increases in overtreatment of men with limited LE in men with favourable intermediate-, unfavourable intermediate-, and high-risk disease, though treatment of men with low-risk disease has decreased across the board for all men [7]. These findings were also observed in populations from Sweden [8].

Difficulty calculating LE and integrating these estimates into clinical decision making are contributing factors to the persistence of overtreatment. A national survey of radiation oncologists and urologists in 2013 found that only 23% used LE estimate tools in practice [9]. There are likely a number of reasons for this, chief of which being the difficulty of use in the clinical environment as they can be cumbersome and time consuming. Additionally, we recently conducted semi-structured interviews of 20 multidisciplinary physicians who routinely counsel men with PC and found that 50% had concerns about the accuracy and variability of LE estimates [10]. The vast majority of physicians (95%) had low or moderate confidence in LE estimates and noted that validated LE prediction tools (45%) and data on LE variability (30%) would improve their confidence.

The prostate cancer comorbidity index (PCCI) is an externally validated tool for prediction of other-cause mortality in patients with localized PC based on age and presence and severity of comorbidities. It is based on data from 1598 men with PC from two VA medical centers, and was previously externally validated for prediction of all-cause and other-cause mortality in 181,009 national VA patients and 4,857 patients from the SEARCH database, respectively [11–13]. The PCCI was originally operationalized using ICD-9 claims codes [12]. This analysis has since been superseded due to the transition to the ICD-10 coding system, which occurred in the US in October, 2015 [14].

To answer clinicians’ desire for validated LE prediction with variability estimates, we sought to calculate LE estimates along with variability at the 10th, 25th, 75th, and 90th percentiles for each PCCI score in a large cohort of men from the VA with long-term follow up. Importantly, we also sought to operationalize the PCCI for using ICD-10 codes to improve utility in current clinical practice. We then applied the PCCI using updated codes to the SEER-Medicare database to calculate LE estimates and variability in a generalizable population of non-VA patients. We believe this additional validation in a nationally representative cohort more closely resembling patients in typical urology clinics and the inclusion of variability estimates will help physicians execute guidelines-concordant management for men with clinically localized PC and limited longevity.

Methods

Study cohort

Approval for the study was obtained from the VA Palo Alto Health Care System. Health data was obtained from the VHA CDW (Corporate Data Warehouse) to identify a national cohort of 243 928 men with clinically localized PC diagnosed between 2000 and 2019. Another cohort of 916 890 men with clinically localized PC diagnosed during the same period was identified in the SEER-Medicare database. All patients had at least 5 years follow-up. The study received IRB approval for the VA cohort (Stanford IRB #39077). The SEER-Medicare portion of the study was exempt from IRB review.

Primary predictors

The PCCI is an age-adjusted, weighted comorbidity index that predicts LE for men with PC, calculated by adding points for the patient’s age to the sum of weighted scores for each comorbid condition. The age score starts at 1 and increases by 1 for every 6 years over the age of 60. The weighted scores for each comorbid condition have been previously described [12] (Supplementary Table 1). PC is not included in the PCCI score calculation. Comorbidity information collected was within 12 months of PC diagnosis. Comorbidities were identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 claims codes from previously validated Charlson and Elixhauser classification systems (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2) [15].

Covariates

Patient age, date of diagnosis, race/ethnicity, and primary treatment were obtained from the national VHA CDW database. Clinical tumour data was abstracted using the American Joint Commission on Cancer staging criteria.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome of overall survival was defined as the time from PC diagnosis to death. This was determined from the VA Vital Status File which uses data from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid, the Social Security Administration, VHA patient treatment files and the National Cemetery Association. Overall and cause-specific mortality data was available as a data element within the SEER-Medicare database.

Statistical analysis

Baseline patient characteristics across categories of age-adjusted Prostate Cancer Comorbidity Index (PCCI) scores were compared separately in the SEER and VA databases. Differences across PCCI score groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-squared test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Overall survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with patients stratified by age-adjusted PCCI score categories. Differences in survival curves were evaluated using the log-rank test. We estimated LE as the timepoint at which Kaplan–Meier curves reached 50% mortality. For subgroups that did not reach 50% mortality by 20 years, LE was noted as 20+ years, which would make such estimates actionable for guidelines that use 20-year cutoffs. Within the SEER database, the incidence of mortality from causes other than PC was estimated using the cumulative incidence function, accounting for death from PC as a competing risk. Differences in cumulative incidence curves were evaluated using Grey’s k-sample test for equality of cumulative incidence functions. Furthermore, the association between PCCI score and other-cause mortality was examined using the Fine and Grey subdistribution hazards model, which appropriately accounts for competing risks by treating PC mortality as a competing event. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value <.05.

Discussion

Accurate prediction of LE is critical in the triage of patients with localized PC towards aggressive or non-aggressive treatment, but epidemiological data from both the US and Europe suggests that overtreatment of men with limited LE is worsening in the active surveillance era for men with high-grade disease [8, 16–18]. The PCCI is an externally validated age-adjusted comorbidity index that permits LE prediction up to 20 years after initial PC diagnosis. The current study expands the PCCI follow-up period to 20 years, operationalizes the PCCI for electronic health record (EHR) integration using ICD-10 codes, provides estimate variability for clinicians, and externally validates the PCCI in a national cohort of Medicare patients. These changes allow clinicians to improve LE prediction for patient triage and facilitates the integration of automated PCCI scoring into clinical practice.

Unlike the commonly used CCI that was designed to predict short-term LE for medical inpatients [19], the PCCI was developed and validated to predict long-term LE for patients with PC with a high degree of risk discrimination (C-index 0.773) [12]. In an effort improve the utility of the PCCI, we provided additional data not incorporated in its prior publications which address physician preferences for LE integration documented in the literature [10]. This includes: [1] providing LE estimates (rather than mortality estimates) by PCCI score and [2] providing variability of LE estimates for men in the 10th, 25th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of mortality for each PCCI score. We hope that providing long-term LE estimates in a format preferred by physicians will help facilitate shared treatment decisions that maximize patient quality of life and oncologic outcomes [20].

In this study we also sought to modernize the PCCI for EHR integration by validating LE predictions using ICD-10 claims codes. The PCCI was originally based on manual comorbidity assessment via chart review. We previously operationalized the PCCI using ICD-9 codes to reduce the burden of manual comorbidity abstraction; PCCI LE prediction remained robust when applied to a national population of 181 009 VA patients with PC [12]. In October, 2015 the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) mandated claims codes transition to the ICD-10 system necessitating an update of PCCI operation codes for use moving forward. Our data show that when using ICD-10 codes among patients diagnosed since 2015, the PCCI achieves similar risk discrimination to that observed prior to 2015 using ICD-9 codes and remains an accurate predictor of overall mortality.

One important limitation on analyses in VA patients, including our prior work, stems from differences in co-morbidities, sociodemographic status, and resources use between VA populations and the general public [21, 22]. However, prior work has demonstrated similar CCI mortality estimates between VA patients and cohorts more representative of the average US population [23, 24]. In order to address this potential limitation and perform additional external validation of the PCCI, we assessed the association between PCCI scores and LE in the SEER-Medicare database. This analysis found adequate dispersion of LE across PCCI scores. Compared with the VA population, patients with the same PCCI scores from the SEER-Medicare database had slightly longer LE estimates on average.

While it is known that longevity is critically important to help identify which patients are likely to live long enough to benefit from definitive treatment, overtreatment of localized PC has been well described in the literature [4, 7, 25, 26]. This concept is addressed in the NCCN guidelines recommending observation for patients with less than 10 years LE and very low, low, favourable-intermediate, or unfavourable-intermediate risk PC and for those with less than 5 years LE and high or very-high risk disease [27]. Despite these guidelines and those by AUA [6] and EAU [5], we recently showed that overtreatment has increased in the era of active surveillance for men with high-grade disease [7, 28]. Better incorporation of LE prediction tools, such as the PCCI, into clinical practice may help identify patients at risk for overtreatment.

Numerous factors likely contribute to PC overtreatment; however, we have focused our efforts on physician barriers to incorporation of LE into clinical decision making as these are potentially modifiable. Previous work has identified physician-patient communication of LE and limitations of available tools at POC as important barriers. In a study analysing recordings of treatment consultations for localized PC, physicians only provided specific LE estimates in 36% of consultations while completely omitting LE from discussion in 17% [29]. Physicians have identified patient receptiveness and anxiety as barriers to LE discussions [10], however when patients with PC were interviewed 87% believed LE discussions should be part of their consultations and 88% preferred LE communicated as a patient specific numeric estimate [30]. Physicians also identified limited prediction tools for longevity as a barrier to LE discussions. Many were concerned about tool validation and estimate variability while citing their training and time consuming calculation as reasons for not using LE tools [10]. Our data supporting long-term LE prediction using the PCCI may help, however more work needs to be done alleviate these concerns.

There are many PC-specific LE estimation tools available, however few address the concerns of the physician that limit their use in clinical practice [31–33]. Many of these tools are not operationalized for EHR integration with ICD codes, instead requiring manual extraction. They often do not report LE estimates and are therefore not optimized for clinical care which is based on LE cutoffs. The commonly used NCCN method requires physicians to make a gestalt assessment of who is in the highest or lowest quartiles of health and adjust their LE by 50% higher or lower; however, we have previously documented the high variability observed within the highest and lowest quartiles, even when identified in an objective manner. The PCCI, while not perfect due to the limitations discussed below, addresses each of these short-comings [10].

An important drawback of the PCCI and other diagnosis-based tools is the exclusion of other factors known to impact LE, such as functional status, social determinants of health, and self-reported health status [34, 35]. Measures of cardiovascular health such as VO2max, routine clinical laboratory results, strength, functional status, engagement in harmful behaviours, and psychiatric health also play a role, but are not accounted for in the PCCI [36–38]. Assessments for these factors may be used in conjunction with the PCCI, however validation will be necessary. Additional tools may be helpful for geriatric patients and those with greatly reduced LE, since the PCCI was developed and validated in populations with localized prostate cancer—who on average have LE of 10–20 years—to predict long-term outcomes. This is in contrast to the Charlson Comorbidity Index, for example, which was created and validated on medical inpatients to predict 1-year mortality. Similarly, the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool (SPICT) and ‘the surprise question’ are highly specific for patients at risk of dying within 1 year [39, 40]. A number of tools for frailty assessment are also available [41–43]. These and other tools may assist clinicians in treatment decisions for geriatric patients which can be particularly challenging due to increased variability in functional status and independence, though in the ideal these frail patients would not have been screened for prostate cancer in the first place.

While the guidelines-based cutoffs for aggressive local treatment are quite distinct (e.g. less than 10 year life expectancy), real-world implementation of LE prediction tools such as the PCCI into clinical decision making offers some challenges due to variability of estimates. Physician reported barriers to LE implementation include the applicability of such population-based LE estimates to individuals [10]. This is exemplified by the wide interquartile range of LE estimates for patients with PCCI scores ranging 4–9, which often span the pivotal 10 year estimate at which definitive PC treatment may be deferred. The factors discussed in the previous paragraph (e.g. functional status, biomarkers, disease severity) can help the physician judge where a patient is likely to fit within the spectrum of longevity observed within a given PCCI score, and the variability estimates themselves can even be discussed when counselling the patient. It should also be noted that treatment benefits of localized prostate cancer increase slowly with time [44], so the marginal benefits of treatment in a patient with intermediate risk disease whose predicted LE is 9 years but actually lives 12 years are likely to be small, even if their longevity exceeded the guidelines-based LE cutoff for treatment of 10 years. Ultimately, a patient’s LE estimate is one component of the shared-decision making paradigm for PC treatment decisions, which include clinical factors, physician judgment, and patient preferences.

Limitations of this study include its methodology and those of the PCCI specifically. The PCCI by design incorporates only age and comorbidities to facilitate LE estimation in clinical practice, however other factors that are not included such as functional status, risk behaviour, secondary cancer details, social determinants of health, and others may be relevant to mortality prediction. Additionally, while the PCCI integrates severity of liver, renal, pulmonary disease, and diabetes, the spectrum of comorbidity severity for all conditions that may impact LE is not captured. Another limitation is the use of data exclusively from the US. The characteristics of the US claims-based healthcare system and health challenges within the US may limit the applicability of these findings to populations in other countries. Importantly, while population LE prediction may inform policy and guidelines decisions, communication of LE and its incorporation into treatment decisions for individuals is a challenge that requires further resources.

Conclusion

The PCCI is a robust predictor of LE in men both the Medicare and VA systems with clinically localized PC up to 20 years after their initial diagnosis. The PCCI can be calculated using ICD-10 claims codes, which allows for EHR integration using contemporary claims reporting. LE predictions are provided with variability estimates to allow for physicians to judge the actionability of predictions based on patient or provider preferences for risk tolerance. Integration of claims-based measures such as these into EHRs has potential to improve integration of LE into PC treatment decisions and increase guideline-concordant care.
