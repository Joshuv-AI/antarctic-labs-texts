Real-world treatment patterns and outcomes in moderate-to-severe SLE: findings from the Spanish SPOCS cohort
Authors: Nuria Lozano-Rivas, Iñigo Rúa-Figueroa, Mercedes Freire-González, Ana Turrión, Iván Castellvi, Julia Barbado Ajo, Jaime Calvo-Alén, Norberto Ortego-Centeno, Monserrat Díaz-Encarnación, José María Pego-Reigosa, Bo Ding, Marta Galvez-Fernandez, Noemí Bahamontes-Rosa, Ricard Cervera, The Spanish SLE Prospective Observational Cohort Study (SPOCS)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis analysis describes disease activity, organ damage and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in patients with active SLE and moderate-to-severe disease activity from the Spanish SLE Prospective Observational Cohort Study (SPOCS) cohort.MethodsSPOCS (NCT03189875) is a multicentre, prospective, observational cohort that followed-up adults with SLE every 6 months for up to 3 years. Disease activity was assessed using SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K) and Physician Global Assessment. Remission and Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS) definitions were therapeutic objectives; organ damage by the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics/American College of Rheumatology Damage Index (SDI); HRQoL by Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy-Fatigue (FACIT-F), Patient Health Questionnaire-8 (PHQ-8) and EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L). Patients were recruited from 18 Spanish sites (2017–2019).Results99 patients were enrolled. At baseline, median (IQR) SLEDAI-2K was 8 (7-12); 45% of patients had organ damage (mean (SD) SDI 1.1 (1.6)). Remission was achieved by 13% at 6 months and at 24 months; sustained remission occurred in 9%. 24% achieved LLDAS at 6 months and ≤22% thereafter; 8% maintained LLDAS for ≥70% of follow-up. Median SLEDAI-2K declined to 4 at 6 months. New or worsening organ damage occurred in 11%, 8% and 12% of patients at 12, 24 and 36 months, respectively; mean SDI (SD) increased to 1.8 (2.0) by 36 months. Musculoskeletal, cutaneous, neuropsychiatric and renal systems were most frequently involved. HRQoL remained poor: 60%–75% had depressive symptoms (PHQ-8 ≥5), 35%–50% moderate-to-severe depression (PHQ-8 ≥10), mean FACIT-F ≈30 (indicating persistent severe fatigue) and mean EQ-5D-5L index 0.70–0.75. Pain/discomfort affected up to 76% and anxiety/depression up to 66% of participants.ConclusionsIn Spanish clinical practice, sustained remission and low disease activity state remain uncommon in moderate-to-severe SLE. Nearly half had organ damage at baseline, with continued accrual and impaired HRQoL. These findings highlight ongoing challenges in achieving lasting disease control and underscore the need for broader adoption of treat-to-target approaches to improve outcomes and prevent irreversible organ damage in this patient population.Trial registration numberNCT03189875.Keywords: Lupus Erythematosus, Systemic; Health-Related Quality Of Life; Therapeutics

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

It is common knowledge that ongoing disease activity and glucocorticoid use in SLE lead to irreversible organ damage, and that achieving remission or low disease activity helps prevent flares and further damage and reduces mortality. However, implementing treat-to-target (T2T) approaches in routine practice has been limited, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) remains poor for many patients.

Introduction

Despite advances in the understanding of immunopathogenesis and improved treatments, SLE continues to be associated with substantial morbidity and mortality.1 Persistent disease activity and the cumulative effects of immunomodulators, in particular glucocorticoids, contribute to irreversible organ damage, a major determinant of long-term prognosis.1 2 Organ damage accrual is associated with higher mortality, reduced health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and increased healthcare costs.3 4 Accordingly, minimising disease activity while preventing damage has become a central goal of modern lupus care.

The treat-to-target (T2T) strategy has been proposed for SLE to optimise clinical outcomes.5 6 T2T has been extensively applied in the management of chronic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperuricaemia and hyperlipidaemia, where specific quantitative parameters serve as therapeutic goals.6 Nevertheless, applying T2T principles to autoimmune diseases is a more complex proposal, as the targets are composite indices integrating multiple clinical and laboratory parameters that act as proxies for overall disease activity.5 6 Within this framework, two therapeutic endpoints have gained broad acceptance in SLE management: remission, as defined by the Definition of Remission in SLE (DORIS) criteria; and the Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS) for patients who do not achieve remission.5,9 Meeting these targets is associated with a lower risk of flares, reduced damage accrual and improved survival.10 11 In routine clinical practice, however, maintaining patients in these optimal states remains challenging due to the complex and fluctuating nature of SLE, treatment limitations and variability in clinical decision-making. For these reasons, T2T is an essential, although insufficiently implemented, strategy in real-world settings.9 10

In addition to clinical measures, patient-reported outcomes (PROs) are essential for a holistic understanding of the impact of SLE.12 Fatigue, pain, emotional distress and impaired functioning are common and persistent features of SLE that profoundly affect the daily lives of patients.13 Integrating PROs, such as fatigue, physical functioning and mental health, into disease monitoring allows for a more comprehensive assessment of treatment effectiveness and supports shared decision-making between patients and clinicians.14 Improving HRQoL is therefore recognised as a key component of optimal disease control.14

Real-world studies on disease burden in SLE are needed to assess how new treatments influence disease status and the HRQoL of patients, and to evaluate the validity of the T2T approach. The SLE Prospective Observational Cohort Study (SPOCS) was designed to describe real-world disease burden, treatment patterns and outcomes in patients with active SLE and moderate-to-severe disease activity. Previous analyses from the global SPOCS cohort have provided valuable insights into disease activity and treatment use across different healthcare settings.15 This study provides an integrated view of disease control targets, organ damage and PROs among patients with SLE in Spain.

Methods

Study design

SPOCS (NCT03189875) is an international, multicentre, prospective observational cohort study designed to describe the clinical features, disease activity, treatment patterns, organ damage and PROs of patients with active SLE and moderate-to-severe disease activity.16 This analysis focuses on the Spanish population from the SPOCS cohort. Participants were recruited from 18 sites across Spain between October 2017 and November 2019 and followed two times a year for up to 3 years (follow-up was completed in 2022). Part of the follow-up period overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic, which affected routine clinical practice across participating centres. The index date was defined as the routine visit during which the patients signed the informed consent.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with European Union General Data Protection Regulations (GDPR).

Eligibility criteria

Eligible participants were adults ≥18 years with a physician-confirmed diagnosis of SLE according to the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) or the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) classification criteria. Patients were required to have active disease, defined as a modified SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K) score of ≥4 excluding points from urine, immunological measures and lupus headache, or a total SLEDAI-2K score of ≥6. All participants had positive serology for ANAs or anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies and had received at least 6 months of systemic therapy for SLE other than non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or analgesics.

Patients with active severe lupus nephritis requiring induction therapy at baseline were excluded, as were those with severe concomitant conditions that could interfere with disease assessment, follow-up or treatment.

Outcomes and assessments

At baseline and every 6 months thereafter, investigators recorded detailed demographic, clinical and laboratory data. Disease activity was assessed using the SLEDAI-2K, clinical SLEDAI-2K and the Physician Global Assessment (PGA) on a 0–3 visual analogue scale (VAS).17,19 The SLICC/ACR Damage Index (SDI) was used to quantify irreversible organ damage accrued since SLE diagnosis.

Information on medication use, including antimalarials, immunosuppressants, biologics and oral glucocorticoids (OGC), was collected at each visit. OGC doses were expressed as mean daily prednisone-equivalent doses, and cumulative exposure over time was calculated for each participant. PROs were obtained using validated instruments, including the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy–Fatigue (FACIT-F), Patient Health Questionnaire-8 (PHQ-8) and EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L) index.20,22 FACIT-F <30 indicates severe/intense fatigue. Depressive symptoms were categorised according to PHQ-8 cut-offs, with scores ≥5 indicating at least mild depressive symptoms and scores ≥10 representing clinically relevant depression. The EQ-5D-5L is a standardised generic instrument for assessing HRQoL and has been validated for use in patients with SLE.22 The EQ-5D-5L was analysed according to its five dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression) and summarised using the utility index score (for which no categorical cut-offs are defined; range <0–1, where one indicates full health and values below 0 represent health states considered worse than death). Utility index scores closer to 1 indicate better perceived health status.

Remission was defined according to the 2021 DORIS criteria.7 Sustained remission was defined as maintaining remission for at least 50% of the follow-up time. Patients met the LLDAS definition when they had total SLEDAI-2K ≤4, no new SLEDAI-2K items since preceding time point, no major organ activity, PGA VAS score ≤1 and OGC ≤7.5 mg/day.8

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as absolute and relative frequencies (percentages), and continuous variables as means and SD or median and IQR. In the event that a patient was at risk of loss to follow-up, at least three contact attempts were made; missing data were not included in the denominator for summary analyses.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R V.4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

The analyses included 99 participants with a mean (SD) age of 45.0 (12.02) years, of whom 92% were women. The mean age at SLE diagnosis was 33.4 (13.4) years, with an average disease duration of 11.6 (10.0) years. A high interferon signature was observed in 77% of participants. The complete baseline data are summarised in online supplemental table 1.

Remission and disease activity

Remission was achieved by fewer than 13% of patients at any time point (figure 1). At 6 months, 12.6% of patients were in remission, 5.3% at 12 months, 5.8% at 18 months, 12.7% at 24 months and 9.8% at 36 months. A total of 8.6% of patients maintained remission over time.

LLDAS was achieved by fewer than 24% of patients at any assessment. The proportion of patients in LLDAS was 23.5% at 6 months, 8.0% at 12 months, 15.4% at 18 months, 14.3% at 24 months and 22.0% at 36 months. Sustained LLDAS was achieved by 15% of patients and 8% maintained LLDAS for more than 70% of their follow-up.

Between 3% and 14% of patients presented flares during the study period (figure 2). Among the 41 patients who experienced a flare at baseline, 6 (14.6%) suffered more than one severe flare. Over the course of the study, up to 50% of the flares were classified as severe according to SELENA (Safety of Estrogens in Lupus Erythematosus National Assessment)-SLEDAI criteria.

Burden of flares in the Spanish patients of the SPOCS study. SPOCS, SLE Prospective Observational Cohort Study.

Disease activity improved markedly after baseline and then remained variable but generally low-to-moderate over follow-up (table 1). Median (IQR) SLEDAI-2K decreased from 8 (7–12) at baseline to 4 (0–6) at 6 months; it was 5 (2–8) at 12 months; and remained at 4 from 18 months to 36 months. In parallel, the proportion of patients with SLEDAI-2K <6 increased from 1% at baseline to 64% at 6 months and thereafter remained within 50–70% across visits. Approximately 30–50% of the cohort continued to show moderate-to-severe activity (SLEDAI-2K ≥6) at each time point.

Organ damage

At baseline, 45% of patients had accumulated organ damage and the mean (SD) SDI score was 1.1 (1.6) (table 2). Over the 3-year follow-up, the mean (SD) SDI increased progressively to 1.8 (2.0) at 36 months. Within the first 12 months, 11% of patients developed new or worsened organ damage; similar rates were observed at 24 months (8%) and 36 months (12%). By the end of follow-up, 45% of the cohort presented an SDI greater than 1.

The organ systems most frequently affected were the musculoskeletal, cutaneous, neuropsychiatric and renal systems (online supplemental table 2). Musculoskeletal involvement included deforming or erosive arthritis in 11% of patients, osteoporosis in 5% and avascular necrosis in 4% of patients at baseline, with slightly higher frequencies at 12 months (13%, 6% and 8%, respectively). Neuropsychiatric manifestations, such as cognitive impairment and cerebrovascular events, were present in 9% and 7% of patients at baseline and persisted at 12 months (8% each). Skin damage, mainly scarring chronic alopecia, affected 5% of patients at baseline and 2% at 12 months. Renal complications, including proteinuria greater than 3.5 g per 24 hours, were observed in 4% of patients at baseline and 8% at 12 months. Additional damage, such as premature gonadal failure (6%) and malignancy (1%), was also noted.

Health-related quality of life and patient-reported outcomes

PROs measures (PROMs) revealed persistent impairment in HRQoL during the study period (table 3). According to the PHQ-8 questionnaire, between 60% and 75% of patients presented depressive symptoms (PHQ-8 ≥5) during the study period. Moderate-to-severe depression (PHQ-8 ≥10) was observed in 35–50% of patients at the different visits. Mean PHQ-8 scores remained above 7 throughout the 3 years of follow-up, confirming the persistence of psychological distress.

Fatigue was also highly prevalent among the study population. The mean (SD) FACIT-F was 31.4 (11.5) at baseline, 30.7 (13.0) at 6 months, 32.0 (11.6) at 12 months, 33.6 (11.1) at 18 months, 31.7 (12.6) at 24 months and 29.7 (12.6) at 36 months, consistently indicating severe levels of fatigue. At each assessment, approximately 45–51% of patients had FACIT-F scores≤30, demonstrating a substantial and persistent burden of fatigue over the entire follow-up period.

The EQ-5D-5L index showed a sustained reduction in HRQoL. The mean (SD) index value ranged between 0.70 (0.28) and 0.75 (0.21), well below normative population values. Specifically, pain and discomfort were reported by the highest proportion of patients, with up to 76% of patients experiencing these symptoms and between 53% and 66% reporting anxiety or depression. Limitations in mobility (36–45%), usual activities (53–64%) and self-care (19–30%) were also common, confirming a sustained impact of SLE on daily functioning and well-being.

Discussion

The findings from the Spanish population of the SPOCS study provide an updated view of the clinical status and disease control of patients with active SLE and moderate-to-severe disease activity in routine practice. The results show that remission and LLDAS were achieved by a minority of patients, that organ damage remained frequent and progressive, and that physical and psychological symptoms continued to impose a significant burden over time. The first subanalysis of the Spanish SPOCS cohort, which focused on OGC use, showed that many patients continued to receive doses above those now advised by the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) 2023 and the ACR 2025 recommendations, highlighting an unmet need for effective glucocorticoid-sparing strategies.23,25 Overall, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of disease control and patient burden in Spanish patients with moderate-to-severe SLE.

Achieving sustained remission in SLE remains a major challenge. As noted in this study, target attainment was uncommon and often not durable over the follow-up period, with prior data indicating that only about one-third of patients are able to maintain these targets over time.26 The duration of remission or LLDAS is crucial in clinical terms, as longer persistence in these states has been strongly associated with reduced cumulative organ damage.27 Preventing damage is of utmost importance, as it tends to develop early in the disease course and correlates with poorer prognosis.27 The low frequency of DORIS remission and LLDAS attainment observed in the Spanish SPOCS cohort is consistent with the overall SPOCS disease progression analysis, which included 826 patients from eight countries and represents the broader international SPOCS population. In that analysis, DORIS remission and LLDAS were achieved by fewer than 15% and fewer than 20% of patients, respectively, at any time point.28 In line with this broader SPOCS pattern, the proportions of Spanish patients achieving DORIS remission (5%–13%) or LLDAS (6%–23%) remained limited throughout the 36-month follow-up. These results are notably lower than those reported in both clinical trials and real-world cohorts.29,41 In randomised controlled trials, remission rates of up to 30–37% and LLDAS attainment ranging from 12% to over 50% have been reported depending on study design, therapy and duration. Real-world evidence from large cohorts has shown higher rates as well: patients in the Hopkins and Asia Pacific Lupus Collaboration cohorts achieved remission on treatment for approximately 27–36% of follow-up time,37 38 while the Peking University and Pisa cohorts reported remission in 30–45% of patients and LLDAS attainment in up to 50% of follow-up.39 40 Moreover, a recent Polish 5-year real-world study41 reported substantially higher proportions, with 55.7% of patients achieving DORIS remission and 61.4% meeting LLDAS criteria, underscoring the variability in disease control across populations. The comparatively low remission and LLDAS rates observed in our cohort could likely reflect higher baseline disease activity. Other contextual factors, such as treatment availability and the absence of a structured T2T approach in routine care, may also have contributed. Persistent glucocorticoid exposure may have been another relevant barrier, since OGC thresholds are embedded within both DORIS remission and LLDAS definitions. In the Spanish SPOCS glucocorticoid analysis, 7–37% of glucocorticoid-treated patients received >5 mg/day to ≤7.5 mg/day, and 21–54% received >7.5 mg/day at several timepoints during follow-up, which may have contributed to failure to meet T2T endpoints.23 This interpretation is supported by the recent international T2T framework proposed by Piga et al,9 which highlights that, despite over a decade of advocacy, implementation of T2T in SLE remains limited. The task force identified irregular monitoring, persistent glucocorticoid overuse and insufficient treatment adaptation as key barriers, and proposed a practical framework comprising five overarching principles and 11 actionable statements to guide daily practice. Our findings align with these observations and emphasise the need to translate T2T concepts into structured, real-world management protocols. Strengthening systematic monitoring, standardising therapeutic algorithms and incorporating PROs into clinical follow-up are crucial steps toward achieving this goal. In line with the most recent EULAR 2023 and ACR 2025 recommendations, remission or alternatively the LLDAS should be pursued as mandatory therapeutic targets in all patients with SLE.24 25 The integration of a structured T2T approach, centred on early disease control, regular assessment and timely treatment optimisation, has the potential to prevent irreversible organ damage, improve long-term survival and enhance HRQoL for individuals living with SLE.

Another potential factor that may explain the comparatively low remission and LLDAS rates observed in our cohort is the limited use of biological therapies. At that time, only two biological options were available in clinical practice, whereas anifrolumab had not yet been approved. In this study, only 17.2% of patients were receiving belimumab (11.1%) or rituximab (6.1%) at baseline. Recent publications from the Spanish clinical setting indicate higher remission rates among patients treated with belimumab or anifrolumab42 43; however, these estimates are derived from cohorts selected for and retained on biological therapy and are therefore not directly comparable with cases observed in an unselected, real-world population such as ours. Taken together, these findings reflect the therapeutic landscape and treatment strategies in place during the study period and highlight the challenges in achieving sustained disease control in routine clinical practice. The evolving evidence, updated recommendations and the growing availability of targeted therapies all suggest that the earlier incorporation of biological agents within a structured T2T framework could significantly improve remission and LLDAS rates and ultimately enhance long-term outcomes for patients with SLE.

The prevalence and accrual of organ damage in our cohort were comparable to those reported in recent population-based studies, although important contextual differences should be considered. At baseline, 45% of our patients had irreversible damage in at least one organ (mean SDI=1.1), increasing to 1.8 after 36 months. This rate lies between the lower prevalence observed in the Saudi Arabian cohort by Alharbi et al44 (38.8%) and the higher proportions reported in large European registry analyses such as the 60–83% reported by Schultze et al45 in the German claims-based study and the 65% described by Gómez et al46 using the Swedish RBODI (Register-Based Organ Index). The differences are likely multifactorial. Our population had a shorter observation period and a relatively younger age at diagnosis, whereas registry-based cohorts typically include longer disease durations and capture accumulated preindex damage. Moreover, organ-specific patterns diverged: musculoskeletal and neuropsychiatric involvement predominated in our cohort, while ocular and cardiovascular domains were more frequent in the Scandinavian and German data, possibly reflecting ethnic and lifestyle variations or differences in coding sensitivity between clinical and administrative sources.45 46 Treatment exposure also differed across studies and may have influenced damage trajectories. Biological therapy use in our cohort was 17%, nearly 20-fold higher than in Schultze et al and fourfold higher than in Gómez et al, probably because we recruited patients from tertiary rheumatology centres with more active disease profiles.45 46 In the European registry cohorts, biological therapy use was low, and the majority of patients were treated primarily with glucocorticoids and conventional immunosuppressants.45 46 The heavy reliance on glucocorticoids in those settings may partly explain their higher burden of irreversible organ damage, consistent with the established association between steroid exposure and long-term organ injury. Recent data from pivotal biological studies further support the notion that biological therapy can meaningfully modify long-term damage trajectories in SLE.47 48 In real-world comparative analyses, treatment with targeted biological agents has been associated with a lower mean SDI increase over time compared with standard of care.47 48 Together, these findings indicate that biological agents may offer genuine disease-modifying potential by preventing or delaying irreversible organ injury. Although the available data did not allow us to distinguish damage accrual among patients with and without baseline damage, the progressive increase in SDI observed throughout follow-up and the occurrence of new or worsened damage at each timepoint reinforce the importance of early disease control and damage prevention strategies in routine SLE management.

HRQoL remained noticeably impaired throughout the study, despite partial improvements in disease activity. Pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression were the most frequently affected EQ-5D-5L dimensions, reported by most patients across visits, indicating sustained impairment in perceived health status. According to PHQ-8, between 60% and 75% of participants experienced depressive symptoms, and around half of patients presented moderate-to-severe depression. This was consistent with the elevated proportion of patients reporting anxiety/depression problems in the EQ-5D-5L, reinforcing the substantial psychological burden associated with SLE. Fatigue was also highly prevalent, confirming a persistent burden of tiredness and reduced vitality. Previous studies have shown that depression, anxiety, fatigue and glucocorticoid exposure are closely related to PROMs in SLE, sometimes more strongly than conventional measures of disease activity or accumulated damage.49 50 Our findings indicate that emotional distress, fatigue and functional limitations persisted over time, even when disease activity appeared to stabilise, highlighting the multifactorial nature of HRQoL impairment in SLE.

In the latest international recommendations (EULAR 2023 and ACR 2025), improving HRQoL is recognised as a fundamental objective of SLE management, together with preventing flares and organ damage.24 25 Furthermore, the recent Spanish Delphi consensus on PROMs in SLE emphasised that incorporating validated PROMs such as FACIT-F and EQ-5D into routine care helps to better capture the experience of the patient and support shared decision-making, leading to more holistic and patient-centred disease management.14 These results reinforce the importance of integrating PROMs and systematic HRQoL assessment into daily clinical practice for patients with SLE.

From a clinical perspective, the findings of the Spanish SPOCS study highlight the need to optimise current management strategies for SLE. Despite the availability of advanced therapies, most patients with moderate-to-severe disease did not achieve remission or sustained LLDAS, and many continued to experience organ damage and impaired HRQoL. In light of these results, there is a pressing need for healthcare systems to demonstrate a clear and structured commitment to the T2T strategy, which should be implemented as a standard of care, in accordance with the latest EULAR and ACR recommendations.24 25 Moreover, improved monitoring and systematic follow-up are essential to achieve better disease control and long-term outcomes. In this context, HRQoL assessment should be fully integrated into routine practice, using PROMs and other validated tools capable of capturing the full spectrum of disease burden and its impact on daily lives of the patients.

This study has several strengths. Patients were recruited from multiple centres across different regions of Spain, increasing the representativeness of the cohort. In addition, the longitudinal collection of clinical outcomes and PROMs, including EQ-5D-5L, PHQ-8 and FACIT-F, allowed an integrated assessment of disease control, organ damage, HRQoL, depressive symptoms and fatigue in routine clinical practice. This is particularly relevant because PROMs capture dimensions of SLE burden that are not fully reflected by conventional clinical activity or damage indices. The study has also several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The study included a relatively small sample of patients, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to the broader Spanish SLE population. Missing data were observed during follow-up, particularly in the later visits, partly due to the COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted routine clinical assessments and may have affected data completeness.

Conclusions

In SPOCS, sustained remission and low disease activity remained uncommon. Nearly half of patients had organ damage with continued accrual, indicating persistent activity despite treatment. Depression, fatigue and pain were highly prevalent, reflecting a substantial burden on HRQoL. These findings support implementing T2T strategies to improve disease control and prevent irreversible damage in moderate-to-severe SLE.
