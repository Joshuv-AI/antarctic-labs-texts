Development and validation of a nomogram for predicting advanced chronic kidney disease
Authors: Shuyu Huang, Lun Liu, Shuhang Huang, Shenhui Lin, Kaiwen Zheng, Ling Li, Jingyi Su, Jiahao Chen, Yanfen Zeng, Jianbin You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe objective of this study was to develop and validate a nomogram for predicting advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) through the utilization of routine clinical parameters. To achieve this, we integrated internal and external datasets, employed LASSO and logistic regression to identify independent predictors of advanced CKD for nomogram construction, conducted Bootstrap validation on the training set, and evaluated discrimination, calibration, and net clinical benefit utilizing both internal and external test sets. The results identified six predictors: serum creatinine, age, hematocrit, hypertension, serum ALT, and serum LDH. Furthermore, it was demonstrated that the nomogram exhibited excellent discrimination (AUC: 0.961/0.965/0.939) and good calibration (Brier score: 0.058/0.056/0.073) across the training, internal, and external sets, alongside significant net clinical benefit within the 0–1 threshold. Consequently, it was concluded that this robust and clinically practical nomogram facilitates accurate, individualized predictions of advanced CKD risk, thereby supporting precision kidney disease management.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) poses a substantial and growing global public health burden, with its progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) leading to high morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs.1,2 A critical challenge in managing CKD is the marked heterogeneity in disease progression among individuals, which underscores the urgent need for precise, early risk stratification to enable timely intervention.

Current CKD risk stratification primarily relies on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urinary albumin excretion rate (UACR).3,4 Yet, this framework provides a static, cross-sectional assessment that fails to capture the dynamic, multisystem pathophysiology of CKD progression.5,6,7,8 Thus, a comprehensive predictive tool integrating such multisystem information is essential for precise CKD diagnosis and treatment. Despite efforts to develop CKD predictive models and biomarkers, critical gaps remain: many models lack rigorous external validation (limiting generalization),9,10 rely on costly novel markers (restricting clinical applicability),11 or focus solely on discrimination (neglecting calibration and net clinical benefits, undermining decision support).12

To address these limitations, this study aims to develop and validate a nomogram based on routine clinical data. This tool will offer individualized risk prediction with high accuracy, robustness, and clinical applicability, shifting advanced CKD management from passive response to active, prospective precision intervention.

Univariate and multivariate analyses of factors associated with advanced CKD

Univariate logistic regression analysis indicated that serum creatinine (OR, 8.42, p < 0.001), age (OR, 7.51, p < 0.001), hematocrit (OR, 0.845, p < 0.001), hypertension (OR, 2.53, p < 0.001), serum ALT (OR, 0.506, p < 0.001), and serum LDH (OR, 1.46, p < 0.001) were associated with advanced CKD. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that serum creatinine (OR, 24.5, p < 0.001), age (OR, 7.77, p < 0.001), hypertension (OR, 1.26, p = 0.003), and serum LDH (OR, 1.34, p = 0.001) remained risk factors for advanced CKD. Hematocrit (OR, 0.368, p < 0.001) and serum ALT (OR, 0.604, p < 0.001) were protective factors for advanced CKD (Table 2 and Figure 3). Construction of nomogram model to predict advanced CKD

To visually represent the predictive model, we constructed a nomogram based on the final multivariate logistic regression analysis (Figure 4). The model incorporated the following independent predictors identified in this study: serum creatinine, age, hematocrit, hypertension, serum ALT, and serum LDH. Each variable is assigned a specific score on the nomogram scale. As shown in Figure 5A, the AUC of the nomogram in the training set for predicting the risk of advanced CKD was 0.961, with high sensitivity (0.927) and specificity (0.867). Bootstrap distributions and scatterplots of AUCs further confirmed the robustness of the model (Figures 5B and 5C). The calibration of the nomogram was also evaluated, and the calibration curve yielded a Brier score of 0.058, indicating good agreement between the predicted probabilities and the observed results (Figure 5D). Validation of nomogram model to predict advanced CKD

The predictive performance of the established nomogram was rigorously evaluated in an internal test set and an independent external validation set. Figure 6A shows that in the internal test set, the nomogram demonstrated strong discrimination (AUC = 0.965) with a sensitivity of 0.948 and a specificity of 0.865. This performance is consistent with the results observed in the training set, indicating robust internal validity. When applied to the independent external dataset of NHANES, the model had an AUC of 0.939 with excellent sensitivity (0.855) and specificity (0.889), as shown in Figure 6D. This indicates good generalization of the model and confirms its ability to discriminate between different patient populations. The calibration of the nomogram was further evaluated in both datasets. Brier scores of 0.056 and 0.073 for the test and external validation sets, respectively, demonstrate close agreement between the predicted probabilities and observed frequencies of advanced CKD (Figures 6B and 6E). The net clinical benefit of the nomogram in these populations was assessed using DCA. In addressing the intrinsic correlation between creatinine and CKD stage, we have additionally developed and validated the creatinine-free prediction model. The results revealed that the AUC of the creatinine-free model in the training set, internal validation set, and external validation set were 0.915, 0.923, and 0.879, respectively, demonstrating good calibration and clinical practicality. The model effectively addresses the risk assessment requirements in scenarios where creatinine is absent or fails to be detected in a timely manner, thereby further enhancing the clinical application value of the study (Figures S4–S9 and Table S2).

Subgroup analyses

The prediction model remained stable and well differentiated across age subgroups. In the internal validation set, AUC values of <60 years old and ≥60 years old were 0.92 and 0.91, respectively. The AUC of the external validation set was 0.972 and 0.863. The Brier scores of all age subgroups were less than 0.15, indicating that the prediction model showed good calibration in different age groups. DCA showed consistent net benefits across age strata, comparable to the overall model (Figure S2).

The prediction model remained stable and well differentiated in both male and female subgroups. In the internal validation set, the AUC was 0.966 in males and 0.981 in females. In the external validation set, the AUC was 0.929 in males and 0.951 in females. The Brier scores of all gender subgroups were less than 0.15, indicating that the prediction model showed good calibration in different gender subgroups. DCA showed a consistent net benefit across gender subgroups, comparable to the overall model (Figure S3).

Discussion

This study developed and validated a nomogram for predicting progression of advanced CKD with two key aspects at its core value. First, it uses only routine clinical and laboratory indicators to achieve high accuracy risk prediction in a heterogeneous patient population, avoiding reliance on testing for expensive specialist treatments. Second, it underwent rigorous internal and external validation, including in the NHANES cohort, confirming strong generalizability across diverse populations.

In this study, LASSO regression was used to screen six variables including serum creatinine, age, hematocrit, hypertension, serum ALT, and serum LDH to construct a nomogram for predicting the progression of advanced CKD. This set of variables was critically assessed to ensure that multicollinearity was not present (VIF <3 for all variables) and that there was a strong association with CKD progression, providing a solid basis for model reliability.

In terms of predictive performance, the model demonstrated strong discrimination across all datasets: the AUC was 0.961 in the training set, 0.965 in the temporally and spatially independent test set, and 0.939 in the NHANES external validation set. The attenuation of AUC in the NHANES cohort, which was only 0.022 lower than that in the training cohort, is particularly noteworthy, as it suggests that the model captures central, pervasive pathophysiological mechanisms of CKD progression, such as impaired glomerular filtration reflected by creatinine, renal dysfunction effects on systemic oxygen delivery indicated by the changes in hematocrit levels.13,14 This finding indicates that the model does not overfit random noise in the training data. This level of discrimination surpasses that of most existing tools for predicting CKD progression. For instance, Zou et al. developed a nomogram for CKD progression using a single hospital cohort, achieving an AUC of 0.9, which was validated solely internally.15 Shi et al. incorporated the new biomarker neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) into their model, achieving an AUC of 0.911, but lacked external validation.16 In contrast, our model maintained high levels of discrimination even in the externally independent NHANES cohort, underscoring its superior ability to generalize across diverse populations.

In addition to discrimination, the calibration and clinical applicability of the proposed model further confirmed its practical value and advantages over previous tools. The calibration curves demonstrated Brier scores <0.15 for all cohorts, with a high degree of agreement between predicted risk and actual outcomes, providing clinicians with interpretable and reliable probability values rather than ambiguous risk levels.17 This represents a key improvement over some existing models that report only vague risk stratification without rigorous calibration.18,19 DCA further demonstrated that the nomogram yielded a significant net benefit at all clinical probability thresholds, surpassing the “treat all” or “treat nothing” strategies. This supports targeted interventions aimed at optimizing resource allocation by identifying high-risk patients for intensive surveillance, detecting underappreciated high-risk cases, and avoiding unnecessary treatment of very low-risk patients. Importantly, improvements in model accessibility further enhance these advantages. All six variables were routine clinical measures commonly assessed in primary and tertiary hospitals, and did not incur additional testing costs. This contrasts with many existing models that rely on specialized or expensive biomarkers or complex multi-omics data, which limit the applicability of models in resource-limited settings.20,21

Subgroup analyses, stratified by age and gender, further validated the robustness of the model. AUC values exceeded 0.86 for all subgroups, and calibration and DCA results were consistent, confirming that the model is not limited to the original study population but can be extended to CKD patients with varying demographic characteristics. It also addresses the issue of external validity being influenced by age or gender differences between internal and external datasets.22,23 This stability, coupled with the triple-validation design of the model addresses a common limitation of previous studies, as most published models for predicting CKD have undergone only internal validation, rendering their generalizability uncertain. Our NHANES cohort was also rigorously screened to exclude cases with missing key variables, aligning with the internal cohort definition of CKD and ensuring comparable and credible external validation results, which represents another strength.

In summary, these findings demonstrate that the current model not only excels in discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility, but also outperforms previous tools for predicting CKD progression across three core dimensions: consistent and reliable discrimination across cohorts, greater variable accessibility (without reliance on expensive specialized tests), and greater validation rigor. These features make it highly suitable for a wide range of clinical applications, from tertiary hospitals to primary care settings with limited resources.

Limitations of the study

This study also has several limitations, which provide guidance for future research. Although rigorous validation has mitigated some inherent biases, prospective multicenter studies remain necessary to confirm the model’s validity in real-world clinical workflows. Second, the model does not incorporate specific damage markers, such as NGAL, KIM-1, or multi-omics data, which could potentially enhance performance. In addition, we employed only LASSO regression for variable selection and modeling, without a systematic comparison to machine learning algorithms, such as random forest or XGBoost, which could be explored in future studies to optimize prediction performance. Furthermore, the model has not yet been integrated into the electronic medical record system, and its actual impact on improving patient outcomes remains to be evaluated.

Method details

Study design and data source

Figure 1 illustrates the study design. Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) from January 1998 to December 2018, involving 8391 patients, were used for internal validation.Additionally, the data used to develop the prediction model in this study were obtained from CKD patients treated at the Provincial Hospital Affiliated with Fuzhou University from January 2013 to December 2024, forming an external dataset that included clinical information from 5398 patients. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged at least 3 months, with any of the following conditions: albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) ≥ 30 mg/g (3 mg/mmol), abnormal urine sediment, persistent hematuria, electrolyte imbalance, renal tubular disorder, histological abnormality, structural abnormality detected by imaging, or a history of renal transplantation24; (2) patients aged ≥ 18 years. The definition of each CKD stage was based on the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) classification (G1-G5).24 The eGFR was calculated using the CKD-EPI formula, based on the serum creatinine level. There were 2760 cases in the early-stage group (G1-G2) and 766 cases in the advanced-stage group (G3-G5) in the internal dataset, and 342 cases in the early-stage group (G1-G2) and 1109 cases in the advanced-stage group (G3-G5) in the external dataset. This study followed the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Provincial Hospital Affiliated with Fuzhou University (K2025-02-116). As a retrospective study, all data were anonymized, and informed patient consent was not required.

Data analysis and processing

A total of 43 clinical variables commonly encountered in clinical practice were collected from both internal and external datasets, including demographic information, comorbidities, and laboratory measures. All variables included in the analysis were evaluated for missing data rates. A total of 17 variables with missing data rates exceeding 20% were excluded, including: alcohol consumption history, smoking history, anemia, body mass index (BMI), serum inorganic phosphorus (IP), serum bicarbonate, serum glucose, serum Low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDLC), serum total protein (TP), serum apolipoprotein B (apoB), serum apolipoprotein AI (apoAI), serum total cholestero (TC), serum triglycerides (TG), plasma fibrinogen, urinary albumin, serum ferritin, and serum C-reactive protein (CRP) (Figure 1 and Table 1). For the remaining variables with missing data rates of 20% or less, cases with missing values for any key variable were excluded. A total of 4,865 cases were excluded from the NHANES dataset, resulting in an initial inclusion of 8,391 cases and a final inclusion of 3,526 cases in the analysis. In the dataset of the Provincial Hospital Affiliated with Fuzhou University, 3,947 cases were excluded, 5,398 cases were initially included, and 1,451 cases were ultimately included in the analysis (Figure 1 and Table 1).

Variables selection

Using a simple random sampling method, the internal CKD cohort was divided into a training set (70%) and an test set (30%) at a 7:3 ratio. A comparative analysis of the baseline characteristics between the two groups revealed that all key indicators were not statistically different (P > 0.05), indicating that the grouping was balanced and comparable (Table S1). Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis was employed for the initial screening of variables. This is accomplished by imposing an L1 regularization penalty on the regression coefficients, which compresses the coefficients of insignificant variables to zero, thereby eliminating them.25 Additionally, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify the optimal variables, which help control bias introduced by confounding variables and enhance both the predictive and explanatory power of the model.

Construction and validation of nomogram model to predict advanced CKD

Based on the training set, a nomogram model was constructed using the optimal variables, which was subsequently validated by both the test set and the external dataset. The performance of the nomogram model was evaluated using the AUC, sensitivity, and specificity. Additionally, calibration curves were employed to assess the agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities of the model. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the practical value of the models by quantifying their net benefit at various decision thresholds.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed stratified by age and gender to evaluate the robustness of the prediction model in different demographic subgroups. Based on the age distribution of the CKD population and clinical practice guidelines, age was divided into 2 clinically meaningful subgroups: <60 years and ≥60 years. Gender was stratified by male and female. Subgroup analyses included an internal dataset (n=3526) and an external validation set (n=1451) to avoid single-dataset bias. Model performance in each subgroup was assessed using metrics consistent with the overall model: discrimination (AUC, sensitivity and specificity), calibration (calibration curve and Brier score), and clinical utility (DCA).

Statistics

Statistical analysis was conducted using R (version 4.5.0, R Foundation). For continuous variables with a normal distribution, the data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the t-test. For continuous variables with skewed distributions, the data are presented as medians with interquartile ranges and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables are presented as counts with percentages and compared using the chi-square test. All continuous variables were z-normalized prior to their inclusion in the logistic regression analysis to mitigate the amplification effect on coefficients caused by differences in variable units. Variance inflation factors were used to assess multicollinearity between variables before constructing the final multivariate logistic regression model. All included variables had variance inflation factors (VlFs) <5, indicating that collinearity issues were not significant. In the variable selection process using LASSO regression, a 10-fold cross-validation (CV) procedure was employed to determine the penalty coefficient λ. The final λ value was selected according to the '1 standard error (1-SE) rule' to obtain a more concise and robust model while maintaining prediction accuracy. The AUC was used to evaluate predictive power, and the optimal cutoff value was determined by maximizing the Youden index. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
