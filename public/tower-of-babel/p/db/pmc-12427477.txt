Cost-effectiveness and epidemiological impact of gender-neutral vaccination against human papillomavirus in economically developed metropolises of Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou, China
Authors: Xiaoya Fu, Qian Zhang, Abram L. Wagner, Weiyu Zhou, Yilan Xia, Yihan Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman papillomavirus (HPV) causes multiple diseases in both sexes. This study evaluates the cost-effectiveness and epidemiological impact – defined as reductions in HPV-related disease cases – of a gender-neutral vaccination (GNV) strategy in China’s economically developed metropolises: Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou. A discrete-time Markov model simulated no vaccination, female-only vaccination (FOV), and GNV strategies among 12-year-olds. The cost matrix included vaccine procurement, administration, and treatment costs from Chinese data and literature. The case matrix translated model-estimated reductions in HPV infections into decreases in disease incidence by applying baseline incidence rates adjusted for HPV genotype attribution, relative risk reductions, and disease-specific lag times. Outcomes assessed were costs (2023 USD), quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), and cases averted. Compared to FOV, the ICERs for GNV were USD 323,134/QALY (Beijing), USD 305,816/QALY (Shanghai), and USD 251,853/QALY (Guangzhou), exceeding local willingness-to-pay thresholds, indicating cost-ineffectiveness. The GNV strategy averted 3.9%, 4.7%, and 3.9% more HPV-related cases over 30 years in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou, respectively. Sensitivity analyses showed that GNV became cost-effective when the 9vHPV uptake rate was below 20% in girls (Shanghai) or below 10% in girls (Beijing and Guangzhou), regardless of the uptake rate (≤80%) in boys. It was also cost-effective if vaccine price dropped to USD 21.4 (Shanghai), USD 18.9 (Beijing), or USD 19.1 (Guangzhou). Price thresholds rose substantially with decreasing female uptake but changed little with male uptake variation. The GNV strategy may be cost-effective when the 9vHPV uptake rate remains low in girls or the vaccine price significantly decreases.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most prevalent sexually transmitted infections worldwide.1 Although most HPV infections are asymptomatic in both men and women, they can result in long-term health complications and even death.2 HPV infection is associated with genital warts, anal cancer, and oropharyngeal cancer in both sexes; furthermore, in women, it is correlated to cervical, vaginal, and vulvar cancers, and in men, it increases the risk of penile cancer.3 HPV 16 and 18 are the most prevalent oncogenic genotypes, collectively responsible for approximately 71% of cervical cancers and the majority of HPV-related cancers in men.4 Additionally, HPV 6 and 11 are primarily associated with genital warts.5

HPV infection is highly prevalent across both sexes, with previous studies indicating that the lifetime risk of acquiring HPV exceeds 80%.6 On a global scale, HPV infections are responsible for approximately 9% of cancers in women and 1% of cancers in men.7 In China, the age-standardized incidence rates for oropharyngeal, penile, and anal cancers in men are 0.55, 0.49, and 0.25 cases per 100,000, respectively, whereas the rates for cervical, vulvar, vaginal, oropharyngeal and anal cancers in women are 13.80, 0.32, 0.23, 0.12 and 0.19 cases per 100,000, respectively, according to the report by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).8

As of February 4, 2025, a total of 148 countries have incorporated the HPV vaccine into their national immunization programs, either partially or fully.9 However, screening for non-cervical cancers, such as oropharyngeal cancer, remains limited, and HPV vaccine coverage among men remains low.10 The gender-neutral vaccination (GNV) strategy offers direct protection to men against HPV infection and related diseases, potentially reducing the burden of HPV-related diseases in women as well.11 Consequently, a total of 76 countries (as of February 4, 2025) have transitioned from female-only vaccination (FOV) programs to GNV programs in an effort to lower the incidence and burden of HPV-related diseases and cancers.9 Current economic evaluations of GNV strategies generally suggest that the GNV strategy can be cost-effective under specific conditions, such as low female vaccination coverage or reduced vaccine prices.12–14 Nevertheless, the cost-effectiveness of GNV strategy remains uncertain in multiple countries. A systematic review of economic evaluations reveals considerable variation in the outcomes, with the cost-effectiveness of male vaccination largely contingent on country-specific factors, such as vaccination coverage and vaccine price.15 Notably, the majority of existing analyses have been conducted in high-income countries, with a paucity of evidence from low- and middle-income regions. To date, no economic evaluation has been undertaken to assess the cost-effectiveness of GNV strategies in the China’s mainland context.

In the metropolises of China, such as Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou, the three most prevalent high-risk HPV genotypes are HPV 52, 58, and 16 among HPV-positive women.16–18 These genotypes are included in the 9-valent HPV vaccine (9vHPV; Gardasil® 9), which targets HPV 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58, offering protection against approximately 90% of all HPV-related cancers and more than 90% of genital warts.19,20 Currently, the HPV vaccine has not been incorporated into China’s national immunization program. The HPV vaccine uptake remained low in women aged 9–45 years across the country, of which it was highest in Beijing and Shanghai.21 In January 2025, 4-valent HPV vaccine has been licensed for men aged 9–26,22 and more HPV vaccines are waiting for approval for men in the coming years in China. It has been predicted that male HPV vaccination might be more acceptable in the metropolitan regions.23 Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the cost-effectiveness and epidemiological impact (defined as the reduction in HPV-related disease cases) of the GNV strategy, compared to the FOV strategy, thereby providing evidence to inform the potential implementation of a GNV strategy in economically developed metropolises of China.

Methods

Evaluation overview

This study aims to determine the cost-effectiveness and epidemiological impact of GNV strategy, FOV strategy, and no vaccination in girls and boys aged 12 years. We selected three metropolises, including Beijing (capital and in northern China), Shanghai (eastern China), and Guangzhou (southern China), which have larger population size (approximately 22 million, 25 million, and 10 million, respectively) and GDP (USD 594 billion, 638 billion, and 412 billion, respectively),24–26 compared to any other municipalities/cities in China’s mainland. These metropolitan regions, with relatively higher levels of economic development, may also be characterized by stronger health awareness among residents, a potentially higher understanding regarding HPV vaccination, and better local capacity for public health resource allocation.27,28 These factors collectively provide a more practical foundation for piloting GNV strategies in regions.

We employed a discrete-time Markov model to assess the cost-effectiveness of GNV strategy from the perspective of local healthcare system in three metropolises. The model was designed with a 100-year time horizon, primarily simulating the outcomes for girls and boys aged 12 years. Three vaccination strategies were evaluated: 1) no HPV vaccination; 2) FOV with the 9vHPV vaccine; and 3) GNV with the 9vHPV vaccine. The benefits of vaccination were quantified in terms of quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), which were derived from reductions in the incidence of HPV-related diseases. In the base-case scenario, the model included the following HPV-related conditions: cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), cervical cancer, anal cancer, vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN), vulvar cancer, and vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia (VaIN). Oropharyngeal cancer, along with penile cancer, was considered only in sensitivity analyses, as the vaccine effectiveness against these conditions has not been definitively established.29

Model description

The discrete-time Markov model utilized in this study was adapted from the population-based model developed by the Health Information and Quality Authority (HIQA) of Ireland in 2018.30 Originally designed to assess the cost-effectiveness of expanding the GNV program in the United States, the model was refined by the Norwegian Health Technology Assessment Agency (HTA) and has since been validated across multiple countries and extensively used for health economic evaluations of GNV strategy.12,31,32

This model employs a discrete-time Markov approach, in which the population transitions between five mutually exclusive states: susceptible, vaccinated and uninfected, infected and unvaccinated, vaccinated and infected, and death (Figure 1). Transition probabilities between these states are influenced by factors such as age, sex, vaccination probability, HPV infection risk, and vaccine effectiveness. Although the original Irish model was developed using R version 3.4.2, this study adapted it to the context of economically developed metropolises of China using R version 4.4.2. All assumptions were detailed in the supplementary materials (Supplement I). Demographic parameters

The model utilized demographic data specific to Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou. The simulated population consisted of girls and boys aged 12 years in each metropolis. According to the 2020 population census yearbooks, Beijing had 63,344 12-year-old girls and 68,508 12-year-old boys, Shanghai had 63,658 12-year-old girls and 69,337 12-year-old boys, and Guangzhou had 63,292 12-year-old girls and 74,597 12-year-old boys.33–35 Age-specific mortality rates were sourced from the 2023 statistics yearbooks.24–26 Additionally, the model assumed a stable population, with natural decline controlled by age-specific mortality rates.

Epidemiological parameters

The model utilized epidemiological data specific to Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou, with the original values from the Irish model retained only for global parameters or missing local data, such as vaccine efficacy and probabilities of HPV infection. Data on HPV-related invasive cancers were obtained from the cancer incidence statistics for each metropolis as presented in the IARC report.36 Incidence rates for precancerous lesions and genital warts were derived from relevant literature,30,37 as were the proportions attributable to HPV infection.30,38,39 Survival rates for invasive cancers were derived from the survival analysis of cancer patients in China.40 Detailed epidemiological parameters were provided in the supplementary materials (Supplement II).

Vaccine-related parameters

In this study, we determined the cost-effectiveness of GNV strategy by simulating only 9vHPV, as 9vHPV may effectively prevent the most prevalent HPV genotypes in the three metropolises. Also, multiple 9vHPV candidates domestically designed and manufactured in China are ongoing in the clinical trials, which may contribute to the GNV strategy in the near future.41,42 The model required both historical and projected 9vHPV uptake rates. The historical rate was 1.5% in girls,21 while it was zero in boys as the HPV vaccine had not been approved for male vaccination until 2025 in China’s mainland. The projected rate in girls was set at 90%, according to the goal of achieving 90% of HPV vaccination coverage proposed in the Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer as a Public Health Problem.43 In boys, the projected rate was set at 87.7% of the uptake rate in girls, based on previous health economic evaluations of male HPV vaccination strategy.12

The model also accounted for the incidence of adverse events following immunization (AEFI) potentially linked to the vaccine. In China, the incidence rates of non-serious and serious AEFI for the 9vHPV during 2021–2022 were 15.99 and 0.17 per 100,000 doses, respectively.44

Vaccine efficacy for preventing persistent HPV infection was obtained from high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs).45–47 It was assumed that, in 12-year-old children, immune response following a two-dose regimen of the HPV vaccine was not inferior to the three-dose regimen, with proven efficacy in both young female and male adolescents.48 Data on vaccine efficacy related to clinical outcomes were provided in the supplementary materials (Supplement III).

Health-related quality of life

The calculation of QALYs was conducted based on health-related quality of life data from general population in China, assessed using the EQ-5D-5 L scale.49 Disutility data, representing quality of life losses due to diseases and adverse events, were sourced from relevant literature (Supplement IV).

Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio

In the study, we calculated the costs and benefits of each strategy under comparison and ranked them based on the outcomes. The Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio (ICER) was then calculated to assess the cost-effectiveness of the strategies. A strategy was considered more cost-effective than another when its ICER fell below the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold. In this study, the WTP thresholds were set at USD 28,624 in Beijing, USD 27,254 in Shanghai, and USD 23,153 in Guangzhou, corresponding to one times the per capita GDP of each metropolis in 2023.53 This threshold was based on the Guideline for Economic Evaluations of Vaccines and Immunization Programs in China issued by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention.54

Sensitivity and scenario analysis

A univariate sensitivity analysis was performed by adjusting a single parameter within a specified range while holding other parameters constant, in order to evaluate its impact on the outcomes. The effect on the ICER was visualized using a tornado diagram. However, since the key factors affecting the cost-effectiveness are interdependent, variations in one factor may influence others. Consequently, a bivariate sensitivity analysis was performed on the 9vHPV uptake rates in both girls and boys. The Probabilistic Sensitivity Analysis (PSA) involved 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations, with repeated sampling from the parameter distributions to quantify the uncertainty in the outcomes. The probability distribution functions used in the PSA were as follows: lognormal for costs, HPV acquisition probability, relative risks, and duration of HPV-related diseases; beta for vaccine uptake, incidence rates, and disutility; and Dirichlet for the proportion of events attributable to HPV.

Additionally, the study performed uncertainty testing by modifying key model assumptions, evaluating scenarios involving oropharyngeal and penile cancers, as well as variations in vaccine prices and efficacy, to provide a more comprehensive reflection of cost-effectiveness under different scenarios.

Sensitivity analysis

The probabilistic sensitivity analysis revealed that, when the WTP threshold was set at 1×per capita GDP, the probability of the GNV strategy being cost-effective was 0.40% in Beijing, 0.60% in Shanghai, and 0.44% in Guangzhou, compared to the FOV strategy (Supplement V). When a more conservative threshold of 0.5× per capita GDP was applied, these probabilities further declined, reinforcing the robustness of the base-case conclusion. Furthermore, the univariate sensitivity analysis indicated that as the discount rate, 9vHPV uptake rate in girls, vaccine efficacy in preventing persistent HPV16/18 infection in girls, and vaccine price increased, the probability of the GNV strategy achieving cost-effectiveness diminished (Supplement VI). In contrast, an increase in cervical cancer incidence and vaccine efficacy in preventing persistent HPV16 infection in boys enhanced the probability of the GNV strategy being cost-effective.

Moreover, when the 9vHPV uptake rate in both girls and boys increased from 10% to 90%, the impact on the ICER differed significantly between the sexes (Supplement VII). Specifically, compared to the FOV strategy, an increase in the 9vHPV uptake rate in girls had a substantial effect on the ICER in the GNV strategy, whereas the impact of increasing 9vHPV uptake rate in boys was relatively minimal. Consequently, the GNV strategy became cost-effective when the 9vHPV uptake rate was below 20% in girls (Shanghai) or below 10% in girls (Beijing and Guangzhou), regardless of the uptake rate (80% or below) in boys.

A two-way sensitivity analysis was further conducted to assess the price threshold for the GNV strategy, compared to the FOV strategy, considering varying 9vHPV uptake rates in both girls and boys (Figures 2 and 3). The analysis revealed that for every 10% reduction in 9vHPV uptake rate in girls, the price threshold increased substantially by USD 18.5, 19.7, and 18.8 in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou, respectively. In contrast, when the 9vHPV uptake rate in boys ranged from 10% to 90%, the price threshold showed only slight changes of USD 4.6, 3.6, and 2.9 in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou, respectively. Scenario analysis

A scenario analysis was performed by adjusting the values of key parameters (Figure 4). When the vaccine price per dose was reduced to at least USD 21.4 in Shanghai, USD 18.9 in Beijing, and USD 19.1 in Guangzhou, the ICER for the GNV strategy compared to the FOV strategy decreased to USD 27,155 per QALY, USD 28,163 per QALY, and USD 22,691 per QALY, respectively, making the GNV strategy cost-effective. Moreover, additional protection against oropharyngeal and penile cancers, along with the effect of waning vaccine efficacy, had a relatively minimal impact on the ICER. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first to assess the cost-effectiveness of GNV strategy in China’s mainland. Our study indicated that the ICERs for GNV with 9vHPV strategy in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou, compared to FOV strategy, all far exceeded their respective WTP thresholds, indicating that GNV strategy was cost-ineffective. Nevertheless, the GNV strategy became cost-effective when the 9vHPV uptake rate was below 20% in girls (Shanghai) or below 10% in girls (Beijing and Guangzhou), regardless of the uptake rate (80% or below) in boys. The GNV strategy was also cost-effective when the 9vHPV price per dose dropped to USD 21.4 (Shanghai), USD 18.9 (Beijing), or USD 19.1 (Guangzhou). Furthermore, the price threshold dramatically increased by USD 18.5–19.7 for every 10%-reduction of 9vHPV uptake in girls, whereas it slightly changed by USD 2.9–4.6 when the uptake rate ranged 10–90% in boys. It has further raised a public health concern about whether to recommend males to receive HPV vaccination, as discussed in Japan and Singapore,14,55 even if the 9vHPV supply would be sufficient in China in the near future.

In this study, we adopted a cost-effectiveness threshold of 1×per capita GDP, primarily based on the Guideline for Economic Evaluations of Vaccines and Immunization Programs in China issued by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention.54 However, the appropriateness of applying such a threshold has been increasingly questioned in recent studies. Several studies have argued that this threshold may overestimate the actual WTP in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in the context of publicly funded health programs.56 Although our base-case analysis applied the 1×per capita GDP threshold, we further conducted a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve (CEAC) analysis using a more conservative threshold (0.5×per capita GDP) to assess the robustness of our findings. The results remained consistent, thereby strengthening the credibility and policy relevance of our conclusions.

The findings in the study suggested that the modeling outcomes were highly sensitive to several key parameters, consistent with numerous economic evaluations of HPV vaccination.57,58 Vaccine uptake rate, particularly in girls, had a profound impact; however, vaccine uptake in boys showed a modest influence. The two-way sensitivity analysis indicated that the GNV strategy became cost-effective only when the 9vHPV uptake rate in girls remained at a low level, consistent with previous studies.12,30 This is primarily because the majority of health benefits accrue to females that bear a substantially higher burden of HPV-related diseases – especially cervical cancer. Vaccination in girls thus confers both individual protection and broader herd immunity, while vaccination in boys contributes mainly to indirect population-level benefits.59 As female coverage increases, herd immunity strengthens, and the marginal benefit of vaccinating boys diminishes. This trend is particularly pronounced in the contexts where heterosexual transmission dominates, as in our model, which did not separately account for the men who have sex with men (MSM) population. In such settings, high coverage among girls effectively disrupts the transmission chain, leaving limited incremental gains from including boys.59 In 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) similarly reported that male vaccination could only be cost-effective if female vaccination rates were below 50%.60 Moreover, with changes in vaccine pricing, policy adjustments, and the inclusion of additional HPV-related diseases in cost-effectiveness analyses, the impact of HPV vaccine uptake on the cost-effectiveness could be modified. Thus, it remains crucial to collect real-world data on the HPV vaccine effectiveness against HPV-related diseases among men.61 Consequently, economic evaluations of GNV strategy might be conducted more precisely.

The vaccine price was also a critical parameter in determining the cost-effectiveness of vaccination against various vaccine-preventable diseases, such as HPV-related diseases and influenza.30,62 In our study, sensitivity analysis indicated that the GNV strategy became increasingly likely to be cost-effective along with declining vaccine price, compared to the FOV strategy. It was consistent with multiple studies in Singapore, Ireland, and Spain.12,14,30 Currently, HPV vaccines remain self-paid in China, with varying prices across 2-valent, 4-valent, and 9-valent HPV vaccines of domestic and international manufacturers. In recent years, a growing number of local Chinese governments have provided subsidies for HPV vaccination programs among girls aged 9–14, which have substantially reduced the out-of-pocket payment.63 However, the 9vHPV (Gardasil® 9) remained the highest price compared to other HPV vaccines. In the future, domestically designed 9vHPV candidates may have lower price, thereby enhancing the cost-effectiveness of GNV strategy.64 Further study may look to the sensitivity of cost-effectiveness and the impact of uncertainty by varying parameter values. Additionally, scenario analyses may simulate different assumptions and parameter values, thereby providing policymakers with a clearer understanding of potential public health implications of various decision-making options.

So far, multiple international studies have assessed the cost-effectiveness of GNV strategies.12,13,31,55,65 A systematic review highlighted significant uncertainties in the cost-effectiveness of these strategies, with substantial variations across different countries and regions.15 In Japan, a cost-effectiveness analysis revealed that the GNV strategy became cost-effective when the female HPV vaccination rate remained at 30%, with an ICER of USD 35,741 per QALY compared to the FOV strategy.55 In Spain, a study concluded that expanding male vaccination with the 9vHPV was cost-ineffective; however, it could become cost-effective when the vaccine price per dose dropped from EUR 45 (USD 51.3) to EUR 27.9 (USD 31.8).12 In Hong Kong of China, a study on male HPV vaccination reported an ICER of HKD 248,354 (USD 31,718) per QALY for the GNV strategy, compared to the FOV strategy.13 In contrast, cost-effectiveness analyses of GNV strategies remain limited in China’s mainland. In our study, the ICERs of the GNV strategy, compared to the FOV strategy, were notably higher than those reported in other countries and regions. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, our model assumed a relatively high uptake rate in girls, in accordance with the WHO’s Global Strategy to Eliminate Cervical Cancer, which targets 90% coverage by age 15.43 Higher uptake rate in girls generates stronger herd immunity, thereby reducing the incremental benefit of male vaccination.59 Second, the vaccine prices applied in our analysis remained substantially higher than those used in many international evaluations – where 4vHPV or 9vHPV prices often fall below USD 80 per dose.12,55 These differences in baseline assumptions, particularly in target population coverage and vaccine pricing, likely contribute to the less favorable cost-effectiveness estimates observed in our context. Overall, the cost-effectiveness of the GNV strategy remains inconclusive, both in China and globally. However, male HPV vaccination plays an important role in reducing the burden of HPV-related diseases.61 In China, the 4-valent HPV vaccine has been licensed for men aged 9–26,22 and a phase III clinical trial of 9vHPV among men is ongoing in China,41,42 reflecting the increasing acknowledgment of HPV vaccination as a vital element of gender health. Consequently, it is essential to further economic evaluations of HPV vaccination for men in China to provide robust scientific evidence for optimization of HPV vaccination strategies.

Economic development levels play a critical role in shaping both vaccine accessibility and acceptability, thereby contributing to regional disparities in HPV vaccine coverage. For instance, in the economically developed Pearl River Delta region of Guangdong Province, robust fiscal capacity and well-established public health infrastructure have enabled vaccination coverage to exceed 80%.27 In contrast, less-developed regions in western and northern Guangdong Province report lower coverage rates (approximately 60%–70%), largely due to limited financial support, delayed policy implementation, and prevalent vaccine hesitancy.66,67 These disparities are further influenced by a combination of factors, including public awareness of HPV vaccines, effectiveness of health communication strategies, cultural attitudes, and prevailing social norms.68,69 Existing evidence suggests that higher vaccine acceptability is generally associated with greater educational attainment, higher household income, and urban residency.68,69 Consequently, in economically developed metropolitan regions, male HPV vaccination is likely to face higher social acceptance and greater policy feasibility. Prioritizing GNV strategy in these regions is both practical and strategic, enabling pilot implementation, effectiveness evaluation, and experience accumulation to inform broader national rollout.

This study had several limitations. First, data on HPV infections and related diseases in China remain incomplete, necessitating further study to address these gaps. While most key parameters were drawn from data specific to Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, or national sources – enhancing representativeness – some were obtained from other countries or adapted from the original model, which may limit the generalizability. Specifically, some key parameters related to HPV infection were adapted from the Irish model due to the current lack of comprehensive, high-quality local data in China. This reliance on external data may introduce some uncertainty and limit the model’s precision in fully capturing the Chinese epidemiological context. Future research with more detailed local data would help refine the model for improved accuracy. Second, the discrete-time Markov model used in our study simulated annual transitions between a limited number of mutually exclusive health states. Although other models employ a greater number of states and continuous-time approaches, our simplified model reduced the data requirements for parameterization and allows for comprehensive probabilistic sensitivity analysis. Third, the model did not separately simulate the MSM population, potentially underestimating the immune protection this group might gain under a GNV strategy. Fourth, the selection of study regions also induced study limitation. We included only a few economically developed regions, which did not fully represent the national landscape, particularly in less developed regions where HPV awareness, vaccine intent and accessibility, disease burden, and health resource allocation differ substantially. Therefore, our findings might not be directly generalized to the nationwide level. Future study should incorporate a broader range of regions with varying economic development to assess the applicability and feasibility of GNV strategies across diverse settings, thereby supporting the formulation of more inclusive and equitable vaccination policies. Additionally, this study adopted a healthcare system perspective, as relevant data are more readily available and reliable in the Chinese context; moreover, this perspective is commonly used in international health technology assessments. However, by focusing solely on direct medical costs, this approach does not account for broader societal impacts, such as productivity losses due to illness or caregiving, potentially underestimating the full economic benefits of vaccination. Future studies should consider incorporating a societal perspective, where feasible, to provide more comprehensive evidence for policy making.

Conclusions

In comparison to FOV strategy with the 9vHPV, the GNV strategy with the 9vHPV achieved greater reductions in the incidence of HPV-related diseases. However, under base-case conditions, the GNV strategy was cost-ineffective relative to the FOV strategy. Moreover, the GNV strategy became cost-effective when the 9vHPV uptake rate remained low, or the vaccine price significantly decreased.
