Long Distance Air Ambulance Transfers for Endovascular Thrombectomy
Authors: Vivien TY Yong, Joseph Donnelly, Jae Beom Hong, William K Diprose, James R Caldwell, Shane S Lee, Ben J McGuinness, Stefan Brew, Peter Alan Barber
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndovascular thrombectomy (EVT) is limited to comprehensive stroke centers (CSCs), with patients who first present to primary stroke centers (PSCs) transferred for treatment by road ambulance or helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS). We aimed to determine if there were outcome differences in patients transferred for EVT via HEMS.MethodsA retrospective observational study was performed in the setting of a large regional network with 1 CSC receiving patients directly, or via road ambulance from 3 metropolitan PSCs or HEMS from 13 more distant PSCs. Consecutive patients were identified from a mandatory national EVT registry. HEMS transfers were compared with 2 metropolitan patient groups: direct‐to‐CSC and metro PSC‐to‐CSC patients. The primary outcome was day 90 functional independence (modified Rankin scale score, 0–2). Secondary outcomes included day 90 death rate.ResultsA total of 1183 EVT‐treated patients were identified (45% women; median age, 69 years; 88% anterior circulation strokes; median baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, 16). A total of 332 (28%) patients were HEMS transfers, 428 (36%) were direct‐to‐CSC, and 423 (36%) were metro PSC‐to‐CSC transfers. The median transfer distances to the CSC were 11 (8–11) miles for road ambulance and 81 (70–121) miles for HEMS. HEMS patients were matched at baseline with the 2 metropolitan groups, except for a lower prevalence of congestive heart failure (P<0.001) and a higher thrombolysis rate (54% versus 42% and 45%; P=0.004). HEMS patients had longer onset‐to‐EVT‐groin time (median minutes 370 versus 195 direct‐to‐CSC patients and 255 metro PSC‐to‐CSC; P<0.001). Direct‐to‐CSC patients were more likely to be independent at day 90 (P=0.026), but there was no difference between the 2 transfer groups (P=0.911). There were no differences among the 3 cohorts in day 90 mortality rate for all stroke types (P=0.321) or independence in anterior circulation strokes (P=0.124).ConclusionIn this large regional stroke network, patients transferred via HEMS for EVT had similar day 90 outcomes to their metropolitan counterparts despite longer transfer times. CSCs should not be discouraged from considering patients from more distant PSCs who require HEMS.Keywords: helicopter ambulance, ischemia, outcome, registry, stroke, thrombectomy

The role of helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS) in transferring patients for endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) is still to be defined. HEMS is 1 strategy to improve access to specialized stroke care that may not otherwise be available for patients who live at a distance from a comprehensive stroke center (CSC). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Previous studies have focused on the role of HEMS in thrombolysis, including evaluation of in‐flight safety, 5 , 6 cost‐effectiveness, 7 and improvement of thrombolysis rates. 8 , 9 , 10 Studies in the pre‐EVT era report no differences in outcomes of patients transferred between hospitals by HEMS compared with road ambulance. 11 , 12 , 13 Studies exploring the use of HEMS for patients receiving EVT report impact on clinical process times, but few describe functional outcome measures 14 ; most have small patient numbers 15 , 16 or have a median difference between HEMS and road transfer times of <30 minutes. 16 , 17 Others have suggested that HEMS is faster than road ambulance when the distance between hospitals is >40 miles. 18

We performed a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data comparing treatment process times and outcomes for patients receiving EVT transferred via HEMS from distant centers with metropolitan patients transferred by road ambulance.

Methods

The analytic codes and anonymized data that support the findings are available on reasonable request from the corresponding author. The first author had full access to all the data in the study and takes responsibility for its integrity and the data analysis. The National Ethics Advisory Committee approved the National Stroke Reperfusion Therapy Registry and did not require informed patient consent, as this study was considered an audit of clinical practice. The study was performed in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. 19

The setting is a large regional stroke network that provides care for a population of 2.8 million people and consists of a single CSC and 3 metropolitan primary stroke centers (PSCs), where patients are transferred by road ambulance, and 13 more distant PSCs, where patients are routinely transferred for EVT by HEMS (Figure 1). HEMS is a helicopter service, with most patients transferred by helicopter but occasionally by fixed‐wing aircraft or road ambulance when there are adverse weather conditions, and it operates 24 hours 7 days per week. Information about patients who were not transferred due to failure to fly because of inclement weather or technical factors, or who were transferred but did not go on to have EVT, is not routinely recorded but anecdotally is infrequent.

Map of the regional stroke network North Island of New Zealand showing the comprehensive stroke center and 3 metropolitan primary stroke centers in Auckland (red), and the 13 more distant primary stroke centers (black).

Consecutive patients with anterior and posterior circulation territory large vessel occlusion (LVO) treated with EVT between March 25, 2011, and June 30, 2022, were included, with eligibility for EVT determined before transfer on the basis of established clinical guidelines. 20 , 21 Patients with incomplete follow‐up data were excluded from the study. In 2017, an after‐hours direct‐to‐CSC ambulance bypass system was established for patients living in the 3 metropolitan PSC areas (Supplement). 22 , 23

Data were prospectively entered into a mandatory national stroke reperfusion therapy registry by a stroke nurse specialist, as previously described. 24 Information recorded included demographic, transport modality, treatment, process times, and outcome information. The Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score (ASPECTS) and posterior circulation–ASPECTS were determined. 25 , 26 Computed tomography‐to‐groin puncture time was used as a surrogate measure of treatment process times for all patients and transfer times for the HEMS patients, as PSC departure time was not routinely recorded. Google Maps was used to estimate the transfer distances and times, with a 30‐minute dispatch time and average HEMS velocity of 124 miles/h used.

HEMS patients were compared with 2 metropolitan patient groups; patients who presented directly to the CSC (direct‐to‐CSC), and those who presented to a PSC and were then transferred via road ambulance to the CSC (metro PSC‐to‐CSC). The primary outcome was functional independence at day 90, defined as a modified Rankin scale score of 0 to 2, as determined by structured phone or in‐person interview by a trained nurse specialist or study investigator. Secondary outcomes included successful recanalization (modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction score 2b, 2c, or 3), 27 early neurological improvement (defined as a reduction in National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale, reduction by ≥8 points or to 0–1), 28 , 29 symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (defined as local or remote parenchymal hemorrhage combined with either a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale increase of ≥4 points from baseline or death) at 24 hours, 30 and day‐90 mortality rate.

Statistical Analysis

Results are reported as median (interquartile range) values for all patients, with comparison between HEMS and the metropolitan patient groups. Univariate intergroup comparisons of nonnormally distributed continuous data were conducted using the 1‐way ANOVA test. Categorical data were compared using the chi‐square test of independence. To account for potential confounding variables, an adjusted multivariable binary logistic regression predicting outcomes from modality of transfer was performed incorporating relevant variables of baseline patient and stroke characteristics, stroke onset‐to‐computed tomography time (to account for time delays unrelated to modality of transfer to CSC), and thrombolysis. Sensitivity analysis was performed for all tests with anterior and posterior circulation LVO subgroups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R (RStudio 2022.02.0 Build 443). 31

Results

A total of 1183 patients (527 [45%] women; median age, 69 [58–78] years; 1149 [97%] with baseline modified Rankin scale score 0–2) were included (Figure 2, Table 1). A total of 1040 (88%) patients had anterior circulation LVO (internal carotid artery, 294 [28%]; middle cerebral artery and its branches, 743 [71%]), and 143 (12%) patients had posterior circulation LVO, almost all (130 [91%]) basilar artery occlusion. The median admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score was 16 (11–20), and the median ASPECTS and posterior circulation–ASPECTS scores were 9 (7–10) and 10 (9–10), respectively.

Of the 1183 patients, 332 (28%) initially presented to a more distant PSC: 292 (88%) were transferred via helicopter, 13 (4%) via fixed‐wing aircraft, and 29 (9%) via road ambulance due to adverse weather conditions or a helicopter not being available. A total of 423 (36%) initially presented to a metro PSC and were transferred by road ambulance, and 428 (36%) were direct‐to‐CSC patients. For metro PSC‐to‐CSC patients, the median road transfer distance was 11 (8–11) miles. For the HEMS patients, the median transfer distance was 81 (70–121) straight‐line miles by air with an estimated median flight time of 40 (38–47) minutes, and the road transfer distance was 100 (77–140) miles; therefore, we estimate a median transfer time saving of 84 (66–126) minutes.

Patients transferred by HEMS were well matched with the metropolitan groups at baseline, except for a lower prevalence of congestive heart failure (10% versus 21% in the metropolitan patients; P < 0.001) and a higher thrombolysis rate (54% versus 42% for direct‐to‐CSC and 45% for metro PSC‐to‐CSC patients; P = 0.004; Tables 1 and 2). A greater proportion of direct‐to‐CSC patients presented with anterior circulation LVO (91% versus 87% metro PSC‐to‐CSC and 85% HEMS patients; P = 0.032), reflecting a bias in the metropolitan ambulance bypass system toward anterior LVO syndromes.

Process and treatment times are noted in Table 2. HEMS patients had longer median onset‐to‐groin puncture time (370 [294–550] minutes versus 195 [149–318] minutes in direct‐to‐CSC and 255 [200–438] minutes in metro PSC‐to‐CSC patients; P < 0.001). Time of departure from the PSCs was not routinely recorded so that door‐in–door‐out time was not available. However, the median time from first CT‐to‐EVT groin time was available and was longer for HEMS patients (216 [176–278] minutes versus 85 [64–106] minutes direct‐to‐CSC and 128 [106–160] minutes for metro PSC‐to‐CSC patients; P < 0.001).

Direct‐to‐CSC patients were more likely to be functionally independent at day 90 than those who first presented to a PSC (244 [57%] direct‐to‐CSC versus 208 [49%] metro PSC‐to‐CSC and 161 [48%] HEMS; P = 0.026), but there was no difference between the HEMS and metro PSC‐to‐CSC patients (P = 0.911). Direct‐to‐CSC patients also had greater early neurological improvement (P = 0.044), but there was no difference between the 2 transfer groups (P = 0.276). There were no differences in other secondary outcomes, including day 90 mortality rate, among the 3 cohorts (P = 0.321).

When limited to patients with anterior circulation, there were no differences in functional independence between the direct‐to‐CSC, metro PSC‐to‐CSC, and HEMS patients (P = 0.124). When adjusted for baseline variables, HEMS patients with posterior circulation LVO were less likely to be functionally independent at day 90 (adjusted odds ratio, 0.25 [95% CI, 0.08–0.70]; P = 0.01; Figure 3). Sensitivity analysis did not reveal any additional differences in patients with anterior and posterior circulation LVO between the HEMS and metropolitan cohorts (Supplement).

Discussion

In this large regional stroke network, patients transferred for EVT via HEMS had a similar rate of functional independence as patients first presenting to a metropolitan PSC with secondary transfer to the CSC. Direct‐to‐CSC patients had higher rates of early neurologic improvement and functional independence than those first presenting to a metropolitan or distant PSC. This is likely due to shorter onset‐to‐EVT groin puncture times, which are associated with improved outcomes. 32

The rate of functional independence in HEMS transferred patients compare well with the 46% rate in a meta‐analysis of EVT randomized trials. 33 Earlier studies have also reported functional independence rates in HEMS patients comparable with road transfer patients, 15 , 17 , 18 but these studies had fewer patients, and in 1 study the transfer time difference between HEMS and road ambulance patients was <30 minutes. At the other end of the transfer time spectrum, EVT was reported to be of benefit in selected patients with ultra‐long transfers of ≥300 miles, but there was no road transfer comparison group in this study. 34

Selection bias may in part explain the good outcomes in HEMS patients. Firstly, we only report patients who were treated with EVT and not patients with futile transfer as these data are not recorded. Secondly, while the same treatment selection criteria were applied across the network there may have been bias in the referring PSC and accepting CSC teams against more “borderline” cases. There is evidence for this with HEMS patients being less likely to have congestive heart failure and more likely to be treated with thrombolysis. However, one of the notable findings of this study is how well HEMS and metropolitan were otherwise matched in terms of other comorbidities, baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, and ASPECTS.

We estimate that HEMS saved a median of 84 (interquartile range, 66–126) minutes in transfer times per patient. A UK study investigated the cost‐effectiveness of HEMS for EVT, using a cost‐effectiveness threshold range of UK£20 000–UK£30 000 (US$23 000–US$36 000) per quality‐adjusted life years gained. 35 HEMS was reported as being beneficial if transport time was reduced by at least 60 minutes, with a <10% futile transfer rate.

Strengths of this study include the large number of real‐world patients selected for treatment with EVT in accordance with established guidelines, prospectively collected data, and a high follow‐up rate. The number of EVT‐treated patients transferred via HEMS is the largest described in the literature to date. Methodological limitations include the retrospective observational design and inclusion of only a single regional network, which reduces the generalizability of the study findings. Information on futile transfers where patients are not treated with EVT are not routinely recorded, and this may be a source of bias, but anecdotally the rate is low. Patients are not routinely reimaged on arrival to the EVT center unless there has been a significant change in clinical state. We included the 9% of patients transferred by road ambulance in the HEMS cohort because they were from distant PSCs and had transfer times that were longer than if they had been transferred by air, and this reflects the reality of servicing distant hospitals.

In this large regional stroke network, patients transferred for EVT from distant PSCs via HEMS had similar day 90 outcomes to those transferred via road ambulance from a metropolitan PCS, with rates that are comparable to meta‐analyses of the pivotal EVT randomized controlled trials. This was despite the longer process and transfer times resulting in an almost 2‐hour delay in onset‐to‐groin puncture times. CSCs should not be discouraged from considering patients from more distant centers who require HEMS.
