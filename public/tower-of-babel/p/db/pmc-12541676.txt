Screen to Screen Versus Face to Face: Evaluating Telehealth Autism Diagnostic Assessments for Young Children in a Diverse Clinical Setting
Authors: Rebecca Sutherland, Marie Antoinette Hodge, Kelsie Boulton, Sarah Baracz, Gillian Brooks, Beverley Bennett, Natalie Ong, Angela Papanicolaou, Gail Tomsic, Marcia Williamsz, Adam Guastella, Natalie Silove
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAccess to autism diagnostic assessments continues to be problematic for many families and children. While telehealth assessments have been shown to be feasible and reliable in research settings, less is known about the agreement between telehealth autism assessments compared with in‐person evaluations in clinical settings with linguistic, cultural, and social diversity. Twenty‐one minimally verbal children (between 23.9 and 51.7 months, mean = 36.5 months, SD = 8 months) participated in a telehealth autism assessment (the TELE‐ASD‐PEDS; TAP) with a parent, and then in an in‐person, multidisciplinary team assessment. Telehealth clinicians were blinded to history and questionnaire information; in‐person clinicians were blinded to the telehealth results. Assessment results in each setting, along with diagnostic impression (telehealth) and diagnostic outcome (in‐person), were compared. Assessment scores across the settings showed very good agreement and were strongly correlated (r = 0.75, p < 0.001). There was diagnostic agreement (either autism/autism or no‐autism/no‐autism) for 19/21 children, or 90% of the participants. This study adds to the growing literature on autism diagnostic assessments administered via telehealth. Our research builds on previous work by comparing telehealth findings directly with in‐person assessment and diagnostic results. The results of the present study yielded high rates of diagnostic agreement as well as strong agreement between telehealth and in‐person assessment scores for young children with limited language and high levels of autism symptoms, which to our knowledge, have not to date been directly compared.

Introduction

Early assessment and identification of autism in young children is an important step that can allow families to access timely supports for learning, development, and well‐being (Ward et al. 2016; Hsieh et al. 2018). These supports are critical in ensuring that parents are well supported, given that delayed access to assessment and supports is associated with increased parental stress (Elder et al. 2017) and smaller gains across a number of areas of development (Fuller and Kaiser 2020; Rotholz et al. 2017; Whitehouse et al. 2017).

However, children in Australia frequently have limited access to high quality and timely assessment and diagnosis for autism, with delays in accessing health professionals and receiving assessment well documented. For instance, Gibbs et al. (2019) found that families experienced gaps of more than 2 years from first contact with a health professional to autism diagnosis, despite early parental concerns. Boulton et al. (2023) reported an even wider gap, with caregivers in their study identifying developmental concerns by 3 years of age but not receiving a diagnostic assessment until 6.5 years of age on average. Barriers to assessment for these city‐based families included waitlists, financial difficulties, and lack of knowledge of services. Other difficulties associated with assessment can include transportation issues, lost income due to time off for evaluation appointments, and the challenges of supporting children with complex needs in a new place such as a clinic. Difficulties accessing assessment services are further exacerbated by distance and limitations of trained and qualified clinicians in regional and rural areas; children living in rural areas often receive diagnoses later than metropolitan peers (Taylor et al. 2016; Bent et al. 2015).

Telehealth services are one proposed way of supporting families to access timely and accessible services. Telehealth assessments and observations were beginning to be established prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic (Sutherland et al. 2018), but the number and types of tools used have expanded considerably over the past few years, as has research and clinical practice in this area (for a review, see Hodge et al. 2023). One tool of particular interest is the TELE‐ASD‐PEDS (TAP) assessment, developed by researchers at Vanderbilt Medical Center (Wagner et al. 2021). This is a purpose‐built tool for the assessment of children under the age of 36 months using toys and equipment that are readily available in family homes. Assessments are conducted in real time by experienced professionals over telehealth, using platforms appropriate to the setting. A series of 8 structured activities is conducted by parents, guided by clinicians, to provide opportunities for the assessing clinician to observe children's play and communication with their parents/carers. Early research indicated that clinicians report the opportunity of seeing children in their home settings as valuable (Wagner et al. 2021) and initial studies with parents indicated that the tool was feasible and acceptable to families during the pandemic (Jones et al. 2022; Stavropoulos et al. 2022). A feasibility study by Hodge et al. (2023) examined the use of the TAP in a small group of children (n = 18) in a hospital‐based, tertiary level public health setting. This study found that it was possible to obtain useful information about a child's autism‐specific behaviors using the TAP, and when combined with other elements of a gold‐standard assessment (clinical history, adaptive functioning) allowed for diagnosis against the DSM‐5 framework.

McNally Keehn et al. (2023) reported on TAP use during the pandemic with 335 children aged between 14 and 78 months. This group, which included the developers of the TAP, extended the use of the TAP with children beyond 36 months and found it feasible and effective for use in older children with limited language (no functional word use, through to those using occasional two‐word phrases). Their findings indicated that the TAP, when used in conjunction with measures of adaptive functioning and other questionnaires, can provide a definitive autism diagnosis (i.e., autism present vs. absent) for most young children, and that clinicians' certainty of diagnosis did not vary across age, ethnicity, or other variables. However, this study did not provide a comparison with in‐person diagnosis that would clearly confirm the validity of the TAP.

Corona et al. (2024), the TAP developers, examined the validity of the TAP by comparing it and another experimental telehealth screening tool (the TELE‐STAT) with in‐person assessments that included developmental assessment and the Autism Diagnostic Observation Scale—second edition (ADOS‐2; Lord et al. 2012). Across the 144 children (73 of whom were remotely assessed using the TAP), diagnostic agreement between the telehealth assessment and in‐person assessment was 92% (ASD‐ASD or No ASD‐No ASD groups). Eight children diagnosed with autism through in‐person assessment were classified as not autistic via telehealth (ASD‐No ASD group); three were diagnosed as not autistic through in‐person assessment following classification as autistic via telehealth (No ASD‐ASD). Group comparison analysis indicated that these three children were significantly younger than other children in the sample and that clinician certainty was higher for the ASD‐ASD group and lower for all others. However, relatively informal analysis of agreement using percentage agreement and analysis of in‐person and telehealth measures at the group level only means that there are limitations in our understanding of the specific role of the TAP as a direct alternative to in‐person assessment elements like the ADOS‐2. In addition, the study was conducted in a research clinic rather than clinical settings, limiting generalization of these findings. A recent study by the developers (Gangi et al. 2025) considered the use of the TAP across a large, diverse group of 142 children who participated in home‐based assessments with a parent. The findings of this study continue to add support for the TAP, finding that in‐home use of the TAP is valid, reliable, feasible, and acceptable to caregivers.

The aim of this study was to apply the TAP in a real‐world, clinical cohort of children accessing public tertiary clinical services for the assessment of child neurodevelopmental conditions. All children referred to the service have concerns around development, and particularly around autism. Accessing the service requires that the child has seen a general practitioner (primary care physician) and then a general pediatrician, who will refer a child for further assessment if they are unable to make a diagnosis themselves and to further assess or confirm a diagnosis of autism and/or developmental delay. On average, 75% of children across the age range receive an autism‐related diagnosis (Boulton et al. 2023); though a recent study with the population in this research reported that 98% of young children assessed in that study's timeframe received an autism diagnosis (Hodge et al. 2023). The clinical service is one of the largest diagnostic and assessment services in the state of New South Wales and services families from regional and remote areas as well as those who reside in Sydney. Past research that has been conducted in this service, using the Sydney Child Neurodevelopment Research Register (Boulton et al. 2023), has shown that families show high levels of cultural and linguistic diversity (50% of population), are largely from financially vulnerable backgrounds, and have experienced long wait lists to diagnosis and many barriers to service (Boulton et al. 2023; Boulton et al. 2024). This is the type of community cohort that may benefit most from improving access to assessments by using telehealth procedures.

The present study aimed to build on the past work of Hodge et al. (2023), McNally Keehn et al. (2023), Corona et al. (2024), Gangi et al. (2025), and others, by comparing TAP scores and clinical impressions of diagnostic status gained through telehealth assessment with children's in‐person ADOS‐2 scores and/or clinical diagnosis that was determined by the clinical team. The service operates within a multicultural, mixed‐SES, multidisciplinary clinic in an urban Australian context. This is the first study we are aware of to directly compare telehealth and in‐person assessment scores as well as assessment outcomes in a publicly funded developmental assessment service outside of the US and in a study that is independent of the developers of the tool.

Methods

Design

A method comparison design (Bland and Altman 1999) was used in order to measure the agreement between the scores for TAP and the ADOS‐2. This design allows researchers to quantify the variation between two methods of measurement for the same subject. Parents of all children referred to the Child Assessment Service were contacted by the clinical team and invited to participate in the study if they were 5 years of age or under, had significant language limitations (no words through to occasional two‐word phrases, as determined on intake questionnaires and conversation, as per usual practice) and if no interpreter was required for assessment (as per Hodge et al. 2023). All children who met these criteria were invited to participate; there was no further selection on the basis of likelihood of diagnosis or severity of symptoms. The names and contact details of interested families were sent to the research team, who confirmed interest and availability via telephone calls. The research assistant then worked with families to discuss equipment requirements (e.g., toys, relevant technology) and to explain the procedures. The TAP assessment was undertaken without clinical history or interview to provide as objective an assessment using the TAP as possible by the research team. All TAP assessments were led by the research assistant and observed and co‐scored by another member of the research team in 15/21 assessments. Two to 3 weeks later, the ADOS‐2 was administered in person by an independent team of clinicians as part of the standard multidisciplinary diagnostic assessment.

Participants

Children and Families

The participants in this study were 21 children, aged between 23.9 months and 51.7 months (mean = 36.5 months, SD = 8 months). All children were referred to the multidisciplinary Child Assessment Service for a diagnostic assessment for autism. Four of the children were assigned female at birth (girls, 19%), which is broadly in line with the gender ratio in young children with autism (Loomes et al. 2017). The reported communication abilities of the children attending the diagnostic assessment ranged from no words with some open vowel sounds through to 2–4 word phrases; 15 of the 21 children were described as using a few single words. Adaptive functioning of all children was assessed by the clinical team for the in‐person assessment using the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales—third edition (Vineland‐3; Sparrow et al. 2016) and their skills ranged from < 1 percentile to the sixth percentile (these results were not available to the research team prior to the TAP assessment). Parents participated in all the assessments with their children; two fathers and 19 mothers were involved in the TAP assessments. At least one parent in each family spoke English well enough to converse with the research assistant and to follow directions. Culturally and linguistically diverse families were well represented in the sample, with 14 of the 21 families (67%) speaking at least one language other than English as their home language. In terms of parent education, two mothers left high school prior to Year 12 (10%), four had a high school qualification (19%), eight had a diploma or trade (38%), and seven had a bachelor's or master's degrees (33%). Similarly, 5 fathers had a high school qualification (24%), 3 had a diploma or trade (14%), and 10 had a bachelor's or master's degree (48%); one father left school prior to Year 12, and the education level of two fathers was unknown. The rate of post school education qualifications in our sample is just above the national average for women (71% current sample, 63.6% national average; ABS 2024) and on par for men (62% current sample; 63% national average; ABS 2024).

Clinicians

Telehealth: One clinician (author M.W.) led all the telehealth assessments, with co‐scoring, for the purpose of validity checking and consensus from authors A.H. and R.S. for 15/21 assessments. All clinicians have extensive experience in autism assessment (including ADOS‐2 and ADI‐R training) and diagnosis in multidisciplinary teams, with each having more than 20 years clinical experience in their disciplines (speech‐language pathology, neuropsychology).

In‐person: The clinicians involved in administering the in‐person assessments of the children were all employed in the Child Assessment Service and were all qualified to administer the assessments required. Different team members assessed the children, with all seen by a highly experienced pediatrician plus one or more allied health clinicians (psychologist or neuropsychologist, speech‐language pathologist and/or occupational therapist), and all clinicians were trained in delivering and interpreting the ADOS‐2. All were accredited with, or members of, their respective professional organizations. All in‐person clinicians were blinded regarding the children's participation in the telehealth assessment and the results.

Telehealth Platforms and Equipment

The telehealth assessments were conducted via secure, password‐protected Zoom meetings. The clinicians used laptop computers with built‐in webcams and headphones. Families used the technology available to them at the time and included mobile phones, tablet devices, and laptop computers. Most families used earbuds to listen to instructions and information from the clinician where possible; but this was not consistent. Where earbuds were not used, the clinician/s and parent checked whether the child noticed or was distracted by the information from the clinician, but no children showed interest in this.

Measures

TAP and ADOS‐2

The telehealth tool used, the TAP (Corona et al. 2021; Wagner et al. 2021) was developed using a computational model using the ADOS‐2 (toddler module) and other assessments and enables clinicians to observe children's skills and actions within a short, structured, parent‐led play session using materials available in a nonclinical setting (Corona et al. 2021). The clinician guides parents through activities that are designed to provide opportunities for the assessment of seven specific behaviors including socially directed speech sounds, eye contact and repetitive play, which are then rated by clinicians using a Likert scale (3 = behaviors characteristic of autism clearly present; 2 = behaviors characteristic of autism present at subclinical levels; 1 = behaviors characteristic of autism not present). A total raw score is obtained from the behaviors coded. Based on analyses of cut off scores (Wagner et al. 2021), a score of 10 indicates 85% sensitivity to autism. The TAP form includes two further summary questions regarding diagnostic impressions for clinicians: “ASD if forced to choose?” with available responses “absent,” “unsure,” and “present,” and “How certain are you of your diagnostic impression?” with a 4 point Likert scale from 1 = “Completely uncertain” to 4 = “Completely certain.”

The ADOS‐2 (Lord et al. 2012) is a standardized, semi‐structured assessment tool used to assess language and communication, social interaction, play, imagination, and repetitive behaviors and strong interests. Five modules are available for use and are chosen based largely on the language abilities of the person undergoing assessment. All modules include diagnostic algorithms with cut‐off scores to determine diagnostic classification of autism, autism spectrum, or non‐spectrum. In this study, all children were assessed using Module 1, which is appropriate for minimally verbal young children. The ADOS‐2 is internationally recognized as part of a gold‐standard autism assessment and has strong validity, internal consistency, and inter‐rater reliability (Lord et al. 2012). Results of in‐person ADOS‐2 have been shown to correlate with online adaptations of the tool in adults (Blackmore et al. 2023). In the clinical setting in this research, the ADOS‐2 is one part of the assessment process, but it is important to note that the child's history and observations in the clinic and other settings are also considered when determining a diagnosis of autism. In cases where the child's history, presentation in the clinic, and descriptions in naturalistic settings (e.g., preschool, external speech‐language pathology sessions) are clearly indicative of autism, and where an ADOS‐2 assessment would cause distress or not provide useful additional information, a diagnosis may be made by the team without this tool. The raw scores of the ADOS‐2 assessments were used where available (n = 19) and the diagnostic outcomes of all children were recorded (autism or not‐autism).

Analysis

Two methods were adopted to compare the telehealth and face‐to‐face assessments: comparison of the TAP and ADOS‐2 scores, and comparison of telehealth diagnostic impressions and diagnostic outcomes following the in‐person assessment. Bland–Altman method comparison analysis was used to determine the degree of agreement between the reference assessment (the established method of assessment, in this case the ADOS‐2 assessment) with a novel assessment (in this case the TAP). This analysis aims to determine whether two methods can be used interchangeably, as would be needed in the case of remote, telehealth assessments. Graphical representation is used to allow any bias to be visualized and outliers identified. Pearson's correlation was used to determine the level of validity of the assessment. A p value of < 0.05 was considered significant. To compare the ADOS‐2 and TAP scores appropriately, the TAP scores were adjusted using a conversion formula that converted the scale of the TAP to have the same lower and upper levels as the ADOS‐2 scores (y = ((X − X min)/X range)n) where Y is the adjusted variable, X is the original variable, and X min is the minimum observed value on the original variable.

Results

TAP

All families who agreed to the TAP assessment were able to engage with the assessors and the telehealth equipment as required, and all assessments were completed. All sessions were completed in less than 30 min. There were no discontinuations due to technology issues, or child or family distress. Possible total scores on the TAP range from a minimum of 7 through to a maximum of 21; scores for children in the current study ranged from 9 to 20 (M = 16.68, SD = 3.38). A total of 19 children were rated as “autism present,” one child was rated as “somewhat uncertain,” and one child was rated as “autism absent.”

A total of 71% of sessions were co‐scored by a second researcher. Agreement between scores for the two researchers averaged 98% (SD = 3.8, range 87.5%—100%) across the sessions, with 100% agreement on diagnostic impressions.

In‐Person Assessment

All 21 children completed in‐person assessments with their families at one of two sites of the Child Assessment Service. The in‐person assessment followed gold‐standard recommendations for diagnostic assessment and included free play, parent interview, developmental assessment (i.e., Mullen Scales of Early Learning (Mullen 1995), the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development—third (Bayley‐III) or fourth edition (Bayley‐4); Weiss et al. 2010), or the Griffiths Scales of Child Development—third edition (Griffiths‐III; Green et al. 2016) and autism specific assessment/s including the ADOS‐2 and/or the Childhood Autism Rating Scale—Second Edition Standard Version (CARS‐2 ST; Schopler et al. 2010). The assessments allow for a thorough clinical investigation of any co‐occurring conditions, overall development, and any environmental or psychosocial factors that may be involved in the child's presentation. The in‐person assessment is typically completed over several hours, with families traveling between 20 and 90 min to the tertiary hospital. Nineteen of the 21 children completed an ADOS‐2 assessment, while two received a diagnosis of autism based on observed behavior and history that clearly showed sufficient characteristics of autism without necessitating further assessment. The raw ADOS‐2 scores ranged from 2 to 27 (M = 20.1, SD = 7.1), indicating that the sample largely consisted of children with quite significant characteristics of autism. Two children did not receive a diagnosis of autism based on the team assessment; their ADOS‐2 scores were 2 and 8, both well below the cut‐off on the ADOS‐2 Module 1 algorithms. Agreement Between Measures

Once the TAP scores were adjusted, Bland–Altman analyses were used to assess levels of agreement between the adjusted TAP scores and the ADOS‐2 scores. In Bland–Altman analyses, zero equals perfect agreement, and the larger the number for each plot point, the greater the disagreement between assessors (Altman and Bland 1983). Calculations of 95% limits of agreement for each comparison (average difference 1.96 standard deviations of the difference) were made and plotted (dotted lines) on Figure 1. Visual inspection shows that all but one child's scores were within the 95% limits of agreement, and most were well within these limits. The TAP score (not adjusted) and the available ADOS‐2 scores (n = 19) were also found to be strongly correlated (r = 0.75, p < 0.001).

However, visual inspection of the individual scores showed that the greatest differences between the TAP and ADOS‐2 scores was on the unusual or repetitive play item; seven of the 19 children were rated on the TAP as having signs that were obviously consistent with autism, such as repeatedly pushing buttons, watching how objects move, lining things up, or scrambling/dropping toys, but were rated as minimal or no difficulty on the ADOS‐2. No children were rated as showing more repetitive behavior in the ADOS‐2 than the TAP assessment. In contrast, scores for the sensory item were very similar overall, with three children showing much more unusual sensory interest in the ADOS‐2 than on the TAP and one showing many more signs on the TAP.

Diagnostic Agreement

In the telehealth condition (see Table 2), 19 children were rated as “autism present” (“autism TAP”), with one child rated “unsure” (although they met the threshold on the TAP with a score ≥ 11) and one child rated as “autism absent” (and a TAP score under 11, “no autism TAP”). In the in‐person condition, 19 children received a diagnosis of autism and two were not diagnosed with autism. One of these children was also the child rated as “autism absent” (no‐autism TAP/no‐autism) in the telehealth condition, and one was rated as “autism present” on telehealth (autism TAP/no‐autism). The child rated as autism TAP/no‐autism was young (less than 3 years) and showed mostly age‐appropriate to low average skills on their developmental assessment. However, the in‐person description on the child noted social communication difficulties with repetitive vocalizations, repetitive behaviors and difficulties with transitions and a reassessment was recommended. The child rated as “unsure TAP/Autism” showed social strengths in the telehealth assessment with the parents, including coordinated eye contact, joint attention and gesture, though did show differences in play and an inconsistent response to their name. More characteristics of autism were seen in the in‐person assessment for this child. Overall, there was diagnostic agreement (either autism TAP/autism or no‐autism TAP/no‐autism) for 19/21 children, or 90% of the participants.

Discussion and Implications

This study applied the TAPS assessment in the context of a busy public neurodevelopmental assessment clinic and is the first to determine agreement with standard neurodevelopmental assessment procedures conducted within the clinic.

The results of the present study yielded high rates of diagnostic agreement as well as strong agreement between telehealth and in‐person assessment scores among young, minimally verbal children referred for an autism assessment, which to our knowledge, have not previously been directly compared. The sample recruited into this study also reflected typical populations seen by this service, with families from culturally and linguistically diverse communities and with variable education and socioeconomic levels. Our findings add an understanding of the validity of the telehealth assessments against gold standard face‐to‐face assessments within a clinical Australian context with this group of young children.

The scoring of the TAP showed high levels of agreement with the in‐person ADOS assessments; however, the recommended cut‐off scores of the TAP have developed over time. Original guidelines around the use of the TAP suggested that scores of 11 or more indicated an increased likelihood of an autism diagnosis (Wagner et al. 2022). More recent research has suggested that slightly increasing this score to 12+ provides a more accurate indication of autism and reduces the likelihood of inaccurate identification (Corona et al. 2024). In our cohort, however, using this higher number would have resulted in two false negative results; both the children who received a TAP score of 11 were subsequently diagnosed with autism by the in‐person team, and the child who was rated as autistic on the TAP but not by the in‐person team scored 13 on the TAP. The child who was rated as not autistic by both the in‐person and TAP evaluators scored 9 on the TAP, well below either suggested cut‐off scores. It may be the case that clinical judgment plays an important role in identifying potential autism using the TAP. As with the ADOS‐2, a decision about an autism diagnosis would not be made solely based on a score, but by using a combination of parent history and current concerns, reports from a variety of contexts, and clinical observations. Using the TAP as part of a multifaceted approach may mean that while scores provide a guide, diagnostic decisions are more robust when made by triangulating information within an experienced team that includes parents and other caregivers.

There were a few discrepancies noted between the diagnostic impressions using the TAP and the eventual diagnostic decision by the in‐person team, with one false positive (autism TAP/no‐autism) and one potential false negative (unsure TAP/autism). The child rated as autism TAP/no‐autism showed a range of autism characteristics in both the telehealth and the in‐person conditions, but based on parent reports of rapid gains and good skills observed in the developmental assessment, a watch and wait approach, with reassessment recommended, was adopted for this child by the in‐person team. The pattern with this child shows some similarity with those seen in the Corona et al. (2024) paper in which children with the autism/no‐autism pattern tended to be younger, with higher developmental skills. The second child (unsure TAP/autism) appears to have shown a somewhat different profile in the in‐person condition to the telehealth condition. In the telehealth condition, the child showed communicative and coordinated eye contact, gesture, and vocalization during the child's interactions with their parent, along with some difficulties with response to their name and some rigidity in their play. It is apparent that these features, as well as sensory differences, were much more obvious during the in‐person assessment, leading to a clear autism diagnosis for this child. These differences again highlight the importance of observations of a child across a range of settings and activities. Current guidelines for best practice autism diagnoses (Whitehouse et al. 2018) recommend assessments using a holistic framework with evaluations across personal, activity, and environmental contexts. Gaining information from clinical, home, and other settings, including education settings, allows clinicians to triangulate information and understand both the strengths and the difficulties the child is experiencing in order to make accurate diagnoses.

It is possible that the TAP provides additional information for diagnosticians that may not be easily seen in a one‐off clinic‐based assessment. The individual scores at the item level indicated that repetitive behaviors were more clearly seen on the home‐based TAP for 7 of the 19 children, and that no children showed more repetitive behaviors in the clinic‐based ADOS‐2 assessment. The reasons for this are unclear, but may be related to the familiarity of toys and objects at home and the routines and repetitive behaviors that have developed, versus the novelty of toys in the clinic. It is also worth noting that test–retest reliability measures on the ADOS‐2 (Lord et al. 2012) are lower for the repetitive behaviors items than for social affect and the overall test, showing questionable reliability (0.68) on this domain. McNally Keehn et al. (2023) found that the presence of specific repetitive behaviors was a strong predictor of an autism diagnosis; this suggests that a clear understanding of children's behaviors at home is crucial and that the TAP may be an important tool to consider as an adjunct to in‐person clinical assessment in some instances.

The current research has continued to show that the TAP is feasible in a community clinical setting. As with Hodge et al. (2023), all telehealth sessions were able to be completed by parents, using readily available, consumer‐grade equipment (phones and laptop computers that families had access to; standard laptops with built in camera and microphones for the clinicians). Families were adept at improvising with technology where needed, using earbuds, speakers, and additional phones to manage audio and video needs. The feasibility of the tool was also noted in its use across the age range of this cohort. The age range of the current cohort was 23.9 months to 51.7 months, and good agreement across telehealth and in‐person assessments was seen at both the younger and older ends of the group. While the TAP was originally designed for children up to the age of 36 months, it has been shown to be useful and feasible beyond this age with children with limited language (McNally Keehn et al. 2023; Hodge et al. 2023; Gangi et al. 2025). Our research indicates that it may also show validity with older but minimally verbal children. Importantly, the inclusion of children and families that are diverse in many ways (language, culture, education) and the feasibility of using this tool across the cohort suggest that telehealth assessments may provide an additional acceptable way to provide support to a wide range of families.

Limitations and Strengths

This study has a number of limitations, including small sample size and a small group of children without autism. A larger sample size of both clinical and nonclinical cohorts would permit exploration of the precision of the TAP through calculation of the positive and negative predictive values. Feedback from parents/carers would have added to the findings of this research. We did not engage the autistic community in the codesign process. The value of a codesigned approach has been previously demonstrated to contribute to the sustainability of a program (Abimbola et al. 2019). The children were enrolled in the study in a non‐randomized manner. Thus, it was not possible to exclude the role of selection bias. Further, the nature of the clinical cohort meant that the majority of the children assessed met criteria for an autism diagnosis, meaning that the tool has shown to be useful in ruling in an autism diagnosis, but the usefulness in ruling out a diagnosis is less clear. It will be important that future studies include children from community cohorts to further assess the diagnostic validity of the tool. The strengths of the study include the real‐world clinical contexts in which the children were assessed, the heterogeneous nature of the cohort in terms of language background and parent education, and the direct comparison of the TAP and ADOS‐2 scores.

Future Directions

Our study has added to the growing body of work considering telehealth or remote assessments for autism. The findings suggest that the TAP may be useful in identifying autism reliably in some young children, particularly those with significant signs of autism, when the tool is combined with strong case history, questionnaires, and observations from other settings. It is also likely that the TAP may be a useful adjunct assessment in many cases where additional information and observations are required, such as when parent reports differ significantly from observed behavior in a clinic setting. In the current study, the mean of the ADOS‐2 for the group was 20, indicating a high level of autism symptomology among the group. While this study has shown good agreement between assessments for this group, the accuracy of the TAP on a broader range of children, including those with lower scores close to the cut‐off, still must be evaluated. Examination of the convergent validity of the newly developed Tele ASD Kids (designed for use with children with phrase speech, and fluent speech) with the ADOS‐2 should be explored in the future. Future studies could also examine the feasibility in non‐urban populations where access to diagnostic and assessment services is limited. Furthermore, there is a need to examine the feasibility of undertaking assessments in other settings, such as childcare centers and preschools with educators as assessment facilitators. This may contribute to the growing evidence base for the scalability of the teleassessments. Finally, this paper provides researchers and clinicians further insights into the practical considerations associated with feasible telehealth autism assessments. Ensuring that families have access to appropriate devices is crucial; it was reassuring to note that families were able to use a range of devices (phones, tablets, laptops) without compromising audio or video quality. Choice of platform for bandwidth, particularly when working with families where internet access is limited or patchy, can make a difference to the assessment, as can location; some families may need to use community spaces such as preschools to access the internet. Preparation was also key in providing these assessments, as it is in any telehealth service. Working with families prior to the assessment allowed them to prepare the appropriate materials and to understand their role in the session, leading to feasible and useful assessments for the family.
