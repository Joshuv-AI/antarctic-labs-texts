Predicting adverse prognostic outcomes in hospitalized breast cancer patients: development and validation of a risk model
Authors: Liya Ma, Endi Cai, Zhiyuan Zhou, Mengchun Gong, Chen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer prognosis is influenced by diverse clinical factors, necessitating advanced predictive tools for personalized treatment. This study developed machine learning models to predict adverse outcomes in breast cancer patients using comprehensive clinical data.MethodsWe analyzed data from 4,242 patients (3,836 with stable progression and 406 with adverse outcomes) at a single institution. Feature selection via univariate/multivariate analysis and machine learning importance ranking identified 10 key predictors, including metastasis stage, hospitalization duration, total healthcare costs, and laboratory test results. Ten machine learning models were evaluated using 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 undersampling ratios to address class imbalance. Performance was assessed via accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, the area under the curve (AUC), and precision.ResultsThe Random Forest (RF) model achieved an optimal balance with 88.69% accuracy, 0.733 sensitivity, and 0.952 specificity (AUC range: 0.881–0.898) across sampling ratios, demonstrating superior class separation.SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis revealed metastasis (SHAP = + 6.0) as the strongest risk factor, while marital status and Luminal A subtype were protective. Logistic Regression(LR) also performed robustly (AUC > 0.88), excelling in specificity (0.950 under 1:3 undersampling).ConclusionMachine learning models, particularly RF and LR, effectively predicted prognosis by integrating clinical and laboratory features. RF’s stability in imbalanced data and interpretability via SHAP enhanced clinical utility. Future multi-center studies should validate these findings and explore causal mechanisms.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Breast cancer prognosis, Machine learning, Random forest, Logistic regression, Data imbalance, SHapley Additive exPlanations

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignant tumor among women [1]. According to global cancer statistics released by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), there were approximately 2.31 million new cases of breast cancer and 665,700 related deaths worldwide in 2022, making it the leading cause of both incidence and mortality among all female malignancies [2]. Women aged 50 and above are considered a high-risk group for breast cancer [3]. However, recent studies have shown an increasing, imposing a significant burden on patients, families, and society [4, 5]. Globally, by 2021, the number of new cases and age-standardized incidence rates of early-onset female breast cancer had increased compared to 1990. In China, new cases rose from 36,700 to 110,400, and the age-standardized incidence rate increased from 6.83 to 13.49 per 100,000, representing a relative increase of 97.51% [6].

Despite significant advancements in breast cancer treatment in recent years, including the comprehensive application of various methods such as surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, some patients still face adverse prognostic outcomes such as recurrence, metastasis, and death. These outcomes severely impact patients’ quality of life and long-term survival rates. Statistical data indicate that approximately 20%-30% of breast cancer patients experience recurrence within five years of treatment, while the five-year survival rate for metastatic breast cancer patients is only about 30%. These adverse outcomes not only impose heavy psychological and economic burdens on patients and their families but also present significant challenges to the healthcare system [7–8].

With the rapid development of machine learning and big data technologies, the analysis and modeling of vast amounts of medical data using advanced techniques hold promise for further improving the accuracy and practicality of predictive models. Methods such as decision trees (DT) [9], random forests (RF), neural networks, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), logistic regression (LR), and support vector machines (SVM) have been widely explored [10–14]. Clift and Dodwell et al. focused primarily on treatment-related factors including medication use, c-erbB-2 status, surgical approach, lymph node involvement, cancer stage and tumor grade, ultimately concluding that traditional Cox and competing risk models may offer greater clinical utility than machine learning approaches [15]. Meanwhile, Sammut and Crispin-Ortuzar employed LR, SVM, and ensemble models to integrate clinical, genomic, and digital pathology data, demonstrating that their integrated model (AUC = 0.87) substantially outperformed clinical-only predictors (AUC = 0.70), thereby highlighting genomic markers as key determinants of treatment response [16]. In another approach, Kalafi et al. applied deep learning techniques to analyze standard demographic and clinical parameters including diagnostic methods, tumor characteristics and nodal status, finding that neural network architectures outperformed conventional machine learning models while confirming established prognostic factors like tumor size, stage and nodal involvement [17]. Despite these advances, current prognostic models remain limited by their predominant focus on diagnostic staging parameters and their general lack of interpretability [18, 19], while largely overlooking the potential predictive value of routine inpatient laboratory measures.

Machine learning (ML) offers powerful capabilities for analyzing complex clinical data to enhance prognostic accuracy. Techniques including Decision Trees (DT) [9], Random Forests (RF), Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and gradient boosting methods (e.g., XGBoost) have been extensively applied in oncology [10–14]. However, existing models exhibit limitations. Some studies prioritize traditional statistical approaches like Cox models over ML for clinical interpretability, focusing primarily on static diagnostic factors (e.g., tumor stage, grade, nodal status) [15]. Others integrate advanced data types (e.g., genomics, digital pathology) to boost performance but often lack immediate clinical deployability in routine inpatient settings [16]. While deep learning shows promise, its “black-box” nature and computational demands hinder interpretability and practical integration into clinical workflows, especially for structured electronic health record (EHR) data [17]. Crucially, most models neglect the potential predictive value of dynamic, routinely collected inpatient laboratory indicators (e.g., serial albumin, potassium levels), which reflect a patient’s real-time physiological status and treatment response during hospitalization [18, 19].

To address these gaps and meet the specific demands of real-time risk stratification in the hospital environment, we developed and validated interpretable ML models leveraging readily available clinical data. This study presents—to our knowledge—one of the first prognostic models to integrate comprehensive inpatient laboratory measures with established predictors such as molecular subtype and metastasis status. Our approach prioritizes clinical utility and interpretability – essential for bedside decision-making. We employed a rigorous methodology: (1) Univariate and multivariate analysis to screen candidate predictors from a broad feature set; (2) Feature importance ranking using diverse ML algorithms (LR, SVM, RF) to identify the most robust predictors; (3) Comprehensive evaluation of multiple classical ML models (including RF, LR, XGBoost) across balanced datasets to ensure generalizability; and critically, (4) SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to elucidate model logic and feature contributions.We deliberately focused on well-established, efficient, and inherently interpretable ML methods (particularly RF and LR) rather than deep learning or large language models. This choice was driven by key considerations for clinical deployment: the predominance of structured EHR data, moderate dataset size (N = 4,242), the imperative for high model interpretability to gain clinician trust, and the need for computational efficiency within hospital IT infrastructure. RF and LR excel in these aspects, offering transparency alongside strong predictive performance.

By developing this interpretable risk prediction tool, we aim to provide clinicians with actionable insights during a patient’s hospitalization, enabling earlier identification of high-risk individuals and facilitating tailored management strategies to ultimately improve outcomes.

Outcome Variable. Adverse outcome was defined as a composite endpoint comprising either disease progression (including local recurrence or distant metastasis) or all-cause mortality during the follow-up period. This composite measure captures clinically significant events reflecting treatment failure and long-term disease burden, and was selected for its relevance to prognosis and clinical decision‑making.

Clinical relevance and diagnostic criteria:

Local recurrence: Histologically confirmed recurrence in the ipsilateral breast, chest wall, or regional lymph nodes (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD-10]: C50.0-C50.9 with recurrence code Z85.3).

Distant metastasis: Radiologically or pathologically confirmed metastasis to distant organs (ICD-10: C78.0-C79.9 for secondary malignant neoplasms).

All-cause mortality: Death from any cause during follow-up (ICD-10: R96-R99 for ill-defined causes, supplemented by specific cause-of-death codes when available).

This composite endpoint was chosen to provide a comprehensive assessment of treatment failure and long-term disease burden, reflecting both disease-specific progression and overall survival outcomes.

Data preprocessing

To address missing values in the dataset, we utilized the micepackage in R for multiple imputation. This method generates multiple complete datasets by imputing missing values based on observed data, thereby reducing bias and preserving data variability. After imputation, we first performed univariate analysis to identify potential risk factors associated with the outcomes. Subsequently, multivariate analysis was conducted to adjust for confounding factors and determine independent predictors.

Addressing class imbalance​

Given the significant imbalance between the stable progression group (N = 3,836) and the adverse outcome group (N = 406), we employed random undersampling of the majority class to create balanced datasets for model training and evaluation. Three distinct balanced datasets were generated with majority-to-minority ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3. Performance was evaluated across all ratios to assess model robustness to varying degrees of balance. The original imbalanced dataset was also analyzed for comparison.

Data analysis and feature selection

In the Baseline characteristics analysis, normally distributed data were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. For between-group comparisons, independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed data, Mann-Whitney U test (for two groups) or Kruskal-Wallis test (for multiple groups) was used for non-normally distributed data, and chi-square test was used for categorical variables.

Notably, deep learning or large language models were not employed, as their “black-box” nature, high computational demands, and data requirements are less suited to the structured EHR data, moderate sample size, and paramount need for interpretability in this clinical risk stratification context. Hyperparameters for each model (e.g., number of trees and depth for RF/XGB/LGBM, regularization strength for LR/SVM, number of neighbors for KNN) were optimized using 5-fold cross-validation on the training set, aiming to maximize the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

Model evaluation and validation

Model performance was comprehensively assessed using metrics critical for clinical prognosis:

Accuracy: Overall correct prediction rate.

Sensitivity (Recall): Ability to correctly identify patients with adverse outcomes (minimizing false negatives).

Specificity: Ability to correctly identify patients with stable progression (minimizing false positives).

Precision: Proportion of predicted adverse outcomes that are correct.

Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC): Overall discriminative ability across all classification thresholds.

Validation followed a rigorous two-stage process: (1)Internal Validation & Robustness to Imbalance: Models were trained and evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation on four datasets: the original imbalanced data and the three undersampled balanced datasets (1:1, 1:2, 1:3). Performance metrics were averaged across folds. (2) External Validation: To assess generalizability and real-world applicability, we employed a strict temporal split:

Training/Internal Validation Set:Patients admitted between 2005 and 2020;

Training/Internal Validation Set:Patients admitted between 2005 and 2020;

External Test Set:Patients admitted between 2021 and 2024.Models trained on the 2005-2020 data (using the best-performing sampling ratio identified internally) were evaluated solely on the unseen 2021-2024 data.

Model Interpretability​

To enhance clinical utility and trust, we employed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis using the Python shaplibrary. SHAP values quantify the contribution of each feature to the prediction for an individual patient, providing intuitive, model-agnostic explanations. This allows clinicians to understand why a patient is predicted to be high-risk, identifying key driving factors.

Overall, Random Forest achieved the highest accuracy (93.43%) and AUC (0.906), demonstrating robust classification capability, though its sensitivity (0.462) remained suboptimal, indicating limited detection of positive cases. AdaBoost exhibited a balanced performance, with competitive accuracy (93.29%), the highest sensitivity among all models (0.508), and strong specificity (0.974), suggesting its potential as a reliable classifier for imbalanced data. In contrast, Support Vector Machine (SVM) showed poor sensitivity (0.077) despite high specificity (0.994), rendering it unsuitable for scenarios requiring minority class detection. While XGBoost and LightGBM delivered moderate accuracy (92.60–93.15%) and AUC (0.845–0.875), their sensitivity (0.415–0.446) remained inadequate, reflecting a common limitation across models in identifying positive instances. These results highlight the persistent challenge of class imbalance, as evidenced by universally low sensitivity (< 0.55) despite high specificity (> 0.95) and accuracy (> 90%). Future work should prioritize techniques to enhance sensitivity, such as resampling or cost-sensitive learning, particularly for applications where minority class detection is critical.

The 1:1 undersampling analysis revealed significant improvements in model performance compared to the original imbalanced dataset (Table 7). Random Forest emerged as the top-performing model, achieving 86.61% accuracy with balanced sensitivity (0.813) and specificity (0.906), while maintaining high AUC (0.881) and precision (0.867). AdaBoost demonstrated exceptional specificity (0.953) and the highest precision (0.921), albeit with slightly lower sensitivity (0.729), suggesting it may be particularly suitable for applications where false positives carry high costs. XGBoost and LightGBM showed comparable performance, with sensitivity around 0.77–0.79 and AUC > 0.86, indicating robust predictive capability. Notably, Logistic Regression exhibited unexpectedly strong performance (83.93% accuracy, 0.875 precision), challenging the conventional wisdom that linear models underperform on balanced datasets. In contrast, SVM and KNN remained suboptimal, with KNN showing particularly poor specificity (0.516), likely due to their inherent limitations in handling the feature space of this dataset. These results collectively demonstrate that 1:1 undersampling effectively mitigates class imbalance issues, with tree-based ensemble methods (Random Forest, AdaBoost, XGBoost) delivering the most balanced performance across all metrics. The strong performance of multiple models suggests that the predictive features in this dataset contain substantial signal for classification when balanced sampling is employed.

The 1:2 undersampling (majority-to-minority ratio) results demonstrate further optimization of model performance compared to both the original imbalanced data and 1:1 balanced approach (Table 7). Gradient Boosting and Random Forest jointly achieved the highest accuracy (88.69% and 88.10%, respectively), with Gradient Boosting showing marginally better sensitivity (0.733 vs. 0.733) and precision (0.936 vs. 0.917). Notably, these models maintained exceptional specificity (> 0.96) and AUC (> 0.89), indicating robust discriminative power while preserving detection capability for the minority class. AdaBoost continued to excel in precision (0.932) and specificity (0.972), though its sensitivity (0.683) slightly trailed the top performers, reinforcing its suitability for precision-critical applications.

The XGBoost and LightGBM models exhibited balanced performance with sensitivity around 0.68–0.70 and AUC > 0.87, though LightGBM showed a small but notable precision drop (0.872 vs. XGBoost’s 0.875). Logistic Regression maintained surprisingly strong performance (83.93% accuracy, 0.902 precision) despite its linear nature, though its sensitivity (0.617) remained suboptimal. Strikingly, SVM demonstrated extreme specificity (0.991) and high precision (0.923) but catastrophically low sensitivity (0.200), effectively rendering it useless for minority class detection despite its apparent high accuracy (70.83%). KNN performed poorest overall (67.26% accuracy, 0.350 sensitivity), likely due to the curse of dimensionality in this feature space.

The 1:3 undersampling results reveal several important trends in model performance (Table 7). Random Forest, AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, and LightGBM demonstrated identical overall accuracy (88.39%) and sensitivity (0.679), though their underlying characteristics differed. Gradient Boosting achieved the highest AUC (0.875) among these models, suggesting marginally better class separation capability. Notably, all four models maintained excellent specificity (0.952) and comparable precision (0.826), indicating robust performance across evaluation metrics.

The limitations of certain algorithms became particularly apparent in this ratio. SVM showed extreme specificity (0.976) but catastrophic sensitivity (0.196), while KNN performed poorly across all metrics (74.11% accuracy, 0.250 sensitivity), reinforcing their unsuitability for this classification task.

Random Forest demonstrated consistent superiority across all sampling ratios, achieving optimal performance balance at the 1:2 ratio with 88.69% accuracy, 0.733 sensitivity, and 0.952 specificity. The model exhibited remarkable stability in discriminative ability, maintaining AUC values between 0.881–0.898—significantly outperforming competing models in class separation.

Figure 6 displays the DCA (Decision Curve Analysis) of the model. The DCA results demonstrate that, across the threshold range of 0.0 to 0.5, the predictive model constructed in this study (“Our Model”) exhibits greater clinical net benefit than the strategies of “treating all patients” (“All”) and “treating no patients” (“None”) at the majority of threshold points. Specifically, within the critical decision range of approximately 0.07 to 0.30, the model curve consistently lies above the two reference lines, indicating its clear clinical utility in effectively assisting physicians to balance the risks of overtreatment and undertreatment. Particularly in the threshold range of 0.10–0.20, the model’s net benefit reaches a relatively high level, suggesting that it can provide significant benefits for guiding clinical decision‑making within this range. In summary, the DCA validates the model’s good clinical applicability and potential for decision improvement at reasonable risk thresholds.

Discussion

This study included 4242 patients, divided into a stable progression group (3836 cases) and an adverse prognosis group (406 cases). Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted for indicator screening, followed by the use of logistic regression, SVM, random forests models to select indicators with importance above the median for further analysis. The selected indicators included M_Stage, LOS, THC, Pro_Alb, Adm_K, Pro_TC, Pro_TP, Adm_TP, Adm_lb, Pro_AGR.

The study employed various models, including Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, K-Nearest Neighbors, AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, LightGBM, and Linear Discriminant Analysis. To address data imbalance, 1:2 undersampling was applied. The results demonstrated that in 1:2, 1:1, and 1:3 undersampled datasets, models such as logistic regression and random forest performed exceptionally well, with Random Forest showing the best overall performance (AUC = 0.881). These models excelled in key performance metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, AUC, and precision, indicating that undersampling effectively mitigated data imbalance and enhanced the models’ ability to identify both positive and negative classes.

In handling imbalanced datasets, undersampling at ratios of 1:2, 1:1, and 1:3 significantly improved the issue of data imbalance, enhancing the models’ ability to identify positive classes (e.g., adverse prognosis) while maintaining high recognition rates for negative classes (e.g., no adverse prognosis).

Our key finding is that traditional machine learning algorithms—particularly Random Forest (RF) and Logistic Regression (LR)—achieved robust and interpretable performance when integrating novel predictors derived from inpatient laboratory monitoring alongside established clinical features. This approach demonstrates significant potential for clinical deployment in resource-constrained hospital settings.

Machine learning models have demonstrated superior performance in various aspects of breast cancer management, including efficient screening, diagnosis, precise prognosis assessment, and personalized treatment plans. For instance, machine learning has been used to predict survival rates at different stages of breast cancer [20] and to identify non-indications for radiotherapy using DIBH [12]. Widely adopted models encompass traditional machine learning algorithms such as K-nearest Neighbors (KNN), random forests (RF), and support vector machines (SVM) [13, 14]. In contemporary machine learning research, it is common practice to employ a variety of algorithms to construct different models and evaluate their performance to identify the optimal one. For example, Huang Q, Yang Y et al. developed models using different machine learning algorithms to predict high-risk symptom burden clusters in breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, identifying the RF algorithm as the best performer with an AUC of 0.91, while logistic regression outperformed in calibration metrics [14]. Song X and Chu J et al. included 6,477 patients from affiliated hospitals, using eight different machine learning algorithms and varying training/testing set ratios to validate 15 clinical features significantly associated with breast cancer survival. They found that XGBoost exhibited the highest performance, with age, tumor size, lymph node status, and hormone receptor status being important prognostic predictors [21].

In the analysis of 1:1 undersampled data, Random Forest (RF) models demonstrated superior performance (AUC = 0.881), followed by XGBoost (AUC = 0.875) and Logistic Regression (LR, AUC = 0.859).The selection of classical machine learning algorithms (RF, LR, SVM, XGBoost, etc.) over deep learning or large language models was deliberate and grounded in the specific demands of clinical risk stratification. Our primary objectives were to develop a model that is: (1) highly interpretable to foster clinician trust and guide decision-making, (2) computationally efficient for potential integration into real-time hospital workflows, and (3) robust with the available structured electronic health record (EHR) data and sample size (N = 4,242). Deep learning models, while powerful in domains like image analysis, often function as “black boxes,” requiring substantial computational resources and larger datasets to avoid overfitting—characteristics incompatible with the need for transparent, actionable risk scores at the point of care. RF and LR, enhanced by SHAP analysis, provided the optimal balance of predictive accuracy, stability across validation sets, and critically, the ability to elucidate the driving factors behind predictions.

While previous breast cancer prognostic studies have achieved optimal results, their feature selection lacked comprehensiveness. Prior research by Ganggayah and Taib considered demographic characteristics, surgical approaches, and tumor grade but omitted clinical laboratory indicators [18]. Similarly, Zuo’s analysis of recurrence risk focused solely on clinical features such as tumor size, cancer grade, and molecular subtypes [19].

Our study innovatively incorporates both admission laboratory tests (Adm_K, Adm_TP, Adm_lb) and follow-up indicators (Pro_Alb, Pro_TC, Pro_TP, Pro_AGR) alongside conventional clinical features. Through univariate analysis, multivariate analysis, and feature importance ranking, we identified the top 10 predictive indicators. Our findings establish that admission laboratory markers and prognostic indicators serve as significant predictors of adverse outcomes (mortality or recurrence) in breast cancer patients.

The pronounced class imbalance (406 adverse vs. 3,836 stable outcomes) presented a major challenge. Our systematic application of random undersampling (1:1, 1:2, 1:3 ratios) effectively mitigated this imbalance, substantially improving model sensitivity (identification of adverse outcomes) while preserving high specificity. Among the evaluated algorithms, RF consistently demonstrated the best overall performance, achieving peak accuracy (88.69%), sensitivity (0.733), specificity (0.952), and AUC (0.881–0.898) in the 1:2 undersampled data.LR also performed exceptionally well, particularly excelling in specificity (0.950 in 1:3) and offering inherent interpretability. This finding aligns with prior studies utilizing ML for breast cancer prognosis. For instance, Huang et al. similarly identified RF as the top performer (AUC = 0.91) for predicting high-risk symptom clusters during chemotherapy, while Song et al. found XGBoost optimal for survival prediction, highlighting tumor characteristics like size and nodal status [14, 21]. Our results reinforce that RF and LR are highly effective for structured clinical prognostic modeling.

SHAP analysis quantified the risk contribution of individual features, identifying metastasis (SHAP = + 6.0) as the predominant risk driver, with an impact substantially exceeding that of treatment-related variables (chemotherapy: +2.5; reconstruction surgery: +1.2). Protective factors primarily included marital status (–2.8) and the Luminal A molecular subtype (–2.1). In this model, the individualized prediction for this patient (–1.044 versus a baseline of 0.514) was positively influenced by biochemical markers (e.g., THC = + 1.12) and negatively influenced by metastatic progression. Notably, the modest protective effect associated with reconstruction surgery is more likely to reflect clinical selection bias rather than a direct biological protective mechanism. These findings not only enhance the interpretability of the model but also provide a quantitative basis for understanding the relative weight of risk factors and advancing personalized diagnosis and treatment.

Limitations and future research

Limitations

.This study has several limitations. First, the data were derived from a single institution, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings due to regional variations in healthcare resources, patient demographics, and clinical practices. Second, the study focused primarily on establishing predictive performance without investigating causal relationships between the identified indicators and adverse outcomes, thereby limiting its contribution to mechanistic understanding. Third, the temporal validation design (training set: 2005–2020; validation set: 2021–2024) may introduce confounding, as significant advances in breast cancer management—such as the widespread adoption of HER2-targeted therapies—during this period could independently influence patient outcomes and affect the interpretation of model performance across different treatment eras. Finally, this work remains a proof-of-concept study and has not yet been developed into a readily applicable clinical tool, such as a nomogram or risk score, for point-of-care use.

Future Research

To address these limitations, future research should consider the following directions: First, multi-center collaborations incorporating data from diverse geographic and healthcare settings are needed to enhance the external validity and clinical applicability of the model. Second, integrating multi-omics data (e.g., genomics, proteomics, transcriptomics) could help elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying the predictive indicators and bridge the gap between prediction and pathological insight. Third, methodologically, feature selection could be refined using regularization approaches such as Lasso regression, and robustness could be improved through multi-algorithm voting schemes; moreover, methods such as propensity score matching should be applied in validation designs to control for confounding due to temporal changes in treatment standards. Fourth, efforts should be directed toward translating the model into user-friendly clinical tools (e.g., risk calculators) and conducting implementation studies to evaluate their feasibility and utility in real-world settings. Finally, systematic comparison with established prognostic models is essential to clarify the incremental value and clinical positioning of the proposed tool.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated machine learning models to predict adverse prognosis in breast cancer patients using a cohort of 4,242 cases (3,836 stable progression, 406 adverse outcomes). Through rigorous feature selection (univariate/multivariate analysis and machine learning-based importance ranking), we identified key predictive indicators, including tumor metastasis stage (M_Stage), hospitalization duration (LOS), total healthcare costs (THC), and critical laboratory markers (Adm_K, Adm_TP, Pro_Alb, etc.). Among multiple algorithms evaluated, Random Forest (RF) demonstrated superior performance, achieving an AUC of 0.881 in 1:2 undersampled data, with balanced sensitivity (0.679) and specificity (0.952). SHAP analysis further revealed metastasis as the strongest risk predictor (SHAP = + 6.0), while marital status and Luminal A subtype exhibited protective effects.Overall, these methodological strategies—including rigorous handling of class imbalance, selection of robust algorithms, and interpretable explainability techniques—collectively contribute to a clinically applicable and transparent prognostic tool. Future validation in multi-center, prospective cohorts, along with benchmarking against existing clinical scores, will further establish its utility in real-world oncology practice.
