Clinical and pathologic correlations of machine learning quantification of Aβ deposits across 3 brain regions of decedents with Alzheimer disease
Authors: David Garcia, Shivam Rajendra Rai Sharma, Naomi Saito, Laurel Beckett, Louise Nicole C Sevilla, La Rissa Vasquez, Charles S DeCarli, David Gutman, Juan Vizcarra, David G Coughlin, Andrew F Teich, Lorena Garcia, Dan M Mungas, Chen-Nee Chuah, Brittany N Dugger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMachine learning enables scalable quantification of neuropathology, offering deeper phenotyping of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). In this validation study, we quantified amyloid-beta (Aβ) deposits, evaluating multiple brain regions across institutions, and evaluated associations with clinical, demographic, and genetic factors in persons pathologically diagnosed with AD. All linear models were adjusted for sex, age of death, ethnicity, and center. We analyzed densities (#/mm2) of cored plaques, diffuse plaques, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) in 273 individuals from 3 Alzheimer’s Disease Research Centers. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections of frontal, temporal, and parietal cortices were immunostained and digitized, generating 799 whole-slide images (WSIs). Following log transformation, mixed-effects modeling revealed the parietal cortex had the highest cored plaque densities (P < .001); the temporal cortex had the highest diffuse plaque (P < .001); CAA showed no regional differences. Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and covariates adjusted linear models showed ApoE ε4− status was associated with higher cored plaque densities in the temporal lobe (P = .04). ApoE ε4+ status was associated with diffuse plaques in the temporal lobe (P = .001), and CAA in the frontal lobe (P = .004). These findings provide further validation and provide exploratory associations advancing deeper phenotyping of AD.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a devastating neurodegenerative brain disorder that deteriorates memory and other vital cognitive abilities, causing severe disruption to daily life. Globally, AD is the leading cause of dementia and the most prevalent neurodegenerative disease.1,2 In the United States, almost 7 million people are currently living with AD, and it is projected this number will rise to nearly 13 million by 2050.3,4 The cardinal neuropathological features of AD include amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs). These features are formed from the extracellular aggregation of Aβ protein and the intraneuronal aggregation of hyperphosphorylated tau protein, respectively.5,6 Aβ plaques are observed in diverse morphologies throughout the brain.7 Two subtypes of Aβ plaques are cored and diffuse.8 Furthermore, vascular pathologies such as cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), wherein Aβ accumulates in the walls of blood vessels, can be found within the setting of AD.9–11 Postmortem histopathological assessment of Aβ deposition is critical for the final diagnosis of AD.6,12

The postmortem assessment of Aβ deposits typically involves binary and/or semi-quantitative evaluation of the brain by an expert.13–15 Although these evaluation methods have their advantages,16 the advent of digital pathology has presented an opportunity to achieve scalable deeper phenotyping by quantifying pathology burden in brain tissues. The emergence of novel imaging technology, such as whole-slide imaging, has revolutionized histopathological evaluations, becoming a compelling modality.17,18 The digitization of whole slide images (WSIs) has aided in the development of automated ways to assess AD pathologies, including plaques.19–25 Furthermore, many groups, including our own, have developed and refined methods to quantify many pathologies using digital slide images.20,22,26–28 Studies have shown quantitative methods can distinguish between cored plaques (CPs) and diffuse plaques (DPs),23,29 as well as providing density measures in alignment with current semi-quantitative scores.30 Previous studies have employed quantitative methods to examine relationships between Aβ pathologies in the temporal lobe for a cohort consisting primarily of non-Hispanic White decedents.29 However, further validation is needed to determine how generalizable these workflows are to the type of digital scanner used, anatomic area, and/or institute. Furthermore, with respect to clinicopathologic correlations, most prior studies focus on the spectrum from “normal” to AD; few have examined heterogeneity within AD, an approach to enhance precision medicine strategies.31–34 Studies have shown the presence of multiple cardiovascular comorbidities in persons with AD, and the literature has not reached definite conclusions regarding their associations.35–38 How the data are evaluated (binary vs. semi-quantitative vs quantitative), as well as the types of cohorts examined (ie, persons not having many concomitant diagnoses outside of AD or examined across the spectrum of normal to AD), may explain mixed findings. Quantitative pathology data have the potential to enhance disease phenotyping by providing additional insights into AD and clinical comorbidities, demographic, and genetic factors.

Our laboratory modified a ResNet model to develop a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based machine learning (ML) pipeline for end-to-end automated region-level identification, quantification, and localization of different Aβ deposits in gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) areas for entire WSIs.21 This harmonized approach has enhanced efficiency and effectiveness over other computational approaches39,40 for neuropathological evaluations by demonstrating higher Intersection over Union (IoU), dice similarity coefficient (DICE), and F-1 scores. A 2019 study was effective in demonstrating ML/CNN-based approaches can quantify Aβ deposits with an accuracy comparable to a neuropathology expert.23 This was further substantiated by others in at least 1 independent cohort.24 Furthermore, our laboratory expanded upon these reports by leveraging our BrainSec pipeline on the temporal lobe of a cohort from the UC Davis Alzheimer’s Research Center consisting of 131 consecutive autopsies of increased Aβ deposition denoting correlations to Aβ burden in both GM and WM, including but not limited to severity of AD neuropathologic change (per NIA-AA criteria), and ApoE ε4+/− status.29

Despite progress, there are still many variables to consider when validating the robustness, scalability, and generalizability of ML methods. Our Aβ deposits classifier23 was trained and tested on WSIs, of the SVS file type, from the temporal lobe; its performance has been further validated only for the temporal region.29 Other neuroanatomic regions, file types, and including cases from multiple institutes with different demographic backgrounds (such as ethnicities) are important to examine the workflow’s generalizability. To date, research publications reporting on digital pathology have examined select imaging files (such as SVS).18,19,41 Given the range of digital slide scanner models within US brain banks,18 it is essential to determine whether the utilization of alternative imaging file types yields comparable results, especially since there are reports indicating pre-analytic variables can influence ML algorithm performance.42 Furthermore, differences in sampling and staining techniques throughout centers may impact ML outcomes.26,43

We sought to examine additional variables to validate these existing pipelines with the ultimate goal of achieving deeper disease phenotyping, providing valuable insights into the complex mechanisms underlying AD progression. We utilized a previously published pipeline29 on a cohort derived from 3 different institutes (University of California, San Diego [UCSD], University of California, Davis [UCD], and Columbia University),44,45 examined 3 different brain regions (frontal, temporal, and parietal cortices), in addition to evaluating images scanned on a Zeiss Axioscan Z.1 scanner.

METHODS

Data selection/sample selection/cohort selection

Details on the cohort have been previously published.44,45 In brief, 273 participants were chosen based on a pathological diagnosis of intermediate/high AD defined utilizing the NIA Reagan criteria for intermediate or high likelihood of AD, and/or the NIA-AA criteria for intermediate or high AD neuropathologic change.46,47 Both criteria were applied as this retrospective study included historical cases spanning multiple decades. While alive, participants were enrolled in IRB-approved studies at each institution; for further details, see previous papers.44,45 After passing, brain procurements with subsequent neuropathologic evaluations were performed on persons who had legal consent for autopsy provided by the next of kin and/or the decedent during life.

Clinical characteristics data

Information regarding clinical comorbidities was collected by each institution and recorded based on forms provided by the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC) Uniform Data Set (UDS).48 Comorbidities were identified through self-reported or informant-reported prior diagnoses made by a medical professional at the last visit prior to death. Conditions were classified as present if they occurred within the last 12 months or still required treatment at the time of the visit, and if they occurred more than 12 months prior and no longer required active treatment. Pathologic diagnoses (eg, AD only, AD + Lewy body dementia [LBD], AD + cerebrovascular disease [CVD]) were derived from each site’s final neuropathologic diagnostic report, which included up to 4 diagnostic entries per case.

Region sampling and histology

We evaluated the frontal, parietal, and temporal cortices (Figure 1). Preceding processing and assessment, 12 batch numbers were assigned within center, sex, and ethnicity strata utilizing permuted block randomization. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections were cut at 5 µm from the selected anatomic regions available in each institution and stained in the randomized 12 batches at 1 location (UCD) as a measure to minimize potential staining differences across the 3 sites and due to variations in pathological criteria and antibody staining over time. All sections were deparaffinized through a graded series of alcohols; placed into 2 changes of 3 min each into Xylene (HistoPrep-Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA, United States) and placed into 2 changes of 100% alcohol (StatLab Medical Products, McKinney, TX, United States) for 2 min each, followed by 2 changes of 95% alcohol for 2 min each. After completion of deparaffinization, the slides were put into distilled water. For assessment of Aβ deposits, slides were pretreated prior to staining for 10 min in 87% formic acid, endogenous peroxidases were blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide with following applications of primary and secondary antibodies. The 4G8 monoclonal antibody against Aβ (1:1600; Covance Labs, Madison, WI, United States) was utilized to perform immunohistochemical staining.

Slide digitization

4G8 immunostained slides (total of 799) were digitized to acquire WSIs via the Zeiss Axio Scan Z.1 scanner at 40× magnification (0.11 µm/pixel) (Figure 1). Files were saved in the proprietary CZI format at a 60% compression rate. Semi-quantitative histopathological assessments of each area/stain were conducted as stated in previous work by our laboratory.44,45

Data preprocessing

Our BrainSec pipeline was trained on WSIs scanned at a resolution of 0.5 microns per pixel21 (Figure 2). The average resolution of images in the BrainSec training dataset is 60 000 × 50 000 pixels, while the CZI images in our cohort are nearly in the order of 160 000 × 130 000 pixels. The CZI file format presents its own challenges for analysis due to its high scanning resolution of 0.11 microns per pixel.21 More details on the preprocessing and downsampling steps are included in our Supplemental Material and GitHub repository (https://github.com/ucdrubinet/BrainSec).

Schematic depicting limitations of plaque counting. Whole-slide images (WSIs) are tiled by a sliding window into 1536 × 1536-pixel tiles. Within each tile, candidate amyloid deposits are localized and extracted as 256 × 256-pixel patches. Cored plaque (CP), diffuse plaque (DP), or cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) are represented by red, purple, and dark blue colors, respectively. (A) Each patch is input to BrainSec’s convolutional neural network classifier that assigns 1 of 3 labels, ie, CP, DP, or CAA, using an 8-connectivity contour algorithm to delineate plaque boundaries. This 8-connectivity algorithm has many failure cases because adjacent plaques within a single patch or extending across neighboring patches share connected pixels that are often merged into 1 object; this may lead to underestimation of true plaque counts. The Final Heatmap shows localizations of the amyloid deposits in GM (gray matter) and WM (white matter) areas, shown in cyan and yellow colors, respectively. The GM and WM predictors are obtained from BrainSec’s GM/WM segmentation model. (B) Examples of underestimation of true plaque counts due to the blob effect, detection failures, or both. Blob effect is when multiple adjacent plaques are bundled together to be represented by a single continuous plaque instead. Detection failures occur when the machine learning pipeline fails to detect the correct class inside the patch. When both problems occur simultaneously, these failures can be further exacerbated. The original plaque counts can be much higher than the initial raw counts displayed in the bottom-right corners of the blob-effect and detection-failure images. Figure created using BioRender.

Deep learning framework

Our deep learning pipeline, BrainSec, consists of 2 CNNs: (1) Aβ deposit classifier f(⋅), trained on 33 111 independent 256 × 256 pixel tiles to discriminate CPs, DPs, and CAA21; (2) GM/WM segmentation g(.), trained on a total 710 000 tiles of size 256 × 256 pixels belonging to GM, WM, and background classes.21 Additional details are located within the Supplemental Material.

Aβ-density calculation

Algorithm 1 (found in the Supplemental Material) describes the process of density calculation. The WSIs were first converted to physical dimensions using the scanning resolution. Downsampled heatmaps were used to identify the fraction of heatmap pixels classified as GM or WM was calculated and then scaled by the total WSI area to obtain GM and WM areas in mm2 (Algorithm 1). Deposit densities were then defined as the number of detected objects per brain area (#/mm2). These scale factors were used to convert pixel-based GM and WM Aβ object counts (cored, diffuse) into densities per mm2. BrainSec does not identify the leptomeninges region of the brain tissue, where CAA are usually located. Therefore, we compute total (GM + WM) object densities for CAA, denoting the total density of CAA in the entire brain tissue, for a fair comparison. We compare the variation of these densities with semi-quantitative scores of the WSIs (Figure S1).

Statistical analysis

Extracted data were assessed for normality using histograms, Q-Q plots, and the Shapiro-Wilk test.49,50 Demographic, clinical, genetic, and neuropathologic characteristics were summarized separately by center (UC Davis, UC San Diego, and Columbia) (Table 1). Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations, and comparisons across groups were conducted using 1-way ANOVA. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared across groups using the chi-square test. ML-derived densities of Aβ deposits were summarized as means and standard deviations. These values were log-transformed prior to statistical modeling. A mixed-effects model was then used to assess group differences, followed by post hoc analysis with false discovery rate (FDR) correction. To assess unadjusted associations between Aβ deposits and continuous variables (eg, age at death, years of education) were examined using the Spearman rank-order correlation test. For binary (2-group) comparisons (eg, APOE ε4 status, diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, sex, ethnicity), the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used, and linear model was used for adjusted associations. To satisfy the assumptions of linear modeling, density outcomes were log-transformed. For outcomes containing zero values, a small constant (either 0.01, 1, or 10, depending on the scale of the measure) was added prior to log transformation to permit computation of the logarithm. All linear models were adjusted for sex, age of death, ethnicity, and center. For continuous predictors, standardized regression coefficients (β*) were reported from models using standardized predictors; for categorical predictors, regression coefficients were exponentiated to obtain geometric mean ratios (GMR) with 95% confidence intervals.

Chi-square test.

To evaluate the relationship between ML-derived scores and The Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD)-like scores (0 = none, 1 = sparse, 2 = moderate, 3 = frequent), Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated across 3 brain regions (frontal, temporal, and parietal) and 3 amyloid pathologies (CPs, DPs, and CAA). To assess group differences, a 2-sided Student’s t-test, with adjusted P-values (to control the family-wise error rate), was performed to test the null hypothesis of equal means between CERAD categories, using ML-derived scores from WSIs. We then formulated linear models, while controlling for center effect, and verified group differences using Tukey’s multiple comparison.

Results were visualized using box plots with individual data points overlaid. Box plots display the interquartile range (box edges), median (central line), and outliers (points beyond the whiskers). Each WSI was represented as a discrete data point. Analyses were limited to decedents with complete data for each variable; missing data were not imputed. Statistical significance threshold was set at α = .05. All analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc.) and R (version 4.2.3) in RStudio, with the tidyverse package. Figures were generated using Microsoft PowerPoint, ggplot2, and BioRender.

RESULTS

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Our cohort consisted of 273 deceased participants drawn from 3 Alzheimer’s Disease Research Centers (ADRCs): UCD (19.8%), UCSD (32.6%), and Columbia University (47.6%) (Table 1). All participants had a final neuropathologic diagnosis of intermediate/high AD. Table 1 summarizes the demographic, clinical, genetic, and neuropathologic characteristics of the participants across sites; some data, albeit in different formats, have been reported previously.42,43 The overall mean age at death was 82.0 years; the mean years of educational attainment was 13.1 years. Women comprised 59.7% of the total cohort, with Columbia contributing a majority (70.8%) of women, compared to UCD (48.2%) and UCSD (50.6%) (P = .002). The prevalence of apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 carrier status across the cohort was 55.6%, with no significant differences observed among institutions (Table 1).

We observed notable differences in the distribution of contributing pathologies across the 3 ADRCs (Table 1). The proportion of participants with AD as the sole contributing pathology was highest at UCSD (47.2%) and UCD (42.6%), and significantly lower at Columbia (27.7%) (P = .009). In contrast, the proportions of participants with mixed AD and Lewy body disease (LBD) pathology were similar across sites, ie, 25.9% at UCD, 25.8% at UCSD, and 18.5% at Columbia, with no significant difference (P = .34) (Table 1). However, mixed AD and CVD pathology was significantly more common at Columbia (58.5%) vs UCD (27.8%) and UCSD (10.1%) (P < .001). CAA presence was assessed in the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes. Overall, CAA prevalence was 76.8% in the frontal lobe, 69.5% in the parietal lobe, and 73.5% in the temporal lobe (Table 1). Notably, UCSD exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of temporal lobe CAA (86.4%) compared to UCD (69.4%) and Columbia (66.8%) (P = .006) (Table 1).

Regarding clinical comorbidities, the prevalence of comorbid vascular risk factors across the cohort was 13.6% for diabetes, 54.3% for hypertension, and 54.7% for hypercholesterolemia (Table 1). UCD had significantly lower rates of hypertension (35.9%) compared to UCSD (60.8%) and Columbia (63.5%) (P = .006). Similarly, UCD had a lower prevalence of hypercholesterolemia (27.6%) than both UCSD (64.1%) and Columbia (55.8%) (P = .003).

Neuropathology

We observed that our ML-derived density scores correlated significantly with the expert-annotated semi-quantitative scores across all amyloid deposit categories (CP, DP, and CAAs, Figure S1). After log transformation, we applied a mixed-effects model along with FDR correction to examine ML-derived densities of gray matter cored plaques (GM-CPs), gray matter diffuse plaques (GM-DPs), and total CAA deposits across the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes (Figure 3). GM-CP densities were significantly higher in the parietal lobe compared to both the frontal (P < .001) and temporal (P < .001) cortices (Figure 3). For GM-DPs, the temporal lobe exhibited a significantly higher burden than both the frontal (P < .001) and parietal (P < .001) lobes (Figure 3). Additionally, the frontal lobe showed significantly higher GM-DP density than the parietal lobe (P < .001). Regarding CAA, the parietal lobe showed a higher burden relative to the temporal lobe based on the mixed-effects model; however, this difference did not remain statistically significant after FDR correction. No significant differences in CAA burden were observed between the frontal and temporal lobes or between the frontal and parietal lobes (Figure 3).

Aβ deposits count grouped by demographics

We investigated associations between ML-derived Aβ deposit densities (GM-CP, GM-DP, and total CAA) and select demographic factors (age of death, education, sex, ethnicity) (Tables S2-S7).

Age at death

Spearman correlation and linear models adjusted for sex, age of death, ethnicity, and center revealed GM-CP density in the frontal (ρ = −0.22, β* = −0.25, P < .001), as well as across all brain regions combined (ρ = −0.21, β* = −0.22, P = .003), was significantly inversely related to age at death (Table S1). The parietal region was significant in the Spearman correlation (P < .001), but the association was no longer significant in the adjusted model (P = .115). Likewise, GM-DP density in the frontal (ρ = −0.20, β* = −0.13, P = .038), parietal (ρ = −0.18, β* = −0.16, P = .017), and temporal lobes (ρ = −0.31, β* = −0.20, P = .001), and in the aggregate analysis, demonstrated a similar negative association with age at death (Table S1). In contrast, total CAA burden did not show a significant relationship with age at death in the frontal (P = .93), parietal (P = .28), and temporal lobes (P = .88), in addition to all brain regions combined (P = .51).

Education

When examining the role of education, we identified a significant negative correlation between years of education and total CAA density in the frontal lobe (ρ = −0.11, β* = −0.17, P = .033) and parietal lobe (ρ = −0.16, β* = −0.19, P = .022) as well as across all brain regions combined (ρ = −0.14, β* = −0.22, P = .012) (Table S2). However, no significant associations were observed between education and any of the 3 amyloid pathologies, GM-CP, GM-DP, or total CAA, in the temporal region (Table S2).

Sex and ethnicity

Additionally, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests indicated no significant differences in the density of GM-CP, GM-DP, or total CAA across sex or ethnicity, in individual brain regions, or in the combined dataset (Tables S3 and S4).

Aβ deposits count grouped by APOE ε4 carriers

ApoE ε4 genotyping data were available for 75.1% of cases in our cohort (n = 205). Among these, 55.6% (n = 114) were classified as ApoE ε4 allele carriers (ApoE ε4+), while 44.4% (n = 91) were non-carriers (ApoE ε4−) (Table 1). The female-to-male ratio was 1.04 in the ApoE ε4+ group and 1.76 in the ApoE ε4− group. A chi-square test revealed a trend toward a sex-based difference in ApoE ε4 status (χ2 = 3.41, P = .07), with a higher proportion of males being ApoE ε4+ (62.92%) compared to females (50%). However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (data not shown).

Using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and adjusted linear model analyses, we revealed GM-CP density in the temporal lobe was marginally lower with ApoE ε4+ status (GMR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.83-0.996, P = .040) (Figure 4; Tables S5 and S6). No significant associations were observed between GM-CPs and ApoE ε4 status in the frontal or parietal lobes, or when combining all 3 regions. In contrast, GM-DP density in the temporal (GMR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05-1.0, P = .001) was significantly higher with ApoE ε4+ status, and combined regional sum (GMR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.01-1.28, P = .034). The parietal lobe was significant in the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (P = .007), but no longer significant in the adjusted model (P = .061). For total CAA, a significant association with ApoE ε4+ status was observed only in the frontal lobe (GMR = 1.67, 95%CI: 1.18-2.35, P = .004) (Figure 4; Table S6). No significant associations were found in the temporal or parietal lobes, or in the combined analysis across all 3 regions.

Aβ deposits count grouped by cardiovascular comorbidities

Comorbidity data were available for 67.4% of cases in our cohort (n = 184). We used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and linear model analyses to assess associations between the 3 Aβ pathologies and clinical comorbidities, including diabetes, hypertension, and hypercholesterolemia. No significant differences were observed in GM-CPs, GM-DPs, or total CAA across the 3 brain regions when comparing present or absent diabetes status (Figure 5). When evaluating hypertension, we found higher ML-derived densities of total CAA across the combined brain regions with present status in the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (P = .03), but this lost significance after adjusting the model (P = .144) (Figure 6). No significant associations were identified for GM-CPs or GM-DPs across the 3 regions in relation to hypertension. Finally, in comparing hypercholesterolemia with amyloid pathology, we observed that higher densities for total CAA in the temporal lobe were significantly associated with present hypercholesterolemia (P = .007) in the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Figure 7), but this was no longer significant after adjusting for covariates (P = .063). No other significant associations were found between hypercholesterolemia and GM-CPs or GM-DPs in any of the 3 brain regions.

Aβ deposits count grouped by CAA

Using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and adjusted linear models, we compared neuropathology measures between decedents with CAA deposits present or absent, classified with a CERAD-like assessment (0 = absence, 1-3 = presence), and the 3 Aβ pathologies. No significant differences were observed in GM-CP or GM-DP densities across the 3 brain regions or in their combined totals. As expected, and serving as an internal control, ML-derived CAA densities in each individual lobe, as well as in the aggregate across all regions, were significantly associated with the expert denoted presence of CAA (P < .001 in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses) (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

In this validation study, incorporating 273 participants from 3 institutions, we applied an ML algorithm29 to quantify densities of GM-CPs, GM-DPs, and total CAA across frontal, temporal, and parietal regions. We revealed region-specific differences, ie, GM-CP densities were the highest in the parietal cortex, with significant differences across all regions, while GM-DP densities were significantly highest in the temporal lobe, followed by frontal and then parietal cortices, and CAA burden was higher in the parietal cortex compared to the temporal, but this did not remain significant after FDR correction. No other regional differences in CAA were detected. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to apply WSI analysis across the frontal, parietal, and temporal cortices in an autopsy cohort using ML-based quantification of amyloid pathology. While previous work from our laboratory validated the BrainSec pipeline in the temporal lobe,29 this study expands its use to enable region-specific comparisons across multiple neocortical areas in addition to expanding to cases from other institutions, and evaluation of a plethora of WSIs from a Zeiss scanner.

Our analytic framework builds directly upon previously published work related to the ML pipeline. The landmark study by Tang et al in 2019 demonstrated high area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) for plaque detection, explicit threshold sensitivity analyses, and blinded expert correlations.23 In 2020, Vizcarra et al extended this validation across institutions and evaluated different annotation strategies (ie, utilization of region of interest instead of individual image classification).24 BrainSec provided further means for anatomic specificity segmenting GM and WM with Scalco et al, combining segmentation and classification workflows on over 100 WSIs, with amyloid loads correlating with NIA-AA severity and diagnoses.21,29 By integrating these components into a unified workflow and expanding to additional variables, our study provides a benchmark complete with publicly accessible code and images.

To the best of our knowledge, there are no neuropathological studies that have used ML to quantitatively compare amyloid densities across cortical regions providing open source code and accessible WSIs, although there are other quantitative studies, including that of Kapasi et al.20 Existing staging schemes, such as Thal phases, describe the sequential involvement of amyloid (based on presence/absence) in select anatomic regions but do not provide region-specific and plaque-specific quantification.11 Positron Emission Tomography (PET) imaging studies have reported the highest amyloid signal in the frontal cortex, followed by the temporal and parietal regions,51,52 reflecting overall amyloid burden. To the best of our knowledge, there is no current in- vivo imaging means to determine specific amyloid plaque types.

Our study provides the first ML-based region-specific, quantitative assessment of both CPs and DPs across cortical regions. DPs are amorphous Aβ deposits lacking tau-positive neurites, whereas CPs are compact lesions that may contain tau-positive dystrophic neurites.7 Regions with higher amyloid burdens (ie, temporal and frontal cortices) also exhibited proportionally greater DP density, which may relate to differences in plaque morphology and microenvironmental factors such as neuronal connectivity, metabolic activity, or local glial responses, rather than progression of disease.53–56 Conversely, the greater CP density observed in parietal cortex may indicate enhanced plaque compaction or regional susceptibility to neuritic transformation within advanced AD pathology.57–60 Future work evaluating these areas to specifically denote neuritic plaques using more optimal staining (such as tau immunostaining and/or silver techniques) is warranted. Together, these findings highlight the spatial and biological heterogeneity of amyloid deposition in AD and support the value of distinguishing plaque subtypes.

In contrast to earlier reports of regional CAA variability, particularly implicating the temporal lobe,61,62 our study did not find significant regional differences in total CAA. This discrepancy may reflect methodological differences, including the enhanced sensitivity of ML-based quantification, which may capture more subtle and diffuse CAA patterns and/or cohort inclusion/exclusion criteria.

We examined relationships between these continuous pathology measures and select demographic (age at death, educational attainment, ethnicity), genetic (APOE ε4 status), and clinical variables (diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia). Beyond anatomical distinctions, GM-CP and GM-DP densities were inversely correlated with age at death across multiple regions. This finding is consistent with other autopsy studies reporting attenuated or even reversed plaque-age relationships, especially in persons of advanced age, ie, oldest-old with AD.63–65 In the current cohort, there were 62 persons (23%) over the age of 90, with the range for age of death being 51-103, median of 84 years. At the same time, we note automated plaque counts may be underestimated at high burden due to the “blob effect” (Figure 2), wherein the 8-connectivity contour algorithm merges adjacent plaques into single deposits, disproportionately affecting cases with dense amyloid pathology (Figure 2). These 2 mechanisms, ie, biological plateauing of plaque accumulation and algorithmic underestimation, likely may both contribute to the observed relationship between plaque density and age at death. Figure 2 shows cases in which our ML algorithm is effective at merging and counting the blobs, as well as ineffective cases.

APOE ε4 stratification revealed region- and pathology-specific effects. GM-DP densities in the temporal and parietal cortices, as well as frontal CAA, were significantly higher in APOE ε4 carriers, while temporal GM-CP density was greater in non-carriers. These findings align with the study of Scalco et al, who reported elevated GM-CP and GM-DP densities in the temporal lobe of APOE ε4 carriers but minimal differences in CAA burden.29 The established role of the allele in promoting DP accumulation,30,66 alongside a more regionally variable influence on vascular amyloid deposition,67,68 underscores the complex impact of APOE ε4 on AD pathology.

There were no significant differences in the density of GM-CPs, GM-DPs, or total CAA by ethnicity. These findings are partially consistent with prior reports showing no differences in semi-quantitative CAA densities between Hispanic and non-Hispanic White decedents.45 In contrast, a previous study from our laboratory identified higher semi-quantitative CP densities in the temporal cortex of non-Hispanic White decedents,44 diverging from the present analysis, which showed no ethnicity-related differences in plaque or CAA burden. Notably, another investigation using binary data reported greater CVD pathology among persons who identified as Hispanic, with a significant difference compared to persons who identified as non-Hispanic White.69 Taken together, these studies highlight variability in the distribution of amyloid- and vascular-related pathologies, suggesting associations with ethnicity may be dependent on the analytic method.

Regarding cardiovascular risk factors, exploratory analyses revealed nominal associations between total CAA burden and hypertension across combined cortical regions, and between temporal-lobe CAA and hypercholesterolemia, while no significant relationships were observed with diabetes. However, these associations did not remain significant after covariate adjustment and should therefore be interpreted with caution. These findings are consistent with trends reported prior using semi-quantitative approaches, in which temporal CPs were associated with hypertension and parietal CAA with hypercholesterolemia, but no amyloid associations were detected with diabetes status.70 The potential link between vascular risk factors and CAA aligns with prior evidence suggesting hypertension and dyslipidemia may preferentially influence amyloid deposition in cerebral vessels rather than in parenchymal plaques.71,72 These results should be viewed as exploratory, warranting confirmation in larger cohorts. Conversely, the absence of diabetes associations with amyloid pathologies suggests diabetes may drive AD progression through mechanisms such as tau pathology and neuroinflammation rather than amyloid accumulation73,74 or the notion plaques may asymptote off early in the disease process.75 These distinctions emphasize the need to consider pathology subtypes when evaluating cardiovascular influences on AD. Delineation of region-specific associations between AD pathology and cardiovascular risk factors may provide mechanistic insights and facilitate the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Our study has some limitations. The ML model may underestimate amyloid counts due to the “blob effect,” merging closely spaced predicted deposits and undercounting them (Figure 2). Additionally, BrainSec requires significant computational time due to an outdated sliding-window architecture, limiting scalability; ongoing work aims to modernize and optimize this tool. BrainSec only distinguishes between GM and WM regions; future work will incorporate the classification of the leptomeninges region. Anatomically, we focused on 3 cortical regions; future studies will address these gaps by expanding anatomical coverage and improving segmentation capabilities. Furthermore, all cases were at intermediate/high Alzheimer’s Disease Neuropathologic Change (ADNC) levels association should be denoted as exploratory and not generalizable across the ADNC spectrum. Also, there are many guidelines for assessing CVD; hence, diagnostic criteria could have differed across institutions and may have contributed to differences in the frequency of mixed AD + CVD diagnoses across centers. Furthermore, site-specific inclusion criteria, such as UCSD’s exclusion of participants with diabetes and major stroke,44 may have influenced diagnostic distributions. In addition, clinical data limitations inherent to the retrospective design and reliance on self-reported cardiovascular histories (Form A5 in NACC) may limit accurate representation of comorbidity onset and severity; prospective studies with detailed medical records are warranted. In addition, our present study leveraged already validated components,21,23–25 and did not provide any new annotations. This aids in methodological rigor while extending analyses to regional and demographic associations in a large, multi-institutional cohort. Furthermore, as cases were compiled from brain banks that have been in existence for many decades and with available resources, the current study focused on select pathologies; other pathologies, such as TDP-43 deposits and age-related tau astrogliopathy (ARTAG), were not included. We aim to evaluate these pathologies in future studies. Lastly, although we adjusted for site in our linear models, we did not conduct specific analyses based on ADRC. To aid in understanding site-level heterogeneity, we aim to pursue this in future works.

Despite limitations, this study uniquely makes WSIs and algorithm code publicly available, while enhancing the generalizability of ML approaches for quantitatively assessing Aβ pathology in AD on postmortem tissues and validating prior clinical and pathological associations. By analyzing GM-CPs and GM-DPs, as well as total CAA densities across multiple cortical regions, we provide detailed insights into the regional and neuropathological landscape of amyloid deposition in the context of AD. Our results contribute to the use of ML-derived metrics alongside traditional semi-quantitative assessments, offering enhanced sensitivity and spatial resolution to further understand the interplay of demographics (age at death and education), genetics (APOE ε4 status), comorbidities (hypertension and hypercholesterolemia), and neuropathology in AD.

In summary, this study leverages ML to provide high-resolution quantification of Aβ pathologies across key cortical regions in AD. We identify distinct regional distributions of GM-CPs and GM-DPs, differential relationships with demographic factors such as age, education, and APOE ε4 status, and selective associations of vascular amyloid with cardiovascular risk factors. Our findings emphasize the heterogeneous and complex nature of amyloid pathology and the critical role of advanced digital pathology tools to deepen phenotypic understanding, as has been emphasized in a recent review summarizing the history of digital pathology in neurodegenerative diseases.76 Continued methodological refinement and expanded anatomical and clinical data integration will further enhance the utility of ML-based approaches in elucidating AD mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets.
