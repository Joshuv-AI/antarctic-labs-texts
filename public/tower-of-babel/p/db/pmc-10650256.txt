Evaluation of the differences in proteomics of high‐quality bovine colostrum and low‐quality bovine colostrum
Authors: Yiğit Kaçar, Hasan Batmaz
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough there are studies on colostrum and milk proteomics of different species in the literature, there is no published report about different quality bovine colostrums’ proteomics.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to compare the proteome content of high‐ and low‐quality bovine colostrums for the first time.MethodsColostrum samples were collected from 32 Holstein cows from the same farm that had just calved. Brix% levels of colostrums were measured, and then, those with a Brix% value of ≥27% were classified as high‐quality and those with a Brix% value of <22% as low‐quality. Three samples from high‐quality and low‐quality colostrums were selected and proteomic analyses were performed by pooling separately.ResultsTotally 95 proteins were identified in the colostrums, and 19 of them showed significant changes between high‐ and low‐quality colostrums. Expressions in colostrum of glycosylation‐dependent cell adhesion molecule‐1, cofilin‐1, alpha‐S2‐casein, alpha‐lactalbumin, alpha‐1B‐glycoprotein, actin_cytoplasmic‐1, nucleobindin‐1, cathelicidin‐4, inter‐alpha‐trypsin inhibitor heavy chain H4, chitinase‐3‐like protein 1 and monocyte differentiation antigen CD14 were lower, whereas tetranectin, secreted frizzled‐related protein‐1 (SFRP1), perilipin‐2, coatomer subunit epsilon (COPE), butyrophilin subfamily 1 member A1, polyubiquitin‐B, lactadherin and albumin levels were higher in high‐quality colostrum than low‐quality colostrum. Moreover, SFRP1, COPE and cathelicidin‐4 proteins were identified for the first time in bovine colostrum. In high‐quality colostrum, the most prominently down‐regulated proteins were cathelicidin‐4 (26.01‐fold) and cofilin‐1 (17.42‐fold), and the most prominently up‐regulated proteins were COPE (3.37‐fold) and tetranectin (3.07‐fold).ConclusionsIt was detected that the proteome contents of high‐ and low‐quality bovine colostrums were different from each other. As new functions are added to the protein databases regarding these proteins detected in colostrums, the interactions of proteins with each other and with other molecules will be detailed and the effects of high‐quality colostrums on passive transfer immunity and calf health will be understood in full detail.Keywords: bovine, Brix%, colostrum, proteomic analysis

INTRODUCTION

Colostrum is defined as a yellowish, dense lacteal secretion (complex body fluid) containing more than 90 biologically active components, produced in the mammary gland in the first few days after parturition in mammals (Dzik et al., 2017; Godden et al., 2019; Thapa, 2005). Colostrum is the first and basic nutrient for calves with the protein, fat, lactose, vitamins and minerals it contains (Godden et al., 2019; Hernández‐Castellano et al., 2014). Apart from these nutritional properties, colostrum also includes immunoglobulins, maternal leukocytes, growth hormones (insulin, transforming growth factor beta‐2, insulin‐like growth factor 1 and 2 etc.), non‐specific antimicrobial factors, cytokines, enzymes, anti‐inflammatory agents and antioxidant substances. Thanks to these factors, colostrum is essential for calf health and survival by playing a role both in the passive transfer immunity and the maturation and function of the neonatal immune system and the gastrointestinal system (Dzik et al., 2017; Godden et al., 2019; Hernández‐Castellano et al., 2014; Thapa, 2005). In addition, colostrum contains many proteins called high abundant proteins (HAP) and low abundant proteins (LAP), which are involved in passive transfer immunity and many immunological processes (Hernández‐Castellano et al., 2014; Yamada et al., 2002). In this sense, it was stated that, however, the main HAP in the colostrums are mucin‐1, xanthine dehydrogenase, β‐lactoglobulin, α‐lactalbumin, bovine serum albumin, immunoglobulins and lactoferrin; leading LAP in the colostrums are plasmin, serum amyloid A, fibrinogen, α‐1 antitrypsin, lipopolysaccharide‐binding protein, antimicrobial peptides, defensins and cathelicidins, fibrinogen β chain, chitinase 3‐like 1, complement c3 α‐chain, gelsolin, apolipoprotein H (Hernández‐Castellano et al., 2014; Yamada et al., 2002).

The importance of colostrum is not limited to its role in passive transfer immunity in calves. Colostrum is also used in the treatment and protection of many different diseases in human and animal health, thanks to the effects of various bioactive substances it contains on tissue differentiation, growth, regeneration and development (Dzik et al., 2017; Kaçar et al., 2022; Menchetti et al., 2016; Playford et al., 2001; Thapa, 2005). In addition, colostrum with its unique structure and naturality has been the subject of many studies in humans and animals in both in vitro (Chae et al., 2017) and in vivo (Playford et al., 2001) studies, within the scope of antibiotic resistance and alternative medicine searches (Dzik et al., 2017; Menchetti et al., 2016).

It has been reported that proteomic analyses take advantage of the identification of many micro‐molecular cellulars, biological and metabolic processes and the understanding of disease pathophysiology (Addis et al., 2016; Hernández‐Castellano et al., 2014; Maity et al., 2020; Smolenski et al., 2011). In order to understand the structure and properties of colostrum, which has a unique importance in mammalian nutrition, many studies have been conducted on colostrum protein expression differences in humans and different animal species (Akın et al., 2014; Ferreira et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2020; Yamada et al., 2002). In this sense, the proteome expression differences of colostrum and milk have been studied in humans (Bardanzellu et al., 2017; Li et al., 2020), goats (Sun et al., 2020), cattle (Nissen et al., 2017; Yamada et al., 2002) and pigs (Ferreira et al., 2021). In addition to these, although there are studies comparing colostrum or milk proteomes of different species in the literature (Akın et al., 2014; Li et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2017), there is no research on comparing the proteome profiles of high‐quality and low‐quality bovine colostrums. In light of this information, the aim of this study was to compare the proteomics of high‐ and low‐quality bovine colostrums for the first time.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and sample material

This study was carried out with the approval of the Bursa Uludag University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (2019–10/03). The colostrum samples used in the study were obtained from 32 Holstein cows that were kept in Bursa Uludag University Faculty of Veterinary Medicine Research and Application Center and were just calved. All cows were cared for and fed according to the normal procedures of the farm, dried 60–65 days before calving, and the Rota–Corona–Escherichia coli vaccine (Rotavec Corona‐MSD) was given to them a month before their calving. Colostrum samples of cows that had just calved without any complications and did not contain blood, clots and pus were determined, and after the amount (4–5 L) to be consumed by the calves was separated, samples were taken into sterile 50 mL containers and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Brix% values of colostrum samples were measured with a digital Brix refractometer (Milwaukee MA882), and those with Brix% value ≥27% were classified as high‐quality colostrum and Brix% value ˂22% were classified as low‐quality colostrum (Bielmann et al., 2010; Batmaz, 2021). Then, by selecting and pooling three colostrum samples of each group (high‐quality and low‐quality), proteomic analyses were performed on them.

Protein extraction and NanoLC–ESI–MS/MS analysis

Protein expression differences in colostrum samples were analysed by the LC–MS/MS method (Waters M‐Class UPLC and Xevo G2‐XS QTOF MS). Before the analysis, the fat layer was removed from the colostrum samples with centrifuge series (Tacoma et al., 2017). Afterwards, albumin depletion was achieved in these samples (Bilić et al., 2018). After the depletion process, the pellets were mixed in half with a Universal Protein Extraction buffer (Expedeon‐44101) for protein extraction. Samples were sonicated with a 5 × 10 s cycle to completely dissolve the pellets. After sonication, the samples were incubated at 95°C for 10 min after cooling by keeping them on ice. Then, in order to remove large particles, the samples were centrifuged at 18,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was transferred to new tubes. Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford method. Peptide extraction was done using the FASP Protein Digestion Kit (Expedeon‐44250) and trypsin enzyme (Pierce‐90057). The peptide concentration was measured with the Pierce Quantitative Colorimetric Peptide Assay (Thermo‐23275). The samples were diluted with 0.1% formic acid to a final concentration of 200 ng/μL and transferred to appropriate tubes (LC vials) for the device. Next, ACQUITY UPLC M‐Class, SYNAPT XeVo G2‐XS system (Waters‐186008821) was used for chromatographic separation. The MS device was operated in positive ion mode, and the SONAR method was applied for data collection. Before starting the analysis, detector and calibration settings were made using the MassLynx programme (V4.1‐Waters), which is specific to the Xevo G2‐XS Q‐TOF (Waters) device. By putting the method SONAR in positive ion and sensitivity mode, the formed tryptic peptides were fractionated with an acetonitrile gradient in the HSS T3 (Waters‐186008818) column according to their hydrophobicity. Peptides, which were separated from the column by increasing acetonitrile in the range of 5%–35%, were analysed in mass spectrometry as a result of electrospray ionization. In the analyses performed using the Xevo G2‐XS QTOF (Waters) mass spectrometry system, data were collected for peptides that could be identified in the m/z 50–2000 range. Information about the whole and amount of the peptide was collected by the MS analysis performed for 0.5 s. Subsequently, peptide fragmentation and sequence information were obtained by MS/MS analysis performed for 0.5 s. At the end of the analysis, while defining the obtained proteins, the protein sequence information of Bos taurus (UniProt, Bos taurus, Reviewed) in the UniProt protein database was used. Protein identification and statistical analysis were performed using Progenesis QI‐P software (Nonlinear Dynamics). Among the identified proteins, those with a significance of p < 0.05 and fold change of ≥1.2‐fold (power ≥ 0.57) were considered significant proteins.

In the statistical evaluation of the results, the “Mann–Whitney U test” was applied to Sigma's statistical programme to compare the paired groups. In all evaluations of statistical results, p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

RESULTS

Within the scope of the study, colostrums collected from 32 cows were divided into 2 as high‐quality and low‐quality colostrums according to Brix% value (Figure 1). In this sense, it was determined that 18 colostrums with a Brix% value of at least 27% were of high quality, and the average Brix% was 30.08% ± 0.65%. Four colostrums with Brix < 22% were of low quality, and their average Brix% was found to be 17.97% ± 1.47% (p = 0.002).

Interactions of colostral proteomes (n = 95) that were described for Bos taurus according to protein gene bank. Source: Figure created by string‐db (www.string‐db.org).

The mean Brix% values of high‐quality (n = 3) and low‐quality (n = 3) colostrum groups which were selected for proteomic analysis were found to be 32.56% ± 1.53% and 19.16% ± 1.18%, respectively.

Totally 95 different proteins were identified in the colostrum samples by proteomic analysis, and 19 of them showed significant changes between high‐quality and low‐quality colostrums. Expressions in colostrum of glycosylation‐dependent cell adhesion molecule‐1 (GLCM1), cofilin‐1, alpha‐S2‐casein (CASA2), alpha‐lactalbumin (LALBA), alpha‐1B‐glycoprotein (A1BG), actin_cytoplasmic‐1, nucleobindin‐1, cathelicidin‐4, inter‐alpha‐trypsin inhibitor heavy chain H4 (ITIH4), chitinase‐3‐like protein 1 (CH3L1) and monocyte differentiation antigen CD14 were lower, whereas tetranectin, secreted frizzled‐related protein‐1 (SFRP1), perilipin‐2, coatomer subunit epsilon (COPE), butyrophilin subfamily 1 member A1 (BT1A1), polyubiquitin‐B, lactadherin and albumin levels were higher in high‐quality colostrum than low‐quality colostrum (Tables 1 and 2). Moreover, SFRP1, COPE and cathelicidin‐4 proteins were identified for the first time in bovine colostrum. In high‐quality colostrum, the most prominently down‐regulated proteins were cathelicidin‐4 (26.01‐fold) and cofilin‐1 (17.42‐fold), and the most prominently up‐regulated proteins were COPE (3.37‐fold) and tetranectin (3.07‐fold) (Tables S1 and S2).

DISCUSSION

As immunoglobulin G is the most abundant protein in bovine colostrum and is more important in providing passive transfer immunity, measuring IgG level by ELISA or RID is indicated as the gold standard in determining colostrum quality (Godden et al., 2019; Weaver et al., 2000). Thus, colostrums containing at least 50 g of IgG per litre are considered quality, whereas colostrums containing 60 g or more of IgG are considered high quality (Bielman et al., 2010; Elsohaby et al., 2017; Godden et al., 2019; Quigley et al., 2013; Weaver et al., 2000). Again, colostrum quality can also be determined practically and quickly with semiquantitative tests, such as Brix refractometer, total protein, hydrometer and GGT measurements (Bartier et al., 2015; Elsohaby et al., 2017; Godden et al., 2019; Quigley et al., 2013; Zanker et al., 2001). Brix refractometer, which is one of these indirect tests, is widely used in the field with its advantages such as being more durable and less affected by colostrum temperature changes (Godden et al., 2019). Colostrums with a Brix% value of at least 21% (Quigley et al., 2013) and 22% (Bielman et al., 2010) have been reported to contain 50 g/L and more IgG so these colostrums have been classified as quality colostrums (Godden et al., 2019). In addition, recently it has been stated that colostrums with a Brix% value of 27% and above also contain at least 60 g/L immunoglobulins, and these colostrums are classified as high‐quality colostrums (Bartens et al., 2016; Batmaz, 2021). In light of this information, the presented research has been built on colostrums with Brix% values of ˂22 and ≥27. As the Brix% value was below 22% in 4 (12.5%) of the 32 colostrums evaluated in the present study, the rate of low‐quality colostrum was found to be similar to the previous literatures (Kara & Ceylan, 2021: 12.12% and Levieux & Ollier, 1999: 18.3%).

Tacoma et al. (2017) detected 162 proteins in bovine colostrum, whereas Le et al. (2011) detected 253 proteins in bovine colostrum and found that 217 of them were common in milk and colostrum. In the present study, 95 proteins were detected in colostrum samples, and 19 of them showed significant changes. This variety of detected protein counts has been associated with the differences in the samples and analysis methods used in the analysis. In previous many studies, proteomic differences in bovine colostrum and milk were researched and detailed (Le et al., 2011; Yamada et al., 2002); here, the proteome expression differences of high‐ and low‐quality colostrums were examined, and the discussion was detailed on significantly changing proteomes.

Cathelicidin‐4 (CTHL4) was found to be down‐regulated in high‐quality colostrum with a change rate of more than 26‐fold, and it has the highest rate of change among all proteins (19 proteins) that showed significant changes in colostrum. Although different types of cathelicidin have been detected in bovine colostrum in previous studies, the presence of CTHL4 in colostrum samples has been demonstrated for the first time in this research (Hernández‐Castellano et al., 2014; Le et al., 2011; Nissen et al., 2017). It has been stated that cathelicidins play a role in the defence response against fungi, gram‐negative and gram‐positive bacteria with their antibiotic, antimicrobial and fungicidal properties (Hernández‐Castellano et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2015a). In this sense, CTHL4 has been reported to be up‐regulated in milk with mastitis by being secreted by milk neutrophils and epithelial cells following intramammary infection (Boehmer, 2011; Ibeagha‐Awemu et al., 2010; Puggioni et al., 2020; Smolenski et al., 2011). Similarly, the relationship between cathelicidin and the somatic cell count in cattle (Addis et al., 2016; Ibeagha‐Awemu et al., 2010; Smolenski et al., 2011), it has been suggested that CTHL4 can be used as a biomarker in the diagnosis of preclinical mastitis and be used in determining the severity of infection. Zhang et al. (2015b) stated that milk with high somatic cell count had a 10‐fold higher level of CTH4 than those with low somatic cell count. Related to the higher somatic cell counts in low‐quality colostrums (Puppel et al., 2020), it can be suggested that this proteome is up‐regulated on them.

Cofilin‐1 (COF1), which was not detected before in bovine colostrum (Le et al., 2011; Nissen et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2015a), was one of the most prominent down‐regulated proteins in high‐quality colostrums. Previous studies have shown the presence of cofilin‐1 in bovine milk (Maity et al., 2020) and dry period udder secretion (Reinhardt & Lippolis, 2020). Overexpression of COF1 was shown especially in milk with mastitis (Boehmer, 2011; Ibeagha‐Awemu et al., 2010; Maity et al., 2020), and under stress and stimulation conditions (Maity et al., 2020) associated with oxidative damage. It has been stated that the down‐regulation of COF1 level is effective in protecting mitochondrial integrity and functions against oxidative damage, and it has been suggested that with these effects, it can offer an effective therapeutic strategy in neurodegenerative diseases (Hoffmann et al., 2021). With these properties, the down‐regulation of this protein in high‐quality colostrums may provide an advantage in preventing cellular damage.

Tetranectin (TETN) is a protein produced by many different cell types, such as neutrophils, monocytes and fibroblasts (Borregaard et al., 1990; Nielsen et al., 1993), and it plays a role in mainly bone mineralization, osteogenesis, myogenesis, regeneration of tissue, activation of plasminogen, regulation of proteolytic processes and binding of calcium, carbohydrate and heparin (Park et al., 2015; Wewer et al., 1998; Zhang et al., 2015c). In previous studies, the presence of TETN was reported in bovine milk and colostrum (Le et al., 2011; Nissen et al., 2017). In this study, the TETN levels of high‐quality and low‐quality colostrums were compared for the first time, and the up‐regulation of TETN (3.07 max fold) was determined in high‐quality colostrum. In line with this information, considering that TETN levels are high in colostrum in order to meet this need due to rapid tissue regeneration and development in newborns, the importance of high‐quality colostrums in calf nutrition has been demonstrated once again.

The COPE is involved in the cytosolic protein complex which plays role in cellular protein transport (St‐Louis et al., 2017). As COPE has not been detected in cow colostrum and milk before (Le et al., 2011; Mather, 2000; Nissen et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2015a, 2015c), it was revealed for the first time in the presented study. The high protein content and the up‐regulation of COPE in high‐quality colostrums suggest that this protein may play a role in passive transfer immunity.

The presence of SFRP1 in human milk was previously reported (Molinari et al., 2012), but as SFRP1 was not detected in previous research about bovine milk and colostrum (Le et al., 2011; Nissen et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2015a, 2015b), it was detected in bovine colostrum (up‐regulation on high‐quality colostrums) for the first time in this study. As SFPR‐1 is known to regulate the growth and differentiation of certain cell types and to have antiproliferative effects on vascular cells (Bodine et al., 2009; Molinari et al., 2012, Ipposhi et al., 2021) it is thought that it may be similar to TETN, which plays a role in supporting high mitotic activity in newborns.

Albumin (ALBU) is one of the four main milk serum proteins together with IgG, β‐lactoglobulin and α‐lactalbumin (Levieux & Ollier, 1999). The main source of ALBU is maternal blood, and it is known to infiltrate the mammary gland from the blood like immunoglobulins during colostrogenesis (Godden et al., 2019). It has been reported that ALBU plays a role in the transport of many molecules, such as small molecule proteins, free fatty acids, minerals and hormones. It is also stated that it can limit the growth of enteric bacteria such as E. coli by binding to the bacterial siderophore enterobactin (McGrath et al., 2016). It was stated in the literature that the ALBU level was highest in the first milking colostrum in cows followed in the early postpartum period and tended to decrease in continued milking (Levieux & Ollier, 1999; Godden et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2015a). Parallelly, the up‐regulation of ALBU with a change rate of 1.69 in high‐quality colostrums in our study may be an indicator of high‐quality colostrum, just like immunoglobulins.

Perilipin‐2 (PLIN2) and BT1A1 proteins are the members of the two main proteins among the milk fat globule membrane proteomes and are known to play a role in the synthesis and secretion of milk fat droplets (Ibeagha‐Awemu et al., 2010; Mather, 2000; Reinhardt et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2015c). Therefore, the up‐regulation of these proteins in high‐quality colostrums has once again demonstrated its importance in providing energy and such vitamins to calves, with higher levels of fat and fat‐soluble vitamins (Godden et al., 2019). In addition, it has been reported that apart from milk fat secretion, BT1A1 has been effective in various immunologic processes such as the regulation of cytokine production and regulation of immune response (Ibeagha‐Awemu et al., 2010; Mather, 2000; Reinhardt et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2015b). Similarly, polyubiquitin‐B (UBB) has been reported to have many roles, including signalling, DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, kinase modification, protein binding, transcription and cellular regulation (Le et al., 2011). It has been reported in previous studies that although BT1A1 was down‐regulated in milk containing high somatic cell counts (Zhang et al., 2015b) and bovine milk with mastitis (Ibeagha‐Awemu et al., 2010), similarly UBB levels were down‐regulated in the transition from subclinical mastitis to clinical mastitis (Maity et al., 2020). Therefore, the up‐regulation of these proteins (BT1A1 and UBB) in high‐quality colostrums may be associated with high immunological activities and low inflammatory indexes of these colostrums.

Lactadherin (MFGM), one of the major milk fat globule membrane proteins (Mather, 2000; Reinhardt et al., 2012), is known to have a role in removing apoptotic cells, maintaining intestinal epithelial homeostasis, promoting mucosal healing and protecting against viral infections (especially rotavirus) (Le et al., 2011; Mather, 2000). It has been demonstrated that MFGM is 2.7 times up‐regulated in bovine colostrum than in milk (Le et al., 2011). Considering the content and similarity of low‐quality colostrums to normal milk, the up‐regulation of this protein in high‐quality colostrums is consistent. It can be argued that feeding high‐quality colostrum, which is rich in this protein, to newborns will provide advantages in ensuring intestinal maturation, maintaining intestinal mucosal integrity and regeneration and also preventing infections that have a significant share in neonatal calf diseases (Blum, 2006).

It has been observed in this research that many of the up‐regulated proteins in low‐quality colostrums are mostly proteins that play a role in the inflammatory process or are proteins that result from the similarity of low‐quality colostrums to normal milk in content. In this sense, it has been stated in the previous research that proteomes, including CASA2 (Boehmer, 2011; Dallas et al., 2014; Miotto et al., 2016), A1BG (Boehmer, 2011; Ibeagha‐Awemu et al., 2010), CH3L1 (Breyne et al., 2018; Ibeagha‐Awemu et al., 2010) and ITIH4 (Ibeagha‐Awemu et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2015b), were up‐regulated in milk with mastitis, and they played a role in the inflammatory response process. Again, it has been reported that Monocyte differentiation antigen CD14 (CD14) is among the acute phase proteins by playing a role in cytokine secretion and inflammatory process (Boehmer, 2011; Filipp et al., 2001; Nissen et al., 2017).

When comparing bovine milk and colostrum, it has been reported that nucleobindin‐1 (NUC‐1) and GLCM1 levels are higher in milk (Le et al., 2011). Considering the similarity of low‐quality colostrums to milk in terms of content, the higher abundance of these proteins in low‐quality colostrums seems compatible with the literature (Godden et al., 2019).

In the presented research, actin cytoplasmic 1 (ACTB) and LALBA levels were found to be down‐regulated in high‐quality colostrum. In previous studies comparing proteomes of colostrum and milk, ACTB (Le et al., 2011) and LALBA (Hernández‐Castellano et al., 2014) levels were reported to be found in higher concentrations in colostrum than in milk. Similarly, studies in cattle (Levieux & Ollier, 1999) and goats (Levieux et al., 2002) reported that LALBA concentration was higher in first milking colostrum samples and gradually decreased in subsequent milkings. On the other hand, as it has been reported that milk contains higher concentrations of lactose than colostrum (Godden et al., 2019), the up‐regulation of LALBA concentration in low‐quality colostrum can also be explained by the similarity of low‐quality colostrum to normal milk.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, it was revealed by this research that the proteomics of high‐ and low‐quality bovine colostrums also differed. Significantly changing proteins have been found to be involved mainly in biological regulation, organization of cellular components, biogenesis, cellular, developmental, immune system, metabolic, multi‐organism, multi‐cellular processes, localization, locomotion, stimulus‐response and signalling pathways. As new tasks are added to the protein databases regarding these detected proteins in colostrums, the interactions of proteins with each other and with other molecules will be detailed. And so, the effects of high‐quality colostrums on the treatment and protection of various diseases in humans and different animal species, especially their role in passive transfer immunity and calf health, will be explained molecularly.
