Building heat resilience through district-wide training of frontline health workers in India: a pre-post evaluation of the climate care champions programme in Ananthapuramu district
Authors: Cecilia Sorensen, Danielly de P Magalhães, Michelle DePhillips, Nymisha Herrera Nimmagadda, James Sullivan, Vedika Tikmani, Kristie Hadley, Hannah N W Weinstein, Nithya Shree, Umashankar K, Syama B Syam, Margaret O’Donnell, Angela Chaudhuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionExtreme heat is a growing public health emergency in India, with rural and low-income communities most at risk. Early symptoms are often overlooked, delaying care and increasing mortality risk. Frontline health workers (FHWs) are critical for prevention and early intervention yet major knowledge and preparedness gaps persist. This study aimed to evaluate a district-wide training programme designed to improve FHWs’ knowledge and confidence in identifying, managing and preventing heat-related illnesses.MethodsThe Climate Care Champions Programme (CCCP) was designed to strengthen the capacity of FHWs to detect, manage and prevent heat-related illnesses. In February 2025, a 2-day Training of Trainers workshop prepared 90 trainers from Ananthapuramu District, Andhra Pradesh, India, who went on to train 2266 FHWs across 76 primary health centres, reaching an estimated 4.5 million residents. A baseline household survey (n=1007, including 241 FHWs) explored knowledge and perception before the training. Longitudinal surveys evaluated confidence and knowledge among trainers and trainees in four heat-vulnerable blocks.ResultsAlthough 70% of FHWs reported prior climate-health training and most expressed confidence in providing preventive advice, knowledge deficits were identified. During CCCP, a longitudinal evaluation revealed improvements in knowledge: recognition of early symptoms increased from 19% to 70%, severe symptoms from 42% to 95% and infant-specific signs from 32% to 89%. Misconceptions around first-aid priorities were corrected, and confidence rose across all domains. Data from 1453 FHWs confirmed effective knowledge transfer across the cascade training model, particularly in protective actions (+29.5%) and understanding heat-illness aetiologies (+18.6%).ConclusionThese results demonstrate that localised, evidence-based training can strengthen frontline health worker capacity and close critical knowledge gaps. Scaling such programmes—supported by policy, infrastructure and sustained government commitment—is essential to protect health, reduce inequalities and strengthen community resilience in the face of escalating extreme heat.

Introduction

Climate change is intensifying health impacts, with South Asia among the hardest-hit; by 2030, over 500 million people in the region may face extreme heat for more than a month each year.1 India is highly vulnerable to extreme heat, with its 1.4 billion people increasingly exposed to dangerous events and climate-sensitive diseases. Heatwaves, once limited to May and June, are now starting earlier and lasting longer. In April 2023, New Delhi experienced seven consecutive days above 40°C, leading to school closures, crop losses and strain on the energy grid, while water and electricity shortages limited access to cooling.23 By 2050, heatwaves may surpass survivability thresholds even for healthy individuals, potentially affecting 310–480 million people and reducing labour productivity by up to 15%.4 Rising temperatures increase illness and death from heat-related conditions, exacerbate chronic diseases and heighten pregnancy risks.5–7 Heat stroke, the most severe form, can be fatal without prompt care.89 Older adults, children, pregnant women, outdoor workers and low-income groups are most vulnerable, with climate-driven heat deepening health inequities and straining public health systems.

India has launched national policies to address heat-related risks, including the National Action Plan on Heat-related Illnesses,10 the Emergency Cooling Guidelines11 and the Autopsy Guidelines.12 However, implementation remains fragmented, with limited awareness among health workers and weak integration into routine preparedness.1314 Although heat-related illness (HRI) content is included in annual training for community health workers, a 2020 knowledge, attitudes and practices survey found only moderate awareness—77.7% of public health professionals knew of the national climate plan, 66.1% of doctors were aware of local heat action plans and 80.9% linked heat illness to climate change.15

Still, early-stage heat illness is difficult to identify due to vague symptoms like fatigue or thirst, which can rapidly worsen.16 Individual vulnerability further complicates diagnosis. Advances in research underscore the need for continuous training to keep health workers updated on detection, management and prevention.17

Frontline health workers (FHWs) are crucial links between the health system and communities and are well-positioned to promote awareness and implement heat-related preventive actions.18 Strengthening their role through education and practice is key to building community resilience.19

To address these gaps, the Global Consortium on Climate and Health Education (GCCHE) at Columbia University, in partnership with Swasti—a public health non-governmental organisation in India—developed the Climate Care Champions Programme (CCCP). Leveraging established community health networks, the CCCP aims to build FHWs’ capacity to recognise and manage the spectrum of HRIs. This manuscript describes the curriculum development, course methodology, pre-training and post-training knowledge assessment and current heat exposure among communities to inform heat adaptation recommendations.

Methodology

Design and setting

The CCCP is a train-the-trainer (ToT) programme designed to build critical knowledge, self-efficacy and communication skills related to heat preparedness and action among FHWs. The ToT approach was selected to enable scalable and effective knowledge transfer across the cascade capacity building model, leveraging existing health system hierarchies to achieve rapid district-wide dissemination of knowledge. An initial pilot of the training was held in Bengaluru in 2024 with a subsequent district-wide ToT in Ananthapuramu, Andhra Pradesh, in 2025.

Andhra Pradesh is India’s second most climate-vulnerable state, ranking highest in aggregated heat risk, with all districts classified as ‘high’ (62%) or ‘very high’ (38%) risk.2021 Within the state, Ananthapuramu district faces intense heat exposure due to its semiarid climate, with summer temperatures often exceeding 40°C. It was selected as a CCCP priority site for its high heat sensitivity and low adaptive capacity.

The district’s large population of outdoor workers, mainly in agriculture and manual labour, faces severe occupational heat stress. Low rural literacy (59.2%) limits the population’s ability to act on health advisories, further increasing vulnerability. Ananthapuramu also has among the lowest service usage rates at primary health centres (PHCs), indicating a weak health system response.22 These overlapping vulnerabilities made it a strategic location for piloting a scalable heat preparedness model for other high-risk districts in the state.

The CCCP was implemented in sequential phases. Curriculum development and pilot testing were conducted in 2024, followed by contextual adaptation for Ananthapuramu in early 2025. The study was conducted in February 2025. Baseline assessments—including a community-level household survey and an FHW knowledge and confidence survey—were carried out in parallel with the training to identify knowledge and behavioural gaps. The ToT workshop was conducted over 5 days. District-wide training of FHWs was delivered over a 10-day period, approximately 1 week after completion of the ToT workshop. Following the training, monthly virtual meetings were held throughout the heat season to reinforce learning, discuss field cases and address emerging challenges. Participation was made mandatory for the FHWs by the state and district government authorities, as the programme was a capacity-building initiative to strengthen their skills and competencies, integrated into their routine workflows.

Ananthapuramu community-level and FHWs baseline survey

Digital surveys were conducted across four heat-vulnerable blocks in Ananthapuramu District, Andhra Pradesh (Pamidi, Tadipatri, Peddapappur and Singanamala) in February 2025, in parallel to the CCCP training. The survey instruments and data were reviewed and approved by the Catalyst Foundation Ethics Committee (Application No. 45) (online supplemental appendix A). The sample size was calculated using a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error and a population of 4.1 million, yielding 384 participants (infinite sampling, SD=0.5). Adjustments for a design effect of 2 and 20% attrition increased the sample to 922 individuals. A stratified random sample of 1007 households ensured representation of rural and heat-exposed populations (only one response per house). The survey was administered after obtaining informed consent from the participants following the protocol submitted and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (online supplemental appendix A). The survey captured knowledge of heat effects and exposure, their attitudes towards heat-related risks and their economic and adaptive responses to these challenges (online supplemental appendix B). In addition, 241 FHWs were surveyed to verify their knowledge and confidence on heat effects on health (online supplemental appendix C).

Curriculum development

The CCCP curriculum was developed by GCCHE at Columbia University, in collaboration with Swasti, to strengthen FHWs’ capacity to identify, manage and prevent HRIs and was implemented within the public health system in Ananthapuramu District, India, targeting community-based health workers, including accredited social health activists (ASHAs), auxiliary nurse midwives (ANMs) and community health workers (CHOs).

The initial curriculum, grounded in adult learning principles, included 17 learning objectives across five modules on heat and health, covering topics from basic concepts to the role of health professionals.23 A virtual meeting was held to contextualise content prior to the pilot training, conducted in February 2024 in Bengaluru. Pilot postsurvey feedback informed curriculum revisions, expanding to 20 learning objectives and adding two new modules: visual mapping HRI and self-care during heat (table 1).

Ahead of the 2025 workshop, the curriculum was further tailored to address vulnerable groups—pregnant women, children, older adults and farmers. The training content remained consistent across all cadres; however, trainers comprising medical officers (MOs), ANMs and CHOs had additional sessions on facilitation and adult learning principles. A facilitator guide was also developed in English and translated into Telugu for the trainers, featuring a 1-day training agenda, facilitation tips and detailed overview modules with learning objectives and activities. After the training, FHWs received a practical advisory note as a job aid to help identify and respond to HRIs during fieldwork. The technical concepts were simplified to enhance FHWs’ understanding while maintaining content fidelity by limiting simplification to the use of simple language, visual materials and context-specific examples.

ToT workshop

A ToT workshop was held on 3–7 February 2025, with support from the Ananthapuramu district administration, to prepare for the upcoming summer extreme heat. Facilitated in person by GCCHE and Swasti, the workshop trained 90 participants, including ANMs, CHOs and MOs. Trainers were nominated by the district’s health department authorities based on their proficiency in both English and Telugu demonstrated leadership and communication skills and supervisory roles within the health system. To ensure reproducibility and consistent knowledge levels, MOs provide mentorship to ANMs and CHOs through regular supervision, thereby ensuring accountability and maintaining quality during the subsequent training of FHWs.

The 2-day ToTs were delivered in two sequential groups of participants to accommodate the large number of trainees and logistical constraints over 5 days, in English with subsequent Telugu translation (online supplemental appendix D). The sessions were led by a team of five—two GCCHE staff (a nurse and a doctor) and three Swasti representatives. Participants received certificates of completion. Monthly virtual meetings were held throughout the heat season with the objective of reinforcing content based on the post-training survey results, reviewing field cases and addressing questions. Sessions included refresher content and case discussion with topics including the B.E.A.T. the Heat Framework, symptom severity of HRIs and identifying vulnerable populations.24

Training frontline workers

Between 17 and 28 February 2025, 90 trained trainers conducted sessions for over 2266 FHWs across 51 PHCs and 25 urban PHCs (UPHCs), achieving full district-wide coverage in Ananthapuramu. The trained workforce included ASHAs, ANMs and CHOs that serve all 76 PHCs/UPHCs, each covering 1000–2000 people, collectively reaching an estimated 4.5 million residents.

The B.E.A.T. the heat framework guided training, but local translation challenges and literacy barriers required simplified, action-focused messaging, extended delivery time and regular refresher sessions to reinforce key concepts.

Training assessment and provider surveys

A presurvey and postsurvey with curriculum-aligned questions were conducted for the trainers. The survey included nine multiple-choice questions aligned with the curriculum’s learning objectives and five questions assessing communication skills and confidence (online supplemental appendix E). The trainer assessment tool was informed by prior pilot testing, with minor contextual adaptations.

A subsequent presurvey and postsurvey were developed for frontline workers to assess their knowledge and confidence in applying heat-related practices. This survey was modified and shortened to five knowledge-based questions and four confidence questions, both aligned with the curriculum’s learning objectives, to reduce survey fatigue and accommodate their limited time and demanding workload (online supplemental appendix F).

Community of practice

A community of practice—defined as a group of individuals who share a common concern and learn how to improve their practice through regular interaction—was established in Ananthapuramu to facilitate case-based learning among local trainers, GCCHE physicians and the Swasti team. The community of practice was implemented as part of the routine programme activities, with participation mandated for all the CCCP-trained trainers. Three 90-minute sessions were conducted over a period of 5 months, in both English and Telugu. During these sessions, trainers presented challenging clinical cases and discussed their management approaches, while GCCHE physicians reinforced key concepts and addressed knowledge gaps. The topics of discussion included how to protect against HRIs, their risk factors and how to recognise their symptoms. These topics were selected based on the results of pre-training and post-training assessments in Ananthapuramu to address the gaps identified during the training evaluation. Following each session, trainers disseminated the discussed topics to all FHWs during the monthly facility day. However, dissemination activities were not formally monitored or documented. The district programme officer also participated to support continued team engagement and programme sustainability. Attendance varied across sessions—62 (68.9%) in the first, 10 (11.1%) in the second and 34 (37.8%) in the final session. Participation can be underestimated due to shared device use, and lower attendance in later sessions coincided with FHW strikes and service disruptions.

Data analysis

Community-level surveys and provider assessments were analysed using SPSS V.27.1. Descriptive statistics summarised categorical variables as percentages and frequencies, and continuous variables as means and SD.

Knowledge and confidence of trainers and FHWs were assessed using descriptive statistics to calculate the percentage of correct responses (treated as binary) and the distribution of confidence levels, respectively. Knowledge improvement was measured by comparing pre-training and post-training percentages and the absolute change (∆%). Significant changes (p<0.05) were tested using McNemar’s test with Bonferroni correction. All analyses were conducted in RStudio (V.2024.12.0+467). McNemar’s test was used as it is appropriate for assessing changes in paired categorical (binary) responses between pre-training and post-training measurements. To control for multiple comparisons across item-level analyses, Bonferroni-adjusted p-values were applied to all McNemar tests.

Confidence responses were categorised as ‘very confident’, ‘somewhat confident’ and ‘not confident’ and analysed descriptively to calculate pre-training and post-training percentages. Changes in the proportions reporting ‘very confident’ and ‘somewhat confident’ served as key indicators of the training’s impact.

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Community-level survey

Community surveys (1007 households) revealed high vulnerability to extreme heat and an urgent need for affordable local adaptation measures. The area is predominantly rural (95%, n=957) and low-income. Most people rely on fans (91.4%, n=920, figure 1a) as cooling systems, facing some electricity (45.9%, n=583, figure 1b) and water (75.2%, n=757, figure 1c) disruptions. When asked how they cope with the health impacts of heat (online supplemental question C5, appendix B), most respondents reported drinking more water or other fluids (95.3%, n=960), followed by taking breaks in shaded or cooler areas (69.8%, n=703) and using cooling devices such as fans or air conditioners (53.3%, n=537)—a challenge in the context of unreliable water and energy access. When asked how their livelihoods were affected (online supplemental question C6, appendix B), 77.3% (n=778) reported experiencing physical exhaustion. About half indicated losses to crops and livestock (53%, n=537) and decreased income (53.7%, n=541). Nearly half (49.9%, n=502) also reported reduced productivity. Taken together, these findings suggest that climate-related stressors are not only straining individuals physically but are also directly undermining household income and overall economic stability.

When asked directly about specific impacts on daily life, 74.3% (n=748) answered ‘yes’ to experiencing difficulty sleeping, 49% (n=493) to reduced ability to concentrate and 54% (n=542) to strain on household resources (eg, energy bills and water usage). Only ~40% (n=398) had ever received information on coping with heat (figure 2). Among those who received any information, respondents often reported more than one source. The most reported sources were FHWs (81.7%, n=325), followed by government campaigns (43.7%, n=174), PHCs (32.2%, n=128), among other sources (figure 2). Knowledge of symptoms was low, and just 2% of community members reported feeling fully prepared.

Among 241 FHWs surveyed, most were female (95%, n=229) and had over 10 years of experience (66%, n=159). More than half (55%, n=132) reported encountering climate-linked health issues. About 70% (n=169) had received formal training on HRI and 90% (n=216) integrated climate-sensitive counselling into community interactions. Common practices included group awareness sessions (54.3%, n=131) and the distribution of information, education, and communication materials (36%, n=86). While 90% (n=216) reported collecting HRI data, effective reporting and response remained constrained by challenges such as limited awareness or insufficient staff training (31.5%, n=76), as well as the absence of standardised reporting systems (27.4%, n=66). When asked about their confidence in identifying populations at risk of heat-related health effects, 99.6% of respondents reported feeling either very confident (67.2%, n=162) or somewhat confident (32.4%, n=78). Similarly, 99.6% (n=240) reported feeling very confident (69.7%, n=168) or somewhat confident (29.9%, n=72) in providing appropriate preventive or protective recommendations to patients vulnerable to HRI.

ToT workshop assessment

The 90 trainers, including 51 CHOs, 13 MOs and 26 ANMs, undertook a pre-training and post-training survey to assess knowledge and confidence related to HRIs. The assessment revealed that participants entered the training with a solid baseline understanding of general heat exposure and basic prevention strategies (table 2). However, their knowledge was more limited in key areas. Notable gaps included identifying symptoms of HRIs, knowing the appropriate actions to take when someone is affected, and understanding specific risk factors and protective measures.

Post-training, knowledge significantly improved across most areas, especially in identifying signs of HRIs (Q4, 19%–70%), severe signs (Q5, 42%–95%) and infant-specific symptoms (32%–89%). There were also improvements in understanding risk factors (Q8), which rose from 16% to 59%, and in grasping the basic causes of heat illness (Q3), which improved from 56% to 90%.

Smaller gains were seen where baseline knowledge was already high (Q1 and Q9—table 2). One area needing further emphasis was immediate action without first aid—only a modest improvement, but not statistically significant, from 67% to 73% (Q6, table 2). Misconceptions about prioritising rehydration were corrected during training, emphasising the importance of lowering body temperature first. Confidence was high pre-training (90%+) and post-training, all participants reported being ‘very’ or ‘somewhat’ confident across all domains (Q10–Q13; table 2).

Frontline worker training assessment

Of the 2266 FHWs trained, 1453 completed both pre-training and post-training surveys, including 722 ASHAs, 413 ANMs, 25 multipurpose health workers and 243 CHOs. Their responses were used to evaluate knowledge gained from the training, which showed meaningful improvements in knowledge and confidence (table 3).

The largest knowledge gains were in protective actions (Q5), with correct responses rising from 19.9% to 49.4% (+29.5%), causes of HRIs (Q3) improved from 56.4% to 74.9% (+18.6%) and humidity’s effects on HRIs (Q1) increased from 54.4% to 71.0% (+16.6%). The recognition of severe symptoms (Q4)—where two answers were accepted—rose from 78.1% to 85.3% (+7.2%). Areas with high baseline knowledge, such as geographic heat risk (Q2), showed smaller gains, increasing from 87.4% to 90.4% (+3.0%) postcourse (table 3).

Confidence improved across all domains. ‘Very confident’ responses rose in recognising severity (Q6) from 74.7% to 92.2% (+17.5%), identifying at-risk populations (Q7) rose from 74.4% to 91.9% (+17.5%), taking action (Q8) rose from 73.4% to 93.1% (+19.8%) and advising communities (Q9) increased from 75.3% to 92.7% (+17.4%), with corresponding declines in lower confidence categories (table 3).

Discussion

This study reveals that the communities served by trained health professionals face severe and compounding vulnerabilities to extreme heat, driven by high exposure, poverty and limited access to reliable cooling, water and electricity that constrains the effectiveness of common coping strategies. Despite these risks, only about 40% of households had ever received information on coping with heat—most often from FHWs (82%). Despite reporting prior training and high confidence pre-dated the CCCP, FHWs demonstrated notable knowledge gaps in recognising and responding to HRIs, highlighting a clear mismatch between perceived and actual preparedness. These knowledge gaps are clinically significant. Inability to recognise early or atypical symptoms of HRI or to correctly assess severity can delay timely intervention, allowing progression to severe conditions such as heat stroke, which is associated with high mortality if not promptly treated.25 In resource-limited settings, where FHWs are often the first and sometimes only point of care, these gaps can result in missed opportunities for early management, increasing the risk of complications, avoidable hospitalisations and death. This gap in health worker knowledge, combined with weak communication channels to communities, inadequate infrastructure and limited public policy focus, underscores the essential role of this programme in preparing the health workforce as first responders for heat prevention and response—particularly in rural and other high-risk communities. The CCCP addressed these gaps through a cascading ToT model and improved their communication skills.

FHWs (ASHA, ANM and Anganwadi workers) are the most trusted and accessed sources of heat-related information, yet they were underutilised as agents of community resilience due to a lack of structured support and integration into adaptation strategies.14 The evaluation of the ToT and subsequent FHW sessions demonstrated clear improvements in knowledge and confidence, particularly in recognising vulnerable populations, symptom severity and emergency responses. Although confidence outcomes were self-reported and may not directly reflect actual competency or clinical performance, the observed increases—together with improvements in knowledge—support the effectiveness of the curriculum and training approach. These findings underscore the importance of continued investment in strengthening health workforce capacity as a frontline defence against rising climate-related health threats. Although no group achieved full mastery and some misconceptions persisted post-training, all trainers engaged in three follow-up online sessions focused on case-based learning and peer discussion. These sessions were instrumental in clarifying doubts, adapting knowledge to real-world situations and reinforcing key concepts, with emphasis on practical, scenario-based learning focused on rapid decision-making and prioritisation of life-saving actions in low-resource environments.

Training programmes like the CCCP demonstrate that locally tailored, well-evaluated capacity building can effectively strengthen climate-resilient health systems. 26–28Razzak et al29 demonstrated that trained community health workers exhibit improved heat-health knowledge and practices, resulting in reduced hospital visits and improved skills to assess risk and take preventive actions, which is crucial since many HRIs, including heat stroke, are largely preventable when recognised and managed promptly.30 Early identification and management at the community level can prevent unnecessary hospitalisations and enhance public health resilience.29 A postseason follow-up will evaluate the community-level impact of the CCCP, reinforcing evidence that community-based communication effectively reduces heat-related health risks.283132 In addition, new information can be forgotten over time. Continued reinforcement of heat-related health knowledge—particularly ahead of the heat season—can be critical to ensure consistent, accurate messaging and strengthen community preparedness and therefore reduce preventable HRIs and deaths, as well as pressure on India’s already overburdened health system.33

The data paints a clear picture of communities striving to cope with escalating heat stress through individual action while facing profound structural and systemic barriers. Without targeted, comprehensive and sustained government action, the compounding impacts of heat on health, livelihoods and social well-being will continue to deepen existing inequalities. Investing in heat resilience—through policy, infrastructure and community-based action—is not only a health imperative but also a development necessity.34

Effective adaptation also depends on raising community awareness and ensuring access to essential resources.3536 Public education campaigns in local languages and through accessible channels, tailored to cultural and contextual needs, can strengthen preparedness and behavioural change.37 In addition, regular monitoring of community vulnerability should guide responsive, data-driven action.

To maximise the impact of training programmes and protect vulnerable populations, there is an urgent need to integrate such initiatives into primary healthcare systems through supportive policies, budgetary commitments and operational alignment. This requires coordinated action across national, state and local levels, aligned with successful models of integrated climate-health programming.38 A multisectoral, equity-focused approach should include hyperlocal heat alert systems delivered through trusted networks like ASHAs and ANMs, improved access to water and electricity, passive cooling solutions for vulnerable households, targeted infrastructure, such as cooling centres and green spaces and occupational protections.

Limitations and future directions

This study has some limitations that should be considered. First, because there was no control group, we cannot say with certainty that all observed improvements were caused only by the training. However, by comparing participants’ knowledge and confidence before and after the intervention, we were able to observe important changes associated with the programme. At the same time, we cannot rule out the influence of other external factors or parallel activities that may have contributed to these improvements.

Second, some of the evaluations relied on surveys, including self-reported measures of confidence. This means that participants reported how confident they felt, but these responses may not fully reflect their actual skills or performance in practice.

Third, the study was implemented within a specific public health system context in which the training was supported by state and district government authorities, and participation was integrated into routine workforce development activities. This context is an important strength because it facilitated high participation, institutional engagement and potential sustainability. However, it may also limit the generalisability of the findings to settings where similar government support, workforce structures, supervisory systems or resources are not available. In addition, trainers were selected based on local supervisory recommendations rather than standardised criteria. Differences in trainers’ experience, communication and facilitation skills may have influenced the consistency of training delivery across locations and, consequently, participant outcomes. Future programmes would benefit from a more structured process for selecting, training and evaluating trainers while maintaining strong institutional and government support.

Finally, this study mainly evaluated short-term improvements in knowledge and confidence and did not assess whether participants retained this knowledge over time, changed their clinical practices or contributed to improved community health outcomes. To address this, the research team is currently working with the local government to incorporate HRI as a health outcome to be routinely monitored. The initiative also includes annual refresher trainings, expansion of the programme to train additional community health workers in other districts and the development of a WhatsApp-based chatbot tool to support health workers in identifying and responding to community symptoms more effectively. Nevertheless, future studies should include longer follow-up periods, more objective evaluation methods and stronger study designs to better assess long-term impact.

Conclusion

In conclusion, findings from the ToT, FHW sessions and community assessments underscore the urgent need for a coordinated and comprehensive strategy to address extreme heat in India. The programme significantly boosted health workers’ knowledge and confidence in managing HRIs, though ongoing training is essential to close remaining gaps. Community surveys revealed high vulnerability, with grassroots adaptations insufficient in the face of inadequate institutional support, limited early warning systems and poor access to essential services. To reduce preventable deaths and protect livelihoods, the government must invest in localised, equity-driven heat adaptation—embedding preparedness into primary healthcare, strengthening infrastructure and expanding early warning communication through trusted networks. These efforts directly support the objectives of India’s National Programme on Climate Change and Human Health, which focuses on integrating climate resilience into health systems at all levels.

A sustained, multisectoral approach is critical to building climate-resilient health systems for the most at-risk populations. With focused action, India can not only safeguard vulnerable populations but also set a precedent for climate-health leadership in the global South.
