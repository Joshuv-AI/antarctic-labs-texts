TRAUMA: making trauma clinical guidance more implementable
Authors: Danielle J Wilson, Gabriela Zavala Wong, Christopher Tignanelli, Mary Nix, Ashley N Moreno, Lacey N LaGrone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTrauma clinical guidance (guidelines, protocols, algorithms, etc) has been shown to improve patient outcomes; however, it is only used in about half of the patients to whom it applies. Guidance implementation is affected by intrinsic factors (eg, guidance format) as well as extrinsic factors (eg, the clinical environment). Recommendations and frameworks have been created to aid in the development of implementable guidance. We hypothesize that existing trauma clinical guidance lacks elements important for implementation.MethodsThe Framework for Guideline Implementability by Gagliardi, which consists of 22 elements arranged into eight domains, was used to evaluate trauma clinical guidance. A sample of 20 pieces of guidance, crafted by 11 professional organizations, were reviewed. Data were extracted to identify the presence or absence of each implementability element.ResultsAll guidance provided a clear objective and 85% allowed for individualized application of recommendations based on clinical scenario. Approximately half of the guidance included formatting elements, such as graphic aids, to enhance usability, and 50% incorporated formal evidence grading. Patient-friendly tools accompanied 10% of guidance, and few discussed implementation strategies (25%) or quality metrics (30%) to evaluate guidance implementation.DiscussionClinical guidance exists on a spectrum, from narrative (eg, written documents) to executable tools (eg, automated decision support based on patient context). While integration of computable guidance into clinical workflows may be the ultimate goal in high-resource settings, there are other more feasible and even cost-free modifications developers may integrate into new guidance to improve implementation across settings.ConclusionUtilization of trauma clinical guidance is crucial for improving healthcare quality. To achieve this, guidance developers might leverage the elements in the new TRAUMA (Transparency, Robust inclusivity, Adaptability, Usability, Measurability, Accessibility) framework that enhance implementability. Future research is needed to validate this theoretical new framework’s impact on clinical implementation and patient outcomes.Level of evidenceIV.Keywords: guidelines, implementation

Introduction

In the 1970s, studies revealed wide clinical practice variation for similar patient populations in the USA, with procedures like coronary angiography and carotid endarterectomy used inappropriately in up to 32% of cases.1,3 This precipitated a call for evidence-based medicine.1 Additionally, medical research began to skyrocket, with 8.1 million journal articles indexed on MEDLINE and a nearly fivefold increase in randomized controlled trial publications between 1978 and 2001.4 Clinicians struggled to keep up with the volume and validity of the data, prompting the development of evidence-based clinical practice guidelines (CPGs).1 5 Medical societies have been heavily involved in guideline development since the 1980s, as is true for trauma clinical guidance, which is now comprised of algorithms, best practices, consensus statements and protocols, in addition to traditional guidelines.1 Clinical guidance differs from guidelines in that it is not always underpinned by a systematic evidence review.

Trauma clinical guidance utilization has been shown to improve patient outcomes.6 7 In a prospective, randomized, double-blinded, multicenter trial, major deviations in guidelines on damage control, transfusion and ventilation management resulted in a threefold increase in mortality at 30 and 90 days.7 Additionally, a 50% reduction in mortality compared with baseline, and a decrease in the cost of patient care, were identified after 22 hospitals implemented the Brain Trauma Foundation (BTF) Guidelines on traumatic brain injury during a prospective quality improvement initiative.6 Despite these benefits, clinical guidance implementation (the uptake and use) is lacking throughout medicine, including trauma, where it is applied in only 53%–66% of eligible cases.7,12

Barriers to implementation include complex patient characteristics, provider workload and hospital resources.7 Prior research has shown that guidance implementation relies on intrinsic factors (ie, the guidance itself) and extrinsic factors such as provider characteristics, clinical environment, access to relevant guidance and implementation strategy.8 9 13 While both factors are important, addressing intrinsic factors is often more feasible and cost-effective.14 Gagliardi et al proposed a framework to enhance ‘implementability,’ defined as the features of clinical guidance that facilitates its use. This framework emphasizes the domains of adaptability, usability, validity, applicability, communicability, accommodation, implementation and evaluation to improve implementation.13 15

Clinical guidance supports provider decision-making and is categorized into knowledge levels, each with its own purpose (figure 1).16 17 Level 1 is narrative text, which is broadly applicable but difficult to implement at the bedside. Level 2 includes semistructured tools such as algorithms or clinical calculators, while level 3 involves computable guidance, integrating recommendations into digital platforms such as electronic health records (EHRs) to enhance precision and ease implementation.16 18 19 Level 4, executable guidance, automates computable recommendations based on a patient’s context.16 18 Standards-based, Machine-readable, Adaptive, Requirements-based, and Testable Guidelines, endorsed by the WHO, leverage big data with a precision health model to generate dynamic, context-specific recommendations.19 Regardless of where guidance falls on this spectrum, there are specific elements that developers can incorporate to aid in implementation.

Despite the positive impact of trauma clinical guidance on patient mortality, there are reasons for concern about current poor implementation. This study evaluated current trauma guidance for implementability. The authors hypothesized that trauma clinical guidance in the USA lacks elements that aid in implementability, hindering the uptake and utilization of existing recommendations.

Methods

We used the Framework for Guideline Implementability developed by Gagliardi et al to score guidance developed by US professional organizations to evaluate the current implementability of trauma clinical guidance.15 The framework includes 22 elements organized into eight domains (table 1). Formatting elements are included within the ‘usability’ and ‘adaptability’ domains such as employing a table of contents and creating alternative versions such as a summary document. The ‘validity’ domain captures the amount of evidence supporting the recommendations and evaluation of its quality, while ‘individualization’ looks for the inclusion of clinical information to guide the application of recommendations to specific clinical scenarios (eg, indication for treatment or referral). Patient education resources and information for guiding clinicians in relevant risk–benefit discussions are covered under ‘communicability.’ The ‘accommodation’ domain seeks clear documentation of the guideline’s purpose and the necessary resources to implement recommendations, from professional competency to the necessary equipment. Finally, ‘implementation’ aids in understanding barriers to guidance utilization and suggests strategies to overcome them, and ‘monitoring’ includes suggestions to evaluate the integration of recommendations into clinical practices.15

A pragmatic sample of 20 trauma clinical guidance documents from 11 US organizations was chosen for review. Although not an exhaustive list, the selected organizations, most of which are core partners in the Coalition for National Trauma Research, were chosen for their leadership in trauma research and care. Additional organizations were chosen to represent select subspecialty perspectives in trauma clinical guidance. These organizations include the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma (EAST), American Association for the Surgery of Trauma, Joint Trauma System, American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma (ACS COT), Pediatric Trauma Society, American Pediatric Surgical Association, BTF, American Urologic Association, Orthopedic Trauma Association, American Burn Association and American College of Emergency Physician (ACEP).

To further refine the pragmatic sample and ensure diverse guidance was obtained, the following clinical categories were identified based on the major regions or systems that sustain traumatic injury, along with other key clinical considerations in trauma care: neurologic, thoracic, abdominal, urologic, orthopedic, vascular, analgesic, critical care and miscellaneous (eg, geriatric trauma care). At least one piece of guidance from each category was included.

A maximum of two pieces of guidance on the care for injured patients were selected from each organization. This selection was based on a PubMed search for clinical guidance published between 2019 and 2024 (search terms included in online supplemental 1). If no relevant guidance was identified from the selected organizations or clinical categories during this timeframe, society webpages were reviewed to identify guidance, even if it fell outside of the specified date range. Studies show that clinical guidance recommendations can become outdated within 3 years due to the emergence of new evidence.1 20 21 Considering the time required to create and revise guidelines, reviewing them for updates every 5 years is perceived as more feasible.22 When possible, clinical guidance released in the last 5 years was selected to ensure review of the most current guideline development practices. When no guidance from this period was available, the most recently developed document was chosen.

One reviewer extracted data (DJW), assessing each guidance on the 22 elements described by the Framework for Guideline Implementability.15 If an element was explicitly stated as not applicable to the guidance, credit was given for that element. For elements with multiple components (eg, industrial standards for equipment or technology, or policy regarding their use), any explicitly stated component received credit. A second reviewer (GZW) performed a quality audit by independently reviewing the guidance documents. After four were analyzed by each reviewer, results were compared and elements with discrepancy were discussed. When the number of recommendations was not explicitly highlighted or stated in the guidance, the reviewers developed a consistent method to count each key theme as one recommendation. The remaining guidance was independently reviewed, and results were compared. Analysis and summary statistics including sample size, median and range were calculated using Microsoft Excel (V.2308). Finally, the reviewed guidance was ranked as knowledge level 1, 2, 3 or 4 by one reviewer (DJW) based on being narrative only, including algorithms or clinical calculators, and employing computable or executable recommendations, respectively.16

Results

Twenty trauma clinical guidance documents were reviewed including the following clinical categories: neurologic (n=3), thoracic (n=2), abdominal (n=2), orthopedic (n=2), urologic (n=3), vascular (n=1), analgesic (n=2), critical care (n=3) and miscellaneous (n=2).23,42 The full list of guidance and analysis is available as online supplemental 2. The quality audit revealed a strong consensus between reviewers. The mean agreement on framework elements was 95% (range 75%–100%). Guidance was developed by the above organizations, with three resulting from partnerships between two societies. The majority (80%) of guidance was produced between 2019 and 2024, one was released in 2018, one in 2015 and one had no date available. Finally, one was dated as 2018 on the webpage, however, the full guidance was from 2011.37

Evaluation of the selected guidance formats based on the ‘usability’ domain showed that 70% of guidance included a table of contents and 45% displayed the supporting evidence in tabular format in addition to narrative text (table 2). A graphic display of recommendations was included in 55% of guidance including tables summarizing recommendations (n=8), algorithms (n=3), pictorial displays (n=2) or a ‘pocket card’ (n=1), with some guidance utilizing more than one format.

For ‘adaptability,’ most of the guidance had a journal-published version (70%) or was displayed on a society webpage (65%); however, fewer included a summary (35%) or a patient-friendly (10%) version. These patient-friendly formats were the only form of patient education with no guidance including physician–patient discussion points or contact information for patients to acquire resources, making ‘communicability’ the least used domain. Additionally, patients were not included in development of the reviewed guidance.

Analysis of ‘validity’ showed that each guidance document contained a median of 9.5 unique recommendations, ranging from a singular recommendation to 33. These recommendations were supported by a median of 65.5 (range 4–354) references, and half of the guidance incorporated a systematic evaluation of the evidence quality. The methods used to assess evidence quality included the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework (n=3), Oxford Centre for Evidence-based Medicine-Levels of Evidence (n=2), Guidelines for Critical Review Form (n=1) or their own described methods (n=5).43,45

‘Applicability’ was frequently addressed (85%) by guideline developers, who incorporated specific clinical scenarios to which the recommendations applied (table 3). Examples include suggesting that the benefit of anticoagulation be weighed against the risk of bleeding in patients with solid organ injuries or reporting recommendations based on desired outcomes (eg, the intervention does not improve disease-specific outcome measures but minimizes intensive care unit (ICU) days).

The largest domain, ‘accommodation’, includes nine elements with utilization rates ranging from 15% to 100%. The guidance objective was always stated and included assistance with clinical decision-making (n=19) and education (n=1). The guidance users, or those specifically needed to carry out the recommendations such as neurosurgery or orthopedic surgery, were explicitly stated in 35% of the guidance documents. User needs, or the values and interests of key groups, were included in 60%. Examples of user needs include framing the recommendation based on perceived patient interest (eg, patients prefer the risks of additional imaging to avoid a potentially devastating outcome) and providing recommendations that allow selective utilization of resources based on patient presentation, rather than mandating a uniform level of treatment for all patients with a given injury. The equipment or technology necessary to carry out recommendations included items such as radiologic imaging, equipment for fracture stabilization or laboratory testing and was reported by 60%. While specific industrial regulations for technology were not found in any guidance, the authors gave credit for references to the risk of technology utilization, such as radiation exposure, which was included in 15% of reviewed guidance. Necessary human resources, usually those who should be included in a multidisciplinary team such as pharmacy, social work or the transfer center, were specified in 20%, and education required for guidance implementation was addressed by 35%. This ranged from simply stating that education was necessary to including step-by-step technical directions for guidance users. Workflow impacts like changes in documentation or assessment protocols were mentioned in 20% of guidance. Additionally, the costs associated with the recommendations, including medication prices or savings incurred by reducing time in the ICU, were incorporated in 25% of guidance.

The ‘implementation’ domain revealed that three guidance documents (15%) identified specific barriers to recommendations, such as location or availability of supplies, while none identified facilitators. The tools and strategies provided for implementation included providing a documentation template or an implementation guide, which were used in 25% and 20% of guidance, respectively. Finally, for the ‘evaluation’ domain, a monitoring strategy in the form of suggested performance metrics was incorporated in 30% of the guidance.

Overall, the reviewed trauma clinical guidance showed mostly knowledge level 1 (n=17) and some level 2 (n=3) due to the inclusion of algorithms. Levels 3 (computable) or 4 (executable) guidance may exist, but none was identified with our search.

Discussion

Clinical guidance implementation has been investigated in many healthcare fields and various provider groups, but this is the first study to our knowledge evaluating the implementability of trauma clinical guidance. While several intrinsic and extrinsic factors influence clinical guidance implementability, guideline factors serve among the greatest barriers and facilitators.46 Specifically, concise, flexible, evidence-based and user-friendly guidance has greater uptake than guidance that is complex, lacks clarity or challenges clinician autonomy.8 14 46 Additional practical actions to improve implementability include stating which populations the recommendations are relevant to, tailoring guidance to the intended audience and creating a graphic that conveys recommendations.14

The reviewed trauma clinical guidance was categorized as knowledge level 1 and level 2, representing narrative text and algorithms, respectively. While each level plays an important role, narrative guidance is challenging to implement at the bedside. Inclusion of algorithms improves implementability, however, further progress can be made by adapting recommendations into computable format (level 3).47

Our results show that trauma clinical guidance excels in stating clear, concise objectives and considering clinical scenarios that impact implementation (‘applicability’), allowing providers to tailor the guidance utilization. However, only about half of guidance tabulated evidence or provided a graphic aid, and fewer provided a summary version (35%). Additionally, grading evidence quality, which was performed in half of the reviewed guidance, allows clinicians to understand the strength of recommendations, thus enhancing trustworthiness.9 14 46

Elements from the ‘accommodation,’ ‘implementation’ and ‘evaluation’ domains had low utilization, despite their practical importance. For instance, detailing the equipment and human resources required helps users assess feasibility based on their available resources and encourages guidance developers to include recommendations for when equipment or specialists are unavailable. Similarly, considering workflow impact, costs and barriers allows developers to incorporate tools and strategies to overcome these situations. A lack of diversity in clinical guidance authorship including race, ethnicity, gender and discipline (ie, methodological expert, clinician, etc) may limit inclusion of these elements.1 48 Thus, trauma guidance development should prioritize diverse authorship, including representatives from all trauma center levels.49

The reviewed guidance showed minimal patient involvement and lacked patient-friendly materials. While clinical guidance is focused on the provider, patient and caregiver engagement is essential to reflect their preferences in medical decision-making.10 Patients often raise key concerns that medical professionals have not considered, and their involvement has been shown to enhance guidance implementation.10 50 51 Methods to elicit preferences include patient panels, focus groups, surveys or literature reviews. However, standardized methods to report and incorporate their preferences into recommendations are lacking.50 51 Codevelopment of patient-friendly versions can empower patients with understandable, actionable and accessible tools for better healthcare interactions.51

To contextualize our findings, we compared our results to those of Gagliardi’s review of non-surgical guidance and identified several differences.15 Trauma guidance was more likely to include tabulated evidence (45% vs 25%), a published version (70% vs 50%), an electronic/webpage version (65% vs 25%) and address the needs of guidance users (60% vs 0%). However, trauma guidance lagged in other critical areas, such as evidence grading (50% vs 95%), patient involvement (10% vs 50%) and the inclusion of monitoring strategies (30% vs 50%). Similar to our results, Gagliardi’s review showed limited consideration of equipment/technology regulations, human resources, education and training requirements, workflow impact, costs and the broader ‘implementation’ domain.

In addition, we compared our findings to previously published research in other surgical subspecialties. One study, which utilized the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research & Evaluation II framework to evaluate the American Academy of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery Foundation Guidelines, reported comparable findings.52,54 While the study showed that these guidelines excelled in domains such as scope and purpose, stakeholder involvement and clarity of presentation (>90% compliance), it also highlighted limited attention to applicability elements. Specifically, the authors reported a lack of facilitators and barriers to implementation, adoption tools, resource implications and monitoring criteria, elements that correspond to the under-represented ‘accommodation’ and ‘implementation’ domains identified in our review of trauma guidance.52

Our analysis found minimal difference in the utilization of elements across clinical categories, though certain organizations exceled in specific domains. For example, EAST’s ‘Evaluation and Management of Blunt Cerebrovascular Injury’ exemplifies a comprehensive structure through its table of contents, tabulated and narrative evidence, and graphical summary of recommendations.42 ACEP’s ‘Mild Traumatic Brain Injury’ stands out in the ‘Adaptability’ domain with a webpage summary, full policy, additional summaries and a patient-friendly resource.38 ACS COT guidance excels in practical implementation with specific implementation strategies and outcome measures.23 28 Interestingly, guidance developed by two societies yielded similar results to single-organization guidance. This is likely due to guidance taking on the formatting of the leading society, rather than the creation of a hybrid format that blends elements from each organization.

The 2020 Focused Updates to the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute’s Asthma Management Guidelines serve as an exemplary model of implementable guidance.13 55 The developers ensured recommendations were feasible for the target audience based on resource availability and included dedicated implementation sections. Developed with input from focus groups of patients, caregivers and providers, these sections clearly describe which population(s) the recommendation does not apply to, highlight risk–benefit trade-offs for patient discussions and address topic-specific considerations such as evidence limitations. Additionally, the guideline developers partnered with informaticists to create computable recommendations.13

Computable guidance, which facilitates efficient and accurate use of recommendations in digital formats, contrasts with narrative guidance, which is often inefficient in clinical settings.47 The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s ‘Adapting Clinical Guidelines for the Digital Age’ initiative integrates informatics, implementation strategies and evaluation metrics directly into guidance development, enabling seamless clinical implementation and continuous improvement (figure 2).13 56 The initiative also introduced technical standards, called Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources for Computable Guidance, which support interoperability and broad integration into EHRs, clinical decision support (CDS) tools and quality metrics.47 56 57 These tools, including alerts and standardized order sets, have improved care outcomes but require thoughtful design to avoid alert fatigue.58,61 Computable formats also simplify guidance updates, supporting ‘living guidelines’ that adapt to new evidence in real time and push updates to healthcare systems efficiently.56 62

Numerous factors and interventions exist to improve clinical guidance implementation. While complex, multifaceted interventions yield success, singular, focused interventions have also been shown to be effective.10 11 63 This is valuable as organizations often face limited resources for guidance development and implementation. Familiarity with tools like the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality CDS Connect, which serves as a repository of publicly available CDS resources, ease digital integration, but developers can still improve implementability by focusing on intrinsic guidance factors and ensuring accessibility (ie, guidance not kept behind paywalls).13 46

Our review of 20 trauma guidance documents revealed several gaps in implementability, prompting the development of the TRAUMA Framework for Guidance Implementation (figure 3). Transparency emerged as a critical need given that not all guidance had a clear date of creation or update. Half did not grade evidence quality, and some lacked a clear documentation of development methods, which can undermine trust and hinder adoption. Robust inclusivity was another deficiency, with only 10% of guidance incorporating patient involvement. Additionally, diverse authorship is essential to ensure guidance addresses the needs of patients and providers across all levels of trauma systems. Adaptability builds on the fact that while 85% of guidance considered some specific clinical scenarios, many were still lacking implementation strategies for diverse contexts and resource-limited settings. Flexible recommendations that can be adapted across the spectrum of trauma care are crucial.

TRAUMA (Transparency, Robust inclusivity, Adaptability, Usability, Measurability, Accessibility) framework for guideline implementability.

Usability is vital, however, only 55% of guidance included a graphic and 70% had a table of contents. In a time-sensitive environment, guidance must be both easily accessible and quickly interpretable. Measurability addresses the lack of monitoring strategies identified in the reviewed guidance. Without performance metrics, assessing implementation success and identifying areas for continuous improvement is challenging. Lastly, accessibility emerged as a crucial consideration. While most guidance was freely available, some remained behind paywalls, limiting access in resource-constrained settings. While some domains overlap with Gagliardi’s Framework, the TRAUMA Framework addresses challenges unique to trauma care, including time-critical decision-making, resource variability and the need for rapid implementation. The framework synthesizes our empirical findings with practical considerations for trauma care delivery but requires further investigation and validation.

The main limitation of this study is the application of a framework specifically developed for CPGs to a variety of trauma clinical guidance (best practices, consensus documents, etc). While this may impact some implementability elements, the authors pose that these clinical guidance documents have the same intended use as traditional CPGs. Thus, recognizing which implementability elements are underused is important to improve future guidance. Additionally, this study includes both peer-reviewed and non-peer-reviewed guidance. Although these types of guidance differ in scientific rigor, all were included to reflect the broad spectrum of resources available for clinical use.

The inclusion of a pragmatic sample of selected guidance introduces the possibility of selection bias. To combat this, the authors developed a sampling strategy based on organization and clinical category to elicit a diverse sample. Additionally, some elements in the Framework for Guideline Implementability required subjective assessment. To ensure consistency, a second reviewer analyzed the guidance, which showed a mean agreement of 95% for each framework element. Finally, as a theoretical analysis, this study does not directly measure the impact of guideline formatting on clinical practice. Further research assessing patient outcomes before and after guideline changes would provide valuable insights into the practical effects of these modifications on care for injured patients.

Conclusion

Enhancing clinical guidance implementability is imperative to improving healthcare quality throughout medicine.46 This is especially true in trauma care, where inappropriate practice variation has been recognized to increase patient morbidity and mortality.64 Additionally, care of the injured patient often requires timely decision-making, and most trauma clinical guidance represents level 1 and level 2 decision support. These narrative formats prove time-consuming to read and interpret, hampering the implementation of evidence-based care. Many features that aid in the implementation guidance are lacking, such as formatting for ease of use, grading evidence, incorporation of key partner views and preferences, and addressing practical requirements for recommendations based on a diversity of resource availability and clinical context, which strongly affect a clinician’s ability to implement guidance. Utilizing the proposed TRAUMA Framework for Implementability can assist trauma guidance developers in creating practical and effective guidance. Furthermore, advancement to computable and executable formats is one mechanism to enhance ease of use. Translation of narrative guidance into at minimum bedside-ready, and ideally computable and executable forms may ultimately provide vast enhancement in both the dissemination and implementation of guidance with the goal of improving patient outcomes.
