Mapping the Phosphorylation Regulatory Network of Single‐Celled Fibre Elongation Mediated by GhBIN2 in Cotton
Authors: Le Liu, Mengli Yu, Longfei Wang, Ghulam Qanmber, Liqiang Fan, Yujun Li, Zhao Liu, Xuwen Wang, Li Liu, Fuguang Li, Shuangxia Jin, Zuoren Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProtein phosphorylation plays a pivotal role in cellular signal transduction and plant development. The plant steroid hormone Brassinosteroids (BRs) signal transduction relies primarily on protein kinase‐mediated phosphorylation cascades. However, the specific mechanisms of phosphorylation regulation in BR signalling remain to be fully elucidated. This study focuses on BIN2, an indispensable protein kinase in the BR signalling pathway, utilising single‐celled cotton fibre to investigate the mechanisms by which phosphorylation regulates cell elongation. Firstly, we confirmed the inhibitory role of GhBIN2 in fibre elongation through its overexpression. Subsequently, we employed 4D‐fastDIA quantitative phosphoproteomics and proteomics analysis to map the GhBIN2‐mediated phosphorylation regulatory network. Through a comprehensive analysis of this network, we identified six credible substrates of GhBIN2. Further investigation revealed that GhBIN2 interacts with substrate GhIQD14 and increases its abundance through phosphorylation to negatively regulate fibre elongation. This study deepens the understanding of BR signalling in cotton fibre elongation and provides experimental evidence and new insights for comprehending the regulatory role of protein phosphorylation in plant cell elongation processes.Keywords: 4D‐fastDIA quantitative phosphoproteomics, Brassinosteroids, cotton fibre, GhBIN2

Introduction

Brassinosteroids (BRs) are endogenous plant hormones that were first discovered and isolated from rapeseed ( Brassica napus ) (Clouse and Sasse 1998). They play crucial roles in regulating various aspects of plant growth and development, including cell elongation, lateral and primary root development, vascular differentiation, pollen tube elongation, photomorphogenesis and resistance to abiotic stresses (Planas‐Riverola et al. 2019; Sampaothong et al. 2024; Solonkin et al. 2024; Serdiati et al. 2024). BRs effectively activate the inherent potential of plants, promoting growth and development, thereby enhancing plant productivity and stress tolerance. This makes them highly promising for applications in agricultural production and ecological restoration (Yang et al. 2011; Cherry et al. 2024; Tharwat et al. 2024).

At the molecular level, BRs primarily modulate plant growth and development by regulating the expression of downstream target genes through a sophisticated signal transduction cascade (Zhang et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2021; Liu, Qanmber, et al. 2018). Current research indicates that BRs are synthesised in the endoplasmic reticulum through a series of enzymatic reactions and subsequently transported to the plasma membrane via vesicular trafficking or other mechanisms (Nolan et al. 2020). At the plasma membrane, BRs bind to the transmembrane receptor kinase BRI1 (BRASSINOSTEROID INSENSITIVE 1) (He et al. 2000) and its homologues BRL1 and BRL3 (BRI1‐LIKE 1/3) (Kinoshita et al. 2005). This binding leads to the recruitment of the co‐receptor SERK3/BAK1 (SOMATIC EMBRYOGENESIS RECEPTOR KINASE 3/BRI1‐ASSOCIATED RECEPTOR KINASE 1) (Gou et al. 2012). The formation of this ligand‐receptor complex triggers autophosphorylation and transphosphorylation of the receptor kinases, thereby activating the downstream BR signal transduction cascade (Li and Nam 2002). In the BR signal transduction cascade, phosphorylation of the BSK (BR‐SIGNALLING KINASE) (Sreeramulu et al. 2013) family proteins and CDG1 (CONSTITUTIVE DIFFERENTIAL GROWTH 1) (Kim et al. 2011) activates the BSU1 (BRI1 SUPPRESSOR 1) phosphatase (Kim et al. 2009). Activated BSU1 inhibits the GSK3 (GLYCOGEN SYNTHASE KINASE 3)‐like kinase BIN2 (BRASSINOSTEROID INSENSITIVE 2) through dephosphorylation, which reduces BIN's phosphorylation of downstream transcription factors BES1/BZR1 (BRI1‐EMS‐SUPPRESSOR 1/BRASSINAZOLE‐RESISTANT 1) (Youn and Kim 2015). This process promotes dephosphorylation and nuclear localization of BES1/BZR1, enhancing their transcriptional regulatory activity. Ultimately, these core effectors synergistically modulate BR‐responsive gene expression, including the activation of BR‐induced genes and the repression of BR‐inhibited genes (Yu et al. 2011).

The glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) family comprises highly conserved serine/threonine protein kinases in eukaryotes, playing indispensable roles as key signalling mediators in numerous biological processes (Beurel et al. 2015). Plants exhibit a diverse array of GSK3 homologues, with research revealing 10 GSK3‐like kinases in the dicot model Arabidopsis thaliana ( A. thaliana ) and 9 in the monocot model Oryza sativa (Qi et al. 2013). This suggests that GSK3 genes have experienced multiple duplication events during plant evolution, leading to the diversification and specialisation of their biological functions (Qi et al. 2013). BIN2, a key member of the GSK3 family, has become a focal point in research on plant growth and development. It primarily regulates various physiological processes through protein phosphorylation modifications. For instance, during stomatal development, BIN2 interacts with MITOGEN‐ACTIVATED PROTEIN KINASE (MAPK) cascades to promote the phosphorylation and proteasome‐dependent degradation of SPEECHLESS (SPCH) (Gudesblat et al. 2012). BIN2 also phosphorylates transcription factors PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PIF4) and BRASSINAZOLE‐RESISTANT 1 (BZR1), promoting their ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby finely regulating hypocotyl elongation and overall plant growth (Li et al. 2017). In response to cold, BIN2 phosphorylates the transcription factors INDUCER OF CBF EXPRESSION 1 (ICE1) and CESTA (CES), promoting their degradation and thus influencing plant cold tolerance (Chen et al. 2020). Additionally, BIN2 stabilises various functional proteins and transcription factors through phosphorylation, including the root development regulator UPBEAT1 (UPB1) (Li et al. 2020), abiotic stress response factors TINY and RESPONSIVE TO DESICCATION 26 (RD26) (Jiang et al. 2019), chloroplast development regulator GOLDEN2‐LIKE 1 (GLK1) (Zhang et al. 2021), BRASSINOSTEROID (BR) signalling negative regulators MYB‐LIKE 2 (MYBL2) and ATBS1‐INTERACTING FACTOR 2 (AIF2) (Kim et al. 2017), and seed germination regulator ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE 5 (ABI5) (Hu and Yu 2014). Through these roles, BIN2 participates in the regulatory networks of multiple physiological processes. Moreover, BIN2 serves as a key node in the crosstalk between various hormones and environmental signal transduction pathways, such as those involving abscisic acid, auxin and light, via its protein kinase function, positioning it centrally in the plant growth regulation network (Yang et al. 2017; Wang, Zhao, et al. 2018). Despite significant progress in elucidating the phosphorylation‐dependent role of BIN2, most studies are still limited to studying individual genes using the model organism Arabidopsis. Consequently, there remains a substantial gap in understanding the broader regulatory network orchestrated by BIN2 phosphorylation and its functional conservation across diverse plant species.

Cotton fibres, uniquely elongated single cells in the plant kingdom, serve as an ideal model for investigating cellular elongation and its intrinsic regulatory mechanisms (Yang et al. 2020; Qanmber et al. 2024). Cotton fibre development is governed by a complex interplay of regulatory mechanisms in which phytohormones play a pivotal role, with BR, auxin, gibberellin and abscisic acid being particularly significant (Liu et al. 2023). Transcription factors, crucial proteins in gene expression regulation, are extensively involved in the regulatory network governing cotton fibre development (Liu et al. 2023). Concurrently, epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation and histone modifications, substantially modulate the expression patterns of genes associated with cotton fibre development (Xing et al. 2023). Additionally, the biosynthesis and structural remodelling of the fibre cell wall represent critical stages in fibre development, involving the coordinated action of numerous functional genes (Chen, Li, et al. 2023). Protein posttranslational modifications (PTMs) are essential for plant growth and development (Dai Vu et al. 2018; Rumiyati et al. 2024), with phosphorylation—a prevalent and key PTM—playing a pivotal role in numerous biological processes such as signal transduction, metabolic regulation and cell cycle progression (Kusakina and Dodd 2012; Zarikian et al. 2024). Nevertheless, the role and regulatory network of phosphorylation modifications during cotton fibre development remain areas requiring extensive investigation.

In this study, we employed cotton fibre, a uniquely elongated single‐cell model system, to map the GhBIN2‐mediated phosphorylation regulatory network at the cellular level and elucidate the mechanism of phosphorylation during cotton fibre development. Firstly, we elucidated the negative regulatory role of GhBIN2 in cotton fibre elongation through its overexpression. Subsequently, we utilised high‐throughput 4D‐fastDIA quantitative phosphoproteomics to systematically identify GhBIN2‐mediated differentially phosphorylated proteins in upland cotton, investigating the regulatory mechanisms of phosphorylation during cotton fibre elongation. By integrating 4D‐fastDIA quantitative proteomics data, we revealed the direct impact of GhBIN2‐mediated phosphorylation on functional protein abundance and established a comprehensive phosphorylation regulatory network for cotton fibre elongation. Finally, we validated the interaction and phosphorylation between GhBIN2 and its key downstream target, GhIQD14 and confirmed the negative regulatory role of GhIQD14 in fibre elongation through genetic approaches, deepening our understanding of the GhBIN2 phosphorylation regulatory network. This study provides a comprehensive phosphorylation map of GhBIN2 in upland cotton and establishes its phosphorylation regulatory network, laying the foundation for further exploration of the molecular mechanisms of phosphorylation in regulating cell elongation and providing multi‐omics datasets for subsequent investigations.

Results

GhBIN2 , A Member of the GSK Family, Negatively Regulates Cotton Fibre Elongation

We identified six orthologs of AtBIN2 (AtSK21) in upland cotton and designated them GhSK21 to GhSK26 (Wang, Yang, et al. 2018) (Figure S1). GhSK21 and GhSK26 exhibited high homology with AtBIN2, and both contain a conserved STKc_GSK3 domain (Figure S1B). To further investigate the functionality of GhSK21 and GhSK26, we characterised their expression profiles. Both genes are ubiquitously expressed across all tissues, with GhSK21 showing higher expression levels than GhSK26 (Figure S1C). GhSK21 and GhSK26 share high homology and belong to the At and Dt subgenomes of upland cotton, respectively, and their expression levels were further evaluated by qRT‐PCR with specific primers. The results were consistent with the transcriptome data, with GhSK21 showing a higher expression in fibres compared to GhSK26 (Figure S2A). Additionally, in 15 DPA fibres from cotton varieties with significantly different fibre lengths, GhSK21 expression was lower in the long‐fibre varieties and higher in short‐fibre varieties, whereas GhSK26 did not exhibit this expression pattern (Figure S2B). Based on this analysis, GhSK21 appears to play a unique role in fibre development, leading us to rename it as GhBIN2 for further study.

In Arabidopsis, AtBIN2 produces two transcripts through alternative splicing, with both encoding proteins that have biological functions (Cheng et al. 2017). It has also been reported that mutating the TREE sequence in the AtBIN2 coding region can enhance its biological activity and amplify its regulatory effect (Li and Nam 2002). To further investigate the function of GhBIN2, we generated transgenic cotton plants overexpressing GhBIN2, incorporating its genomic DNA (Ghbin2) with a mutated TREE sequence replaced by TRKE (Figures S1B and S3). qRT‐PCR and Western blot (WB) experiments confirmed a significant increase in GhBIN2 expression and the presence of the GhBIN2‐Flag protein (Figure 1A, Figure S4). Compared to control plants, Ghbin2 plants exhibited significant reductions in the size of cotton bolls (Figure 1B–E). During the initial stage of fibre development from −1 to 1 DPA, Ghbin2 plants showed a reduction in the initial number of cotton fibres and a slight delay in development (Figure 1F). Observations and measurements of fibre lengths at 10 DPA, 15 DPA and 20 DPA in Ghbin2 and its background plant ZM49 revealed that fibre development in Ghbin2 plants was delayed, and fibre length was significantly shortened during the rapid elongation phase (Figure 1G,I). Statistical analysis of mature fibre length revealed that Ghbin2 plants exhibited significantly shorter fibres compared to wild‐type (WT) plants (Figure 1H,J). Conversely, silencing of the GhBIN2 gene resulted in elongated fibres (Figure S5). These findings demonstrate that GhBIN2 is a functional gene that negatively regulates cotton fibre elongation.

GhBIN2 Is Involved in the BR Signalling Pathway

To confirm that GhBIN2 is a functional member of the GSK family and participates in the BR signalling pathway, we conducted in vitro ovule culture experiments. First, ovules of Ghbin2 plants were cultured with varying concentrations of Bikinin, a specific inhibitor of the GSK family (Figure S6A,B). Compared to the control group, Bikinin treatment restored fibre elongation in Ghbin2 plants, with the effect becoming more pronounced as the Bikinin concentration increased. The best recovery was observed at a Bikinin concentration of 25 μM, whereas higher concentrations began to inhibit fibre elongation (Figure S6C). Additionally, when the ovule culture of control and Ghbin2 plants was treated with 1 μM brassinolide (BL), the control group showed a significant increase in fibre length. In contrast, the Ghbin2 group exhibited only a slight, nonsignificant increase (Figure S7A–C). These results indicate that GhBIN2 is involved in the BR signalling pathway in upland cotton as a member of the GSK family.

GhBIN2 Mediates the Phosphorylation of Various Motifs in Upland Cotton

BIN2 is a serine/threonine protein kinase that participates in various signal transduction processes by phosphorylating different substrates (Wang et al. 2012). To investigate whether GhBIN2 also plays a role in the phosphorylation process in upland cotton and regulates fibre elongation through phosphorylation, we used serine/threonine‐specific phosphorylation antibodies to detect overall phosphorylation levels in Ghbin2 fibres at 10 DPA and 15 DPA. The results showed a significant increase in phosphorylation levels in Ghbin2 cotton fibres (Figure 2A).

To further explore the mechanism by which GhBIN2 regulates fibre elongation through phosphorylation, we performed quantitative 4D‐FastDIA quantitative phosphoproteomics on 10 DPA and 15 DPA fibres. This analysis identified a total of 25 259 peptide segments, of which 14 247 were phosphorylated, corresponding to 25 106 phosphorylated modification sites. Phosphorylation quantification revealed that 12 814 modification sites could be compared for phosphorylation levels, and we identified 4488 quantitatively comparable phosphorylated proteins (Figure 2B).

It is valuable for understanding the substrate preference of kinases by identifying protein phosphorylation motifs and providing insights into the biological pathways they regulate. To characterise peptide sequences in upland cotton, we used the MoMo analysis tool, which is based on the motif‐x algorithm, to analyse sequences containing six amino acids located upstream and downstream of the identified differential phosphorylation sites. The frequency distribution of amino acids surrounding these sites was visualised using a heatmap, which facilitated the identification of enriched phosphorylation recognition motifs. In differentially phosphorylated peptides centred on serine residues, a distinct preference for amino acids D, E, P, R and S was observed around phosphorylation sites (Figure 2C). Notably, the motifs XXXRPXSPXXXXX and XXSXXXSPXXXXX were significantly enriched in Ghbin2 samples (Figure 2D, Figure S8). Similarly, for peptides centred on threonine residues, amino acids E, P and S were more prevalent near phosphorylation sites (Figure 2E), with motifs XXXXPXTPXXXXX and XXXRXXTPXPXXX displaying significant enrichment in Ghbin2 samples (Figure 2F, Figure S9). These findings suggest that GhBIN2 mediates phosphorylation at various sites within upland cotton fibres and may play a role in regulating cotton fibre elongation through phosphorylation.

GhBIN2 Regulates Cotton Fibre Elongation Through Phosphorylation

To understand how GhBIN2 phosphorylation regulates cotton fibre elongation, we performed a differential phosphorylation analysis on modification sites in cotton fibres at 10 DPA and 15 DPA. At 10 DPA, 873 differentially phosphorylated sites were identified in ZM49 and Ghbin2 fibres, corresponding to 556 upregulated and 196 downregulated phosphoproteins. At 15 DPA, there were 1501 differentially phosphorylated sites, with 617 upregulated and 552 downregulated phosphoproteins (Figure 3A). Specific BIN2 substrates, including BZR1, PUB40, G6PDH, CESAs and PIF4, were also among the differentially phosphorylated proteins (Li et al. 2021) (Figure 3B).

Proteins localised in different subcellular structures regulate plant growth and development through various pathways. Annotations of differentially phosphorylated proteins from 10 DPA and 15 DPA fibres showed that while GhBIN2‐mediated phosphorylated proteins were distributed across multiple cellular structures, there was a higher tendency for GhBIN2 to mediate the phosphorylation of nuclear proteins, which may regulate fibre elongation (Figure 3C,D). Further functional classification using COG/KOG for 10 DPA and 15 DPA phosphorylated proteins indicated GhBIN's involvement in multiple processes, including signal transduction, posttranslational modification, intracellular transport, synthesis of cytoskeleton and cell ratio, cell morphogenesis, material transport and metabolism (Figure 3E).

To identify the functional proteins regulated by phosphorylation during the rapid fibre elongation phase in Ghbin2 lines, we identified proteins consistently differentially phosphorylated at both 10 DPA and 15 DPA. A total of 65 downregulated and 147 upregulated phosphorylation genes were identified (Figure S10A,B). The results indicated that GhBIN2 influences cotton fibre elongation through two phosphorylation mechanisms: directly phosphorylating functional proteins and phosphorylating certain kinases, thereby indirectly regulating the phosphorylation levels of other proteins. This regulation involves protein phosphorylation, cell cycle, cell morphogenesis, cell differentiation, cell wall assembly, microtubule organisation and ion transport (Figure S10C).

GhBIN2 Modulates Functional Protein Levels to Regulate Fibre Elongation

Functional proteins are vital for plant growth and development, and their abundance is directly influenced by phosphorylation, which can either stabilise proteins or signal them for degradation via ubiquitination (Kim et al. 2017). To determine which proteins involved in fibre elongation are affected by GhBIN2, we performed 4D‐fastDIA quantitative proteomics on 10 DPA and 15 DPA fibres from Ghbin2 and WT plants. We identified 74 290 peptides, corresponding to 13 069 proteins, of which 13 048 were quantifiable (Figure 4A). At 10 DPA, 356 proteins showed differential abundance in Ghbin2 fibres, with 79 upregulated and 277 downregulated. At 15 DPA, 1361 differential proteins were identified, including 447 upregulated and 914 downregulated proteins (Figure 4B).

Cotton fibre development is a dynamic process involving numerous biological changes. Combining data from 10 DPA and 15 DPA fibres, we found 270 unique differential proteins in 10 DPA fibres and 1275 in 15 DPA fibres (Figure 4C). Cluster analysis showed that GhBIN2 regulates various processes at different developmental stages, including ubiquitin‐mediated proteolysis, splicing and transport of mRNA, nuclear and membrane components organisation at 10 DPA, and suberin and wax biosynthesis, DNA replication, modification−dependent protein binding and transmembrane transport at 15 DPA (Figure S11). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that downregulated proteins were significantly associated with photosynthesis and spliceosome‐related pathways. In contrast, secondary metabolic pathways, including flavonoid biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, were significantly enriched among upregulated proteins (Figure 4D). Across both stages, 58 proteins, including those involved in fibre elongation such as auxin synthesis protein (IAA8) (Arase et al. 2012), microtubule‐associated protein (IQD14) (Bürstenbinder, Möller, et al. 2017), and cell elongation regulators (EXLs) (Li et al. 2023), showed consistent trends in abundance (Figure 4E). These results suggest GhBIN's involvement in the dynamic regulation of fibre elongation by modulating the levels of specific proteins related to various biological processes.

Multi‐Omics Profiling Reveals GhBIN2‐Mediated Phosphorylation Networks in Fibre Development

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which GhBIN2 regulates cotton fibre development, we integrated transcriptomic, phosphoproteomic and proteomic data from 10 DPA and 15 DPA fibres of ZM49 and Ghbin2 plants. Initially, we combined proteomic and transcriptomic datasets to filter out nonoverlapping genes, thereby minimising transcriptional influence on protein abundance (Figure S12). Next, non‐overlapping proteins were cross‐referenced with phosphoproteomic data to identify shared proteins whose abundance changes might be regulated by phosphorylation. This integrative analysis identified 38 shared differentially expressed proteins at 10 DPA and 175 at 15 DPA (Figure 5A,B). Notably, six proteins showed significant changes in both phosphorylation and abundance at both developmental stages (Figure 5C). By combining multi‐omics data with functional annotation and pathway enrichment, we delineated a molecular network through which GhBIN2‐mediated phosphorylation affects cotton fibre development. GhBIN2 participates in multiple biological pathways, including hormone biosynthesis and metabolism, cell differentiation and elongation, microtubule cytoskeleton dynamics and posttranslational modifications like phosphorylation and ubiquitination, forming a regulatory network that modulates fibre elongation (Figure 5D).

GhBIN2 Phosphorylates GhIQD14, a Negative Regulator of Fibre Elongation

Integrated analysis revealed six proteins that showed significant differences in both phosphorylation and protein abundance in 10 DPA and 15 DPA fibres from Ghbin2 and WT lines (Figure 5E–H). These proteins are likely direct substrates of GhBIN2 phosphorylation and may be crucial in regulating fibre elongation. We conducted further experiments to validate this hypothesis.

Yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) and split‐luciferase complementation (Split‐LUC) assays confirmed in vivo interactions between GhBIN2 and GhIQD14, as well as GhNIPA (Figure 6A,B). In vitro pull‐down assays using GST‐GhBIN2 and His‐tagged GhIQD14 and GhNIPA showed that GhBIN2 specifically binds to GhIQD14 but not GhNIPA (Figure 6C,D). Subsequent in vitro kinase assays showed that GhBIN2 effectively phosphorylates GhIQD14, but not GhNIPA (Figure 6E). Mass spectrometry‐based phosphoproteomic analysis identified Ser97 as the phosphorylation site of GhIQD14 in vivo (Figure S13). To validate the role of GhBIN2 in phosphorylating GhIQD14, we generated a Ser97‐to‐Ala mutant (GhIQD14S97A). In vitro kinase activity assays demonstrated that GhBIN2 specifically phosphorylates GhIQD14, whereas the GhIQD14S97A mutant remained unphosphorylated (Figure 6F,G). Proteomic analysis further revealed a significant increase in GhIQD14 levels in Ghbin2 plants. Detection of endogenous GhIQD14 using a specific antibody confirmed that both protein abundance and phosphorylation levels of GhIQD14 were markedly elevated in Ghbin2 (Figure 6H,I). However, Bikinin treatment attenuated the increase in GhIQD14 protein levels and phosphorylation (Figure 6J,K). Collectively, these results suggest that GhBIN2 interacts with and phosphorylates GhIQD14, leading to its stabilisation and accumulation.

To determine the role of GhIQD14 in fibre elongation, we created GhIQD14 overexpression (GhIQD14‐OE) cotton lines. qRT‐PCR analysis confirmed significant upregulation of GhIQD14 in these lines (Figure S14). Morphological analysis revealed a significant reduction in boll size and fibre length in GhIQD14‐OE overexpression lines compared to controls (Figure 7A–D,F,G). Phenotypic analysis of mature fibres revealed a significant reduction in fibre length in Ghbin2 plants (Figure 7E,G), whereas silencing of GhIQD14 resulted in increased fibre elongation (Figure S15), indicating that GhIQD14 negatively regulates fibre elongation. To further explore the regulatory role of phosphorylation on GhIQD14 function, we generated Arabidopsis lines overexpressing three GhIQD14 variants: WT (GhIQD14), phosphomimetic (GhIQD14‐P) and dephosphorylated (GhIQD14‐N) forms (Figure S16A). Following confirmation of GhIQD14 expression at both RNA and protein levels (Figure S16B–E), phenotypic analysis demonstrated that all three overexpression lines exhibited significantly shorter root hairs compared to WT controls. Notably, the phosphomimetic GhIQD14‐P‐OE lines displayed the most pronounced reduction in root hair length (Figure S16F,G), suggesting that phosphorylation enhances the functional activity of GhIQD14.

Based on our experimental findings, we propose a hypothetical model in which GhBIN2 mediates phosphorylation‐dependent regulation of cotton fibre elongation. Specifically, GhBIN2 influences microtubule dynamics, cell development, RNA splicing, protein methylation and ubiquitination. These interconnected processes constitute a complex phosphorylation cascade that orchestrates cotton fibre elongation at multiple regulatory levels (Figure 8).

Discussion

Protein phosphorylation, a ubiquitous posttranslational modification, is essential for cellular signal transduction and metabolic regulation (Graves and Krebs 1999). BRs, a class of important plant hormones, primarily transmit signals through protein kinase‐mediated phosphorylation cascades (Yang et al. 2011). Nevertheless, a comprehensive understanding of the phosphorylation‐mediated regulatory networks in BR signalling pathways remains to be fully elucidated. Cotton fibres, as extremely elongated single cells, serve as an ideal single‐cell model for investigating cellular signal transduction. This study focused on BIN2, a key protein kinase in the BR signalling pathway and utilised 4D‐fastDIA quantitative phosphoproteomics and proteomics analyses to systematically unravel the function of GhBIN2 and its central phosphorylation regulatory network in cotton fibres. Our findings uncovered the regulatory mechanisms of phosphorylation during cotton fibre elongation and enhanced our understanding of phosphorylation cascades in BR signal transduction.

GhBIN2 Regulates Microtubule Organisation During Cotton Fibre Elongation

Microtubules, key components of the cellular cytoskeleton, play crucial roles in cell morphogenesis, intracellular transport and signal transduction (Baas and Qiang 2005; Sjahril et al. 2024; Yessenbayeva et al. 2024). Their dynamic balance and function are intricately regulated by Microtubule Associated Proteins (MAPs) (Hamada 2007), which bind to microtubules and affect their stability, orientation and processes of assembly and disassembly, thereby influencing cell growth. MAPs regulate microtubule depolymerisation and polymerisation and modulate interactions between microtubules. By interacting with MAPs, microtubules can alter their dynamics and structure to perform diverse functions (Perrin et al. 2007). The IQD protein family, as an important class of MAPs, can modify cell morphology by influencing the arrangement of cortical microtubules (Mitra et al. 2019). For example, in Arabidopsis, overexpression of AtIQD16 alters microtubule organisation and cell shape (Wendrich et al. 2018), whereas AtIQD5 localises to and binds microtubules, regulating their dynamic assembly and affecting cell morphology (Liang et al. 2018).

Cotton fibres are extremely long, unbranched single cells that develop from ovule epidermal cells (Yang et al. 2020). During fibre cell development, microtubules influence fibre elongation and cellulose microfibril deposition in secondary cell walls by altering their organisational patterns (Graham and Haigler 2021). Previous studies have shown that BIN2 interacts with tubulin and stabilises microtubules, thereby affecting stomatal cell shape (Liu, Yang, et al. 2018). However, direct experimental evidence identifying specific MAPs phosphorylated by BIN2 and their role in regulating cotton fibre elongation has been lacking. By integrating phosphoproteomic and proteomic analyses, we identified GhIQD14, a protein with a conserved IQD domain (Figure 5). Further experiments demonstrated that GhBIN2 interacts with and continuously phosphorylates GhIQD14 (Figure 6). Proteomic analysis revealed that GhIQD14 protein abundance was significantly upregulated during the rapid elongation phase in Ghbin2 fibres. These findings suggest that phosphorylation may regulate GhIQD14 protein accumulation by enhancing its stability or inhibiting its degradation pathway. As fibre development progresses in Ghbin2, the gradual accumulation of GhIQD14 protein ultimately leads to growth inhibition during the rapid elongation phase, thereby establishing a negative regulatory mechanism for fibre elongation. Although the precise molecular mechanisms underlying regulation need further investigation, increased GhIQD14 levels may influence microtubule arrangement during rapid fibre elongation, causing microtubules to align parallel to the cell elongation axis. This alignment promotes longitudinal cellulose deposition, which could inhibit fibre cell elongation. However, microtubules undergo dynamic changes during cotton fibre development, and numerous IQD homologues exist in upland cotton. Therefore, more research is needed to elucidate the dynamic regulatory mechanisms of microtubules during cotton fibre development.

Notably, recent studies have revealed that IQD proteins are also involved in calcium signalling pathways and the regulation of PIN protein polarity, both of which are critical for cell elongation and directional growth. IQDs can bind calmodulin in a calcium‐dependent manner, thereby linking microtubule dynamics to calcium signalling (Bürstenbinder, Möller, et al. 2017). Additionally, IQDs have been implicated in the spatial distribution of PIN auxin transporters, affecting auxin gradients and thus influencing cell expansion (Bürstenbinder, Mitra, and Quegwer 2017). These findings suggest that GhBIN2‐mediated phosphorylation of GhIQD14 may have broader regulatory effects beyond microtubule organisation, potentially integrating multiple signalling pathways to coordinate fibre cell elongation. Further research is required to dissect these complex interactions and fully understand the multifaceted roles of IQDs in cotton fibre development.

GhBIN2 's Multidimensional Regulation of Cotton Fibre Elongation

BR signalling primarily promotes cell elongation (Szekeres et al. 1996). BIN2, a key negative regulator in the BR signalling pathway, is known for its significant dwarf phenotype in gain‐of‐function mutants, highlighting its crucial role in BR‐mediated cell elongation (Li and Nam 2002). Research on the mechanisms by which BR regulates cell elongation has mainly focused on typical tissues, such as root tips, hypocotyls and leaves (Takatsuka et al. 2018). This study used cotton fibre, a unique model of extremely elongated single cells, to explore the inhibitory effect of the BR signalling negative regulator GhBIN2 on cell elongation in upland cotton.

As a protein kinase, BIN2 regulates the stability and activity of target proteins through phosphorylation to exert its biological functions (Li and Nam 2002). We investigated the regulatory mechanism of GhBIN2 on cotton fibre elongation in this specialised cell type by 4D‐fastDIA quantitative phosphoproteomics. Fibre elongation is regulated by multiple mechanisms, including cell wall elongation, cell wall synthesis, microtubule assembly, cell elongation and the synergistic effects of various hormones. Phosphoproteomics analysis in this study revealed that multiple proteins involved in these functions, such as ACO, CESA and BZR1 (Li et al. 2021), exhibited significant changes in phosphorylation levels in Ghbin2, indicating that GhBIN2 regulates fibre elongation through multiple pathways (Figures 4 and 5).

Phosphopeptide enrichment and identification analysis of Ghbin2 fibres revealed 20preferentially phosphorylated motifs (Figure S8,S9). These motifs included not only the canonical BIN2 kinase recognition site (SXXXS) but also characteristic phosphorylation motifs of other protein kinases, such as PXS/TP for ERK and RXXSP for CDK (He et al. 2002; Morris 2001; Joubès et al. 2000). These findings suggested that GhBIN2 may act as a central node in the phosphorylation signalling network, regulating the activity and function of multiple downstream kinases. Further phosphoproteomic analysis demonstrated significant alterations in the phosphorylation levels and protein abundance of various kinases and phosphorylation‐related proteins in GhBIN2‐modified contexts. Collectively, these observations strongly indicated that GhBIN2 precisely regulates fibre development by modulating the phosphorylation status and expression levels of other protein kinases, thereby influencing the overall cellular phosphorylation network and establishing a complex phosphorylation cascade during fibre elongation in Gossypium hirsutum . Additionally, several functional proteins involved in epigenetic regulation (such as DNA methylation), posttranscriptional regulation (such as RNA splicing) and protein stability regulation (such as protein ubiquitination) showed altered abundance and phosphorylation levels in Ghbin2 lines. This indicates that GhBIN2 may mediate protein abundance at multiple levels (DNA, RNA and proteins) to comprehensively regulate fibre elongation (Figure 8).

Investigating the Integrated Regulation of Gene Expression (IRGE) Provides Insights Into Plant Development

Genes and proteins, as fundamental carriers of biological functions, have been extensively studied for their direct effects on plant development (Brenner 1988). However, recent advances in plant biology have shown that growth and development are regulated by a sophisticated, multitiered system beyond the basic functions of these molecular units. These regulatory mechanisms include DNA‐level modification (such as DNA methylation, histone modifications and chromatin remodelling), RNA‐level regulation (such as RNA methylation, alternative splicing, capping and polyadenylation) and protein‐level osttranslational modifications (such as phosphorylation, glycosylation, ubiquitination and SUMOylation). Together, these processes are referred to as the integrated regulation of gene expression (IRGE).

Recent studies have increasingly focused on these regulatory mechanisms, elucidating their significant roles in plant growth and development. For instance, DNA methylation is crucial for maintaining genome stability, suppressing transposable elements and regulating gene expression in Arabidopsis (He et al. 2022). Histone modifications, such as acetylation and methylation, regulate transcriptional activity (Shvedunova and Akhtar 2022). In Arabidopsis, Histone Deacetylase 9 (HDA9) represses the expression of FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC) (Zeng et al. 2020). Similarly, in rice ( Oryza sativa ), INDETERMINATE SPIKELET 1 (IDS1) interacts with histone deacetylases and transcriptional repression complexes to modulate the transcriptional activity of downstream target genes (Cheng et al. 2018). At the RNA level, alternative splicing enhances protein diversity, allowing plants to adapt more effectively to environmental changes (Lam et al. 2022). Protein‐level posttranslational modifications can rapidly alter protein function, localization and stability, significantly impacting growth, development and environmental responses in rice and Arabidopsis (Matamoros and Becana 2021). These multilevel regulatory mechanisms interact to form a complex regulatory network, enabling plants to precisely control their growth and development while responding swiftly to environmental changes.

In cotton, IRGE also plays a crucial role. Studies have shown that DNA methylation contributes to both abiotic stress responses and fibre elongation control in cotton (Jin et al. 2013). Notably, m6A RNA methylation in cotton fibres directly regulates the mRNA stability of fibre elongation‐related genes, affecting fibre development (Xing et al. 2023). Additionally, the phosphorylation and ubiquitination of certain proteins, such as GhWRKY16, GhWRKY59, GhSUS2 and GhHUB2 influence cotton growth and development (Wang et al. 2021). However, research on these integrated regulatory networks in cotton is still limited, particularly concerning kinases and proteins directly involved in these multilayered gene expression regulatory mechanisms, which require further investigation.

In summary, this study reveals that the phosphorylation kinase GhBIN2 acts as a negative regulator of cotton fibre development by orchestrating a complex phosphorylation regulatory network. We identified multiple GhBIN2‐mediated phosphorylation substrates involved in IRGE, which together construct a multilevel regulatory network governing fibre elongation. These findings advance the understanding of the intricate regulatory mechanisms underlying cotton fibre growth and provide a foundation for future research into the dynamic interplay of signalling pathways that control plant development.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material and Growth Conditions

The upland cotton genotype ZM49 ( Gossypium hirsutum L.) was used as the recipient for genetic transformation in this study. Cotton plants were grown in a controlled environment chamber, maintained at a constant temperature of 28°C with a 16‐h light/8‐h dark cycle. During the first month post‐germination, seedlings were cultivated in a soil mix composed of 3 parts vermiculite to 1 part commercial potting soil (v/v). After this initial period, the plants were transferred to a mixture of loess and potting soil in a 1:2 ratio (v/v). A regular irrigation schedule was followed, with irrigation every 72 h to ensure adequate soil moisture.

Amplification‐Mutagenesis and Construction of Overexpression Plants

To create specific point mutations of the GhBIN2 gene, site‐directed mutagenesis was performed using PrimerX (http://www.bioinformatics.org/primerx/cgi‐bin/DNA_1.cgi) to introduce point mutations at previously reported loci (Li and Nam 2002). Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from ZM49 cotton using the Tiangen DNA isolation kit, and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesised via reverse transcription. Mutated GhBIN2 sequences were amplified from gDNA and cDNA templates using fusion PCR. The GhIQD14 gene fragment was also amplified from cDNA by standard PCR methods. The WMV067 vector containing the 35S promoter was double‐digested with BamHI and SalI restriction enzymes, and the GhBIN2 and GhIQD14 genes were then inserted into it. The recombinant plasmid was verified by sequencing and subsequently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105. Positive clones were screened using colony PCR. Finally, transgenic cotton plants overexpressing the genes of interest were obtained using the shoot apical meristem cell‐mediated transformation system (SAMT) (Ge et al. 2023).

Virus‐Induced Gene Silencing

A highly specific 300 bp fragment of the GhBIN2/GhIQD14 coding sequence was cloned into the pCLCrV‐A vector. Recombinant and empty vector plasmids were individually introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. When cotton seedlings developed fully expanded cotyledons, they were infiltrated with Agrobacterium suspensions using a syringe until saturation was achieved. Following infiltration, seedlings were incubated in darkness for 24 h and then transferred to a greenhouse maintained at 28°C under a 16‐h light/8‐h dark photoperiod for continued growth.

Construction of Arabidopsis Overexpression Lines

The Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Columbia‐0 (Col‐0) was used as the background genotype for transformation. Plants were grown in a controlled growth chamber set at 22°C with a 16‐h light/8‐h dark cycle. The full‐length and mutated cDNA sequences of GhIQD14 were cloned into the pCAMBIA‐2300 vector, and the resulting constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Col‐0 plants were transformed using the floral dip method. Transgenic seeds were collected and germinated on ½ Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin for selection. Positive transgenic lines were confirmed at both RNA and protein levels before being subjected to phenotypic evaluation.

Evolution and Expression Analysis of the GhSK2s Family Genes

The amino acid sequence of the Arabidopsis SK21 gene was retrieved from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/), whereas sequences of cotton GhSK2s family members were obtained from the CottonFGD database (https://www.elabcaas.cn/). Multiple sequence alignments of the full‐length protein sequences were performed using MEGA7.0 software (Kumar et al. 2008), and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbour‐Joining method with 1000 bootstrap replications and a 95% site coverage cutoff (Qanmber, Liu, et al. 2019; Qanmber, Lu, et al. 2019; Qanmber, Ali, et al. 2019). The alignments were visualised using Jalview software (https://www.jalview.org/). Expression patterns of GhSK21 and GhSK22 in different cotton tissues were analysed by examining published transcriptome sequencing data, and heatmaps were generated using TBtools software to illustrate their expression profiles (Chen, Wu, et al. 2023).

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real Time PCR Analysis

Various cotton samples, including different organs from ZM49 plants grown in the Anyang (Hennan, China) field and 10 DPA fibres from transgenic plants and their WT controls grown in the laboratory, were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into fine powder. Total RNA was extracted using the TianGen plant RNA extraction kit, following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA quality and quantity were assessed before reverse transcription into cDNA. Quantitative PCR was performed using TaKaRa's Premix Ex Taq II on a Roche LightCycler 480 real time PCR system, with the following amplification conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 s.

Phenotypic Analysis of Cotton

Morphometric analysis of the cotton bolls was performed using a precision digital calliper with an accuracy of ±0.03 mm to measure longitudinal and lateral dimensions. Fibre length was quantitatively assessed using the ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/) at 10, 15 and 20 DPA, as well as at the mature fibre stage. Each time point included 15 biological replicates for statistical accuracy. High‐resolution environmental scanning electron microscopy (ESEM) was used to analyse the ultrastructural characteristics of early fibre development in transgenic and control samples at 0 DPA.

Evaluation of Protein Phosphorylation and Abundance Using Western Blotting

Fibre samples from Ghbin2 and WT plants at 10 DPA and 15 DPA, along with in vitro cultured ovules (14 days, with or without Bikinin treatment), were harvested and ground in liquid nitrogen. Total protein was extracted using a plant protein extraction kit (Yeasen Biotechnology) and quantified using the Bradford assay. Proteins were separated via SDS‐PAGE using a 12% polyacrylamide gel, initially run at 80 V for 30 min, followed by 120 V until the bromophenol blue dye reached the bottom. Proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes using a wet transfer system (200 mA, 60 min). Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) nonfat milk in TBST buffer for 1 h at room temperature, followed by three washes with TBST (10 min each). Primary antibodies (Phospho‐(Ser/Thr) Phe Antibody/anti‐GhIQD14) diluted in 2.5% BSA/TBST were incubated with the membranes overnight at 4°C. After three additional TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pierce, Goat anti‐Rabbit IgG (H + L), Peroxidase Conjugated) for 1 h at room temperature. Following a final set of TBST washes, protein visualisation was performed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate for 2 min, and signals were detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system. All antibodies were diluted at a ratio of 1:5000 in TBST buffer (Tris‐buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween‐20, pH 7.4). Protein phosphorylation assays were conducted according to the manufacturer's protocol using the Phos‐Assay Acrylamide Kit (Vazyme).

4D‐FastDIA Quantitative Phosphoproteomics and Proteomics Analysis

Fibre samples from Ghbin2 and WT plants were collected at 10 and 15 DPA, and 4D‐FastDIA quantitative phosphoproteomics and proteomics analyses were conducted by Hangzhou Jingjie Biotechnology Co. Ltd. Total proteins were extracted from the samples and quantified using the BCA assay. After protein reduction and alkylation, digestion was carried out with mass spectrometry‐grade trypsin at 37°C for 12 h. Phosphopeptides were then enriched using affinity chromatography. The samples were analysed using an ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography–high resolution mass spectrometry (UPLC‐MS/MS) system. MaxQuant software (v1.5.2.8) was used for database searching and quantitative analysis, resulting in comprehensive phosphoproteome and proteome datasets for Ghbin2 cotton fibres during the elongation stage. Each sample was analysed in triplicate for biological replicates.

Transcriptome Sequencing and Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cotton fibre samples of ZM49 and Ghbin2 plants using the RNA extraction kit (TianGen). The extracted RNA was used to construct sequencing libraries, which were subjected to quality validation before high‐throughput sequencing on the Illumina platform. Raw sequencing data were processed using fastp for quality filtering, followed by alignment to the reference genome using hisat2. Differentially expressed genes were identified based on a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05 and a log2 (fold change) ≥ 1, and selected for further analysis.

In Vitro Ovule Culture

For the ovule culture experiments, 1 DPA cotton bolls from WT and Ghbin2 lines were collected. The bolls were surface‐sterilised by immersing them in 75% ethanol for 5 min, followed by a brief 5‐s dip in 95% ethanol. They were then rinsed five times with sterile distilled water. Ovules were aseptically excised from the sterilised bolls and transferred to a BT liquid medium for preservation. In the treatment setup, BL (1 μM) was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol, and Bikinin at various concentrations (5, 15, 25, 35 μM) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). These solutions were added to the BT medium for ovule culture. Ovules from Ghbin2 lines were cultured in darkness at 30°C for 8 days with Bikinin treatment, whereas both WT and Ghbin2 ovules were cultured under the same conditions for 10 days with BL treatment.

Yeast Two‐Hybrid Experiment

The coding sequence of GhBIN2 was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector, whereas potential interacting partners of GhBIN2 were cloned into the pGADT7 vector. The pGBKT7‐GhBIN2 and pGADT7‐interacting gene plasmids were co‐transformed into the yeast reporter strain Y2HGold using a co‐transformation method. Positive transformants were initially screened on SD/‐Trp/‐Leu selective medium. Clones were then transferred to SD/‐Ade/‐His/‐Leu/‐Trp quadruple dropout medium, which imposes high selective pressure, to confirm and assess the strength of protein–protein interactions.

Dual‐Luciferase Assay

The coding sequence of GhBIN2 was inserted into the Cluc vector, whereas the coding sequences of GhIQD14 and GhNIPA were inserted into the Nluc vector. The resulting fusion protein constructs (Cluc‐GhBIN2, Nluc‐GhIQD14 and Nluc‐GhNIPA) and their corresponding empty vector controls were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 (pSoup‐p19). Using an Agrobacterium‐mediated transient expression system, the different bacterial suspensions were co‐infiltrated into young leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana. Following a 24‐h dark incubation period and an additional 24 h of light exposure, luciferase activity was detected using a Tanon 5200 multifunctional chemiluminescence imaging system.

In Vitro Pull‐Down Assay

The coding sequence of GhBIN2 was cloned into the pETX‐4T vector, and the coding sequences of GhIQD14 and GhNIPA were cloned into the pET30a vector. Recombinant GST‐GhBIN2, His‐GhNIPA and His‐GhIQD14 proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) and purified via affinity chromatography. Purified GST‐GhBIN2 was incubated separately with His‐GhNIPA and His‐GhIQD14 at 4°C for 1 h. The reaction mixtures were then incubated with pre‐equilibrated Glutathione Sepharose 4 Fast Flow resin (YEASEN) and eluted using a buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5) and 5 mM reduced glutathione. The eluted proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes and probed with anti‐His tag antibodies to detect His‐GhNIPA and His‐GhIQD14. The results were visualised using a Tanon 5200 Multi chemiluminescence imaging system.

In Vitro ADP‐Glo Kinase Assay

In vitro kinase activity assays were performed using the ADP‐Glo Kinase Assay Kit (Promega). A 25 μL reaction mixture was prepared, consisting of 2.5 μL of 10× phosphorylation buffer (200 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5; 500 mM KCl; 100 mM MgCl₂), 1 μL Ultra Pure ATP (10 mM), 5 μL GST‐GhBIN2 (0.3 mg/mL), 5 μL His‐GhNIPA (0.3 mg/mL) or GhIQD14 (0.3 mg/mL) and 11.5 μL nuclease‐free water. The reaction mixture was equilibrated at room temperature before adding 25 μL of ADP‐Glo Reagent. Following a 40‐min incubation, 50 μL of Kinase Detection Reagent was added and the mixture was incubated for an additional 60 min at room temperature. Luminescence signals were detected and analysed using a Tanon 5200 Multi chemiluminescence imaging system.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Differences among groups were assessed by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference post hoc test for multiple comparisons when significant differences were detected. Comparisons between the two groups were performed using independent‐sample t‐tests. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.01 (**) and p < 0.05 (*). Unless otherwise stated, all experiments included three independent biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).
