NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions cause retinal neurodegeneration in neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease mouse model
Authors: Haibo Li, Ying Jiang, Lusi Zhang, Xinhui Wang, Yun Tian, Qiying Sun, Lu Shen, Hong Jiang, Beisha Tang, Zhengmao Hu, Yongcheng Pan, Qiong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuronal intranuclear inclusion disease (NIID) patients frequently exhibit ocular abnormalities, yet the pathogenic mechanisms remain unclear. Using a transgenic mouse model ubiquitously expressing NOTCH2NLC with 98GGC repeats, we revealed that polyglycine aggregates, translated from the expanded GGC repeats, predominantly localize in PAX6- and RBPMS-positive cells, accompanied by retinal neurodegeneration and thinning especially in the inner retinal layers. Functional assessment through visual evoked potentials demonstrated significantly reduced amplitudes and prolonged latencies in NIID mice, indicating compromised visual pathway integrity. Transcriptomic profiling revealed dysregulation of glutathione redox homeostasis and antioxidant pathways in the NIID retina. We further demonstrated that GGC expansions induce mitochondrial abnormalities accompanied by glutathione depletion and accumulation of reactive oxygen species. Crucially, intravitreal administration of the mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant Mito-TEMPO significantly alleviated retinal damage and improved visual function. Our findings establish NOTCH2NLC GGC expansions as direct drivers of retinal pathology through mitochondrial-oxidative damage, while identifying antioxidant therapy as a promising treatment strategy for NIID-associated retinopathy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01542-x.Keywords: Neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease, NOTCH2NLC gene mutation, GGC repeat expansion, Aggregation of polyglycine, Retinal neurodegeneration

Introduction

Neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease (NIID) is a rare autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder caused by the expanded GGC repeats in the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) of the NOTCH2NLC gene [1–3]. The pathological hallmark of NIID is the presence of intranuclear inclusions, which are widely distributed not only in neurons and glial cells of the central nervous system but also in various peripheral tissues, including the skin, visceral organs, and peripheral nerves [4, 5]. Clinically, NIID exhibits high heterogeneity, with manifestations including cognitive impairment, peripheral neuropathy, cerebellar ataxia, parkinsonism, paroxysmal symptoms, autonomic dysfunction, and muscle weakness [1, 6–8].

Although most research on NIID has focused on the involvement of the central nervous system, emerging evidence indicates that the visual system is also affected [6, 7, 9]. Several clinical studies have reported convergent ophthalmic manifestations in NIID patients, including peripapillary atrophy, perifoveal photoreceptor loss, and rod-cone dysfunction, along with abnormalities in electroretinography (ERG) and visual-evoked potential (VEP) [10–15]. These findings collectively suggest lesions of the outer retinal layers in NIID. Notably, Sone et al. provided solid pathological evidence of widespread intranuclear inclusions throughout all retinal layers, from the outermost retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) to the innermost ganglion cell layer (GCL) in genetically confirmed NIID patients, accompanied by structural and functional retinal impairments [13]. In addition, severe gliosis was observed in the retina and optic nerve. These findings strongly implicate NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions in retinal pathology. However, the underlying mechanisms by which these GGC repeat expansions lead to visual system dysfunction remain poorly understood. In particular, there is a lack of in vivo experimental evidence that directly links NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions to retinal degeneration, limiting our ability to investigate the underlying pathogenesis and evaluate potential therapeutic strategies.

In this study, a transgenic mouse model (hereafter referred to as NIID mice) [16], in which NOTCH2NLC with 98 GGC repeats is ubiquitously expressed, was used to investigate the in vivo effects of NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions on retinal structure and function. Through systematic analysis of retinal abnormalities in NIID mice, we identified significant structural and functional impairments in the retina, especially in inner retinal layers, including inner nuclear layer (INL), inner plexiform layer (IPL) , and ganglion cell layer (GCL). Further pathological analyses demonstrated the accumulation of polyG-containing aggregates in retinal cells, leading to cellular dysfunction and oxidative stress-associated mitochondrial damage. Notably, mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant therapy partially rescued the observed defects, highlighting mitochondrial protection against oxidative stress as a potential therapeutic target. Taken together, our findings provide direct in vivo evidence that NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions contribute to retinal pathology in NIID, although our animal model does not fully replicate the structural and functional alterations of the outer layer observed in patients. This study not only broadens the understanding of NIID pathogenesis beyond the central nervous system but also underscores the potential of antioxidant-based interventions for mitigating visual impairment in NIID patients.

Results

Ocular abnormalities in the NIID mouse model

Since the NIID mouse model exhibits rapid disease progression, with behavioral deficits emerging by P30, and mortality occurring by P60 [16], we first assessed retinal abnormalities at P50 using fundus imaging and OCT B-scans. Fundus imaging revealed numerous yellow-white spots scattered throughout the retina of all tested mice (Fig. 1A), while OCT analysis demonstrated significant retinal thinning (Fig. 1B). Layer-specific quantification showed significantly reduced thickness in the INL, outer plexiform layer (OPL), and outer nuclear layer (ONL) (Fig. 1C), suggesting structural alterations of the retinas in NIID mice.

Histological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining further confirmed generalized retinal thinning, most severe within 100–1000 µm from the optic nerve (Fig. 1D, E). The IPL and INL showed the most pronounced reduction, while the ONL and inner segment/outer segment (IS/OS) exhibited mild thinning (Fig. 1F). Notably, the preferential thinning of inner retinal layers in mice contrasts with the predominant outer-layer pathology seen in human patients, highlighting a potential species-specific divergence in disease manifestation. Together, these findings demonstrate that NIID mice develop retinal structural defects, implicating GGC repeat expansions of NOTCH2NLC in retinal pathology.

Expression of NOTCH2NLC-(GGC)98 forms aggregates in the inner retinal layers and causes neurodegeneration

Previous studies have demonstrated that NOTCH2NLC-(GGC)98 can be translated into polyglycine (polyG) and other repeat protein aggregates, which exert cytotoxic effects in various tissues and organs [16–18]. To determine polyG expression patterns across retinal layers, we performed immunostaining on frozen retinal sections, co-labeling polyG aggregates with cell type-specific markers. VSX2, a key transcription factor in early retinal development, is primarily localized to bipolar cells (within the INL) in the mature retina [19, 20]. PAX6, initially expressed in retinal progenitors, predominantly localized to amacrine cells (within the INL) and retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) in the mature retina [21, 22]. RBPMS serves as a specific RGC marker [23]. For photoreceptor characterization, we used Rhodopsin, the rod-specific visual pigment restricted to outer segments (OS), and Arrestin C, expressed throughout rod and cone photoreceptors from their ONL somata to IS/OS processes. Additionally, RPE65 was employed to label retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells.

Immunostaining demonstrated that NOTCH2NLC-polyG aggregates were predominantly localized to VSX2-, PAX6- and RBPMS-positive cells, but were absent in Rhodopsin-, Arrestin C- and RPE65-positive cells (Fig. 2A–F and Fig. S1A). These findings indicate that polyG aggregates are primarily distributed in the inner retinal layers, particularly the INL and GCL. Quantitative analysis revealed polyG aggregates in approximately 5.09% of VSX2-positive cells, 24.75% of PAX6-positive cells, and 26.77% of RBPMS-positive cells (Fig. 2G). Notably, NIID retinas exhibited significant reductions in PAX6- and RBPMS-positive cell populations (Fig. 2H). Leveraging the retina’s well-defined laminar organization and distinct cellular morphologies, we performed ultrastructural analysis using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and revealed membrane-less filamentous inclusions within nuclei of cells in the INL and GCL (but not ONL) (Fig. 2I and Fig. S2A-B), which closely resembled those observed in patient-derived brain and retinal tissues, and NIID mouse brain [2, 13, 16, 24]. Intriguingly, those inclusions in the INL consistently contained electron-dense cores with compact morphology, whereas GCL inclusions displayed less condensed architectures (Fig. 2I and Fig. S2C). These findings support the preferential distribution of polyG aggregates in the inner retinal layers.

To assess the broader impact of NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions on RGCs and their axonal projections, we performed retinal whole-mount staining (Fig. 3A). The results demonstrated polyG aggregates in RGCs, accompanied by a significant reduction in RGC numbers (Fig. 3A, B). Electron microscopy revealed that the myelin sheaths surrounding RGC axons exhibited loose wrapping, as well as stratification and collapse (Fig. 3C, D). We also observed a significant increase in GFAP fluorescence intensity in the RGC layer, along with a slight increase in IBA1 fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3E, F and Fig. S1B-C), indicating gliosis and further supporting neurodegeneration in RGCs. These findings are consistent with pathological changes observed in postmortem retinal analyses of NIID patients [13].

Together, these findings indicate that NOTCH2NLC-polyG aggregates are primarily localized within the INL and GCL, resulting in selective neuronal loss and reactive gliosis. These pathological alterations suggest that NOTCH2NLC-(GGC)98 expression contributes to retinal degeneration in NIID mice.

NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions induce abnormal visual electrophysiology in the retina of NIID mice

To further evaluate the functional impact of NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions on the retina, we assessed the visual electrophysiology of P50 NIID mice using ERG and VEP. First, we examined retinal function using scotopic and photopic ERG. The recordings revealed no significant differences in the amplitudes of a-waves and b-waves, nor in b-wave latency, between NIID and control mice (Fig. 4A–F). These results suggest that photoreceptor and bipolar cell function may remain largely unaffected in NIID mice at this stage.

To further investigate higher-order visual processing, we performed flash VEP recordings in response to alternating gratings. Representative VEP waveforms from control and NIID mice were shown in Fig. 4G. The P1 wave latencies were significantly prolonged in NIID mice (Fig. 4H), indicating delayed signal transmission along the visual pathway. In terms of amplitude, the P1 wave showed a significant reduction (Fig. 4H), demonstrating impairment in signal strength. Given that VEP responses reflect the integrity of the entire visual pathway, we next examined whether post-retinal visual structures are also affected in NIID mice. Accordingly, polyG aggregates were detected in major post-retinal visual structures of NIID mice, including the dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus (dLGN) and visual cortex (V1) (Fig. S1D). Taken together, these findings indicate that while outer retinal function remains relatively intact, RGC electrophysiology and visual pathway integrity are notably compromised in NIID mice. This suggests that NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions predominantly affect visual processing, consistent with retinal inner layers degeneration observed in histological analyses.

Dysregulated glutathione redox and antioxidant transcriptomes in the retina of NIID mice

To investigate the molecular clues of NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions in the retina degeneration, we performed RNA sequencing on retinas from P30 NIID and littermate control mice. A total of 223 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 177 downregulated and 46 upregulated genes in NIID mice compared to controls (Fig. 5A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant alterations in biological processes associated with axon guidance, glutamatergic synapse, and GABAergic synapse (Fig. S3), consistent with the observed loss of cells in the INL and GCL (Figs. 2 and 3). Given that RGCs predominantly utilize glutamate as a neurotransmitter and amacrine cells in the INL primarily employ GABA as an inhibitory neurotransmitter, these transcriptomic changes further support the neurodegenerative phenotype observed in NIID retina. In addition, pathways involved in environmental information processing, including the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions, cAMP signaling, and calcium signaling, were significantly enriched (Fig. S3), similar to what we previously observed in brain tissues from NIID mice [16].

Intriguingly, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of DEGs highlighted dysregulation of pathways associated with stimulus response, detoxification, and antioxidant defense (Fig. 5B). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed abnormal oxidative stress-related pathways in NIID mice (Fig. 5C). In addition, approximately 20%-30% of the mice exhibited lens opacity and corneal damage (Fig. S4A), similar to the report in NIID patients [13, 15, 25]. Beyond suggesting eye and lens dysfunction, RNA-seq analysis of whole eyeballs from NIID mice also revealed significant alterations in glutathione (GSH, a critical antioxidant that protects cells from oxidative stress) metabolism and oxidative stress-related pathways (Fig. S4B-F). Further quantitative-PCR analysis on GSH and oxidative stress related genes revealed significant downregulation of Chac1 (GSH degradation) and Gsta3 (detoxification) in NIID mouse retinas, while Slc7a11 (GSH synthesis) expression remained unchanged (Fig. 5D). This alteration was also observed in rat retinal precursor cells (R28) expressing NOTCH2NLC-(GGC)98 (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, direct measurement of glutathione content in retinas of NIID mice confirmed a significant reduction in GSH levels (Fig. 5F). These findings suggest dysregulation of glutathione redox and antioxidant pathways in NIID mice.

NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions lead to mitochondrial abnormalities and ROS accumulation in the retina

GSH is a crucial non-enzymatic antioxidant that plays a key role in neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS). Mitochondria, as the cell’s primary energy producers, are also a major source of ROS, which are generated during ATP production via the electron transport chain. Given the observed GSH reduction and dysregulated oxidative stress-related pathway in NIID mice, we hypothesized that NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions induced mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS overproduction in the retina, which may contribute to retinal degeneration.

To test this, we first examined mitochondrial ultrastructure by electron microscopy. Compared to control mice, NIID mice exhibited severe mitochondrial abnormalities, including loss of mitochondrial cristae and swelling in cells in the INL and GCL (Fig. 6A, B), but not in the IS/OS and ONL (Fig. S2D). We next isolated P50 retinas and detected significantly elevated ROS levels in NIID mice (Fig. 6C). Then, we performed MitoSOX staining in R28 cells to assess mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) production. Overexpression of NOTCH2NLC-(GGC)98 resulted in a significant increase in mtROS levels, further supporting that NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions induce oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in retinal cells (Fig. 6D, E). It is well-established that ROS accumulation can trigger apoptotic cell death. In line with this, TUNEL staining revealed a significant increase in positive signals in the inner retinal layers of NIID mice (Fig. 6F), consistent with our previous observation of cell loss in these retinal layers. These findings collectively suggest that NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions induced mitochondrial abnormalities and glutathione depletion, contributing to oxidative stress and retinal neurodegeneration in NIID mice.

Mitochondria-targeted antioxidant therapy alleviates retinal degeneration in NIID mice

Currently, there is no effective treatment for NIID. The eye provides a valuable system for exploring treatment. Building upon our findings that mitochondrial dysfunction and mtROS overproduction in NIID mice, we next sought to determine whether enhancing mitochondrial antioxidant capacity could mitigate retinal abnormalities. Mito-TEMPO, a mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant, effectively scavenges mitochondrial superoxide and other ROS, thereby reducing pathologically high intraocular pressure-induced oxidative stress and RGCs death [26].

To evaluate its therapeutic potential, we performed intravitreal injections of 200 μM Mito-TEMPO (1 μl) into the right eye and 0.1% DMSO as a control into the left eye of NIID mice at P15 and P30. At P50, we assessed the treatment effects through electrophysiological and pathological analyses. Our results revealed that Mito-TEMPO treatment did not significantly alter the number of polyG aggregates. However, compared to the DMSO-injected retinas, the number of PAX6- and RBPMS-positive cells was significantly increased in the Mito-TEMPO-treated retinas (Fig. 7A–C). Retinal whole-mount staining further supports the protective effect of Mito-TEMPO on RBPMS-positive cells (Fig. 7D, E). Functionally, VEP recordings demonstrated that visual pathway deficits in NIID mice were significantly improved following Mito-TEMPO administration compared to the DMSO-treated controls (Fig. 7F, G). These findings indicate that enhancing mitochondrial antioxidant defenses can effectively alleviate retinal pathological and functional impairments in NIID mice, highlighting a potential therapeutic strategy for NIID-associated ocular dysfunction.

Ocular abnormalities in NIID patients

The observation of predominant inner retinal damage in our NIID mouse model led us to re-examine the retinal function in NIID patients. Retinal abnormalities were identified by ophthalmologic examination on three genetically and biopsy-confirmed NIID patients (Fig. 8A, B and Fig. S5) from our hospital cohort; the full ophthalmologic results are summarized in Tables S3 and S4. In NIID case 1, fundus photography demonstrated degeneration and atrophy of the RPE in the posterior pole (Fig. 8C). OCT showed segmental atrophy of the ellipsoid zone (EZ) (Fig. 8D). ERG revealed decreased amplitudes and prolonged latencies of both a-waves and b-waves under both light-adapted and dark-adapted conditions, indicating severe photoreceptor dysfunction (Fig. 8E). Pattern-reversal visual evoked potentials (VEP) results showed delayed latency and decreased amplitude were noted (Fig. 8F).

Consistent with previous reports [10–15], NIID patients in our study mainly exhibited outer retinal damage, including structural disorganization and segmental atrophy of the ellipsoid zone, as well as rod-cone dysfunction. However, we also identified functional abnormalities suggestive of inner retinal and visual pathway involvement, as indicated by abnormal VEP (Tab. S3 and S4), which align with earlier studies [6, 27]. In summary, these findings confirm the presence of typical outer retinal impairment in NIID patients, while also implicating involvement of the inner retina and visual pathway.

Discussion

In this study, using a transgenic mouse model that ubiquitously expresses NOTCH2NLC-(GGC)98, we provide both pathological and functional evidence linking NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions to retinal abnormalities. Our findings suggest that expression of the expanded GGC repeats in the retina induces the formation of polyG inclusions, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress, ultimately leading to neurodegeneration primarily within the inner retina.

Our findings support a correlation between polyG aggregate formation and retinal neuronal damage. While OCT B-scans and HE staining revealed significant thinning of the inner retinal layers in NIID mice, immunostaining and electron microscopy demonstrated that polyG aggregates were predominantly localized to the INL and GCL, co-localizing with PAX6- and RBPMS-positive cells. These aggregates were accompanied by mitochondrial structural abnormalities (including swelling and cristae loss), a reduction in the corresponding neuronal populations, and an increase in TUNEL signal. Consistent with this distribution, VEP exhibited prolonged latencies and reduced amplitudes, reflecting impaired visual pathway integrity in the inner retina. In addition, mild thinning of the ONL was also observed in NIID mice. This subtle thinning may result from damage to inner retinal cells, which could lead to secondary effects on the outer retina. Given the relatively large number of photoreceptors in the ONL, it is possible that a small degree of photoreceptor loss was insufficient to cause measurable functional impairment. This is supported by the absence of polyG aggregates and mitochondrial abnormalities in the outer retina, as well as preserved photoreceptor function revealed by ERG recordings.

Interestingly, ultrastructural analysis revealed structural abnormalities in the myelin sheaths surrounding RGC axons in NIID mice, suggesting potential impairment in axonal conduction. Consistent with this, VEP recordings revealed prolonged latencies and reduced amplitudes. Although the human data were not derived from the visual system, postmortem analyses of NIID patient brain tissue have also reported demyelination [5], and electromyography studies in patients indicate possible demyelinating changes in motor neurons [28, 29]. Together, these findings suggest that structural alterations in RGC axonal myelin may contribute to the disrupted visual pathway integrity observed in NIID mice. In addition, we also observed morphological heterogeneity among polyG inclusions: aggregates in the INL contained compact, electron-dense cores, whereas those in the GCL were more loosely organized. While structurally diverse inclusions have been observed in both patients and animal models [2, 13, 16, 24], the presence of distinct inclusion morphologies across different neuronal subtypes, as seen in our study, is particularly intriguing. The underlying mechanisms and functional consequences of this heterogeneity remain to be determined.

Our study supports a critical role for oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in NIID-associated retinal degeneration, as evidenced by reduced glutathione levels, elevated mitochondrial ROS production, and structural mitochondrial abnormalities. Importantly, mitochondrial defects in NIID are not confined to the retina but have also been reported in other affected tissues, including the brain, heart and muscle. Across multiple model systems, expression of expanded NOTCH2NLC GGC repeats is consistently associated with impaired mitochondrial respiration, disrupted energy metabolism, and abnormal mitochondrial morphology [18, 30, 31], indicating that mitochondrial dysfunction represents a shared pathological feature across organs in NIID.

Notably, mitochondrial dysfunction is also a shared pathogenic mechanism in other repeat-associated disorders. In fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome (FXTAS), mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress are critical drivers of disease progression, characterized by impaired oxidative phosphorylation, accumulation of lipid peroxidation products, and elevated ROS levels in patient-derived fibroblasts and blood. Similarly, in myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1), patients exhibit enhanced oxidative stress together with mitochondrial defects, such as reduced ATP synthesis, decreased mitochondrial DNA copy number, and abnormal mitochondrial morphology in muscle and brain tissues [32–34]. These parallels further support the concept that mitochondrial impairment is a common downstream consequence of repeat expansion toxicity. Consistent with this concept, we show that treatment with the mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant Mito-TEMPO partially rescues retinal cellular integrity and visual pathway function in NIID mice. Together with prior evidence that idebenone restores mitochondrial function and alleviates neurodegeneration in NIID Drosophila models [30], these findings highlight mitochondrial oxidative stress as a convergent and therapeutically tractable target in NIID.

Although the predominant clinical feature of NIID is outer retinal degeneration, inner retinal involvement has also been documented in patients. Postmortem studies have consistently identified intranuclear inclusions in inner retinal layers such as the INL and GCL, accompanied by histopathological features such as RGC loss and axonal degeneration [13, 27]. Furthermore, abnormal VEP responses have been reported in NIID patients [6, 27]. These pathological and electrophysiological features are consistent with the phenotype observed in our mouse model. Nevertheless, clear differences exist between the mouse model and human NIID, particularly with respect to the predominant retinal layers affected and the distribution of intranuclear inclusions. One possible explanation is that NOTCH2NLC-(GGC)98 expression in our model is driven by the EIIa-Cre, which may not fully recapitulate the endogenous NOTCH2NLC promoter activity in humans, particularly in outer retinal cell types. In addition, given the relatively short lifespan of the mouse model (approximately 2 months), it is possible that the expression of NOTCH2NLC-(GGC)98 in outer retinal cells may not be sufficient to form polyG aggregates at this stage.

A limitation of the present study is the relatively small number of NIID patients included in the ophthalmic analyses, which reflects the rarity of the disease. Although the observed retinal structural and functional features are consistent with previous reports, the limited sample size precludes definitive conclusions regarding the full spectrum of human retinal involvement in NIID. Therefore, the patient-related findings should be interpreted as preliminary and primarily intended to provide translational context for the animal data, rather than to support broad generalization. Future studies with larger patient cohorts will be required to validate and extend these observations. Beyond patient-related constraints, a further limitation concerns the pathogenic mechanisms associated with expanded GGC repeats. This study focused on polyG protein toxicity, a major product of the repeats. However, accumulating evidence indicates that NOTCH2NLC transcripts containing these expanded repeats may also exert RNA-mediated toxicity [35, 36]. Whether such RNA-dependent mechanisms contribute to retinal pathology in NIID remains an open question for further investigation.

In summary, our study provides compelling in vivo evidence that NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansions contribute to retinal degeneration in NIID via mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. These findings underscore the importance of ocular screening in NIID and highlight mitochondrial-targeted antioxidants as a promising therapeutic strategy.

Materials and methods

Study participants and clinical assessments

Three participants were recruited from Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. Each case was independently evaluated by at least two neurologists. The diagnosis of NIID was established based on clinical features, genetic analyses and skin biopsy as described in a previous report [1]. The number of GGC repeats within NOTCH2NLC was confirmed by Repeat-primed PCR (RP-PCR) and GC-rich PCR (GC-PCR) assays. The intranuclear inclusions were verified by HE staining, or immunohistochemical staining using antibodies against p62 or ubiquitin on skin biopsy specimens. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital (Approval No. 202202050549), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Ophthalmologic examinations on NIID patients

All ophthalmologic examinations in this study were conducted at the Xiangya Eye Center, Central South University (Changsha, China). All procedures were performed by experienced technicians and reviewed by senior ophthalmologists to ensure data accuracy and consistency. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) assessments were performed using a device manufactured by Carl Zeiss Meditec (Cirrus HD-OCT 4000, Germany), following the standard scanning protocols provided by the manufacturer. Electroretinography (ERG) and visual evoked potential (VEP) were recorded using a METROVISION Vision Monitor system in compliance with the International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision (ISCEV) standards. Dark-adapted (scotopic) ERG responses were elicited at stimulus intensities of 0.01, 3.0, and 10.0 cd s/m2, and light-adapted (photopic) responses were obtained at 3.0 cd s/m2. VEPs were evoked using a full-field pattern-reversal checkerboard stimulus with a reversal rate of 2 Hz and equal black-and-white checks. Two consecutive trials were performed for each eye, with approximately 200 artifact-free sweeps averaged per trial. Central fixation was maintained on a gray target throughout the recording. Scalp electrodes were arranged according to the international 10–20 system, with the active electrode placed over the occipital cortex (Oz). The primary VEP components analyzed included N75, P100, and N135. For each eye, the N75-P100 amplitude and P100 latency were measured. Amplitude was calculated as the voltage difference between the P100 peak and the preceding N75 trough; latency was defined as the time from stimulus onset to the peak of the P100 component.

Animals

The NIID mice were generated previously [16]. Briefly, the loxP-stop-loxP-NOTCH2NLC-(GGC)98 conditional transgenic mice were crossed with EIIa-Cre mice to express NOTCH2NLC-(GGC)98 universally. Heterozygous NIID mice and their littermate controls were used in this study. All mice were bred and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in the animal facility of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at Xiangya Hospital (Approval No. 2022030278), Central South University. For intravitreal drug delivery, following anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine/xylazine (120 mg/kg + 10 mg/kg), 1 μl of Mito-TEMPO (200 μM in 0.1% DMSO; Selleckchem, China) was slowly injected into the vitreous chamber of the right eyes of NIID mice using a 33-gauge microsyringe at P15 and P30. Contralateral left eyes received an equal volume of 0.1% DMSO vehicle as internal controls.

Antibodies

Primary antibodies used in this study include: RBPMS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-31231, 1/300), VSX2 (Proteintech, 25825-1-AP, 1/300), PAX6 (Abcam, ab195045, 1/300), Rhodopsin (Proteintech, 30438-1-AP, 1/200), Arrestin C (Proteintech, 85067-1-AP, 1/200), Flag (Sigma, F1804, 1/300), Flag (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-31231, 1/300), Tuj1 (Sigma, MAB5564, 1/300), GFAP (Cell Signaling Technology, 3670S, 1/300), IBA1 (Wako, 019-19741, 1/300), GFP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-15256, 1/300). Secondary antibodies were donkey anti-rabbit, donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 or 594 from Jackson ImmunoResearch.

Immunostaining of mouse retinas

Mice were anesthetized with ketamine (120 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally and sacrificed at P50, and the eyeballs were enucleated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 2 h at room temperature (RT). Then the eyeballs were dehydrated with 10%, 20% and 30% sucrose at RT. Subsequently, the dehydrated eyeballs were embedded in O.C.T. mounting medium, and 20 μm sections were cut with a cryostat microtome (CM1860; Leica, Nussloch, Germany).

For immunostaining of frozen sections, the sections were washed with PBS and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The nuclei were stained with DAPI for 2 min. Slides were examined using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). PAX6 and RBPMS-positive cells were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

For immunostaining of flat-mounted retinas, the eyes of mice were enucleated and fixed in 4% PFA for 1 h at RT. The retina was peeled off, prepared as flattened whole mounts, and permeabilized in 0.5% Triton-X100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 15 min at RT. The flat-mounted retina was then washed with PBS and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The nuclei were stained with DAPI for 2 min, and the flat-mounted retina was examined using a fluorescence microscope. The number of RGC axons from the optic nerve head and RBPMS and Tuj1-positive cells in each image was quantified using ImageJ software. RBPMS-positive and Tuj1-positive cells were acquired from each frame and their numbers were manually quantified. Data are given as densities (number of cells/mm2) as described previously.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Briefly, NIID mice and the littermate control mice were anesthetized at P50, their eyes were quickly enucleated, and the retina of each group was peeled away. After fixation in 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight at 4 °C, retinal samples were processed for TEM following standard ultrastructural protocols. Ultrathin sections were examined using an HT7700 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan), with particular attention to pathological features including inclusion bodies, myelin sheath integrity, and mitochondrial morphology.

ERG examination on mice

Mice at 50 days old were anesthetized with ketamine (120 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally (n = 8 for each group). Gold wire rings placed onto the corneas of both eyes served as working electrodes. Standard ERGs were recorded simultaneously on both eyes, with scotopic and photopic flash ERGs from 0.0003 to 100 cd s/m2 on a commercial ERG system (RETI-scan 21, Roland Consult Systems, Bradenburg, Germany). The scotopic and photopic ERG test 3.0 and the maximal scotopic ERG test 10.0 were recorded. Amplitudes of a-waves were measured from the baseline to the bottom of the a-wave; b-wave amplitudes were measured from the bottom of the a-wave to the peak of the b-wave.

VEP examination on mice

Flash visual-evoked potential (VEP) was detected at P50 using a VEP Testing Device (RETI-scan 21, Roland Consult Systems, Bradenburg, Germany) (n = 8 for each group). Briefly, mice were anesthetized, and placed on a thermoregulated heating pad and secured using a tooth-holder headset. Three silver plate electrodes were separately inserted under the skin of the occipital bone (anode), anterior bregma (cathode), and ear (ground electrode). The contralateral eye was completely covered and unilateral scotopic flash VEPs were recorded. The mice were first adapted to darkness for 5 min, then the recordings were obtained under very dim room light with a stimulus light. Three distinct records were detected for each eye. Peak latency and amplitude of P1 and P2 of the VEP waveform were measured for each eye. Mean P1 and P2 latency and amplitude were compared by unpaired Student’s t-test.

GSH measurement

Total glutathione (GSH + GSSG) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) were quantified using the GSH/GSSG Assay Kit (S0053, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Frozen eyeballs were weighed, pulverized under liquid nitrogen with a pre-chilled mortar and pestle, and the tissue powder was homogenized in 3 volumes (w/v) of ice-cold Protein Removal Reagent M. To minimize degradation and maintain redox stability, all procedures were performed in a 4 °C cold room, and protease inhibitors were included throughout. After 10 min on ice, lysates were centrifuged at 10,000×g, 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected for immediate measurement.

The glutathione content in each group was quantified using the kit’s standard curve.

ROS detection in mouse retinas

Retinas were aseptically dissected from euthanized NIID mice and the littermate control mice and immediately immersed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS). Tissue fragments (1–2 mm3) were generated using micro-dissection scissors under a stereomicroscope and enzymatically digested with 0.125% trypsin (w/v) in a 37 °C shaking water bath at 80 rpm for 30 min. Trypsinization was terminated by adding DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cell homogenate was sequentially filtered through 70 μm nylon meshes to remove undigested debris. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation (300 g, 5 min, 4 °C) and washed twice with calcium/magnesium-free PBS. All sample handling was performed at 4 °C whenever possible, and procedures were conducted promptly to prevent artificial alterations in ROS levels.

2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (S0033S, Beyotime, China) was diluted to 10 μM in serum-free HBSS. Cell pellets were resuspended in 10 μM DCFH-DA dissolved in serum-free HBSS and incubated in a humidified 37 °C incubator with 5% CO₂ for 20 min. To ensure uniform probe loading, the suspension was gently vortexed for 10 s at 5-min intervals. Post-staining, cells underwent three cycles of differential centrifugation (300 g, 5 min, 4 °C) with ice-cold HBSS to remove extracellular probes. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using a Synergy H1 hybrid multi-mode microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) with excitation/emission set to 488 ± 9 nm/525 ± 20 nm. Total fluorescence was calculated and analyzed. Data normalization was performed against the total protein concentration determined by the BCA assay. In control mice, this value was set to 1, and each sample’s relative fluorescence was expressed as a percentage of the control.

Cell culture and Mito-SOX red staining

Immortalized rat retinal precursor cell line R28 cells, obtained from the Department of Anatomy and Neurobiology, Central South University, were cultured in low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C with an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. NOTCH2NLC with 17GGC or 98GGC plasmids were constructed in a previous study [16]. Transfection of NOTCH2NLC with 17GGC or 98GGC plasmids into R28 retinal precursor cells was performed using Lipofectamine® 2000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All relevant reagents were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

For Mito-SOX Red staining, R28 retinal precursor cells were transfected with NOTCH2NLC with 17GGC or 98GGC plasmids for 48 h, then the cells were first incubated with 2 μM Mito-SOX Red for 30 min, and the nuclei were stained with DAPI for 2 min. After being washed with PBS, the fluorescence was detected using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The mean fluorescence intensity of NOTCH2NLC with 17GGC or 98GGC transfected cells was calculated as the average number of pixels at each group using ImageJ.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from mice retinas using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA (1 μg) using a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and oligo dT primers. Quantitative real-time PCR was analyzed with the comparative cycle threshold method. Primers used for quantitative real-time PCR were listed as follows: for Chac1, Chac1-F: GGC TTC GTT CGT GGC TAT AG and Chac1-R: GCT CCC CTC GAA CTT GGT AT; for Gsta3, Gsta3-F: TGC CCA TGG TAG AGA TCG AC and Gsta3-R: GAT CCG CCA CTC CTT CTG T; for Slc7a11, Slc7a11-F: TGC CTG TGG AGT ACT GTC AC and Slc7a11-R: AAG CCA GCA AAG GAC CAA AG; for GAPDH, GAPDH-F: CAT GGC CTC CAA GGA GTA AGA AAC and GAPDH-R: TGT GAG GGA GAT GCT CAG TG.

RNA-seq and data analysis

A total amount of 1 μg of total RNA per sample was processed for library preparation using NEBNext UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The library fragments were purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, USA). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. The raw reads were further processed with a bioinformatic pipelinetool, BMKCloud (www.biocloud.net) online platform. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using Hisat2. Gene expression levels were estimated by fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. Genes with adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)|≥ 0.6 were assigned as significantly differentially expressed. Hierarchical cluster and GO, KEGG pathway, and ReactomePathways enrichment analysis of DEGs were performed using R (v 3.2.0). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.3.1). Two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons test was used in analyzing the retinal thickness at different distances from the optic nerve head. Two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to compare two groups. Data are presented as the mean percentage of mean ± SEM. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
