Giant T-Wave Inversions and Apical Thickening in a Patient with Central Nervous System Lymphoma
Authors: Omar Baqal, Luke Dreher, Reza Arsanjani, Dan Sorajja, Steven Lester
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Cerebral T-waves, CNS lymphoma, Myocardial thickening, Strain imaging, Takotsubo syndrome

Introduction

Giant T-wave inversions, also known as “cerebral T waves,” are a rare electrocardiogram (ECG) finding classically associated with acute intracranial pathology, particularly subarachnoid hemorrhage and hemorrhagic stroke. They are thought to reflect neurocardiogenic autonomic dysregulation and may occur in association with intracranial mass effect, although intracranial pressure is not always directly measured in clinical practice. Cerebral T waves are uncommon in the setting of a subacutely growing intracerebral neoplastic lesion, and the diagnostic challenge is amplified when they occur alongside rapidly evolving echocardiographic abnormalities. This case highlights the role of echocardiography, strain imaging, ultrasound-enhancing agents (UEAs), and planned multimodality imaging in evaluating new apical-predominant myocardial thickening that mimicked apical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) in the setting of progressive central nervous system (CNS) lymphoma.

Case Presentation

We present the case of a 46-year-old man with hypertension and relapsed primary CNS lymphoma receiving ibrutinib, previously treated with lenalidomide, rituximab, and autologous stem cell transplant. Ibrutinib had been held before surgery. The patient presented for an elective right temporal-occipital craniotomy for image-guided biopsy of a growing right posterior temporal-occipital mass. Compared with magnetic resonance imaging performed 2 weeks earlier, perioperative brain magnetic resonance imaging showed interval enlargement of the enhancing intra-axial mass within the right posterior temporal-occipital junction, now measuring 37 × 50 mm compared with 31 × 40 mm, with increased vasogenic edema involving the right temporal and occipital white matter and an unchanged slight right-to-left midline shift. A second enhancing intra-axial mass in the inferior left frontal lobe also increased, measuring 20 × 16 mm compared with 16 × 13 mm. Apart from worsening evening headaches and word-finding difficulty over the preceding few weeks, the patient was neurologically intact with no reported seizures. Lumbar puncture was deferred, and intracranial pressure was not directly measured.

Baseline ECG obtained 2 months earlier showed sinus bradycardia with a heart rate of 59 bpm (Figure 1A). Baseline transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) performed 2 months earlier showed normal left ventricular (LV) chamber size, biplane left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 60%, global longitudinal strain (GLS) of −17.9%, normal LV filling pressure, normal right ventricular size and systolic function, and no hemodynamically significant valvular disease (Figure 2, Video 1). In the preoperative suite, ECG demonstrated new, diffuse, deep T-wave inversions compared with the baseline ECG (Figure 1B). The patient denied chest pain, shortness of breath, orthopnea, swelling, or weight gain. There was no known history of atrial fibrillation, cardiovascular disease, or heart failure. A recent computed tomography of the chest had no evidence of calcified coronary plaque.

Twelve-lead ECG obtained 2 months before presentation (A) showing sinus bradycardia without giant T-wave inversions and immediately before surgery (B) demonstrating new, diffuse, deep T-wave inversions, most prominent in the precordial leads.

Two-dimensional TTE, apical 4-chamber diastolic view (A) and LV longitudinal strain bullseye (B) obtained 2 months before the index presentation, demonstrates normal LV chamber size, normal myocardial wall thickness, and preserved GLS of −17.9%.

A TTE was acquired the same day as the abnormal ECG, demonstrating normal LV chamber size, biplane LVEF of 53% with UEA, and new apical-predominant myocardial thickening, with maximal apical lateral wall thickness of 13 mm (Videos 2-3). A small apical pouch was identified with UEA (Video 4); formal interpretation did not identify regional wall motion abnormalities. GLS was markedly abnormal at −9.5%, compared with −17.9% on the previous study, with abnormal basal, mid, and apical segmental strain averages. Right ventricular size, systolic function, and LV filling pressure were normal; there was no hemodynamically significant valvular disease and no pericardial effusion (Figure 3).

Two-dimensional TTE, apical 4-chamber diastolic view (A) obtained on index presentation, demonstrates interval apical-predominant myocardial thickening; a GLS bullseye (B) demonstrates markedly abnormal GLS of −9.5% with basal, mid, and apical segmental impairment; contrast-enhanced diastolic views in conventional apical 4-chamber (C) and after additional contrast and slight angulation (D), demonstrates improved endocardial border definition, apical-predominant myocardial thickening, and a small apical pouch.

An ischemic evaluation was considered to further investigate the new ECG and TTE abnormalities. However, possible coronary intervention and initiation of antiplatelet therapy would have delayed brain biopsy and any necessary surgical management of the brain mass. Considering the fast-growing CNS neoplasm, the requirement of tissue characterization to determine treatment strategy, and the absence of cardiovascular symptoms or hemodynamic instability, a decision was made to proceed with biopsy after multidisciplinary discussion and shared decision-making.

The patient underwent an uncomplicated craniotomy and brain mass biopsy with an unremarkable postoperative recovery. Biopsy results were consistent with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma with a nongerminal center B-cell phenotype. One month later, the patient was seen for follow-up in the cardiology clinic with no new concerns and continued to stay physically active with swimming, without cardiovascular symptoms. An ECG demonstrated partial improvement, but incomplete resolution, of the T-wave inversions. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide, and high-sensitivity troponin were ordered for further evaluation, but the patient deferred additional cardiovascular testing because of overall oncologic prognosis and goals of care. No follow-up TTE was performed. Upon follow-up with the oncology team and review of biopsy results, the patient was planned for rechallenge with high-dose methotrexate therapy, followed by pomalidomide.

Discussion

T-wave abnormalities in the setting of CNS neoplasms have been previously reported.1 The mechanism behind cerebral T-waves is incompletely understood but is thought to involve hypothalamic stimulation, autonomic dysregulation, and excessive sympathetic cardiac stimulation after neurologic injury. This manifests as deep, symmetric T-wave inversions, often most apparently in the precordial leads.2 In our patient, the new ECG abnormalities occurred in the setting of an enlarging CNS lymphoma with increasing vasogenic edema and a slight midline shift. A lumbar puncture was deferred, and intracranial pressure was not directly measured; therefore, these findings should be interpreted as occurring in the setting of progressive intracranial tumor burden rather than proven intracranial hypertension. The partial improvement in T-wave inversions at follow-up supports a transient component, although incomplete ECG resolution and the absence of follow-up myocardial imaging prevent definitive causal attribution.

The central imaging challenge in this case was the rapid development of apical-predominant myocardial thickening with abnormal strain. When viewed in isolation, the echocardiographic appearance resembled apical variant HCM, with a maximal apical lateral wall thickness of 13 mm and a small apical pouch identified with UEA. However, primary apical HCM was unlikely given the absence of comparable apical hypertrophy on TTE 2 months earlier and before serial echocardiograms. The short-interval change instead favored an acute or subacute myocardial process, particularly transient myocardial edema, neurocardiogenic myocardial injury, or a takotsubo-spectrum phenotype. The globally abnormal GLS also broadened the interpretation beyond isolated apical thickening, suggesting more diffuse myocardial involvement than would be expected from a focal apical morphologic variant alone. Matta et al. previously reported a case of transient apical myocardial thickening mimicking apical HCM in the setting of an acute distal left anterior descending coronary artery myocardial infarction, likely related to myocardial edema, resolving on a 3-month follow-up TTE.3

Given shared neurocardiogenic mechanisms, cerebral T waves have been associated with transient LV dysfunction, including stress-induced cardiomyopathy.4,5 Takotsubo syndrome was an important consideration in this case because intracranial disease and other neurologic stressors can trigger catecholamine-mediated myocardial stunning. Although the formal TTE interpretation did not identify classic apical ballooning or regional wall motion abnormalities, takotsubo syndrome can evolve dynamically, and the absence of a classic acute ballooning pattern does not exclude a recovery-phase or atypical phenotype. Previous work has shown that myocardial edema and increased LV mass may persist into the subacute phase, even after the recovery of LV systolic function.6 Case reports have also described takotsubo syndrome initially mimicking apical HCM because of transient apical edema.7 In this context, the combination of giant T-wave inversions, mildly reduced biplane LVEF, markedly abnormal GLS, new apical-predominant thickening, and partial ECG improvement was compatible with a transient neurocardiogenic or takotsubo-spectrum myocardial process, although this could not be confirmed without biomarkers, coronary evaluation, CMR, or follow-up echocardiography.

Other diagnostic considerations remained important. New giant T-wave inversions warrant evaluation for myocardial ischemia, and an invasive ischemic evaluation was considered. However, the patient had no cardiovascular symptoms or hemodynamic instability, and coronary intervention could have required antiplatelet therapy that delayed an urgent neurosurgical biopsy. Ibrutinib-associated cardiotoxicity was also considered, particularly given recent exposure and the patient's history of hypertension. This was less likely to be the unifying explanation because typical manifestations include hypertension, atrial fibrillation, ventricular arrhythmias, and arterial thrombotic events rather than isolated rapidly evolving apical myocardial thickening. Infiltrative disease, including cardiac involvement by lymphoma, and inflammatory conditions, such as myocarditis, also remained in the differential diagnosis. Although the patient was not treated with immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis should be considered in patients receiving such therapy who present with new ECG changes and echocardiographic abnormalities.

This case also highlights the role and limitations of echocardiography in a patient whose competing oncologic and neurosurgical priorities constrained cardiac testing. TTE demonstrated that the ECG abnormality was accompanied by new structural and deformation abnormalities rather than being an isolated electrical finding. Deformation imaging showed marked global impairment, although UEA improved endocardial definition and helped characterize the apical abnormality. CMR would have been the preferred subsequent modality to assess myocardial edema, late gadolinium enhancement pattern, ischemic scar, myocarditis, infiltration, cardiac lymphoma involvement, and features supporting or arguing against true HCM.8 Follow-up echocardiography would also have been valuable to determine whether the apical thickening and strain abnormality resolved in parallel with the ECG changes. These tests were recommended but ultimately deferred in the setting of poor oncologic prognosis and patient goals of care.

Conclusion

In conclusion, cerebral-type T-wave inversions may occur in the setting of progressive intracranial neoplastic disease, even without directly measured intracranial pressure or hemorrhage. When these ECG findings are accompanied by rapidly evolving myocardial thickening or strain abnormalities, echocardiography can identify an apical HCM mimic and help guide the differential diagnosis. Follow-up echocardiography and CMR, when feasible, are important to distinguish transient neurocardiogenic or takotsubo-spectrum myocardial injury from ischemia, myocarditis, infiltration, drug-related cardiotoxicity, and primary cardiomyopathy.
