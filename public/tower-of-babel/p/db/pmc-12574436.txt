Antibiotic self-medication and home storage among university students in Vietnam: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Dung Anh Doan, Viet Xuan Nguyen, Giang Ba Le, Thuy Thi Xuan Nguyen, Dai Xuan Dinh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAntibiotic self-medication and home storage are two common behaviours that can result in inappropriate drug use. This study elucidated their prevalence and associated factors among university students in Vietnam.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingVietnam.Participants2230 university students.Primary and secondary outcome measuresAntibiotic storage was evaluated at the time of the survey, while self-medication was assessed for 1 year preceding this time. Associated factors were determined via multivariable logistic regression models.ResultsAmong 2230 participants, 418 (18.7%) reported self-medicating with antibiotics. The three main reasons for this behaviour included saving time (48.6%), mild diseases (45.0%) and the ease of purchasing antibiotics from community pharmacies (43.8%). Community pharmacies (88.3%) and home storage (36.4%) were the primary sources of antibiotics for self-treatment. Students mainly selected antibiotics based on community pharmacists’ consultation (59.8%) and their knowledge/experience (37.8%). They commonly read the patient information leaflet (64.6%) and asked for the community pharmacist’s advice to obtain information about antibiotic usage (52.9%). Besides, 296 students (13.3%) stored antibiotics at home. Over two-thirds had antibiotic leftovers from previous treatment and stored them for future use (69.3%), while 46.3% purchased antibiotics from community pharmacies without a prescription. Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid was the most commonly used antibiotic for self-medication (59.8%) and home storage (54.4%). Antibiotic self-medication was significantly associated with older age (adjusted odds ratio (aOR)=1.25; 95% CI 1.15 to 1.35), non-healthcare students (aOR=0.47; 95% CI 0.34 to 0.63), higher knowledge score (aOR=1.05; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.07) and storing antibiotics at home (aOR=7.12; 95% CI 5.34 to 9.51). Factors associated with home storage of antibiotics included non-healthcare students (aOR=2.77; 95% CI 2.01 to 3.83), region (north: aOR=6.50; 95% CI 3.49 to 13.54; south: aOR=5.92; 95% CI 3.06 to 12.65), having a family member working in the medical field (aOR=1.70; 95% CI 1.29 to 2.25), higher knowledge score (aOR=1.05; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.08) and antibiotic self-medication (aOR=7.58; 95% CI 5.69 to 10.14) (p values<0.001).ConclusionsThe prevalence of antibiotic self-medication and home storage among students was relatively low but still raises public health concerns. Some interventions, such as integrating targeted health education programmes into university curricula, orientation sessions, extracurricular activities and campus campaigns, may help effectively promote rational antibiotic use.

Introduction

Antibiotics are one of the vital groups of medications used to prevent and treat bacterial infections. This medicine group is usually used to combat bacterial infections by targeting and killing bacteria or stopping their growth. They can also help prevent infections in certain situations, like postoperative care to prevent secondary infections. Recently, the inappropriate use (misuse and overuse) of antibiotics in humans, animals and plants has significantly contributed to the emergence and spread of many drug-resistant pathogens, thereby fueling the global threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR).1 2 A previous study estimated that approximately 4.71 million deaths were associated with AMR, including 1.14 million deaths attributable to AMR in 2021. It is anticipated that roughly 8.22 million deaths associated with AMR and 1.91 million deaths attributable to AMR could occur globally in 2050.3 Vietnam is among the countries that have recently witnessed an alarming increase in AMR. The widespread practice of purchasing antibiotics without prescriptions and irrational self-medication has made a contribution to the worsening of AMR in this country.4 5 The burden of AMR can affect not only individual patients but also public health and overall societal development. This issue demands urgent and multisectoral attention; as in many cases, common infections and minor injuries may once again become life-threatening.2 6

According to the WHO, self-medication involves ‘the use of medicinal products by the consumer to treat self-recognised disorders or symptoms, or the intermittent or continued use of a medication prescribed by a physician for chronic or recurring diseases or symptoms’. It also includes the use of family members’ medicines.7 Self-medication empowers patients/users to manage minor illnesses independently and may help reduce the burden on healthcare systems by improving access to medicines and lowering prescription costs. However, it also carries risks such as misdiagnosis, inappropriate dosing, prolonged use and potential drug interactions.8

University students represent a transitional population group, changing from depending on their families to leading an independent life. During this period, they may experience psychological and lifestyle changes that can affect their ability to manage personal health. Limited life experience and a need for self-reliance may increase students’ vulnerability when contracting diseases. The findings from many previous studies showed that antibiotic misuse was prevalent among this group. A systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that students were identified as the major users of antibiotic self-medication.9 The prevalence of self-medication with antibiotics among university students was 93.8% in Uganda (2022),10 67.1% in Eritrea (2019–2020),11 60.8% in Sudan (2020–2021)12 and 44.8% in Iran (2021).13 In China, a study with the participation of 11 192 university students indicated that 63.1% stored antibiotics at home, and this behaviour was five times more likely to engage in self-medication.14

In Vietnam, two studies reported the proportions of university students self-medicating with antibiotics. However, both of them were limited in scale (small sample sizes or only collecting data of pharmacy/medical students) and had methodological limitations (such as simplified tools or insufficient analysis of self-medication behaviours).15 16 To the best of our knowledge, to date, no studies have comprehensively investigated self-medication behaviour among Vietnamese university students. Besides, only a few previous studies provided detailed data on antibiotic storage at home. Our study aimed to investigate the prevalence and factors associated with antibiotic self-medication and home storage among university students in Vietnam.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

This was a multicentre, cross-sectional study. We purposively selected four universities where we were working to collect data from November 2023 to March 2024 (including two universities in the northern region: Hanoi University of Pharmacy and Phenikaa University, one in the central region: Da Nang University of Medical Technology and Pharmacy and one in the southern region: Pham Ngoc Thach University of Medicine). The study subjects were Vietnamese undergraduate students. Inclusion criteria included (1) being university students, (2) agreeing to participate in this study and (3) being able to read and understand Vietnamese. Since the primary outcomes were proportions and a cluster sampling method was employed, the formula n=deff.Z(1-α/2)2.p.(1-p)/d2 was used to calculate the required sample size. With Z1-α/2=2.575 (α=0.01), p=0.5, d=0.05 and a design effect (deff)=2, the minimum sample size was 1327 students.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

The questionnaire

An initial version of the questionnaire was developed based on a review of the literature1730 and subsequently reviewed by three academic experts to ensure content relevance and clarity. We used a portion of this questionnaire to conduct a study for the general population.5 A pilot study was conducted with the participation of 50 university students to adapt questions for this study subject, check the clarity of questions and assess the reliability of this questionnaire. Three months later, the same students were also invited to retake this questionnaire to evaluate its test-retest reliability.

The questionnaire included four main parts, and questions were mandatory (using the required-response feature in Google Forms). The first part consisted of 15 questions involving the students’ characteristics, including sex, year of birth, region (current location), area (hometown), university type, major, year of study, grade point average for the last semester, monthly allowance/income, marital status, living arrangement, the number of people living with the participant, whether having a family member working in the medical field, frequency of seeking health information and sources used to seek information about antibiotics and health.

The second part included 15 questions to measure students’ knowledge about antibiotics and antibiotic resistance (the knowledge scale). For each question, they had to choose one among five answer options (including totally correct, probably correct, totally incorrect, probably incorrect and do not know). The third part consisted of 11 questions involving their health beliefs and attitudes towards antibiotics (the attitude scale). Students had five answer options: totally agree, agree, normal/neutral, disagree and totally disagree (a 5-point Likert scale). These two parts and their scoring can be seen in online supplemental table S1. We computed the knowledge and attitude scores for each student. The possible scores of one student for the knowledge and attitude scales can range from 0 to 30 and 11 to 55, respectively. Regarding reliability, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.83 (the knowledge scale) and 0.89 (the attitude scale), indicating good internal consistency. The good test-retest reliability of this questionnaire was proved via intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). ICCs for the knowledge and attitude scales were 0.914 (95% CI 0.845 to 0.952) and 0.797 (95% CI 0.641 to 0.885), respectively. The differences in knowledge and attitude scores of 50 students in the pilot study between the two measurements were insignificant (knowledge: 18.58 and 19.74; attitude: 42.58 and 44.18, p values>0.05). Regarding validity, we performed face validity by consulting three university lecturers who were experts in the field to review the questionnaire content and ensure its appropriateness and clarity.

The last part embraced questions involving antibiotic self-medication and home storage (the two primary outcomes). First, we identified whether or not the participants self-medicated with antibiotics in the past year and stored antibiotics at their home at the time of surveying (not for the current use). For those self-medicating with antibiotics, we continued to collect data about their course of self-medication (eg, rationales behind this behaviour, where to obtain/purchase antibiotics and the name of the used antibiotics) (online supplemental table S2). Those storing antibiotics at home were also asked about where they had antibiotics to store, the name of the stored antibiotics, dosage forms, the place for storage and their expiration date. It is noted that a student was only classified as having self-medicated with antibiotics if they both indicated doing so and could name at least one antibiotic used. A similar requirement was applied to identify the behaviour of storing antibiotics at home.

Sampling technique, procedures and data collection

First, we purposively chose four aforementioned universities (first-stage cluster). The data collectors (the authors) included lecturers and students from these universities. At each university, one or several classes per academic year were selected for data collection, depending on the class size (second-stage cluster). Within each class chosen, university students were approached in lecture halls during their breaks. After a brief introduction to the study objectives, methodology and the assurance of confidentiality, a Quick Response code linking to the questionnaire on Google Forms was displayed, allowing students to access and complete the survey. Students completed the questionnaire within roughly 15–20 min. Their participation was voluntary. During the survey, data collectors were available to clarify participants’ questions. In addition, the class representatives also shared the Google Forms link in their respective class group chats (such as Messenger or Zalo) when necessary. Students with insufficient time during their breaks could complete this questionnaire later at home.

Statistical analysis

From the Google Forms link, data were exported and merged into an Excel file and then analysed using R Console V.4.5.0. Categorical variables (such as sex) were reported via numbers and percentages. Numeric variables (such as age) were described via means and SDs. Medians and IQRs were also mentioned if the data were not normally distributed. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression models were employed to identify factors associated with antibiotic self-medication and home storage among Vietnamese university students. Independent variables in the multivariable logistic regression models were selected by using the Bayesian model averaging (BMA) method. The goodness-of-fit of these multivariable models was evaluated via the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. A p value lower than 0.001 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Main characteristics of the sample

A total of 2250 students responded to the first question, ‘Do you agree to participate in this study?’. Among them, 20 students declined participation. Hence, 2230 students were included in the final analysis, yielding a response rate of 99.1%. The mean age of all participants was 21.07±1.50 years. About three-fifths were females (60.6%) and students from the north (60.1%). Students were relatively evenly distributed in terms of their areas and year of study. Over two-thirds (69.5%) were enrolled in public universities, and nearly three-quarters (74.3%) were healthcare students. Most students (91.3%) had a monthly allowance and income below ten million Vietnamese dongs. Only 1.9% were married. Regarding living arrangements, 30.9% were living with their families, 58.7% were renting a room/house and 10.4% were residing in university dormitories at the time of the survey. About one-third (33.9%) usually sought health information, whereas more than half (51.7%) did it only from time to time. Common sources used to seek information about antibiotics and health included the Internet (83.8%), medical personnel (59.5%), books/printed materials (47.8%), websites of reliable organisations (46.5%) and mass media (45.5%). Regarding students’ knowledge and attitudes toward antibiotics, their average knowledge score was 17.68±5.83 (minimum: 0, maximum: 30, median: 18, IQR: 14–22), and their average attitude score was 42.53±6.50 (minimum: 15, maximum: 55, median: 43, IQR: 39–47) (table 1).

Self-medication with antibiotics in the past year

Among the 2230 participants, 418 students (18.7%) self-medicated with antibiotics at least once during the previous 12 months. The three most common reasons for this behaviour were saving time (48.6%), mild diseases (45.0%) and the ease of purchasing antibiotics from medicine outlets (43.8%). Community pharmacies (88.3%) and home storage (36.4%) were the primary sources of antibiotics for self-treatment. The primary factors influencing students’ choice of antibiotics during self-medication were therapeutic indications (82.3%) and the brand names/manufacturers (67.7%). Their selection was commonly based on community pharmacists’ consultation (59.8%) and personal knowledge/experience (37.8%). To have information about antibiotic usage, students mainly read the patient information leaflet (64.6%) and asked for community pharmacists’ advice (52.9%). Notably, among 270 students using the patient information leaflet, only 126 (46.7%) indicated they could comprehend its content (table 2).

Antibiotics self-medication among Vietnamese university students (main results)

Students reported using antibiotics to self-treat various symptoms, such as sore throat (51.7%), cough/common cold (42.3%), fever (34.9%) and runny/stuffy nose (33.5%). The five most commonly used antibiotics during self-medication were amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (59.8%), cefuroxime (9.8%), penicillin (6.2%), cefixime (5.5%) and cephalexin (5.3%). Sixty-five university students (15.6%) adjusted the dosage of antibiotics on their own, while 77 (18.4%) switched antibiotics during the self-treatment course. Notably, 6.7% used different brand-name formulations of the same antibiotic concurrently. Adverse drug reactions were reported by 14.6% of the students during self-medication. Regarding treatment adherence, nearly three-fifths (59.6%) completed the full course of antibiotics, whereas 40.4% discontinued use after symptoms subsided. Additionally, approximately two-thirds (65.3%) expressed a willingness to engage in self-medication with antibiotics again in the future (table 2 and online supplemental table S2).

Home storage of antibiotics

Of the 2230 participants, 1015 (45.5%) reported that they were storing at least one type of medicine at home at the time of the survey, including 296 students (13.3%) storing at least one antibiotic (excluding those who were currently undergoing antibiotic treatment due to illness). Over two-thirds had antibiotic leftovers from previous treatment and stored them for future use (69.3%), while 46.3% purchased antibiotics from community pharmacies without a prescription. Tablets (50.7%) and capsules (40.5%) were the two most frequently stored dosage forms of antibiotics in their households. The four most commonly stored antibiotics were amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (54.4%), cefuroxime (6.8%), cephalexin (6.4%) and tobramycin (6.1%). About 69.9% stored antibiotics in drawers or containers, whereas 24.3% kept antibiotics on desks or shelves. Most students reported that the stored antibiotics were still within their expiration dates (84.8%), while only two (0.7%) acknowledged storing expired antibiotics (table 3).

Factors associated with antibiotic self-medication and home storage among Vietnamese university students

As per the multivariable logistic regression model, results showed that antibiotic self-medication was significantly associated with the participants’ age, major, knowledge about antibiotics and storing antibiotics at home. In particular, this behaviour was more prevalent among students storing at least one kind of antibiotics at home (adjusted odds ratio (aOR)=7.12; 95% CI 5.34 to 9.51) compared with those who did not. In addition, for each one-unit increase in participants’ age (1 year) and knowledge score (one point), the odds of antibiotic self-medication increased by 25% (aOR=1.25; 95% CI 1.15 to 1.35) and 5% (aOR=1.05; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.07), respectively. Besides, non-healthcare students were less likely to self-medicate with antibiotics when compared with healthcare students (aOR=0.47; 95% CI 0.34 to 0.63) (table 4).

Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive investigation in Vietnam into antibiotic self-medication and home storage among university students. Our results showed that 18.7% of the participants self-medicated with antibiotics. The three main reasons for this behaviour included saving time, mild diseases and the ease of purchasing antibiotics from community pharmacies. Community pharmacies and home storage were the primary sources of antibiotics for self-treatment. Students mainly selected antibiotics based on community pharmacists’ consultation and their knowledge/experience. The patient information leaflet and the advice of community pharmacists were the two primary sources of information about antibiotic usage. About 13.3% stored antibiotics at home. Many students purchased antibiotics from community pharmacies without a prescription and/or had antibiotic leftovers from previous treatment and stored them for future use. Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid was the most commonly used antibiotic for self-medication and home storage. Antibiotic self-medication was significantly associated with the participants’ age, major, knowledge about antibiotics and home storage. Factors associated with home storage of antibiotics included major, region, having a family member working in the medical field, knowledge about antibiotics and self-medication.

Self-medication with antibiotics is a common behaviour involving inappropriate antibiotic use among university students around the world. In this study, 18.7% reported engaging in this behaviour during the past year. Our finding is considerably lower than the results of many previous studies in Eastern Uganda in 2022 (93.8%),10 Zambia in 2022 (76.7%),31 Eritrea during 2019–2020 (67.1%),11 Bangladesh in 2022 (61.0%),32 Sudan during 2020–2021 (60.8%),12 Tanzania in 2019 (57.0%),33 Iran in 2021 (44.8%)13 and Malaysia in 2018 (39.3%).34 However, some studies reported low data, such as a study in Rwanda (12.1%).35 The differences in this prevalence among studies may stem from the differences in location, policies on trading antibiotics in countries, student type, data collection period and the questionnaires used. By way of illustration, our study included both healthcare and non-healthcare students and was conducted after the COVID-19 pandemic. Meanwhile, some studies only collected data from medical students.10 12 Healthcare students may believe they possess adequate knowledge of pharmacology and pathology to accurately self-diagnose and manage their health conditions, resulting in self-treatment. Besides, several studies were conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. During this time, people had to stay home and be isolated, thereby increasing the likelihood of self-medication.10 13 31 32

In Vietnam, numerous students self-medicated with antibiotics by reason of time saving, mild diseases and the ease of purchasing antibiotics from community pharmacies, in line with the results of several previous studies. In Rwanda and Bangladesh, illness not severe enough to have a consultation was the primary reason, while in Karachi, Pakistan, it was time-saving.32 35 36 Besides minor illness, previous experiences in using antibiotics and the perception of being health workers were also the common reasons for self-medication among students in Uganda.10 Sore throat, cough, common cold, fever and runny/stuffy nose were the common symptoms/diseases self-treated with antibiotics by students not only in Vietnam but also in Rwanda, Sudan, Malaysia and Eritrea.11 12 34 35 In many cases, the above symptoms are typically viral in origin, and using antibiotics is likely inappropriate.3739 Similar to the results of studies in Sudan and Eritrea,11 12 community pharmacies were the primary source to obtain antibiotics for self-treatment among Vietnamese university students. It is noted that community pharmacists might further contribute to our students’ antibiotic use by providing recommendations regarding the selection and appropriate administration of these medications. In Vietnam, antibiotics belong to a class of prescription-only medicines. Accordingly, community pharmacists can play a crucial role in counselling and educating students to help them understand relevant regulations and rational antibiotic use.

Approximately 13.3% of Vietnamese university students kept at least one type of antibiotic at home, far lower than the result of a study in China: among 11 192 university students, 63.1% reported storing this type of medication at home when surveyed.14 A high prevalence of home storage of antibiotics was also found among medical students in Sudan (68.4%).12 The relatively low rate of antibiotic storage among students in our study may be attributable to the following reasons. In our research, antibiotic storage behaviour was recorded only if the participants reported storing antibiotics at home and could specify at least one antibiotic by name. To our knowledge, this specific criterion was not applied in previous studies. Besides, the survey in Sudan was conducted from 2020 to 2021, when the COVID-19 outbreak occurred. During this period, social distancing and isolation were applied, and many people stored medicines in their households to use when necessary.12 Regarding the sources of antibiotics for home storage, our findings were in line with a study in China: leftovers from previous treatment and purchasing in community pharmacies without a prescription were the two common sources.14 Tablets and capsules were among the most preferred dosage forms for storage by Vietnamese university students, as they were easy to use and had favourable stability characteristics that facilitate storage. Besides, in Vietnam, amoxicillin alone or in combination with clavulanic acid was highly available in medicine outlets with various pharmaceutical dosage forms by virtue of affordable costs and its safety profile.40 41 This can be a rationale for why this active pharmaceutical ingredient emerged as the most frequently reported antibiotic in home storage and self-medication practices among Vietnamese students. In Rwanda, Uganda, Pakistan and Eritrea, this substance was also the most common antibiotic used for self-medication.10 11 35 36

In China, students storing antibiotics at home were five times more likely to engage in self-medication when ill and 2.6 times more likely to self-medicate with antibiotics for prophylaxis than the other students.14 Our study showed a similar finding when self-medication was more prevalent among students storing at least one kind of antibiotics at home (aOR=7.12; 95% CI 5.34 to 9.51) compared with those without. It seems that easy access to stored antibiotics could reduce the perceived need to seek medical advice, and previous experiences with self-medication might reinforce the future use of stored antibiotics among students. Besides, our study indicated that antibiotic self-medication was more prevalent among healthcare students, older students and those with higher knowledge scores. Some reasons can explain these results. First and foremost, students with higher ages and higher antibiotic knowledge scores may have previous experience with similar conditions and successful self-treatment. Meanwhile, healthcare students can obtain academic knowledge during their university studies, thereby developing greater confidence in self-diagnosis and treatment decisions. Furthermore, having greater knowledge and prolonged exposure to the healthcare environment could increase students’ ability to access antibiotics without prescriptions, resulting in self-treatment and antibiotic accumulation. Students with higher knowledge may also foster proactive behaviours, such as storing antibiotics at home to avoid the perceived inconvenience of seeking medical care for minor ailments.

Besides self-medication behaviour and knowledge about antibiotics, other factors associated with home storage of antibiotics included major, region and having at least one family member working in the medical field. In comparison with students in the central region, those living and studying in the northern and southern regions were significantly more likely to store antibiotics at home. This difference may be attributed to several reasons. Many areas in central Vietnam have lower socioeconomic conditions. Students in this region may have a frugal lifestyle and not tend to store medicines. Additionally, in lieu of selling antibiotics in full blister packs/boxes that can potentially result in leftovers after treatment, community pharmacists in the central region are more likely to dispense antibiotics in exact quantities based on prescribed doses, thereby minimising the likelihood of having antibiotic leftovers available for storage. Nevertheless, the influence of regional differences requires further exploration, given that the present study was limited to data collected from several provinces and may not fully represent the entire regional context.

Our study also reported a higher prevalence of home storage of antibiotics among non-healthcare students, when compared with healthcare students. Non-healthcare students may have a limited understanding of appropriate antibiotic use and the menace of AMR, making them more likely to store antibiotics without considering the potential consequences. They may follow informal advice (from peers and relatives), leading to unnecessary antibiotic purchases and storage.42 Besides, having family members in the healthcare sector may facilitate easier access to medicines (including antibiotics), thereby increasing the likelihood of home storage. These students may be educated to adopt a proactive approach to health management, seeking to protect their well-being rather than waiting until illness occurs to use medication. Furthermore, family members working in healthcare often store medicines at home, which can establish habitual behaviours or role models that students tend to emulate. Consequently, students may learn from their relatives’ approaches to health issues by preparing and keeping medications readily available. Ongoing exposure to medical practices within the household may normalise the storage behaviour for perceived future use, even without a formal prescription.14

This study provided a comprehensive investigation conducted in Vietnam following the COVID-19 pandemic, a period during which medication purchasing and usage behaviours of the public (including university students) have changed. The relatively large sample size can help strengthen the external validity of the findings and enhance their generalisability and representativeness. Using a p value threshold of 0.001 may help strengthen the reproducibility of research findings. Furthermore, we selected independent variables in multivariable logistic regression models using the BMA method, which can help avoid multicollinearity and overfitting. However, our study also has several limitations. First, the causal relationships between antibiotic self-medication/home storage and independent variables cannot be established by virtue of the cross-sectional nature of this study. Second, self-reported data may bring recall bias and social desirability. For self-medication behaviour, we collected data based on students’ recall over the 12 months prior to the survey. This extended recall period may have affected the accuracy of their responses due to potential memory bias. The participants might have misreported their actual behaviours. Moreover, antibiotics are a specialised category of medications requiring a certain level of understanding. Students may also not know all antibiotics, since many active ingredients are categorised as antibiotics, and numerous brands are on the market. It is plausible that some students have used or stored antibiotics without recognising them as such, leading to possible underestimation of these behaviours in our study.

Conclusions

The prevalence of antibiotic self-medication and home storage among students in Vietnam was relatively low but still raises public health concerns. Community pharmacists played a crucial role in providing antibiotics for both behaviours and information about antibiotic usage. Interventions and health education programmes may be developed to enhance students’ awareness of antibiotic resistance and the rational use of antibiotics (eg, the importance of completing the full course of treatment). Targeted health education programmes should be integrated into university curricula, orientation sessions, extracurricular activities or campus campaigns to promote rational antibiotic use. In addition, stricter regulations on non-prescription antibiotic sales at community pharmacies should be enforced to limit inappropriate access.
