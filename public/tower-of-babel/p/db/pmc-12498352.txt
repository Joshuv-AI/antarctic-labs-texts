“The physical environment is so important, but it's not everything”—nursing staffs' experiences of moving to a new purpose-built forensic psychiatric clinic
Authors: Eleni Amico, Ulrica Horberg, Hanna Tuvesson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThe redesigning of psychiatric care environments has been increasingly recognized as a factor that could influence therapeutic outcomes and staff experiences. This study aimed to describe nursing staff's experiences of caring for patients after moving to a new purpose-built forensic psychiatric clinic.MethodsNine semi-structured interviews were conducted with the nursing staff. A thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's approach was used, resulting in five themes describing nursing staff's experiences of caring for patients in this new setting.ResultsThe findings showed that the care environment was pleasant and provided possibilities for mental recovery for the patients; however, the design of the environment in combination with a lack of staff presence led to a decrease in interactions and connections with the nursing staff.ConclusionThe findings indicate that moving to the new purpose-built facility may benefit patients' well-being and autonomy through an improved physical care environment. The beneficial impact of a number of psychosocial factors in the care environment has been contemporaneously undermined, which affects the working environment and the possibility for the nursing staff to develop caring relationships. This study emphasizes the importance of continuous staff presence in ensuring good and safe care.KEYWORDS: Care environment, compulsory care, forensic psychiatry, nursing, qualitative research, work environment

Background

The focus of this Swedish study was on the experiences of nursing staff in providing care in a forensic psychiatric setting after a relocation from outdated to modern newly built facilities. Forensic psychiatric care is a multifaceted field where psychiatry and law are intertwined thus making it a complex care context (Carroll et al., 2004). Legal frameworks governing forensic psychiatric care vary significantly across countries (Beis et al., 2022; Sampson et al., 2016). Forensic psychiatric care in Sweden is regulated by the Swedish Health and Medical Services Act and is provided in 25 clinics, with a total of approximately 1200 inpatient beds. Care is provided in accordance with the Forensic Psychiatric Care Act (SOSFS, 1991:1129) at three different security levels (SOSFS, 2006:9). An offender who has been adjudged to have acted under the influence of a serious mental disorder can be sentenced by a court of law to receive compulsory forensic psychiatric care as a consequence of committing the crime (Forensic Psychiatric Care Act, SOSFS 1991:1129, § 3).

A clean, pleasant, and safe care environment provides an opportunity to enhance the well-being of patients and can be a valuable investment for patients' health. Their sense of safety can contribute to increased well-being for both nursing staff and patients in terms of a decrease in conflict and an improved work environment. The latter can in turn result in greater trust in the nursing staff and the care provided (Molin et al., 2021). Edvardsson et al. (2006) emphasize the importance of upgrading the interior, as it can have importance for the care provided contributing to feelings of confirmation and value for both patients and nursing staff. The care environment has often been described as including both tangible features and intangible qualities, such as mood or atmosphere, that may be perceived differently by individuals (Tuvesson, 2011). Bodryzlova et al. (2024) showed that architecture and design in psychiatric settings play a crucial role in addressing complex factors, such as patient stress, social interaction, autonomy, dignity, and privacy. Thoughtful designs can contribute to patient recovery by avoiding both overstimulation and sensory deprivation.

The character and ambiance of psychiatric inpatient settings have long been considered as important factors for the well-being of both patients and staff. Concepts such as therapeutic milieu (Thomas et al., 2002) and ward atmosphere (Moos, 1997) describe the interplay of physical, social, and organisational elements. These include spatial design, social and relational aspects, organisational structures and culture, and individual experiences of the environment. Moos (1997) refers to this as the unique personality of a place.

The setting in forensic psychiatric care is often perceived as paradoxical, combining therapeutic intentions with legal constraints (Walker et al., 2023). Schweitzer (2004) maintained that care and interventions provided on a locked ward can severely restrict patients' privacy. Porskrog-Kristiansen and Strand (2019) further emphasized that the patient group receiving care in a forensic psychiatric context is a vulnerable group, with exposed privacy and limited autonomy. Salzmann-Erikson (2015) also showed how the wards' rules restrict patients' freedom, as patients are not allowed access to all the rooms in the ward, and they cannot move freely as the exit is locked. These rules and procedures are put into practice in the routines on the ward, which stipulate when activities and meals take place, and visits from friends and relatives require planning and approval (Moyles et al., 2023). Walker et al. (2023) stated that actions to maintain security restrict patients' autonomy. The power imbalance in the care environment also makes it difficult to develop trusting relationships between nursing staff and patients (Hörberg et al., 2012).

A Swedish study showed that patients who have moved to new, purpose-built forensic psychiatric settings have experienced increased satisfaction and security in new premises. Opportunities to participate in their own care planning have increased because of more individualized care. Patients also experienced an increased quality of care and showed a greater willingness to be readmitted to the ward (Alexiou et al., 2016). The physical design has also been shown to play a significant role in shaping patients’ perceptions of power and control. A systematic review of various psychiatric settings, including forensic psychiatric care, found that specific environmental features, such as the ability to regulate room temperature, access to lockable storage, and the removal of glass barriers at nursing stations, can positively influence patients' sense of autonomy and control (Rodriguez-Labajos et al., 2024). Da-Som and Sang-Hee (2022) maintained that a healthy working environment meets the basic requirements for ensuring the working environment and quality of care. They also highlighted the importance of the nursing staff's responsibility to actively apply health-promoting behaviours to promote health and care for patients.

Forensic psychiatric care is a complex form of care that requires a delicate balance between security and care. However, it can be challenging to care for patients in a forensic psychiatric setting, both in terms of the physical and psychosocial care environment, as the nursing staff must navigate power dynamics while maintaining a caring approach. Research shows that intentional changes to the physical setting can support meaningful daily activities and improve patient well-being (cf. Bodryzlova et al., 2024; Edvardsson et al., 2006; Molin et al., 2021).

Taking part of the nursing staff's experiences of caring for patients in newly built premises (following a move from an outdated forensic psychiatric facility) can provide valuable insights into both the enabling and limiting factors within the care environment. These insights are particularly important for understanding how the setting influences the staff's ability to establish and maintain caring relationships with patients. The present study thus aimed to describe the experiences of nursing staff caring for patients in a new purpose-built forensic psychiatric clinic.

Methods

This descriptive qualitative study was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board in Linköping (Reg. No: 2016/85−31) which is a national-level ethics review board responsible for all ethical approvals in the country under the Ethical Review Act. Inductive thematic analysis, as described by Braun and Clarke (2006), was used both as the method of data analysis and as a guiding framework for the study design. This approach aligns with a flexible qualitative methodology that allows themes to emerge from the data, rather than being driven by existing theoretical constructs. The study was conducted in connection with the relocation of a forensic psychiatric clinic from an older institutional setting to newly constructed, purpose-built premises.

Setting

This study explores staff experiences of relocating from older, centrally located, and worn-out premises with a lower security classification to a newly designed facility situated on the outskirts of a smaller city. The new environment was developed to better support therapeutic goals, with attention to spatial organisation, natural surroundings, and a more integrated approach to safety. All the patients are now accommodated in single-occupancy rooms, providing enhanced privacy and opportunities for rest and self-reflection. These rooms face green outdoor areas, allowing for natural light and views of nature, which contribute to a calm and restorative atmosphere. The facility operates within a higher security classification, incorporating advanced technologies and architectural zoning to ensure safe movement and interaction throughout the premises. While the design emphasizes therapeutic qualities, it also meets strict safety requirements through features such as secure perimeters and controlled access points. The ward buildings are long and narrow, a layout that supports daylight access and energy efficiency. However, this design also introduces challenges, including extended walking distances and reduced visual overview.

Participants and recruitment

Potential participants were recruited through a previously distributed survey in which nursing staff interested in being contacted for more information about an interview study noted their contact information. A purposive sampling approach among those who provided contact information was used to ensure variation in key participant characteristics; diversity in age, gender, work experience, and type of ward. This form of sampling is commonly used in qualitative research to capture a wide range of perspectives and enhance the richness of the data (Braun & Clarke, 2022). The potential participants received written and oral information about the study before agreeing to participate. Signed informed consent was obtained from the participants prior to the interview. Nine nursing staff members (registered nurses and nurse assistants) were interviewed: five women and four men. Participants' work experience varied between 5 and 16 years. Seven worked at one of the forensic psychiatric wards and two worked at the joint activity centre available for the wards.

Data collection

The data were collected by the second author and a former colleague nine months after the move to new buildings. Both interviewers were trained in qualitative methods and were aligned on style and approach through joint preparation, pilot interviews and the use of a shared interview guide. Regular debriefings ensured consistency throughout data collection. The participants were verbally reminded of their rights before the interviews began. These were previously stated in the information letter and included the right to withdraw and confidentiality of participation. Nine semi-structured interviews were conducted via telephone and lasted between 34 and 56 min. An interview guide containing a few open questions about how they experienced the transition to the new premises and how this influenced the support and care provided to the patients was used. Examples of interview questions included: “How would you describe the current care environment?”, How would you describe the previous care environment?”, “What reflections do you have regarding the care you provide in relation to the previous and current care environments?”, “What has the move to the new care environment meant for you in terms of how you support patients’ health and well-being?”. The open questions were followed by probing questions to obtain richer descriptions of what the participants spoke. All interviews were securely recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Analysis

Interview transcripts were read several times and analysed using thematic analysis, according to Braun and Clarke (2006, 2019). Thematic analysis is a flexible approach for analysing qualitative data that searches for themes or patterns and describes data in rich detail (Braun & Clarke, 2006). After the initial readings, the first author thoroughly marked the relevant data in the text, which was then discussed with the last author. These data were then transferred and organized in a new document, where an initial open coding was performed by the first author, followed by a revision and discussion of codes with the last author. The code documents were then printed, and all codes were cut into slips of paper that were used to explore initial patterns, sub-themes, and themes. These were then collectively reviewed and validated by all authors to reach the final and refined themes. The first draft was created by the first author with guidance from two other authors, and quotations were identified. A final comprehensive presentation of the findings was organized collectively. Rigour was ensured through co-author review, regular analytical discussions and thorough documentation, supporting transparency, consistency and conceptual depth in the analysis.

A therapeutic but poor physical care environment

The new premises contribute to a physical care environment that is experienced as creating “a personal space for well-being”. Participants described the environment as “new, bright, and dignified”, yet also noted that it felt “cold and sterile”. The experience of the new environment is that it can provide the opportunity for recovery by providing each person with a personal space, but the environment can also be limited through its cold and sterile design. This duality highlights how the physical environment can simultaneously promote therapeutic benefits and impose limitations, depending on how its aesthetic and functional qualities are experienced.

Personal spaces for well-being

The nursing staff feel that the opportunities for breathing spaces in the new premises are beneficial as patients do not “get in each other's way,” it does not become crowded, and the risk of conflicts is therefore reduced. All patients have their own space, and because of the larger floor space at the wards, patients can choose to go away if they want or sit with everyone else if they want. The cramped spaces in previous premises resulted in many conflicts in situations where the patient's well-being was exacerbated by too many impressions and relationships. Nursing staff believe that the newfound ability to step away and reflect reduces many patients' anxiety and that this has resulted in patients being able to apologize face-to-face in situations where a conflict has arisen.

“They can step away and they don’t need to confront the others, and they don’t do it. I’m fairly sure that it’s due to the patients that they sometimes step away from people they avoid and are able to do it. Because they have that possibility” (Interview 3).

The increased number of rooms for individual meetings with patients in the ward has served a useful purpose when staff need to divert patients and have private dialogue away from the rest of the ward. However, nursing staff lack their own areas where they can go in order to relax and find their own space.

New, bright and dignified but sterile

Nursing staff experienced mixed feelings about the new environment compared to the old. While the new setting was more modern and comfortable, some found it impersonal and too stripped down, lacking the warmth of the previous ward. These accounts reflect how different aspects of the environment shaped varied emotional responses and experiences of care.

The previous care environment was experienced as undignified for the patients to stay in, and according to the nursing staff, the patients themselves expressed that they felt that the environment was bad because of how worn down, crowded, and untidy. Despite this, it was experienced as a cozy and welcoming for patients. It was warm and had a high coziness factor, and if it had been refurbished, many nursing staff said they would have preferred it to the new one. The nursing staff expressed that for patients, the new, nice care environment creates a positive feeling. The nursing staff observed that patients experienced a sense of comfort comparable to being in their own homes.

“The patients reacted and looked around. They thought it looked so good. When they could, that is with the presence, the capacity, the ability to be able to reflect on the environment here that a patient can have, it was just positive” (Interview 1).

The nursing staff experienced that the patients sometimes do not want to be discharged because of the standard, comfortable, and home-like feeling that the ward provides, as they do not want to come home to something worse, run-down, and without having staff close by.

The new premises are experienced as bright and airy, with large open spaces, high ceilings, and large windows. These aspects should generate a sense of calm when looking out, but the neutral colours, spartan decor, and cleanliness make the ward feel stripped down and empty. The stillness, peace, and quiet of the ward have therapeutic benefits that promote patients’ well-being. Furniture for different purposes was placed on the premises to promote contact. Opportunities to play ping-pong, chess, and video games have created beneficial connections between patients. The outdoor environment is designed to promote social interaction and a wide range of activities of well-being.

Supporting patients' growth

The sub-themes “Enabling spaces” and “Preparing for the future” reflect the nursing staff's experiences of supporting patients' personal development and promoting long-term well-being. These sub-themes illustrate how the new care environment and activities are experienced to support patients' growth, both in terms of immediate engagement and in preparing for life beyond the ward. The nursing staff describe their role as facilitators of recovery, autonomy, and future readiness.

Enabling spaces

The nursing staff experience that visits to the activity centre are a positive change in the environment from the ward for the patients. The visits took place on the patients' own initiative and terms. The nursing staff believe that this helps build interest and reduce patients' boredom.

“It’s created happiness and well-being, one patient described it as being like a trip, that one travelled ahead in time. When he played the drums it was like travelling ahead in time where other problems were forgotten” (Interview 1).

The activity centre generates the possibility of preserving and maintaining each patient's skills and opportunities to develop new functions. The nursing staff believe that it also gives the patient the freedom to try something new, to playfully improvise and develop mentally, to think: “I'd like to try this thing that I've never done before” and have the opportunity to do so. It is appreciated that patients are given the opportunity to have new experiences and environments that are different from anything they have tried before and may ever try. This opens a door for new possibilities and supports nursing staff in facilitating patients’ recovery through therapeutic engagement, skill-development, and opportunities for personal growth.

Preparing for the future

The design of the new premises is experienced as simplifying and creating opportunities for a natural, honest, and solid insight into the patients' well-being in different environments and during different activities, which helps the nursing staff plan what the patient needs for the future. However, the nursing staff felt that there was a lack of preparation for discharge for the patients, and that patients did not get to practice for life outside the ward before their discharge.

“They'll be leaving here sooner or later and then we'll perhaps want to teach them the most, how shall I say it, elementary things, as a patient one has no responsibility here at all. I think we make them more handicapped than they really are” (Interview 5).

The nursing staff described the patients' need to learn different things, such as how to take care of their hygiene and how to keep their rooms tidy. The nursing staff do all this for patients in the ward, even though they can do it by themselves. The nursing staff expressed a concern that the care period is shorter now compared to that in the previous premises, and that patients are therefore more often believed to fall back into, for example, addiction with the need for further care in the ward.

The importance of being able to build caring relationships

The sub-themes “Opportunities for Conversation” and “Availability and Presence of Nursing Staff” reflect the nursing staff's experiences of how relational aspects of care have been affected by changes in the physical and organisational environment. Staff described a reduction in spontaneous interactions and a diminished presence in shared spaces, both of which were seen as crucial for establishing trust and building caring relationships with patients. These disruptions were experienced to negatively impact the quality of care and the emotional well-being of patients.

Opportunities for conversations

Nursing staff reported a lack of spontaneity when it came to conversations with patients. In the past, nursing staff could take an unscheduled walk with a patient, where an alliance and trust were built, and conversations flowed naturally about anything. These walks reassured patients with anxiety. Such walks require advanced planning in the new care environment, which hinders nursing staff from being sensitive to and acting on patients’ needs for contact and conversation.

“We have a lot of conversations with the patients and the best is really when one can go out and walk together, the conversation occurs in a more natural way. When you're sitting in a room for conversations, sitting opposite each other and staring at each other, it feels more like a forced conversation, I don't know how to describe it, but you lose a bit of the contact with the patient there, it's a great pity that it's become like that as we don't have the same possibilities to go out and walk. First of all, there has to be two of us when we go out with a patient and the surroundings here are not so good to walk in either. I think we've lost a lot there” (Interview 9).

However, the nursing staff said that there is more room for conversations in the new premises, which means that patients do not have to wait for their turn to have a private conversation with nursing staff, and that more conversations could be held at the same time, but this was not possible because of low staffing levels. Patients have to look for and ask for the nursing staff to get time for conversations that have to be scheduled, which results in the dialogues being experienced as rigid and impersonal, and tends to focus on things that are negative, and things that the patients urgently need help with that they have not been able to solve on their own. The activity centre's nursing staff felt that many good, honest conversations were generated when the patients were in the relaxed environment of the activity centre.

Availability and presence of nursing staff

The nursing staff felt that there was more movement in the ward before the relocation to the new premises, and the atmosphere was homely and convivial because the nursing staff were present. They socialized with the patients, sat together in common areas, and felt safe being able to turn to the nursing staff when needed.

“Being as we don't sit with them at all as much as we used to do so I think that the sense of security is not as high as it was before. Even though we anyway try to see them as much as possible” (Interview 6).

The nursing staff believe that they are not as present in the new premises; colleagues are perceived to cut themselves off from the patients and stay in the office, while others refuse to provide care to the patients in favour of finishing their shift earlier. This experience has a negative impact on care because the presence and support of nursing staff can reassure patients and reduce their anxiety.

The experience of nursing staff is that accessible, empathetic, and trusting nursing staff creates an alliance, which leads to a positive psychosocial environment and less violence, which in turn is beneficial for patients' discharge to outpatient care. Alliances are considered important for patient care and recovery. What is required is for someone to listen to and take the patient seriously. Lack of a trusting relationship is experienced as diminishing the patient and creating a sense of insecurity. However, nursing staff find it difficult to form an alliance due to lack of time; they feel that they are not as attentive and responsive as before relocation due to stress.

The work situation for nursing staff

The sub-themes “Calmer Atmosphere but a More Stressful Work Environment” and “The Importance of Collaboration” capture the nursing staff’s experiences of working in the new care environment. While the physical setting is experienced as more calming and comfortable for patients, the nursing staff report a contrasting experience of increased stress and workload. The new environment, although often believed to be beneficial for patient well-being, has introduced logistical and staffing challenges that hinder the staff’s ability to provide consistent and responsive care. These tensions between environmental improvements and organisational strain shape the everyday work situation and impact both staff well-being and the quality of given care.

Calmer atmosphere but a more stressful work environment

Thanks to the new large, open spaces and the fact that each patient has their own toilet, shower, and TV in the room, the new environment is experienced as more comfortable and calmer for patients, which reduces the number of alarm signals, as well as the risk of incidents and conflicts. The nursing staff also expressed that they had not had to use coercive measures since moving to the new premises. They experience a high workload because there are too few nurses on hand in the new care environment, and they describe that they are unable to finish what they have started because of constant interruptions and tasks that have to be done in between to make things work in the ward.

“I do as well as I can based on the conditions I have to work from but in the old premises I wasn't so stressed” (Interview 6).

Some nursing staff described the longer commuting distance to the new premises as tiring and demanding, contributing to feelings of exhaustion and reduced energy during the workday. One staff member expressed concern that this added strain made it more difficult to manage daily challenges and maintain resilience in emotionally charged situations. The long distances between the buildings within the hospital area, such as walking to the activity centre, are experienced negatively as two nurses have to accompany the patient and they are away from the ward for a longer period, and the care for the patients on the wards consequently suffers. The nursing staff spoke of this initially, leading to frustration among the patients who expressed their dissatisfaction. Staff shortages on the ward are compensated for by limiting one-on-one activities with patients in order to be able to oversee the ward. The nursing staff also feel that the reduced workforce results in many activities, pursuits, and outings not being able to take place, and that medical appointments have to be cancelled, which is experienced as generating disappointment among patients.

The importance of collaboration

The feeling among nursing staff is that it is easier to have contact with the team around the patient since the move to the new premises, as all the team members can now meet more easily. Communication and collaboration are therefore experienced as having improved, and proximity to the team provides a greater holistic understanding of the patients' needs and situation, which is experienced as positive. As a result, they feel that patients' participation in their own care has increased. Receiving positive feedback from colleagues generates a positive feeling and sense of affirmation, and communication and collaboration between professionals are described as being important for both patients and nursing staff.

Despite the positive experiences presented above, there is still a lack of consensus among nursing staff concerning daily nursing practice and that they do not work uniformly or maintain the same routines and procedures. Communication between nursing staff is perceived to be lacking, which leads to time being spent on the wrong things, which sends the wrong message to patients and ultimately affects their care. The staff’s focus shifted from being about patients to about their situation.

“It's the simplest example, if some of us serve marmalade on the rusks and others don’t then a forum to discuss this suddenly arises. This takes time from the patients, from the actual nursing” (Interview 1).

The nursing staff's ethical approach

The sub-themes “Safety, Overview and Control” and “Professional Approach and Values” illustrate how the nursing staff navigate ethical care within the context of significant environmental and organisational changes. The relocation to new premises introduced stricter routines, enhanced safety protocols, and a more structured physical layout. These changes were experienced as both supportive and challenging: while some staff appreciated the increased clarity and control, others felt that the rigidity limited flexibility and responsiveness in care. The redesigned care environment has altered the dynamics of staff–patient interaction, challenging staff to uphold person-centred and respectful care within a more structured and regulated setting.

Safety, overview and control

Opinions are divided among the nursing staff about the reviews of new routines and training, quality assurance, and safety culture on the new premises. Some feel that safety takes up too much time and space and makes their way of working less flexible, while others feel that the clarity and strict rules are beneficial to everyone and reduces the “slack” as it was before.

“Everyone benefits from stricter rules. There were perhaps too few restrictions before. When there was another safety classification then you could perhaps go to the activity centre with patients by yourself. We don't always think about the safety culture. So, it's clearer for everyone” (Interview 4).

Entrance control was initially experienced as negative but is now experienced as positive due to the new order and control that exists for both entrance and exit. All rooms in the previous premises were located on the same floor, and the open-floor space provided a better overview of the ward. The nursing staff talked about knowing where the staff and patients were at all times and felt that they had better insight into the patients' everyday lives, and the feeling of control generated a sense of security.

The overall view of the ward is now reduced, which the nursing staff experience as stressful as it leads to reduced transparency and a sense of control. Unlike before, they feel that they cannot guarantee safety, as fights and conflicts can break out at any time, despite the fact that the level of security is higher in the new ward.

Professional approach and values

The nursing staff feel that some staff members do not share the core values of being respectful, welcoming, showing consideration to the patients, and being driven to help them. Instead, they are perceived to work with setting limits and consequences; they are judgmental and more punitive and believe that patients have it too good and are spoiled. The nursing staff felt that the attitude of staff should be more humble and less judgmental and that they need to keep in mind that they work at a hospital and not a prison, where care should be provided and punishment should not be met.

“These are people who are sick and have committed their crime under the influence of their illness. Our job is to help them to be able to move into the community with the help of therapy, medication and other ways. We help them to get on the right track, they're not to be here to be punished because they've had a psychosis” (Interview 2).

Those in the staff group who motivate and show respect for patients feel that they are being ostracized by other colleagues who are restrictive and reprimanding. Many feel that it is even more important to respect patients as they are now deprived of their freedom than before. Nursing staff feel that material things have no value and that what is fundamental is the way each nursing staff treats patients. If each patient encounter is not based on or lacks respect, consideration, knowledge, genuine presence, good care, or a professional approach, life becomes undignified for patients. The work performed should lead to every patient feeling as good as possible.

“The physical environment is very important but that's not all. One's approach. If you can't do that then it doesn't help if it's been a luxury hotel, then it's inhuman anyway” (Interview 3).

Discussion

The findings showed that the nursing staff experienced that the move to new purpose-built premises resulted in both benefits and new challenges. The updated physical environment contributed to a greater sense of calm and well-being among patients, who now had more opportunities for privacy as well as access to a variety of spaces. Staff described how these changes allowed patients to withdraw when needed, helping them manage emotional states and reducing the frequency of conflicts. These observations align with earlier research emphasising the importance of designing care environments that promotes opportunities for patients to regulate social interactions, as the feeling of intrusion into personal space provokes stress that can lead to aggressive behaviour in patients (Bodryzlova et al., 2024; Ulrich et al., 2018). Having a private room and bedroom was experienced as creating opportunities for being able to reconnect with oneself and having a sense of worth in a study focusing on patients' experiences of a new forensic psychiatric care environment (Olausson et al., 2021). Furthermore, crowding of patients in forensic and general psychiatric care has also been found to negatively impact both patients and staff by, for example, fewer contacts and more use of restraints (Bodryzlova et al., 2024), thus illustrating the importance of space and privacy in a forensic psychiatric care environment.

On the other hand, our study also found that the modern and secure design introduced new tensions. Staff described the environment as clean and orderly but also sterile and emotionally distant, which complicated efforts to create a warm and relational atmosphere. This duality reflects the ongoing challenge of designing spaces that are both safe and supportive. Research suggests that a home-like care environment can reduce feelings of stigma, patient stress, and communicate a sense of dignity (Bodryzlova et al., 2024). Marshall et al. (2019) further emphasize that staff perceptions of safety are closely tied to relational factors such as teamwork, support, and the quality of staff–patient interactions. Their findings suggest that emotional and relational safety, together with physical safety, plays a critical role in shaping therapeutic environments. This further highlights the need to balance institutional safety with emotional and relational warmth in forensic psychiatric care settings.

The findings of this study complement previous studies by highlighting the importance of patient autonomy, freedom, and decision making in creating a supportive care environment. Freedom can for example be stimulated when patients feel empowered to make decisions about their situation on the ward, and when combined with the undemanding support of staff it can lead to a feeling of increased well-being and enables a strengthening of mutual trust (Olausson et al., 2021). Patient participation and autonomy can be challenging to support in coercive settings, such as forensic psychiatric care. However, participation can be stimulated in an attentive and human-ward atmosphere and ward culture (Söderberg et al., 2022). The study's findings indicate that nursing staff experience that patients are given more autonomy by being allowed the opportunity to control their personal environment, seek mental recovery, and have social interaction on their own initiative. This aligns with findings from Rodríguez-Labajos et al. (2024), who highlight that control over environmental factors such as temperature, lighting, and privacy contributes to a greater sense of autonomy and control in psychiatric care settings. Such autonomy in everyday choices not only supports emotional regulation but also lays the foundation for more active participation in care. As Watson (2008) argues, patient involvement in decision-making and mutual understanding are central to building trusting relationships between nursing staff and patients. Our study indicates that nursing staff can benefit from a care environment and culture that promotes patient autonomy but that the nursing staff’s diverse opinions on how to follow routines and ward rules might create challenges that influence the care and atmosphere on the ward. This highlights the importance of not only relying on a new physical design to improve care but also to continuously reflect on and have a dialogue about the care culture and organisation of the ward.

The study findings also show a high level of dissatisfaction with reduced staff interaction in the newly built ward. The nursing staff emphasized that their physical presence was essential and that the lack of presence resulted in reduced security and increased anxiety for the patients. Nursing staff shortages and stress were experienced, resulting in reduced attention and responsiveness. Previous research in general psychiatric care highlights the disappointment of nursing staff when they have a large workload, a pressurized work environment, and little time for patient-centred care. The nursing staff wished for more time and opportunities to be available for and to confirm patients (Molin et al., 2021). The nursing staff in our study expressed a desire for more time and opportunities to be present with patients and to affirm their needs and experiences. As Molin (2016) also notes, everyday social interactions, such as relaxed conversations and shared laughter, can strengthen patients’ abilities and further support their recovery.

Our study also found that nursing staff who were able to build caring relationships was a crucial aspect of the care environment. A positive association between the quality of the caring relationship and the genuine presence and supportive capacity of caregivers is presented in research by Woodward et al. (2017), who further maintained that care environments that provide a sense of safety and security, where staff are available, supportive, and take time to converse with patients, increases patients’ self-esteem and willingness to participate in their care. Research has also found that the nature of caring relationships is associated with patient satisfaction with forensic psychiatric services (Bressingtion et al., 2011). These findings altogether underscore that relational aspects of care can be foundational for patient recovery and engagement in forensic psychiatric settings.

Our findings further show that the nursing staff experience that the new premises provide fewer opportunities for patients to prepare for life after discharge. Shorter treatment periods, combined with limited possibilities to observe patients' everyday behaviours, were perceived to hinder effective discharge planning. This was particularly evident for patients with substance use disorders, where staff noted an increase in readmissions due to insufficient time for preparation and support. Similar aspects have also been expressed as important in a recent study with forensic psychiatric patients with substance use disorder, where patients lacked support for preparation for return to a life without substance abuse or criminality (Svensson et al., 2024). Having the opportunity to feel more confident and empowered and desiring normality has also been found to be important by patients in forensic psychiatric care (Lumén et al., 2024), illustrating the importance of being prepared for discharge and giving opportunities to grow.

Furthermore, our results indicate that discharge planning was experienced as more challenging in the new environment. The physical design and shorter admissions limited the depth of interaction and assessment, making it harder to tailor discharge plans to individual needs. This finding aligns with previous research where the discharge process in various psychiatric contexts has been found to affect the patient's adaptability after discharge from the ward (Brenisin et al., 2023; Wright et al., 2017). Research has emphasized the importance of support and patient participation during the transition from the ward to different environments and when planning for discharge (Breninsin et al., 2023; Wrightel et al., 2017). These studies also underscore the importance of patient participation and structured support during the transition from inpatient care to life outside the ward.

Methodological considerations

A limitation of this study is that patients were not interviewed, which means their direct perspectives regarding the new facilities are not represented in the findings. The sample in this study was limited to nursing staff at a Swedish forensic psychiatric hospital, which might limit their transferability to other countries and hospitals. However, the sample had adequate variation with nursing staff from different wards with various professional backgrounds and experiences. Furthermore, detailed descriptions of the changes entailed with the new purpose-built setting and the rich data underlying the findings of this study enable others to transfer the findings to other situations and settings. A strength of this study is the timing of data collection, which was conducted nine months after the move to the new premises. This timeframe enabled the nursing staff to have time to build a reflective approach to the move, with opportunities to convey experiences relating to work processes and care that might change during the first time after the move. Another strength is the analytical process, which comprehensively followed the process of thematic analysis, as described by Braun and Clarke (2006, 2019). The methodological principles of openness and reflective attitude guided the authors during the data collection, analysis, and presentation of results (Sundler et al., 2019). A collaborative analytical process took place, where the first author continuously discussed the analysis with the last author, and in the finalising stages the sub-themes and themes were discussed in the entire author group.

Conclusion

Relocating to new purpose-built forensic psychiatric premises has many advantages; however, improvements in the physical design and environment are not sufficient for supporting health and recovery. The physical environment could be beneficial by offering space and privacy for both patients and staff, while simultaneously providing opportunities to interact and for nursing staff to provide an overview of the ward. The interior design may benefit from being modern, clean, and safe but could be less sterile. Although a new and modern physical environment is available, having resources and opportunities to build human relationships that can support patient participation and recovery seems crucial. Being able to provide care in accordance with ethical and holistic values and supporting patient learning and growth might require a re-focus on staff recruitment, staff training, and arranging opportunities for staff reflection.
