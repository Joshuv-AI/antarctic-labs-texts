Expression and prognostic value of MCM2 in type B thymomas
Authors: Xin Du, Jian Cui, Xintao Yu, Dingfang Cao, Ying Zhang, Lei Yu, Shanqing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThymic epithelial tumors (TETs) are the most common mediastinal malignancies, lacking reliable prognostic biomarkers. This study aimed to identify a potential prognostic marker for TETs.MethodsmRNA microarray analysis of 30 tumor and peritumoral tissues identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Hub genes were selected via protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis, with MCM2 chosen as the target gene. Survival and enrichment analyses were performed, and a validation cohort assessed MCM2’s association with prognosis and clinicopathological features.ResultsSeven hundred thirty-four DEGs were identified, with MCM2 significantly associated with prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) (HR = 0.17; 95% CI: 0.05–0.54; p = 0.003) and identified as an independent risk factor for PFS (HR = 0.26; 95% CI: 0.08–0.91; p = 0.035). MCM2 expression decreased from type B1 to B3 thymomas.ConclusionsMCM2 is an independent prognostic factor for TETs, with higher expression linked to better PFS and decreasing expression across B-type thymomas, suggesting its role as a favorable prognostic marker.

Introduction

Thymic epithelial tumor (TET) is the most common primary anterior mediastinal tumors which derived from thymic epithelium. The incidence of TETs is estimated to be 1.5 per million a year [1]. In the 5th edition of the WHO classification of thoracic tumors, thymic tumors are mainly divided into thymoma (including type A, AB, B1, B2, B3 thymoma, micronodular thymoma with lymphoid stroma, metaplastic thymoma and lipofibroadenoma), thymic carcinoma (TC) and thymic neuroendocrine neoplasms (TNN) according to the histological features [2]. TCs and TNNs are far rarer than thymomas and are much more likely to metastasize. Nearly one third of thymomas are associated with systemic autoimmune syndromes, the most common of which is myasthenia gravis (MG) [3]. Previous researches showed that TNM stages, completeness of resection and histologic types were the most significant clinicopathological factors which have been identified to be associated with prognosis of TETs [4, 5].

The pathogeny of TETs is under intensive studied. Only general transcription factor 2I (GTF2I) mutation has been detected and confirmed in most of type A and AB thymoma [6]. Until now, prognostic biomarkers for TETs have remained undiscovered. Therefore, it is urgent to find a reliable biomarker that can predict the prognosis of TETs. In this study, we tried to dig out hub genes based on the mRNA microarray analysis and bioinformatics analysis, and explore a new prognostic biomarker of TETs.

Methods and materials

Sample datasets and processing

In this study, the chip sequencing datasets were collected at Beijing Tongren Hospital (Myasthenia Gravis & Thymoma Diagnosis and Treatment Center, Capital Medical University). The study was approved by the Beijing Tongren Hospital Ethics Committees. The 30 fresh tumor and peritumoral tissue specimens used for analysis were all obtained from patients who were initially diagnosed and underwent surgery at Beijing Tongren Hospital, without receiving any neoadjuvant treatments such as radiotherapy or chemotherapy prior to surgery. The tumor tissue specimens were obtained from viable solid areas near the core of the tumor, avoiding regions of necrosis, hemorrhage, calcification, or fibrosis, with a minimum required tumor volume of no less than 1 cm³. The peritumoral tissue specimens were obtained from relatively normal thymic tissue located 1–2 cm from the tumor margin. The mRNA microarray analysis was provided by CapitalBio Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). At first, the total RNA was extracted from the fresh frozen tissues by using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, California, USA) and purified with mirVana miRNA Isolation Kit (Ambion, Texas, USA) respectively. Next, the RNAs were amplificated and labeled by using CapitalBio cRNA Amplification and Labeling Kit (CapitalBio, Beijing, China). Then, microarrays were hybridized in an Agilent Hybridization Oven overnight. Finally, The RNA array data were analyzed for data summarization, normalization and quality control by using the GeneSpring software V13.0 (Agilent, California, USA).

Differentially expressed gene screening

The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between thymoma tissue and peritumoral thymic tissue were analyzed by limma package of R software. The significantly up-regulated and down-regulated genes were screened on the following standards: |log2 fold change|>1 and p-value < 0.01. According to the identified DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways analysis were performed on a free online platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn). The threshold criterion was a p-value < 0.01.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction

The online database STRING (https://cn.string-db.org/) was utilized to identify the relevant PPI for the key genes. In the PPI network, confidence score > 0.7 was considered as statistically significant proteins. Cytoscape (version 3.9.0) was used to visualize and analyze molecular interaction of PPI network. The Cytoscape plug-in Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) was employed to analyze the highly interconnected clusters with criteria as follows: degree cutoff = 2, node score cutoff = 0.2, K-score = 2 and Max depth = 100. The Cytoscape plug-in CytoHubba (version 0.1) was used for screen out hub genes by the three common algorithms (MCC, Degree, Closeness), and the common genes were considered as hub genes by the intersect function.

Survival analysis and enrichment analysis of hub genes

The Kaplan-Meier-plotter database (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) was used to analysis the potential prognostic value of the hub genes, in which the hub genes were divided into two groups based on the level of RNA transcript level and median value. The hazard ratio (HR) with 95%CI and the log-rank p-value was marked and calculated. Furthermore, GO and KEGG pathways enrichment analysis of the hub genes were performed on the free online platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn).

Collected clinicopathological data of validated cohort

The total of 167 patients with TET undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) at Beijing Tongren Hospital (Myasthenia Gravis & Thymoma Diagnosis and Treatment Center, Capital Medical University) from January 2005 to January 2015 were selected. The clinicopathological data of gender, age, thymoma-associated MG, completeness of resection, histologic type, TNM stage and PFS were reviewed. The criteria of the histologic type and TNM stage classification were based on the 5th edition of the WHO classification of thoracic tumors [2]. The study was granted by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital (Registration NO. TRECKY2021-175).

Constructed tissue microarrays (TMAs) and assessed by immunofluorescence double-labeling (IDL)

The TMAs of 167 tissue specimens were constructed following the standard methods [7]. First, tumor cells rich region as judged in hematoxylin and eosin-stained tissue sections were taken from the paraffin-embedded tissue specimens loaded into 0.6 mm cylinders and transferred to recipient block for TMAs. Then, the TMAs containing 5-µm sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated in graded alcohols. TMAs were staining the sections with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for pathologic assessment. For the IDL, sections were incubated with rat monoclonal anti-CK19 antibody (ab7754, diluted 1:200) and rabbit monoclonal anti-MCM2 antibody (ab108935, diluted 1:100) from Abcam plc (Cambridge, UK).

The assessment of the TMAs was blindly scored by two independent pathologists. The MCM2 expression score was calculated based on staining intensity and percentage of MCM2-positive thymic epithelial cells. The staining intensity was scored as 0 = negative, 1 = weak, 2 = moderate and 3 = strong. The percentage was scored as 0 for 0–5% MCM2-positive thymic epithelial cells, 1 for 6–25%, 2 for 26–50%, 3 for 51–75% and 4 for 76–100%. The MCM2 expression score was obtained by multiplying intensity by percentage of positive cells. The MCM2 expression was scored as G0 (negative) IHC score 0–2, G1 3–5, G2 6–8 and G3 9–12.

Statistical analysis

Statistics were performed using SPSS v.27.0. The date of Kaplan-Meier curve was presented as mean PFS and 95% CI. Log-rank test was used to compare differences of Kaplan-Meier curves, and the significance was set at p < 0.05. Cox regression model was used to research the risk factors of PFS by univariate and multivariate analysis. Data are presented as HR, 95% CI and p value. Significance was set at p < 0.05 two tailed. Kruskal-Wallis H test was used to analyze the association between the target gene and independent risk factors. Significance was also set at p < 0.05 two tail.

Results

Identification of DEGs in TETs

The chip sequencing datasets included 30 specimens of tumor tissue and peritumoral thymic tissue, including 5 of type AB, 6 of type B1, 12 of type B2, and 7 of type B3. In the mRNA microarray, 34,000 mRNA probes mapped more than 7000 mRNA. There were 734 DEGs (255 up-regulated and 479 down-regulated genes) in TETs were indeed differentially expressed between thymoma and peritumoral thymic tissue (|log2 fold change | >1 and p-value < 0.01, Fig. 1A, B, Table S2).

Functional enrichment and pathway analysis of DEGs

The biological classification of DEGs was performed by functional and pathway enrichment analyses. GO analysis result showed that changes in biological process (BP) of DEGs were mainly enriched in regulation of angiogenesis, epithelial cell proliferation, extracellular matrix organization, extracellular structure organization, regulation of epithelial cell proliferation, regulation of vasculature development, and so on (Fig. 1C). The cellular component (CC) analysis of DEGs were significantly enriched in collagen containing extracellular matrix. In addition, centromeric region, condensed chromosome, condensed chromosome kinetochore and kinetochore were included (Fig. 1D). The molecular function (MF) analysis of DEGs were mainly enriched in extracellular matrix structural constituent, glycosaminoglycan-binding protein, heparin binding, sulfur compound binding, and so on (Fig. 1E). KEGG pathway analyses were mainly involved in malaria, complement and coagulation cascades, Hematopoietic cell lineage, primary immunodeficiency, cell cycle, and so on (Fig. 1F).

PPI network construction and hub genes selection

The 734 DEGs were constructed into PPI network by the STRING online database, which contains 487 nodes and 2785 edges. By the plug-in MCODE of Cytoscape, we revealed the most significant module of interconnected subcluster (Fig. 1G). Using the plug-in CytoHubba of Cytoscape, we screened out 9 hub genes, including MCM2, CDK1, MYC, CCNB1, CCNB2, TOP2A, CCNA2, NCAPG and IL6, according to three common algorithms (MCC, Degree, Closeness, Fig. 1H).

Target gene identification

The prognostic value of the 9 hub genes was evaluated scientifically by Kaplan-Meier-Ploter, including 118 thymoma patients’ RNA-seq. Among the 9 hub genes, 7 genes, including MCM2, CDK1, CCNB1, CCNB2, TOP2A, CCNA2 and NCAPG have a close correlation with prognosis (p < 0.05; Fig. 2A). The results of GO enrichment analysis showed that the 9 hub genes were mainly about DNA replication in biological process, cellular component and molecular function (Fig. 2B, C). The KEGG pathways analysis for the 9 hub genes were mainly involved in cell cycle (Fig. 2D, E). MCM2, as a DNA replication licensing protein, plays an important role in both DNA replication and the cell cycle [8]. In addition, through extensive review of the literature related to these 7 genes associated with thymoma, we found that previous studies had demonstrated that MCM2-deficient mice developed cancer, and all the MCM2def/def mice on the 129 Sv background died of thymic tumor [9]. Besides, another study showed that reducing MCM2 expression to one-third of normal levels caused stem cell deficiency and tumorigenesis in mice, and the mice died of thymic tumor were always accompanied with rapid shallow respiration and generalized muscle weakness, which were similar to the symptoms of MG [10]. Therefore, we selected MCM2 as the target gene for this study.

Survival analysis

We have been continuously following up the survival of all these 167 patients. The mean onset age was 51.01 ± 11.82 years, and the age was grouped by median age of 52. Up to January 2023, 16 patients (9.58%) appeared with tumor progress and 19 patients (11.38%) were dead, of whom 5 died of TETs or MG, and 14 died of other diseases. A total of 12 (7.19%) patients were lost follow-up. The mean PFS was 150.65 months (95% CI: 141.74-159.55) and the 1-, 5- 10-year PFS rate were 99%, 86%, and 77%, respectively (Fig. 4A).

Univariate and multivariate analysis of potential prognostic factors

These covariates with a p-value of less than 0.2 at univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis, except completeness of resection which is determined by TNM stage (Table 2). The results showed that age (HR = 2.40; 95% CI: 1.14–5.03; p = 0.021), TNM stage (HR = 2.49; 95% CI: 1.11–5.57; p = 0.027), histologic type (HR = 4.06; 95% CI: 0.88–18.68 and HR = 8.53; 95% CI: 1.66–43.84; p = 0.017) and MCM2 expression (HR = 0.26; 95% CI: 0.08–0.91; p = 0.035) were all the independent risk factors for PFS (Table 2).

In order to explore the relationship between MCM2 expression and these independent risk factors of clinicopathologic features, we analyzed these data with Kruskal-Wallis H test (Table 3). There was no statistical difference of the expression of MCM2 between Age (H = 3.58; p = 0.059). The MCM2 expression was statistical difference in the groups of TNM stages (H = 18.66; p < 0.001), but there was no significant statistical difference between I and II (mean rank 96.93 vs. 88.94; p = 1.000), II and III (mean rank 88.94 vs. 68.77; p = 0.480), III and IV (mean rank 68.77 vs. 61.04; p = 1.000). The MCM2 expression was statistically different in the groups of Histologic types (H = 66.01; p < 0.001), and the mean rank of B1 was higher than B2 (mean rank 125.65 vs. 89.21; p = 0.004), B2 was higher than B3 (mean rank 89.21 vs. 56.27; p = 0.008). Therefore, the levels of MCM2 expression gradually decreased from type B1 to B3 thymoma. Meanwhile, the PFS declined gradually from type B1 to B3 thymomas (HR = 3.04; 95% CI: 0.68–13.62 and HR = 5.82; 95% CI: 1.29–26.35; p = 0.047; Fig. 4I).

Discussion

TETs are histologically heterogeneous tumors of thymic epithelial cells origin. The exact molecular basis underlying tumorigenesis of TETs still remains elusive. This leads to a lack of prognostic biomarkers for TETs in clinic. In this study, we analyzed 30 specimens of TETs by mRNA microarray and detected 734 DEGs. All the DEGs were constructed into PPI network, and then, we selected the most 9 significant hub genes in thymoma including MCM2, CDK1, MYC, CCNB1, CCNB2, TOP2A, CCNA2, NCAPG and IL6. By GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, we found, as a DNA replication licensing protein, MCM2 involved multiple biological processes, including cell cycle and DNA replication. Furthermore, Kaplan-Meier-plotter database indicated that MCM2 is also a prognosis-related gene. Therefore, MCM2 was selected as the target gene for prognosis of TETs in this study. The result of the validation cohort analysis indicated that age, completeness of resection, histologic type, TNM stage and MCM2 expression were associated with prognosis of TETs in univariate analysis. In multivariate analysis, age, histologic type, TNM stage and MCM2 expression were all the independent risk factors for TETs. By analyzing the relationship between MCM2 expression and other independent risk factors, we found that levels of MCM2 expression were gradually decreased from type B1 to B3 thymoma. Therefore, the expression of MCM2 may be a prognostic biomarker in type B thymoma.

Previously, many researchers have also been trying to seek the prognostic biomarkers for TETs. Girard et al. reported that the high expressed level of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) was significantly associated with stage III to IV thymic tumors [12]. However, this conclusion is challenged by Ionescu et al. [13]. Similarly, Zucali’s study demonstrated that the expression of insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor (IGF1R) was associated with the worse prognosis of thymoma [14], which is in contrast with Girard’s results [15]. In addition, vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGF), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER-2) and P53 have also been proposed to be as the prognostic biomarkers for TETs. However, their potential clinicopathological factors affecting prognosis have not been confirmed, so they all lacked the sufficient evidences to be the prognostic biomarker for TETs [16–19].

MCM2 is a component of the minichromosome maintenance proteins 2–7, which bind to chromatin as replicative helicases, controlling of DNA replication and cell cycle [8]. As a DNA replication licensing protein, high expression of MCM2 has been shown to be associated with poor prognosis in various types of cancer, such as breast cancer [20, 21], non-small cell lung cancer [22], prostate cancer [23], renal cell cancer [24, 25], bladder canrcer [26], oral squamous cell carcinoma [27] and so on. This is generally believed to be due to the role of MCM2 in promoting tumor cell proliferation. Interestingly, our study found that high MCM2 expression in thymic epithelial tumors was associated with a favorable prognosis. This finding is contrary to what has been observed in other cancers but is not without scientific basis. Previous studies have shown that reduced MCM2 expression can act as a driver of tumorigenesis in certain cancers [28]. Nearly all MCM2def/def mice on the 129 Sv and NHD13 + background developed thymic leukemia/lymphoma [10, 29], while MCM2def/def mice with a mixed genetic background developed not only thymic tumors but also liver tumors, lung tumors, and spleen involvement [9]. This may be due to the precise regulation of MCM2 levels in DNA replication. In fact, cells produce a much higher quantity of MCMs than is needed for the formation of the Cdc45/Mcm2-7/GINS (CMG) helicase complex during DNA replication. Some studies have suggested that this excess of MCMs helps to suppress the replication speed in eukaryotic cells, thereby enhancing genomic stability. Consequently, reduced levels of MCMs can lead to DNA strand breaks, checkpoint activation, genomic instability, and apoptosis, ultimately increasing the likelihood of tumor formation [30–34].

Although this study confirmed the association between MCM2 expression in TETs and prognosis using IHC in a validation cohort, and speculated that the underlying mechanism might be related to the precise regulation of MCM2 levels during DNA replication, it is important to note that gene transcription levels are not equivalent to protein expression levels, and protein expression levels do not necessarily reflect functional activity. Therefore, the exact biological mechanism by which MCM2 influences the initiation and progression of TETs remains unknown. Nevertheless, this finding offers a new direction for further investigation, which requires validation in both cellular and animal models. We look forward to breakthroughs in the development of cell and animal models for TETs will be achieved soon, providing a foundation for subsequent mechanistic studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, MCM2 maybe a prognostic biomarker in TETs, and its high expression was a favorable prognostic factor for TETs, especially in type B thymoma. The discovery could contribute to evaluate the prognosis of type B thymomas, and may be help to investigate the pathogenesis and target therapy of TETs.
