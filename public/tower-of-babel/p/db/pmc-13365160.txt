Case 24: A 42-Year-Old Woman With Fever and Shock
Authors: Sangjun Bae, Jeongmin Lee, Jeonghoon Ha, Dong-Jun Lim, Jaekyung Lee
Published: 2026

CASE PRESENTATION

Dr. Sangjun Bae: A 42-year-old woman with a history of hypertension presented with dyspnea and a high-grade fever (38.7°C). Upon arrival, she was in a state of shock (blood pressure 80/73 mmHg, heart rate 180 beats/min). Laboratory findings revealed leukocytosis (16,340/µL), elevated C-reactive protein (6.09 mg/dL), and markedly elevated procalcitonin (52.29 ng/mL).

Transthoracic echocardiography showed severe global left ventricular dysfunction with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 12%. Despite aggressive fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support, the patient developed refractory cardiogenic shock requiring veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) and continuous renal replacement therapy for metabolic acidosis (pH 7.13, lactate 7.7 mmol/L).1,2

Empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics were initiated; however, repeated blood and urine cultures remained negative. Notably, procalcitonin levels declined rapidly, whereas the fever and leukocytosis persisted.

Computed tomography (CT) revealed a 6.7-cm heterogeneous mass in the right adrenal gland. Biochemical evaluation demonstrated markedly elevated catecholamines (urine metanephrine 14,306 µg/day; reference < 315 µg/day) and severe adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)-dependent hypercortisolism (ACTH > 4,000 pg/mL, serum cortisol 122.4 µg/dL).3,4,5,6

A high-dose dexamethasone suppression test (8 mg) resulted in a paradoxical rise in ACTH (from 113.7 to 459.8 pg/mL).7,8

After hemodynamic stabilization with alpha-blockade and ketoconazole, a laparoscopic right adrenalectomy was performed. Histopathology confirmed a pheochromocytoma with positive immunostaining for ACTH.

Postoperatively, the patient developed severe pancytopenia and hypotension. Cytomegalovirus (CMV) polymerase chain reaction revealed profound viremia (737,534 copies/mL). She was successfully treated with ganciclovir and stress-dose hydrocortisone, leading to a full recovery. Follow-up echocardiography showed normalization of the LVEF to 53.4%. CLINICAL IMPRESSION

Dr. Sangjun Bae: At presentation, the patient exhibited clinical features highly suggestive of septic shock complicated by fulminant myocarditis or septic cardiomyopathy. The extremely elevated procalcitonin level, leukocytosis, fever, and circulatory collapse strongly supported an infectious etiology. However, the absence of positive cultures and the dissociation between declining procalcitonin levels and persistent fever raised suspicion of a non-infectious inflammatory or endocrine cause. The identification of a large adrenal mass subsequently shifted the diagnostic focus toward a hormonally active adrenal tumor. IMAGE PRESENTATION

Dr. Jaekyung Lee: Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a 6.7-cm heterogeneous right adrenal mass with areas of internal necrosis. Fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography (PET)/CT showed increased uptake within the adrenal mass and diffuse brown fat activation in the neck and mediastinum, a characteristic manifestation of catecholamine excess. Echocardiography revealed severe global hypokinesia, which was consistent with stress-induced or catecholamine-mediated cardiomyopathy rather than a focal ischemic injury. Furthermore, fluorine-18 fluoro-L-dihydroxyphenylalanine (18F-FDOPA) PET/CT demonstrated intense tracer uptake confined to the adrenal mass, providing functional confirmation of a catecholamine-producing neuroendocrine tumor. Pathological findings

Dr. Jeonghoon Ha: Histologic examination demonstrated tumor cells arranged in a classic zellballen architectural pattern. Immunohistochemical staining was positive for chromogranin and synaptophysin, confirming a pheochromocytoma. Crucially, the tumor cells demonstrated strong cytoplasmic immunoreactivity for ACTH, firmly establishing the diagnosis of an ACTH-producing pheochromocytoma. Differential diagnosis

Dr. Sangjun Bae: This 42-year-old woman presented with fever, leukocytosis, shock, and severe left ventricular dysfunction. The initial clinical impression was strongly indicative of septic shock with septic cardiomyopathy or fulminant myocarditis. Markedly elevated procalcitonin further corroborated a presumed infectious etiology. However, repeated negative cultures and the rapid decline in procalcitonin levels despite persistent fever and leukocytosis raised substantial doubts regarding an ongoing bacterial infection.

Shock with severe left ventricular dysfunction

The differential diagnosis for cardiogenic shock with global left ventricular hypokinesia includes acute myocarditis, septic cardiomyopathy, stress-induced (Takotsubo) cardiomyopathy, and catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy. The absence of focal wall motion abnormalities and the subsequent dramatic recovery of cardiac function favored a reversible, stress-related process over ischemic heart disease. Given the profound tachycardia and labile hemodynamics, an endocrine cause driven by catecholamine excess was heavily considered.

Hyperinflammatory state mimicking sepsis

Although procalcitonin is widely used as a biomarker of bacterial infection, non-infectious conditions such as major surgery, trauma, and certain tumors can also cause marked elevations.9 In this patient, the dissociation between declining procalcitonin levels and persistent systemic inflammatory features suggested a cytokine-driven inflammatory state rather than uncontrolled sepsis.

Adrenal mass with endocrine abnormalities

The identification of a large adrenal mass on CT shifted the diagnostic focus toward a hormonally active adrenal tumor. Biochemical testing revealed massive catecholamine excess, consistent with pheochromocytoma. Concurrently, severe ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism was identified, indicating an ectopic source of ACTH rather than primary adrenal cortisol overproduction.

Source of ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism

The major differential diagnoses for ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism include Cushing disease (pituitary adenoma) and ectopic ACTH syndrome.10 The presence of a large adrenal mass and extremely elevated catecholamine levels strongly suggested pheochromocytoma as the culprit tumor. Furthermore, a paradoxical rise in ACTH after high-dose dexamethasone suppression provided additional support for an ectopic ACTH-producing tumor expressing glucocorticoid receptors, a phenomenon reported in some pheochromocytomas.7,8

Endocrine crisis mimicking septic shock

Dr. Jeongmin Lee: Several endocrine emergencies can present clinical features that closely resemble septic shock, including fever, leukocytosis, hypotension, and multiorgan dysfunction. Among these conditions, pheochromocytoma crisis represents a particularly challenging diagnostic entity because its manifestations overlap substantially with severe infection or systemic inflammatory states. Massive catecholamine release may trigger profound cardiovascular instability, including tachyarrhythmia, vasoconstriction, and acute myocardial dysfunction, which can lead to cardiogenic or mixed shock.

In addition to direct hemodynamic effects, catecholamine excess can induce a systemic inflammatory response. Elevated circulating catecholamines stimulate the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α, which may result in fever, leukocytosis, and markedly elevated inflammatory biomarkers.11 Consequently, patients with pheochromocytoma crisis may initially be misdiagnosed with septic shock, particularly when procalcitonin levels are markedly increased. Although procalcitonin is widely used as a biomarker for bacterial infection, several noninfectious conditions—including major surgery, trauma, and certain malignancies—can also cause substantial elevations.9

Another important endocrine condition that may mimic septic shock is severe hypercortisolism due to ectopic ACTH secretion. Excess cortisol exerts profound immunomodulatory effects and predisposes patients to opportunistic infections while simultaneously masking typical inflammatory responses.12,13,14 In rare cases such as ACTH-producing pheochromocytoma, simultaneous catecholamine excess and hypercortisolism create a complex clinical picture characterized by both cardiovascular collapse and immune dysregulation.

Recognition of endocrine disorders as potential causes of shock is therefore critical when patients with presumed sepsis fail to respond to appropriate antimicrobial therapy. The presence of unexplained adrenal masses, extreme catecholamine elevations, or paradoxical biochemical findings should prompt consideration of pheochromocytoma crisis and related endocrine emergencies.

FINAL DIAGNOSIS

Dr. Sangjun Bae: ACTH-producing pheochromocytoma presenting as septic shock–like cardiogenic collapse with severe catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy and ectopic Cushing’s syndrome.

GENERAL INTRODUCTION OF THE DISEASE MANAGEMENT

Dr. Dong-Jun Lim: Pheochromocytomas are catecholamine-producing tumors arising from chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla. A small subset of these tumors produces ACTH, resulting in ectopic Cushing’s syndrome.3,4,5,6 In such cases, patients experience a unique “double endocrine crisis,” characterized by simultaneous catecholamine excess and severe hypercortisolism. Excess catecholamines can cause vasoconstriction, tachyarrhythmia, myocardial stunning, and stress-induced cardiomyopathy, while hypercortisolism contributes to immunosuppression, metabolic alkalosis, muscle weakness, and increased susceptibility to infection.

Preoperative management of ACTH-producing pheochromocytoma requires careful control of both hormonal axes. Alpha-adrenergic blockade with agents such as phenoxybenzamine or selective alpha-1 blockers is essential to prevent perioperative hypertensive crisis. Adequate volume expansion is also required because chronic catecholamine excess leads to contracted plasma volume. In patients with severe hypercortisolism, steroidogenesis inhibitors such as ketoconazole, metyrapone, or etomidate may be used to reduce cortisol levels and improve metabolic stability prior to surgery.

Definitive treatment is surgical adrenalectomy. However, perioperative management is particularly challenging in ACTH-producing pheochromocytoma because abrupt withdrawal of cortisol after tumor removal may lead to acute adrenal insufficiency. Therefore, perioperative glucocorticoid replacement is mandatory and should be gradually tapered according to recovery of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis.

In addition, clinicians should anticipate immune reconstitution phenomena after correction of severe hypercortisolism. Chronic cortisol excess suppresses cellular immunity, and rapid normalization of cortisol levels may unmask latent infections or trigger inflammatory responses.12,15 Careful monitoring for opportunistic infections is therefore recommended during the postoperative period.

PROGNOSIS

Dr. Jaekyung Lee: The long-term prognosis of pheochromocytoma depends primarily on tumor behavior, completeness of resection, and the presence of metastatic disease. Most pheochromocytomas are benign, and surgical resection is curative in the majority of localized cases. However, recurrence or metastasis may occur even years after initial surgery, and long-term biochemical and imaging surveillance is therefore recommended.

In ACTH-producing pheochromocytoma, prognosis is influenced not only by tumor biology but also by the systemic consequences of severe hypercortisolism. Chronic cortisol excess is associated with increased cardiovascular risk, metabolic complications, and susceptibility to infection. Prompt surgical removal of the tumor can reverse many of these effects, but perioperative morbidity may be substantial in patients presenting with endocrine crisis.

Catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy, as observed in this case, is typically reversible after removal of the tumor. Recovery of left ventricular function usually occurs within weeks to months once catecholamine excess is eliminated. The dramatic improvement in cardiac function in this patient supports the diagnosis of stress-induced cardiomyopathy rather than irreversible myocardial damage.

An important prognostic consideration unique to patients with severe ectopic Cushing’s syndrome is the risk of postoperative adrenal insufficiency and immune reconstitution–related complications. Abrupt normalization of cortisol levels after tumor resection may restore immune function, potentially leading to inflammatory reactions or reactivation of latent infections, as illustrated by the development of CMV viremia in this patient.12,15 Careful postoperative endocrine and infectious monitoring is therefore essential.

Overall, with complete tumor resection and appropriate perioperative management, patients with ACTH-producing pheochromocytoma can achieve good long-term outcomes. Nevertheless, lifelong follow-up with periodic biochemical testing for catecholamine excess and clinical evaluation for recurrence is recommended.

DISCUSSION

Dr. Sangjun Bae: This case demonstrates how endocrine emergencies can closely mimic septic shock. The patient presented with fever, leukocytosis, circulatory collapse, and markedly elevated procalcitonin, all of which strongly suggested severe bacterial infection. However, the absence of microbiologic evidence and the rapid decline in procalcitonin despite persistent inflammatory signs suggested a non-infectious inflammatory state. Catecholamine excess has been shown to stimulate cytokine release, including interleukin-6, which may explain the extreme elevation of procalcitonin observed in this patient.9,11

Dr. Jaekyung Lee: From a cardiovascular standpoint, the profound but reversible left ventricular dysfunction is characteristic of catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy. Excess catecholamines exert direct myocardial toxicity through calcium overload, oxidative stress, and microvascular spasm. The dramatic recovery of cardiac function after tumor removal supports myocardial stunning rather than irreversible structural damage. Recognition of this mechanism is crucial because cardiac function may normalize completely once catecholamine excess is corrected.

Dr. Dong-Jun Lim: A particularly instructive feature of this case is the coexistence of severe hypercortisolism due to ectopic ACTH production. ACTH-producing pheochromocytoma is rare, but when present, it creates a “double endocrine crisis.”3,4,5,6,10 Hypercortisolism contributes to immunosuppression, hyperglycemia, muscle weakness, and increased infection risk, while catecholamine excess leads to cardiovascular instability. The paradoxical rise in ACTH during high-dose dexamethasone suppression further highlights the complex tumor biology. Some pheochromocytomas express glucocorticoid receptors, and dexamethasone may paradoxically stimulate ACTH secretion in these tumors rather than suppress it.7,8

Dr. Jaekyung Lee: Functional imaging provided additional diagnostic insight. FDG PET demonstrated a metabolically active adrenal mass along with diffuse brown adipose tissue activation, a recognized marker of catecholamine excess. In contrast, F-18 FDOPA PET/CT showed intense uptake confined to the adrenal lesion, reflecting increased catecholamine precursor transport and decarboxylation activity. This complementary imaging approach helped confirm the neuroendocrine origin of the tumor and supported the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma in a critically ill patient.

Dr. Jeonghoon Ha: The postoperative course also carries important teaching points. Long-standing hypercortisolism suppresses cellular immunity, and abrupt correction after tumor removal may lead to immune reconstitution phenomena. In this patient, CMV reactivation likely resulted from restoration of immune responsiveness after rapid cortisol normalization. Clinicians should therefore maintain a high index of suspicion for opportunistic infections in the postoperative period of severe Cushing’s syndrome.12,13,14,15

Dr. Dong-Jun Lim: Overall, this case underscores the importance of considering endocrine causes in patients with apparent septic shock that fails to respond to antimicrobial therapy. Early recognition of catecholamine and cortisol excess can be lifesaving, as appropriate hormonal control and definitive surgical treatment may reverse both cardiovascular and systemic complications.
