Pediatric fibromuscular dysplasia presenting with acute right lower extremity limb ischemia requiring endovascular treatment and amputation – a case report
Authors: Amanda Louiselle, Olivia Hoffman, Kathleen Ludwig, Ayesha Zia, Angela Scheuerle, Halima Janjua, Dinesh Rakheja, Jeanne Dillenbeck, Sheena Pimpalwar, Corey Gill, Paymon Sanati-Mehrizy, Russell B Hawkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 13-month-old female presented with acute right lower extremity discoloration, pain, and inability to bear weight. Examination revealed a purple/red limb with absent pulses and no Doppler signal. Imaging demonstrated aneurysmal dilation of the superior and inferior mesenteric arteries and right common iliac artery thrombosis with absent distal flow. Anticoagulation with a heparin infusion was initiated. During anesthesia for MRA of the head, neck, and body to evaluate vasculopathy, she developed a renovascular hypertensive crisis. Her limb worsened in appearance with progressive mottling, prompting left axillary access for thrombectomy and thrombolysis catheter placement which was effective. Aortogram and selective renal and visceral arteriograms revealed beaded appearance of the renal arteries suggesting fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD). Given demarcation of her ischemic right lower extremity, she underwent a right through-knee amputation. Pathology from the specimen confirmed extensive arteriopathy consistent with fibromuscular dysplasia. Persistent wound breakdown and lack of pulsatile flow required revision to an above-knee amputation with skin grafting and eventually revision amputation. She recovered well and is now ambulatory with a prosthesis. She remains on long term anticoagulation, antihypertensive therapy for renovascular hypertension, and beta blockade to possibly reduce visceral aneurysm rupture risk. This case illustrates a rare, severe presentation of FMD and underscores the need for prompt multidisciplinary evaluation and coordinated management.Keywords: Fibromuscular dysplasia, Visceral aneurysm, Renovascular hypertension, Critical limb ischemia, Amputation, Thrombolysis, Thrombectomy

Background

Acute limb ischemia (ALI) is rare in children and typically related to traumatic or iatrogenic access-related injury [1]. Visceral aneurysms are rare in children and are usually due to underlying connective tissue disorder. Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD), a rare, non-inflammatory, non-atherosclerotic, multifocal segmental angiopathy of unknown cause [2], represents an additional but less frequently recognized cause of vascular compromise in children. Although more often diagnosed in adult females, in children, it typically involves the renal arteries or cerebral vessels and can be found during evaluation of neurologic dysfunction or renovascular hypertension. We report the case of a 13-month-old girl who presented with non-traumatic ALI and was found to have multiple visceral arterial aneurysms and renovascular hypertension.

Case presentation

A 13-month-old previously healthy female presented to the emergency department with several hours of right lower-extremity coolness, discoloration, and refusal to bear weight. She was followed outpatient by her pediatrician and dermatologist for chronic discoloration of the right lower extremity, thought to represent a capillary malformation (port wine stain) or cutis marmorata telangiectatica congenita. Prior outpatient physical exams noted did not mention the pulse exam in either extremity. Physical exam revealed a cool, red/purple discolored right lower extremity with no palpable pulse or Doppler signal in the right femoral artery or beyond. Normal palpable pulse and Doppler signals were present in the left lower extremity. This qualified as Rutherford class IIa (Rutherford classification is a system to grade the severity of peripheral arterial disease and acute limb ischemia for adults). Ultrasound revealed arteriopathy/wall thickening of the right common iliac artery and common femoral artery, with distal thrombus and minimal flow. Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) revealed fusiform focal dilation of the superior and inferior mesenteric arteries a few centimeters from their origin, aneurysmal right common iliac artery with thrombus present and no distal flow to the right lower extremity (Fig. 1). The patient was admitted, started on a therapeutic heparin infusion, and underwent a thrombophilia evaluation and genetic testing. Although thrombophilia testing should be interpreted with caution in a patient on heparin infusion, the patient was deemed to need long-term anticoagulation and it was not feasible to discontinue anticoagulation for testing in any reasonable time period. Despite improvement in her right lower extremity appearance and warmth, doppler signal remained absent, remaining Rutherford class IIa. The following day, sedated magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) was completed to evaluate suspected diffuse arteriopathy. The patient suffered a hypertensive crisis on induction, requiring labetalol and nicardipine infusions. After the MRA, which showed no cerebral involvement, and associated hypertensive event, her right lower extremity appeared duskier and more mottled, the motor function became impaired with fixed flexion, and the skin temperature became cool. Given progressive signs of worsening ALI (now Rutherford class III), endovascular intervention was offered. We recognize that fixed flexion and Rutherford class III limb ischemia are poor prognostic signs in adults, but the Rutherford classification has not been validated in the pediatric population in terms of limb salvage options/outcomes. After extensive discussion of the risks of aneurysm creation or vessel disruption/bleeding, the family wished to proceed. The patient went to the interventional radiology (IR) suite and left axillary arterial access was obtained. Angiography, thrombectomy (with RED Penumbra catheter), and thrombolysis catheter placement (5Fr Craig McNamara lysis catheter) in the right common iliac artery were performed (Fig. 2). The catheter was left in place overnight for directed delivery of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA). The patient returned to IR the next day and had bilateral renal angiography, aortogram, bilateral lower extremity angiography, right lower extremity angioplasty, and thrombectomy. The angiographic appearance was consistent with fibromuscular dysplasia, with beaded appearance of the bilateral renal arteries and subsegmental arteries (Fig. 3). The patient was also noted to have chronic occlusion of the left popliteal artery with collateral runoff to the tibioperoneal trunk. The patient had significant stenosis and recurrent thrombosis of the right common iliac artery despite therapeutic anticoagulation and thrombectomy/thrombolysis.

The patient’s vasculitis and thrombophilia evaluations were unrevealing. She had normal ANCA titer, myeloperoxidase, serine protease, DNase antibody, C3, and C4 complement levels. She had a mildly elevated C-reactive protein (3.3 mg/dL). Anti-nuclear antibodies were not detected. Her factor V Leiden, antithrombin III, protein C, and S activity levels were normal. Rapid exome sequencing revealed a variant of uncertain significance in MYH11. Pathogenicity of this gene has previously been implicated in occlusive arterial disease with FMD-like pathology [3]. However, this variant was inherited from the unaffected father and has not previously been reported in any patient affected by FMD. Lack of abnormal genotype in COL3A1 was reassuring against a collagen-based vascular connective tissue disorder [4].

After anticoagulation and thrombectomy, the patient’s limb necrosis appeared to halt, and a weakly palpable right femoral pulse was appreciated. However, on day six of admission due to demarcation of necrotic and healthy tissue at the knee level, orthopaedic surgery performed a right through-knee amputation and used indocyanine-green to ensure perfusion of the tissue flap used for closure (images unavailable). Through-knee amputation level was selected as the first step in an effort to preserve the growth plate and maximize chances of a functional limb. Pathologic examination of the patient’s amputation specimen revealed diffuse skin and soft tissue necrosis with extensive arteriopathy, comprising transmural fibromuscular hyperplasia and fibromyxoid change without fibrinoid necrosis or any other features of primary vasculitis, most consistent with FMD with superimposed thrombi (Fig. 4).

Her postoperative course was complex. She developed a left axillary pseudoaneurysm at the prior access site, successfully treated with ultrasound guided compression and thrombin injection. There were multiple episodes of skin flap breakdown requiring varying levels of care including multiple operative debridements over several months, wound VAC placement, revision of amputation to transfemoral/above knee amputation and a second revision of the above-knee amputation at a later date. Despite appropriate medical therapy including beta-blocker and anticoagulation, with low molecular weight heparin, she developed progressive thrombosis in her right common iliac artery, necessitating transition to a direct thrombin inhibitor, Bivalrudin. Unfortunately, this also required emergent thrombectomy with access obtained via the left femoral artery, which was further complicated by significant left femoral artery spasm and hematoma, necessitating the procedure to be stopped. Both femoral pulses gradually regained doppler signals, and the lower extremities remained perfused without further intervention despite the medical therapy already discussed. Given her previous complications, we employed a dual-pathway strategy —combining an antiplatelet agent (Aspirin) with systemic anticoagulation—to address both platelet-driven arterial thrombosis and coagulation-mediated thrombus propagation. Given the exceedingly excellent multidisciplinary care, eventually her healing was satisfactory enough that she regained mobility with intensive physical therapy and was able to use a prosthetic limb, approximately five months after her last operation. She remains on therapeutic low-molecular-weight heparin and aspirin with no plans at this time to stop either medicine.

Discussion and conclusions

FMD is a rare, non-inflammatory, non-atherosclerotic, multifocal segmental angiopathy of unknown etiology. Pediatric cases are uncommon, and diagnosis is frequently delayed by 4 to 9 years from symptom onset [2]. First described by Leadbetter and Burkland in 1938 in a child with renovascular cured by nephrectomy, FMD is classified by the arterial layer predominantly affected — intima, media, or adventitia — with medial disease being most common. Notably, our patient demonstrated transmural fibromuscular changes, an unusual pattern [5]. Data from the largest cohort of over 1000 patients show that 58% involved the renal artery, 32% carotid or vertebral, and only 10% involved other arterial beds (mesenteric, iliac, or intracranial) [6]. However, adult FMD patterns cannot be directly extrapolated to children. The marked female predominance seen in adults (94%) is reduced in pediatric populations (58%) [7]. In addition, the distribution of vascular disease differs substantially with renal and mesenteric vasculature more involved among pediatric patients compared to adults (97% versus 69.7% for renal, respectively, and 38.9% versus 16.2% for mesenteric, respectively) [7].

This case represents a particularly unusual presentation of pediatric FMD, with ALI as the initial presentation and progression to above-knee amputation. The pattern of our patient’s disease is unusual, with cerebral sparing, renovascular involvement, multi-visceral aneurysms, and aneurysmal change and thrombosis of the iliac and femoral system. Further, the recurrent arterial thromboses highlight the challenges of managing diffuse arteriopathy in early childhood. While rare infantile FMD cases have been reported with rapidly progressive, diffuse disease and early mortality due to intracranial hemorrhage [8], to our knowledge, no prior report describes a pediatric patient requiring limb amputation due to FMD. Given repeated thrombotic events despite therapeutic anticoagulation, the decision to employ dual-pathway antithrombotic therapy with LMWH and aspirin reflects an emerging framework supported by adult vascular trials to address both platelet-driven and coagulation-mediated thrombotic risk in severe vascular disease. Adult FMD literature includes a few cases of iliac artery involvement, typically with emboli and focal digital ischemia and some requiring iliac angioplasty [9], but these cases differ substantially in severity and distribution.

The complexity of this patient’s presentation underscores the need for early multispecialty involvement in suspected pediatric FMD. Indeed, several teams had input on this patient’s care and outcome, including general pediatric surgery, vascular surgery, orthopaedic surgery, plastic surgery, pediatric critical care, interventional radiology, genetics, nephrology, and hematology. Long-term management includes lifelong anticoagulation, aspirin, beta blockade, and blood pressure management. The patient is undergoing regular surveillance imaging with multidisciplinary review and discussion. Beta blockade and hypertension management remain key strategies to reduce the risk of visceral aneurysm rupture.
