Maternal and child nutrition insecurity, microbiome, and early neurodevelopment: an intricate interplay. Results from a systematic review
Authors: Maria Lucia Specchia, Flavia Beccia, Maria Gabriella Cacciuttolo, Luigi Petrella, Teresa Mungo, Sofia Thiella, Angelica Lucarelli, Drieda Zace, Maria Luisa Di Pietro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging research suggests nutrition insecurity influences microbiome composition, which in turn affects early neurodevelopment through the gut–brain axis. This systematic review aimed to evaluate evidence on these relationships. A comprehensive search of the scientific literature was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Studies investigating the links between nutrition insecurity in mothers and children, microbiome, and early neurodevelopment were included. Data on maternal characteristics, microbiota composition, neurodevelopmental outcomes, and nutritional status were extracted from eligible studies. The review included 11 studies, primarily cohort studies, conducted in various countries. According to the study findings, gut maternal and infant microbiota composition in early life appear to be closely connected to early neurodevelopment both in terms of cognitive/motor skills and temperament. Nutrition insecurity has a significant influence in shaping these outcomes as it can alter microbiota balance and contribute to gut dysbiosis and delayed neurodevelopmental milestones. Breastfeeding emerges as a crucial factor in modulating the infant microbiome and supporting neurodevelopment. Also, other factors such as pre-pregnancy overweight/obesity and environment seem to influence offspring gut colonization and neurodevelopmental outcomes. This systematic review highlights the intricate interplay between maternal and child nutrition insecurity, microbiota, and early neurodevelopment. These findings underscore the critical need for targeted interventions addressing maternal and child nutrition to mitigate the adverse effects of nutrition insecurity and support optimal early-life neurodevelopment. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to explore the causal pathways and to develop nutrition-based strategies to prioritize microbiome health in vulnerable and at-risk populations.

Introduction

Nutritional insecurity is defined as lack of access to sufficient and nutritious food to promote optimal well-being and a healthy lifestyle. Nutrition insecurity represents a significant public health issue. It is of particular concern for mothers and children because of its impact on multiple aspects of health and development. Among these, the interaction between nutrition security, the microbiome, and early neurodevelopment is a complex and evolving field that requires comprehensive exploration. The microbiome, which encompasses the diverse microbial communities inhabiting the human body, has emerged as a pivotal factor in modulating health outcomes. From birth, the human gut is colonized by microorganisms, along with their genomes and metabolic products—collectively referred to as the gut microbiome [1].

Gut microbiota composition can be affected by several factors such as health status, mode of birth delivery, and genetics, but diet is thought to have the greatest influence. Moreover, mounting evidence suggests a bidirectional interaction between gut and brain known as the gut–brain axis.

According to recent research, nutrition insecurity may influence the microbiome composition and functionality in both mothers and their children [2, 3]. Gut microflora in turn affects neural development through complex mechanisms, via neuronal, hormonal, and immune signalling pathways [4]. For example, alterations in gut microbiota composition, or dysbiosis, have been correlated with neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [5].

Understanding the intricate interactions between maternal and child nutrition, microbiome, and early neurodevelopment is crucial for developing targeted public health strategies and interventions. On these grounds, the main objective of the present study is to analyse the existing evidence on the relationships between nutrition insecurity in mothers and children, microbiome, and early neurodevelopment. The secondary objective is to identify literature gaps and highlight areas requiring further investigation.

Methods

A Systematic Review of the scientific literature was conducted according to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [6].

The review protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (ID: CRD42025630363).

Search strategy

An online search was carried out using the following electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science to identify studies on the relation between nutrition insecurity in mothers and children, microbiome, and neurodevelopment in early life.

The search was limited to articles published until March 1, 2024. The following search query was adopted:

(‘mother’ OR ‘pregnancy’ OR ‘pregnant’ OR ‘infant’ OR ‘newborn’ OR ‘baby’ OR ‘babies’ OR ‘childhood’ OR ‘fetal’ OR ‘fetus’ OR ‘maternal’ OR ‘offspring’ OR ‘prenatal’) AND (‘microbiome’ OR ‘microbiota’ OR ‘gut flora’ OR ‘gastrointestinal metagenome’ OR ‘microbiomic’) AND (‘neural development’ OR ‘neurodevelopment’ OR ‘neurodevelopmental disorder*’ OR ‘neurodevelopmental disease*’ OR ‘brain development’ OR ‘neural malformation’ OR ‘neural tube defect*’) AND (‘food’ OR ‘diet’ OR ‘nutrition’).

Study selection process

Literature search results were downloaded to Rayyan [7] for inclusion/exclusion screening. To identify eligible studies, titles, abstracts, and full texts of the retrieved papers were reviewed by two researchers independently. Results were cross-checked, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Moreover, a snowball strategy, which involved a manual search of references cited in the studies retrieved from the online databases, was used to identify possible additional studies. A PRISMA flow diagram was used to summarize studies selection.

Eligibility criteria

Only studies published in English, investigating the relationships among nutrition insecurity in mothers and children, microbiome, and early neurodevelopment, were deemed eligible.

We defined nutrition insecurity as inadequate access to amounts of food for healthy growth and development [1].

The microbiome was interpreted as a microbial community existing in a particular environment, for instance, the infant and the maternal gut [8]. We referred to early child neurodevelopment as the process of brain growth and maturation up to 5 years [9].

We included articles reporting primary studies, such as trial, cross-sectional, cohort, and case-control studies. In addition, we conducted a snowball search, examining primary studies cited in reviews or in the reference lists of the included articles.

Commentaries, editorials, conference abstracts, reviews, case reports, case series, book chapters, and articles addressing non-human populations and articles for which full text was unavailable were excluded.

Data extraction and presentation of results

From each eligible study, the following information were extracted: first author, year, country, study design and timeframe, sample size, population, factors and outcomes analysed, methods, and main results.

The results are presented beginning with an overview of the included studies, summarizing study types, publication years, and countries. We then examined the variables of interest investigated in each study, as well as the variable pairs used to assess specific relationships. Given the complexity of the topic, which can be approached from multiple perspectives, the results are structured to highlight the specific themes and viewpoints addressed in the articles.

Quality assessment

Quality assessment was performed according to the study design using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort and case-control studies and the Risk of Bias (RoB2) tool for trials [10].

Summary of the main findings

This paragraph provides a summary of the main findings from this systematic review according to the relationships explored by the included studies.

We reported quantitative results and association power when possible. The heterogeneity in the methods of analysis adopted among the articles included and the different ways to present the results, in some cases only in a graphic way, allowed us to report the results in a qualitative manner. More details are given in Table 1.

Microbiota and neurodevelopment

Several studies [11, 12, 15, 17, 19] reported relations between microbiota and mental development, motor skills, and temperament.

According to Van Den Berg et al. [11], higher Bifidobacteria levels in stool samples of preterm infants correlated with better mental development outcomes (higher Mental Development Index [MDI]) at the corrected age of 2 years. In this study, lower Bifidobacteria levels in stool samples evaluated by the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) at day 7 and day 14 (F = 3.8, P = .05 and F = 5.0, P = .02 respectively) and higher interleukin-1β and interleukin-8 levels at day 7 (F = 4.0, P = .04 and F = 8.0, P = .01, respectively) were found to be associated with lower MDI (mental development outcomes) at 24 months corrected age. Higher interleukin-2 and interleukin-4 levels at birth (F = 4.0, P = .05 and F = 6.0, P = .02 respectively) and interferon γ levels at day 7 (F = 4.4, P = .04) resulted to be associated with lower Psychomotor Development Index (PDI) (psychomotor outcomes) at 24 months corrected age. Tamana et al. [15] highlighted the role of Bacteroides dominance in infant gut microbiota in influencing cognitive and language development in the first 2 years of life. The study revealed more favourable BSID-III cognitive and language development at 2 years in infants with microbiota dominated by Bacteroidetes at 1 year (4.8-point and 4.2-point higher cognitive and language development scores). Sun et al. [19] pointed out an association between both maternal and infant gut microbiome and infant motor skills. Moreover, they underlined the higher relevance of mothers’ prenatal gut microbiome composition (third pregnancy trimester) than that of infants to neurodevelopment in the first year of life (maximum Q2 = 0.212 and 0.096 separately using the taxa at the class level), so emphasizing the importance of vertical microbial transfer. Moreover, Fusobacteriia in the maternal prenatal gut microbiota was found to be more associated with high fine motor skills, but Fusobacteriia in the infant gut microbiota resulted to be more associated with low fine motor skills (rank = 0.084 and −0.047 separately).

As for temperament, an association between a decrease in the normalized abundance of Prevotella at 12 months and internalizing problems at 2 years of age was reported by Loughman et al. [12], reporting a Prevotella abundance in only 4% (1/22) of behavioural case infants (T ≥ 60, using Child Behaviour Checklist, CBCL) compared with 44% (79/179) in non-case infants. Fox et al. [17] too supported a relationship between infant gut microbiota and child temperament describing associations between gut microbiota in the first 3 weeks of life and emotional reactivity in the first year (Tables 1 and 2). Specifically, an association was found between beta diversity at the age of 1–3 weeks and surgency/extraversion at 12 months (R2 = 0.276, P = .012) as well as its subscales, including approach (R2 = 0.285, P = .010), high-intensity pleasure (R2 = 0.275, P = .013), and smiling/laughter (R2 = 0.273, P = .013). Bifidobacterium and Lachnospiraceae abundance at 1–3 weeks of age was found to be positively associated with surgency/extraversion at 12 months. Klebsiella abundance at 1–3 weeks was found to be negatively associated with surgency/extraversion at 12 months. Concurrent composition was associated with negative affectivity at 12 months, including a positive association with Ruminococcus-1 and a negative association with Lactobacillus.

Nutrition insecurity, microbiota, and neurodevelopment

Two studies [13, 20] pointed out the relationship between nutrition security, microbiota, and neurodevelopment in early life. A maternal prenatal healthy diet was found by Dawson et al. [13] to indirectly relate to lower infant internalizing behaviour (in turn strongly associated with subsequent anxiety disorders) via higher alpha diversity of maternal faecal microbiota, although there was no evidence of a direct association between dietary pattern scores and child behavioural dysregulation. Moreover, Ma et al. [20] suggested a possible protective role of vitamin D intake against adverse effects of gut microbiota clusters on infant negative emotionality (Tables 1 and 2). With regard to the relationship between individual genera and the three temperament scales, an association between gut microbiota and early infancy neurodevelopmental outcomes was observed: Firmicutes genus was found to be associated with higher on the periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining reaction scores and therefore positive neurodevelopmental outcomes, whereas Clostridioides was associated with lower PAS scores and therefore adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes (q values < 0.001, 0.25, and 0.25, for DESeq2, ANCOM-BC, and MaAslin, respectively).

Nutrition insecurity, other factors, and microbiota

One study by Raspini et al. [14], carried out on a cohort of mother–infant dyads, investigated the link between nutrition insecurity, several other factors, and microbiota. The authors concluded that the gut microbiota development in infants is influenced, rather than by a single factor distinctly, by a complex interaction of multiple maternal and neonatal factors such as pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), gestational weight gain, type of delivery, type of feeding, time of weaning, and environmental influences. Significant differences in alpha diversity of infant gut microbiota at 6 months of age were not found according to the explored maternal and perinatal factors except for the type of feeding. Indeed, the study results underlined the role of breastfeeding reporting a higher gut microbiota alpha diversity in infants not exclusively breastfed (NeBF) compared to those exclusively breastfed (P = .018). In particular, NeBF group was mainly characterized by high abundances of Ruminococcaceae (P < .0001) and Flavonifractor (P < .001).

Other factors, microbiota, and neurodevelopment

One study by Guzzardi et al. [16] analysed the relation between maternal overweight, microbiota, and neurodevelopment, describing a reduced abundance of beneficial genera such as Bifidobacterium, Blautia, and Ruminococcus, in meconium of infants from overweight mothers, which was linked to poorer cognitive outcomes at preschool age.

Nutrition insecurity, other factors, microbiota, and neurodevelopment

Two studies [18, 21] assessed multiple interactions among different factors. Nieto-Ruiz et al. [18] showed that children aged 3.5 years from overweight or obese OV/OB mothers were found to have higher scores in behavioural problems than those from normoweight NW mothers (anxiety, P = .027 and total problems, P = .039). Moreover, children from OB mothers with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) showed higher scores in attention/deficit hyperactivity (P = .011) and externalizing problems (P = .015) than those from OV/NW mothers with GDM. Concerning their gut microbiota composition at 18 months of age, negative correlations were shown between Fusicatenibacter abundances and total problems, internalizing problems and the related subscales of somatic complaints and withdrawn behaviour, and pervasive developmental problems; positive correlations were shown between the abundances of unclass_Clostridiales and Flavonifractor and anxiety/depression and somatic complaints subscales, respectively. In the same cohort of children from overweight mothers, the authors also reported higher childhood anxiety and internalizing, externalizing, and total problems related to breastfeeding. Oliphant et al. [21] observed that an Enterobacteriaceae-dominant microbiome negatively impacted cognitive and language development in Black infants, specifically in males, living in high-stress urban environments with scarce resources, whose mothers consumed less than two portions of sea fish per week (P < .05). They also highlighted a key role of vitamin B12 depletion in infant microbiome dysregulation and poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes (Tables 1 and 2). Blautia spp. also were identified as substantial contributors of important microbiome functions, including vitamin B12 biosynthesis and related vitamin B12-dependent microbiome functions.

Adjustment of covariates across included studies

Across the included studies (Table 1), the adjustment of covariates varied substantially, reflecting differences in study design and available data. Most studies controlled for key perinatal and demographic factors potentially influencing outcomes, such as gestational age, birth weight, and infant sex [11, 21]. Several studies also accounted for maternal or environmental factors, including birth mode, breastfeeding practices, and household characteristics [12, 15], while others included maternal lifestyle or psychosocial variables such as prenatal diet, smoking, stress, and maternal education [13, 18, 20]. In contrast, a few studies did not report covariate adjustments [14, 16, 19], although Sun et al. noted that relevant clinical covariates were considered as potential confounders [19]. Overall, the heterogeneity in covariate adjustment underscores variability in the control of potential confounders across studies, which may contribute to differences in reported associations.

Discussion

The findings of this systematic review highlight the central role of the maternal and infant microbiota in early neurodevelopment and the significant influence of nutrition insecurity in shaping neurodevelopmental outcomes. Indeed, infant neurodevelopment appears to be closely connected to gut microbiota composition in early life. For instance, studies such as those by Tamana et al. [15] and Oliphant et al. [21] identified specific infant microbiota profiles characterized by the dominance of Bacteroides or Enterobacteriaceae as associated with enhanced or worse infant neurodevelopmental measures respectively. The identified association between specific infant microbiota profiles (Bacteroides dominance or Enterobacteriaceae dominance) and neurodevelopmental outcomes is consistent with earlier work by Carlson et al. [22], who demonstrated that infant gut microbiome composition at 1 year was associated with cognitive outcomes at 2 years. Their study specifically highlighted Bacteroides as potentially beneficial for cognitive development, supporting the findings by Tamana et al. mentioned in this review.

The inverse relationship between maternal prenatal microbiota alpha diversity and child behavioural problems reported by Dawson et al. [13] extends research by Kimura et al. [23], who found that maternal microbiome diversity during pregnancy was predictive of infant temperament at 18 months. Both studies underscore the importance of maternal microbial health for offspring neurodevelopment, suggesting potential transgenerational effects operating through the gut–brain axis.

The maternal–infant microbial transfer pathway highlighted by Sun et al. [19] builds upon foundational work by Ferretti et al. [24], who mapped the vertical transmission of microbes from mother to infant and its implications for immune development. These findings extend this concept to neurodevelopmental domains, suggesting that the inheritance of maternal microbiota may have far-reaching consequences beyond immunity.

The connections between maternal prenatal nutrition, microbiota composition, and toddler behavioural outcomes described by Dawson et al. [13] and Oliphant et al. [21] complement earlier findings by Sarkar et al. [25], who demonstrated that maternal diet during pregnancy shapes offspring microbiome and neurodevelopment in animal models.

The specific focus on vitamin B12 deficiencies in food-insecure environments by Oliphant et al. [21] parallels research by Jankovic-Karasoulos et al. [26], who found that maternal B-vitamin status influenced infant cognitive outcomes through alterations in one-carbon metabolism pathways. Both studies highlight the critical role of micronutrients in early brain development, with this review adding the microbiome as a mediating factor.

Similarly, Ma et al. [20] suggested a protective effect of dietary supplementation of vitamin D on adverse effects of infant gut microbiota on infant emotionality. These results expanded on work by García-Serna and Morales [27], who demonstrated vitamin D’s neuroprotective properties, framing vitamin D’s benefits within a microbiome context, and suggesting an additional mechanism for its neurodevelopmental effects. Lastly, as for feeding practices, breastfeeding, in line with previous evidence [28–30] emerged from this review as pivotal in modulating infant microbiome and neurodevelopment [14, 18].

Other evidence from this review [14, 16, 18, 21] showed how also other maternal and neonatal factors besides nutrition can influence microbiome and neurodevelopment. The multifactorial influences on gut colonization and neurodevelopment described by Raspini et al. [14] mirror findings from the CHILD Cohort Study reported by Azad et al. [31], which demonstrated how delivery mode, antibiotic exposure, and breastfeeding collectively shape infant microbiome development. This review extends these observations to neurodevelopmental outcomes, suggesting that early environmental factors affect brain development through their impact on the microbiome. Oliphant et al. [21] highlighted the link between living in high stress, under-resourced, urban environments and changes in gut microbiome function and poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes in Black infants. These findings complement work by Callaghan et al. [32], who demonstrated how early adversity affects neurodevelopment through stress pathways. This review adds the microbiome as a crucial mediator in this relationship, particularly for underserved populations.

Guzzardi et al. [16] and Nieto-Ruiz et al. [18] underlined the relation between maternal overweight/obesity before and during pregnancy and GDM, infant gut bacterial colonization, and toddler reasoning skills and behavioural outcomes. This relationship builds upon research by Rivera et al. [33], who documented altered microbiome profiles in offspring of mothers with metabolic disorders and suggests that maternal metabolic health affects offspring brain development partially through microbial mechanisms.

Based on the above, regarding the main objective of the study, the cumulative evidence points to a clear interplay between maternal and infant nutrition insecurity and the gut–brain axis, highlighting a pathway through which the former can compromise both infant microbiome balance and neurodevelopment. Addressing nutrition security is therefore critical for optimizing microbiome integrity and supporting neurodevelopment during critical early-life windows.

Despite the significant insights provided, this review has limitations that should be acknowledged. First, there was a notable heterogeneity in study designs, methodologies, and outcomes assessed, particularly in the evaluation of infant neurodevelopment, which utilized diverse tools ranging from the CBCL to the Bayley Scales of Infant Development III (BSID-III). This variation precludes direct comparisons across studies. Second, only a limited number of studies [13, 14, 18, 20, 21] explicitly investigated nutrition insecurity, reducing the generalizability of findings to broader populations facing resource constraints and/or with unhealthy dietary habits. Furthermore, most studies analysed the infant microbiota [11, 12, 14–21], with fewer data [13, 19] on the maternal microbiome, limiting conclusions regarding maternal microbial contributions to early neurodevelopment.

The predominant focus on infant rather than maternal microbiota highlighted by this study echoes observations by Dunlop et al. [34], who noted the relative scarcity of studies examining maternal microbiome contributions to infant health. Both suggest that greater attention to maternal microbial factors could yield valuable insights into developmental origins of health and disease.

Another limitation stems from the observational nature of most studies [12–21], which limits causal inferences. Finally, the scarcity of longitudinal data on microbiota changes over time hampers the ability to explore their dynamic effects on infant neurodevelopment.

Conclusion

This systematic review highlights the intricate interplay between maternal and child nutrition insecurity, microbiota and early neurodevelopment. Disruptions in maternal and infant nutrition, often driven by food insecurity, can contribute to gut dysbiosis. The resulting changes in the infant gut microbiome have far-reaching implications for metabolism and brain development. Feeding practices, particularly breastfeeding, are vital for modulating microbiota composition and supporting neurodevelopmental health. These findings underscore the critical need for targeted interventions addressing maternal and child nutrition to mitigate the adverse effects of nutrition insecurity and support optimal early-life neurodevelopment. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to explore the causal pathways and to develop nutrition-based strategies and interventions, to be implemented during pregnancy and the first stages of life, to prioritize microbiome health in vulnerable and at-risk populations.
