Educational attainment of children with congenital heart disease in the United Kingdom
Authors: Daniel G W Cave, Zoë E Wands, Kirsten Cromie, Amy Hough, Kathryn Johnson, Mark Mon-Williams, James R Bentham, Richard G Feltbower, Adam W Glaser
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEducational attainment in children with congenital heart disease (CHD) within the UK has not been reported, despite the possibility of school absences and disease-specific factors creating educational barriers.Methods and resultsChildren were prospectively recruited to the Born in Bradford birth cohort between March 2007 and December 2010. Diagnoses of CHD were identified through linkage to the congenital anomaly register and independently verified by clinicians. Multivariable regression accounted for relevant confounders. Our primary outcome was the odds of ‘below expected’ attainment in maths, reading, and writing at ages 4–11 years.Educational records of 139 children with non-genetic CHD were compared with 11 188 age-matched children with no major congenital anomaly. Children with CHD had significantly higher odds of ‘below expected’ attainment in maths at age 4–5 years [odds ratio (OR) 1.64, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.07–2.52], age 6–7 (OR 2.03, 95% CI 1.32–3.12), and age 10–11 (OR 2.28, 95% CI 1.01–5.14). Odds worsened with age, with similar results for reading and writing. The odds of receiving special educational needs support reduced with age for children with CHD relative to controls [age 4–5: OR 4.84 (2.06–11.40); age 6–7: OR 3.65 (2.41–5.53); age 10–11: OR 2.73 (1.84–4.06)]. Attainment was similar for children with and without exposure to cardio-pulmonary bypass. Lower attainment was strongly associated with the number of pre-school hospital admissions.ConclusionChildren with CHD have lower educational attainment compared with their peers. Deficits are evident from school entry and increase throughout primary school.

Introduction

Medical and surgical advances for congenital heart disease (CHD) mean that >90% of children now survive into adulthood.1 The long-term survival of these children raises critical questions about their ability to thrive in education and, thus, their quality of life. The majority of studies assessing neurodevelopmental outcomes have included children with single ventricle physiology or other severe cyanotic CHD.2,3

These have demonstrated consistently poorer outcomes in various domains, including psychomotor, visuospatial, memory, speech, and language.2–4 Whilst this is highly important, these children make up only around 10% of the entire CHD population.5 Neurocognitive assessments can be complex and difficult to facilitate clinically. Assessment of educational achievement provides an accessible method of monitoring the holistic neurodevelopmental outcomes of all children with CHD.

There is currently limited available data describing the educational attainment of children in the UK with CHD. Wray et al. compared the educational and behavioural outcomes of 65 children after cardiac transplantation with 52 children with CHD after surgery and 45 non-matched healthy children.6 They found similar results for maths and reading in all groups. However, only four (6%) post-transplant patients had CHD.6 Families that did not speak English as a first language were excluded. A second study evaluated 47 children undergoing surgery for cyanotic and acyanotic CHD.7 Cyanotic conditions were associated with lower scores in reading, spelling, and mathematics before and after surgery, whereas children with acyanotic CHD performed similarly to controls. Patients were initially recruited in the 1980s and so may not reflect current practice. Due to the small sample size, and 25% of CHD patients being lost to follow-up, the study may have been underpowered to detect true differences for the CHD group.7

Several large registry studies have examined early educational attainment for children with CHD in the USA, Australia, and Europe.8–10 These indicate that children with CHD may be at higher risk for educational difficulties.8 However, the existing evidence is difficult to conclusively interpret due to large amounts (>50%) of missing data,8–10 or a lack of a healthy control group.9–12 Parent-rated educational achievement is often used in place of school assessments.10,11 Some were not able to adjust for ethnicity and socioeconomic status,10,12 key factors in educational development, where others over-adjusted for medical mediating factors such as birthweight and gestation.8,13 Additionally, differences in UK medical and surgical care for CHD, differences in the education system, and sociodemographic characteristics mean these data cannot be generalized to UK children.

Interventions for CHD result in significant disruption to early childhood. Surgical intervention is common during infancy, and during this time, children may suffer subclinical neurological insult associated with anaesthesia, acute illness, or surgical complications.14 The long-term effects of cardio-pulmonary bypass (CPB) during early brain development are likely to be significant.3 Hospital admissions and the higher rate of intercurrent illnesses for children with CHD lead to prolonged school absences and a possible delay in educational and social development. This is compounded by poor growth and sometimes chronic hypoxaemia. The cumulative deleterious effects of these experiences are likely to only be displayed during the school years and later in child development.

Our aim was to explore the overall impact of CHD on education in early childhood. Using the unique Born in Bradford (BiB) cohort, we wanted to assess the educational attainment of primary school children with CHD relative to their peers and other congenital anomalies to identify areas where support for these children can be targeted and improved.

BiB is one of the UK's largest birth cohort studies of health and wellbeing in childhood.15 Over 13 500 children in Bradford were recruited from birth between March 2007 and December 2010. They have been extensively followed up through childhood to determine the influence of numerous factors on a child's physical, cognitive, and social development. Through linkage to the Department for Education National Pupil Database (NPD) and the Yorkshire and Humber Congenital Anomaly Register (YHCAR), this cohort provides a unique insight into the long-term effects of disease on education and social outcomes.15,16

Methods

Born in Bradford cohort

Participants were recruited prospectively in pregnancy as part of the BiB birth cohort study. All women attending an antenatal clinic in Bradford between March 2007 and December 2010 were eligible for the study, and 83% of the women approached agreed to participate. This resulted in 12 453 women being enroled, comprising 13 776 pregnancies.15 The Bradford population is highly diverse with a large south Asian population, and native-speaking interpreters were available at recruitment for women who did not speak English. Questionnaire and healthcare data were collected at baseline, and children have been prospectively followed throughout childhood. Additional data have been collected using data linkage to primary and secondary care records, the YHCAR, and the NPD. Details of participant selection and follow-up have been published previously.15

Detailed demographic data were recorded routinely for BiB participants. Ethnicity is recorded for each child at school entry (age 4–5 years) by the NPD. Missing data (<1%) for ethnicity was completed using linked primary care data or the mother's ethnicity. The first language of the children was collected from the educational record. Socio-economic status of each child was assessed by the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD), using the postcode of the mother at enrolment.17 Local authority data were available for families receiving disability living allowance, housing benefits, income support, or job seekers allowance. Self-reported maternal smoking status and alcohol use during pregnancy were collected from the baseline questionnaire.

Case ascertainment

The prevalence of genetic disease and congenital anomalies in Bradford is 50% higher than the population average in England.16 Children in the BiB cohort with a congenital anomaly were identified through linkage to the YHCAR. Anomalies are added to the YHCAR by clinicians using a standardized local reporting system. BiB congenital anomaly cases were subsequently validated by individual case note review by a paediatrician, neonatologist, and geneticist.16 Primary care records were searched for all codes relevant to CHD and other congenital anomalies to ensure that all late diagnoses were captured.

Congenital anomalies were categorized into major and minor anomalies according to the most recent EUROCAT (European network of population-based registries for the epidemiological surveillance of congenital anomalies) guidelines.18,19 Diagnoses that were not specifically stated in EUROCAT guidance were individually reviewed by a Consultant Neonatologist (K.J.), and recorded as either minor or no anomaly. A subgroup of children with CHD requiring surgical intervention with CPB was identified using primary care and diagnosis data, and compared with those never exposed to CPB. Non-CPB CHD diagnoses included atrial septal defects (ASDs), ventricular septal defects, congenital pulmonary stenosis, or patent ductus arteriosus. CHD diagnoses were additionally categorized as cyanotic or acyanotic. A list of all the major congenital heart diagnoses that were included is provided in Supplementary material online, Table S1. Children with a known genetic abnormality were excluded, as these are independently known to be significantly associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes (Supplementary material online, Figure S1).20 All children without a major congenital anomaly were included as the control group. An additional comparison was made to children with non-cardiac major congenital anomalies, and their characteristics are described in Supplementary material online, Table S2.

Outcomes

Educational attainment and special educational needs (SEN) status were identified through the existing linkage between BiB and the NPD. The primary outcome was achievement below the expected level for reading, writing, and maths at age 6–7 years (Key Stage 1). Secondary outcomes included reading, writing, and maths achievement at age 4–5 years (early years), age 10–11 years (Key Stage 2) and provision of SEN support. Assessment results were dichotomized for analysis, with children achieving ‘above expected’ and ‘at expected’ combined into one group and compared with those classified as performing below the expected level.

Statistical analyses

Baseline demographics were summarized for all children in the BiB cohort with linked educational data, and compared between the children with CHD, non-cardiac anomalies, and controls. Continuous variables were summarized using either mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, depending on whether there was evidence of skewness. Multivariable logistic regression was conducted for primary and secondary outcomes. The covariate adjustment set for confounding was selected using causal inference methods.21 A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was created to display the relationship between all measured and unmeasured variables and was agreed upon by the authors prior to analysis. The DAG-implied minimal sufficient adjustment set for the effect of CHD on educational attainment comprised the following covariates: sex, age, consanguinity (parents first cousins or other blood relatives), child ethnicity, overall IMD decile, receipt of benefits (parents receiving any form of benefits), and maternal smoking or alcohol use in pregnancy. Variables such as birthweight and gestation were mediating factors, confirmed by the DAG, and therefore not included in the model. Children with missing data were excluded for analysis of the missing variable. The DAG was created using the DAGitty web application, and is provided in Supplementary material online, Figure S2.22

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for data collection and storage for research is covered by BiB ethical approval by the Bradford Research Ethics Committee (07/H1302/112). Data access was granted to the research team by the BiB data access committee on 25 April 2022. All data was fully anonymized before being accessed by the research team.

Results

For mothers recruited to the BiB study, there were 13 818 live births between March 2007 and December 2010. This included 185 (1.3%) children with CHD, 451 (3.3%) with a non-cardiac major congenital anomaly, and 13 182 (95.4%) with no major anomaly. A linked educational record was available for 11 743 children: 161/185 (87%) with CHD, 394/451 (87%) with a non-cardiac anomaly, and 11 188/13 182 (85%) controls. There were 11 (6%) deaths and 7 (4%) withdrawals from the study in the CHD group compared with 124 (1%) deaths and 421 (3%) withdrawals in the control group. Only 6 (3%) children with CHD were lost to follow-up. After excluding 89 children with a known genetic abnormality, there were 139 children with CHD and 327 with non-cardiac anomalies. Key Stage 1 (age 6–7 years) assessment records for maths, reading, and writing were available for 10 884 (79%) children in the BiB cohort. Early years (4–5 years) assessment records for maths, reading, and writing were available for 10 598 (77%) children; Key Stage 1 (age 6–7 years) records were available for 10 884 (79%) children; and only 3466 (25%) children were linked to a Key Stage 2 (10–11 years) record due to the attained age of the cohort at the time of data extraction. The proportion of missing data was similar between all three groups.

Children with CHD were significantly less likely to achieve the expected standard at all ages compared with healthy peers, and the odds of ‘below expected’ achievement increased with age (Figure 2A). At age 4–5 years, 56 (47%) children with CHD were below expected standards in at least one subject compared with 3850 (38%) healthy children [odds ratio (OR) 1.41, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.92–2.16]. At age 6–7 years, this increased to 53 [41% vs. 2939 (28%); OR 1.98, 95% CI 1.32–2.96] and 23 (64%) at age 10–11 years [vs. 1165 (35%); OR 2.68, 95% CI 1.21–5.95]. Children with non-cardiac major congenital anomalies had similar levels of educational proficiency to those with CHD at age 4–5 years [159 (52%) vs. 56 (47%) and age 6–7 years (135 (44%) vs. 53 (41%)] (Figure 2B). However, children with CHD had lower achievement at age 10–11 years [23 (64%) vs. 50 (45%)] (Table 2).

Children with CHD were more likely to be receiving SEN support at the time of school entry compared with children with no congenital anomaly (8% vs. 3%; OR 4.84, 95% CI 2.06–11.40). However, the odds of SEN provision for children with CHD were reduced at each assessment age relative to controls, despite worsening educational attainment (Figure 2A). The odds of receiving SEN support fell to 3.65 (95% CI 2.41–5.53) for children with CHD aged 6–7 years, and again to 2.73 (95% CI 1.84–4.06) at ages 10–11 years relative to children with no anomaly. SEN support was similar for children with cardiac and non-cardiac congenital anomalies (Table 2). For the 56 (47%) children with CHD who were below the expected early years level in at least one subject, 31 (55%) were not recorded as receiving any form of SEN support before Key Stage 1, and 38 (68%) failed at least one Key Stage 1 subject. Only 6 (4%) children with CHD had an education, health, and care plan, the highest level of SEN support, in place before Key Stage 1 compared with 22 (7%) with non-cardiac anomalies.

Of children with a linked educational record, 42 (30%) underwent surgical intervention with CPB, and 97 (70%) did not. There were 15 (11%) children with cyanotic CHD. We observed no clear differences in educational attainment for non-CPB, CPB, or cyanotic CHD (Figure 3). Children with cyanotic CHD were more likely to receive SEN support (OR 5.86, 95% CI 1.88–18.21) than CPB (OR 4.23, 95% CI 2.02–8.85) and non-CPB groups (OR 3.42, 95% CI 2.08–5.64), but confidence intervals were wide. Children with CHD whose parents were receiving benefits had significantly lower educational attainment across all subjects (Table 3). Compounding this, children with CHD received significantly less SEN support if they were from the most deprived socioeconomic backgrounds (IMD quintile 1 and 2) or were receiving means-tested free school meals (Table 3). Other predictors of improved educational attainment were white ethnicity and prematurity (Table 3).

Lastly, we studied the interaction between hospital admissions during early childhood and educational attainment. Children with CHD never admitted to the hospital had similar educational attainment to their peers without a congenital anomaly (KS1 maths: OR 1.19; 95% CI 0.54–2.62). There was a gradual increase in the odds of underachievement as the number of hospital admissions increased, relative to children with no anomaly (Table 4). There was a similar increase in the odds of SEN provision with the number of hospital admissions.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the most in-depth study of educational attainment for children with CHD in the UK. We have demonstrated that children with CHD have around a two-fold greater odds of prolonged educational problems when compared with age-matched children with no congenital anomalies. These issues are evident from school entry and become more prevalent throughout primary school education. This finding was unique to CHD, as attainment for children with non-cardiac congenital anomalies appeared to show some degree of ‘catch up’ to healthy children. The odds of SEN provision for children with CHD reduced with age, with provision being even lower for children from less affluent socioeconomic backgrounds. These effects remained after adjustment for confounding factors. Exposure to surgical intervention with CPB in early childhood did not result in additional educational difficulties relative to children not exposed to CPB.

Our findings confirm the suggestions of poor educational attainment for children with CHD from studies in the USA and Australia.8,9 Oster et al. conducted a large study of third-grade (age 8–9 years) children with CHD using linked data from North Carolina, USA.8 However, only 50% of their CHD cases were linked to an educational record, compared with 87% in our study. Of 2807 children with CHD, the odds of below-expected reading and maths achievement were slightly higher than controls (adj OR 1.38 and 1.14, respectively).8 This was seen consistently across those with critical and non-critical CHD; however, authors were not able to adjust for consanguinity, maternal smoking, and alcohol use. Our results concur, demonstrating increased odds of poorer reading and maths proficiency, yet with a much larger effect size for children of a similar age (Key Stage 1 reading: OR 2.82; maths: OR 2.03). This could signify that children with CHD in the UK do not receive the same level of support as those in the USA, although this requires further investigation. However, this difference may also be influenced by other characteristics of the UK population, healthcare, or education system that we could not assess, or by the methodology we have used.

We found that 33% of children with CHD received some form of SEN support (compared with 21% in the USA study).8 However, as attainment worsened with age relative to children with no congenital anomaly, the odds of receiving SEN support reduced. For those not meeting the expected standard at age 4–5 years, only 55% were offered SEN support before their next assessment at Key Stage 1. This suggests that the additional educational needs of some children with CHD are not being recognized. Children who achieve below the expected standard at age 4–5 are highly likely to underachieve at subsequent assessments. Therefore, early assessment is critical to ensuring later success. Children with CHD may have no outward signs of disability, which may contribute to the lack of recognition of their educational needs. Only 6 (4%) of children with CHD in our study had an EHCP, the highest level of SEN support encompassing the child's educational, health, and social needs. It is tailored to the child and can be requested by teachers, parents, or healthcare professionals.23 We have demonstrated that children with CHD have a significantly elevated risk of educational underachievement, and early recognition and intervention in the UK must improve to combat this.

A study of Australian children with CHD undergoing surgery in the first year of life demonstrated lower educational achievement at age 7–9 years compared with those not undergoing surgery.9 This effect was more pronounced with low birthweight, non-English-speaking households and an increasing number of hospital admissions, although the authors were unable to compare results with healthy children.9 This is in contrast to our findings that children with all types of CHD performed at a lower level in educational assessments than unaffected children, with approximately two-fold increased odds of being below the expected standard. The reasons behind the finding of lower educational attainment in mild CHD are unclear; however, this has been shown in several other studies.8 Small ASDs have been associated with psychiatric diagnoses and emotional distress, affecting school achievement.24 It is possible that unmeasured de novo genetic variants contribute to this effect, especially as common mutations linked to both CHD and neurodevelopmental disabilities have been identified.25 This is a potentially unrecognized group of children with unmet need at increased risk of significant educational problems and subsequent adverse socioeconomic consequences.

We compared children with CHD to those with non-cardiac major congenital anomalies as a separate control group. We found that educational attainment progressively worsened throughout primary school for children with CHD. This did not occur for children with non-cardiac congenital anomalies, who instead showed some ‘catch-up’ with healthy children between Key Stages 1 and 2. We were not able to study progress beyond primary school due to the maturity of the study cohort; however, we hypothesize that children with CHD will continue to fall behind their peers throughout secondary school and into adulthood. This is supported by evidence from studies in France and Finland showing children with CHD are less likely to graduate from secondary school, enter higher education, or find employment.26,27 This longitudinal pattern of progressive educational impairment has not been studied in the CHD population previously. Educational attainment was lower for children with cardiac and non-cardiac anomalies, suggesting that school absences and hospital admissions have a greater impact than the anatomical type of anomaly. This is supported by a recent systematic review.28 The lack of additional SEN support provided to CHD children between assessment stages is likely to play an important role and may indicate CHD-specific issues in SEN provision.

Strengths

We studied a large and diverse population of children, performing careful adjustment for all relevant confounding factors such as ethnicity, socioeconomic status, consanguinity, maternal smoking, and alcohol use using a causal inference approach.29 We were mindful not to over-adjust our multivariable regression model through the inclusion of ‘mediator’ variables lying on the causal pathway between exposure and outcome, such as birthweight or gestational age.

Children were prospectively recruited through the BiB study, and the majority (85%) were linked to routinely collected health and education data, minimizing selection bias during recruitment or follow-up. The educational data used provide the most objective source of outcome data, and avoid the needs for self-reported attainment. We ensured the accuracy of congenital anomaly diagnoses by using multiple sources, including a regionally standardized congenital anomaly reporting service. The cases were individually verified by a neonatologist, geneticist, and community paediatrician.16 Only major congenital anomalies were included according to the EUROCAT classification, reducing the risk of misclassification bias and ensuring consistency with existing literature. Almost all CHD children were linked to an education record (87%), with only 3% lost to follow-up.

Limitations

Due to the overall number of CHD children in our cohort, we had a relatively small population of children with single ventricle CHD, a group known to be at higher risk of educational difficulties.2,4,10 As a result, we were not able to measure the relative effect of long-term cyanosis on educational outcome for UK children. We were able to study a group of children with surgical intervention using CPB, another significant influence on neurodevelopment. We did not find a difference in educational attainment compared with children who were not exposed to CPB or cyanosis. This finding is important, as these children are less likely to be identified for additional support.

There were some children who entered school but who did not have a linked Key Stage 2 record [8277 (70%)]. This was due to most of the BiB cohort not being old enough to reach this stage of educational assessment. This affected children with and without congenital anomalies equally, and therefore should not bias our results. Data on school absences were missing for the vast majority of children, and therefore could not be analysed. Whilst we did measure all hospital admissions before and after starting school, we could not assess the effects of other absences, such as outpatient appointments and intercurrent illness not requiring admission.

Impact on current practice

We present evidence that children with CHD have lower educational achievement, with the deficits increasing with age. The causes of this are multifactorial, but are likely to include school absences and a lack of SEN support. This finding is important, as these children may continue to fall behind in school during later childhood, reducing their chances of entering higher education and finding employment.26 We have demonstrated that 55% of children with CHD who fall behind are not offered any additional support to help them catch-up to their peers at the next assessment level. Children with CHD from less affluent socioeconomic backgrounds are even less likely to receive additional support. Early school performance predicts later academic success; therefore, early identification and support for these most vulnerable children is vital for children to reach their full potential.30

There is currently very little neurodevelopmental follow-up for children in the UK with CHD. A survey of European paediatric cardiac surgical centres revealed that, even for children with complex CHD, only 40% conducted neurodevelopmental follow-up.31 No European guideline exists; however, American Heart Association guidelines recommend that only children with cyanotic CHD or those undergoing surgery in infancy require neurodevelopmental follow-up.32 However, our study demonstrates that children with all types of CHD are at increased risk of educational difficulties compared with children without CHD. This highlights the urgent need for additional guidance and resources to ensure children receive adequate support. Future studies should focus on defining additional risk factors for educational underachievement and investigate the impact of additional support. Support must be provided fairly to combat the widespread inequalities in health and social outcomes.

Conclusion

Children with CHD are much more likely to have below-expected educational achievement than children with no major congenital anomalies. Exposure to surgical intervention with CPB in early childhood does not appear to confer an additional disadvantage in educational attainment. Deficits are evident from school entry and increase throughout primary school.
