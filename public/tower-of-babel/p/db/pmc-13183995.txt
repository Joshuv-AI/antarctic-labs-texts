Hybrid generative–ensemble approach for predicting recycled aggregate concrete strength properties
Authors: Paul O Awoyera, Lenganji Simwanda, Milica V Vasić, Md Azree Othuman Mydin, Udeme Udo Imoh, Olaolu George Fadugba, Andi Asiz, Majid Movahedi Rad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study proposes a hybrid generative–ensemble framework to predict key mechanical properties of recycled aggregate concrete from mix proportions. An established database of 112 mixes was used to model compressive strength, split tensile strength, flexural strength, and elastic modulus. To mitigate data scarcity, a conditional variational autoencoder was trained on the training data only and used to generate additional physically plausible input samples, after which seven supervised learning algorithms were trained and compared using cross-validation. Gradient boosting and support vector regression achieved the most accurate and stable predictions across all targets, outperforming baseline linear models and commonly used empirical correlations. Feature-attribution analysis was used to identify the dominant drivers of each property, showing that binder-related variables primarily govern strength, while aggregate-related variables dominate stiffness. The results support practical, data-driven screening of recycled aggregate concrete mixes and provide interpretable guidance for sustainable mix design.Keywords: Conditional variational autoencoder, Synthetic data augmentation, Gradient boosting regression, Cross-validation, SHAP valuesSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Concrete remains the cornerstone of modern construction due to its availability, versatility, and structural integrity. It is estimated that over 10 billion tons of concrete are produced annually worldwide, placing significant pressure on natural resources and contributing notably to global CO₂ emissions1–3. As construction demands rise, the consumption of raw materials such as natural sand, gravel, and limestone has increased exponentially, leading to the depletion of natural resources and severe ecological impacts4,5. Furthermore, the construction and demolition (C&D) sector generates a substantial volume of waste, a large portion of which comprises concrete rubble and aggregates6. These environmental challenges have stimulated interest in sustainable alternatives, particularly in the use of recycled aggregates to partially or wholly replace natural ones7.

Despite its sustainability advantages, incorporating recycled aggregates in concrete often leads to increased variability and reduced performance in mechanical properties, including compressive strength, split tensile strength, flexural strength, and modulus of elasticity8. These reductions are attributed to factors such as adhered mortar, increased porosity, and weak interfacial zones in recycled aggregates. To ensure safety and performance while embracing sustainable materials, precise and reliable prediction of mechanical behavior becomes imperative. Traditional empirical models and code-based approaches, however, frequently fall short due to their limited capacity to generalize across varied mix designs and material compositions.

Machine learning (ML) has become a transformative approach for predicting the mechanical behavior of recycled aggregate concrete (RAC), overcoming the limitations of empirical and code-based formulations that struggle to capture nonlinear and highly coupled mix interactions. Over the past decade, the literature has evolved from traditional regression and single-algorithm studies toward hybrid ensemble and generative frameworks capable of modeling multiple strength properties with improved robustness and interpretability. Recent advances in applying ML to RAC have improved strength prediction accuracy. However, most prior studies focused on single-target prediction (typically compressive strength), relied on small or homogeneous datasets, and did not address interdependencies among multiple mechanical properties or feature interpretability.

Early work focused on single-target models using classical algorithms and neural architectures. Alabduljabbar et al.9 employed Gene Expression Programming (GEP) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) to predict the split tensile strength of fiber-reinforced RAC, achieving a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.98 and identifying fiber content and water-to-cement ratio as dominant features. Joshi et al.10 used a Random Forest model to predict compressive strength of RAC with manufactured sand, obtaining R² = 0.95 and root mean squared error (RMSE) = 3.74 MPa, confirming ensemble learning’s reliability. Phoeuk and Kwon11 compared catergorical boosting (CatBoost), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) on a multinational dataset and found CatBoost (R² = 0.938) most effective, highlighting ensemble models’ robustness in heterogeneous databases. Similarly,. Tipu et al.12 evaluated ensemble methods and reported XGBoost as most accurate, with cement content and curing age being key predictors. Jaglan and Singh13 integrated Response Surface Methodology (RSM) with ML to model the effects of ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBS) and polypropylene fibers, with Gradient Boosting providing the highest predictive accuracy. Khan et al.14 combined experiments and ML to study recycled aggregate percentage effects on compressive and flexural strength, underscoring the benefit of hybrid empirical–data-driven modeling.

Subsequent research introduced advanced hybrid and optimization-based approaches. Tipu et al.15 proposed a Newton’s Boosted Backpropagation Neural Network (NB-BPNN), reaching R² = 0.95, while Qi and Li16 implemented a two-level stacked ensemble that achieved R² = 0.964 with low MAE = 2.60 MPa. Falah et al.17 fused deep learning with XGBoost (RMSE = 2.23 MPa), and Wang18 optimized decision trees through Tree-structured Parzen Estimator–Bayesian Optimization (TPE-BO), improving generalization across replacement ratios.

Recent developments reflect a shift toward multi-property prediction, uncertainty quantification, and generative data augmentation. Lin et al.19 analyzed a large dataset of recycled brick aggregate concretes, applying ML to jointly predict compressive strength and elastic modulus.

Xu et al.20 conducted a data-driven analysis of confined and unconfined RAC compressive behavior, emphasizing feature consistency with effective w/c ratio and confinement effects.

Lin et al.21 explored post-fire mechanical behavior of RAC with fine and coarse recycled aggregates using ML models. Chen et al.22 integrated ML into performance-based design of FRP-confined RAC, revealing the potential of ensemble models in structural optimization. Broader methodological innovations have also emerged. Wang et al.23 developed a multi-training Monte Carlo Simulation–Nonlinear Least Squares (MCS–NLS) framework for service-life prediction of marine concrete with RAC. Chen et al.24 proposed an intelligent limit-state design method for RAC-filled steel tubular columns. Lastly, Wang et al.25 introduced a Bayesian-physics informed Conditional Tabular generative adversarial network (cGAN) for low-carbon concrete data augmentation and hyperparameter optimization, offering a scalable route for data generation in low-sample regimes.

Collectively, these studies reveal a clear research trajectory: from single-target ML and regression-based models to multi-stage ensemble and generative frameworks that incorporate physical realism, probabilistic reasoning, and uncertainty quantification. Nevertheless, most prior works remain limited to one mechanical property or a single algorithmic family, without cross-comparative validation across all strength parameters. The present study advances this frontier by combining Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE)-based data augmentation with a systematic evaluation of seven ML models: Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting Regression (GBR), AdaBoost, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) for predicting four key RAC properties—compressive, split-tensile, flexural strength, and modulus of elasticity—supported by SHAP interpretability. This integrated framework bridges data scarcity, model accuracy, and interpretability, providing a physically consistent and generalizable approach for AI-assisted sustainable concrete mix design.

This study addresses these gaps by conducting a comprehensive comparative analysis of seven supervised machine learning algorithms—DT, KNN, SGD, SVM, GBR, AdaBoost, and RF. A curated dataset of 112 concrete mixes with varying proportions of natural and recycled aggregates was expanded using a CVAE by genrating extra 5000 samples to enhance data diversity and model generalization. Separate predictive models were then developed for four key mechanical properties—compressive, split tensile, flexural strength, and modulus of elasticity. Each model was rigorously trained and validated using 5-fold cross-validation and evaluated with multiple performance metrics, including MSE, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and R², ensuring robustness and reliability across all property predictions. The novelty of this study lies in (1) developing a unified ML workflow capable of accurately predicting multiple RAC strength properties within a consistent modeling framework, (2) incorporating CVAE-driven data augmentation to improve generalization and coverage of under-sampled mix proportions, and (3) applying SHAP interpretability to reveal mechanistic insights into binder–aggregate interactions governing RAC performance. These insights have direct implications for the development of smart mix design tools, the optimization of sustainable materials, and the advancement of predictive modeling in structural materials research.

The database contains information on 28-days compressive strength, split-tensile strength, flexural strength, and modulus of elasticity, covering 56 experimental mixes reported in the open literature26–29. This dataset reflects contemporary efforts to promote sustainability in construction by partially or fully replacing natural aggregates with recycled alternatives, thereby minimizing the environmental footprint of concrete production30–32. Furthermore, the inter-correlations among input features, such as a moderately strong negative correlation is observed between the ratio and recycled coarse aggregate (), suggesting mix proportioning adjustments when substitution levels increase. Similarly, cement content shows a moderate negative correlation with natural fine aggregate (). The weak correlation between compressive strength and elastic modulus (ρ = 0.12) reflects the high material heterogeneity of RAC, where variations in adhered mortar and aggregate stiffness decouple stiffness from strength trends. These inter-correlations among features are critical as they may introduce multicollinearity, which can degrade performance in linear ML models39. Given this, the dataset demands a diverse modeling strategy. Linear models like SGD and SVM will be used in this study as baselines even though they are limited in representing nonlinear interactions and feature thresholds. Other models like KNN, DTs, RF, GBR, and AdaBoost can capture nonlinearity and are particularly effective in datasets with mixed feature relevance and complex patterns40. Accordingly, this study addresses the following research questions (RQs):

RQ1How accurately can linear models (e.g., SGD, SVM) capture the effect of recycled aggregate content on the mechanical properties of concrete, including compressive, tensile, and flexural strengths?

RQ2 Can KNN effectively model the nonlinear and instance-specific behaviors introduced by recycled coarse and fine aggregates in concrete mixtures?

RQ3 To what extent do DTs improve predictive accuracy by capturing nonlinear splits and interaction effects involving recycled aggregates?

RQ4Do ensemble-based models (RF, GBR, AdaBoost) outperform simpler models in predicting the mechanical performance of concrete containing recycled aggregates by accounting for complex interdependencies among mix design variables?

RQ5What insights can SHAP analysis provide regarding the relative importance and threshold effects of recycled coarse and fine aggregates in influencing concrete properties, and how can these insights guide the optimal use of recycled materials in sustainable concrete design?

Methodology

This section outlines the methodology adopted in developing and evaluating the ML models, including data preparation, model selection, hyperparameter tuning, and performance assessment techniques used to predict the strength characteristics of RAC.

Data preparation module

Data splitting

To avoid optimistic bias, model development and all preprocessing steps were performed using cross-validation on the training data only. The dataset was first randomly split into a locked hold-out test set (20%) and a development set (80%) using a fixed random seed. Within the 80% development set, 5-fold cross-validation was used for hyperparameter tuning and model selection41. For each fold, scaling parameters were fitted on the corresponding training fold only and applied to its validation fold. The final reported performance metrics are computed on the locked real-only test set, which was not used in tuning, augmentation training, or model selection42. The 80/20 split was selected to retain sufficient data for training given the small sample size, while still reserving an untouched test set; sensitivity checks with 75/25 and 70/30 produced consistent model rankings.

Data standardization

Standardization of input features was performed prior to model training to ensure uniformity in feature scale, particularly for algorithms sensitive to feature magnitudes such as SVM, KNN, and SGD. In contrast, tree-based models (e.g., DTs, RFs, Adaboost) are generally unaffected by scaling due to their split-based structure. However, for consistency across all ML models and to facilitate fair comparison, standardization was uniformly applied in this study. The process involved fitting a StandardScaler on the training data, which calculated the mean and standard deviation of each feature. These parameters were then used to transform both the training and test sets using the formula:

This approach helps avoid data leakage by ensuring that information from the test set is not introduced during training. Such leakage can artificially inflate performance metrics and lead to misleading conclusions43. Recent studies confirm the effectiveness of standardization. Biswas and Rajan44 showed that standardization improves model accuracy in gradient-based and distance-based models, especially in medium to large datasets. Similarly, García-Carrasco et al.45 emphasized the importance of computing scaling parameters exclusively from the training data to maintain the integrity of model evaluation. Furthermore, Bouchene and Fatima46 highlighted that although tree-based models are scale-invariant, applying consistent preprocessing across models simplifies pipeline design and enhances reproducibility.

Data augmentation module

The experimental dataset consisted of only 112 mix designs, covering a limited range of variable combinations. Such data scarcity can restrict the representativeness and generalization capability of data-driven models, particularly when predicting RAC properties across varying mix ratios and aggregate replacement levels. To overcome this limitation and enhance reproducibility, a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) framework was implemented to synthetically expand the dataset while preserving its physical realism and statistical integrity.

Methodology

A CVAE47 was adopted to learn the joint distribution of inputs and targets from the training split only. To prevent information leakage, the generative model was trained inside the cross-validation loop using training folds only. Synthetic samples were generated only from the fold-specific training data. In addition, supervised models were evaluated and benchmarked on a real-only locked test set. Where synthetic samples were used, augmentation was restricted to the input space, while targets for evaluation remained real measurements; synthetic pairs are reported only as an auxiliary robustness check. Previous studies48,49 in structural engineering have used this approach for data augmentation.

Inputs were the eight mix descriptors ; targets were . All variables were Min-Max scaled to . The CVAE structure was optimized with Bayesian optimization using Optuna50,51.

The optimized structure for both the decoder and encoder were fully connected layers (1 layer of hidden size 192; latent space dimension 12, Lr = 0.003, Kullback–Leibler (KL)-weight = 2.3 × 10− 4, y-weight = 0.43) with 720 epochs. With this optimized structure, synthetic mixes were conditionally sampled around the dataset’s mean target vector. Physical plausibility was enforced by the scaler bounds; no test/validation samples were used during training or generation. The overall structure of the CVAE is illustrated in Fig. 3. The encoder maps concatenated input–target pairs to a latent representation defined by its mean and variance vectors. During training, latent sampling is performed via the reparameterization trick, while the decoder reconstructs both and from . This conditional formulation enables generation of realistic input features conditioned on desired target properties.

Hyperparameter tuning and evaluation of model performance

Hyperparameters, set before training, are crucial for optimizing model performance. This study used 5-fold cross-validation (see Fig. 6) to ensure robust evaluation and reduce overfitting. Instead of grid search, Bayesian optimization with Optuna50 with a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator sampler (TPE). was employed for efficient hyperparameter tuning by modeling the performance function probabilistically. For reproducibility, we fixed the random seed (seed = 42) for the sampler and for all stochastic models. The optimization objective was to maximize the mean 5-fold cross-validated computed on the training data. Each Optuna study was run for 1000 trials per target–model combination. The best hyperparameter set was chosen as the configuration achieving the highest mean cross-validated , and the corresponding model was then refit on the full training split before final evaluation on the locked test set. This approach has proven effective in prior studies52,53. Due to space limitations, the full Optuna search spaces and tuning scripts for all model–target combinations are provided in the project’s GitHub repository (see Data/Code Availability). Analysis of relative influence and sensitivity analysis

To enhance the interpretability of the headline boosting model (GBR) across all mechanical properties of recycled aggregate concrete, we performed a sensitivity/feature-attribution analysis using the SHAP framework. For consistency, SHAP was applied to GBR for all targets because it was among the best-performing models and enables stable, efficient SHAP computation for tree-based ensembles. Although SVM achieved comparable accuracy for some targets, GBR was retained as the single explainable headline model to ensure a uniform interpretation basis across properties. SHAP is a game-theoretic approach that assigns each feature an attribution value for a particular prediction, enabling interpretation of complex models by quantifying feature contributions59. Importantly, SHAP values were computed only on held-out data (out-of-fold predictions within the 5-fold cross-validation) and were verified on the locked real-only test set, rather than on the full dataset. Feature importance was obtained by averaging the absolute SHAP values over the held-out samples, which quantifies the mean influence of each mix parameter on the predicted property. Standard dependence diagnostics checks that is Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs) and Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) curves were used to confirm the reported effect directions and trend shapes for the top drivers; however, these diagnostic plots are not presented in the manuscript to keep it concise.

Compressive strength

SHAP analysis indicates that the water-to-cement (w/c) ratio and cement content are the most influential predictors of compressive strength, followed by natural coarse aggregate content and water content. As shown in Fig. 15, these features have the highest mean absolute SHAP values, highlighting the critical role of binder composition and mix proportion in determining strength. In contrast, other variables show notably lower importance: the aggregate-to-cement (a/c) ratio in particular has SHAP values near zero across its range, indicating minimal relevance to compressive strength predictions.

Mean absolute SHAP values for each input feature used in the compressive strength prediction AdaBoost model

SHAP dependence plots (Fig. 16) further illustrate how these features affect the compressive strength. The model predicts higher strength with lower w/c ratios, and this effect is especially pronounced when the recycled coarse aggregate content is low. This aligns with experimental findings60–62 that lowering w/c (thus reducing porosity) significantly boosts strength, particularly in mixes with limited recycled aggregate replacement. Likewise, a higher cement content contributes positively to compressive strength predictions – an expected result since more cement yields a denser, stronger matrix – with the strongest gains observed at moderate water levels (adequate workability without excessive w/c). Natural coarse aggregate shows a weak but generally positive effect on strength, whereas recycled aggregates (coarse or fine) and natural fine aggregate exhibit smaller and less consistent impacts. This suggests that, given a well-proportioned mix, the negative effects of using recycled aggregate on compressive strength can be secondary to the effects of w/c and cement content. Overall, these results confirm that compressive strength in RAC is primarily governed by the binder-related parameters (cement content and w/c ratio), with aggregate type and water amount playing context-dependent, secondary roles. This is consistent with numerous studies reporting that mix design factors like a low w/c and sufficient cement can largely offset the strength reductions due to recycled aggregate use63.

Split tensile strength

For split tensile strength, the SHAP analysis reveals that water content, cement content, and w/c ratio are the most influential features, as shown in Fig. 17. These variables have the highest average SHAP values, indicating that binder quality and mix water availability are key drivers of tensile strength. Aggregate-related features such as recycled coarse aggregate and natural coarse aggregate show only moderate importance, while fine aggregates (natural or recycled) and the a/c ratio contribute minimally to the model’s predictions. In essence, the model learns that the quantity and quality of paste (water and cement) predominantly govern the tensile capacity of RAC, whereas variations in aggregate content (especially fines) are less critical within the data range.

Mean absolute SHAP values for each input feature used in the split-tensile strength prediction AdaBoost model

The SHAP dependence plots (Fig. 18) provide further insight into individual feature effects on split tensile strength. Notably, higher water content generally increases the predicted tensile strength, and this trend is most pronounced when the recycled coarse aggregate content is high. This observation aligns with the understanding that recycled aggregates have higher water absorption; providing additional water can improve the hydration of cement and the quality of the ITZ (interfacial transition zone), which is particularly beneficial for tensile performance in high-RCA mixes63. Meanwhile, cement content shows a positive correlation with tensile strength as well – more cement.

SHAP dependence plots showing the individual contribution of each input feature to the predicted split tensile strength. Each dot represents a data point, with trends capturing the main effects and interactions between features

leads to a stronger paste that can carry tensile stresses better, with the effect being especially strong at intermediate water levels (ensuring sufficient hydration without too high a w/c). The influence of w/c ratio on tensile strength is mixed: at lower w/c values, an increase in w/c can slightly improve tensile strength (perhaps due to improved workability and curing when very low w/c is relaxed), but as w/c becomes larger, the SHAP values become more scattered, and the benefit diminishes. This non-linear behavior is in line with empirical observations in64, which noted that beyond an optimum range, increasing w/c hurts compressive strength more than tensile strength, leading to a less clear trend for tensile strength. The recycled coarse aggregate content itself has a nuanced effect: at lower replacement levels, using some recycled coarse aggregate shows a small positive contribution to predicted tensile strength (possibly due to internal curing effects or microcrack deflection), but as the recycled fraction increases, its marginal effect becomes negative or negligible. This tapering off is consistent with findings by65, who reported that split tensile strength drops markedly when a high percentage of natural aggregate is replaced by recycled aggregate. Other features, including natural fine aggregate and natural coarse aggregate, show minimal SHAP value variation across their ranges, indicating little influence on tensile strength in the model. Overall, the model suggests that split tensile strength of RAC is primarily controlled by binder-related parameters and the water–aggregate interactions (ensuring enough water for high-absorption aggregates), whereas fine aggregate content has limited predictive significance in this context.

Flexural strength

SHAP analysis for the flexural strength model reveals that the w/c ratio is the most influential feature, followed by cement content and water content. As shown in Fig. 19, these three variables have the highest mean SHAP values, indicating that the flexural performance of RAC is largely governed by the binder composition and the mix’s water demand. In contrast, the contributions of aggregate variables are significantly lower. Both fine and coarse aggregates (whether natural or recycled) show only marginal influence on flexural strength predictions, with features like recycled coarse aggregate, a/c ratio, and natural coarse aggregate registering near-zero mean SHAP values. This suggests that, within the range of mixes studied, differences in aggregate type or quantity do not drastically affect the flexural capacity as long as the paste quality and volume are sufficient – a reasonable outcome since flexural strength is known to depend strongly on matrix properties and the bond at the paste–aggregate interface.

Mean absolute SHAP values for each input feature used in the flexural strength prediction AdaBoost model

The dependence plots (Fig. 20) confirm these trends for flexural strength. Interestingly, the model shows that increasing the w/c ratio consistently increases the predicted flexural strength, particularly for mixes with lower recycled coarse aggregate content. While higher w/c generally weakens compressive strength, a slight increase in w/c may improve workability and bonding in flexure, especially when using recycled aggregates that can otherwise cause stress concentrations or incomplete hydration if the mix is too dry. This trend is in line with experimental observations reported in66, which noted that very low w/c ratios can make RAC mixes harsher and more prone to micro-cracking, thereby reducing flexural performance. Cement content also shows a positive contribution to flexural strength – higher cement (especially at adequately low w/c) leads to a richer matrix that improves load redistribution and crack bridging capacity, with pronounced effects observed at the upper range of cement content when water is not limiting. Water content has a mildly positive effect on flexural strength as well, though its influence is more variable and depends on the aggregate condition: for mixes with very high recycled aggregate content, extra water might improve the interface, whereas in mixes with all-natural aggregates, excessive water could simply raise w/c without much benefit. In contrast, the fine and coarse aggregate contents (natural or recycled) individually show very little effect on the model’s flexural strength output – their SHAP values remain clustered near zero, indicating that within this dataset, variations in aggregate replacement or quantities did not create large swings in flexural strength once other factors were accounted for. Overall, the SHAP results indicate that flexural strength of RAC is primarily controlled by binder-related parameters (w/c ratio, cement, water), while the aggregate composition plays a limited secondary role in influencing flexural performance.

Modulus of elasticity

For the modulus of elasticity (Ec), the SHAP analysis (Fig. 21) highlights a starkly different importance ranking: aggregate-related features dominate the influence on Ec predictions. In fact, the recycled fine aggregate content yields the highest mean SHAP value, followed by natural fine aggregate, recycled coarse aggregate, and then cement content. These four features contribute the most to variability in the elastic modulus predictions, confirming that the composite nature of the aggregate phase (especially the inclusion of weaker, more porous recycled aggregates) is central to determining concrete stiffness. This finding aligns with prior experimental evidence that replacing natural aggregates with recycled ones (particularly fine aggregates) significantly reduces the Young’s modulus of concrete due to the lower stiffness of attached mortar and the compromised ITZ. Meanwhile, mix ratio parameters like water content and a/c ratio, as well as natural coarse aggregate content, show minimal influence on Ec (their mean SHAP values are near zero), suggesting that within the studied mixes these factors did not markedly affect stiffness once aggregate type and cement content were set. The w/c ratio has a somewhat mixed influence: it contributes positively to modulus at lower values (a low w/c leads to a denser, stiffer matrix), but its benefit diminishes as w/c increases, especially in mixes with a high fraction of recycled aggregate. This reflects the interplay where a low w/c can improve the matrix quality, but if too much recycled material is present, the overall stiffness is still governed by the aggregate phase more than the matrix.

Mean absolute SHAP values for each input feature used in the elastic modulus prediction AdaBoost model

The SHAP dependence plots (Fig. 22) illustrate these interactions in detail. Increasing the recycled fine aggregate content leads to a strong decrease in predicted elastic modulus – the SHAP values drop sharply – which is most pronounced when recycled coarse aggregate content is also high. This indicates that using a large amount of recycled fines (which often contain old mortar) significantly compromises stiffness, and the effect is exacerbated if the coarse aggregate is also recycled, likely due to the combined reduction in aggregate stiffness and weaker overall aggregate-matrix interfaces. Interestingly, a moderate amount of recycled coarse aggregate on its own shows a slight positive effect on Ec in the model (perhaps due to some internal curing or densification effect at low replacement levels), but at higher recycled coarse contents, the variability increases and the net effect can become negative – especially when paired with a high w/c ratio which further weakens the matrix. Similarly, natural fine aggregate content exhibits an inverse relationship with modulus: at higher natural sand contents (meaning lower coarse aggregate proportion), the model predicts reduced stiffness, particularly when the water content is elevated. This may be capturing the fact that higher fine aggregate ratios (whether natural or recycled) lead to a higher mortar fraction and lower aggregate interlock, thus a lower overall modulus. On the other hand, cement content positively impacts Ec – more cement yields a stronger matrix and better load transfer, with the greatest gains seen when water content is kept low (preventing excess porosity). Features like water content, a/c ratio, and natural coarse aggregate percentage have little to no direct effect on the elastic modulus in the SHAP analysis (their points scatter near zero), implying their influence is largely indirect or accounted for by other variables in this dataset. Finally, as noted, the w/c ratio shows a positive contribution at the lower end (indicating that a tighter mix improves stiffness), but beyond a certain point increasing w/c adds little or no benefit to modulus, especially in mixes rich in recycled aggregates where aggregate deformability governs the behavior. Overall, the SHAP results confirm that aggregate type, quantity, and their interactions are the primary drivers of Ec in recycled aggregate concrete, while mix proportions (cement, water, w/c) play a secondary but context-dependent role. These findings are in line with previous experimental studies67,68 which reported that the modulus of elasticity is significantly reduced by the inclusion of recycled aggregates (particularly fine ones) and can be only partially compensated by a strong cement matrix. In practical terms, this underlines the importance of aggregate selection and quality for maintaining stiffness in RAC, alongside optimizing the binder content.

General trends of parameter influence

Across all mechanical properties, several consistent trends emerge from the SHAP analyses. Binder-related parameters—particularly the water-to-cement (w/c) ratio and cement content—exert the strongest overall influence on RAC performance. Lower w/c ratios and higher cement contents enhance strength and stiffness by producing a denser matrix, while excessive water generally reduces performance. Water content shows a dual effect: it improves tensile and flexural strength at moderate levels (by aiding hydration and workability) but weakens compressive strength when excessive. Aggregate characteristics dominate the prediction of modulus of elasticity, with higher recycled-aggregate fractions—especially recycled fines—reducing stiffness due to their lower intrinsic modulus and weaker ITZ. The effects of natural coarse aggregates are generally positive but secondary compared with binder variables. Overall, binder composition controls strength-related properties, whereas aggregate quality and proportion govern stiffness, confirming that optimizing both paste quality and aggregate selection is critical for achieving balanced mechanical performance in RAC.

Discussion

The strongest performers were ensemble and kernel-based models, which are well-suited to capturing nonlinear relationships and feature interactions in heterogeneous materials data (e.g., gradient-boosted trees and support vector regression69–71. The relatively weak performance of linear baselines is consistent with the presence of multicollinearity and threshold-type behavior that cannot be represented by additive linear forms70,71. While recently proposed high-correlated-variables creator-machine variants and dynamic ensemble selection methods72 may further improve accuracy in related regression settings, the primary contribution here is a transparent hybrid workflow that (i) mitigates small-sample limitations through leakage-safe generative augmentation, (ii) compares multiple learners on multiple RAC properties within one consistent protocol, (iii) benchmarks against common empirical correlations on the same real-only test set, and (iv) provides interpretable drivers of performance suitable for mix design decision support72,73.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study developed and evaluated a leakage-safe hybrid workflow for predicting the compressive strength, split tensile strength, flexural strength, and elastic modulus of recycled aggregate concrete (RAC) from mix proportions. Across the seven tested learners, nonlinear models—particularly GBR and support vector regression (SVR)—provided the most accurate and stable predictions, while linear baselines were consistently less reliable for the heterogeneous RAC dataset. Benchmarking against commonly used empirical correlations on real-only data showed that the data-driven models generally reduce systematic bias and scatter, with improvements becoming more pronounced at higher recycled aggregate replacement levels. Explainability analysis (SHAP computed on held-out data and verified on the locked test set) indicates that binder-related variables predominantly control strength-related outputs, whereas aggregate-related variables, especially recycled fine content, contribute strongly to stiffness prediction.

The main limitation is the small dataset size and coverage, which restricts generalization outside the observed mix-design space. In addition, we emphasize that model accuracy is primarily limited by data coverage; as the dataset grows and includes richer descriptors of recycled aggregate quality, performance is expected to improve. The proposed workflow is designed to be readily extensible to newer learners (including High Correlated Variables Creator Machine (HCVCM)/Stronger Variable Creator Machine (SVCM)/Dynamic Selecting Machine (DSM)-type methods) under the same leakage-safe evaluation protocol. HCVCM) identifies highly correlated variables and transforms them into composite features such as interaction terms or weighted combinations72. Instead of removing correlated predictors, it leverages their relationships to enhance predictive strength and extract deeper structural information from the data. In the case of DSM, it is an adaptive feature selection system that continuously updates the most relevant variables during model training76. Moreover, it improves model accuracy and generalization by dynamically adjusting the feature subset based on performance feedback. However, SVCM generates more powerful predictive features from weaker variables through nonlinear transformations and interactions77. Its purpose is to amplify hidden patterns and increase the overall robustness and predictive capability of the model.

Future work should also incorporate uncertainty quantification (e.g., prediction intervals) and extend the framework to durability indicators to support risk-aware, sustainable mix design. For practical screening and decision support within the studied data range, GBR is recommended as a robust headline model due to its accuracy and consistent interpretability, with SVR as a competitive alternative where appropriate.
