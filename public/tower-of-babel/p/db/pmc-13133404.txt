Multicomponent solid-solution alloy negative electrode for Li-metal batteries
Authors: Jinxi Wang, Jiawen Zhu, Yichao Cai, Huimin Zhang, Xinpeng Li, Zongzi Jin, Zhuoying Zhu, Deguang Liu, Zhen Zhang, Peichen Zhong, Yuansen Xie, Wenhui Zhu, Guolei Cai, Huanyu Xie, Rong Huang, Yuhao Lu, Shuhong Jiao, Song Jin, Hengxing Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLithium metal batteries possess the high theoretical specific energy owing to the high theoretical specific capacity of lithium metal. However, practical implementations necessitate a reduction in both lithium mass fraction and lithium utilization within the negative electrode to enhance cycling life and suppress dendrite formation, resulting in a practical reversible capacity of only 30 – 50% of the theoretical value. Herein, we present a lithium metal-based multicomponent solid-solution alloy comprising approximately 90 wt.% lithium, with equal atomic ratios of cadmium, silver, magnesium, and aluminium constituting the remaining 10 wt.%. The increased mixing entropy enables high lithium-atom diffusivity, facilitating inward lithium transport into the metal foil rather than the surface deposition typically observed in conventional lithium metal negative electrodes, while simultaneously promoting a thermodynamically stable (110) crystal facet. These factors collectively yield a dendrite-free negative electrode with a reversible specific capacity of 3100 mAh g−1. One-ampere-hour pouch cells employing this negative electrode and a LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 positive electrode achieve an specific energies of 385 Wh kg−1 (based on the total mass of the pouch cell) with 82% capacity retention over 600 cycles. This achievement highlights the potential of this alloy negative electrode to enable safe and durable high-energy-density lithium metal batteries for practical applications.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries, Energy, Batteries

Introduction

The pursuit of high-energy-density batteries for emerging electric aircraft and other advancements in the low-altitude economy has stimulated a significant increase in research on Li metal batteries (LMBs) owing to the high theoretical specific capacity (3860 mAh g−1) and the lowest redox potential ( − 3.04 V versus the standard hydrogen electrode) of Li metal negative electrode1,2. Current investigations have predominantly centered on the challenges posed by uncontrolled Li dendrite growth, which results in low Coulombic efficiency (CE), poor cycle life expectancy, and potential safety hazards due to internal short-circuiting3,4. In an effort to mitigate Li dendrite formation and extend the batteries’ cycle life, a variety of extensive strategies have been employed, including the optimization of electrolytes and additives5,6, the development of artificial protective layers7,8, the utilization of three-dimensional host scaffolds9, and the incorporation of Li-Ag binary solid-solution alloy10,11.

In the practical application of LMBs, it is imperative to not only address the challenges associated with Li dendrites and battery life span but also consider the Li mass fraction within the electrode (M) and Li utilization (U) during cycling. These factors directly determine the achievable practical specific capacity of the Li metal negative electrode, which can be expressed mathematically as: Practicalspecificcapacity=3860mAhg×M×U 12. Current designs of Li metal electrodes compromise Li mass fraction, which is below 60 wt.%, or Li utilization, which is below 80 %, in an effort to extend cycling life and avoid the Li dendrite (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1)11,13–22. This trade-off results in a practical capacity typically below 2000 mAh g−1, thereby diminishing the inherent advantage of Li metal’s high specific capacity. Consequently, LMBs still suffered from the unsatisfactory trade-off between specific energy and cycling life (Fig. 1b)14,15,22–33. Taking a 10 Ah Li | |LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 pouch cell as an example, the positive electrode employs a standard commercial positive electrode with an areal capacity of 3.5 mAh cm−2. If the negative electrode applies a 50 μm-thick Li metal, equaling to an areal capacity of 10 mAh cm−2, which is coated on both sides of an 8 μm-thick copper foil—the most commonly used commercial-scale Li-Cu-Li composite foil available for pouch cells—the Li mass ratio and the Li utilization within the negative electrode would be calculated to be 41 wt.% and 35%, respectively (Supplementary Table 2)34. This configuration yields a cell-level specific energy of up to 408 Wh kg−1 (based on the total mass of the pouch cell; Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Should the Li mass fraction within the electrode and Li utilization be improved to 100 wt.% each, a higher specific energy (495 Wh kg−1, based on the total mass of the pouch cell) will be achievable (Supplementary Note 1). Therefore, the development of a dendrite-free Li metal negative electrode with a high Li mass ratio and utilization is essential for advancing the specific energy of Li metal batteries.

We hereby present a multicomponent solid-solution alloy, consisting of 90 wt.% metallic Li, referred to as Li-MSSA, with Cd, Ag, Mg, and Al constituting the remaining 10 wt.% in equal atomic ratios of 1:1:1:1. The increased mixing entropy within the Li-MSSA alloys enhances compatibility among the elements, thereby preventing phase separation and mitigating the formation of intermetallic compounds, which facilitates the establishment of a solid-solution-based metal alloy phase. The Li-MSSA presents rapid Li atom diffusion throughout the bulk of the electrode, rendering inward transfer of Li atoms into the metallic foil while concurrently directing the deposited Li toward the formation of the thermodynamically stable (110) crystal facet. These two effects lead to a reversible and dendrite-free lithiation-delithiation process of Li-MSSA, realize an combination of a high Li mass ratio and Li utilization, which leads to a reversible specific capacity of 3100 mAh g−1 with areal capacity reaching up to 20 mAh cm−2, depending on the thickness of the Li-MSSA foil. The ampere-hour level Li-metal pouch cells, comprising the configuration Li-MSSA | |LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2, achieve a balance of high specific energy and excellent cycling performance.

Results

Structure and electrochemical properties of Li-MSSA

The solid-solution-based metal alloy phase exhibits lower energy barriers for phase transition in comparison to intermetallic compounds. This characteristic results in markedly enhanced reversibility and cycling stability during the Li-alloying/dealloying process, as the solid-solution reaction accommodates a degree of stoichiometric tolerance to variations in Li atom concentrations and leads to significantly less structural alteration10,11. Consequently, we selected metallic elements Ag, Mg, and Cd, which are recognized for their broad solid solution ranges with Li atoms, as alloying constituents35–37. Given the diverse melting points and compatibilities of Mg, Ag, and Cd, Al was chosen as a “binder” due to its negative enthalpy of mixing38, which promotes a high chemical affinity among distinct atomic species and guarantees the homogeneous dispersion of metallic components. The Li-MSSA was synthesized by heating a mixture of Li, Cd, Ag, Mg, and Al metallic powders at 600 °C under mechanical stirring in an argon-filled glovebox (see “Methods” section for detail). The resultant Li-MSSA comprises 90 wt.% metallic Li, with Cd, Ag, Mg, and Al making up the remaining 10 wt.% in an equal atomic ratio of 1:1:1:1 (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3). Atomically resolved high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) spectrum imaging (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 3) shows that Ag, Cd, Mg, and Al are co-spatial within our spatial-resolution and counting-statistics limits, with no detectable nanoscale segregation; large-area scanning electron microscopy coupled with (SEM-EDX) maps corroborate this (Supplementary Fig. 4). Because Li cannot be mapped reliably by EDX, we performed electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) spectrum imaging at the Li K-edge; after standard background subtraction, the Li-K signal is uniform across the probed area, with no evidence of Li-rich/Li-poor domains down to sub-100-nanometer length scales (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 5). Taken together, these microscopy and spectroscopy results indicate that, within our nanometer-scale resolution, the delithiated Li-MSSA is compositionally uniform and behaves as a single-phase random solid solution, rather than a mixture of element-rich regions or intermetallic secondary phases. Of particular interest, as shown in Fig. 2c, a 20-meter-long Li-MSSA foil can be readily fabricated through heating-rolling process. Its thickness can be adjusted to several hundred micrometers or reduced to tens of micrometer (Supplementary Fig. 6). This characteristic enables the Li-MSSA negative electrode to align positive electrodes of varying areal capacities, thus providing a broad range for optimizing full-cell design, which is critical for battery engineering. It is noteworthy that the heating-rolling process for preparing the Li-MSSA is commonly utilized in industrial-scale metal processing, showing the feasibility of scaling the Li-MSSA technology for practical applications.

To evaluate the practical specific capacity of our electrode, Li was extracted from the Li-MSSA within an electrochemical cell comprising Cu foil as the counter electrode. We observed that a 30 μm-thick Li-MSSA can undergo reversible lithiation and delithiation at potentials of −25 mV and 25 mV, respectively (Fig. 2d). During this process, the Li atom stoichiomitry varies from Li9.9M to Li88M (M stands for the Cd, Ag, Mg, and Al in the Li-MSSA), resulting in a high reversible specific capacity of 3100 mAh g−1 and an areal capacity of 6 mAh cm−2 (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Note 2). This value is within the upper range of practical values reported for lithium-metal electrodes when both lithium mass fraction and utilization are considered (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). This high reversible specific capacity corresponds to a substantial Li utilization of 89% for the Li-MSSA (Supplementary Note 3). To underscore the critical importance of high Li utilization in practical LMBs, in the scenario involving the same 30 μm-thick Li-MSSA, a reduction of the reversible areal capacity to 4 mAh cm−2 results in a decrease in Li utilization to 59%, consequently lowering the electrode’s specific capacity capacity to 2060 mAh g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 7). The practical application potential of our negative electrode is further demonstrated using Li-MSSA foils of varying thicknesses (5–100 μm), which achieve a consistent Li utilization of 89% alongside an elevated areal capacity of up to 20 mAh cm−2, compared with 4 mAh cm−2 typically used in Li-ion and Li-metal batteries (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 8). Therefore, the Li-MSSA electrode provides a practical route to meet diverse electrode capacity requirements for practical battery applications.

To underscore the importance of a multicomponent design—comprising at least five elements—for achieving highly reversible cycling at high Li mass ratio and high Li utilization, we prepared a comparison series of quaternary alloy negative electrodes. Specifically, we synthesized Li-MgCdAl, Li-AgCdAl, Li-AgMgAl and Li-AgMgCd. Experimental results revealed that the the Li-MSSA (3100 mAh g−1) exhibited a higher Li storage capacity compared to the Li-based quaternary alloys, achieving capacities of 2620, 2490, 2650, and 2120 mAh g−1 for Li-MgCdAl, Li-AgCdAl, Li-AgMgAl, and Li-AgMgCd, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9). The CE, reflecting the electrochemical reversibility of the Li-MSSA, averages at 99.4 ± 0.2 % over 500 cycles without electrolyte optimization, surpassing its counterparts (Fig. 2f). Under identical conditions, a Li/Cu half-cell (prepared by laminating a Li foil onto Cu) delivers an average CE = 98.3 ± 0.5% over the first 200 cycles; thereafter the CE drops sharply, consistent with dendritic soft-shorting, supporting the practical relevance of the Li-MSSA design over pure Li. These findings illustrate the electrochemical performance of our Li-MSSA electrode, ensures a high Li mass ratio in the alloy along with high Li utilization and highly reversible cycling.

Elemental distribution and morphological evolution of Li-MSSA during lithiation-delithiation cycling

Given that the electrochemical performance of the Li-MSSA negative electrode is intricatedly linked to its lithiation behavior, we investigated the distribution of the constituent elements throughout the depth of the cycled Li-MSSA in its lithiated state. Ex-situ time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) 3D reconstructions were acquired on Li-MSSA electrodes after 10 full delithiation-lithiation cycles at 2 mA cm−2, with 6 mAh cm−2 stripped and 6 mAh cm−2 plated per half-cycle. The ToF-SIMS 3D images (Fig. 3a) of the fully cycled Li-MSSA exhibt nearly overlapping signals corresponding to Li, Cd, Ag, Mg, and Al, thereby indicating a uniform distribution of these elements. This finding is further confirmed by large-area SEM-EDX mapping of the cycled Li-MSSA (Supplementary Fig. 10). The uniform distribution of elements within the cycled Li-MSSA closely resembles the highly reversible delithiation-lithiation behavior of the Li-MSSA characterized by a high Li mass ratio and utility, which collectively contribute to a significant breakthrough in reversible capacity and cycling stability (Fig. 2). To test whether Li-MSSA remains a solid solution after full delithiation, we also acquired atomically resolved STEM-EDX and Li-K-edge EELS on delithiated electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 11). The Ag, Cd, Mg, and Al maps remain co-spatial with no nanoscale segregation or intermetallic domains observed, and the residual Li-K signal after delithiation is likewise spatially uniform across the mapped area (Supplementary Fig. 12). In addition, we further employed hybrid Monte Carlo and molecular dynamics (MCMD) simulations to evaluate phase stability and elemental mixing during lithiation/delithiation across representative Li contents (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14). Across multiple randomized initial configurations, 300 K trajectories (0.5 ns) show no tendency toward elemental clustering either after lithiation or after delithiation; the alloy remains compositionally uniform within the simulation cell.

Furthermore, the uniform vertical distribution of Li, Cd, Ag, Mg, and Al elements in the cycled Li-MSSA indicates that freshly deposited Li during lithiation does not accumulate on the foil surface. Rather, it is likely that Li atoms diffuse within the Li-MSSA foil, a finding that aligns with previous studies on solid-solution alloys, such as Li-Ag and Li-Mg11,39. Given the high electronic conductivity of the Li-MSSA, Li+ from the electrolyte should undergo reduction to formulate Li atoms at the electrolyte/Li-MSSA interface prior to diffusion into the interior. The diffusivity of Li atoms within the Li-MSSA plays a critical role in facilitating the inward transfer of Li into the metal foil. Because the solid-solution alloy has a finite Li solubility, the bulk Li chemical potential varies continuously with composition and transport is therefore chemical-potential-driven10. We therefore employed a non-ideal, chemical-potential-based diffusion formulation to fit the full voltage transients of the GITT dataset, using 0.01 C pulses of 30 s with 1800 s rests between pulses (Supplementary Fig. 15)40. Under this protocol, the fitted Li chemical diffusivities are around 6 × 10−9 cm2 s−1 (Supplementary Fig. 16). As expected, applying other analysis routes to the same dataset—such as the traditional GITT early-time τ approximation, or EIS-based diffusivity extraction—yields values up to one or two orders of magnitude higher relative to our non-ideal, chemical-potential-driven fit40. Notably, these values are higher than the reported room-temperature self-diffusion of Li in metallic Li (typically 10−11 cm2 s−1)41. Taken together, the fitted D is sufficiently high to accommodate the per-pulse Li flux by bulk insertion into Li-MSSA. We believe that the elevated diffusion coefficient of Li atoms in Li-MSSA is attributable to the diverse pathways available for Li atom diffusion, which arise from the increased configurational entropy of the Li-MSSA. With a greater variety of configurations, multiple diffusion pathways, presumably characterized by lower energy barriers, are available, thereby enhancing the kinetics of Li atom diffusion42. Therefore, it is the high Li atom diffusion coefficient inherent in Li-MSSA that facilitates Li atom transfer into the foil, as opposed to surface plating of Li during lithiation process, despite the the framework mass constituting only ~10 wt.% of the entire electrode.

The inward transfer of Li atoms into the Li-MSSA foil effectively inhibits Li-dendrite growth, which markedly contrasts with the behavior observed in conventional Li metal. This is evidenced by the in-situ electrochemical optical microscope (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Movies 1 and 2). Cells configured as Li | |Li and Li | |Li-MSSA were assembled with an areal capacity of 5 mAh cm−2 deposited on the working electrodes. The formation of Li dendrites on the conventional Li metal foil was observed (Fig. 3c and Supplementary 17), revealing heterogeneous growth during the deposition of metallic Li. In contrast, the Li-MSSA electrode exhibited a smooth surface after lithiating, showing only variations in thickness with no uncontrollable mossy Li deposition (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 17). These results indicate that the Li-MSSA facilitates the descent of freshly generated Li atoms at the surface into the metal foil, effectively preventing surface deposition induced Li dendrite growth.

Structure evolution of Li-MSSA in the lithiation-delithiation cycles

We investigated the structural evolution of the Li-MSSA during the delithiation-lithiation process using in-situ X-ray diffraction (XRD). Our observations revealed a transformation in the Li crystallography of the Li-MSSA, shifting from (200)Li orientations to (110)Li orientations (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. 18, and Supplementary Note 4). Initially, the Li-MSSA exhibited the (200)Li and (211)Li peaks at 52.0° and 65.0°, respectively. As delithiation progressed during the first cycle, the intensities of these peaks gradually diminished due to the reduction in Li content (Figs. 4a and 4c). However, upon the completion of re-lithiation, the intensity of the (200)Li peak was significantly weaker compared to its initial state (Fig. 4a). Conversely, a diffraction peak of (110)Li at 36.2° emerged as the most prominet signal at the end of lithiation process (Fig. 4a). Following four cycles of delithiation-lithiation, we observed a marked transformation in the Li crystallography of the Li-MSSA, changing from predominantly (200)Li orientations to (110)Li orientations, with the (200)Li peak becoming undetectable (Fig. 4b). In comparison, the crystallography of the control sample, Li metal coated on a Cu foil (Li/Cu), which initially showed (110)Li, (200)Li, and (211)Li orientations, remained relatively stable even after prolonged cycling (Fig. 4c, d). Furthermore, the electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis elucidated that, in addition to the bulk of Li-MSSA (Fig. 4b), the surface plane of the Li-MSSA foil under went a transformation from (200)Li to (110)Li (Figs. 4e, f). In contrast, the Li/Cu negative electrode consistently exhibited a preference for (200)Li orientations (Fig. 4g, h).

Previous thermodynamic studies suggest that the (110)Li facets exhibit faster lithiation kinetics than the (200)Li and (211)Li43,44. The enhanced lithiation kinetics of our Li-MSSA is evidenced by the exchange current density measurement (Supplementary Fig. 19), whereby the Li-MSSA exhibits an exchange current density almost threefold higher (2.13 mA cm−2) than the Li/Cu negative electrode (0.77 mA cm−2). The (110)Li facets, which facilitate accelerated lithiation kinetics, are expected to diminish the overpotential and reduce the driving force for side reactions, consequently prolonging the cycling life of LMBs.

Unlike the widely used “lithiophilic site” strategy—where alloy additives mainly lower the Li nucleation overpotential to promote more uniform Li deposition19,45,46—our alloy is designed to promote an inward-growth mode, in which Li atoms migrate into the bulk rather than accumulate at the interface10,11. In the lithiophilic approach, once a thin Li layer forms, subsequent cycling still proceeds by Li+ reduction on Li metal at the electrolyte interface; the locus of deposition does not fundamentally change. By contrast, our multicomponent solid solution operates as a single phase with finite Li solubility, so the Li chemical potential varies continuously with composition. Under plating, this establishes a chemical-potential gradient that drives freshly formed Li atoms at the surface to diffuse inward rather than accumulate at the interface—consistent with solid-solution-assisted inward growth10,11. Kinetically, multicomponent solid-solution alloys ( ≥ 5 elements) possess high configurational entropy that induces significant local chemical and strain disorder, thereby broadening the distribution of Li site energies. This, in turn, generates multiple, percolating diffusion pathways and enhances Li mobility—our rationale for employing a multicomponent alloy42,47,48. Consequently, our alloy sustains percolating, quasi-isotropic bulk pathways that provide sufficient subsurface transport to accommodate the interfacial Li flux, thereby preventing interfacial supersaturation. Together with the thermodynamic drive from continuous chemical potential (μLi), this bulk transport relocates Li uptake into the foil interior and suppresses surface deposition. Consistent with this mechanism, our in-situ XRD, TOF-SIMS, and STEM-EDX/EELS indicate a single-phase solid solution across the cycling window.

Electrochemical performance of Li-MSSA in full cells

Our investigations into the Li-MSSA during the lithiation-delithiation cycles, which include an analysis of element distribution (Fig. 3a), Li atom diffusion (Supplementary Fig. 16), and structure evolution (Fig. 4), demonstrate that the Li-MSSA not only facilitates the inward-transfer of freshly generated Li atoms into the alloy metal foil, with a thickness of tens of micrometers, but also promotes the development of preferred (110)Li facets. These two mechanisms are, at least in principle, self-reinforcing, as they mitigate the Li-dendrite growth (Fig. 3d) and reduce side reactions between the electrode and electrolyte (Fig. 2f), thereby enabling the Li-MSSA to achieve a high specific capacity (Fig. 2d) and maintain stable cycling performance (Fig. 2f) in Li metal negative electrodes concurrently.

The Li-MSSA negative electrode exhibits stable cycling and a high capacity, making it suitable for long-term operation in practical LMBs. To facilitate industrial-level evaluation of our Li-MSSA, we designed an Ah-level Li-metal pouch cell featureing rigorous parameters, including an electrolyte usage of 2 g Ah−1, thereby transitioning the Li-MSSA negative electrode into practical applications. This Li-MSSA | |LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 pouch cell achieves an specific energy of 385 Wh kg−1 (based on the total mass of the pouch cell) while sustaining a capacity retention exceeding 82% after 600 cycles (Fig. 5a; and Supplementary Fig. 20; Supplementary Table 4). The charge-discharge curves associated with the Li-MSSA | |LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 pouch cell demonstrate stable behavior over 600 cycles (Fig. 5b). The negligible voltage shifts (i.e., reduced polarization) indicate that the parasitic reaction on the Li-MSSA electrode are significantly mitigated24. Furthermore, based on the performance metrics of this pouch cell (Fig. 5c), the specific energy is estimated to reach 462 Wh kg−1 (based on the total mass of the pouch cell) in a 10 Ah pouch cell (Supplementary Table 5). Li-MSSA-based metal batteries show a combined specific energy and cycling life under the tested conditions, compared with cells using conventional modified Li-metal negative electrodes (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 21). To evaluate the rate capability of Li-MSSA, we assembled Li-MSSA | |LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 and Li/Cu | |LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 full-cells under identical positive electrode loadings (10.8 mg cm−2, Supplementary Fig. 22). C-rates were defined with respect to the positive electrode (1 C = 209 mA g−1, based on a practical capacity of 209 mAh g−1). The Li-MSSA | |LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 cell delivered 208 (0.1 C), 201 (0.2 C), 190 (0.5 C), 175 (1 C), 144 (2 C), 105 (4 C), and 68 mAh g−1 (6 C). By contrast, Li/Cu | |LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 cell delivered a lower capacity of 25 mAh g−1 at 6 C under the same conditions, indicating improved high-rate performance for Li-MSSA (Supplementary Fig. 22). To assess rate-dependent structural evolution of the Li-MSSA negative electrode, we performed ex-situ STEM-HAADF imaging with high-resolution STEM-EDX mapping on Li-MSSA after rate cycling up to 6 C (Supplementary Fig. 23). Elemental maps for Ag, Cd, Mg, and Al are co-localized within our spatial resolution, with no detectable nanoscale segregation or heavy-element clustering. Collectively, the data indicate that (i) under matched full-cell conditions, Li-MSSA delivers a higher usable capacity than pure Li at elevated rates, and (ii) after high-rate cycling (up to 6 C) the negative electrode shows no evidence of rate-induced phase separation or nanoscale compositional partitioning. In conventional Li-MSSA | |LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 full cells, the alloy negative electrode experiences a lithiation-first step upon the initial charge; Li-MSSA therefore accommodates incoming Li interstitially within the matrix. To verify that the structural evolution during charge/discharge follows the same single-phase mechanism analyzed in Figs. 2–4, we harvested Li-MSSA foils from NCM811 full cells at end of charge (lithiated) after 50 cycles and performed cross-sectional SEM-EDX mapping. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 24, Ag, Cd, Al and Mg are spatially uniform across the foil thickness. It is reasonable to assume that if Li metal was formed and deposited at the foil surface, the cross-sectional SEM-EDX would be expected to show a near-surface depletion band of Ag/Cd/Mg/Al in the immediately underlying alloy region due to dilution/redistribution. Neither signature is observed, supporting inward Li diffusion and homogeneous solid-solution lithiation rather than superficial Li plating. To further decouple positive electrode-derived Li, we rebuilt a Li-MSSA | |delithiated-NCM811 full cell in which all active Li originates from the alloy (Supplementary Fig. 25). Commercial NCM811 electrodes (areal capacity 2.3 mAh cm−2) were first charged to 4.3 V vs. Li/Li+ to extract Li (Supplementary Fig. 25a). The fully delithiated NCM811 disks were then assembled opposite Li-MSSA foils (areal capacity 2.5 mAh cm−2) in fresh CR2032 cells (Supplementary Fig. 25b). Because the positive electrode now contains no Li, the cell begins at N/P = 1.1, providing a stringent platform to assess alloy utilization and cycling stability without surplus positive electrode Li. As a control, Li/Cu | |delithiated-NCM811 cells were prepared with areal capacity and N/P ratio matching the Li-MSSA | |delithiated-NCM811 cells. Under identical conditions, the Li-MSSA | |delithiated-NCM811 cell delivered 2.3 mAh cm−2 initially and retained 2.1 mAh cm−2 after 100 cycles, whereas the Li/Cu control showed fluctuating CE and rapid capacity fade (Supplementary Fig. 25c). These results show more stable Li utilization in Li-MSSA compared with the control and suggest that the alloy maintains stable behavior during lithiation-first operation even under low-Li-excess, high-loading conditions. In light of the potential offered by emerging high-energy-density positive electrode materials such as sulfur, Li | |S batteries demonstrate promising prospects of achieving cell-level specific energies exceeding 500 Wh kg−1 (based on the total mass of the pouch cell). Further more, we have developed Li | |S batteries featuring high areal capacities of 3.5 and 7.4 mAh cm−2 (Figs. 5d, e) alongside a low negative electrode-to- positive electrode capacity ratio of 1.1. Both batteries showed minimal capacity degradation, thereby validating the advantages of Li-MSSA and showcasing its practical viability for achieving durable high-loading Li-based cells. It should also be noted that Li-MSSA is compatible with optimized liquid and solid-state electrolytes, in addition to high-voltage high-capacity positive electrodes such as Li-rich manganese positive electrode material, thus affirming the critical role of Li-MSSA in practical batteries boasting specific energies exceeding 500 Wh kg−1 (based on the total mass of the pouch cell) and lifespans of 1000 cycles.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a negative electrode comprising metallic Li-based multicomponent solid-solution alloy, Li-MSSA, aimed to achieve the practical high-energy-density and durable LMBs. The elevated mixing entropy induced by the multi-metal components of Li-MSSA facilitates rapid Li atom diffusion throughout the bulk of the metal foil, rendering inward transfer of Li atoms into the metal foil, as opposed to mere surface deposition, while also promoting the thermodynamically stable (110) crystal facet. Collectively, these effects enable Li-MSSA to maintain a dendrite-free, long-term cycling performance, characterized by both a high Li mass ratio and high Li utilization, ultimately yielding a high reversible specific capacity of 3100 mAh g−1. Consequently, the Ah-level pouch cell achieves a specific energy of 385 Wh kg−1 (based on the total mass of the pouch cell) and retains 82% of its capacity over 600 cycles.

The heating-rolling methodology employed for preparing Li-MSSA is consistent with industrial-scale metal processing standards. This process yields Li-MSSA foils with lengths of several tens of meters and thicknesses ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometers, enabling tunable calculated areal capacities, which provides a considerable range for optimizing battery design — a critical considerable in industrial engineering. The Li-MSSA negative electrode is compatible with positive electrodes of varying chemistries, including Li-rich manganese and sulfur-based positive electrodes, as well as with electrolytes in both liquid and solid states. This versatility highlights the scalability of Li-MSSA for practical LMBs, positioning it as a promising candidate for practical applications, such as emerging electric aircraft in the low-altitude economy. Future research can focus on selecting the contents by incorporating diverse elements or expanding the range of elements within the Li-MSSA to further enhance its electrochemical and mechanical performance. In parallel, it will be important to elucidate the physical origin of the bcc solid-solution stability down to low Li content, through quantitative mapping of the composition-dependent free-energy landscape and defect/segregation energetics. Additionaly, there should be an emphasis on aligning optimized (solid-state) electrolytes to expedite the development of high-energy durable LMBs. Overall, the strategy presented may unlock a scalable, entropy-stabilized approach to tackle the critical bottlenecks associated with LMBs.

Methods

Material synthesis

The Li-MSSA composite was synthesized by heating a mixture of Li, Cd, Ag, Mg, and Al metallic powders to 600 °C at a ramp rate of 5 °C min−1, followed by holding at 600 °C for 2 h under mechanical stirring in an argon-filled glovebox, where the O2 and H2O levels were maintained below 0.01 ppm. The final composite comprises 90 wt.% lithium, with Cd, Ag, Mg, and Al constituting the remaining 10 wt.% in an equimolar ratio (1:1:1:1). Subsequently, the molten mixture was rapidly cooled to −196 °C by immersion in liquid nitrogen, resulting in a dense, gray solid. This solid was then sectioned and rolled to the desired thickness in a dry room with a dew point below −40 °C. Notably, for film thicknesses below 10 μm, rolling was not feasible; instead, a melting and casting approach was employed to achieve the specified dimensions. Lithium foil (Li, 99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), cadmium granules (Cd, ≥99%, Aladdin; purchased via the CASMART procurement platform), silver powder (Ag, ≥99.99% trace metals basis, Sigma-Aldrich), magnesium powder (Mg, ≥99.9% trace metals basis, Sigma-Aldrich), and aluminum foil (Al, ≥99.99% trace metals basis, Sigma-Aldrich) were used as received unless otherwise noted.

Structural characterization

The surface and cross-sectional morphology of the electrodes were analyzed using SEM (FEI Co., Ltd.) at an operating voltage of 10 kV. HADDF-STEM images were obtained by a JEOL JEM-ARF200F TEM/STEM instrument with a spherical aberration corrector operated at 200 KV. For Fig. 2a, STEM mapping was acquired at an electron accelerating voltage of 300 kV with a beam current of 78 pA, a dwell time of 2.0 μs per pixel, and a 1024 × 1024 pixel matrix over an 8.6 × 8.6 nm field of view. The total acquisition time for the image was 205 s, corresponding to a cumulative electron dose of 1.34 × 107 e− Å−2. For Figure S11, STEM mapping was acquired at 300 kV with a beam current of 78 pA, a dwell time of 2.0 μs per pixel, and a 1024 × 1024 pixel matrix over a 6.1 × 6.1 nm field of view. The total acquisition time was 188 s, and the cumulative electron dose was 2.44 × 107 e− Å−2. For Figure S23, STEM mapping was acquired at 300 kV with a beam current of 78 pA, a dwell time of 2.0 μs per pixel, and a 1024 × 1024 pixel matrix over a 12.2 × 12.2 nm field of view. The total acquisition time was 446 s, giving a cumulative electron dose of 1.45 × 107 e− Å−2. For Li STEM-EELS mapping, data were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV with a beam current of 100 pA, a dwell time of 0.005 s per pixel, and an 86 × 80 pixel matrix over a 694.3 × 645.9 nm field of view. The total acquisition time was 34.4 s, corresponding to a cumulative electron dose of 4.79 × 102 e− Å−2. The crystal structures of the electrodes were characterized via X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Rigaku Co., Ltd.) with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) at 40.0 kV and 1 mA. To protect moisture-sensitive samples, a custom transfer box was employed. Elemental distributions on the electrodes and depth profiles were obtained through time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS), conducted over a 50 μm × 50 μm area in positive-ion mode, utilizing a Cs⁺ sputtering gun. With an etching rate calibrated at ~0.4 μm per minute (based on lithium metal foil etching rate), the sample thickness was estimated by multiplying the etching rate by the total etching time. In-situ electrochemical optical microscopy was conducted in a commercial transparent cell (Beijing Scistar) with a glass window. Each cell contained 1.0 mL of electrolyte (1.0 M LiTFSI in DME:DOL, 1:1 v/v, with 2 wt.% LiNO3). Two configurations were examined: Li‖Li and Li‖Li-MSSA. In the Li‖Li-MSSA cells, the working electrode was a Li-MSSA alloy film, and a Li-metal foil served as the counter/reference; in the Li‖Li controls, a Li-metal foil was used as the working electrode. During the optical tests, an areal capacity of 5 mAh cm−2 was deposited on the working electrodes. In-situ XRD measurements were performed using a LIB-XRD cell (Beijing Scistar Technology Co., Ltd.) equipped with a beryllium window to permit X-ray penetration. The alloy electrode, with a diameter of ~16 mm, was cast on the backside of the beryllium window. This in-situ cell was cycled at a current density of 1 mA cm−2.

Electrochemical measurements

All electrochemical measurements were carried out in an environmental chamber maintained at 25.0 ± 0.5 °C and were recorded using LAND CT2001A and NEWARE battery testers. A commercial microporous polypropylene (PP) separator (Celgard 2400, Celgard) was used. The separator has a nominal thickness of 25 μm, a porosity of ~41%, and an average pore size of ~43 nm. For CR2032 coin cells, the separator was punched into 16 mm-diameter disks. To evaluate the capacity, CE, and cycling stability of Li-MSSA, CR2032 coin cells were assembled with Li-MSSA or bare-Cu working electrodes (10 mm diameter), a Li-metal counter/reference (10 mm diameter, 300 μm thick), a Celgard 2400 separator, and 60 μL of 1.0 M LiTFSI in DME:DOL (1:1, v/v) containing 2 wt.% LiNO3. Polyacrylonitrile (PAN, MW = 150,000, Sigma-Aldrich) and sulfur (Sigma-Aldrich) were used to synthesize sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN). PAN and sulfur were first mixed in an agate mortar at a mass ratio of 1: 4, and then heated in an argon-filled furnace to 450 °C and held for 6 h at a ramping rate of 10 °C min−1. For the SPAN positive electrode slurry, SPAN, Super P, poly(acrylic acid) (PAA, average MV ≈ 450000, Sigma-Aldrich), and guar gum (Sigma-Aldrich) were dispersed in deionized water using a high-speed homogenizer at a mass ratio of 8: 1: 0.5: 0.5. The slurry was coated onto a 10 μm Al current collector and vacuum-dried at 80 °C for 12 h. The electrode loading was controlled by adjusting the coating thickness. For Li | |S CR2032 cells, we used a Celgard 2400 separator and 60 μL of 2.0 M LiFSI in n-butyl methyl ether as the electrolyte. Cells were cycled at 0.06 C for high-loading positive electrodes (7.4 mAh cm−2) and 0.2 C for low-loading positive electrodes (3.5 mAh cm−2). Here, 1 C is defined as 1675 mA g−1. The pouch cells were fabricated on a semi-automated pilot line in a dry room (dew-point -55 °C, RH < 0.1 %). In the 1 Ah pouch cells we use a free-standing Li-MSSA foil as both active layer and current collector; no additional Cu substrate is employed. The foil is prepared by hot-rolling a cast Li-MSSA ingot to a thickness of 40 µm, then slit, calendared, and spot-welded to a Ni tab before stacking. The Li-MSSA negative electrode provides an areal capacity of 7.8 mAh cm−2. Commercial NCM811 positive electrode sheets were purchased from Kejing Materials Technology Co., Ltd. The positive electrode contained 96.8 wt.% NCM811. The NCM811 positive electrode was double-side coated onto 10 μm Al foil, delivering 3.5 mAh cm−2 per side (7.0 mAh cm−2 total). Positive electrode and negative electrode sheets were Z-stacked with 12 µm polypropylene separators, tab-welded (Al for the positive electrode, Ni for the negative electrode), packaged in 85 mm × 65 mm laminated pouches, vacuum-dried (60 °C, 12 h), and vacuum-injected with 1.5 mol L−1 LiFSI in 1,2-dimethoxyethane/1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (1.2: 3, v/v) at an electrolyte-to-capacity ratio of 2 g Ah−1. After two C/20 formation cycles, the cells were cycled between 2.6 and 4.3 V using a LAND battery tester, with a 0.1 C charge and 1 C discharge; during cycling, the cells were connected to the tester via leads and placed in an environmental chamber maintained at 25.0 ± 0.5 °C. Pouch cells were cycled under a constant external stack pressure of ~0.2 MPa using a plate-and-clamp fixture. After stacking, electrolyte was injected through the fill port and the pouch was pre-sealed after a wetting/rest period. Following formation, the pouch was vacuum-degassed, and then finally heat-sealed (with the gas pouch trimmed off).

Computational methods

We investigated the distribution trends of each component element in the unit cell of the Li-Ag-Al-Cd-Mg system under charged and discharged states using the GPUMD software package: first, we constructed single-crystal models consistent with experimental observations, featuring quasi-random element arrangements, for the charged state (with an element ratio of Li:Ag:Al:Cd:Mg = 317:8:8:8:8) and the discharged state (with an element ratio of Li:Ag:Al:Cd:Mg = 345:1:1:1:1), where each model contains a total of 9423 atoms; subsequently, we performed simulations at 300 K using the canonical ensemble hybrid Monte Carlo and molecular dynamics (MCMD) algorithm and the NEP89 potential based on neuroevolution potential architecture to achieve system equilibrium and sampling; after the simulations were completed, we determined whether the system tends to form a solid solution with uniform element distribution by analyzing the variation of potential energy with MCMD simulation time and selecting the configuration of the last simulation frame for element distribution visualization. The initial and final configurations from the MCMD simulations are provided in Supplementary Data 1.
