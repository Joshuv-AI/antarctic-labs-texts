Inflammation in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome: key roles and underlying mechanisms–a comprehensive review
Authors: Zhen Xu, Shuo Yang, Yuan Tan, Qian Zhang, He Wang, Jingjin Tao, Qi Liu, Qingchen Wang, Weimin Feng, Zhongxin Li, Chong Wang, Liyan Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome, a newly defined systemic disorder, is characterized by the pathological interplay among diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular disease (CVD). Recent studies have identified chronic inflammation not only as a central mediator in the pathological progression of CKM syndrome but also as a pivotal molecular hub that drives coordinated damage across multiple organ systems. Mechanistic investigations have revealed that aberrant activation of signaling pathways such as NF-κB, Wnt, PI3K-AKT, JAK-STAT, and PPAR constitutes a complex inflammatory regulatory network. Notably, these pathways facilitate inter-organ inflammatory crosstalk, establishing positive feedback loops among the heart, kidneys, and metabolic tissues. This, in turn, amplifies pathological processes such as oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and fibrosis in a cascading manner.This review systematically delineates the multidimensional pathophysiological mechanisms of CKM syndrome, with particular emphasis on the inter-organ inflammatory regulation mediated by key signaling pathways. Furthermore, we explore the translational potential of therapeutic strategies targeting inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) based on the latest clinical evidence, aiming to provide a theoretical framework and novel perspectives for disrupting the vicious cycle of CKM syndrome.Keywords: Obesity, Diabetes, Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, Inflammation, Signaling pathways

Introduction

Obesity, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are intricately interconnected, collectively exerting a profound impact on cardiovascular health and contributing to a complex state of cardiorenal metabolic dysregulation. These chronic conditions interact to influence metabolic pathways, renal function, and cardiovascular homeostasis, posing significant challenges to public health. Currently, China has approximately 140 million individuals with diabetes, 120 million with CKD, and 330 million with cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1–3]. With the ongoing demographic shift toward an aging population, the prevalence of these conditions is projected to rise, further intensifying their societal and economic burden. Given the intricate interplay among these diseases and their widespread health implications, there is an urgent need to establish a comprehensive and systematic framework to characterize this pathological state. Such a framework should not only account for individual risk factors but also integrate the intrinsic connections between metabolic syndrome, CKD, and CVD, thereby enabling a more precise understanding of their interactions and underlying mechanisms.

Recently, the American Heart Association (AHA) introduced the concept of Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome, defining it as a systemic disorder with broad clinical implications [4]. CKM syndrome is characterized by key pathological features, including insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and renal impairment [5]. This condition is associated with increased morbidity and mortality, multiorgan dysfunction, and a substantial economic burden on Healthcare systems. A study investigating the prevalence of CKM syndrome among U.S. adults reported that, between 2011 and 2020, nearly 90% of adults met the diagnostic criteria for CKM syndrome at stage 1 or higher, with 15% already classified in advanced stages [6]. Notably, older adults (≥ 65 years) exhibited a higher propensity for progression to more severe CKM stages [6]. In terms of economic burden, cardiovascular disease alone accounted for an estimated $407.3 billion in direct and indirect healthcare costs between 2018 and 2019 [7]. Given the intricate interplay between metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular dysfunction in CKM syndrome, the associated financial burden is expected to be even greater.

The complexity of CKM syndrome poses significant challenges for early diagnosis, highlighting the need for a comprehensive approach to establish effective screening criteria. Among the key pathological mechanisms, chronic inflammation is increasingly recognized as a central driver of CKM syndrome progression [5]. Inflammatory biomarkers, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), not only serve as indicators of systemic inflammation but are also closely linked to the development and progression of metabolic syndrome, CKD, and CVD [8, 9]. A systematic evaluation of these inflammatory mediators is therefore crucial for elucidating the mechanistic underpinnings of CKM syndrome.

This review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of the roles and mechanisms of inflammation in CKM syndrome, offering an integrative perspective on its contribution to disease onset and progression. By synthesizing current evidence, this review seeks to establish a structured reference framework for researchers and clinicians while informing future investigations and therapeutic strategy development.

CKM syndrome: definition and epidemiology

CKM syndrome is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder involving complex pathophysiological interactions among metabolic risk factors, CKD, and the cardiovascular system. These interactions contribute to multiorgan dysfunction and a higher risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. According to the latest expert consensus from the AHA [10], the diagnostic framework for CKM syndrome encompasses three population categories: (1) individuals at risk for CVD due to metabolic abnormalities (e.g., obesity, insulin resistance, or diabetes) or CKD; (2) those with established CVD attributable to metabolic or renal causes; and (3) individuals concurrently presenting with CVD, CKD, and metabolic dysfunction. Notably, social determinants of health—including public policy, socioeconomic status, and living conditions—may accelerate the progression of CKM syndrome by shaping lifestyle behaviors and influencing patients’ capacity for self-management.

The prevalence of CKM syndrome has been rising in recent years and is strongly linked to adverse health outcomes. A large-scale longitudinal study involving nearly 100000 Chinese adults (the Kailuan study) demonstrated a strong, stage-dependent increase in all-cause mortality associated with CKM syndrome. Specifically, compared with individuals at stage 0, the hazard ratios for all-cause mortality progressively increased across stages 1 to 4, with values reaching 1.24, 1.72, 2.58, and 3.73, respectively. This association was especially pronounced among individuals under the age of 60 [11], underscoring the need for early identification and intervention to delay disease progression and improve clinical outcomes in CKM syndrome.

Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic has significantly altered the epidemiological landscape associated with CKM syndrome. Real-world data analyses revealed a substantial increase in the prevalence of CKM-related components—including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, dyslipidemia, and obesity—between 2020 and 2022, with the prevalence of prediabetes rising by as much as 170% [12]. These findings suggest that external environmental and psychosocial stressors may promote the development or exacerbate the progression of CKM syndrome through both behavioral and metabolic pathways.

Among emerging epidemiological indicators, the triglyceride-glucose body mass index (TyG-BMI)—a simple surrogate marker of insulin resistance—has shown a positive association with CVD risk in individuals with CKM syndrome. Data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) indicated that each 10-unit increase in TyG-BMI was associated with a 6.5% higher risk of developing CVD [13]. Additionally, a study leveraging data from both National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and CHARLS identified a significant association between the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) and all-cause mortality in individuals at CKM stages 0 to 3, suggesting that SHR may serve as a prognostic biomarker for long-term outcomes in this population [14].

Importantly, patterns of adipose tissue distribution—particularly abdominal obesity (AO), elevated waist-to-height ratio (WtHR), and excess adiposity (EA)—have been robustly linked to multiple components of CKM syndrome. A population-based study involving 6,588 adults aged 18 years and older reported a notably high prevalence of EA (65.6%), while the prevalence of AO and elevated WtHR each surpassed 30% [15]. These adiposity indices were independently and positively correlated with CKM-related factors, including hypertension, diabetes, reduced levels of high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and CKD. These findings underscore the central role of adipose tissue dysfunction and fat distribution in the pathogenesis and progression of CKM syndrome, supporting the hypothesis that abnormal lipid metabolism may be a key driving mechanism.

Obesity

Obesity can develop during early life stages, particularly adolescence, influenced by various factors such as genetics, maternal conditions, nutritional intake, and the social environment. This condition is characterized by excessive and dysfunctional adipose tissue, leading to fat accumulation [16]. The abnormal accumulation of fat disrupts the normal differentiation of adipocyte precursors, impairing their ability to regulate circulating lipids effectively [17]. Excess lipids can ectopically deposit in organs such as the liver, skeletal muscle, myocardium, and kidneys, disrupting insulin signaling and ultimately contributing to insulin resistance [18]. TNF-α, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, is highly expressed in response to excess adipose tissue [19, 20]. TNF-α disrupts normal insulin function by activating key intracellular inflammatory signaling pathways [21, 22]. During the development of obesity, macrophages accumulate excessively in adipose tissue and adopt a pro-inflammatory polarized state [23, 24]. These adipose tissue macrophages (ATMs) play a critical role in the onset of glucose intolerance and insulin resistance [24–26]. In obesity, adipocytes also secrete elevated levels of leptin [27]. Leptin not only induces glomerulosclerosis and fibrosis but also promotes oxidative stress in renal tubular epithelial cells, both of which are potential risk factors for CKD [28]. Additionally, leptin triggers hypertension by enhancing sympathetic nervous system activity and reducing nitric oxide (NO) production, thereby exerting detrimental effects on the cardiovascular system [29, 30].

Diabetes

The core pathogenic factor of T2DM is insulin resistance, which refers to the diminished responsiveness of insulin target tissues to physiological levels of insulin [31]. Insulin resistance leads to reduced glucose uptake by cells, resulting in hyperglycemia. This hyperglycemic state can induce non-enzymatic glycation of lipids and proteins, generating advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) [32]. AGEs promote the accumulation and cross-linking of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, contributing to vascular sclerosis and fibrosis, which further progress to diabetic cardiomyopathy and diabetic nephropathy [32–35]. In T2DM, insulin resistance shifts the metabolic profile of tissue cells from glucose metabolism to fatty acid oxidation [36]. This shift leads to excessive uptake of free fatty acids and triglycerides, producing toxic lipid metabolites and inducing mitochondrial dysfunction, which contributes to cardiac fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction [37]. Early in the course of diabetes, renal hemodynamics undergo changes, manifested as compensatory hyperplasia and hypertrophy of the kidneys, an increased glomerular filtration rate, and enhanced tubular reabsorption [38, 39]. As the disease progresses, structural damage occurs in the glomeruli, including thickening of the basement membrane, podocyte loss, and expansion of the mesangial matrix [38, 39]. In the later stages, glomerular sclerosis and fibrosis develop, ultimately progressing to CKD and potentially leading to renal failure.

In T2DM, when the capacity of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) chaperone proteins is overwhelmed, multiple signaling pathways are activated, including the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) and the AGEs-receptor for AGEs (RAGE) signaling pathway [32]. These pathways induce myocardial cell damage and contribute to the development of diabetic nephropathy. Additionally, a reduction in Ca2⁺ ATPase activity increases intracellular Ca2⁺ levels in cardiomyocytes, promoting cell apoptosis and necrosis [40]. During AGEs formation, the excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis, leading to organ dysfunction in diabetic patients [32, 40]. These mechanisms may act synergistically to collectively promote the inflammatory response. Glucose and free fatty acids activate inflammasomes and specific Toll-like receptors (TLRs), particularly TLR4, which stimulate downstream inflammatory pathways [41–44]. Endoplasmic reticulum stress activates intracellular inflammatory signaling pathways, leading to the activation of NF-κB, which further increases the expression of cytokines and chemokines, promoting the recruitment of leukocytes and activation of inflammatory immune cells [32, 34]. This inflammatory response poses a severe threat to cardiovascular and renal health [45, 46].

CKD

CKD is characterized by progressive damage to the glomeruli, renal tubules, and interstitium, leading to glomerulosclerosis and tubular atrophy, ultimately resulting in the loss of nephron function [47]. CKD patients typically present with multiple risk factors, including inflammation, oxidative stress, dysregulated adipokines, vitamin D deficiency, metabolic acidosis, anemia, and microbial toxins, all of which contribute to a systemic insulin resistance profile [48]. Prolonged insulin resistance significantly increases the risk of developing diabetes in CKD patients. Additionally, the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in CKD patients is closely associated with systemic inflammation, exhibiting an inverse correlation [49]. As kidney function declines, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, C-reactive protein (CRP), and fibrinogen progressively increase, alongside elevated urinary protein levels [50]. Notably, elevated levels of IL-6, TNF-α, fibrinogen, and hypoalbuminemia have been identified as independent predictors of CKD progression [51]. In CKD patients, these elevated inflammatory markers are strongly linked to cardiac remodeling and myocardial fibrosis [52]. The decline in glomerular filtration rate also activates compensatory mechanisms, including the RAAS, the sympathetic nervous system, and the calcium-parathyroid axis, all of which have adverse effects on the cardiovascular system [53]. Furthermore, common lipid and lipoprotein metabolic disturbances in CKD patients, such as increased total cholesterol, elevated low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and decreased HDL, promote inflammation and atherosclerosis, significantly increasing the risk of CVD [54].

CVD

Insulin resistance not only contributes to damage to the cardiovascular system, but the occurrence and progression of CVD can also lead to the development of insulin resistance. For instance, studies have shown that heart failure can induce insulin resistance and glucose intolerance [55]. Although the exact mechanisms are not fully understood, it is speculated that during heart failure, cardiomyocytes may increase the uptake of free fatty acids, which disrupt insulin signaling pathways [36]. In CVD states, a decrease in cardiac output leads to reduced renal blood flow, further exacerbating renal dysfunction [56, 57]. Additionally, the activation of the RAAS and excessive sympathetic nervous system activity contribute to increased renal artery pressure. Chronic hypertension induces a series of pathological changes, including structural remodeling of the afferent arterioles, damage to the renal tubules, and glomeruli [58–61]. Simultaneously, elevated levels of asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA) in circulation inhibit the bioactivity of NO, thereby enhancing sympathetic nervous activity and further accelerating the progression of CKD [62–65]. Inflammation plays a critical role in the early stages of atherosclerosis. This process is initiated by the activation of inflammasomes, followed by the adhesion of mononuclear macrophages to the endothelial surface [66, 67]. Stimulated endothelial cells secrete a variety of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, which not only promote the progression of atherosclerosis but also negatively impact kidney function [68]. In conditions of venous congestion, the inflammatory response in endothelial cells is also activated, further exacerbating kidney damage [69, 70].

A brief overview of inflammation

Acute vs. chronic inflammation

Inflammation is a protective response initiated when the body encounters harmful external factors, such as pathogenic infections or tissue injury [71]. Its primary function is to eliminate harmful stimuli, limit tissue damage, and facilitate repair, thereby serving as an adaptive response aimed at restoring homeostasis. In this sense, inflammation functions as a self-regulatory mechanism essential for maintaining physiological balance. Inflammation is generally categorized into acute and chronic inflammation based on its duration. Acute inflammation is a rapid-onset response that occurs within hours to days, typically triggered by infections, trauma, or other external insults. Its primary goal is to swiftly eliminate pathogens or repair tissue damage [72]. In contrast, chronic inflammation is a prolonged inflammatory state that may persist for months or even years, often resulting from unresolved acute inflammation or sustained low-grade stimuli. Chronic inflammation is characterized by persistent tissue damage and repair, which can ultimately lead to fibrosis or irreversible organ dysfunction [73].

With advancements in medical research, scientists have gained a more comprehensive understanding of the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying inflammatory responses, particularly those involved in acute inflammation. The rapid response of the body to infections has been relatively well elucidated [74]. Moreover, significant progress has been made in understanding acute inflammatory responses triggered by tissue injury [72]. At the same time, research has identified key events leading to localized chronic inflammation, especially in the context of persistent infections and autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythematosus [75, 76]. However, the etiology and mechanisms underlying systemic chronic inflammation remain relatively poorly understood. Unlike localized inflammation, systemic chronic inflammation is not confined to a specific organ or system, but is implicated in various diseases, including T2DM, CKD, and CVD [77–79]. Nevertheless, the relationship between systemic chronic inflammation and inflammation triggered by common infections or trauma is not straightforward. Compared to acute inflammation, many aspects of systemic chronic inflammation, particularly its role in complex metabolic and CVD, remain to be fully elucidated.

Chronic inflammation does not appear to rely on traditional inflammatory triggers, such as infection or injury, but is closely associated with abnormalities in the functional systems of internal organs, including the liver, kidneys, heart, and skeletal muscles. Although these systems are not directly involved in host immune defense or tissue repair, they play a crucial role in maintaining overall homeostasis [80]. Therefore, the concept of “homeostatic imbalance” provides a useful framework for understanding the onset of chronic inflammation. Homeostatic imbalance refers to the disruption of the regulatory functions of certain systems within the body, which are unable to maintain normal physiological functions, leading to persistent disturbances in the internal environment and triggering chronic inflammatory responses [81].

Inflammation progression

The inflammatory process is a complex and highly coordinated network. For clarity, it can be broadly categorized into four main components: Inducers, sensors, mediators, and effectors [71]. Inducers refer to exogenous or endogenous stimuli that initiate the inflammatory response, typically including pathogenic infections and tissue damage. Sensors detect these diverse inducers and trigger downstream inflammatory signaling pathways. Mediators, such as cytokines and chemokines, play essential roles in signal transduction and the regulation of inflammatory cascades. Effectors, including macrophages, execute the inflammatory response by eliminating pathogens, clearing necrotic cells, and facilitating tissue repair, thereby contributing to the restoration of homeostasis. Inducers

Inflammatory inducers can be broadly classified into two categories: Exogenous and endogenous. Exogenous inducers primarily include bacteria, viruses, fungi, and their associated toxins. Pathogens initiate immune responses by interacting with pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on host cells. For instance, fungal pathogens can activate innate immune cells via TLRs, leading to the induction of a pro-inflammatory cytokine cascade, including IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α. This activation ultimately contributes to both localized and systemic inflammatory responses [82].

Endogenous inducers, in contrast, originate from within the body and are typically released in response to tissue injury or cellular stress. Common endogenous inducers include AGEs, ECM degradation products, and ROS. AGEs primarily exert their pro-inflammatory effects through interaction with the receptor for RAGE, promoting tissue damage and functional impairment [83]. Upon cellular injury, ECM components such as glycosaminoglycans (e.g., hyaluronic acid) can be degraded into low-molecular-weight fragments with pro-inflammatory activity [84]. Moreover, ROS play a critical role in endogenous inflammatory responses by inducing oxidative stress, which further amplifies cytokine release and exacerbates inflammation [85].

Sensors

In the context of inflammation, inflammatory sensors play a pivotal initiating role by detecting exogenous microbial components or endogenous danger signals. These sensors are primarily composed of a class of proteins known as PRRs, which detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby activating downstream inflammatory signaling pathways, promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and inducing the activation of effector cells [86]. The most extensively studied PRRs include TLRs, nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptors (NLRs), retinoic acid-inducible gene-I (RIG-I)-like receptors (RLRs), C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), and absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2)-like receptors (ALRs) [87].

TLRs are primarily located on the cell surface or within endosomal compartments and recognize bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS), peptidoglycan, and viral nucleic acids, thereby activating inflammatory signaling pathways such as NF-κB. NLRs reside in the cytoplasm and are capable of sensing bacterial components or intracellular stress signals. Notably, activation of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome has been closely associated with metabolic inflammation—such as that observed in obesity and T2DM—by promoting the maturation and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 [88]. RLRs, including RIG-I and melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5), detect viral RNA and mediate the induction of type I interferon responses. CLRs, such as Dectin-1, primarily recognize fungal carbohydrate moieties, whereas ALRs, including AIM2, detect cytosolic DNA and contribute to inflammasome assembly. In addition, the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway plays a critical role in cytosolic DNA sensing and the ensuing inflammatory response [89]. Through the precise recognition of diverse stimuli, these sensors initiate coordinated inflammatory programs and constitute a central component in the initiation and regulation of inflammation.

Mediators

Inflammatory inducers stimulate the production of a diverse array of inflammatory mediators, which, upon activation of downstream signaling pathways, modulate the function of various tissues and organs. As key regulators of the inflammatory response, inflammatory mediators encompass a wide range of molecular types and can generally be classified into seven categories based on their biochemical properties: Vasoactive amines, vasoactive peptides, complement fragments, lipid mediators, cytokines, chemokines, and proteolytic enzymes [71].

Vasoactive amines, such as histamine, are released from mast cells and platelets during degranulation, leading to vasodilation and increased vascular permeability [90]. Vasoactive peptides, such as substance P, are either stored in secretory vesicles or generated through enzymatic processes, exerting dual functions in vasodilation and neural signaling [91]. Complement fragments, such as C5a, are produced via complement activation pathways, with C5a playing a particularly critical role in recruiting neutrophils and monocytes [92].

Additionally, lipid mediators, including prostaglandins and leukotrienes, are enzymatically derived from membrane phospholipids. Among them, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) is a key modulator of vasodilation and pain sensitization [93]. Inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, are primarily secreted by macrophages and mast cells, playing essential roles in endothelial activation, leukocyte recruitment, and the induction of acute-phase responses. Chemokines primarily regulate immune cell migration, directing them toward sites of tissue injury [94]. Lastly, proteolytic enzymes, such as cathepsins, facilitate the progression of inflammation by degrading ECM components and basement membrane proteins [95].

Effectors

Effectors of inflammation comprise various cells and tissues whose responses to inflammatory mediators dictate the nature and outcome of the inflammatory reaction. These effectors include vascular endothelial cells, macrophages, neutrophils, lymphocytes, and other immune-related cell types.

Vascular endothelial cells facilitate immune cell migration and adhesion by modulating vascular permeability and expressing adhesion molecules [96]. Macrophages, as primary immune effector cells, detect inflammatory signals, become activated, and secrete a broad spectrum of cytokines to further amplify the inflammatory response [97]. Neutrophils are key effectors of the early inflammatory response, engaging in pathogen phagocytosis and releasing cytokines and enzymes that contribute to localized immune defense [98]. Additionally, lymphocytes play a significant role in chronic inflammation by secreting cytokines and participating in antigen-specific immune responses, thereby aiding the host in managing persistent infections and tissue damage [99].

Role of inflammation signaling pathways in CKM syndrome

As discussed earlier, the pathophysiological process of CKM syndrome is intricately linked to inflammation. During the activation of the inflammatory response, multiple signaling pathways play critical roles. These pathways not only involve individual tissues but also encompass the coordinated responses of the heart, kidneys, and metabolism-related tissues. As the disease progresses, the interactions and damage between these tissues intensify, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates the pathological condition. NF-κB

NF-κB is a pivotal transcription factor that is ubiquitously expressed in various cell types, including immune cells, endothelial cells, and cells in tissues such as the kidney and heart [100]. In mammals, the NF-κB family comprises five members: NF-κB1 (p105/p50), NF-κB2 (p100/p52), p65 (RELA), RELB, and c-REL [101]. These members can form homodimers or heterodimers, with the p65/p50 heterodimer being the most predominant [101]. Under basal conditions, NF-κB is retained in the cytoplasm through its association with inhibitory proteins (IκBs). Upon stimulation, IκBs undergo phosphorylation and subsequent degradation by the IκB kinase (IKK) complex, leading to the release and nuclear translocation of NF-κB. This process is triggered by various stimuli, including TNF-α, LPS, and fatty acids [102].

NF-κB signaling can be broadly categorized into the classical and non-classical pathways. The classical NF-κB pathway is primarily activated by pro-inflammatory stimuli such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, LPS, and antigen recognition [103]. In contrast, the non-classical NF-κB pathway is predominantly triggered by ligands such as B cell-activating factor (BAFF), CD40 ligand (CD40L), and lymphotoxin β (LTβ) [103]. These stimuli engage specific cell surface receptors, initiating cascades of adaptor proteins that ultimately activate NF-κB signaling [104].

In macrophages, PRRs are broadly expressed and play a critical role in recognizing various microbial components, collectively referred to as PAMPs [105, 106]. In addition to detecting PAMPs, PRRs can also recognize DAMPs, which are released from necrotic cells and damaged tissues [105, 106]. The activation of PRRs typically initiates signaling cascades that converge on the classical NF-κB pathway [107, 108]. Upon activation, NF-κB drives macrophage polarization toward the M1 (pro-inflammatory) phenotype, leading to the robust secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, IL-12, and TNF-α [109]. M1 macrophages not only produce inflammatory mediators themselves but also promote the differentiation of inflammatory T cell subsets, including T helper 1 (Th1) and Th17 cells, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response [110].

Obesity is strongly associated with the activation of chronic inflammatory pathways involving both adipocytes and macrophages in adipose tissue. In obese mice, adipocytes exposed to excessive fatty acids secrete a variety of chemokines and cytokines, including C–C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL-2), macrophage inflammatory proteins (MIP), IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [24]. These pro-inflammatory mediators act synergistically to recruit macrophages into adipose tissue and drive their polarization toward the M1 phenotype via the NF-κB signaling pathway [24]. Compared with lean mice, the proportion of M1 macrophages in adipose tissue increases in parallel with obesity severity, reaching up to 40% in obese mice [23, 111, 112]. Moreover, a significant accumulation of CD8 + T cells has been observed in the adipose tissue of high-fat diet-fed mice, a phenomenon that mirrors the elevated CD8 + T cell levels reported in obese individuals [113–115]. These findings further underscore the pivotal role of inflammation in obesity-related metabolic disorders.

Insulin resistance and T2DM are increasingly recognized as chronic inflammatory diseases, with NF-κB serving as a key regulatory factor in the development of insulin resistance [116]. In response to metabolic stress, pancreatic β-cells undergo a compensatory increase in insulin secretion, which is accompanied by the recruitment of macrophages to clear apoptotic β-cells [117]. During this process, macrophage-derived TNF-α disrupts insulin signaling and diminishes insulin sensitivity [19, 118, 119]. By analyzing publicly available RNA-seq datasets from obese and T2DM patients, He et al. identified elevated mitochondrial ROS levels, increased NF-κB pathway activity in adipocytes, and dysregulated fatty acid metabolism [120]. Mechanistically, activation of the NF-κB pathway promotes the accumulation of the autophagy receptor p62/sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1), which facilitates the clearance of damaged mitochondria by recruiting polyubiquitinated mitochondria. However, excessive mitochondrial damage or hyperactivation of autophagy can elicit inflammatory responses, thereby exacerbating metabolic dysfunction and insulin resistance [120]. Studies have demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB improves glucose and lipid metabolism disorders in mice and slows T2DM progression [120]. Similarly, inhibition of NF-κB activation has been shown to alleviate insulin resistance in L6 muscle cells exposed to hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) [121]. Moreover, findings by Cyphert et al. suggest that NF-κB-mediated airway inflammation reduces systemic glucose utilization, contributing to insulin resistance and triggering secondary inflammation in multiple tissues [122].

NF-κB is a key mediator of renal inflammation in both CKD animal models and CKD patients [123]. Macrophage activation not only mediates acute renal inflammation through the production of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and ROS, but also contributes to the progression of renal fibrosis by excessively producing transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) [124–126]. The role of T cells in kidney disease has been extensively studied, particularly that of CD4 + T cells, especially Th2 cells, which promote the progression of kidney fibrosis in mice by secreting profibrotic Th2 cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13 [127, 128]. CD8 + T lymphocytes can enter the glomerulus and cause damage to podocytes during glomerular capillary rupture [129]. Mechanistically, a study by Li et al. found that NF-κB enhances renal inflammation and promotes the progression of autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease by triggering the transcription of TNF-α and SMYD2 [130]. Moreover, TNF-like weak inducer of apoptosis (TWEAK), as an activator of the non-canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, exacerbates renal inflammation and fibrosis in mice [131–133]. Angiotensin II, by binding to angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R) and activating it, further promotes the phosphorylation of p65 at Ser536, triggering the transcription of inflammatory factors such as monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), IL-8, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-17A [134]. Recent studies suggest that lactate produced by renal proximal tubular glycolytic reprogramming, mediated by 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3), significantly enhances histone lactylation. These lactylation modifications are enriched in the promoter regions of NF-κB signaling pathway genes, thereby activating the pathway and promoting inflammation [135].

The NF-κB-regulated pro-inflammatory pathways also play a critical role in the progression of CVD. During atherosclerosis, endothelial cells activate NF-κB, which induces the expression of cytokines (such as IL-6 and TNF-α) and adhesion molecules (such as E-selectin), thereby contributing to endothelial dysfunction and inflammation [136]. As early as 1996, Brand et al. detected abnormal NF-κB activation in the macrophage nuclei of atherosclerotic lesions [137]. NF-κB essential modulator (NEMO) is a key regulatory protein in the NF-κB signaling pathway, responsible for mediating the activation of the IKK complex, which, in turn, promotes NF-κB activation [138]. Studies have shown that specific knockout of NEMO in endothelial cells protects ApoE −/− mice from high-fat diet-induced atherosclerosis [139]. In NEMO-deficient endothelial cells, the expression of cytokines (such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) and chemokines (such as MIP-1α and MCP-1) is reduced, and the infiltration of macrophages and T lymphocytes into atherosclerotic plaques is also significantly decreased [139]. During the ischemic and repair processes of myocardial infarction (MI), necrotic and damaged cells induce the activation of TLRs, particularly TLR4, which through the MyD88/TAK1 pathway promotes the phosphorylation of IKK, ultimately activating NF-κB p65 and p50. This leads to the activation of a large number of inflammatory factors (such as cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules) [140]. Furthermore, research by Quan et al. demonstrated that inhibiting NF-κB can reduce reperfusion injury following MI [141]. Recent studies also suggest that myocardial cell-derived STING drives heart failure associated with cardiac hypertrophy through NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses [142].

Wnt/β-catenin

The Wnt signaling pathway is essential for multiple biological processes, such as cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and embryonic development [143, 144]. Currently, three primary Wnt signaling pathways have been identified: The canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway, the Wnt/planar cell polarity (PCP) pathway, and the Wnt/Ca2⁺ pathway. Activation of the canonical pathway is initiated by the binding of Wnt ligands to the Frizzled (FZD) receptor and the low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein (LRP) 5/6 co-receptor complex, thereby triggering intracellular signal transduction [145]. Following receptor-ligand interaction, the signal is transmitted via the Disheveled (Dvl) protein, which modulates the activity of several key regulators, including axis inhibition protein (Axin), adenomatous polyposis coli (APC), and glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) [145]. The primary function of this signaling cascade is to inhibit β-catenin degradation, leading to its accumulation and dephosphorylation within the cytoplasm [145]. Dephosphorylated β-catenin subsequently translocates into the nucleus, where it interacts with transcription factors of the T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor (TCF/LEF) family, ultimately promoting the transcription of Wnt target genes [145]. In the absence of Wnt signaling activation, β-catenin is phosphorylated by the degradation complex, marking it for ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby preventing the expression of Wnt target genes [145].

The canonical Wnt signaling pathway plays various roles in the development of adipose tissue [146–148]. Members of the Wnt family, such as Wnt3a and Wnt10b, are involved in the activation of this pathway and inhibit adipogenesis [149, 150]. In contrast, other ligands, including Wnt4, Wnt5a, and Wnt5b, promote adipogenesis by preventing the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [151, 152]. Zeve et al. investigated the effects of enhancing or modifying the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway on adipose tissue development using animal models [153]. Their study revealed that subcutaneous white adipose tissue (sWAT) in Progenitor β-Catenin Activated (P-BCA) mice was almost entirely devoid of adipocytes, leaving only fibroblasts derived from stem cells [153]. While visceral white adipose tissue (vWAT) was retained in these mice, the adipocytes exhibited hypertrophy [153]. Zeve’s research highlights the critical role of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in regulating adipocyte precursor differentiation and site-specific adipogenesis. This suggests that the Wnt signaling pathway not only influences adipocyte development but may also play a significant role in adipocyte turnover, adipose tissue distribution, and metabolic processes.

Leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptor 4 (LGR4) is a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) that acts as a co-receptor of the Wnt-FZD-LRP5/6 complex. In a study by Wang et al., a novel low-frequency functional missense mutation (A750T) in the LGR4 gene was identified in Chinese obese patients compared to controls. This mutation is associated with an increased risk of obesity and metabolic diseases [154]. Carriers of the A750T mutation exhibit more severe fat accumulation than non-carriers, particularly young obese individuals, who show significantly larger waist circumference, waist-to-height ratio, and abdominal visceral fat area [154]. Additionally, other studies have suggested that increased LGR4 activity promotes the development of obesity, further supporting the potential association between obesity and the Wnt signaling pathway [155–157].

The activation of the non-canonical Wnt signaling pathway begins with the binding of Wnt proteins to FZD receptors, which subsequently activate Dvl proteins. Activated Dvl further stimulates Rho-associated coiled-coil kinase 2 (ROCK2) and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK). The activation of ROCK2 contributes to cytoskeletal remodeling, leading to changes in cell adhesion and motility, while JNK activation regulates the expression of downstream genes through the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway [158]. Studies have shown that Wnt5a, a key Wnt ligand that primarily activates the non-canonical Wnt signaling pathway, promotes inflammation in adipose tissue and systemic insulin resistance in obese mice [159, 160]. Based on these findings, Zuriaga et al. further demonstrated that the Wnt5a/PCP signaling pathway is excessively activated in visceral fat, significantly more so than in subcutaneous fat, and contributes to increased local and systemic inflammation associated with visceral fat [161].

Inflammatory responses are commonly observed in the tissues of patients with T2DM, often accompanied by the overexpression of enzymes involved in activating inflammatory pathways, such as cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2). Tian et al. demonstrated that COX-2 inhibitors could alleviate non-alcoholic steatohepatitis and insulin resistance in T2DM rats by inhibiting the Wnt5a/JNK1 signaling pathway and reducing COX-2 expression activated by Wnt5a/NF-κB [162]. TCF7L2, a member of the TCF/LEF family, encodes a high-mobility group transcription factor. Recent genome-wide association studies have identified TCF7L2 as a novel risk gene for T2DM, primarily affecting β-cell function, and it is considered the closest susceptibility gene in the pathogenesis of T2DM [163, 164]. Xavier et al. observed a significant reduction in insulin secretion in response to glucose stimulation following silencing of the TCF7L2 gene in pancreatic β-cells [165]. Figeac et al. found that in newly diabetic rats, an increase in GSK-3β inhibited the canonical Wnt pathway, leading to a significant decrease in TCF7L2 protein levels in pancreatic β-cells [166]. Additionally, variants of the TCF7L2 gene, such as rs12255372 and rs7903146, have been associated with an increased risk of T2DM [167, 168].

Wnt4 knockout mice exhibit severe renal defects at birth, indicating that the Wnt signaling pathway is closely associated with kidney development [169]. Wnt6 promotes the regeneration of renal tubular cells by inducing the formation of new renal tubules and inhibiting the dedifferentiation of epithelial cells [170]. Continuous activation of Wnt1 or β-catenin can prevent renal tubular cell apoptosis and induce the activation of pro-survival pathways [171–173]. In the study by Wong et al., researchers created a transgenic mouse model with conditionally stable expression of β-catenin, specifically activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in renal tubules [174]. This study found that, compared to non-transgenic mice, the transgenic mice exhibited significant proteinuria, elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels, and increased infiltration of M1 macrophages in the renal tubulointerstitium. Inflammatory chemokines, such as CCL-2, IL-17A, IL-1β, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1), and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), were also significantly upregulated in the renal cortex [174]. Further research demonstrated that the Wnt signaling pathway enhances renal inflammation in proteinuria overload through the TLR4/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway [174]. Wnt3a activates renal macrophages and promotes their polarization to the M2 phenotype, driving TGF-β1-dependent ECM production [175]. TGF-β not only exacerbates tubular injury in a mouse model of CKD, but also promotes the activation of the non-canonical Wnt signaling pathway, driving kidney fibrosis [176–178]. These processes can be further amplified by the activation of PRRs, which enhance β-catenin activation, leading to worsened renal dysfunction and increased renal inflammation and fibrosis [179].

Mutations in the LRP6 co-receptor in clinical patients result in elevated levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides, and fasting blood glucose, thereby promoting the development of coronary artery disease [180–182]. Both T2DM and CKD are associated with chronic low-grade arterial inflammation, which may ultimately lead to arterial calcification. One study indicated that TNF-α can induce the expression of bone-related genes, including bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2), Msh homeobox 2 (Msx2), Wnt3a, and Wnt7a, and the upregulation of these genes is closely associated with increased aortic calcification [183]. Additionally, Wnt5a has been shown to increase the expression of pro-inflammatory factors in human aortic endothelial cells in vitro via the non-canonical Ca2+/PKC signaling pathway. These factors include COX-2, IL-6, IL-1α, TLR4, granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), CCL2, and CCL8 [184]. Observational evidence also suggests that the non-canonical Wnt signaling pathway is associated with heart disease. For instance, circulating levels of Wnt5a are significantly higher in patients with heart failure compared to healthy controls, and these levels are correlated with hemodynamic markers of heart failure, such as ejection fraction and filling pressure [185].

However, the role of the Wnt signaling pathway in CVD is not entirely pro-inflammatory. A study by Weinstock et al. demonstrated that IL-4 plays a key role in the resolution of inflammation in atherosclerosis, and this process is dependent on its interaction with the Wnt signaling pathway [186]. In the context of lipid reduction, activation of the Wnt signaling pathway can enhance the anti-inflammatory effects of IL-4, thereby promoting the resolution of inflammation [186]. Additionally, Dickkopf-related protein 2 (DKK2), a negative regulator of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, plays a role in atherosclerosis by modulating macrophage activation. Research by Zhang et al. showed that specific knockdown of DKK2 reduces the polarization of M1 macrophages and increases the polarization of M2 macrophages, which helps alleviate inflammation and promote tissue repair, while attenuating foam cell formation [187]. The inflammatory role of the Wnt signaling pathway across different disease contexts requires further research to validate its diverse biological functions.

PI3K-AKT

The PI3K-AKT signaling pathway is a critical intracellular signaling cascade involved in regulating a wide range of physiological processes, including cell proliferation, survival, metabolism, migration, and differentiation [188]. Activation of this pathway typically begins with the stimulation of receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) or GPCRs, which subsequently activate phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) [189]. PI3K catalyzes the conversion of phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP3), which accumulates locally on the cell membrane and recruits downstream signaling molecules, particularly protein kinase B (AKT) [189]. As the core effector of the PI3K-AKT pathway, AKT regulates several key biological processes through its downstream targets. For instance, activation of AKT promotes cell survival and inhibits apoptosis; it stimulates glycogen synthesis by phosphorylating and inhibiting GSK-3β; and it enhances cellular glucose uptake [190]. Additionally, AKT regulates cell growth and metabolism by inhibiting the Mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), thereby influencing protein synthesis and cell growth [191].

The PI3K-AKT signaling pathway regulates downstream signals and modulates cytokine production through the activation of TLR4 and other pathogen recognition receptors, cytokines, chemokines, and Fc receptors [192]. Under normal conditions, activation of this pathway is crucial for limiting the inflammatory response and promoting the anti-inflammatory response induced by TLRs in macrophages. It has long been recognized as a negative regulator of the TLR and NF-κB signaling pathways and plays an essential role in macrophage polarization [193]. However, in obesity, elevated levels of saturated fatty acids (SFAs) can activate pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, such as JNK and IKK, via TLR4 and the PI3K-AKT pathway. These kinases promote the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, through the activation of transcription factors such as activator protein 1 (AP-1) and NF-κB. The produced pro-inflammatory cytokines further activate other kinases, including mTOR/S6 kinase and MAPK, as well as suppressor of cytokine signaling (SOCS) proteins, which interfere with insulin signaling and disrupt the normal function of adipocytes [194]. Furthermore, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway regulates macrophage polarization through mTOR. For instance, inhibition of the mTORC1 complex enhances the pro-inflammatory response of M1 macrophages [195]. The pro-inflammatory environment induced by obesity also impairs the autophagic function of macrophages [196]. In the obese state, the abundance of intracellular nutrients activates mTORC1, which inhibits macrophage autophagy and leads to the accumulation of cellular waste, further exacerbating inflammation and metabolic dysregulation [197].

Adipose tissue plays a important role in glucose metabolism, which is regulated by the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. This pathway not only promotes the activity of the glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) but also mediates insulin signaling [198]. Studies have shown that inhibition of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway reduces insulin sensitivity by degrading Sort1, a component of GLUT4 storage vesicles [199]. Furthermore, specific knockout of GLUT4 in adipose tissue leads to insulin resistance in skeletal muscle and the liver [200]. As an endocrine organ, adipose tissue not only regulates glucose metabolism but also secretes various cytokines, including adiponectin, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [201]. These cytokines play a critical role in obesity-induced insulin resistance [202]. For example, TNF-α reduces the binding of insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1) to PI3K by phosphorylating inhibitory serine residues, thereby impairing insulin signaling [203]. Meanwhile, TNF-α and IL-1β also antagonize insulin signaling by inhibiting the function of the insulin receptor (INSR), increasing lipolysis [203]. In this context, lipogenesis, IRS-1 activity, and PI3K-AKT-mediated glucose uptake are suppressed, while inflammation, fibrosis, and adipose tissue insulin resistance are aggravated, ultimately resulting in systemic insulin resistance and promoting the development of T2DM.

In skeletal muscle, insulin exerts its effects through the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, facilitating glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis [204]. Studies using AKT2-knockout mice have demonstrated that AKT2 plays an essential role in insulin-stimulated glucose transport in skeletal muscle [205]. Moreover, impairments in skeletal muscle lipid metabolism are closely linked to insulin resistance and the pathogenesis of T2DM. Notably, there is a strong correlation between elevated intramyocellular lipid (IMCL) levels and reduced insulin sensitivity, highlighting IMCL accumulation as a key contributor to insulin resistance [206, 207]. Beyond IMCL, lipid metabolites—particularly diacylglycerol (DAG) and ceramides—are also implicated in insulin resistance development [208, 209]. Additionally, in obese individuals, excessive free fatty acids (FFAs) promote the generation of ROS, which subsequently activate cellular stress kinases, leading to the inhibition of IRS-1 and AKT signaling [210].

In animal models of diabetic nephropathy (DN), the chronic inflammatory state persistently activates the PI3K-AKT pathway, promoting NF-κB nuclear translocation and exacerbating renal inflammation and damage [211, 212]. As a key transcription factor, NF-κB activation further upregulates the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, contributing to tubular epithelial cell injury and a decline in renal function. Studies have demonstrated that inhibition of the PI3K-AKT pathway or its downstream effectors, such as the PI3K/AKT/endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and PI3K/AKT/NF-κB pathways, effectively attenuates inflammatory responses, thereby protecting tubular epithelial cells and slowing the progression of renal damage [213, 214].

Oxidative stress is a key driver in the progression of kidney disease to CKD. For instance, in a 5/6 nephrectomy animal model, upregulation of Nox2, a critical enzyme involved in ROS production, leads to suppression of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. This suggests that excessive ROS may impair the antioxidant functions of this pathway, thereby exacerbating kidney injury [215]. In this process, TNF-α, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, plays a deleterious role by further amplifying oxidative stress-induced renal damage [215]. Notably, activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway exerts protective effects against oxidative stress. For example, stimulation of this pathway has been shown to alleviate arsenic trioxide (ATO)-induced renal inflammation and oxidative stress [216]. Furthermore, fibroblast growth factor 1 (FGF1) mitigates oxidative stress via the PI3K-AKT pathway, thereby slowing the progression of kidney injury in animal models, including the unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) and DN models [217].

Multiple studies have demonstrated that inhibition of the PI3K-AKT pathway can effectively mitigate ventricular remodeling. For instance, Li et al. induced cardiac hypertrophy in mice via transverse aortic constriction (TAC) surgery and stimulated neonatal rat cardiomyocytes with phenylephrine (PE). Their findings revealed that lupeol administration significantly attenuated morphological changes, cardiac dysfunction, and pathological remodeling in TAC mice [218]. Further mechanistic investigations demonstrated that lupeol alleviated inflammation by reducing inflammatory cytokine levels and inhibiting NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation, an effect mediated through the TLR4/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [218]. Moreover, administration of the PI3K-AKT agonist 740Y-P reversed the protective effects of lupeol in both TAC mice and PE-stimulated neonatal rat cardiomyocytes [218]. Zeng et al. reported that TGF-β1 stimulation significantly upregulated the protein expression of p-Smad3, PI3K, and p-AKT, suggesting that activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway may contribute to the progression of cardiac fibrosis [219]. Additionally, recent studies indicate that semaglutide regulates myocardial energy metabolism via the Creb5/NR4a1 axis within the PI3K-AKT pathway, reducing NR4a1 expression and its mitochondrial translocation, thereby improving cardiac remodeling [220]. Furthermore, Pi et al. demonstrated that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis [221]. In murine experiments, inhibition of the P2RY12 receptor promoted autophagy in vascular smooth muscle cells via the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway. Notably, administration of rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor, reversed the cholesterol efflux suppression induced by P2RY12 receptor activation, thereby exacerbating foam cell formation [221].

JAK-STAT

The JAK-STAT signaling pathway is a pivotal communication hub in cellular functions, involving over 50 cytokines and growth factors, including hormones, IFNs, ILs, and CSF [222]. This pathway regulates various biological processes such as hematopoiesis, immune homeostasis, tissue repair, inflammation, apoptosis, and adipogenesis. The JAK-STAT signaling pathway is highly conserved across evolution and primarily consists of the ligand-receptor complex, the JAK family, and the STAT family. The JAK family includes JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2, while the STAT family comprises seven members: STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, STAT4, STAT5a, STAT5b, and STAT6 [223]. The SOCS protein family serves as intracellular negative regulators of this pathway. During its activation, STAT proteins dimerize and translocate to the nucleus, where they induce the expression of SOCS proteins. SOCS proteins, in turn, negatively regulate the activity of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway by binding to phosphorylated JAK and its receptors [223].

In obesity, enlarged adipocytes and infiltrating immune cells, such as macrophages, continuously secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α. Upon binding to their respective receptors, these cytokines often signal through the JAK2-STAT3 pathway [224]. Studies have shown that in obesity animal models, STAT3 activity in the liver and adipose tissue is significantly elevated, which is closely associated with the expression of inflammatory cytokines, insulin resistance, and hepatic steatosis [225]. The use of alternating high-fat diets and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) agonists has been shown to ameliorate the metabolic consequences of hyperinsulinemia, hyperlipidemia, and hepatic steatosis in mice [225]. Additionally, obese patients frequently exhibit abnormal amino acid metabolism, characterized by elevated levels of kynurenine (Kyn) in plasma. Recent research by Huang et al. demonstrated that overexpression of indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) in adipocytes is the primary source of excessive Kyn accumulation [226]. Kyn disrupts adipocyte lipid homeostasis by activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR)/STAT3/IL-6 signaling pathway [226].

Leptin, a hormone secreted by adipocytes, primarily signals through the JAK2-STAT3 pathway [227]. However, in obese individuals, leptin resistance often develops. Despite elevated plasma leptin levels, leptin resistance diminishes its effects, impairing its ability to regulate appetite and increase energy expenditure, which in turn exacerbates local inflammatory responses [228–230]. Studies have shown that, in obesity, inhibition of SOCS3 can maintain leptin sensitivity, suggesting that the potential mechanism of leptin resistance may be closely linked to the overexpression of SOCS3 [231]. Furthermore, the development of leptin resistance is associated with various factors, including the saturation of leptin transport across the blood–brain barrier, inflammatory responses, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and autophagy [232, 233]. The current scientific consensus generally holds that prolonged activation of the leptin/STAT3 signaling pathway rapidly induces the expression of the negative feedback regulator SOCS3, thereby inhibiting leptin signaling and ultimately leading to the onset of leptin resistance.

Chronic subclinical inflammation has been confirmed to play a important role in insulin signaling defects and the development of T2DM [234, 235]. Obesity, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and amyloid deposition associated with diabetes are all closely linked to inflammation, which collectively contributes to the onset of insulin resistance [236]. Inflammatory responses, triggered by the activation of immune and epithelial cells, lead to the release of pro-inflammatory mediators such as cytokines and chemokines. These mediators bind to their cell membrane receptors, initiating the JAK-STAT and NF-κB signaling pathways, which further promote cytokine synthesis [237, 238]. Specifically, the central role of the JAK-STAT pathway in cytokine signaling makes it a crucial factor in the regulation of diabetic insulin resistance.

Overexpression of SOCS proteins can competitively bind to INSR and inhibit the phosphorylation of IRS, thereby inducing insulin resistance [239]. Moreover, elevated cytokine levels not only promote SOCS expression but also stimulate the liver to produce acute-phase reactants such as serum amyloid A (SAA), a protein commonly used as a marker of inflammation and insulin resistance in diabetes [240]. Research by Zhang et al. suggests that amyloid-β, by activating the JAK2/STAT3/SOCS-1 signaling pathway, can induce liver insulin resistance [241]. Additionally, an interesting recent study demonstrates that acetic acid bacteria and lactic acid bacteria can alleviate insulin resistance symptoms through a JNK/JAK/STAT-dependent mechanism. This suggests that supplementation with these probiotics, or modulation of JNK/JAK/STAT pathway activity, could be a potential strategy for future diabetes treatment [242].

Studies have shown that nearly all members of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway are expressed during kidney injury and are involved in the pathological processes following renal damage [243, 244]. For instance, peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with IgA nephropathy demonstrate upregulation of p-STAT production at baseline [245]. Immunofluorescence staining of renal tissue reveals increased levels of JAK2 and p-STAT1 in the glomerular mesangium and endothelium, while p-STAT3 are elevated in the renal tubular interstitium [245]. In diabetic mice, podocyte-specific overexpression of JAK2 exacerbates pathological changes, including urinary albumin excretion, mesangial expansion, glomerular basement membrane thickening, and renal tubular interstitial fibrosis [246]. Additionally, STAT6 expression is upregulated in renal tubular epithelial cells, and STAT6 deficiency alleviates renal fibrosis induced by obstructive nephropathy [247]. In this context, inflammatory cytokines in the JAK-STAT pathway, such as IL-4, IL-6, IL-13, and IFN-γ, are upregulated [248, 249]. Jia et al. found that hederagenin, in its investigation of renal interstitial fibrosis, significantly improved renal fibrosis in CKD by inhibiting interferon stimulated gene 15 (ISG15) and its downstream JAK-STAT signaling pathway [250]. Similarly, a recent study demonstrated that inhibiting the JAK2-STAT3 pathway with stattic to block STAT3 effectively alleviated inflammation and fibrosis in LPS-induced CKD [251].

Mammalian cell and animal studies have demonstrated that the JAK-STAT pathway is closely associated with myocardial injury, primarily through its involvement in immune responses, inflammation, and fibrosis [252]. In a rat model of acute MI, the use of AG490 to block the JAK-STAT signaling pathway following coronary artery ligation resulted in a significant reduction in the infarct area, fewer NF-κB-positive cells, and a substantial decrease in plasma TNF-α levels, indicating that the JAK-STAT signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of acute MI [253]. In atherosclerosis studies, IFN-γ enhances atherosclerosis development by activating JAK1 and JAK2 signaling [254, 255]. Additionally, after IL-6 binds to its receptor, it initiates various cellular events, including those promoting atherosclerosis, with the JAK-STAT pathway playing a key role [256, 257]. In a mouse model of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) associated with CKD, Liu et al. found that retinol accumulation induced myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis by activating JAK2 and phosphorylating STAT5 [258]. Further studies using the small molecule inhibitor AC-4-130 revealed that STAT5 phosphorylation inhibitors could serve as a potential therapeutic target for HFpEF in the context of CKD [258].

On the other hand, the JAK-STAT signaling pathway may also exert a protective effect on the heart. In studies of myocardial reperfusion injury, it has been found that, in the early stages of reperfusion, moderately increased ROS can activate STAT3 phosphorylation, which then translocates to the mitochondria and nucleus, thereby alleviating cytosolic calcium overload during ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury [259, 260]. Furthermore, several studies have demonstrated that when this pathway is activated by the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, STAT3 mediates cardioprotective signaling [261, 262]. This activation may contribute to the heart’s protective effects, including compensatory hypertrophy and reduced apoptosis [263].

PPAR

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) are a class of transcription factors within the nuclear hormone receptor superfamily, primarily involved in regulating energy metabolism. Three main PPAR isoforms have been identified: PPARα, PPARγ, and PPARβ/δ. PPARα is primarily expressed in the liver, where it regulates adaptive responses to fasting and plays a key role in processes such as fatty acid transport, β-oxidation, and ketogenesis [264]. PPARγ is most abundantly expressed in adipose tissue, where it promotes adipocyte differentiation, maintains fat storage, and regulates the secretion of adipokines [265]. PPARβ/δ is highly active in skeletal muscle, where it participates in exercise responses and promotes the formation of oxidative muscle fibers by regulating fatty acid catabolism and glycolysis [266–268].

Long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), such as arachidonic acid (AA), act as ligands for PPARs [269, 270]. Under physiological conditions, PPARs inhibit inflammation induced by NF-κB through various mechanisms upon ligand activation. For instance, PPARγ promotes the ubiquitination of lysine 48 (Lys48) on the p65 subunit of NF-κB, leading to its proteolytic degradation [271]. Additionally, both PPARα and PPARγ can induce the deacetylation of p65, thereby inactivating NF-κB [272]. Furthermore, the promoter of the IκBα gene, which encodes the inhibitory protein that binds to NF-κB, is also regulated by PPARs, with both PPARα and PPARγ promoting the expression of IκBα [273–276]. In addition to inhibiting the NF-κB pathway, PPARs can also modulate the activation of STATs, primarily through PPARγ, which exerts its effects by upregulating the expression of SOCS3 [277, 278]. Furthermore, both PPARα and PPARγ can interact with c-Jun, leading to the suppression of AP-1 activation [279, 280]. Since AP-1 binding sites are present in the promoters of multiple key inflammatory genes, PPARα and PPARγ inhibit the expression of COX-2 via this mechanism [281].

The anti-inflammatory effects of PPARs may be disrupted in obesity. In the early stages of obesity, while the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines remains limited, the expansion of white adipose tissue leads to a substantial accumulation of polyunsaturated fatty acids. In this pro-lipogenic environment, where pro-inflammatory cytokines are not yet abundant, ligand-activated PPARγ may paradoxically induce the expression of COX-2 [282]. The upregulation of COX-2 promotes the synthesis of PGE2, which increases vascular permeability and facilitates immune cell migration to local tissues [283]. Additionally, PGE2 can activate the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby promoting the release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, further amplifying local inflammation [284]. Moreover, TNF-α itself downregulates the expression and function of PPARs, further impairing their anti-inflammatory effects [285]. This positive feedback loop may contribute to the progressive exacerbation of inflammation in obesity, serving as a key driver of sustained obesity-associated inflammatory responses.

In the context of obesity, excessive nutrient intake disrupts metabolic homeostasis. Studies have identified C-terminal binding protein 2 (CTBP2) as a metabolite-sensing transcriptional corepressor that interacts with PPARα in the obese metabolic environment, thereby suppressing its activity [286]. In vivo experiments have demonstrated that this CTBP2-PPARα interaction is enhanced in the obese liver, whereas hepatic CTBP2 gene deletion results in the de-repression of PPARα target genes [286]. Interestingly, a study by Sivasami et al. revealed that PPARγ-expressing regulatory T (Treg) cells in the skin preferentially take up long-chain free fatty acids in obese mice [287]. This preferential uptake induces cellular lipotoxicity, leading to a reduction in the number of these Treg cells and impairing their ability to regulate IL-17A + γδ T cell-mediated skin inflammation [287].

In obesity and T2DM, skeletal muscle is the primary site of insulin resistance [288]. A study by Verma et al. demonstrated that muscle-specific deletion of PPARγ in mice leads to insulin resistance [289]. Consistent with these findings, in vitro experiments have shown that suppression of PPARγ expression in C2C12 skeletal muscle cells similarly induces insulin resistance [289]. Likewise, mice deficient in PPARβ/δ also develop insulin resistance [290]. This phenomenon may be linked to the phosphorylation of PPARs. Notably, several studies have demonstrated that phosphorylation of PPARγ at serine 273 (Ser273) is a key mechanism underlying insulin resistance [291–293]. Based on this premise, a potential mechanism through which PPARβ/δ contributes to insulin resistance may involve the modification of a specific amino acid; however, no studies have directly investigated this possibility.

In a seminal 2007 review, Raffaele De Caterina and colleagues elucidated the specific cardiovascular benefits of the ω−3 polyunsaturated fatty acids eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) in patients with diabetes and comorbid coronary heart disease [294]. Building upon this foundational work, the same group subsequently demonstrated that hyperinsulinemia stimulates the translocation of CD36 to the plasma membrane, thereby enhancing cellular uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL). Notably, this effect could be reversed by treatment with PPARα/γ antagonists, indicating that insulin-induced CD36 upregulation was mediated through the PPARα/γ signaling pathway [295].

Subsequent mechanistic studies revealed that ω−3 fatty acids, particularly DHA, effectively counteracted insulin-induced upregulation and activation of CD36. Specifically, DHA was found to inhibit the binding of both PPARα/γ and NF-κB to DNA, thereby suppressing downstream signaling cascades triggered by insulin and oxLDL [295]. These findings suggest that ω−3 fatty acids may play a protective role in mitigating microvascular injury and inflammatory responses in individuals with diabetes, thus providing mechanistic insight into their potential utility in the prevention and management of diabetes-associated vascular complications.

The downregulation of PPARs expression is closely associated with the development of CKD. For instance, studies by Hajarnis et al. and Lakhia et al. have demonstrated that PPARα expression is downregulated in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) and in primary cells from human ADPKD patients [296, 297]. Additionally, studies have shown that, with aging, PPARα-deficient mice exhibit significant lipid accumulation in renal tubules, highlighting the key role of PPARα in the development of age-related kidney fibrosis [298, 299]. Female mice lacking PPARβ/δ show reduced expression of complement component 1qb (C1qb) and other opsonins, which impairs the clearance of apoptotic cells and reduces the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines, making these mice more susceptible to autoimmune kidney disease [300]. In mouse models, activating PPARγ has been shown to improve renal tubular sclerosis, renal interstitial fibrosis, and inflammation, indicating that PPARγ plays a key role in maintaining the normal epithelial phenotype and exerting anti-fibrotic effects [301–303].

The deficiency of PPARγ in endothelial cells accelerates age-related vascular dysfunction by increasing the activity of NADPH oxidase (NOX) and Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK) [304]. In the context of pulmonary arterial hypertension, PPARγ has been shown to disrupt its role in DNA repair by interacting with the E3 ubiquitin ligase UBR5 and the DNA damage sensor MRN complex [305]. Furthermore, an animal study demonstrated that elevated levels of DNA cytosine-5-methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) in macrophages result in reduced PPARγ expression and increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, further exacerbating atherosclerosis [306]. In heart failure, reduced cardiac output and arterial pressure chronically activate the RAAS and the sympathetic nervous system [307]. Enhanced RAAS signaling and increased sympathetic activity can activate the NF-κB pathway, leading to the inhibition of PPARα and PPARδ expression and activity [308–310]. Ultimately, the decline in oxidative phosphorylation capacity in cardiomyocytes reduces the cardiac metabolic reserve, further aggravating ventricular contractile dysfunction [311].

Rationale for targeting inflammation in the treatment of CKM syndrome

In 2019, the AHA proposed a comprehensive treatment strategy for cardiorenal syndrome, encompassing several key components: Decongestive therapies (e.g., diuretics and ultrafiltration); neurohormonal modulation and vasodilation; positive inotropic support; RAAS inhibitors (including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers, neprilysin/renin–angiotensin inhibitors, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and β-adrenergic receptor blockers); sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors; insulinotropic therapies (e.g., glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors); cardiac device therapy (such as implantable cardioverter-defibrillators, cardiac resynchronization therapy, and mechanical circulatory support); and renal transplantation [312].

These strategies are primarily aimed at alleviating clinical symptoms and improving organ function. However, they do not fundamentally address inflammation. As previously discussed, inflammation plays a central role in the pathophysiological pathways of CKM syndrome, underscoring the importance of exploring the therapeutic potential of anti-inflammatory interventions in this context. As summarized in Table 2, we have reviewed and discussed the rationale for the application of anti-inflammatory agents in the treatment of CKM syndrome.

IL-1 Inhibitors

Anakinra

Anakinra is a recombinant human IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra), structurally identical to endogenous human IL-1Ra, with the sole difference being the addition of a methionine residue at the N-terminus to enhance its stability [313]. Clinically, anakinra is primarily used to treat RA, gout, and periodic inflammatory syndromes [314]. Several clinical studies have now confirmed its significant therapeutic effects in diabetes, kidney disease, and cardiovascular diseases. For example, one study reported on three patients with gout and T2DM who, despite incomplete or unsatisfactory responses to urate-lowering therapy, colchicine, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and prednisone, received anakinra (100 mg/day, subcutaneously). The results showed that anakinra not only rapidly and completely alleviated gouty arthritis within hours, but also significantly improved the patients’ blood glucose and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels over the following 6 months [315]. Furthermore, similar findings were observed in two other studies [316, 317].

Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) is an autosomal recessive inflammatory disorder, often associated with systemic amyloid A amyloidosis, and typically manifests as CKD. In a placebo-controlled trial, 25 FMF patients who were resistant to colchicine were randomly assigned to receive either anakinra or placebo. The results demonstrated that patients in the anakinra group had a significantly lower average number of monthly flare-ups compared to those in the placebo group [318]. Anakinra has also been tested in hemodialysis patients. In an initial trial involving 14 hemodialysis patients, the anakinra group showed a 53% reduction in average high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels [319]. In a study by Loustau and colleagues, which investigated the use of anakinra in patients with advanced CKD and gout, a significant reduction in CRP levels was observed, and the safety of anakinra treatment was confirmed [320].

Additionally, the efficacy of anakinra has been validated in patients with acute decompensated systolic heart failure. One study found that the anakinra treatment group significantly reduced acute inflammatory responses compared to the placebo group, and after 14 days of treatment, LVEF improved [321]. In patients with stable chronic systolic heart failure, anakinra significantly reduced levels of circulating IL-1β, IL-6, and CRP, and patients experienced notable improvements in cardiopulmonary function and health-related quality of life [322].

Canakinumab

Canakinumab is a high-affinity human monoclonal antibody that targets IL-1β [323]. It is approved for the treatment of cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes (CAPS), tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated periodic syndrome (TRAPS), FMF, and gouty arthritis [323]. In a double-blind, multinational phase IIb clinical trial, 556 patients with well-controlled diabetes and high cardiovascular risk were randomly assigned to receive subcutaneous placebo or canakinumab [324]. After four months, the canakinumab group showed improvements in HbA1c, glucose, and insulin levels, while circulating inflammatory markers, including CRP, IL-6, and fibrinogen, were significantly reduced [324]. Another multicenter trial also demonstrated that, compared to the placebo group, patients in the canakinumab group required significantly fewer anti-diabetic medications while achieving similar blood glucose control [325].

In CKD, a clinical trial based on the Canakinumab Anti-inflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study (CANTOS) showed that, compared to the placebo group, both 150 mg and 300 mg doses of canakinumab significantly reduced eGFR and creatinine levels in CKD patients and decreased the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events in high-risk atherosclerotic patients with CKD, particularly among those with a strong anti-inflammatory response to initial treatment [326]. These cardiovascular benefits were not accompanied by any clinical adverse renal events [326].

Furthermore, another milestone study based on CANTOS assessed the therapeutic effects on CVD. In 10,061 patients with a history of MI and hs-CRP levels ≥ 2 mg/L, the effects of three doses of canakinumab (50 mg, 150 mg, and 300 mg, administered subcutaneously every three months) were compared to placebo, with the primary endpoint being nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, or cardiovascular death [327]. The results showed that the 150 mg dose significantly reduced the risk of primary endpoint events and passed multiplicity-adjusted statistical testing, making it the most effective treatment dose [327]. Additionally, secondary endpoints, including hospitalization for unstable angina requiring urgent revascularization, also showed significant benefits [327]. Similarly, canakinumab treatment significantly reduced the risk of hospitalization for heart failure and death due to heart failure, with the effect being dose-dependent and improving as the canakinumab dose increased [328].

Rilonacept

Rilonacept is a recombinant chimeric fusion protein that exerts its effects through the “receptor trap” mechanism. This mechanism mimics cell surface receptors to capture and neutralize IL-1α and IL-1β, thereby blocking their binding to the actual receptors. Research on the use of rilonacept in diabetes treatment is relatively limited, with only two studies specifically on type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM). One study administered subcutaneous rilonacept for 26 weeks in 13 patients with T1DM and found that, while rilonacept may have some effect on reducing inflammation, its protective effect on β-cell function was limited [329]. Another study evaluated the effects of rilonacept in the non-obese diabetic mouse model for IL-1 inhibition therapy and found that, compared to anti-CD3 therapy alone, rilonacept did not provide additional benefits in reversing the onset of diabetes [330].

In phase 3–4 CKD and end-stage renal disease patients, rilonacept was shown to reduce serum hs-CRP levels and demonstrated good tolerability, without affecting mineral and bone metabolism [331, 332]. Additionally, rilonacept may also be effective for certain refractory autoinflammatory diseases, such as FMF [333].

Rilonacept has been extensively studied for the treatment of recurrent pericarditis. In a 2020 study, adult patients with recurrent pericarditis were treated with rilonacept, which resulted in a reduction of pericarditis pain scores from 4.5 to 0.7, CRP from 4.62 to 0.38 mg/dL, and the recurrence rate of pericarditis decreased from 3.9 episodes per year to 0.18 episodes [334]. In another clinical trial, the “Rilonacept Inhibition of Interleukin-1 Alpha and Beta for Recurrent Pericarditis: A Pivotal Symptomatology and Outcomes Study (RHAPSODY),” it was found that rilonacept rapidly alleviated pain and inflammation in recurrent pericarditis, with a lower recurrence risk than placebo and good safety [335].

These findings indicate that rilonacept plays a positive role in the treatment of pericarditis. However, based on its anti-inflammatory properties, whether it can exert beneficial effects in diseases such as atherosclerosis, acute MI, and heart failure remains uncertain. The current clinical trial data are still limited, and further research is needed to validate its potential in these conditions.

IL-6 inhibitors

Tocilizumab

Tocilizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting the IL-6 receptor (IL-6R). It functions by inhibiting the binding of IL-6 to both membrane-bound and soluble IL-6R, thereby blocking IL-6-mediated signal transduction [336]. Tocilizumab is primarily indicated for the treatment of RA, as well as systemic and polyarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis [337, 338]. In addition, growing evidence supports its therapeutic potential in a range of other conditions, including autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammatory disorders, and autoinflammatory syndromes.

In the context of diabetes, a study by Chen et al. demonstrated that tocilizumab reduced plasma glucagon, blood glucose, and HbA1c levels in both rhesus monkeys and humans. Furthermore, a positive correlation was observed between plasma IL-6 and plasma glucagon levels [339]. Tocilizumab also improved glucose tolerance during intravenous glucose tolerance tests (IVGTT) by reducing circulating glucagon levels [339]. In another study involving patients with giant cell arteritis, tocilizumab treatment was associated with a significant reduction in HbA1c levels, independent of glucocorticoid exposure [340].

In renal research, animal studies have shown that tocilizumab significantly inhibits NF-κB activation, reduces kidney inflammation caused by sepsis, suppresses oxidative stress and apoptosis, and lowers serum creatinine and BUN levels. These effects collectively alleviate sepsis-induced acute kidney injury (AKI) and improve survival rates in rats [341]. Additionally, research by Yang et al. demonstrated that tocilizumab analogs could attenuate macrophage activation and differentiation, downregulate profibrotic proteins, and alleviate renal fibrosis and injury in mice subjected to UUO [342]. In a recent case report, a 79-year-old male patient with seronegative polyarthritis and concurrent proteinuria was diagnosed with amyloid A amyloidosis based on renal biopsy findings, which revealed significant interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy. Following monthly administration of tocilizumab in combination with peritoneal dialysis, the patient demonstrated a favorable clinical response, with rapid remission of nephrotic syndrome [343].

In the cardiovascular field, tocilizumab significantly reduced the risk of MI by 88% and major adverse cardiovascular events by 59% in a nationwide cohort study of 1,584 RA patients unresponsive to TNF-α inhibitors [344]. Moreover, tocilizumab markedly improved left ventricular ejection fraction and normalized left ventricular hypertrophy [345]. Another study found that tocilizumab significantly decreased levels of N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), with the magnitude of reduction correlating with changes in disease activity [346]. Furthermore, administration of tocilizumab within the first six hours following symptom onset in patients with ST-segment elevation MI significantly improved the myocardial salvage index, as quantified by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging [347].

Sarilumab

Sarilumab is a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody that targets the IL-6R and is primarily indicated for the treatment of RA in patients with an inadequate response to conventional therapies. By binding to both soluble and membrane-bound IL-6 receptors, sarilumab effectively blocks IL-6 signaling and thereby inhibits IL-6–mediated inflammatory responses [348].

Diabetes is commonly observed among patients with RA, and IL-6 plays a critical role not only in the pathogenesis of RA but also in the regulation of glucose homeostasis. Evidence suggests that sarilumab has a beneficial impact on HbA1c levels in RA patients. In individuals with comorbid diabetes or baseline HbA1c ≥ 7%, sarilumab treatment resulted in a significantly greater reduction in HbA1c at week 24 compared to placebo [349].

Given the pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic roles of IL-6 in CKD, sarilumab may also hold therapeutic potential for the management of CKD. However, to date, no clinical trials have specifically investigated its efficacy in CKD populations.

Furthermore, sarilumab has shown promise in the context of giant cell arteritis and CVD, where inflammation contributes to the progression of atherosclerosis [350]. A recent phase 3, double-blind clinical trial evaluating the efficacy of sarilumab in giant cell arteritis demonstrated that treatment with 200 mg sarilumab combined with a 26-week glucocorticoid tapering regimen achieved the primary endpoint of sustained remission, highlighting a novel therapeutic application of sarilumab in this domain [351].

Ziltivekimab

Ziltivekimab is a fully human IgG1 monoclonal antibody that selectively binds to the IL-6 ligand and has been specifically developed to reduce residual systemic inflammation in patients at high cardiovascular risk.

The RESCUE trial, a multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 study, enrolled 264 patients with stage 3–5 CKD and elevated hs-CRP levels. The trial demonstrated that Ziltivekimab significantly reduced inflammatory biomarkers in a clear dose-dependent manner. After 12 weeks of treatment, the median reductions in hs-CRP levels were 77% in the 7.5 mg group, 88% in the 15 mg group, and 92% in the 30 mg group, whereas the placebo group showed a reduction of only 4% (all P < 0.0001). This anti-inflammatory effect persisted through 24 weeks of follow-up [352]. In addition to hs-CRP, Ziltivekimab also significantly reduced levels of other inflammatory and atherogenic biomarkers, including fibrinogen, SAA, haptoglobin, sPLA2, and LP(a) [352]. The treatment was well tolerated, and no major safety concerns were observed, including severe injection-site reactions, sustained neutropenia, or thrombocytopenia.

To evaluate cross-population efficacy, RESCUE-2—a follow-up study conducted in a Japanese cohort—confirmed the potent anti-inflammatory activity of Ziltivekimab. Administration of 15 mg and 30 mg doses resulted in median hs-CRP reductions of 96.2% (P < 0.0001) and 93.4% (P = 0.002), respectively, while the placebo group exhibited a reduction of only 27.0%. Consistent inhibitory effects on fibrinogen and SAA levels were also observed in this population [353].

Importantly, the magnitude and consistency of hs-CRP suppression achieved through direct IL-6 inhibition with Ziltivekimab has been shown to surpass that of upstream cytokine inhibitors, such as IL-1β blockade [354]. Building on these promising findings, a large-scale phase 3 outcomes trial—ZEUS (NCT05021835)—was initiated in 2021. This global, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial aims to evaluate whether monthly subcutaneous administration of 15 mg Ziltivekimab can reduce the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and renal endpoints in approximately 6,400 patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, CKD, and elevated hs-CRP levels. The study is powered at 95% to detect a 20% relative reduction in MACE, with follow-up continuing until 1,044 primary events have accrued. The estimated duration of the trial is approximately four years [354].

TNF-α inhibitors

Infliximab

Infliximab is a recombinant chimeric monoclonal antibody that effectively inhibits the binding of tumor necrosis TNF-α to both its soluble and transmembrane receptors [355]. It exerts anti-inflammatory effects by promoting the lysis of TNF-α-expressing cell lines through complement-dependent cytotoxicity and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity mechanisms [356]. Clinically, infliximab is primarily used for the treatment of Crohn’s disease, RA, ankylosing spondylitis, and psoriasis [357].

Infliximab has also demonstrated beneficial effects in improving insulin resistance. As early as 2004, Yazdani-Biuki et al. reported that among five patients treated with infliximab for psoriatic arthritis or RA, one patient with diabetes and two patients with significant obesity showed marked improvements in homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) during a 120 to 240 days follow-up period [358]. Subsequent studies have further confirmed that infliximab treatment significantly improves both HOMA-IR and the quantitative insulin sensitivity check index (QUICKI) across various patient populations [359–362]. More recently, a case study described a 53-year-old male melanoma patient who developed immune-mediated diabetes characterized by β-cell dysfunction and peripheral insulin resistance. Initiation of infliximab therapy led to improvements in both β-cell function and insulin sensitivity [363].

With regard to renal protection, infliximab has demonstrated renoprotective effects in animal models. In a rat model of ischemia–reperfusion, infliximab significantly reduced serum creatinine and urea levels [364]. A retrospective analysis involving 70 RA patients with comorbid CKD found that those receiving TNF-α inhibitors (such as adalimumab, etanercept, or infliximab) maintained stable eGFR over a 3-year period, whereas eGFR declined significantly in patients not treated with TNF-α inhibitors [365]. Moreover, Younis et al. investigated the renoprotective mechanisms of infliximab and found that it ameliorated doxorubicin-induced nephropathy by upregulating renal Klotho expression and inhibiting activation of the Wnt/β-catenin/renin-angiotensin axis, thereby improving renal outcomes in rats with CKD [366].

The role of infliximab in CVD remains controversial. An early phase II randomized, double-blind clinical trial found that short-term infliximab treatment did not improve clinical outcomes in patients with moderate to severe chronic heart failure [367]. However, a more recent study using a porcine model of MI demonstrated that intravenous administration of infliximab during reperfusion significantly increased the number of M2 pro-regenerative macrophages in the peri-infarct region, reduced serum cardiac troponin I levels, improved left ventricular ejection fraction, and decreased myocardial scar size [368]. Given the ongoing debate, further clinical trials are warranted to establish the safety and efficacy of infliximab in cardiovascular settings.

Etanercept

Etanercept is a fusion protein with a mechanism of action similar to that of infliximab, but it primarily binds to the active trimeric form of TNF-α [369]. Studies have demonstrated that etanercept has beneficial effects in T1DM. For instance, a 24-week, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial conducted in 2009 found that etanercept treatment significantly reduced HbA1c levels in children with T1DM while increasing endogenous insulin secretion, suggesting a potential protective effect on β-cell function [370]. Additionally, a case report described a girl with a long-standing history of T1DM and autoimmune Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, who experienced a significant reduction in insulin usage following etanercept treatment, possibly due to increased tissue sensitivity to insulin [371].

Diabetic retinopathy is commonly associated with diabetes. In a study using a β2-adrenergic receptor knockout mouse model, etanercept treatment significantly reduced TNF-α, SOCS3, and pro-apoptotic protein levels, while increasing phosphorylation of the INSR at tyrosine residues 1150/1151 [372]. These results suggest that etanercept may protect against diabetic retinopathy by reducing TNF-α-mediated insulin resistance and apoptosis [372].

Inflammation is a key driver of the transition from AKI to progressive fibrosis and CKD. Consequently, inhibiting inflammation to prevent the progression from AKI to CKD has become a research focus. In a mouse model, etanercept treatment blocked the release of TNF-α by immune cells, reducing renal inflammation and fibrosis, thereby improving the transition from AKI to CKD [373].

Moreover, a study investigating the effects of etanercept on lipid metabolism and cardiovascular risk factors in patients with psoriatic arthritis reported minor changes in serum lipid levels over a 5-year treatment period. However, these modest lipid changes were insufficient to explain the beneficial cardiovascular effects of etanercept [374]. In contrast, a study by Plein et al. suggested that etanercept treatment might benefit cardiovascular health. The Plein team used a combination of etanercept and methotrexate to treat patients with early rheumatoid arthritis (ERA), finding that after one year of treatment, arterial distensibility significantly improved, and this improvement was sustained in the second year [375]. This study provides preliminary evidence for the cardiovascular benefits of disease-modifying antirheumatic drug therapy in ERA patients and highlights the importance of early treatment in preventing cardiovascular abnormalities [375].

Adalimumab

Adalimumab is a fully human recombinant IgG1 monoclonal antibody that specifically blocks the binding of human TNF-α to its receptors, with lower immunogenicity compared to infliximab [376]. In 2007, Van Eijk reported two cases of RA with concurrent diabetes, in which adalimumab treatment for active RA significantly reduced the fructosamine levels of the patients [377]. Neither the patients’ antidiabetic medications, dietary habits, nor exercise regimens changed after the initiation of adalimumab therapy, further supporting its potential role in improving blood glucose control [377]. Similar conclusions were drawn from animal studies. For instance, after treatment with adalimumab, blood glucose levels in obese diabetic Wistar rats significantly decreased, suggesting a positive effect on glucose control [378]. However, there are currently no large-scale clinical trial data on adalimumab’s effect on diabetes, and this area remains to be further explored.

The effects of adalimumab on the kidneys are still debated. On one hand, studies have shown that adalimumab significantly reduces BUN, creatinine, and cystatin C levels in diabetic nephropathy mice, indicating a potential protective effect on the kidneys [379]. It may also serve as a protective agent in LPS-induced AKI [380]. On the other hand, adverse reactions such as granulomatous interstitial nephritis or IgA nephropathy may occur in patients using adalimumab in clinical practice [381, 382].

In terms of CVD treatment, adalimumab may exhibit more significant benefits, particularly considering that psoriasis and RA themselves are cardiovascular risk factors. By improving the symptoms of these diseases, adalimumab may indirectly have a positive impact on cardiovascular health. For example, a study by Gkalpakiotis et al. confirmed that Adalimumab treatment significantly improved psoriasis symptoms while markedly reducing cardiovascular disease-related biomarkers, such as E-selectin and IL-22 [383]. Additionally, clinical trial data from the Sidiropoulos team showed that after 18 months of Adalimumab treatment, patients had significant improvements in endothelial function and stable carotid intima-media thickness [384]. However, current data remain insufficient, and further longitudinal studies with more patients are needed to confirm the clinical significance of these findings in relation to atherosclerotic risk.

Overall, TNF-α inhibitors have demonstrated promising potential in improving diabetes control, providing renal protection, and promoting cardiovascular health. However, further clinical trial data are needed to validate their efficacy and safety.

Conclusion and perspectives

CKM syndrome refers to a cluster of clinical conditions arising from the interplay among metabolic dysregulation, renal disease, and cardiovascular pathology, with inflammation serving as a pivotal contributor to this process. Emerging evidence indicates that low-grade chronic inflammation functions as a central driving force in the syndrome, promoting the progression of metabolic abnormalities, renal injury, and cardiovascular complications through multiple molecular pathways. Mechanistic studies have identified the aberrant activation of signaling pathways—including NF-κB, Wnt, PI3K-AKT, JAK-STAT, and PPAR—as key components of a complex inflammatory regulatory network.

At the transcriptional level, NF-κB promotes the establishment of a pro-inflammatory microenvironment via the upregulation of inflammatory mediators. The canonical Wnt pathway, through β-catenin nuclear translocation, facilitates fibrogenesis. The PI3K-AKT pathway exerts dual regulatory effects on inflammation and metabolic reprogramming, thereby contributing to systemic pathophysiological alterations. The JAK-STAT cascade amplifies inter-organ pathological signaling through dysregulated intercellular communication. Meanwhile, dysfunction of the PPAR signaling axis directly disrupts metabolic-inflammatory homeostasis.

Inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α not only serve as important clinical biomarkers but have also emerged as promising therapeutic targets. Pharmacological interventions targeting IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α have demonstrated clinical benefits in improving insulin resistance, preserving renal function, and mitigating cardiovascular pathology. Nevertheless, the effects of some anti-inflammatory agents on CKM syndrome remain controversial, and further clinical validation is required.

Looking ahead, continued research into CKM syndrome will help further elucidate the role of inflammation in its pathogenesis. Novel immunomodulatory strategies and anti-inflammatory agents may provide expanded therapeutic options. With the advancement of precision medicine and the adoption of individualized treatment approaches, early identification and effective intervention for CKM syndrome are becoming increasingly feasible. Moreover, multitargeted therapeutic strategies tailored to distinct pathological mechanisms may enhance treatment outcomes and improve patient prognosis. Therefore, interdisciplinary collaboration and large-scale, long-term clinical trials will be essential to generate robust and definitive evidence, ultimately offering improved solutions for addressing this growing public health challenge.
