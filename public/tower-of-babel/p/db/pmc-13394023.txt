Polypharmacy burden and incident epilepsy among older adults in the United States
Authors: Galen Shearn‐Nance, Long Vu, Hannah L. Fein, Wyatt P. Bensken, Jorden G. Rieke, Martha Sajatovic, Gena Ghearing, David F. Warner, Siran M. Koroukian, Nicholas K. Schiltz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo estimate the prevalence of polypharmacy among older adults with incident epilepsy and to describe the most common combinations of drug classes filled prior to epilepsy diagnosis. Polypharmacy—the concurrent use of multiple medications—is common in older adults with epilepsy, but little is known about its burden and specific patterns at the time of diagnosis.MethodsThis retrospective longitudinal cohort study included 2 768 656 US Medicare beneficiaries aged 67 and older with Part D coverage. Beneficiaries were epilepsy‐free from 2016 to 2018; 13 819 (0.5%) were newly diagnosed with epilepsy in 2019. The prevalence of polypharmacy and combinations of therapeutic drug classes filled in 2018 were compared between those with and without incident epilepsy using frequent itemset mining and regression models to estimate adjusted rate ratios.ResultsBeneficiaries with incident epilepsy filled prescriptions for a greater number of therapeutic drug classes in the year prior to diagnosis compared with those without epilepsy (median 9 [IQR 5–12] vs. 6 [IQR 3–9]) and were more likely to have filled prescriptions for five or more distinct drug classes (81.4% vs. 63.3%). Antidepressants, gastric medications, and loop diuretics were more prevalent among beneficiaries with incident epilepsy. The combination of calcium channel blockers and antihypertensives was most strongly associated with incident epilepsy after adjustment (adjusted rate ratio 1.54 [99% CI, 1.38–1.71]).SignificanceOlder adults with incident epilepsy experience a high burden of polypharmacy at the time of diagnosis, with nearly 90% prescribed five or more drug classes. Certain drug class combinations, such as antipsychotics and antidepressants, were more frequently observed among those with incident epilepsy. These findings underscore the importance of considering overall polypharmacy burden and specific drug class combinations when initiating epilepsy therapy in older adults.Plain Language SummaryOlder adults who develop epilepsy often take many medications for other health conditions. In this study of US Medicare beneficiaries, people who were later diagnosed with epilepsy were taking about nine different types of drug classes in the year before diagnosis, compared with about six drug classes among those who did not develop epilepsy. Certain combinations of drug classes—especially those affecting the brain and nervous system—were more common among people who developed epilepsy. These findings show that many older adults have complex medication regimens when epilepsy is first diagnosed.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is one of the most common neurological disorders among older adults in the United States The population of adults aged 65 years and older is projected to nearly double to 95 million by 2060, 1 leading to an anticipated increase in the number of older adults with epilepsy. 2

The etiology of epilepsy varies with age, and older age itself is an independent risk factor for new‐onset epilepsy. 2 , 3 Prior research has found a high burden of chronic conditions in individuals diagnosed with incident epilepsy. 4 Some central nervous system conditions like stroke and dementia are well‐known risk factors for the development of epilepsy. 3 , 5 , 6 Multiple chronic conditions are associated with higher rates of polypharmacy due to the greater number of medications needed to manage complex health needs. 4 Indeed, polypharmacy is common in people with prevalent epilepsy due to a higher comorbidity burden and the use of multiple antiseizure medications (ASMs) in drug‐resistant epilepsy. However, much less is known about the existing burden of polypharmacy among newly diagnosed epilepsy cases. 7 , 8

Polypharmacy is defined in various ways within the literature. A widely accepted definition is the use of five or more medications 9 , 10 ; though definitions can range from as few as two medications to as many as 11 or more. 9 Polypharmacy may also be characterized by the number of drug classes rather than the sheer number of medications. The use of medications from multiple drug classes may reflect the complexity of underlying chronic disease states in patients. Older adults with epilepsy are at particularly high risk of adverse drug events due to drug–drug interactions between ASMs and other commonly prescribed drugs, with clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interactions especially noted for agents such as carbamazepine. 11 Understanding the patterns and prevalence of polypharmacy among older adults with incident epilepsy can inform efforts to optimize medication management and improve outcomes in this population.

This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of polypharmacy among cases of incident epilepsy and to describe the most common combinations of drug classes filled by older adults with newly diagnosed epilepsy.

METHODS

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study examined US Medicare beneficiaries enrolled in the traditional Fee‐for‐Service (FFS) program from January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2019. 2 During this period, Medicare provided coverage to over 99% of Americans aged 65 and older, with approximately two‐thirds enrolled in the FFS program. 12 Data for the study were sourced from the Master Beneficiary Summary File (MBSF), provided by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS). The MBSF includes demographic information, enrollment details, and summary indicators for chronic and potentially disabling conditions based on claims during the study period. Additionally, prescription drug data were obtained from claims billed through Medicare drug coverage plans (Part D).

Study population

This retrospective cohort study analyzed a representative sample of 4 999 999 US Medicare beneficiaries over the age of 65 enrolled in the FFS program from 2016 to 2019. This sample was part of a larger study designed to calculate the incidence of epilepsy in older adults in 2019. 2 As described in previous studies, 2 , 7 this cohort was selected by randomly sampling beneficiaries who received their care exclusively through the FFS system and had no epilepsy‐related claims in 2016, 2017, and 2018, using a validated 3‐year lookback period. 13 , 14 , 15 Non‐Hispanic Black and Hispanic Medicare beneficiaries were oversampled to allow more precise incidence rate calculations for these groups. Race/ethnicity was adjusted for in the calculation of adjusted incident rate ratios, as described below in the Statistical Analysis section. For the present study, which aimed to describe polypharmacy in older adults with epilepsy, we further limited our study population to those continuously enrolled in Medicare Part D in 2018. Patients on ASMs during the exposure period were excluded from the main analysis, however they were included in sensitivity analyses. This approach was intended to minimize the chance that individuals with unrecorded or pre‐existing epilepsy, who may have been prescribed ASMs but lacked epilepsy diagnosis codes, would be misclassified as epilepsy‐free prior to 2019. The final analytic sample size was 2,372,139.

Variables of interest

Incident epilepsy

Epilepsy in the MBSF is identified based on end of year assessment, requiring at least one inpatient claim or two outpatient nondrug claims occurring at least one day apart (ICD‐10 G40.x) within a 2‐year period. The ICD‐10 G40.x code range encompasses all epilepsy and recurrent seizure disorder subtypes. Incident epilepsy was defined as the first appearance of this condition flag in 2019 among beneficiaries with no epilepsy‐related claims during the 3‐year washout period (2016–2018). This validated ICD‐10 algorithm has a sensitivity of 84.4% (95% CI, 80.8–87.5%) and a specificity of 79.4% (95% CI, 62.1–91.3%) among adults. 16 , 17 The entire data lookback period used the same ICD‐10 codes.

Therapeutic drug classes

All medications billed in 2018 Medicare Part D claims were classified into therapeutic drug classes using the Veterans Affairs Drug Classification system. 18 This classification was accomplished by linking each medication's National Drug Code through the RxNorm database from the National Library of Medicine. 19 Polypharmacy was defined based on the count of unique drug classes for prescriptions filled from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2018, emphasizing the study's focus on the diversity of drug classes rather than count of individual medications.

Beneficiary demographics

All demographic information was obtained from the MBSF. Age, at the end of 2019 (or at death), was grouped as 67–74, 75–84, and 85 years or older. The study population begins at age 67 because we required 3 years of baseline data for each participant. As beneficiaries needed to be at least 65 years old in 2016 to be included, they would be 67 or older by the end of 2018. Sex was coded as male or female. Race and ethnicity were coded as Hispanic (all races), non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, and non‐Hispanic Other using the validated Research Triangle Institute (RTI) race code variable, which supplements the traditional beneficiary race code variable by applying an algorithm that classifies additional beneficiaries of Hispanic or Asian origin based on first and last names. 20 , 21 Due to a small number of cases, beneficiaries identified as Asian/Pacific Islander, American Indian/Alaska Native were grouped into non‐Hispanic Other.

Chronic conditions

Pre‐existing chronic conditions were assessed from 30 conditions identified in the 2018 Chronic Conditions segment of the MBSF, along with 37 additional conditions from the 2018 Other Chronic or Potentially Disabling Conditions segment of the MBSF. Validation of these conditions required meeting diagnostic criteria and sufficient FFS coverage, as outlined by CMS's Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse. 22 Due to the extensive number of individual conditions, we grouped these into 14 clinically meaningful categories: cancer, cardiovascular, congenital, developmental, metabolic, musculoskeletal, neurological, organ failure, psychiatric, respiratory, sensory impairment, stroke, substance use disorders, and other chronic conditions. Conditions comprising the cardiovascular category included acute myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation and flutter, heart failure and non‐ischemic heart disease, ischemic heart disease, and peripheral vascular disease. Conditions comprising the metabolic category included hyperlipidemia, hypertension, hyperthyroidism, diabetes, and obesity. Stroke and transient ischemic attack comprised the stroke category. Additional detailed information on the clinically meaningful categories can be found in Table S1. ICD‐10 codes for each condition are outlined in CMS's Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse. 22

Statistical analysis

Frequent itemset mining, a machine learning algorithm used to identify common patterns in datasets, was employed to discover the most prevalent combinations of drug classes among beneficiaries. 23 , 24 Frequent itemset mining is an exploratory, hypothesis‐generating technique that identifies patterns of co‐occurrence but does not account for confounding; results should be interpreted accordingly. Individual drug classes were ranked based on their support metric, which measures the frequency of a drug class appearing in prescriptions, comparing the group with incident epilepsy to the group without. In this context, support is synonymous with prevalence. Descriptive statistics were used to assess changes in rank. Given the size of our study population, the analysis prioritized the magnitude of differences observed between the incident epilepsy and non‐epilepsy groups, rather than focusing on statistical significance. The combinations most strongly associated with incident epilepsy were identified by calculating the prevalence ratio between those diagnosed with incident epilepsy in 2019 and those without. To estimate the adjusted rate ratio (ARR) for these associations, a modified Poisson regression was used, combining a log Poisson regression model with robust variance estimation, and adjusting for age, sex, and race/ethnicity, and pre‐existing comorbidities. 25 , 26 ARRs are presented with 99% confidence intervals (CIs).

Because the primary analysis excluded beneficiaries with ASM use, we conducted a sensitivity analysis repeating the primary models while including those who filled antiseizure drug classes during the exposure period (2018), recognizing that these medications may be prescribed for non‐epilepsy indications.

Initial data preparation was conducted using SAS version 9.4, while frequent itemset mining was performed with SAS Enterprise Miner version 15.4 respectively. Regression analysis was carried out using R version 4.2.3.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

This study was approved and granted a waiver of informed consent by each of the University of Alabama at Birmingham (IRB‐300008132) and Case Western Reserve University (#STUDY20211334) Institutional Review Boards and was conducted in accordance with a data use agreement with the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services. RESULTS

Among US Medicare beneficiaries with Part D coverage, 8888 beneficiaries (0.4%) were diagnosed with incident epilepsy in 2019. These individuals filled prescriptions for a median of 9 (IQR 5–12) distinct therapeutic drug classes in the preceding year (2018), compared to a median of 6 (IQR 3–9) among those without epilepsy (Table 1). Specifically, individuals with incident epilepsy were more likely than those without incident epilepsy to have prescriptions filled for 5 or more drug classes (81.4% vs. 63.3%), 8 or more drug classes (59.0% vs. 35.2%), and 11 or more drug classes (35.5% vs. 17.2%).

Demographic results show broad similarities between those with and without incident epilepsy (Table 1), but some differences were observed. Beneficiaries with incident epilepsy had a median age of 78 years (IQR 73–84), slightly higher than the non‐epilepsy group, which had a median age of 76 years (IQR 72–82). A greater proportion of beneficiaries with incident epilepsy were aged 85 or older (25.0% vs. 18.5%), non‐Hispanic Black (23.4% vs. 12.7%), and Hispanic (12.2% vs. 9.6%). Compared to those without epilepsy, beneficiaries with incident epilepsy had a higher prevalence of key comorbidities, including stroke (15.2% vs. 3.6%), neurological conditions (35.8% vs. 13.8%), psychiatric conditions (39.8% vs. 24.7%), and cardiovascular disease (62.0% vs. 42.9%). Alzheimer disease was present in 4.4% of the study population and 13.9% of patients with incident epilepsy. Traumatic brain injury (TBI) was identified in 0.3% of the study population and 1.0% of patients with incident epilepsy.

Drug classes were ranked based on the percentage of beneficiaries who filled prescriptions for each class in 2018, for both those with incident epilepsy and those without incident epilepsy in 2019, as shown in Figure 1. The most common drug classes in both groups were antilipemic agents and beta‐blockers. Among those without incident epilepsy, 58.4% of beneficiaries filled prescriptions for an antilipemic agent and 37.8% filled prescriptions for beta‐blockers. Among those with incident epilepsy, 58.9% filled prescriptions for antilipemic agents and 47.3% filled prescriptions for beta‐blockers. Angiotensin II inhibitors were slightly more common in the incident epilepsy group (20.3%) than in the non‐epilepsy group (19.1%), but ranked lower among the most common medication classes due to the higher prevalence of other medications, such as loop diuretics and gastric agents (Figure 1).

In 2018, certain drug class pairings were more frequently observed among individuals who developed epilepsy in 2019 compared to those who did not (Figure 2). For example, the combination of other antihypertensives and beta blockers was more prevalent among those with incident epilepsy (ARR 1.54 [1.38, 1.71]). Similarly, combinations such as central nervous system (CNS) medications with antidepressants (ARR 1.33 [99% CI: 1.20–1.47]), antipsychotics & antidepressants (ARR 1.45 [99% CI: 1.28, 1.58]), and insulin and beta blockers (ARR 1.50 [99% CI: 1.35, 1.67]) were more commonly filled among beneficiaries with incident epilepsy.

To reduce the potential for misclassification bias—where individuals prescribed ASMs but lacking epilepsy diagnosis codes might be incorrectly classified as epilepsy‐free during the exposure period—we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding all beneficiaries who filled prescriptions for ASMs in 2018. The results of this analysis were highly consistent with the primary findings: beneficiaries with incident epilepsy continued to have a substantially higher polypharmacy burden compared to those without epilepsy (Table S3), and the rankings of the most commonly prescribed drug classes remained similar (Figure S1).

Among those with ASMs in 2018, nearly 85% were for gabapentin, a drug commonly used off‐label for pain treatment (Table S4). The most common co‐occurring chronic condition categories in this group are presented in Table S5. The high prevalence of neurological, musculoskeletal, and psychiatric conditions is consistent with the use of gabapentin for non‐epilepsy indications such as neuropathic pain.

DISCUSSION

This study characterizes the burden and patterns of polypharmacy among older US Medicare beneficiaries who were subsequently diagnosed with incident epilepsy. We found that individuals with incident epilepsy had substantially greater medication burden in the year preceding diagnosis, filling prescriptions for a higher number and broader range of therapeutic drug classes compared with beneficiaries who did not develop epilepsy. Nearly 90% of beneficiaries with incident epilepsy filled prescriptions for five or more distinct drug classes prior to diagnosis, underscoring that polypharmacy is highly prevalent at the time epilepsy emerges in older adults.

In addition to greater overall medication burden, specific drug classes and combinations were disproportionately common among beneficiaries who later developed epilepsy. Antidepressants, ASMs, gastric medications, and loop diuretics were filled more frequently, and combinations involving antipsychotics, antidepressants, CNS medications, and cardiometabolic therapies occurred at markedly higher rates than in beneficiaries without epilepsy. These co‐occurrence patterns should be interpreted as exploratory findings that likely reflect the underlying psychiatric and neurological comorbidity burden of this population rather than pharmacologically distinct combinations with independent effects on epilepsy risk. Together, these patterns suggest that older adults with incident epilepsy often enter the diagnostic period with complex, multimorbid medication regimens, emphasizing the importance of considering the broader prescribing context when epilepsy is first identified, which likely reflects the underlying multimorbidity of this population.

Prior studies have demonstrated that polypharmacy is common among people with epilepsy, particularly in older adults, but most of this evidence is derived from cohorts with prevalent epilepsy or focuses specifically on ASM use rather than overall medication burden. 8 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 Terman and colleagues reported a median of 12 medications among older adults with prevalent epilepsy, which is comparable to our finding of a median of 10 medications in newly diagnosed epilepsy. 27 A nationally representative analysis using NHANES data showed that nearly half of adults of all ages with epilepsy also had polypharmacy and that CNS polypharmacy was common, underscoring the complexity of medication regimens in epilepsy populations across the adult age spectrum. 8 However, these studies largely reflect established epilepsy and do not address whether high medication burden precedes the onset of epilepsy or emerges because of long‐term seizure management.

Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating that substantial polypharmacy is already present in the year preceding an incident epilepsy diagnosis in older adults. Using a therapeutic drug class–based approach, we observed that nearly 90% of beneficiaries who developed epilepsy filled prescriptions for five or more distinct drug classes prior to diagnosis, with a median of ten classes, substantially exceeding the burden observed in beneficiaries without epilepsy. Moreover, we identified specific medication class combinations—particularly involving antidepressants, antipsychotics, and other CNS medications—that were disproportionately prevalent among individuals who later developed epilepsy. These patterns complement prior work showing that ASM polypharmacy in newly diagnosed older adults is associated with higher short‐term injury risk, costs, and utilization. 28 , 29 Together, these findings suggest that polypharmacy in older adults with epilepsy is not solely a downstream consequence of seizure treatment but may represent a defining feature of the clinical profile at the time epilepsy emerges, reflecting underlying multimorbidity, neuropsychiatric burden, and complex prescribing environments.

This study is descriptive in nature and does not address the causal relationships between polypharmacy, specific drug class combinations, and the development of epilepsy. The observed associations may reflect underlying health status, comorbidity patterns, or prescribing practices in older adults with complex medical needs. Our findings contribute important epidemiological data on the extent and patterns of polypharmacy in older adults with incident epilepsy, which may inform future research and clinical strategies to optimize medication management in this population. These findings suggest that medication optimization at the time of epilepsy diagnosis in older adults may need to address substantial pre‐existing polypharmacy and CNS‐active drug class combinations—not solely the selection of an initial ASM—to reduce the risk of adverse outcomes.

These findings have several practical clinical implications. First, clinicians evaluating older adults with new‐onset epilepsy should conduct a comprehensive medication review, as the high prevalence of pre‐existing polypharmacy—particularly CNS‐active medications—may complicate both diagnosis and treatment. Certain drug classes, including some antidepressants and antipsychotics, may lower the seizure threshold, and their presence in the medication regimen warrants careful consideration when interpreting new neurological symptoms. Second, the introduction of ASMs into already complex regimens creates substantial potential for drug–drug interactions, particularly with enzyme‐inducing agents such as carbamazepine and phenytoin, which are known to affect the metabolism of a wide range of co‐prescribed drug classes. Third, the high burden of polypharmacy at the time of epilepsy diagnosis represents an important opportunity for structured medication review and deprescribing, ideally involving pharmacist collaboration, to reduce the risk of adverse drug events in this vulnerable population.

Limitations

The most significant limitation of this study is the reliance on ICD‐10 coding to define incident epilepsy, which may lead to misclassification due to coding errors. Although the ICD‐10 algorithm used has demonstrated reasonable sensitivity (84.4%) and specificity (79.4%) among adults, some prevalent epilepsy cases may have been incorrectly categorized as incident. 16 , 17 For example, if epilepsy was not captured by qualifying inpatient claims or by at least two outpatient nondrug claims during the three‐year lookback period, individuals with pre‐existing epilepsy could be misclassified as incident cases. In these instances, medications such as antiseizure drugs prescribed and filled in 2018 may have been appropriately used for seizure management but could appear as polypharmacy preceding incident epilepsy. Additionally, our case definition did not incorporate ASM prescriptions, a strategy that can improve specificity but may reduce sensitivity by excluding untreated cases. 31 This choice was made to balance accuracy and consistency with prior studies, but future research could explore combining these methods to refine case identification. 32

Detection bias is another concern, as individuals with fewer healthcare encounters may be less likely to have epilepsy detected, which could also result in lower observed polypharmacy. This could inflate observed associations between polypharmacy and incident epilepsy. Our data sources do not include a comprehensive healthcare utilization metric suitable for inclusion as a regression covariate. Our data sources do not include comprehensive healthcare utilization metrics, limiting the ability to assess this bias. Future studies with more detailed claims or clinical data could clarify the influence of healthcare utilization on epilepsy diagnosis and medication use. Some cases identified as epilepsy may represent psychogenic non‐epileptic seizures (PNES), which are often associated with psychiatric comorbidities such as depression or post‐traumatic stress disorder. 33 This potential misclassification may confound observed associations between psychiatric medication use and incident epilepsy. 34

A further limitation is the potential for reverse causation or prodromal bias. Medication exposures were assessed in the year immediately preceding epilepsy diagnosis (2018), and it is possible that some medications were initiated in response to prodromal symptoms of epilepsy — such as mood disturbance, pain, or sleep disorders — that preceded formal diagnosis. This is particularly relevant for psychiatric and CNS medications, which may be prescribed for symptoms that are later recognized as epilepsy‐related.

The drug classification system used in this study (VA national formulary) includes broad categories such as “CNS medications,” which encompass drugs with diverse mechanisms of action and indications. This may limit the clinical specificity of our findings. Future research may benefit from analyses at the single‐ingredient level or using alternative classification systems based on mechanism of action. Additionally, the current analysis treats drug class exposure as a static cross‐sectional measure within the 2018 exposure year and does not account for changes in drug class burden over time or the timing of individual drug initiations relative to epilepsy onset. Future studies employing time‐varying exposure models or survival analysis frameworks would better characterize the temporal dynamics of polypharmacy in the period preceding epilepsy diagnosis. Finally, we note that our study period (2016–2019) preceded the FDA approval of anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibody therapies, so contemporary Alzheimer disease treatments were largely unavailable.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that polypharmacy is substantially more prevalent among older US Medicare beneficiaries who are subsequently diagnosed with incident epilepsy compared to those who remain without epilepsy, and that this elevated medication burden is already present in the year preceding diagnosis. Specific drug classes and combinations—particularly involving antipsychotics, antidepressants, and other CNS‐active medications—were more commonly observed among individuals with incident epilepsy, reflecting the complex comorbidity profiles and prescribing environments in this population.

These findings underscore the importance of recognizing polypharmacy as a central feature of the clinical context in which epilepsy emerges in older adults, rather than solely as a consequence of epilepsy treatment. Future research should examine how pre‐existing medication burden and specific drug class combinations influence clinical outcomes following epilepsy diagnosis and whether targeted strategies to optimize medication regimens at or near the time of diagnosis can improve safety and care for this vulnerable population.
