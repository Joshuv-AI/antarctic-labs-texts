Association Between Locomotive Syndrome and N-Terminal Pro B-Type Natriuretic Peptide Concentration in a Health Screening Cohort
Authors: Chiaki Mizuno, Hiroaki Hiraiwa, Shinya Yokoyama, Takanori Ito, Kazuki Sahara, Takashi Honda, Taku Tanaka, Tetsuhito Kojima, Naoyoshi Kariya, Ryota Ito, Shingo Kazama, Toru Kondo, Ryota Morimoto, Hiroki Kawashima, Toyoaki Murohara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLocomotive syndrome (LS), an early predictor of physical frailty, is frequently evaluated during health screenings in Japan. However, its relationship with early cardiac dysfunction remains unclear. We investigated the association between LS severity and N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) concentration, a myocardial stress biomarker, in a general health screening population.Methods and ResultsThis cross-sectional study analyzed 8,593 individuals who underwent health checkups between 2018 and 2023 in Aichi, Japan. LS was assessed using the Short Test Battery for LS, including physical tests and a 25-item questionnaire, and NT-proBNP was measured from blood samples. Individuals were categorized by LS stage (non-LS and stages 1–3) and NT-proBNP concentration (<55, 55–124, 125–299, ≥300 pg/mL). Logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between LS severity and elevated NT-proBNP (cut-off ≥125 pg/mL). A stepwise increase in NT-proBNP was observed with higher LS stage (Ptrend <0.001). Compared with non-LS, LS stages 2 (odds ratio [OR] 2.24; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.35–3.72) and 3 (OR 3.51; 95% CI 2.02–6.12) were independently associated with elevated NT-proBNP.ConclusionsThe increase in NT-proBNP with LS severity suggests a link between myocardial stress progression and physical function decline in asymptomatic individuals. LS assessment may help identify early stage cardiovascular dysfunction, although the causal relationship between LS and cardiac dysfunction remains to be clarified.

Central Figure

Locomotive syndrome (LS) was proposed in 2007 by the Japanese Orthopaedic Association and is defined as an age-related deterioration in mobility due to locomotor system dysfunction.1–3 LS is seen in musculoskeletal conditions such as osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, spondylosis, and sarcopenia,4 as well as in neurological conditions, such as disc degeneration with lumbar spondylosis.5 LS represents an early phase of mobility decline (pre-frailty) that precedes frailty.6 It is frequently assessed during health checkups using the Short Test Battery for LS, which incorporates the stand-up test, the 2-step test, and the 25-question Geriatric Locomotive Function Scale (GLFS-25).5

It has been shown that malnutrition and the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index, which are significantly related to LS, are significant prognostic factors in heart failure.7 Another recent study showed that poor physical function predicts poor prognosis in patients with heart failure.8 Moreover, several studies have illustrated the potential association of LS specifically with cardiovascular health.9–11 LS has been linked to several systemic chronic diseases, such as hypertension, stroke, and coronary artery disease – conditions that can ultimately lead to heart failure.10 Heart failure results in physical, mental, and cognitive decline, as well as reduced quality of life and increased mortality.12 Therefore, early indicators of cardiac dysfunction with the potential to predict cardiovascular disease development would be beneficial.

N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) is a sensitive early indicator of heart failure.13,14 A recent study showed that elevated serum NT-proBNP is independently associated with frailty onset (assessed using the Japanese version of the Cardiovascular Health Study criteria) in community-dwelling older adults.15 It has also been shown that impaired physical function and frailty are associated with poor prognosis in patients with heart failure.16–18 Several studies have shown that widely used assessments of physical performance, such as the 6-min walk test, Short Physical Performance Battery, and gait speed test, are correlated with hospitalization and mortality due to heart failure.19–26 It has also been shown that decreased mobility may indicate preclinical cardiovascular stress, including increased risk of progression from pre-frailty to overt heart failure.27,28 However, the association between LS, an early indicator of frailty, and NT-proBNP has not been investigated. If LS severity is shown to be associated with NT-proBNP elevation, the assessment of LS could serve as a novel indicator for the early evaluation of cardiovascular risk in both clinical and health screening settings. Given that locomotive decline begins decades before clinical frailty becomes evident, evaluating LS in younger individuals and middle-aged adults, as well as older adults, may provide an opportunity for early cardiovascular prevention in a broader population.

Through this study, we aim to determine whether an association exists between LS, an early indicator of mobility decline, and NT-proBNP, a proxy early indicator of cardiac dysfunction. If such an association exists, LS could reflect subclinical or asymptomatic cardiovascular burden. The findings of this study provide new insights that may contribute to the earlier detection and prevention of cardiovascular disease.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed data from individuals who underwent annual health checkups at the health examination center of the Aichi Health Promotion Foundation (Aichi, Japan) between April 2018 and March 2023, as part of a research project led by Nagoya University entitled ‘Research to clarify the accuracy of liver disease detection, complications, clinical course, and prognosis at a health checkup facility’. The health examination center conducts company-sponsored, lifestyle-related, individual, and comprehensive medical checkups.

Participants

The majority of the participants were working-age adults, with many undergoing company-sponsored annual health checkups. The cohort included a broad age range, from individuals in their 20 s to those beyond retirement age, with a large proportion aged in their 30s–60s. All included participants underwent LS assessment and NT-proBNP measurement, as determined by their inclusion in company-sponsored cardiovascular screening packages and/or according to the individual’s decision (the participants were asked whether they wanted to undergo these additional tests for preventive purposes when undergoing their health checkups as part of the center’s preventive cardiovascular risk assessment model) or physician’s recommendation, rather than owing to the presence of symptoms or clinical suspicion. It was explained that the additional tests were voluntary, not mandatory, and that there would be no disadvantage to not undergoing them.

Participants who completed both LS assessment and NT-proBNP measurement on the same day were included in the present analysis. If participants underwent multiple health checkups during the study period, data from the first visit at which LS was assessed were used for the analysis. Participants with missing data on NT-proBNP or LS assessment were excluded. Therefore, the study population included all participants with complete NT-proBNP and LS data, including those with and without pre-existing conditions (primarily cardiovascular conditions, albeit asymptomatic, that could influence NT-proBNP) and those receiving medical treatment for cardiovascular disease.

The included participants were considered to be asymptomatic on the following bases: (1) the cohort consisted of participants undergoing annual voluntary health checkups, including many individuals with chronic but stable conditions who remained capable of daily activities without overt symptoms; (2) NT-proBNP measurement and LS assessment were performed as optional screening tests, not as evaluations prompted by symptoms; and (3) several participants had cardiovascular risk factors or past medical history, but their conditions were not in an acute or decompensated state, and they attended the checkup as part of routine preventive care. We judged that excluding individuals with pre-existing conditions would distort the representativeness of the real-world health screening population and limit external validity, so this population was retained.

Consent for participation and publication was obtained through an opt-out procedure. The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nagoya University Hospital (approval no. 2023-0111; approval date June 22, 2023), and the study protocol complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

LS Assessment

LS was assessed using the Short Test Battery for LS, which combines the stand-up test of motor function, the 2-step test of motor function, and the GLFS-25.5 The results of each test were classified into LS stages 1–3, and the most advanced stage among the 3 tests was adopted as the participant’s final LS classification. Participants who did not meet the criteria for any stage were classified as non-LS. Based on the GLFS-25 score, the participants were categorized into non-LS (<7) or LS stage 1 (≥7 to <16), stage 2 (≥16 to <24), or stage 3 (≥24) groups. Stage 3 was newly introduced in 2020.29,30 Before 2020, a patient with a GLFS-25 score of 24 would have been categorized as LS stage 2, while after 2020, they would be categorized as LS stage 3. As the study period extends back to 2018, which spans both the old and new scoring systems, participants with GLFS-25 scores of >24 prior to 2020 were reclassified from LS stage 2 to LS stage 3.

Classification by NT-proBNP

Serum NT-proBNP was measured by blood tests at health checkups. A concentration of ≥125 pg/mL was considered elevated according to the Universal Definition and Classification of Heart Failure jointly proposed by the Heart Failure Society of America, Heart Failure Association of the European Society of Cardiology, and the Japanese Heart Failure Society.31 Additionally, according to the Japanese Heart Failure Society guidelines,32 NT-proBNP ≥900 pg/mL indicated high-risk heart failure (high likelihood of requiring hospitalization due to worsening heart failure in the near future); 300–900 pg/mL indicated a high likelihood of heart failure; 125–300 pg/mL indicated pre-heart failure (cardiac dysfunction present, but without signs or symptoms of heart failure) or possible heart failure; 55–125 pg/mL indicated a low likelihood of heart failure, but monitoring was recommended, with cardiac evaluation or referral to a cardiologist if the concentration was ≥125 pg/mL; and 0–55 pg/mL indicated an extremely low likelihood of heart failure.

Classification of Metabolic Syndrome (MetS)

MetS was assessed according to the Japanese diagnostic criteria.33 Participants with abdominal obesity (based on waist circumference) and 2 or more of the following risk factors were classified as having MetS: elevated blood pressure, elevated blood glucose, or abnormal serum lipids. Those with abdominal obesity and only 1 of these risk factors were classified as having pre-MetS, a borderline metabolic condition defined in the Japanese criteria, and participants who met none of these criteria and did not have abdominal obesity were classified as non-MetS.33

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as the median (interquartile range [IQR]) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as number (%) and were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test.

The participants were categorized into 2 groups based on the NT-proBNP concentration using a cut-off value of 125 pg/mL (NT-proBNP <125 vs. ≥125 pg/mL). Logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between LS and elevated NT-proBNP, with elevated NT-proBNP (binary variable) as the dependent variable. Both binary LS classification (presence: stages 1, 2, and 3; absence: stage 0) and 4-level LS staging (stages 0, 1, 2, and 3) were used as independent variables. In addition, analyses were conducted using LS assessed by the Short Test Battery for LS, which includes 2 physical function tests and the GLFS-25 questionnaire, as well as LS assessed solely by GLFS-25.

The multivariate model was adjusted for age, female sex, body mass index (BMI), hypertension treatment, diabetes treatment, renal dysfunction based on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), smoking, stroke, heart failure, ischemic heart disease, and atrial fibrillation. Age, female sex, BMI, eGFR, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation have been reported to be associated with NT-proBNP,32,34-37 while hypertension treatment, diabetes treatment, smoking, stroke, and ischemic heart disease are associated with LS.10,38,39 Therefore, these covariates were selected based on previous studies reporting their associations with either NT-proBNP or LS, to minimize potential confounding effects. The maximum variance inflation factor of these variables was 1.31, indicating no multicollinearity.

Moreover, to investigate the interaction between LS and age, the participants were categorized into 6 age subgroups (≤39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and ≥80 years). Logistic regression analyses, including LS stage, age subgroup, and their interaction terms, were performed.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/SE (version 19.0; Stata Corp.), and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

NT-proBNP Concentration by LS Severity

The relationships among NT-proBNP concentration, LS prevalence, and age were evaluated. The prevalence of LS was higher among individuals with elevated NT-proBNP and progressively increased with age (Figure 3). Furthermore, higher NT-proBNP concentrations were associated with more severe LS. The proportion of individuals with LS stage 3 increased with increasing age, especially among older adults with elevated NT-proBNP (Figure 4). No significant interaction between LS stage and age was observed, suggesting that LS progression was associated with NT-proBNP independent of age (Supplementary Table 1). Univariate and Multivariate Logistic Regression Analysis

Univariate logistic regression analysis using a threshold NT-proBNP concentration of ≥125 pg/mL revealed a significant association between LS and elevated NT-proBNP concentration. Individuals with any LS severity had an unadjusted OR of 3.08 (95% confidence interval [CI] 2.49–3.80; P<0.001) compared with those without LS. Univariate analysis showed a progressive increase in the unadjusted odds of elevated NT-proBNP with LS severity (OR 2.55, 5.25, and 8.70 for stages 1–3, respectively; all P<0.001; Table 2).

To assess whether the association between LS and elevated NT-proBNP concentration was independent of potential confounders, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed. In the adjusted model, LS was independently associated with elevated NT-proBNP. The overall OR for LS was 1.67 (95% CI 1.31–2.13; P<0.001). The multivariate analysis showed a progressive increase in the odds of elevated NT-proBNP with LS severity (OR 1.50 [P=0.002], 2.24 [P=0.002], and 3.51 [P<0.001] for stages 1–3, respectively; Table 2).

When LS was defined using the GLFS-25 questionnaire only, the presence of LS showed a trend toward an association with elevated NT-proBNP, but this did not reach statistical significance. When analyzed by LS severity, GLFS-25-defined LS was significantly associated with elevated NT-proBNP from stage 2 onward in the adjusted model (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we demonstrated a significant association between LS severity and elevated NT-proBNP concentration among individuals undergoing routine health checkups. Our findings highlight that individuals with more advanced LS, as assessed using the standardized Short Test Battery for LS, had progressively higher NT-proBNP concentrations – a biomarker of myocardial stress, even in asymptomatic individuals. This association remained significant even after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, renal dysfunction (based on eGFR), and other factors associated with NT-proBNP and LS.34–39 These results indicate that increasing LS severity was independently associated with a higher likelihood of NT-proBNP elevation, even after adjusting for major confounders.

The graded relationship between LS stage (LS 1–3) and NT-proBNP concentration suggests a severity-dependent association. Notably, in the adjusted model, the OR for elevated NT-proBNP increased progressively from LS stage 1 (OR 1.50) to LS stage 3 (OR 3.51). This suggests that even the early stages of mobility decline are associated with measurable myocardial stress. This study was not designed to establish causality between LS and NT-proBNP, as the latter is a proxy biomarker of myocardial stress and dysfunction, not a pathophysiologic driver, and there is no known causal relationship between the two. We simply hypothesized that, as NT-proBNP is elevated in cardiac dysfunction, the presence of an association between LS and NT-proBNP elevation may establish LS as a screening test for early cardiac dysfunction. We suggest that the association between NT-proBNP and LS may be mediated through the common underlying variable of cardiac dysfunction, which can be indirectly measured by NT-proBNP. Causality between LS and cardiac dysfunction itself could not be established in the present study. Therefore, it remains to be clarified whether: early cardiac dysfunction, as indicated by elevated NT-proBNP, reduces exercise tolerance and physical performance, exacerbating mobility decline; decreased physical activity (mobility) and muscle mass (sarcopenia) lead to myocardial damage and cardiac dysfunction, potentially via the increased cardiac workload over time; or the causality is bidirectional. Although the direction of causality between cardiac dysfunction and LS was not established, our findings suggest that impaired mobility may not merely coexist with cardiovascular stress; rather, it may actually reflect subclinical myocardial strain.

In the Hisayama Study conducted in Japan, NT-proBNP concentration >55 pg/mL was significantly associated with an increased risk of systemic vascular events, and NT-proBNP concentration >125 pg/mL was associated with higher incidences of coronary artery disease, ischemic stroke, and cardiovascular mortality.40 Even among asymptomatic individuals undergoing routine health checkups, some may have a high risk of atherosclerosis and correspond to heart failure Stage A, with high atherosclerotic risk or unrecognized structural heart disease.41 A previous study showed that the measurement of B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), which is biochemically related to NT-proBNP, is useful for the early detection of preclinical heart failure in populations with structural heart disease in primary care settings.42 Based on this observation, as well as the findings of the present study, it is plausible that early screening incorporating NT-proBNP measurement is useful for the early detection of cardiac dysfunction.

The findings of this study are consistent with previous research emphasizing the clinical significance of combining biomarker assessment with physical function evaluation. For example, Ueno et al. demonstrated that elevated BNP was associated with a shorter 6-min walk distance.43 In addition, Koshikawa et al. reported that elderly patients with heart failure exhibited excessive skeletal muscle degradation and atrophy compared with control subjects, suggesting that inflammation may be involved in this mechanism. Furthermore, inflammatory cytokines and BNP were positively correlated with increased muscle protein degradation and negatively correlated with muscle mass and strength.44 We acknowledge that participants in the present study were general health checkup examinees and cannot be compared directly with elderly patients with heart failure. Nevertheless, the reported negative correlations between BNP and both muscle mass and strength, combined with the results of the present study, make it plausible that elevations in NT-proBNP, a proxy early indicator of cardiac dysfunction, may be associated with physical function decline, namely LS progression.

Another important finding of this study is that LS remained significantly associated with elevated NT-proBNP regardless of age, underscoring that mobility decline may signal cardiovascular stress, even in younger adults. Previous studies have reported that the prevalence of LS is 21.7–25.0% among individuals aged <40 years and 31.6–42.1% among those in their 40s and 50s.9 Even among a university student population with a mean age of 19.1 years, 20.8% were diagnosed with LS,45 indicating that a certain proportion of young individuals also present with LS. Across a wide age range, including younger adults, mild functional decline in the musculoskeletal system is first recognized as LS, which gradually progresses and may eventually lead to frailty in older age.26 Therefore, LS assessment from a young age could contribute to preventive measures and represent a step toward extending healthy life expectancy. Our findings suggest that LS may serve as an indicator of cardiovascular risk in a broader population, including younger and middle-aged adults. In other words, the clinical significance of LS assessment extends beyond older adults and may also be applied for the early detection of subclinical, asymptomatic, or undiagnosed heart disease in adult populations more generally.

In this study, we used the Short Test Battery for LS, which includes 3 physical performance tests; however, it has been reported that LS can also be assessed using the GLFS-25 questionnaire alone,46 which may represent a more practical approach. While LS assessment using the Short Test Battery was associated with elevated NT-proBNP from LS stage 1, assessment based on the GLFS-25 questionnaire alone showed a significant association only from LS stage 2 onward in the adjusted model, and overall LS diagnosis (not stratified by LS severity) exhibited only a trend toward significance. Questionnaire-based LS assessment is simple, low-cost, and feasible, not only at routine health checkups, but also in community healthcare and general outpatient settings. However, although it is useful for identifying individuals with more advanced LS, its sensitivity for detecting milder LS may be limited. Therefore, questionnaire-based assessment should be considered as an initial screening tool, with further evaluation, including cardiovascular biomarkers or echocardiography, recommended for those identified as positive for LS. At present, there are no established cut-off values or optimal follow-up intervals for cardiovascular risk assessment using LS; however, the ability to evaluate LS through both physical performance tests and self-reported questionnaires enhances the feasibility of implementing LS assessment during health checkups and in community healthcare settings.

Overall, our findings suggest that LS assessment during routine health checkups may help identify individuals at risk of developing overt cardiovascular disease at an early stage. Incorporating LS assessment into routine health screening programs may provide a cost-effective and non-invasive strategy for early cardiovascular risk stratification in the community. Although our findings indicate a severity-dependent increase in myocardial stress beginning as early as LS stage 1, there are currently no established evidence-based thresholds for LS that mandate cardiovascular evaluation. Therefore, future longitudinal studies are needed to establish optimal thresholds for LS that could guide referral for biomarker testing or cardiac imaging. Moreover, as this study was cross-sectional, we were unable to determine how frequently individuals with LS should undergo cardiovascular follow up. However, given the graded association between LS stage and NT-proBNP, LS assessment may help identify individuals who could benefit from closer longitudinal monitoring. Prospective studies are required to determine the appropriate follow-up interval and whether LS progression predicts subsequent cardiovascular events.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, only 8,593 of 38,035 individuals were included owing to missing NT-proBNP or LS data. Moreover, the study population consisted of individuals who voluntarily participated in routine health examinations, potentially representing a more health-conscious subgroup than the general population. Although many participants were working-age adults, as participation in the optional tests (LS assessment, and NT-proBNP measurement) was partly self-selected, the study population cannot be considered a fully representative sample of all working-age adults in Japan, and the findings should be interpreted accordingly. Furthermore, the large number of exclusions may have introduced selection bias. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all populations. Second, although multivariate adjustments were made for several known cardiovascular risk factors and factors associated with NT-proBNP and LS, the possibility of residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Variables such as physical activity level, medication use, and undiagnosed comorbidities may have influenced the results. Last, owing to the cross-sectional nature of this study, causal relationships between LS and cardiac dysfunction could not be established. Longitudinal studies are needed to: determine the direction of causality between cardiac dysfunction and LS; clarify whether LS progression predicts subsequent cardiovascular events and whether LS improvement reduces cardiac dysfunction; and determine the mechanisms underlying the association between NT-proBNP and LS.

Conclusions

LS was independently associated with elevated NT-proBNP in a large health screening cohort in Japan. LS severity was associated with progressively higher odds of myocardial stress, as indicated by NT-proBNP elevation. These findings suggest that LS assessment may serve as a useful, non-invasive screening tool for early cardiovascular risk stratification in asymptomatic individuals.
