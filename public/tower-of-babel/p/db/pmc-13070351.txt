The Effect of Adenotonsillectomy on Sleep Efficiency in Pediatric Patients
Authors: Basir S Mansoor, Matthew Zhang, Asim Mohamed, Ahmed Abdi, Romaine F Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo evaluate the impact of adenotonsillectomy (T&A) on sleep efficiency (SE) characteristics, a marker of sleep quality with implications for behavior and cognition in children with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).MethodsWe analyzed 139 children who underwent full‐night polysomnography (PSG) before and after T&A between January 2021 and September 2023. Patients were stratified into three SE severity groups: normal (greater than or equal to 85%), mild–moderate (70%–84%), and severe (less than 70%).ResultsOur cohort (median age 5.15 years, IQR 7.62; 68% male; 55% Hispanic) showed notable changes post‐T&A: normal SE increased from 42.4% to 48.9%, while severe SE increased from 9.4% to 18.0%. Median AHI significantly decreased from 17.3 (IQR 26) to 6 (IQR 8.8), confirming T&A's effectiveness in treating OSA. Sleep architecture patterns persisted postsurgery (p < 0.001 for NREM and REM differences), but respiratory parameters improved across SE categories. Down Syndrome (DS) was associated with poorer outcomes (p = 0.013). The severe SE group showed a substantial decline in SE (−17%, SD: 23%, p < 0.001), with high individual variability.ConclusionsT&A improves SE and resolves OSA for many pediatric patients. However, a subset experiences worsening SE, particularly those with comorbidities like DS. These findings highlight the importance of individualized postoperative care and the need for further research into predictors of T&A outcomes in pediatric OSA.Level of Evidence4.Keywords: adenotonsillectomy, obstructive sleep apnea, pediatric, sleep efficiency

Introduction

Pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common sleep‐related breathing disorder marked by recurrent episodes of partial or complete upper airway obstruction, leading to apnea, hypopnea, or persistent hypoventilation that disrupts normal sleep and ventilation patterns [1, 2, 3]. Adenotonsillectomy (T&A) is the first‐line treatment for most children with OSA, as it improves airway patency and reduces the frequency of obstructive events during sleep [4, 5, 6]. However, while T&A is known to alleviate respiratory obstruction, its specific impact on overall sleep efficiency (SE), the proportion of time spent asleep relative to time spent in bed, remains less well understood, particularly in large, stratified pediatric cohorts [7, 8].

In children, adequate sleep quantity and continuity are essential for optimal cognitive development, behavior regulation, and physical health [9, 10, 11, 12]. Several studies have examined the impact of T&A on sleep as measured by polysomnographic (PSG) testing. There is a marked reduction in the arousal index and number of positional changes during sleep for children who were treated with T&A [13]. Similarly, meta‐analyses reveal a 4.8 point decrease in the apnea‐hypopnea index (AHI) for children treated with T&A as compared to those who underwent “watchful waiting” with supportive care [14]. Other studies have showcased improved quality of life, sleep architecture, and oxygen saturation postoperatively [15, 16]. While the literature supports the general benefits of T&A on sleep, SE is typically evaluated as a secondary outcome and remains understudied, especially in large pediatric cohorts.

The primary aim of this observational study is to address this gap by stratifying children with OSA by SE severity and directly evaluating the effect of T&A on SE. A secondary aim is to explore how T&A influences related sleep and respiratory parameters, as well as SE in children with comorbidities. We hypothesize that children across all SE strata will experience improvements in SE, though we expect smaller gains in those with the most severe baseline SE impairment or coexisting medical conditions.

Methods

We conducted this retrospective cohort study to examine the causal relationship between adenotonsillectomy (T&A) and sleep efficiency (SE) outcomes in pediatric patients with obstructive sleep apnea. Our analytical framework considers T&A as the primary exposure, change in SE as the primary outcome, and accounts for key confounding variables including age, body mass index, baseline OSA severity, and comorbidities such as Down syndrome and congenital heart disease. We hypothesized that while respiratory improvements (AHI reduction) would mediate some SE benefits, direct effects on sleep architecture and arousal patterns would also contribute to overall outcomes.

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary academic medical center with Institutional Review Board approval from UT Southwestern (STU 042014–001) and waiver of informed consent. We identified 205 consecutive pediatric patients who underwent T&A with both preoperative and postoperative polysomnography (PSG) at Children's Health, Dallas, between January 2021 and September 2023.

Inclusion criteria consisted of patients aged 2 to 18 years diagnosed with OSA who underwent T&A and completed both preoperative and postoperative PSG. Both obese (body mass index ≥ 95th percentile) and nonobese children were eligible. Exclusion criteria included the absence of information in either a preoperative or postoperative PSG (N = 64) or the presence of a tracheostomy (N = 2). A total of 139 patients met criteria for inclusion in the final analysis.

We categorized participants into three groups based on sleep efficiency severity: normal (≥ 85%), mild to moderate (70%–84%), and severe (< 70%). This stratification was applied to both preoperative and postoperative data to track categorical changes following surgery.

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records (EPIC production). Collected demographic variables included age, sex, race, ethnicity, zip code, insurance status, and relevant comorbidities including prematurity, allergic rhinitis, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), asthma, Down syndrome (DS), craniofacial abnormalities, congenital heart disease, hearing loss, and allergies. Body mass index (BMI), BMI percentile, and BMI z‐scores were calculated using weight and height measurements obtained during PSG visits, with weight classification following CDC‐defined, age‐ and sex‐adjusted BMI percentiles [17]. Tonsillar size was documented from otolaryngology physical exams using the Brodsky grading scale [18].

PSG data collected included the apnea‐hypopnea index (AHI), obstructive AHI (oAHI), number of obstructive apneas (OA), obstructive hypopneas (OH), central apnea index (CAI), arousal index (AI), time spent in total non‐REM (NREM) and REM sleep stages, total sleep time (TST), sleep efficiency, mean oxygen saturation, oxygen saturation nadir (SpO2), percentage of sleep time with oxygen saturation < 90% (TB90%), peak end‐tidal carbon dioxide (peak CO2), average CO2, time spent with end‐tidal CO2 > 50 mmHg (TST50 > CO2), and total number of periodic limb movements of sleep (PLMS). Sleep efficiency was defined as the proportion of total sleep time relative to time spent in bed, with all other sleep parameters defined according to the AASM Manual for the Scoring of Sleep and Associated Events [19].

To evaluate time‐dependent recovery patterns, we stratified patients by interval between T&A and follow‐up PSG: short‐term (< 3 months), medium‐term (3–6 months), and long‐term (> 6 months post‐surgery). This temporal stratification allowed us to assess whether SE improvements varied by recovery duration.

All statistics were performed with Stata (StataCorp., 2021; Stata Statistical Software: Release 19, College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC.). Categorical variables are presented as counts with percentages, while continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile range (IQR). Comparisons of demographic, clinical, and polysomnographic parameters across SE severity groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the Pearson chi‐squared test for categorical variables. When the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated significant differences among SE groups, Dunn's post hoc test with Bonferroni correction was used for pairwise comparisons. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. This study adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational studies [20].

Results

Our analysis included 139 children who met all inclusion criteria. The cohort had a median age of 5.15 years (IQR 7.62), with 68% male and 55% Hispanic participants. Key comorbidities included Down syndrome (10.8%), congenital heart disease (13.7%), prematurity (14.5%), asthma (41.3%), gastroesophageal reflux disease (18.7%), and allergic rhinitis (54.7%). Median baseline body mass index was 20.0 kg/m2 (IQR 12.1). This diverse pediatric population represented the typical complexity of children undergoing adenotonsillectomy for obstructive sleep apnea at our tertiary care center.

Prior to surgery, sleep efficiency categorization revealed 42.4% of children with normal efficiency (≥ 85%), 48.2% with mild–moderate impairment (70%–84%), and 9.4% with severe impairment (< 70%). Following adenotonsillectomy, the distribution shifted notably: normal sleep efficiency increased to 48.9%, mild–moderate decreased to 33.1%, while severe sleep efficiency rose to 18.0%. Mean change in sleep efficiency varied dramatically by postoperative category: +5.9% (SD 9.1) in the normal group, −2.9% (SD 11.6) in the mild–moderate group, and −17.3% (SD 22.9) in the severe group (p < 0.001). Despite overall improvements in the proportion achieving normal sleep efficiency, a substantial subset experienced significant deterioration in sleep quality metrics.

Demographic and clinical characteristics showed important associations with sleep efficiency outcomes (Table 1). Baseline sleep architecture differed significantly across preoperative sleep efficiency categories, with NREM duration decreasing progressively from normal to severe groups (348 min vs. 315 min vs. 239.5 min; p < 0.001), as did REM duration (93 min vs. 75 min vs. 41 min; p < 0.001). Down syndrome emerged as a significant predictor of poor outcomes, appearing disproportionately among children with abnormal postoperative sleep efficiency: 18.6% of the normal preoperative group had Down syndrome compared to only 4.5% of the mild–moderate and 7.7% of the severe groups (p = 0.035). Congenital heart disease and prematurity followed similar patterns, suggesting these comorbidities may predispose children to better baseline sleep efficiency but paradoxically worse postoperative outcomes.

Polysomnographic findings demonstrated the expected respiratory improvements following adenotonsillectomy across all groups (Table 2). Median apnea‐hypopnea index declined substantially from 17.3 events/h (IQR 25.8) to 6.0 events/h (IQR 8.8), confirming the procedure's effectiveness in addressing sleep‐disordered breathing. However, the magnitude of respiratory improvement (ΔAHI) did not differ significantly by postoperative sleep efficiency category: normal −10.7 versus mild–moderate −12.2 versus severe −10.3 events/h (p = 0.893). Similarly, oxygen saturation parameters improved uniformly, with SpO2 nadir increasing from 82.0% (IQR 15.0) to 87.0% (IQR 8.0) regardless of sleep efficiency outcomes. These findings suggest that respiratory parameter improvements occur independently of sleep efficiency changes, indicating distinct pathways of adenotonsillectomy benefit.

Absolute NREM and REM duration remained reduced in children with poor postoperative SE (NREM: 228.5 min vs. 347.0 min normal, p < 0.001; REM: 65.0 min vs. 110.0 min normal, p < 0.001); however, post hoc analysis of proportional sleep stage distributions demonstrated preservation of sleep architecture across SE categories. Children with severe postoperative SE showed %NREM of 76.8% (SD 19.5) and %REM of 23.2% (SD 19.5), comparable to those achieving normal efficiency (Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.048). No significant pre‐to‐post change in stage proportions was observed within the mild–moderate (p = 0.90) or severe (p = 0.82) groups, confirming that reduced absolute stage duration reflects reduced total sleep time rather than disproportionate architectural distortion. The normal SE group demonstrated an increase in %REM postoperatively (19.6% to 23.3%, p = 0.0004), consistent with REM rebound following relief of upper airway obstruction. The arousal index declined overall from 100.0 events/h (IQR 83.0) to 74.0 events/h (IQR 43.0), with no significant postoperative differences across SE groups (p = 0.163).

Time‐dependent analysis revealed important recovery patterns that varied by follow‐up duration (n = 139 for temporal analysis). Mean sleep efficiency change differed significantly across time intervals: short‐term follow‐up (< 3 months, n = 64) showed −3.24% change (SD 15.26), medium‐term (3–6 months, n = 47) demonstrated −2.41% change (SD 19.33), while long‐term follow‐up (> 6 months, n = 28) exhibited +6.36% improvement (SD 16.43; F = 3.35, p = 0.038). Children in the long‐term group were more likely to achieve normal sleep efficiency postoperatively, though the distribution across time periods was not significantly different (p = 0.489). These temporal patterns suggest that the full benefits of adenotonsillectomy on sleep efficiency may not manifest until several months after surgery, with early follow‐up potentially underestimating treatment effectiveness.

Postoperative comorbidity associations reinforced the importance of underlying medical conditions in determining sleep efficiency outcomes. Down syndrome was significantly overrepresented among children with persistent sleep efficiency problems: 2.9% of those achieving normal efficiency versus 19.6% of mild–moderate and 16.0% of severe categories (p = 0.013). Congenital heart disease showed similar trends but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.105). Other comorbidities including asthma, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and allergic rhinitis showed no significant associations with sleep efficiency outcomes. The selective impact of specific comorbidities suggests that certain underlying conditions may fundamentally alter sleep physiology in ways that adenotonsillectomy cannot fully address.

Post hoc power analysis showed our N = 139 provided > 95% power (α = 0.05) to detect a 5‐point SE increase (d = 0.3) and > 99% for 10‐point changes (d = 0.6). The observed mean change was 1.7 points (95% CI −1.1 to 4.5). For our secondary outcome of achieving normal SE (≥ 85%), McNemar's test confirmed > 80% power to detect the observed 6.5% point improvement.

Discussion

Adenotonsillectomy improved respiratory parameters in our cohort of 139 children with obstructive sleep apnea. We confirmed the procedure's effectiveness in addressing sleep‐disordered breathing through a substantial median reduction in apnea‐hypopnea index from 17.3 to 6.0 events/h. While we found that more children achieved normal sleep efficiency (42.4% to 48.9%), we also discovered that a paradoxical subset experienced deterioration, with severe sleep efficiency cases rising from 9.4% to 18.0% postoperatively. This disconnect between respiratory parameter improvement and sleep quality metrics demonstrates that adenotonsillectomy's benefits may operate through multiple, potentially independent pathways, with sleep efficiency recovery following a distinct trajectory from traditional polysomnographic markers of surgical success.

Our findings align with prior research demonstrating that adenotonsillectomy improves sleep quality beyond respiratory parameters, though we observed greater variability in outcomes than previously reported. Lee et al. found a modest but significant 1.4% improvement in sleep efficiency postoperatively (95% CI, 0.5%–2.3%) [21], while Chen et al. reported meaningful reductions in arousal indices, particularly in children under age 7 [22]. However, these studies did not report the paradoxical worsening we observed in 18% of patients, suggesting either that our population included more complex cases or that sleep efficiency deterioration represents an underrecognized phenomenon. The divergent outcomes in our cohort—with some children achieving substantial improvements while others experienced significant decline—highlight important gaps in our understanding of which patients benefit most from surgical intervention and underscore the need for more nuanced outcome assessments beyond traditional respiratory metrics [23, 24, 25, 26].

Children with poor postoperative SE demonstrated reduced absolute NREM and REM duration compared to those achieving normal efficiency, reflecting reduced total sleep time rather than disproportionate architectural distortion. This distinction is important: adenotonsillectomy may not fully normalize sleep consolidation in all children, but the residual deficit appears to be one of sleep quantity rather than structural disorganization [27, 28]. The mechanism may involve persistent neurological arousal patterns, sleep–wake regulation abnormalities, or behavioral sleep disturbances that surgical relief of obstruction does not address. Clinically, these findings reinforce that traditional outcome measures focused solely on respiratory parameters may inadequately capture surgical success for children whose primary complaints involve daytime functioning, behavior, or cognition [9, 29, 30].

Down syndrome emerged as a significant predictor of poor sleep efficiency outcomes, with affected children comprising 19.6% of the mild–moderate and 16.0% of the severe categories compared to only 2.9% of those achieving normal sleep efficiency. This finding aligns with prior literature showing limited adenotonsillectomy effectiveness in Down syndrome patients, with studies reporting persistent sleep architecture abnormalities and lower surgical success rates despite respiratory improvements [31, 32]. The anatomical complexity of Down syndrome, including midfacial hypoplasia, macroglossia, and generalized hypotonia [33], likely contributes to persistent sleep fragmentation that surgical intervention cannot fully address. These results suggest that children with Down syndrome and other complex comorbidities may require alternative or adjunctive treatments beyond adenotonsillectomy to achieve optimal sleep efficiency outcomes [34].

Our time‐stratified analysis revealed that sleep efficiency improvements may not manifest until several months postoperatively, with children followed beyond 6 months demonstrating mean improvements of +6.36% compared to deterioration in those assessed earlier. This finding has important clinical implications, suggesting that early postoperative evaluations may underestimate adenotonsillectomy effectiveness and that sleep efficiency assessment should be delayed until adequate recovery time has elapsed [25]. For children who demonstrate persistent sleep efficiency problems despite respiratory improvement, clinicians should consider adjunctive therapies or extended follow‐up rather than concluding that surgical intervention has failed.

Our study strengths include comprehensive polysomnographic data on 139 children with complete follow‐up, novel stratification by sleep efficiency severity, and a diverse population with substantial Hispanic representation and common comorbidities. However, several limitations warrant consideration. The association between adenotonsillectomy and sleep efficiency improvement may be underestimated because most follow‐up occurred within 6 months, while our analysis suggests continued improvement thereafter. Selection bias may have inflated treatment failure rates as postoperative polysomnography typically targets children with suspected persistent symptoms. Laboratory‐based PSG introduces a first‐night effect [35] that may systematically underestimate SE, particularly in the postoperative setting where children return to an unfamiliar environment months after surgery. This effect does not differentially affect SE categories, but may contribute to the absolute rates of severe postoperative SE observed. Persistent low SE in a subset of patients may therefore reflect measurement context rather than a sustained physiological state. Multinight home actigraphy would provide a more ecologically valid estimate of SE and enable stratification by postoperative OAHI category—an important direction for future study. These findings may have limited generalizability beyond tertiary care settings but provide important insights for clinical decision‐making in similar populations.

Conclusion

Adenotonsillectomy improves sleep efficiency in many children with obstructive sleep apnea, but clinicians should anticipate variable outcomes, particularly in patients with comorbidities like Down syndrome. Our findings demonstrate that respiratory improvement does not guarantee sleep quality recovery and that benefits may require months to manifest. These results support individualized postoperative monitoring with extended follow‐up timelines and readiness to pursue alternative interventions for children with persistent sleep efficiency impairment.
