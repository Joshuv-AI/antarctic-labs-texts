EMDR-therapy for child-related PTSD in parents of adolescents with autism spectrum disorder and severe emotional dysregulation: a multiple baseline evaluation
Authors: Anne Stekkinger-de Vries, Elisa van Ee, Rianne Verschuur, Liesbeth Mevissen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Parents of adolescents with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and severe emotional dysregulation (ED) often experience trauma symptoms related to their child's behaviour, including aggression, self-injury and suicide attempts. These traumatic experiences can lead to parental posttraumatic stress symptoms, which are linked to heightened parental stress, reduced capacity for mentalisation, and difficulties co-regulating their child. These challenges have the potential to intensify pre-existing emotional dysregulation, thereby undermining the effectiveness of treatment for the adolescent. Nevertheless, child-related PTSD is rarely addressed in mental health services. This study aims to address this gap in mental health by examining the effects of trauma-focused treatment for these parents.Method: This single-case design study involved seven parents (five mothers and two fathers) of six adolescents (aged 16–21) diagnosed with ASD and emotional dysregulation (ED). Parents received EMDR-therapy and were assessed weekly during a randomised baseline (3–5 weeks), treatment phase (4–6 weeks), post-intervention (3 weeks), and follow-ups at 6 weeks and 3 months.Results: All parents showed a reduction of PTSD-symptoms after EMDR-therapy, with none of the parents meeting PTSD criteria after treatment and follow-up. A significant reduction in parental stress was reported by all parents at post-treatment and follow-up. Furthermore, five out of seven parents reported reduced ED in their adolescents after treatment, an effect maintained in three cases at follow-up.Conclusions: This study demonstrates the value of trauma-focused treatment for parents coping with PTSD as a result of exposure to child-related traumatic events. The findings of this study provide valuable insights into the possible nature of parental stress in parents of adolescents with severe ED, as well as how this can be effectively addressed. The results support the hypothesis that when parents feel emotionally regulated, they may exhibit increased engagement and support for their adolescents’ treatment, potentially leading to improved treatment outcomes.

Introduction

Children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) frequently experience co-occurring emotional and behavioural difficulties (Bougeard et al., 2021). Research shows that among 5- to 16-year-olds with ASD without intellectual disability, 85% show clinically significant levels of hyperactivity, 74% report emotional problems, and 64% exhibit behavioural issues (Weiss, 2014). Adolescents with ASD are also at higher risk of suicide attempts and suicide (Chen et al., 2017; Jokiranta-Olkoniemi et al., 2021; Zahid & Upthegrove, 2017), intentional self-harm (Minshawi et al., 2014), psychotic symptoms (Larson et al., 2018), depression (Matson & Williams, 2014) and post-traumatic stress disorder (Haruvi-Lamdan et al., 2020).

A common thread across these co-occurring issues is emotional dysregulation (ED), often described as the inability or difficulty in controlling or regulating one’s emotional responses (Restoy et al., 2024). Recent studies show that ED is more prevalent in children and adolescents with ASD. Young people with ASD tend to exhibit higher levels of ED, particularly higher levels of emotional reactivity (i.e. experiencing intense and rapidly escalating negative emotion), compared to young people without ASD (Northrup et al., 2021). A review by Dell’Osso et al. (2023) found that young adults with ASD show reduced adaptive behaviour, difficulties identifying and verbalising emotions, elevated levels of anger, rumination, depression, and anxiety compared to young adults without ASD. Moreover, individuals with both ASD and ED are more likely to have a history of psychiatric hospitalisation, use of emergency services and prescription of psychotropic medications (Conner et al., 2021). These findings highlight the central role of ED in the challenges faced by young people with ASD.

These challenges often place a burden on families of adolescents and young adults with ASD. Studies show that families of children with ASD face higher rates of parental stress, family conflict, divorce, substance use, and even domestic violence and abuse (Berg et al., 2016; Enav et al., 2019; Enea & Rusu, 2020; Shawler & Sullivan, 2017). While parental stress appears to be widely acknowledged as a key factor in these family difficulties, most studies lack information on the source and nature of the stress experienced by parents. Understanding the nature of parental stress is crucial, as increased parental stress affects not only the parents themselves, but also the well-being of their child (Yesilkaya & Magallón-Neri, 2024). Addressing the root causes of parental stress may contribute to more effective treatment methods for these families.

Despite the relevance, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) has received limited attention as a potential source of high parental stress in parents of a child with ASD and ED, even though many behaviours associated with ED may meet the definition of an ‘A-criterion traumatic event’. According to the DSM-5-TR (APA, 2025), ‘A-criterion traumatic events’ include exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, witnessing a traumatic event directly, or learned about in close family or friends. Parental experiences such as their childs’ suicide attempts and self-harm, or aggressive and threatening behaviour towards their siblings or parents, can therefore qualify as potential A-criterion traumatic events. To the best of our knowledge, research on this topic is limited to the study by Stewart et al. (2016), in which parents of children with ASD experienced PTSD symptoms including flashbacks, avoidance of places such as hospitals, feeling guilt, sleep disturbances, and hypervigilance. These findings suggest that parents of adolescents with ASD may be at risk of developing PTSD due to their child's severe behavioural and emotional challenges.

PTSD symptoms have been associated with parenting difficulties, such as reduced emotional availability, and more negative perception of the child. This in turn can contribute to further emotional and behavioural problems in their child (Van Ee et al., 2016), potentially leading to a vicious cycle, particularly for families with children with both ASD and ED. In addition, PTSD is associated with impairments in mentalisation: the ability to recognise and understand one's own mental state and that of others (Janssen et al., 2021). Parental mentalisation and reflective functioning has been associated with fewer behavioural and emotional symptoms in children with ASD and higher parental self-efficacy (Enav et al., 2019). Therefore, addressing parental PTSD symptoms may enhance the feasibility and effectiveness of child- and family-based treatment. Scholars have suggested a common theoretical framework by addressing mentalisation and emotion regulation as transdiagnostic mechanisms between PTSD and parenting (Meijer et al., 2023). Given the relationship between parental PTSD and parenting difficulties, treatment of PTSD in parents of adolescents with ASD and ED may reduce parental distress and improve their mentalisation skills and, consequently, support better treatment outcomes for their children. Therefore, the current study aimed to investigate the effect of parental PTSD treatment on PTSD symptoms, parental distress and mentalisation, and adolescent ED. This study used Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing (EMDR)-therapy as it is a leading treatment option for PTSD, alongside with Trauma-Focused Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (TF-CBT), in accordance with international guidelines for evidence-based treatment (NICE, 2018).

The study hypothesised that EMDR-therapy for child-related PTSD would lead to (1) reduced parental trauma symptoms, (2) decreased parental stress, (3) reduced adolescent emotional dysregulation and behavioural problems, and (4) a significant improvement in parental mentalisation skills. This study is particularly relevant because, although parents are commonly involved in adolescent treatment through family therapy or counselling, evidence-based treatment for parental PTSD-symptoms is rarely provided. Furthermore, while child–parent attachment is a focus in early childhood health services, it is not common in adolescent mental health services, despite child–parent attachment remaining highly critical for emotional development during adolescence (Zayde et al., 2023). Therefore, the study's focus on parents as key co-regulators in the emotional lives of adolescents with ASD and ED provides important insights that could enhance treatment effectiveness.

Methods

Setting

The participating adolescents in this study were admitted to inpatient treatment units at a specialised centre for developmental disorders in the Netherlands. All maintained regular contact with their parents, through in-person visits, phone calls, and online communication. Both adolescents and their parents participated in the treatment as usual (TAU). For five adolescents, TAU included psychotherapy and music therapy for the adolescent and support sessions for parents. For one adolescent, TAU consisted of dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT), music therapy, psychomotor therapy, trauma-focused therapy, and DBT-informed training for parents and close caregivers to the admitted patient.

Procedure

Parents of adolescents admitted to the inpatient treatment units of a specialised centre for developmental disorders received an information letter about the study; one version for the parents and another for the adolescent. If both agreed to participate, they were contacted by telephone or by Microsoft Teams. After providing written consent, parents were screened using two brief questionnaires: the PTSD Checklist-5 (PCL-5) and the Strength and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), followed by a standardised interview about the traumatic experiences to determine whether they met PTSD criterion (Clinical-Administered PTSD-Scale for DSM-5; CAPS-5). Adolescents were screened with one questionnaire (SDQ). In this study, ‘adolescent’ is defined as the developmental period between childhood and adulthood. Although early puberty accelerates the onset of adolescence, delayed transitions into adult role such as the completion of education, marriage, and parenthood extend this phase well into the twenties (Sawyer et al., 2018). Parents were included if (1) their child was aged 14–23 and had ASD and ED (SDQ cut-off score 11), (2) the adolescent was admitted to an inpatient treatment facility, (3) at least one parent met PTSD criteria (PCL-5 cut-off score 31) based on child-related experiences, (4) both the adolescent and parent had an estimated IQ above 80 based on education background. All parents agreed to receive EMDR-therapy specifically for child-related experiences. Additionally, they gave consent for the EMDR-therapy sessions to be videotaped for supervision and assessment of the integrity of the therapy. A series of measurements were administered at several phases: baseline (three to five weeks prior to treatment), intervention, post-intervention (three weeks) and follow-up. The use of identical questionnaires during each phase is a fundamental principle of the study. The following instruments were employed in the study: the PTSD Check List (PCL-5), the Strength and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), the Dutch Questionnaire for Distress and Parental Problems (OBVL), and the Parental Reflective Functioning Questionnaire (PRFQ). There were two intervals of follow-up: the first being a short-term follow-up, occurring six weeks after the final EMDR therapy session, and the second being a long-term follow-up, conducted three months after the concluding session. Subsequently, all parents were invited to participate in a short interview. The aim of the interview was to assess their experiences of the treatment in general, potential benefits, the duration of the sessions, the number of sessions attended, and the completed questionnaires. The objective of this interview was to obtain information that may not have been captured through the use of questionnaires. They were invited to provide insights into the impact the treatment had on their daily lives. The responses were used to provide illustrative exemplars drawn from parents’ experiences.

Participants

The study included seven parents and six adolescents – five females and one male – aged 16–21 years (M = 18.9). Prior to admission to the facility all adolescents had been diagnose with ASD according to DSM-5-TR and had at least one comorbid condition such as depression, anxiety disorder, and PTSD (see Table 1). They were receiving treatment in one of three inpatient treatment units of a specialised centre for developmental disorders. The participating parents included two fathers and five mothers (aged 44–52 years, M = 48), comprising one couple and two single parents. All participating parents met the criteria for PTSD according to DSM-5-TR, measured by an interview (CAPS-5). The A-criterion events were directly related to child-specific traumatic experiences (see Table 2). The majority reported exposure to suicide attempts or severe self-inflicted injuries by their child. Most parents had also been exposed to other child-related traumatic experiences (not meeting A criterion), such as emergency calls or involuntary hospitalisations. One parent had a prior diagnosis of ASD and another of ADHD, though neither had received treatment for these conditions. Two parents had previously received trauma-focused treatment before this study. One parent had undergone a single session of EMDR-therapy for a child-related experience two years earlier. EMDR-therapy

Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing (EMDR)-therapy is an evidence-based treatment for PTSD (Shapiro & Maxfield, 2002; World Health Organization, 2013). The intervention phase spanned three to five weeks of EMDR-therapy and included weekly 90-minute EMDR sessions. Before treatment began, all parents received written psychoeducation about PTSD and EMDR-therapy. In the first session, therapists conducted a case conceptualisation based on a timeline of their child's life (see also De Jongh et al., 2010). All major events related to the child that had contributed to the onset and aggravation of the parents’ parental stress were mapped to the timeline. The three most distressing memories were selected as targets. Considering the number of child-related traumatic experiences reported, and the findings of Conijn et al. (2022) that four sessions of EMDR therapy were effective in reducing trauma-related symptoms in parents of children with life-limiting illnesses, it was anticipated that three to five sessions of EMDR-therapy would be sufficient to address these traumatic experiences. Any remaining distressing memories of timeline events were treated after the study, if necessary.

Treatment fidelity/integrity

EMDR-therapy was administered by four registered EMDR Europe therapists. To ensure adherence to the treatment protocol, the therapists were supervised by an EMDR consultant throughout the study. This supervision focused on case conceptualisation and the application of the EMDR standard protocol, using video or audio recordings of therapy sessions. To assess fidelity, a total of 12 videotapes (33%) of EMDR-therapy sessions were randomly selected, stratified by therapist and session number, and independently rated by two registered EMDR Europe practitioners, using an EMDR-specific checklist based on the most recent EMDR protocol. Interobserver agreement was calculated as the average percentage of agreement across the videotapes. The overall mean percentage of agreement was 92.14% (SD = 7.15, range 70–100), indicating good interobserver agreement (Leaf et al., 2017). Treatment adherence appeared to be good (89.15%, SD = 9.18, range 68.75–100).

Instruments

Parental PTSD symptoms

Parental posttraumatic stress symptoms were assessed using the PTSD checklist (PCL-5) (Weathers et al., 2013). The PCL-5 contains 20 questions on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely). PCL-5 scores demonstrate strong internal consistency (α = .94), test–retest reliability (r = .82), and convergent (rs = .74 to .85) and discriminant (rs = .31 to .60) validity (Blevins et al., 2015). A recent validation study confirmed that the PCL-5 is a valid and reliable measure of PTSD symptoms in Dutch adults (Hoeboer et al., 2024). Hoeboer et al. recommended a slightly lower cut-off than those in earlier studies (22 for screening purposes and 29 for estimating prevalence). The PCL-5 also demonstrates excellent diagnostic accuracy.

Parental stress

Parental stress was measured using the Dutch Questionnaire for Distress and Parental Problems (OBVL) (Veerman et al., 2014). This questionnaire contains 34 questions on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (not at all true) to 4 (completely true). Research shows that the OBVL demonstrated high reliability (α = .90) and good validity (Veerman et al., 2014).

Parental reflective functioning and mentalising

The Parental Reflective Functioning Questionnaire (PRFQ) (Fonagy et al., 2016) was used to assess reflective functioning, which reflects parents’ capacity to mentalise. The PRFQ contains 18 questions rated on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (not true) to 7 (true). The PRFQ is a brief, multidimensional measure of PRF or mentalising. Research has shown that the subscales have good internal consistency and are only weakly or moderately correlated with demographic characteristics and symptomatic distress (Luyten et al., 2017). In this study, the Pre-mentalisation scale was used as an indicator of parental pre-mentalisation mode.

Adolescents’ emotional dysregulation and behavioural problems

The Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) – Informant Report was used to assess adolescent emotional dysregulation and behavioural difficulties according to their parents (Goedhart et al., 2003). The SDQ-IR contains 25 questions on a 3-point scale ranging from 1 (not true) to 3 (true). Both the parent and teacher versions of the questionnaire have shown good validity, effectively distinguishing between child psychiatric patients and representative community samples, and identifying different categories of disorders within clinical samples (Becker et al., 2004). The self-report version of the SDQ was used to measure emotional dysregulation and behavioural difficulties in adolescents.

Although the OBVL, SDQ and PRFQ have all been validated for children up to the age of 18, equivalent questionnaires for adolescents aged 18–23 years are not available. Therefore, the same questionnaires were administered to participating adolescents over the age of 18. This decision was based on two considerations. First, in clinical practice, the developmental age of adolescents with ASD is often assumed to be younger than their actual age (Shan et al., 2022). Second, in single case designs, questionnaires are used to examine individual change over time points, reducing the necessity to validate the questionnaires for all ages.

Design

Data were collected using a single case series design (Kazdin, 2016), which is often used to bridge research and clinical practice (Maric, 2020). Given the exploratory nature of the study, this design was selected to provide detailed and in-depth information about the cases and to understand the similarities and differences between the cases (Gustafsson, 2017). In the current study, a non-concurrent multiple baseline single case design was used. Participants were randomly assigned to different baseline durations (three, four or five weeks with weekly measurements). The intervention phase lasted three to five weeks, also with weekly measurements. The post-intervention period was three weeks with one measurement per week. There was a follow-up after six weeks and again after three months, both three weeks with one measurement per week. All questionnaires were used at every time point. Data were analysed both within each phase and between phases.

The present study was subject to review by the research committee of the mental healthcare organisation in which the study was conducted, as well as by the Faculty Ethics Assessment Committee (FETC) (file number 2022-13510).

Data-analysis

The data-analysis consisted of both visual and statistical analysis. Following the guidelines of Lane and Gast (2014), visual analysis began with a systematic analysis of trends and levels within and between subsequent phases for each participant. For the baseline phase, trend was calculated using the split-middle method of trend estimation. Level was analysed between phases (baseline with intervention, post-intervention and follow-up) by comparing median values. Visual analysis was also used to assess clinical improvement based on cut-off scores and to analyse trend lines.

Effect sizes were calculated using Tau-U (Parker et al., 2011) to analyse the effect of the intervention between baseline and post-intervention, and subsequently between baseline and follow-up. Tau-U is used in single-case research to examine the proportion of non-overlapping data between two phases, while also controlling for unwanted positive baseline trends. For one parent (P4), a positive baseline trend was found for PTSD symptoms, parental stress, and parental reflective functioning and mentalising. As a result these data were excluded from further statistical analysis. For participants without positive baseline trend, taunovlap was calculated.

All Tau calculations were conducted using Single Case Research, a web-based tool for analysing single-case data. Tau has a value between −1 and 1, where values of −1 indicate all data points lower than baseline, and 1 indicate all data points higher than baseline, signifying no overlap between phases. In addition, combined effect sizes (all participants) were calculated using SCR. Analyses were two-tailed, with p-value set at .05. Based on the guidelines of Vannest and Ninci (2015), overall effect sizes were interpreted as small (< = 0.20), moderate (0.21–0.60), large (0.61–0.80) or very large ( = >0.80) (Figure 1). Parents’ experiences

All parents expressed satisfaction with the number of sessions. However, two of seven parents considered the 90-minute EMDR treatment to be excessively intensive and would have preferred shorter sessions. All parents were able to provide examples of effects they had observed in their daily lives, such as being able to go to the supermarket or take the dog for a walk without having to concentrate constantly on the phone, being able to sleep without having to take sleeping pills, feeling comfortable hugging their child again without having to worry about their self-harm, being able to leave the adolescent alone at home again, and regaining trust in mental health services.

Discussion

Parents of adolescents diagnosed with ASD and ED may experience traumatic events related to their children, which can lead to PTSD symptoms. Despite the significant impact of these symptoms on parental functioning, the treatment of parental PTSD remains under-represented within child and adolescent mental health settings. This study examined the effectiveness of EMDR-therapy in addressing child-related PTSD-symptoms in parents of adolescents with ASD and severe ED. As hypothesised, posttraumatic stress symptoms, parental stress and parent-reported adolescent's emotional dysregulation decreased.

Not only had parents been exposed to child-related traumatic experiences that met the A-criterion of PTSD according to the DSM-5-TR, such as aggression, severe self-harm and suicide attempts, but they also cited other traumatic events, such as having to call an ambulance and search for a missing child amid fears of the worst possible outcome. This led us to believe that a much broader perspective of child-related experiences that may be traumatic for parents of children with ASD needs to be considered. Parents experience a profound effect on their wellbeing when distressing experiences involve their child. De Vries and Kuiper (2017) also found a relationship between unprocessed – non A-criterion – parenting experiences and parental stress. They showed that the amount of unprocessed parenting experiences predicted the level of parental stress.

The findings of the present study demonstrate that EMDR-therapy for child-related traumatic experiences substantially reduces parental PTSD symptoms and parental stress. This is particularly relevant given the well-documented impact of parental stress on children’s wellbeing and development. Conijn et al. (2022) found similar results in their study of EMDR-therapy for parents with PTSD related to their child’s rare, life-limiting illnesses. They recommended introducing this time-limited form of trauma treatment into routine paediatric care (Conijn et al., 2022). Terrell and Resnick (2023) also identified a comparable outcome that was deemed promising in their research on the effect of EMDR-therapy on parental stress for parents of children with special needs. In light of the present study's findings, further research on this subject is recommended to gather more valuable information on how EMDR-therapy can contribute to better treatment outcomes for adolescents with ASD and ED, as well as their parents.

Contrary to our hypothesis, no effect was found on parental reflective functioning or mentalisation skills. The participating parents appeared capable of reflective functioning and mentalisation before treatment, and their ability did not change significantly after treatment. Research increasingly recognises that parental reflective functioning may not be a stable characteristic, but rather, may be shaped by context. A study by Enav et al. (2020) shows that parents of children (ages 3–18) with ASD exhibit higher levels of reflective functioning when interacting with their child with ASD than with their child without ASD. This suggests that parents of adolescents with ASD have become particularly sensitive to their children's internal emotional states.

In addition to the decrease of parental PTSD symptoms and parental stress, the current study also found a significant decrease in parent-reported emotional dysregulation and behavioural problems in the adolescent. Adolescents’ self-reports turned out the be different in some aspects. Adolescents did not report changes between baseline and post-intervention or follow-up measurements. It is acknowledged that participation in inpatient therapy by adolescents may be a confounding factor in the reduction of parent-reported ED. Another plausible explanation for this could be the ‘sleeper effect’, a well-known phenomenon in psychotherapy with people with ASD, referring to less awareness of their own behaviour and limited capacities to notice behavioural changes immediately (Leuning et al., 2023). Also, another explanation is that parents after EMDR-therapy may perceive their child's behaviour and emotions as less severe. Although the reflective functioning questionnaires did not show a significant effect, one could imagine that being more regulated and a more positive view on the child as a result of reduced PTSD symptoms and parental stress, parents would be better able to co-regulate their child and provide parental support during treatment. This is consistent with the findings of a study (Mevissen et al., 2020) that examined the effectiveness of an intensive, inpatient, trauma-focused programme for families with mild intellectual disabilities. In this programme, parents received EMDR-therapy, followed by EMDR-therapy for their child(ren). Five of the ten participating children were diagnosed with comorbid ASD. Most children had already lost their PTSD diagnosis by the time they started their own EMDR-therapy, likely due to their parent(s)’ improved functioning. It is likely that trauma-focused therapy for parents will not only improve their well-being, but also positively impact their child with ASD.

Although a methodological strength of the current study is the use of a multiple baseline design and the correction for positive baseline trends, there are also some limitations that need to be addressed. First, parents received other interventions in addition to the EMDR-therapy, such as parental counselling or family therapy. However, confounding on non-specific treatment effects is not likely because these interventions started prior to the baseline and did not change during the study. Second, since the questionnaires had only been validated for individuals up to 18 years old, they were not suitable for all adolescents and parents. Third, the PCL-5 was used, but this instrument is intended for screening purposes rather than diagnostic purposes. Fourth, the adolescents were hospitalised and only went home for some weekends. Although they had frequent contact with their parents, they weren't home for all daily parent–child interactions. Finally, the current study has a limited sample size, which is a limitation. Despite this, single-case design studies can control threats to internal and external validity by using repeated measures, sequentially introducing the intervention, and replicating intervention effects across different participants (Kratochwill & Levin, 2025). These studies provide detailed information about specific cases that can be used in future studies with larger sample sizes.

The interviews with participating parents, which were used to evaluate treatment, provided valuable information about changes in quality of life experienced during the long-term follow-up period. Parents gave examples of these changes, such as being able to go to the supermarket or take the dog for a walk without constantly worrying about their phone; being able to sleep without taking sleeping pills; feeling comfortable hugging their child again without worrying about self-harm; being able to leave their teenager home alone again; and trusting mental health services again (see Results). These important examples, together with the data, underscore the importance of introducing time-limited, trauma-focused therapy for parents of children and adolescents with severe emotional dysregulation. The present study provides a valuable foundation for future systematic research on this subject, with numerous aspects yet to be explored. These include trauma-focused treatment for parents experiencing stressful child-related life events, as well as parents of adolescents with ED without ASD, and parents of adolescents’ in out-patient facilities. It is hypothesised that starting treatment for parents prior to that for adolescents themselves may be a viable option because it may increase the capacity of parents to provide support to their adolescents during treatment. Furthermore, this strategy may help to disentangle confounding factors in research. Additionally, more research is needed on parent-focused approaches to reduce PTSD symptoms and enhance quality of life. In conclusion, this study provides substantial evidence supporting the development of family-focused mental healthcare models that prioritise the well-being of not only the identified patient, but also their family members and their narratives. It is imperative that clinicians and researchers are aware of the experiences of adolescents themselves, their parents, and their siblings, and how these experiences impact mental health, parenting, and family functioning. By offering appropriate support, clinicians and researchers can facilitate positive outcomes for all family members.
