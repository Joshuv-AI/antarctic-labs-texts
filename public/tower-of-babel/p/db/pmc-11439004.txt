Platelet count has a nonlinear association with 30-day in-hospital mortality in ICU end-stage kidney disease patients: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Pan Zhou, Jian-hui Xiao, Yun Li, Li Zhou, Zhe Deng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study addresses the relationship between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality in End-Stage Kidney Disease (ESRD) patients in the intensive care unit (ICU), a topic with limited existing evidence. Utilizing data from the US eICU-CRD v2.0 database (2014–2015), a retrospective cohort study was conducted involving 3700 ICU ESRD patients. We employed binary logistic regression, smooth curve fitting, and subgroup analyses to explore the association between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality. The 30-day in-hospital mortality rate was 13.27% (491/3700), with a median platelet count of 188 × 109/L. After adjusting for covariates, we observed a relationship between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality (OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.97, 0.99). Subgroup analyses supported these findings. More importantly, a nonlinear association was detected, with an inflection point at 222 × 109/L. The effect sizes (OR) on the left and right sides of the inflection point were 0.94 (0.92, 0.96) and 1.03 (1.00, 1.05), respectively. The most significant finding of this study is the revelation of a nonlinear relationship between baseline platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality in ICU patients with ESRD. This discovery explicitly suggests that when ESRD patients are admitted to the ICU, a platelet level closer to 222 × 10⁹/L may predict a lower 30-day in-hospital mortality risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73717-w.Keywords: Platelet count, ICU ESRD patients, 30-day in-hospital mortality, Nonlinear relationship, Multicenter studySubject terms: Diseases, Nephrology

Introduction

End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) is a costly and disabling condition marked by high mortality and reduced quality of life1. ESRD patients primarily rely on kidney replacement therapy or transplantation. In 2010, approximately 2.6 million people worldwide received treatment for ESKD, with one to three times more possibly succumbing due to the unavailability of renal replacement therapy2,3. Alarmingly, the number of ESRD patients continues to rise4, posing challenges that many countries struggle to meet5.

Although previous studies have conclusively identified aging, diabetes, hypertension, and glomerulonephritis as the leading causes of ESKD4–8, these underlying conditions are prevalent and intertwined in ICU-hospitalized ESKD patients. Consequently, some studies have begun to focus on the relationship between laboratory biomarkers and the prognosis of patients with chronic kidney disease9,10.

Platelets, produced by 2–4 μm megakaryocytes, circulate for 7–10 days, primarily responsible for hemostasis and coagulation11. Recent studies have revealed that platelets are not only with thrombosis but also with various physiological and pathological processes. Studies link platelet count and mean platelet volume to tumor metastasis12,13. Moreover, thrombocytopenia occurred in 43% of critically ill patients, correlating with worse outcomes, including increased mortality14. A retrospective analysis of 2056 ICU patients associates thrombocytopenia with acute respiratory distress syndrome and death15. In a prospective study of 84 critically ill patients, elevated platelet volume after ICU admission independently associates with higher in-hospital mortality16. However, the association between platelet count and short-term prognosis in ESRD patients lacks epidemiological evidence. Given the unique physiological basis of critically ill patients, we hypothesize that baseline platelet count before treatment may be associated with the short-term prognosis of ESRD. High-quality multicenter data elucidating this association can provide perspectives for future research.

Therefore, we conducted an analysis using the eICU-CRD v2.0 to explore the association between baseline platelet count on the 30-day in-hospital mortality rate of ICU-hospitalized ESRD patients.

Methods

Study design

This study is a multicenter retrospective cohort, focusing on platelet count as the independent variable and 30-day in-hospital death in ESRD patients as the dependent variable (dichotomized into death or survival).

Data source

The eICU Collaborative Research Database is a freely accessible multi-center resource designed for critical care research (Pollard TJ, Johnson AEW, Raffa JD, Celi LA, Mark RG, & Badawi O, Scientific Data, 2018, DOI: 10.1038/sdata.2018.178, Available at: https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata2018178). This database contains high-quality data from about 200,859 patients admitted to ICUs across 208 U.S. hospitals during 2014 and 2015. It encompasses essential clinical information, including vital signs, nursing plans, disease severity, diagnosis, and treatment details17. Access to the data is granted to the public upon registration, adhering to collection standards set by the MIT Ethics Committee (No: 0403000206) and aligning with the principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

This study analyzed 3700 eligible individuals, identified through specific exclusion criteria: (1) non-ESRD patients: n = 195,829 excluded; (2) Age < 18 years: n = 0 excluded; (3) ICU stay time < 24 h or > 30 days: n = 1202 excluded; (4) Missing in-hospital mortality data: n = 36 excluded; (5) Missing platelet count: n = 49 excluded; (6) Extreme platelet count values (three standard deviations above or below the mean): n = 43 excluded. Following the application of these criteria, 3700 participants remained for analysis. Figure 1 illustrates the participant selection process.

Flowchart of study participants.

Variables

The platelet count was initially recorded as a continuous variable. Subsequently, it was categorized based on quartiles: Q1 (9.0 × 109–137.0 × 109/L), Q2 (138.0 × 109–187.0 × 109/L), Q3 (188.0 × 109–248.0 × 109/L), and Q4 (249.0 × 109–503.0 × 109/L). Additionally, based on that platelet counts are considered to be between 150 × 109/L and 400 × 109/L11,18, we defined the following standards for the three groups: the lower than normal(< 150 × 109/L), the normal (150 × 109/L to 400 × 109/L), and the more than normal group (≥ 400 × 109/L).

Covariates

The covariates were chosen based on clinical experience and prior studies19–22. Included variables comprised: (1) categorical variables—sex, ethnicity, acute respiratory failure (ARF), atrial fibrillation (AF), acute coronary syndrome (ACS), congestive heart failure (CHF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes mellitus, gastrointestinal bleeding (GB), hypertension, sepsis, use of anti-platelet medications, anticoagulants, glucocorticoids, carbapenems, cephalosporins, vancomycin, and mechanical ventilation; (2) continuous variables—age, hemoglobin concentration (Hb), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (SCr), albumin (ALB), and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation-IV score (APACHE-IV score). Baseline parameters, including platelet count, were collected within 24 h of ICU admission.

Missing data processing

In this study, Hb, BUN, SCr, ALB, and APACHE-IV score data were missing for 0.03%, 1.08%, 1.08%, 10.22% and 12.54% of participants, respectively. To address missing covariate data, multiple imputations were employed23,24. The imputation model included ethnicity, age, Hb, ARF, AF, ACS, CHF, COPD, diabetes mellitus, GB, hypertension, sepsis, use of anti-platelet medications, anticoagulants, glucocorticoids, carbapenems, cephalosporins, vancomycin, and mechanical ventilation. We assumed that the missing data followed the missing-at-random assumption (MAR)25. This approach ensures a comprehensive analysis of participant characteristics while accounting for missing values in the dataset.

Statistical analysis

Patients were grouped into four or three categories according to their platelet counts. We presented continuous variables as mean (standard deviation) for normally distributed data and median (range) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (%). To compare platelet count groups, we utilized one-way analysis of variance for normal distribution, the χ2 method for categorical variables, or the Kruskal–Wallis H test for skewed distribution. The clinical significance of a 1 × 109/L increase or decrease in platelet count is considered limited. Therefore, dividing platelet count by 10 in multivariate regression analysis and curve analysis is more clinically meaningful.

To analyze the independent linear relationship of the platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality

After screening for collinearity (Table S1 shows no excluded covariates), we employed univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression to investigate the association between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality, following the STROBE statement26. Three models were established: (1) crude model (no covariate adjustments); (2) minimally-adjusted model (model I: adjusted for sex, age, and ethnicity); (3) fully-adjusted model (model II: adjusted for sex, ethnicity, age, Hb, BUN, SCr, ALB, APACHE-IV score, ARF, AF, ACS, CHF, COPD, diabetes mellitus, GB, hypertension, sepsis, anti-platelet, anticoagulant, glucocorticoid, carbapenems, cephalosporins, vancomycin, and mechanical ventilation). Recorded effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Covariate adjustments were applied if they resulted in a 10% or more change in the odds ratio (OR)26. Platelet count was categorized by quartile, and we calculated P for trend to assess continuous variable results and explore non-linearity. Additionally, we investigated potential unmeasured confounding between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality using E-values.

Interaction and subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were performed using a stratified binary logistic regression model across various categories, including sex, ethnicity, and medical conditions such as ARF, AF, ACS, CHF, COPD, diabetes mellitus, GB, hypertension, sepsis, and the use of mechanical ventilation. In each stratum, adjustments were made for all relevant factors, such as sex, ethnicity, age, Hb levels, BUN, SCr, ALB, APACHE-IV score, ARF, AF, ACS, CHF, COPD, diabetes mellitus, GB, hypertension, sepsis, as well as medications including anti-platelet drugs, anticoagulants, glucocorticoids, carbapenems, cephalosporins, and vancomycin. Furthermore, interaction tests were conducted using the likelihood ratio test, comparing models with and without interaction terms27,28. This comprehensive analysis provides valuable insights into the relationships between the specified subgroups and the studied outcomes.

To analyze the nonlinear relationship of the platelet and 30-day in-hospital mortality

Binary logistic regression models may have limitations in addressing nonlinearity. To overcome this, we employed generalized additive models (GAM) with a smooth curve fitting technique (penalized spline method) to explore the nonlinear relationship between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality. To handle nonlinearity, we first identified the inflection point using a recursive algorithm. We then established a two-piece binary logistic regression model on each side of this point29. The log-likelihood ratio test was subsequently used to determine the most suitable model describing the association between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality.

All results adhere to the STROBE statement26. Analyses were conducted using statistical software packages R (R Foundation)2 and EmpowerStats3 (X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA). Statistically significant findings were determined at a threshold of P < 0.05 (two-sided).

30-day in-hospital mortality results

Table 2; Fig. 3 specifically highlight the outcome measure: the differences in 30-day in-hospital mortality rates among Q1 to Q4. Table 2 summarizes data from 3700 participants, noting 491 mortality events, resulting in an overall mortality incidence of 13.27% (95% CI 12.18–14.36%). In Q1, 164 mortality events occurred among 919 participants, yielding a mortality incidence of 17.85% (95% CI 15.37–20.33%). For Q2 (930 participants), there were 112 mortality events, with an incidence of 12.04% (95% CI 9.95–14.14%). Q3, involving 926 participants, observed 96 mortality events, resulting in a mortality incidence of 10.37% (95% CI 8.40–12.33%). Q4, comprising 925 participants in the highest platelet count quartile, noted 119 mortality events, with an incidence of 12.86% (95% CI 10.07–15.03%). A significant trend in 30-day in-hospital mortality across platelet count quartiles was identified (P for trend = 0.0019). Notably, as platelet count increased from Q1 to Q3, a discernible trend of decreasing mortality rates emerged (Fig. 3).

Multivariate analyses results using the binary logistic regression model

Table 3 investigates the correlation between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality using crude, adjusted, and fully adjusted logistic regression models. The crude model showed that a 10 × 109/L increase in platelet count was associated with a 2% lower risk of mortality (OR: 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–0.99, p = 0.0005). In the minimally adjusted model (Model I), adjusting only for demographic variables (age, sex, and ethnicity), a 10 × 109/L rise in platelet count resulted in a 2% reduction in mortality risk (OR: 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–0.99, p = 0.0011). The fully adjusted model (Model II) also indicated a 2% decrease in mortality for a 10 × 109/L increase in platelet count (OR: 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–0.99, p = 0.0007). Importantly, in all three models, the mortality risk in Q2, Q3, and Q4 was lower than that in Q1, and the dose-response relationship remained significant (all P for trend < 0.05). Additionally, we assessed sensitivity to unmeasured confounding using an E-value, which was determined to be 1.16. This value, surpassing the relative risk of unmeasured confounders and platelet count, indicates that unknown or unmeasured confounders minimally impacted the relationship between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality.

Our aim was to assess the consistency of the relationship between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality across different subgroups. The results of interaction and subgroup analyses are presented in Table 4, examining the link between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality. We found significant interactions between diabetes mellitus and mortality (P for interaction = 0.0002), indicating that the effect of diabetes on mortality varied depending on the presence or absence of this comorbidity. Specifically, a stronger association between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality was observed in non-diabetic patients compared to diabetic patients.

Addressing nonlinearity with generalized additive models (GAM)

In this study, we utilized GAM to examine non-linearities in the connection between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality (see Fig. 4). After adjusting for various factors, including sex, ethnicity, age, Hb, BUN, SCr, APACHE-IV score, AF, ACS, CHF, COPD, diabetes mellitus, GB, hypertension, sepsis, anti-platelet, anticoagulant, glucocorticoid, carbapenems, cephalosporins, vancomycin, and mechanical ventilation, a significant non-linear association surfaced (Log-likelihood ratio test P < 0.0001). To delve deeper into this relationship, we employed a two-piecewise linear regression model (refer to Table 5). The model identified a critical point at 222 × 109/L platelets. Below this threshold, a 10 × 109/L decrease in platelet count was significantly linked to 6% higher odds of mortality (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.92–0.96, P < 0.0001). Conversely, above 222 × 109/L, a 10 × 109/L increase in platelet count was marginally associated with 3% higher odds of mortality (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.00-1.05, P = 0.0288). The log-likelihood ratio test comparing the piecewise model to a standard linear model was highly significant (P < 0.001), signifying the superior fit of the piecewise model to the data. These findings imply a non-linear relationship between platelet count and mortality, with lower platelet counts strongly predicting higher mortality risk, while higher platelet counts above 222 × 109/L only weakly predict elevated risk.

Discussion

This study, utilizing multicenter eICU-CRDv2.0 data, investigates the correlation between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality in ICU patients with ESRD. Although the results reveal a linear relationship between platelet count and the risk of in-hospital mortality within 30 days, employing a nonlinear model to depict the relationship between these two variables can achieve a more precise and appropriate fit. The threshold effect curve reveals distinct correlations between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality on either side of the inflection point. These findings provide valuable insights into the nuanced relationship between platelet count and mortality risk in ICU patients with ESRD.

The link between platelet count and adverse outcomes in critically ill patients is gaining attention. Thachil et al. highlighted low platelet count as a common hematological issue and a risk factor for poor prognosis in ICU-treated patients30,31. In a cohort study of 215,098 ICU patients, Jonsson AB et al. found thrombocytopenia associated with increased mortality32. In a retrospective study involving 167 COVID-19 ICU patients, Zhu Y et al. revealed that thrombocytopenia not only correlated with respiratory function deterioration but also associated with mortality in severe COVID-19 cases33. These studies emphasize the correlation between platelet count and the prognosis of critically ill ICU patients. Additionally, Alyaa Abdelmaguid et al. reported platelet dysfunction in chronic kidney disease patients34, highlighting endothelial dysfunction as a characteristic of vascular disease in ESRD patients35. This further suggests the correlation between thrombocytopenia and the prognosis of ESRD patients. Despite this wealth of information, a noticeable gap exists in the literature regarding the relationship between baseline platelet count and the 30-day risk of in-hospital mortality in ICU ESRD patients, particularly the saturation effect relationship. Prompted by these observations, we aimed to explore the correlation between baseline platelet levels and the 30-day in-hospital mortality risk in ICU ESRD patients.

This study not only found a linear correlation between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality rates among ICU patients with ESRD, but more importantly, also revealed a more subtle non-linear relationship between the two. Initially, we examined the linear relationship between baseline platelet count and the 30-day in-hospital death risk in ICU ESRD patients using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis. The results show that there is a correlation between higher platelet counts and lower 30-day in-hospital mortality rates. However, it is noteworthy that we have observed an intriguing phenomenon: the mortality rate in Q4 presented in Table 2 is unexpectedly higher than that in Q3; moreover, the OR in Q4 shown in Table 3 is significantly higher than the OR in Q3. This finding suggests that there may be a more complex, nonlinear relationship between the platelet count and the 30-day in-hospital mortality rate that underlies this association. Furthermore, Fig. 4; Table 5 demonstrate the non-linear relationship between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality rate, as revealed by GAM and smoothing curve fitting analysis. This non-linear relationship exhibits a saturation effect, indicating that an increase in platelet count is associated with a decrease in 30-day in-hospital mortality rate when the platelet count is below 222 × 109/L. However, when the platelet count exceeds 222 × 109/L, the situation reverses, and further increases in platelet count are associated with an increase in 30-day in-hospital mortality rate. Therefore, from a clinical perspective, monitoring platelet count in ESRD patients can provide important risk stratification information for clinicians.

Building upon prior research, we speculate on the underlying mechanisms of the correlation between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality rate in ESRD patients in the ICU. Platelets play a crucial role in hemostasis and coagulation11, and thrombocytopenia escalates the risk of primary or secondary hemorrhage in vital organs, reducing overall survival rates. This risk is particularly pronounced in ICU settings where severe organ hemorrhage can be fatal. Furthermore, thrombocytopenia serves as an indirect marker of increased illness severity30,36–39. Patients with lower platelet counts often require intensified medical interventions. As shown in Table 1 of this study, the Q1 group had higher APACHE-IV scores, indicating that patients with low platelet counts are prone to prolonged ICU hospitalization and increased mortality. Additionally, platelets act as the first responders in innate immunity, interacting with pathogens such as bacteria and viruses through various surface receptors40–42. Operating as immune cells, platelets engage with other immune entities like neutrophils, monocytes, dendritic cells, and lymphocytes43. Thrombocytopenic individuals lose their ability to deposit fibrinogen, guide neutrophil migration, and block damaged vascular systems after diapause44. These factors suggest that patients with lower baseline platelet counts may experience compromised immunity, implying that these patients are at a higher risk of mortality. The aforementioned reasons likely contribute to the observed negative correlation between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality on the left side of this study’s inflection point. Conversely, on the right side of the inflection point, the correlation between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality rate reverses to a positive one, which may be attributed to the enhanced impact of other confounding factors in patients.

Our study has several strengths that significantly contribute to the depth and reliability of our findings. The multicenter design and large sample size enhance the generalizability of our results. Our research is unique as it focuses on ICU ESRD patients, a population not extensively studied in this context before. Exploring non-linearity adds nuance to our understanding of the platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality relationship, surpassing previous studies. Using multiple imputations to handle missing data mitigates potential bias, maximizing statistical power. We strengthen our findings through meticulous subgroup analysis and calculating E-values to explore potential unmeasured confounding. Practically, our study’s implications are heightened by the cost-effectiveness and accessibility of platelet count as a general laboratory measure, highlighting its applicability in various clinical scenarios.

This study, however, has limitations. Firstly, we exclusively focused on Americans, so further validation across diverse geographic and ethnic groups is essential for generalizability. Secondly, our observational study design prevents the establishment of definitive causality. Third, the lack of data on ESRD etiology classification in our database hinders the detailed exploration of the relationship between platelet count and specific ESRD subtypes, even though diabetes is the primary cause of ESRD. Notably, our study included only ICU-hospitalized ESRD patients, and verification is necessary for those not admitted to the ICU. These considerations emphasize the importance of incorporating more detailed variables in future study designs.

In summary, this study has profoundly revealed a complex nonlinear association pattern between platelet count and 30-day in-hospital mortality rate in patients with ESRD in the ICU. Notably, the research has identified a significant threshold effect: when the platelet count is ≤ 222 × 10⁹/L, a pronounced negative correlation between the two is observed. However, once the platelet count exceeds 222 × 10⁹/L, this correlation trend reverses, becoming positive. These distinctive findings provide new references for clinical assessment of in-hospital mortality risk in ICU patients with ESRD.
