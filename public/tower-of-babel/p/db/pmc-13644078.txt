Cough burden in progressive pulmonary fibrosis: a systematic review with narrative synthesis
Authors: Ningxia Yu, Shuguang Yang, Zhihui Liang, Haiyang Hu, Yi Zhang, Luguang Li, Xueqing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCough is a common and burdensome symptom in patients with progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF), yet existing evidence remains fragmented. This systematic review provides a synthesis of available evidence on cough in PPF, specifically examining its association with health-related quality of life (HRQoL), the instruments used to assess cough, current treatment evidence, and identifies priorities for future research.MethodsWe searched the Embase, PubMed, Cochrane, and Web of Science (WOS) databases from their inception to January 12, 2026, for studies focusing on the burden of cough in patients with PPF. We extracted information on study details, participant features, and outcome measures, and assessed the risk of bias in the included studies. A narrative synthesis was then performed to summarize the evidence.ResultsOf 11,683 records, 9 studies met inclusion criteria. Cough was most frequently assessed using VAS and LCQ, while HRQoL was primarily measured with SGRQ and L-PF. Two studies highlighted wearable devices for objective cough monitoring. Antifibrotic therapy showed no consistent or statistically significant benefit on cough in descriptive synthesis. Qualitative studies revealed substantial cough‑related burden, including daily activity limitations, fatigue, and social embarrassment. Regarding economic impact, although no dedicated studies exist, one study indicated that cough severity was associated with workplace productivity loss.ConclusionsCough negatively impacts HRQoL in PPF, but evidence is limited by heterogeneous tools and lack of economic studies. Standardized assessment and targeted research on cough management and its economic impact are urgently needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12955-026-02585-x.Keywords: Progressive pulmonary fibrosis, Interstitial lung disease, Cough, Burden, Health-related quality of life

Introduction

The 2022 ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT guidelines introduced the term progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF), replacing the broader concept of progressive fibrosing interstitial lung disease (PF-ILD). As a subset of PF-ILD, PPF is defined as a collective term for patients with interstitial lung disease (ILD) of known or unknown etiology, excluding idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), who present with radiological evidence of pulmonary fibrosis and have experienced worsening of respiratory symptoms, radiological progression, or physiological decline within the past year, meeting at least two of these three criteria [1–3].

PPF carries a poor prognosis, with a high incidence, heavy disease burden, and dismal five-year survival rate (< 50%) [4–6]. PF-ILD, the broader category, affects nearly 30% of all ILD patients, representing a major global public health issue given the global ILD incidence of 1-31.5/10⁵ person-years and prevalence of 6.3–71/10⁵ [7, 8]. Despite recent advances in antifibrotic therapies, the symptomatic burden on patients remains substantial [9].

PPF is a common subtype of ILD, and cough is one of its most frequent symptoms, often serving as the initial manifestation of the disease [10, 11]. Cough in patients with ILD tends to persist and progressively worsens with disease progression [12–14]. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying cough are complex, potentially involving multiple factors such as increased cough reflex sensitivity, distortion of airway structures, enhanced mucus secretion, heightened neuronal sensitivity, and comorbidities [15–17]. It has been suggested that cough may drive disease progression through a pro-fibrotic positive feedback loop [18]. Although some studies have indicated that cough may be an independent predictor of prognosis in ILD, the conclusions remain inconsistent [19].

Although advances have been made in the treatment of ILD [20], cough remains a major problem for patients due to the lack of specific therapeutic approaches [9] and is ranked as one of the most severe symptoms in patients with ILD [21]. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the breadth and severity of the burden of cough in this population is essential for optimizing future treatment strategies and care models.

Despite this, the widespread and multifaceted impact of cough has been preliminarily explored in different populations [22–25], no systematic review has specifically focused on its effects in patients with PPF. Although cough is a common symptom across various chronic respiratory diseases, the burden of cough in PPF may be distinct from that in conditions such as IPF or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Unlike IPF, where the fibrotic process is restricted to a single diagnostic entity, PPF encompasses a heterogeneous group of progressive fibrosing ILDs arising from diverse underlying etiologies (e.g., hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), connective tissue disease-associated ILD (CTD-ILD), non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP)). This heterogeneity may result in variable cough mechanisms, symptom profiles, and treatment responses. Furthermore, the available evidence on cough in PPF remains fragmented, with no prior systematic synthesis specifically addressing this population.

Therefore, this review provides the first systematic synthesis of evidence on the humanistic burden of cough in patients with PPF. The objectives are as follows: (1) to summarize the available evidence on cough and its association with health-related quality of life (HRQoL); (2) to identify the cough assessment instruments reported in PPF studies; (3) to evaluate the evidence for current treatment approaches (where reported); and (4) to identify evidence gaps and prioritize future research directions. Economic burden, when reported in included studies, is described as an exploratory outcome. This review does not aim to provide clinical practice recommendations but rather to synthesize existing evidence and highlight areas requiring further investigation.

Materials and methods

A systematic literature review was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses [26]. All steps of this systematic review were performed according to protocol and prospectively registered in the International Prospective Registry of Systematic Reviews database under the number CRD420261280939. We employed the PRISMA checklist to standardize the reporting of this overview, and the specific page numbers where each checklist item was addressed were recorded. The completed PRISMA checklist is provided as a supplementary material.

Search strategy

Two members of our group (Ningxia Yu and Zhihui Liang) systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science (WOS) from database inception to January 12, 2026. The search strategy is detailed in Supplemental Tables 1 and included the following subject terms: “progressive pulmonary fibrosis,” “PPF,” “cough,” and “burden.”If the abstract of an article suggested potential relevance, the full text was retrieved. The same two reviewers then independently assessed the full-text articles against predefined eligibility criteria. Based on this assessment, they determined whether each article should be included in the descriptive review or the meta-analysis of effectiveness outcomes.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) Studies in patients with ILD other than IPF; (2)Original studies refer to those that employed any of the following study designs: randomized controlled trials (RCTs), observational studies, or cohort studies; (3)Studies reporting relevant cough-related outcomes: a: the humanistic burden of cough—patient experience reported through HRQoL instruments or qualitative assessments (interview series); b: The economic burden of cough—healthcare resource utilization, direct and indirect costs, and productivity loss.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Letters, reviews, commentaries, abstracts, and editorials were excluded; (2) secondary publications from the same study group were not automatically excluded; however, studies derived from the same patient cohort using the same outcome measurement tool were assessed for sample overlap—only the study with the most complete data was retained; (3) studies for which full text was not available; (4) studies focusing on animals only.

Definitions and inclusion rationale

The term PPF is relatively new, defined by the 2022 ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT guidelines [1]. The literature specifically focusing on cough burden in clearly defined PPF populations remains nascent and limited. In our search, only six studies met strict PPF criteria. Therefore, to capture the available evidence and avoid omitting relevant data, we made a pragmatic decision to include studies on broader progressive fibrosing ILD phenotypes that are clinically comparable to PPF. A formal sensitivity analysis excluding non-PPF studies was not feasible due to the small number of strictly defined PPF studies (n = 6).

Data extraction

The two members of our group (Haiyang Hu and Yi Zhang) carried out data extraction independently. The discrepancies were resolved by discussion with a third author. The data of the selected articles, including name of the first author, country or area, year of publication, sample size, study design, duration of follow-up, treatment measures, cough-related outcomes (such as Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ), St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) etc.), and quality assessment of the included studies. A narrative synthesis approach was used to summarise and interpret the findings from the included studies. This method involved the use of text and tables to provide a thorough overview of the data and identify key trends.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers (Ningxia Yu and Zhihui Liang) independently assessed the quality of included studies using appropriate tools based on study design: the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [27] for cohort and case-control studies (score 0–9; ≥7 = low risk of bias), the Cochrane RoB 2 tool [28] for RCTs (five domains rated as low/some/high risk), the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist [29] for Analytical Cross‑Sectional Studies (eight items rated yes/no/unclear/not applicable), and the CASP Qualitative Checklist [30] (10 items rated yes/no/unclear). Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by discussion. Following established guidance, we did not calculate total quality scores or exclude studies based solely on quality. The appraisal results were used to inform the narrative synthesis.

Data synthesis and analysis

Due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included studies (e.g., differences in study designs, patient populations, outcome measures, and the use of non-validated instruments such as the LCQ in PPF), a quantitative meta-analysis was not considered appropriate or feasible. Instead, we adopted a narrative synthesis approach. Findings are summarised descriptively, organised by outcome domains (e.g., cough severity, quality of life, cough impact), and presented in tables and figures. Where appropriate, results are discussed in relation to study characteristics and limitations. This approach follows established guidance for systematic reviews where statistical pooling is precluded by heterogeneity [31].

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was the humanistic burden of cough, defined as patient experience reported through HRQoL instruments or qualitative assessments (e.g., interview series).

A secondary, exploratory outcome was the economic burden of cough, defined as healthcare resource utilization, direct and indirect costs, and productivity loss, where reported in the included studies.

Results

The literature search retrieved a total of 11,683 records. After removing 2,366 duplicate entries, 9,317 unique articles remained for preliminary screening based on titles and abstracts. A total of 9,270 irrelevant records were excluded, leaving 47 articles eligible for full-text evaluation. In the full-text assessment stage, 14 study protocols lacking valid outcome data and 22 studies without available cough-related indicators were further excluded. Three publications [32–34] were confirmed to originate from the identical INBUILD trial cohort and adopted the same L-PF cough assessment scale. To eliminate overlapping sample data, only the study [34] with comprehensive cough outcome information was retained. Following successive screening and exclusion procedures, 9 studies [34–42] fully satisfied the established inclusion criteria and were ultimately incorporated into this systematic review (Fig. 1). The kappa statistic for screening agreement was 0.96 with a 99.9% percent agreement.

Among the four cohort studies, based on the NOS assessment, one was rated as having a low risk of bias and three as having a moderate risk of bias. Although treatment efficacy was not the primary focus of this review, we still discussed and analyzed the impact of treatments on cough outcomes. The specific therapeutic agents primarily included antifibrotic drugs (pirfenidone and nintedanib), corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, among others.

Study characteristics and measures

Among the 9 studies in ILD populations, there were two RCT studies [34, 41], two cross-sectional studies [36, 38], two retrospective studies [35, 39], one prospective study [37, 42], and one qualitative study [40]. Multiple assessment instruments were adopted to evaluate cough outcomes across the nine studies. The VAS for cough severity was the most commonly applied tool, used in two studies [36, 41]. One study [36] including 248 patients reported mean baseline VAS scores ranging from 23 mm to 32 mm across different ILD subgroups. The RD-PPF subgroup had the largest sample size (n = 136) and the narrowest confidence interval, reflecting relatively reliable estimates. The second VAS-related study [41] enrolled 253 participants. Over 24 weeks of follow-up, the mean change in VAS score from baseline to week 24 was 0.78 mm in the placebo group and − 2.52 mm in the pirfenidone group, with no significant intergroup difference (Fig. 3).

Most studies also adopted validated questionnaires to assess cough-associated health status. For HRQoL evaluation, the St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) was used in two studies [39, 41]. One study [39] with 396 patients reported an average SGRQ score of 29.6. The other study [41] recorded a minor mean score change of 0.05 from baseline to week 24.

The LCQ was used in three studies [36, 41, 42]. Baseline LCQ scores ranged from 14.9 to 16.6 across distinct ILD subtypes, with the RD-PPF subgroup showing the most stable estimates [36]. One RCT [41] compared pirfenidone with placebo in patients with PPF. After 24 weeks, the mean change in LCQ score from baseline to week 24 was 0.36 (95% CI: −0.22 to 0.94) in the pirfenidone group and 0.04 (95% CI: −0.65 to 0.73) in the placebo group, with no statistically significant difference between groups (Fig. 4). Another small-scale study [42] with eight participants suggested that wearable device-based long-term cough monitoring could facilitate disease progression assessment in PPF.

Two additional questionnaires were also used in relevant research. The King’s Brief Interstitial Lung Disease (K-BILD) questionnaire suggested that objective cough assessment could assist clinical evaluation of PPF [42]. Two studies adopted the Living with Pulmonary Fibrosis (L-PF) questionnaire [34, 40]. The RCT [34] involving 663 patients confirmed significant improvements in L-PF cough scores in the nintedanib group after 52 weeks of treatment, with an intergroup difference of -6.1 points (p < 0.05). The qualitative study [40] with 20 participants identified cough, dyspnea and fatigue as predominant symptoms, all exerting multidimensional adverse impacts on patients’daily quality of life.

Objective cough monitoring via wearable devices was conducted in two studies [37, 42]. Another two studies [35, 38] did not adopt standardized scoring scales and only recorded the presence or absence of cough symptoms.

Comparisons with other populations

Five studies [34, 36, 38, 39, 41] compared cough and/or its impact across different types of ILD. Among these, one study found that among 248 patients with PPF, 193 (77.8%) experienced cough symptoms, with the highest prevalence observed in patients with fibrotic nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (fNSIP) [36]. Another study demonstrated that for patients with different subtypes of PPF, the use of nintedanib as an antifibrotic therapy attenuated cough worsening over 52 weeks [34]. A cross-sectional survey showed that among patients who experienced symptoms prior to a confirmed diagnosis of PPF (72.8%), cough was one of the most common symptoms (57.8%), suggesting that diagnostic delay in ILD exacerbates patient symptom burden and that improving diagnostic rates is key to enabling early treatment [38]. Furthermore, an RCT found no significant differences between the pirfenidone and placebo groups in changes from baseline in UCSD-SOBQ, LCQ, cough VAS, and SGRQ scores at 24 weeks of treatment [41] (Fig. 3). Finally, one study indicated that among patients with non-IPF fibrotic ILD, those who developed PPF had significantly worse SGRQ and COPD assessment test (CAT) scores compared to those who did not develop PPF [39].

A study focusing on primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) reported no statistically significant difference in cough incidence between the pSS-PPF and pSS-non-PPF groups [35].

Environmental factors and cough

Among the studies involving patients with PPF where the specific ILD type was not specified, one prospective observational study [37] was included. This study employed wearable cough monitoring devices in 8 patients with PPF over a period of 6 months to objectively measure cough frequency, while ambient PM2.5 and ozone levels were obtained from local monitoring stations. The findings support the hypothesis that PM2.5 and ozone levels are associated with an increase in cough episodes among patients with PPF, suggesting potential mechanisms of acute and subacute inflammatory response. These findings highlight the potential role of environmental factors in cough among PPF patients. However, given the limited sample size and lack of individual-level exposure data, these results should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger, multi-center studies.

Association of cough with impact/HRQoL measures

The relationship between cough and HRQoL has garnered increasing attention in the PPF/ILD population. An observational study [36] involving 248 patients demonstrated a significant negative correlation between LCQ scores and cough VAS scores (R=-0.738), suggesting that patients with milder cough symptoms experience better HRQoL. However, research on the association between cough and HRQoL in PPF/ILD patients remains relatively limited, particularly lacking systematic evidence for comparisons across different etiological subgroups and disease stages. Current assessment tools (such as LCQ, VAS, K-BILD and SGRQ cough domain) exhibit variability in their capacity to capture the multidimensional aspects of cough, and a unified standardized tool is still absent. Therefore, until a standardized cough assessment tool specific to the PPF/ILD population is developed and validated, it is advisable to adopt a multi-assessment strategy combining subjective scales with objective cough monitoring, as well as quality of life questionnaires with symptom diaries. This approach would more comprehensively capture the multifaceted impact of cough and provide a more reliable foundation for clinical intervention and treatment evaluation.

Experiences of cough

Only one qualitative study [40] identified in this review systematically explored patient experiences of cough in PPF. This study is given particular emphasis because it provides unique, in-depth insights into the patient perspective that quantitative studies cannot capture, directly addressing our objective of summarizing evidence on cough burden.

In this study, semi-structured interviews with 20 patients revealed that cough was one of the most widely endorsed symptoms (19/20 patients). Patients reported multidimensional negative effects on physical well-being, emotional state, social participation, and daily activities. The study also provided evidence supporting the content validity of the L-PF questionnaire.

Importantly, the fact that only one qualitative study was identified highlights a significant evidence gap: patient-reported experiences of cough in PPF remain underexplored. This finding directly addresses our objective of identifying evidence gaps to inform future research directions.

Economic burden of cough

With respect to economic burden in the PPF population, no direct evidence was identified from the included studies. However, one observational study in patients with PPF found an association between cough severity and workplace productivity loss [38]. Specifically, the study showed that only 25% of patients were engaged in full-time employment at the time of the survey, and among those not working full-time, 19% were unable to work due to ILD (including part-time work, retirement, long-term sick leave, or unemployment). This aligns with economic burden studies of PPF [43]. In a published economic model of PPF and SSc-ILD, the estimated annual aggregate income loss in the European Economic Area (due to sick days, early retirements, and permanent disability) was €1,433 million and €220 million, respectively. Additionally, productivity loss from unemployment reached €194 million for PPF and €26 million for SSc-ILD [43].

Discussion

Our study provides the first systematic synthesis to comprehensively evaluate the humanistic burden of cough in patients with PPF. The lack of available evidence precluded a meaningful analysis of the economic impact, which itself represents a critical evidence gap requiring future investigation. The findings confirm that cough is a common and persistent symptom in this population and is associated with substantial impairments in quality of life, including sleep disturbance, fatigue, and social withdrawal.

Although awareness of cough as a cardinal symptom of PPF is increasing, this review highlights a notable gap between its clinical impact and the evidence base available to guide its management, revealing a marked lack of robust data on effective targeted interventions. Current evidence does not support a significant effect of antifibrotics on cough severity or cough-related quality of life based on VAS and LCQ measurements. While a limited number of studies suggest that antifibrotic therapy may have beneficial effects on cough trajectory, these findings are preliminary and heterogeneous, and are not consistently supported by the available evidence. Therefore, no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding the efficacy of antifibrotics for cough outcomes. Accordingly, this review not only quantifies the humanistic burden of cough in PPF but also underscores the urgent need for dedicated research into the mechanisms and treatment of cough in this vulnerable population.

Findings from quantitative studies of patients with PPF, including cross-sectional, prospective, retrospective, cohort and RCTs, demonstrate that cough correlates with impaired HRQoL. Meanwhile, qualitative research offers distinct perspectives on the multifaceted burden of cough, indicating its profound impacts on daily activities, physical condition, sleep status, mental health and social interaction.

While this review identified substantial evidence on the humanistic burden of cough in patients with PPF, a notable gap remains regarding the economic burden. Currently, no studies have specifically investigated the economic burden or healthcare resource utilization associated with cough in PPF. No included study reported direct economic outcomes, such as healthcare resource utilization, direct or indirect costs. However, one observational study in patients with PPF found that cough severity was associated with workplace productivity loss. This absence of direct economic evidence is itself an important finding, as it highlights a critical area for future investigation. Studies in other chronic cough populations suggest that cough is associated with increased comorbidities, healthcare resource use, and costs [44], future research should consider incorporating economic endpoints to better understand the full burden of cough in PPF. Such evidence would be valuable for health economic modeling, cost-effectiveness analyses, and informing resource allocation decisions.

Among the 9 included studies, although the L-PF questionnaire was used in four studies, it was not the most frequently used cough assessment tool because three of these studies originated from the same data source. The LCQ was the most commonly used questionnaire in patients with PPF (three studies [36, 41, 42] , despite not having been validated in this population. In addition, only two studies [37, 42] employed objective cough monitoring devices, indicating that the use of such tools in cough assessment for PPF remains very limited. Objective cough monitoring devices can provide continuous, quantifiable cough data, addressing the limitations of subjective scales in terms of recall bias and sensitivity. However, their widespread application in PPF is constrained by equipment costs, operational complexity, and the lack of standardized interpretation criteria. Future research should focus on validating subjective tools such as the LCQ in PPF and exploring the feasibility of objective monitoring devices in both clinical trials and routine management, with the goal of establishing a multidimensional cough assessment system.

Variability in cough severity may arise not only from differences in cough characteristics but also from heterogeneity in patient populations, disease severity, and treatment regimens. Even in relatively homogeneous cough cohorts, VAS and LCQ scores exhibited some degree of variability, underscoring the substantial influence of study design, population selection, treatment interventions, and assessment tools on outcomes. These findings further support the need for establishing standardized assessment criteria. Future research should prioritize the harmonization of cough inclusion criteria and assessment instruments to enhance the comparability and reliability of findings.

Cough data in PPF are mostly derived from secondary endpoints of large studies, with significant heterogeneity in assessment tools. Disease-specific instruments such as VAS and LCQ are used alongside broader tools such as K-BILD, SGRQ, and CAT. Differences in tool versions and scoring standards further limit data comparability. Although LCQ was the primary tool used in this study, it was not specifically developed for the PPF population, and its ability to capture the multidimensional impact of cough in this context remains to be validated.

The discrepancies in current research findings can largely be attributed to the lack of a well-established and validated cough-specific assessment tool in the field of PPF. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a standardized cough assessment system tailored for PPF and to rigorously validate the applicability of existing tools in this population. Such efforts would significantly enhance the comparability and reliability of findings across different studies. Furthermore, the establishment of such standardized tools would provide a scientific foundation for guiding individualized treatment strategies based on cough severity, advancing PPF management into the era of precision medicine.

The key strengths of this study are reflected in three aspects. First, a comprehensive search strategy was employed to systematically synthesize the multidimensional impact of cough in patients with PPF. Second, the use of wearable devices provided objective, real-world cough data, which may be useful for monitoring disease progression and characterizing cough patterns in PPF, though further validation is needed. Third, the findings raise the possibility that environmental exposure data (e.g., air pollution) may be worth incorporating as an independent variable in future PPF studies and trials.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, substantial heterogeneity among the included studies precluded a quantitative meta-analysis; therefore, a narrative synthesis was adopted without formal heterogeneity assessments or subgroup analyses. The small number of PPF‑specific studies also challenged bias assessment and direct comparisons. Second, differences in study design, patient characteristics, disease severity, and assessment tools limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, including studies on broader progressive fibrosing ILD phenotypes may introduce selection bias, and sensitivity analyses excluding non‑PPF studies were not feasible due to the limited number of strictly defined PPF studies (n = 6). Fourth, the LCQ has not been formally validated in PPF, which may introduce bias and limit cross‑study comparability. Additionally, excluding letters, abstracts, protocols, non‑English publications, and case series may have omitted some aspects of cough burden. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the current state of research on cough burden in PPF.

Specific therapeutic and management approaches for cough in patients with PPF remain lacking, representing an urgent clinical issue that needs to be addressed. The evidence base for existing interventions is weak, with most studies yielding negative results, necessitating further validation through large-scale research [16, 45]. By systematically elucidating the breadth and depth of cough burden, this study aims to provide a reference and impetus for future research in this field.

Conclusions

In conclusion, current evidence indicates that cough negatively impacts HRQoL in patients with PPF, although relevant studies remain limited. Heterogeneity in study populations and assessment methods constrains the comparability of findings, and research on the economic burden of cough in PPF is notably lacking. Given these gaps, the development of standardized tools for cough assessment in PPF is essential to advance the understanding of its clinical impact, facilitate the design and implementation of high‑quality studies, and provide a scientific foundation for optimizing treatment strategies.
