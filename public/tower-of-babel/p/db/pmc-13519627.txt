Carbohydrate and enzymatic activity profiling for the quality assessment of Canadian honeys
Authors: Mile Shao, Asma Mdimagh, Lan Liu, Shaghig Bilamjian, Lei Tian, Stéphane Bayen, Salwa karboune
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe compositional profiles of 163 Canadian honey samples from four botanical sources -buckwheat, clover, goldenrod, and blueberry—were analyzed for enzymatic activity and sugar composition using spectrophotometry and LC-QToF-MS. Results showed that clover and goldenrod honeys exhibited higher diastase and invertase activities (11.90 DN/4.24 IN and 12.52 DN/5.33 IN), while buckwheat honey displayed the highest acid phosphatase activity (683.202 mg P/100 g). Blueberry honey demonstrated elevated glucose oxidase and catalase activities (11.843 μg H2O2/g/ h and 27.629 μg H2O2/g/ min). Fructose and glucose concentrations ranged from 37.0%–38.1% (w/w) and 32.0%–34.1% (w/w), respectively, across the four floral types, with maltose identified as the most abundant disaccharide. Multivariate analysis demonstrated significant negative correlations between diastase activity and HMF concentration (p ≤ 0.0001), and between glucose concentration and the levels of nigerose, isomaltose, and gentiobiose (p ≤ 0.0001). The predictive models for botanical origin classification achieved an accuracy of 80%–90%. Acid phosphatase and catalase were identified as the most impactful features to differentiate clover, blueberry, and buckwheat honey across various models based on importance scores.Keywords: Honey, Enzyme, LC-QToF-MS, Fructose, Glucose, PCA, Quality

Introduction

With a long history of human consumption, honey continues to be a preferred natural sweetener among consumers. Honeys are categorized into honeydew honey and nectar honey, also referred to as floral honey (FAO, 2022). Honeydew honey originates from plant secretions or the excretions of plant-sucking insects, whereas the latter comes from the nectar of blossoms (Gajek et al., 2026). From a market perspective, nectar honey is often categorized into two types: monofloral and multifloral honey. Monofloral honey is characterized in most countries by a predominant nectar source, generally, comprising more than 45% of the total pollen count from a single floral source, with some exceptions such as citrus, lavender or chestnut (Serra Bonvehi et al., 2019; Uršulin-Trstenjak et al., 2013). In contrast, multifloral honey is composed of nectar from multiple floral sources, with none of these sources contributing more than 45% to the total pollen count (Nedić et al., 2022). Most commercially available honey is produced from a blend of botanical sources without a dominant type. Monofloral honeys have attracted recent interest attributable to their unique and favorable sensory properties. In addition, the growing awareness of the nutraceutical benefits linked to certain monofloral honeys, like Manuka honey, has further increased their popularity and economic value. (Edo et al., 2023; Seraglio et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024).

Beekeeping provides essential pollination services and generates a wide range of bee products including honey. Recovering from the overwinter colony losses in 2022, the 2023 honey production in Canada reached a new height of 91.8 million pounds with a total value of 277.22 million dollars (AAFC, 2024; Shahbandeh, 2024). Honey is widely identified as one of the food commodities that are most prone to fraud on a global scale. This has led to high interest in developing and implementing novel authentication methods to identify the botanical origin of honeys (CFIA, 2024; Ennahli et al., 2026; Priyadharshini et al., 2026). The traditional method for honey authentication is pollen analysis; however, its efficiency is greatly affected by the analyst's experience and is susceptible to issues such as pollen addition or honey filtration (Jaafar et al., 2020). Moreover, a high variability in the pollen content in honey was reported due to the differences in plants' pollen-producing capacity and geographic origins, leading to diverse regulatory requirements across the different countries (Bodor et al., 2021). Therefore, there is an urgent need for rapid and accurate routine authentication methods (Bayen et al., 2024). In this field, integrating chromatographic and spectroscopic techniques with chemometric tools offers strong potential for practical applications (Bose & Padmavati, 2024; Brar et al., 2023).

Enzymes play a crucial role in the ripening of honey. For example, diastase and invertase, excreted by workers bees, are accumulated during the transportation of nectars and catalyze the hydrolysis of amylose and sucrose into glucose, maltose and fructose. In addition, invertase facilitates the formation of oligosaccharides via its transglycosylation activity (Alaerjani et al., 2022; Schievano et al., 2020; Silva et al., 2019). Both diastase activity and 5-hydroxy-methylfurfural (HMF) content, a by-product of Maillard reaction, are indicators of freshness and temperature discrimination during processing and storage (Shapla et al., 2018). Another important enzyme, glucose oxidase (GOX), secreted from the hypopharyngeal glands of honeybees, catalyzes the conversion of glucose into gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide, which are associated with the antimicrobial properties of honey (Bouzo et al., 2020; Sahin et al., 2020). GOX deactivates with increasing glucose concentration, storage and exposure to light (Alshareef et al., 2022). Conversely, catalase counteracts with the oxidation reactions of GOX by reducing hydrogen peroxide into oxygen and water, and its level in honey varies with the botanical origin (Brudzynski, 2020). Similarly, acid phosphatase, another plant-derived enzyme sourced from pollen and nectar, can serve as a potential indicator of the floral origin of honeys (Miłek et al., 2021).

Sugars are the main components of honey, comprising up to 70–85% of its composition. The sugar profile of honey is dominated by monosaccharides (about 75–95%) but also contains10–15% of disaccharides, and about 2% of trisaccharides along with oligosaccharides/oligomers (Crăciun et al., 2020). Honey also contains bioactive microcomponents such as protein, free amino acids, minerals, vitamins, volatile compounds, and phenolic compounds, which contribute to its sensory properties and nutritional and health-promoting benefits. Many studies have focused on the profiling of phenolic compounds for honey authentication (Gašić et al., 2017; Ntakoulas et al., 2024). Carbohydrate profiling has also been reported as an effective tool for identifying honey adulterated with other syrups (Megherbi et al., 2009; Nyarko et al., 2024; Qu et al., 2019). To date, only few studies have been conducted to investigate correlations between the complex carbohydrate profiles and the botanical origins of honey through the application of multivariate analysis in European countries (de la Fuente et al., 2011), and similar data are needed for North America honeys. In the present study, carbohydrates and enzymatic activities were profiled in 163 Canadian honeys. Pairwise correlations were then investigated among the parameters, and classification models were built to identify the key markers for authenticity of Canadian honeys.

Materials and methods

Honey samples

Honey samples were collected from local markets and retail stores in Montréal region (Canada) and on-line stores between 2021 and 2022 and were stored in amber vials at −20 °C before further processing for the analysis. A total of 163 honey samples were selected for the study based on their floral type, as indicated on the self-reported product labels. The selection included four key types of honey relevant to the Canadian market: 59 of buckwheat (Fagopyrum spp.) honey, 52 of clover (Trifolium) honey, 11 of goldenrod (Solidago) honey, and 41 of blueberry honey. These samples were primarily sourced from British Columbia, Ontario, and Quebec.

Materials

Deionized water used was from a Millipore Milli-Q Academic water purification system. Acetonitrile, hydrogen peroxide, methanol, LC/MS graded water, o-dianisidine (98%), p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (98%), p-nitrophenyl phosphate (97%), potassium iodide (99%), sodium chloride (≥99%), sodium citrate (≥99%), tris (hydroxymethyl) aminomethane (≥99.8%), cellobiose (≥98%), gentiobiose (≥98%), isomaltose (97%), isomaltotriose (97%), maltose monohydrate (≥95%), maltotriose (98%), melezitose hydrate (99%), palatinose hydrate (98%), panose (≥98%), raffinose pentahydrate (≥99%), sucrose (≥99%), trehalose (98%), and turanose (≥98%) were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Ontario, Canada). Ammonium acetate (≥98%) was purchased from Honeywell Research Chemical (Morris Plains, US). Ammonia, α-glucosidase, amylase, citric acid, glucose oxidase, glacial acetic acid, hydrochloric acid, iodine, myo-inositol (≥99%), peroxidase from horseradish, phosphatase, p-nitrophenol, sodium acetate (≥99%), sodium hydroxide, sodium phosphate dibasic dihydrate (≥99%), sodium phosphate monobasic (≥98%), soluble starch, fructose (≥99%), glucose (≥99.5%), erlose (≥94%), kojibiose (≥98%), and nigerose (≥90%) were purchased from Millipore Sigma (Oakville, Canada).

Enzymatic activity profiling of selected Canadian honeys

The activity of five enzymes (diastase, invertase, glucose oxidase, catalase, and acid phosphatase) were determined in the 163 Canadian honey samples. Enzyme (honey sample) blanks and substrate blanks were incubated simultaneously with all enzymatic reaction triplicates to eliminate the matrix effect and serve as procedural blanks.

Diastase activity assay

The diastase activity was determined by measuring the consumption rate of starch in a honey/starch mixture incubated at 40 °C. The method described by the International Honey Commission (IHC) was used (Bogdanov, 2009). A standard curve was constructed using starch solution (60–400 mg/L) and iodine assay which forms a complex between the amylose helix and iodine atoms, with a maximum absorbance between 600 and 680 nm (Brust et al., 2020; Pesek et al., 2022). To perform the iodine assay, a diluted iodine solution was prepared by mixing 4 g of potassium iodide and 0.4 mL of an iodine stock solution (2.2% w/v sublimated iodine and 4.4% w/v potassium iodide) in 100 mL deionized water. The 20% (w/v) honey reaction solution was prepared by mixing 0.2 g honey with 100 μL acetate buffer (1.3 M, pH 5.3, pH adjusted with glacial acetic acid), 60 μL of 2.9% (w/v) sodium chloride solution and deionized water was used to make up the final volume to 1 mL. The reaction mixture consisted of 1 mL of honey reaction solution and 1 mL of 2% (w/v) soluble starch solution and was incubated in an amber tube at 40 °C for 1 h. Every 20 min, 25 μL of the aliquot was sampled and mixed with 1 mL of deionized water and 250 μL of diluted iodine solution. The absorbance was then recorded at 660 nm (DU 800 UV/Visible Spectrophotometer, Beckman). The diastase activity was expressed in diastase number (DN), which represents the quantity of starch (in 10 mg) hydrolyzed by the diastase in 1 g of honey in 1 h at 40 °C (Rodríguez-Flores et al., 2016).

Invertase activity assay

The invertase activity was determined using the official IHC method and validated by substituting the honey portion with commercial α-glycosidase (Bogdanov, 2009). This assay is based on the quantification of p-nitrophenol (p-NP) released upon the hydrolysis of p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG) substrate by invertase. A standard curve of p-NP (0–100 μM) was established at 400 nm absorbance under basic conditions. Honey samples were dissolved in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) to yield a final concentration of 20% (w/v). 0.1 mL of the honey solution was added to 1 mL of 20 mM pNPG substrate solution. For each honey sample, reaction mixtures were prepared in triplicates and placed in a 40 °C shaking incubator. Starting from time 0 until 1 h, one triplicate was taken from the incubator every 15 min, and 0.1 mL of Tris-HCl buffer (3 M, pH 9.5) was added. Adjusting the pH to 9.5 allowed the termination of the honey invertase (α-glycosidase) reaction and enabled the transformation of the p-NP into nitrophenolate anion which had an optimum absorption at 400 nm. The common expression of invertase activity is in invertase number (IN), which indicates the amount sucrose (in 10 mg) hydrolyzed by 1 g of honey per h. IN was calculated by dividing the invertase activity in international unit (μmol of pNPG/min/kg of honey) with a conversion factor of 7.34 (Julika et al., 2020).

Acid phosphatase activity assay

The acid phosphatase activity in honey was measured using a method described by Miłek (2021). For the method validation, commercial acid phosphatase enzyme was used as a positive control. The substrate solution consisted of 5 mM p-nitrophenyl phosphate (p-NPP) in citrate buffer (0.1 M, pH 4.8). To initiate the enzymatic assay, 1 mL of the substrate solution was mixed with 1 mL of a 20% (w/v) honey solution (citrate buffer, 0.1 M, pH 4.8), and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 80 min. Every 20 min, 200 μL aliquots were collected from the reaction mixture and added to 1 mL of 0.25 M NaOH to terminate the reaction and enable the formation of nitrophenolate under basic condition. Absorbance measurements were recorded at 400 nm. The enzymatic activity was estimated from the released p-NP and expressed in mg P/ 100 g honey/ 24 h.

Glucose oxidase activity assay

The determination of glucose oxidase activity in honey was conducted using the modified method described by Flanjak (2016). The glucose oxidase activity was quantified by measuring the generated hydrogen peroxide from the oxidation of glucose to gluconic acid. This was achieved through a peroxidase-o-dianisidine reaction system, in which the oxygen released from hydrogen peroxide reduction, via the addition of peroxidase, oxidizes o-dianisidine, and forms a chromophore with a maximum absorption at 420 nm. The reaction mixture contained 600 μL phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 6.1), 100 μL o-dianisidine solution (0.1% w/v, 1:49 methanol/phosphate buffer), 100 μL peroxidase solution (500 U/mL, 0.1 M pH 6.1 phosphate buffer), 200 μL honey solution (20% w/v, 0.1 M pH 6.1 phosphate buffer). The mixture was incubated in an amber vial at 37 °C for 2 h. To terminate the reaction, 100 μL of HCl (1 M) was added. The absorbance was measured at 420 nm against a blank reaction mixture in which the honey was substituted with 200 μL phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 6.1). The baseline (time zero) was established by adding the honey sample and the peroxidase-o-dianisidine reaction system after the hydrochloric acid. A hydrogen peroxide calibration curve (0–200 μM) was constructed using peroxidase and o-dianisidine. Glucose oxidase activity was expressed as μg of released hydrogen peroxide per gram of honey per h.

Catalase activity assay

The catalase activity in honey was determined using a modified method adapted from Huidobro & Sánchez (2005) in which the consumption rate of hydrogen peroxide was measured using a peroxidase-o-dianisidine reaction system. The reaction mixture comprised of 15% honey (w/v) and 3 mM hydrogen peroxide in 60 mM, pH 7 phosphate buffer. The reaction mixtures in amber containers were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. To determine the quantity of the remaining hydrogen peroxide, 50 μL of the reaction mixture was mixed with 750 μL phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 6.1), 100 μL o-dianisidine solution (0.1% w/v, 1:49 methanol/phosphate buffer), and 100 μL peroxidase solution (500 U/mL, 0.1 M pH 6.1 phosphate buffer) at 37 °C for 5 min. The reaction was terminated by the addition of 100 μL of 1 M hydrochloric acid. Absorbance was measured at 420 nm. Catalase activity was expressed as μg of consumed hydrogen peroxide per gram of honey per min.

Carbohydrate profiling

Determination of monosaccharides

The quantification of the fructose and glucose in honey was carried out using high performance anion exchange chromatography (HPAEC) on a Dionex ICS-3000 system. To separate and detect the monosaccharides, the CarboPac PA20 column (3 × 150 mm) and a pulsed amperometric detector (PAD) were used with an isocratic mobile phase made of 10 mM sodium hydroxide at a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min. Honey samples were diluted to 20 mg/L using deionized water and filtered through 0.22 μm syringe filters, and 250 μL sample were injected using an autosampler. Sugar concentrations were estimated using the calibration curve of the standard fructose and glucose solution (1–10 mg/L), which covers the theoretical fructose and glucose concentration in 20 mg/L honey solution. Chromatograms were processed and integrated using the Chromeleon™ Chromatography Management System.

Determination of di-, trisaccharides

Disaccharides and trisaccharides were profiled in honey using liquid chromatography (LC) on an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC system coupled to the 6560 ion mobility quadrupole time of flight-mass spectrometry (QTOF-MS) (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). The analytes were separated on an Hypercarb™ Porous Graphitic Carbon HPLC Column (1 × 150 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phases consisted of LC/MS grade water with 0.25% ammonia and 5 mM ammonium acetate (Solvent A) and acetonitrile with the same concentrations of ammonia and ammonium acetate (Solvent B). The flow rate was set at 0.12 mL/min, the column temperature maintained at 60 °C, and the injection volume was 0.5 μL. The gradient profile of mobile phase started with 2% B for the first 0.5 min, increased to 10% B over 14.5 min, held at 10% B for 3 min, then ramped up to 99% B over 8 min and held it for 3 min, before dropping back to 2% B over 3 min and held at 2% B for the final 3 min for stabilisation. The mass spectrometer was equipped with a Dual AJS ESI ion source operating in negative ionization mode. The MS conditions were set as follows: the drying gas temperature at 150 °C, drying gas flow at 11 L/ min, sheath gas temperature at 200 °C, sheath gas flow at 11 L/ min, pressure on the nebulizer at 35 psi, capillary voltage at 4000 V, nozzle voltage at 500 V, and the fragmentor voltage at 175 V. Full scan MS data were recorded between mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) 50–1700 with a scan rate of 0.63 spectra/s, and were collected at both centroid and profile mode. Data treatment was conducted using Quantitative Analysis 10.0 from Agilent MassHunter Workstation Software. Sugar standards (disaccharide: sucrose, maltose, cellobiose, gentiobiose, isomaltose, melibiose, kojibiose, turanose, trehalose, palatinose, nigerose; trisaccharide: erlose, maltotriose, isomaltotriose, raffinose, melezitose, panose, kestose) were dissolved in LC/MS graded water and diluted to 60 mg/L for retention time identification purposes. Sugar mixtures of all standard solutions were prepared at 0–100 mg/L to construct the calibration curve, which would cover the anticipated concentration ranges of these sugars in honey. The honey samples were prepared in LC/MS graded water, diluted and filtered to a final concentration of 0.5% (w/v). A final concentration of 5 mg/L of myo-inositol was used as the internal standard for all the sugar standards, calibration mixtures, and honey samples.

Characterization of other physicochemical properties

Color

In order to assess the color of the honey, liquid honey, with no residues or bubbles, was filled in the sample cup and compared to the Jack's scale honey color grader under natural light.

HMF

For the quantification of HMF, honey samples were analyzed by LC-MS as described by Tian et al. (2024) using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC system coupled to the 6545 QToF-MS.

Pollen analysis

Pollen analysis is performed to assist with botanical origin confirmation and prediction model generation following the procedures described by Tian et al. (2025).

Multivariate statistical analyses

Visual examination of sample grouping patterns was performed using heatmaps, PCA, and Biplots distribution rather than a formal clustering. A distribution-based cluster analysis was performed using the enzymatic activity profiling data as well as the fructose and glucose content and the fructose/glucose ratio to visually observe sample grouping and differentiation. A heatmap was generated after submitting all quantitative results to a correlation analysis to assess the correlations among the enzymatic and carbohydrate profiles and other honey properties (HMF, color). A principal component analysis (PCA) was performed with all the quantitative data for the clover (n = 52), buckwheat (n = 59), and blueberry (n = 41) honey samples to investigate groupings according to floral types. Goldenrod samples (n = 11) were excluded from the analysis due to their disproportionately small sample size, which risked compromising the reliability and interpretability of the ordination. Data normalization was carried out for the unidentified sugars and HMF content. The python and various open-source libraries, including Pandas, Scikit-learn, Scipy, Matplotlib, and Seaborn were used for all data analyses and visualizations.

Honey classification and prediction

Using the python library Pandas, various mathematical classification models were investigated for honey botanical origin prediction using the data of enzymatic and carbohydrate profiling. Twelve machine learning algorithms were screened, including Logistic Regression, Ridge Regression, Random Forest, K-Nearest Neighbors, Support Vector Classifier, XGBoost, Linear Discriminant Analysis, SGD Classifier, AdaBoost, Nearest Centroid, Decision Tree, and Gaussian Naïve Bayes. All models were examined for their classification accuracy by generating the confusion matrix based on the actual label (self-claimed botanical origin obtained from the label) and the predicted botanical origins. For each dataset configuration, the best-performing model were picked and then validated using 10-fold cross-validation. Due to the limited number of goldenrod samples, only buckwheat, blueberry, and clover honey samples were subjected to this analysis. Three final models were determined with each trained on a different data split designed for a specific analytical goal:

Results and discussion

Enzymatic activity profiling of selected Canadian Honeys

The enzymatic activity profiles (diastase, invertase, acid phosphatase, glucose oxidase, and catalase) was established for 163 Canadian honeys of varying botanical sources (Fig. 1, complete data set in Supplementary Table 1). All results were obtained in parallel with standards each day to ensure accuracy.

Levels of enzymatic activities in monofloral honey samples (clover: 52, buckwheat: 59, blueberry: 41, goldenrod: 11). (a): diastase (DN); (b): invertase (IN); (c) acid phosphatase (mg P/ 100 g honey/ 24 h); (d) glucose oxidase (μg H2O2/g honey/ h); (e): catalase (μg H2O2/g honey/ min).The boxes display the median and interquartile range (IQR) and whiskers extend up to 1.5 IQR.

The diastase activity in honeys is responsible for breaking down the starches into simple sugars (e.g maltose and glucose) and is a key indicator of honey quality and freshness. Low activities can reflect indirect assessment of excessive heating or prolonged storage or even adulteration through the addition of syrups (Takahashi et al., 2023). Large variations in the diastase activity (Fig. 1. (a)) were observed across four types of monofloral honeys. While the minimum value determined in each floral type is 0 DN, the maximum value differed among the four types. One sample among the clover honeys exhibited the highest maximum value of diastase activity at 30.127 DN, while the highest maximum value for the other floral types was 25.716 DN for buckwheat honey, 21.583 DN for blueberry honey, and 26.572 DN for goldenrod honey. Outliers beyond or below the range of the whiskers were not found in all floral types except for one blueberry sample at 21.58 DN. Indicated in Fig. 1 (a), the median value and IQR for the four types of honey are as follows: clover honey has a median of 10.88 DN with an IQR of 7.31–16.7 DN; buckwheat honey has a median of 9.36 DN with an IQR of 2.34–15.11 DN; blueberry honey exhibits a median of 9.52 DN with and IQR of 5.43–12.11 DN; goldenrod honey shows a median of 9.89 DN with an IQR of 8.03–19.23 DN (Supplementary Table 1). The median values were found to be similar across the four floral types, however, blueberry honey, exhibits a narrower range of diastase activity comparing to the other three. The diastase activity for clover honey in this study was generally higher than that reported by Can et al. (2015), ranging from 6.6 to 9.2 DN, but aligned with the results obtained by Bell and Grainger (2023) and Ghoniemy et al. (2022), with the average activity ranging between 10.16 and 11.80 DN. The buckwheat honey exhibited a lower diastase activity compared to the values reported in previous studies for Polish and Italian buckwheat honeys, ranging from 21.1 to 66.43 DN; however, it aligns with the values (8.1–17.7 DN) reported for a blend of imported and EU buckwheat honey (Kowalski & Lukasiewicz, 2017; Miłek et al., 2021; Pasini et al., 2013; Tomczyk et al., 2022). The average diastase activity in goldenrod honeys from this study is also lower than the values reported for fresh goldenrod honeys from Podkarpackie (Poland), which had an average of 33.08 ± 3.34 DN, but aligns with the values reported in 2022 from the same region, ranging from 9.8 to 26.38 DN (Dżugan et al., 2022; Wesołowska & Dżugan, 2017). These differences revealed the variability in the enzymatic activities in honey over different years and underline the complexity of factors that impact honey's enzymatic activity.

The quality of honey, as a key factor tied to its economic value, is regulated for international trade by the CODEX with specific guidelines varying across different countries (Mădaş et al., 2020; Thrasyvoulou et al., 2018). According to the regulation by Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) graded Canadian No.1 honey must have a diastase content higher than 8 if the HMF content is less than or equal to 40 mg/L, or higher than 3 if the HMF content is no more than 15 mg/L (CFIA, 2021). Most of the honey samples tested in this study complied with Canadian regulations, with 14 clover, 25 buckwheat, 15 blueberry, and 2 goldenrod honey samples showing diastase activities below 8. Of these, 15 were not claimed to be Canadian No. 1 graded, and 5 were labeled as Ontario No. 1, thus not required to meet the diastase activity standards set for Canada No 1. grade honey (Government of Ontario, 2021). The lower diastase activity in some samples can be attributed to the storage conditions before purchasing them, as several studies have shown that diastase in honey is sensitive to heat and light (Can et al., 2015; Ghoniemy et al., 2022; Pashayanm, 2024).

Similar to diastase, the level of invertase activity can be an indicator for assessing freshness, purity, processing history and compliance with quality standards. The results of invertase activity are presented in Fig. 1 (b). Outliers were detected in all floral types except for goldenrod honeys. The median value and IQR for the four types of honey are as follows: clover honey has a median of 2.21 IN with an IQR of 0.83–4.92 IN; buckwheat honey has a median of 0.42 IN with an IQR of 0–1.48 IN; blueberry honey exhibits a median of 1.58 IN with and IQR of 0.6–4.74 IN; goldenrod honey shows a median of 2.15 IN with an IQR of 1.04–11.88 IN. Goldenrod honey had the lowest maximum value of 14.58 IN, followed by blueberry honey with a maximum of 17.59 IN. Clover honey had the highest maximum of 22.76 IN (Supplementary Table 1). Despite a high maximum of 19.00 IN, 20 out of 59 buckwheat honey samples exhibited the invertase activity below detection limit of the method in use. Upon the unit conversion to IU via multiplying the IN value by the conversion factor 7.34, the majority of the invertase activities for buckwheat honeys in this study are significantly lower than that from the study conducted by Kowalski and Lukasiewicz (2017), which were ranged between 39.1 and 82.8 IU. The wide range of differences, low median value and presence of multiple outliers in invertase activities within each floral type can be related to the weaker stability of the enzyme when exposed to heat. In comparison to diastase, as a study suggested, invertase activity decreased significantly after 24 h, even at temperatures as low as 35 °C (Kamboj et al., 2024). In addition, differences in geographical origin and potential thermal exposure prior to purchase may have contributed to the observed discrepancies.

The levels of glucose oxidase (GOX) in honey samples are also presented in Fig. 1 (d). GOX in honey serves not only as a natural preservative upon the generation of gluconic acid but also an indicator of quality, authenticity and processing. Outliers were found in all floral types, spreading in a wide range beyond the upper extremes and showing large variations between the GOX activities measured for the same floral type. The buckwheat honey had a significantly lower GOX activity compared to the other three floral types, with a median value of 0.24 μg H2O2/g/h and an IQR of 0.06–0.47 μg H2O2/g/h. Although clover and goldenrod honey display comparable IQRs of 0.37–4.63 μg H2O2/g/h and 0.36–3.72 μg H2O2/g/h, respectively, goldenrod honey has a larger median value of 2.38 μg H2O2/g/h compared to clover honey, which has a median value of 0.78 μg H2O2/g/h. With a median of 1.87 μg H2O2/g/h, the blueberry honey exhibits a wider IQR of 0.63–12.38 μg H2O2/g/h (Supplementary Table 1). The levels of GOX detected in the clover honeys in this study are lower than the values reported by Ghoniemy (2022) from Giza Governorate of Egypt, ranging between 22.37 and 26.00 μg H2O2/g/h. Ghoniemy (2022) also investigated the effects of storage conditions on the enzymatic activities, reporting that the GOX activity decreased to 11.70 μg H2O2/g/h and 14.13 μg H2O2/g/h for samples stored at room temperature for 12 months in transparent and amber vessels, respectively. This suggested that GOX is highly sensitive to light and heat, and may loss its activity faster than diastase, the enzyme commonly recognized as a freshness indicator in honey regulations (Sahin et al., 2020; Guttentag, et al., 2021; Alshareef et al., 2022; Ghoniemy et al., 2022). The lower GOX activity observed in the buckwheat honey samples in this study, along with previously noted reductions in diastase and invertase activities for the same floral type, suggests that regional differences may influence enzymatic activities between Canadian buckwheat honeys and those from Polish studies (Kowalski & Lukasiewicz, 2017; Miłek, Bocian, et al., 2021; Tomczyk et al., 2022). In addition to geographic locations, factors related to bees, such as age, diet, the seasonal changes in their physiological status, the density of the population, and the colony strength may also account for the observed differences (Al-Sherif et al., 2017; Babacan & Rand, 2005; Lichtenberg-Kraag, 2015). Moreover, the nectar flow, nectar viscosity and sucrose concentration are also suggested to be negatively correlated with the abundance of salivary enzymes in honey (Flanjak et al., 2016; Lichtenberg-Kraag, 2015).

Acid phosphatase in honeys is another enzyme that plays multiple roles from helping in honey production to serving as a biomarker for freshness, quality, and origin. While no outliers were detected in clover and buckwheat sample, 4 blueberry samples and 1 goldenrod sample were identified as outliers (Fig. 3.1 (c)). Buckwheat honey exhibited the highest acid phosphatase activity levels ranging from 30.50 to 1139.88 U, with a median of 770.62 U and an IQR of 427.21–939.14 U. Clover honey has a median activity of 33.58 U and an IQR of 20.2–72.03 U. Similarly, blueberry honey exhibits a similar median activity of 32.49 U and an IQR of 20.25–51.67 U. For goldenrod honey, the sole outlier is the maximum value of 738.34 U, while the median activity is the median value is 53.32 U with an IQR of 27.86–62.44 U (Supplementary Table 1). The high levels of acid phosphatase in buckwheat honey sample align with the findings reported by Miłek et al. The catalase activity in honeys contributes to their chemical stability by regulating the level of hydrogen peroxide and can be an indicator of raw or processed honeys. Outliers were identified in clover and blueberry honeys, with all 2 outliers for clover honey found above the upper extreme and all 5 outliers for blueberry honey found beneath the lower extreme (Fig. 1 (e)). The highest catalase activity was obtained in blueberry honey with a median value of 29.96 μg H2O2/g/min and an IQR of 26.21–33.74 μg H2O2/g/min, followed by buckwheat honey with a median value of 23.77 μg H2O2/g/min and an IQR of 18.35–28.42 μg H2O2/g/min, and goldenrod honey with a median value of 21.02 μg H2O2/g/min and an IQR of 12.25–26.94 μg H2O2/g/min. The lowest activities were observed for clover honey with a median value of 9.80 μg H2O2/g/min and an IQR of 6.43–15.71 μg H2O2/g/min (Supplementary Table 1). To date, very few studies were conducted on the quantification of catalase in honey samples. Large variations of catalase activities were found in honey with different botanical origin sourced from the same locality, which further suggests the impact of plant source on the presence of the enzyme (Alshareef et al., 2022; de Abreu Franchini et al., 2011). Low activity of catalase in clover honey compared to other floral types in this study agreed with the observations previous reported (Borutinskaite et al., 2018). Positive correlations were suggested between the antimicrobial properties and the glucose oxidase or catalase in honey (Lu et al., 2013; Osés et al., 2024).

Carbohydrate profiling of selected Canadian honeys

Carbohydrate profiling of 163 Canadian honeys from four different self-claimed botanical sources was conducted, and results are summarized in Fig. 3, with details presented in Supplementary Table 2–4 and Fig. 3. Several carbohydrate dimers, including trehalose, isomaltose, sucrose, maltose, and nigerose, as well as trimers such as erlose, kestose, melezitose, and panose, were confidently identified using analytical standards based on accurate mass measurements, retention times, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. 3). Some targeted carbohydrates, including turanose, kojibiose, melibiose, and maltotriose, exhibited low ionization efficiency and/or coeluted with higher-abundance carbohydrate species, which hindered reliable quantification; therefore, these compounds were excluded from quantitative analysis (Fig. 2). As expected, fructose and glucose were the predominant carbohydrates detected across all floral types. In contrast, sucrose, maltose, isomaltose, nigerose, gentiobiose, and erlose were present at relatively low levels, ranging from non-detectable (ND) to 4.01%. Palatinose, cellobiose, and raffinose were either absent or detected below the method detection limit in most samples and, consequently, were not considered for further discussion.

Extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) of (a) disaccharides and (b) trisaccharides in standards and in four selected honey samples (clover, buckwheat, goldenrod, and blueberry).

Fructose was the dominant sugar in all honeys with an average concentration of 37.8% in blueberry honeys, 37.0% in buckwheat honeys, 38.1% in clover honeys and 37.9% is goldenrod honeys. Similarly, glucose, the second most abundant sugar in honey, showed minor variations, with an average concentration varying between 32.0 and 34.1% across the four floral types. The differences in the fructose/glucose (F/G) ratio were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) among the blueberry, buckwheat, and clover samples, with the average values ranging between 1.12 and 1.135. However, the goldenrod honey had an average F/G ratio of 1.87 ± 0.063. The F/G ratio in buckwheat honey aligned with the results reported by Pasini (2013), in which 9 out of 10 samples showed an F/G ratio between 1.1 and 1.2.

The third most abundant sugar in honey is maltose with an average value in each floral type ranging from 0.94% - 1.12%. Consistent with previous studies, maltose is identified as the most abundant disaccharide among the sugars identified in this study (de la Fuente et al., 2011; Pascual-Maté et al., 2018; Pasini et al., 2013). However, some studies had shown a higher sucrose (0.6%, 1.26% and 1.79%) than maltose (0%, 0.26% and 0.74%) content in buckwheat honey from China, USA, and Poland, respectively (Corey et al., 2022; Deng et al., 2018; Nešović et al., 2020). All samples in this study, including the outliers (Fig. 3 (d)) comply with the sucrose requirement (less than 5%) for Canadian blossom honey, with a median of 0.15%, 0.13%, 0.10% and 0.11%, and an IQR of 0.10–0.47%, 0.09–0.20%, 0.08–0.14% and 0.09–0.24% for clover, buckwheat, blueberry, and goldenrod honeys, respectively (CFIA, 2021). These values align with the sucrose content reported in previous studies of 0.18–0.27% for clover honey, and 0.1–0.9% for buckwheat honey (Pascual-Maté et al., 2018; Pasini et al., 2013). Lower sucrose content in honey is an indicator of a higher level of honey maturation, as sucrose is converted to fructose and glucose via invertase during the ripening process (Pascual-Maté et al., 2018; Pasini et al., 2013; Yan et al., 2023). Isomaltose was also quantified across all samples, and the median values and the IQRs for the four floral types (Fig. 3 (e)) were estimated as follows: 0.57% and 0.49–0.66% for clover honey, 0.57% and 0.53–0.67% for buckwheat honey, 0.63% and 0.54–0.74% for blueberry honey, and 0.71% and 0.51–0.77% for goldenrod honey. Compared to previous studies of Pascual-Maté (2018) and Pasini (2013), the isomaltose contents were either slightly lower or closer to the lower range. Trehalose was detected in most honey samples, except for 4 clover, 1 blueberry and 1 goldenrod honey sample, and the average concentration varied between 0.051% - 0.066%. Gentiobiose was detected in all honeys, except for 4 clover and 3 buckwheat samples. The highest gentiobiose content of 1.463% (w/w) was observed in one clover honey sample, although goldenrod honeys generally had a higher average concentration of 0.428% (w/w). Nigerose was detected in all samples with an average concentration ranging from 0.489 to 0.547% (w/w) across the four floral types. Erlose, the only trisaccharide quantified in this study, was present in all honey samples, with an average concentration ranging from 0.245 to 0.461% (w/w). The highest erlose content was detected in clover honey at 1.495% (w/w).

Correlation statistical analyses

Fig. 5 shows the biplot distribution of sample grouping patterns with enzymatic activities, fructose concentration, glucose concentration, F/G ratio date. The distribution cluster with acid phosphate activity distinguished the buckwheat honeys from the other types. Buckwheat honey exhibited by far the highest mean acid phosphatase activity (683.2 ± 295.4 mg P/100 g/24 h), more than 10-fold higher than clover (45.4 ± 32.6), blueberry (48.9 ± 55.9), and goldenrod (109.3 ± 209.4) honeys. The high standard deviation observed in buckwheat and goldenrod suggests considerable variability within these floral types. The one-way ANOVA revealed a highly significant overall effect of floral type on acid phosphatase activity (F = 139.68, p-value ≤0.0001), confirming that botanical origin is a significant source of variation in this enzymatic marker. This agrees with Fig. 1, where buckwheat honeys exhibited significantly higher acid phosphatase activity (p-value<0.0001) compared to clover, blueberry and goldenrod honey samples. Additionally, the cluster plot of catalase activity reveals the separation of clover, buckwheat and blueberry honey samples based on the level of this activity. Catalase activity contributed significantly to the botanical discrimination of honey samples, as confirmed by one-way ANOVA (F = 36.46, p ≤ 0.0001). Blueberry honey exhibited the highest mean catalase activity (27.6 ± 8.1 μg H₂O₂/g/min), followed by buckwheat (23.6 ± 6.2), goldenrod (20.9 ± 8.8), and clover (11.9 ± 8.6) honeys. Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons revealed that clover honey was significantly different from all other floral types (p ≤ 0.0001 vs. blueberry; p ≤ 0.0001 vs. buckwheat; p = 0.003 vs. goldenrod), driven by its markedly lower catalase activity. In contrast, blueberry and buckwheat honeys were not significantly different from each other (p = 0.053), nor were buckwheat and goldenrod (p = 0.712) or blueberry and goldenrod (p = 0.054), indicating partial overlap among these three floral types based on catalase alone. These results suggest that while catalase activity effectively separates clover honey from the remaining botanical groups, it does not provide sufficient discriminatory power to distinguish blueberry, buckwheat, and goldenrod honeys in isolation.

Both acid phosphatase and catalase are enzymes of plant origin (Alonso-Torre et al., 2006), which significantly contributed to clustering of honey samples. With catalase serve as a key component in decomposing hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen in pollen (Osés et al., 2024). Other enzymes - diastase, invertase, and glucose oxidase - which are secreted from the hypopharyngeal or salivary glands of bees during nectar and pollen collection, did not contribute significantly towards the grouping of samples based on the botanical origin (Fig. 5).

A heatmap was generated to visualize the correlations among all quantitative data of honeys including enzymatic activities, carbohydrate profiling, HMF content and the Jack's scale pfund (Fig. 6). The strongest negative correlations (p < 0.0001) were observed for the glucose content with the F/G ratio (r = −0.8), and the nigerose content (r = −0.67). In addition, inverse correlations between HMF content and diastase activity (r = −0.45) or invertase activity (r = −0.39) were found to be moderately negative and statistically significant (p < 0.0001). Glucose concentration with diastase activity (r = −0.36), isomaltose concentration (r = −0.5), gentiobiose concentration (r = −0.57), or with the unknown trisaccharide (r = −0.35), tetrasaccharide (r = − 0.46), and pentasaccharide (r = −0.37) concentrations also exhibited mildly negative correlation with a high level of significance (p < 0.0001). The slight negative correlation (r = −0.21) between glucose and invertase activity can be attributed to the inhibitory effect of high glucose concentrations on invertase activity, as previously documented (Zhou et al., 2020). The formation of di- and oligosaccharides in honey with α-, and β-glycosidic linkages, such as maltose, isomaltose, gentiobiose, kestose, and panose has been associated with both enzymatic (invertase) and non-enzymatic transglycosylation (Silva et al., 2019). Hence, the mildly negative correlations observed in this study, between the glucose content and other di-, tri-, and oligosaccharide concentrations may be due to the transglycosylation process that uses glucose as a starting substrate to generate the oligomers.

The blueberry honey samples clustered negatively along PC3 and are perpendicular to PC1 and PC2 (Fig. S1 (b) (c)). Buckwheat and clover honey were grouped positively along PC3, with clover positioned on the left and buckwheat on the right along PC2 (Fig. S1 (c)). Predicted classification of botanical origins of honey

Different mathematical models were constructed using carbohydrate and enzyme activity profiling data and were assessed for the prediction of botanical origins of honey. The 10-fold cross-validated accuracy scores of three sets of models configured with different training and predicting sample sets are shown in the appendix (Supplementary Fig. 2 (a-c)). Samples were deemed a match if they contained over 45% of the targeted pollen or had less than 45% of the targeted pollen but exhibited sensory attributes (eg. color and taste) consistent with the targeted monofloral honey (self-claimed label). Goldenrod samples (n = 11) were excluded from classification analyses due to insufficient sample size for stable group representation. The overlapping between the profiles of goldenrod honey with other floral types also limited their discriminative values and increase the possibility of introducing noise and potentially affect model reliability. Feature importance was extracted directly from each fitted model using its native scoring approach. For the XGBoost classifier, importance scores reflect the gain-based metric, the average improvement in classification accuracy contributed by each feature across all splits in the ensemble of decision trees. For the Ridge Regression model, importance was derived from the magnitude of the regularized coefficients assigned to each feature, where larger absolute values indicate stronger influence on the classification boundary. For the Logistic Regression model, importance was similarly based on the magnitude of the log-odds coefficients assigned to each feature by the fitted model.

The twelve algorithms were selected to cover a broad and diverse range of machine learning families, ensuring that the model selection process was unbiased and data-driven rather than assumption-based. Linear models such as Logistic Regression, Ridge, SGD, LDA were screened to assess whether the botanical discrimination signal was linearly separable in the feature space. Distance-based models such as KNN, Nearest Centroid were screened to evaluate whether proximity in biochemical space was sufficient for classification. Tree-based and ensemble models such as Decision Tree, Random Forest, AdaBoost, XGBoost were screened to capture non-linear relationships and feature interactions that linear models may miss. The probabilistic model Gaussian Naïve Bayes were screened to provide a baseline based on feature distributions and the kernel-based model Support Vector Classifier were screened to handle potential non-linear boundaries between floral types. The three final models: XGBoost, Ridge Regression, and Logistic Regression, were retained solely based on their highest cross-validated accuracy scores within their respective dataset splits, ensuring that model selection was objective and reproducible.

To develop a classification model considering all 152 samples of interest (buckwheat: 59, blueberry: 41, clover: 52), XGB was selected due to its highest average accuracy score of 0.9 (90%) upon 10-fold cross-validation (Fig. S2 (a)), indicating robust overall performance. According to the confusion matrix (Fig. 8 (d)) correctly classified 14 out of 15 clover samples (93%), all 7 buckwheat samples (100%), and 7 out of 9 blueberry samples (78%). The only misclassifications involved blueberry honey, with 2 samples incorrectly predicted as clover. No buckwheat sample was misclassified, confirming that its distinct biochemical profile, particularly its exceptionally high acid phosphatase activity (683.2 ± 295.4 mg P/100 g/24 h), renders it unambiguously separable from the other floral types. The misclassification of blueberry as clover is consistent with the statistical overlap between these two groups in both catalase (p = 0.053) and acid phosphatase (p = 0.9998) activities, suggesting that their biochemical profiles are insufficiently distinct for perfect separation based on enzymatic and carbohydrate markers alone. Buckwheat honey (Table 1) achieved perfect precision, recall, and F1-score (1.00), confirming its unambiguous biochemical separability driven by its distinctively high acid phosphatase activity. Clover performed strongly with a recall of 0.93, meaning 93% of true clover samples were correctly identified. Blueberry showed the weakest performance (F1 = 0.82), with a recall of 0.78 indicating that 22% of true blueberry samples were missed, predominantly misclassified as clover which is consistent with the biochemical overlap between these two groups.

Lastly, the Logistic Regression model was employed among the models trained on a set of 84 non-outlier samples and predicted on a 68-sample set containing at least one outlier (enzymatic activity or carbohydrate profiling) (Fig. S2 (c)). With 10-folds cross validation, this model yields 87% accuracy with a standard deviation of 0.121, and the confusion matrix (Fig. 8 (e)) suggested that this model correctly identifying all 19 clover samples (100%) with zero false positives or negatives. Buckwheat classification was also strong, with 26 out of 28 samples correctly identified (93%) and 1 misclassified as each of clover and blueberry. Blueberry again showed the weakest performance, with 15 out of 21 samples correctly classified (71%), 5 misclassified as clover, and 1 as buckwheat. Importance score shown in Fig. 8 (c) shows that catalase activity (1.7) and acid phosphatase activity (1) are two of the most influential features to the model. The perfect identification of clover honey in this model is likely driven by its distinctively low catalase activity (11.9 ± 8.6 μg H₂O₂/g/min), which was significantly lower than all other floral types (p ≤ 0.0001), providing an unambiguous biochemical threshold for this class. Buckwheat achieved the highest F1-score across all three models (0.95), reflecting its robust biochemical distinctiveness. Across all three models, buckwheat honey was the most reliably classified floral type, while blueberry honey consistently showed the lowest recall and F1-score and the highest misclassification rate, predominantly confused with clover. These recurring patterns reflect the fundamental biochemical similarity between blueberry and clover honeys as they share statistically indistinguishable acid phosphatase (p = 0.9998) and partially overlapping catalase activities, making them the most challenging pair to discriminate using enzymatic and carbohydrate markers alone. Future work incorporating complementary chemical markers such as phenolic profiles or volatile compounds may help resolve this persistent overlap.

Conclusion

The diastase activity results from this study confirmed the quality of 163 monofloral honeys complying with the CFIA standards. All the targeted enzymes, namely diastase, invertase, glucose oxidase, catalase and acid phosphatase, were detected in Canadian honeys and they were quantified. The PCA analysis further confirms the significance of acid phosphatase and catalase activities alongside other quantifiable features in establishing a more robust honey authentication model for buckwheat, clover and blueberry honeys. This further confirmed the significance of the plant originated enzymes, catalase and acid phosphatase, on the authentication of the botanical origins of monofloral honeys. Promising identification of buckwheat honey was achieved by using the XGB model. Catalase and acid phosphatase have been identified as the most significant parameters in all models examined.

Regarding class imbalance, the dataset used in this study comprised unequal class sizes across the three botanical groups (buckwheat: 59, clover: 52, blueberry: 41), with goldenrod excluded due to insufficient representation (n = 11). While this imbalance is moderate rather than severe, it is acknowledged that this may have influenced model performance, particularly for the blueberry class which had the smallest sample size and consistently showed the lowest recall and F1-scores across all three models.

Honey samples came from several regions across Canada. This introduces natural biochemical variation within each floral type. This variability reflects real conditions and makes the models more relevant in practice. At the same time, it increases variation within each class. This likely contributed to some of the misclassification patterns, especially for blueberry and goldenrod honeys, which showed the highest variability in enzymatic activity.

By identifying and incorporating multiple markers pertinent to assessing the profile of honey quality, our study significantly enhances the practical relevance of the developed models by capturing real-world biochemical variation. Moreover, future studies should focus on expanding floral variety representation, elucidating unidentified sugar constituents, and validating these markers under broader conditions to further strengthen their applicability in honey authentication.
