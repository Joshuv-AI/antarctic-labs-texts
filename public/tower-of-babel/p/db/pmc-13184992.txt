Delayed Diagnosis of Unilateral Choanal Atresia in an Adult: A Case Report
Authors: Bimarsh Acharya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnilateral choanal atresia can remain undiagnosed until adulthood, often mimicking chronic sinusitis. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in adults with persistent unilateral nasal obstruction. Endoscopic canalization offers a highly effective, minimally invasive surgical solution to restore nasal patency and improve quality of life.

Introduction

Choanal atresia (CA) is a rare congenital anomaly defined by the lack of communication between the posterior nasal cavity and the nasopharynx, typically resulting from a bony, membranous, or mixed obstruction. Its estimated incidence ranges from 1 in 5000 to 8000 live births, with unilateral cases accounting for approximately 50%–60% of all affected individuals [1]. Most cases present as mixed bony‐membranous obstructions, whereas purely bony atresia is less common [2]. Bilateral choanal atresia is usually diagnosed shortly after birth due to neonatal respiratory distress, as newborns are obligate nasal breathers [3]. In contrast, unilateral choanal atresia often escapes early detection because the patent contralateral passage provides sufficient airflow, allowing affected individuals to compensate for years. Consequently, patients may present later in life with non‐specific symptoms such as persistent unilateral nasal obstruction, chronic rhinorrhea, or recurrent sinusitis, which are frequently misattributed to more common conditions like deviated nasal septum, allergic rhinitis, or chronic rhinosinusitis [4, 5]. Advances in imaging and nasal endoscopy have improved diagnostic accuracy; computed tomography (CT) of the paranasal sinuses delineates the anatomy of the atretic plate, while nasal endoscopy enables direct visualization and surgical planning [6, 7].

Despite these diagnostic advancements, unilateral choanal atresia remains an underrecognized entity in adult populations, often leading to prolonged diagnostic delays and suboptimal management. The present case describes a 29‐year‐old male with intermittent right‐sided nasal discharge and obstruction, along with left‐sided nasal dryness, whose symptoms were initially suggestive of septal deviation and sinusitis. Diagnostic nasal endoscopy revealed a left‐sided posterior bony obstruction with no visualization of the nasopharynx, and CT imaging confirmed unilateral choanal atresia associated with bilateral maxillary sinusitis and frontal sinus aplasia. The patient underwent successful endoscopic recanalization with temporary stenting and concurrent septoplasty, resulting in significant symptomatic improvement and maintained patency at one‐month follow‐up. Therefore, the aim of this case report is to highlight the diagnostic challenges of unilateral choanal atresia presenting in adulthood, emphasizing the role of nasal endoscopy and CT imaging, and to describe an effective minimally invasive surgical approach that restores nasal patency and improves quality of life.

Case History and Examination

A 29‐year‐old male presented to the outpatient department with complaints of intermittent right‐sided nasal discharge and nasal obstruction. He also reported dryness of the left side of the nose. The right‐sided nasal discharge was typically mucoid, non‐purulent, and occurred 2–3 times per week without seasonal variation. The left‐sided nasal dryness was described as a persistent sensation of mucosal cracking, without epistaxis. The patient reported that these symptoms had been present intermittently for approximately 12 years, beginning in late adolescence. He had previously tried intranasal corticosteroids and oral antihistamines for suspected allergic rhinitis, with minimal relief.

The patient denied any prior nasal surgery, facial trauma, or formal allergy testing. There was no history of fever, anosmia or hyposmia, postnasal drip, headache, itchy eyes, ear pain or discharge, reduced hearing, shortness of breath, chest pain, or altered sensorium. The patient had a history of hypertension for 3 years and was on regular antihypertensive medication (amlodipine 5 mg daily). There was no significant family or drug allergy history. Vital signs were within normal limits (blood pressure 118/76 mmHg, heart rate 72 bpm, respiratory rate 14/min, oxygen saturation 98% on room air).

Otoscopic examination revealed an intact tympanic membrane on the right side, while the left tympanic membrane could not be visualized due to canal tortuosity. Anterior rhinoscopy showed right‐sided deviation of the nasal septum. The left nasal cavity demonstrated inferior turbinate hypertrophy with mucoid discharge obscuring visualization. Oral examination revealed mouth opening greater than 3 cm with a temporomandibular distance of approximately 6 cm. No other systemic abnormalities were detected.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Differential Diagnoses Considered

Deviated nasal septum, chronic rhinosinusitis, nasal polyposis, allergic rhinitis, and congenital choanal atresia.

Investigations

Diagnostic nasal endoscopy was performed using a 0° rigid endoscope with a 4 mm diameter. The left nasal cavity revealed a deviated nasal septum with hypertrophied middle turbinate and an obstructed posterior bony wall with no visualization of the nasopharynx, suggestive of choanal atresia (Figure 1). The atretic plate in the left posterior choana appeared smooth, pale, and demonstrated a bony‐hard consistency on gentle palpation with a suction tip. Endoscopic examination of the right nasal cavity showed a deviated nasal septum with clear inferior and middle meatus and a normal opening into the nasopharynx (Figure 2).

Left nasal endoscopic view showing deviated nasal septum, hypertrophied middle turbinate, and a smooth posterior bony obstruction with no visualization of the nasopharynx, consistent with choanal atresia.

Right nasal endoscopic view demonstrating a deviated nasal septum with patent inferior and middle meatus and a normal opening into the nasopharynx.

A computed tomography scan of the paranasal sinuses (CT‐PNS) was performed for further evaluation. CT imaging was acquired using 1 mm axial slices with coronal and sagittal reconstructions. No intravenous contrast was administered. The atretic plate measured approximately 3.5 mm in thickness and was of mixed bony composition. The axial CT scan demonstrated left‐sided unilateral choanal atresia (Figure 3). In addition, inflammatory mucosal thickening of the maxillary sinus was noted bilaterally suggestive of sinusitis, along with aplasia of the frontal sinus.

Axial CT image of the paranasal sinuses showing left‐sided unilateral choanal atresia (atretic plate indicated by arrow), with bilateral maxillary sinus mucosal thickening and frontal sinus aplasia.

Allergic rhinitis was ruled out based on normal serum total IgE (35 IU/mL; reference < 100 IU/mL) and negative skin prick testing to common aeroallergens (dust mite, pollen, mold, animal dander). Chronic rhinosinusitis without nasal polyposis was considered less likely given the absence of anterior or posterior purulent discharge and the endoscopic finding of a complete posterior bony obstruction.

Treatment

The patient underwent septoplasty with endoscopic recanalization after clearance from the anesthesia department. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with oral endotracheal intubation. The nasal mucosa was decongested using cotton pledgets soaked with 1:100,000 epinephrine for 5 min. Using a 0° 4 mm rigid endoscope, the atretic plate was visualized in the left posterior choana. An ‘I’‐shaped incision was made on the medial aspect of the atretic plate using a sickle knife, extending from the 12 o'clock to the 6 o'clock position. A backbiting forceps and a 2.9 mm diamond burr were used to remove the bony atretic plate, creating an approximately 8 mm communication between the left nasal cavity and the nasopharynx. The edges were carefully preserved with mucosa lining. The choana was stented using a size 7 endotracheal tube, cut to appropriate length (approximately 3 cm), to maintain patency. The stent was secured transnasally with a 2–0 silk suture fixed to the nasal vestibule. Concurrent septoplasty was performed via a left hemitransfixion incision to correct the deviated nasal septum. Total operative time was 75 min, with estimated blood loss less than 50 mL.

Postoperative management included nasal decongestants (oxymetazoline twice daily for 5 days), broad‐spectrum antibiotics (amoxicillin‐clavulanate 625 mg three times daily for 7 days), analgesics (paracetamol 500 mg as needed), and antiseptic gargles. The stent was planned for removal at 4 weeks postoperatively. The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery and was discharged on day 2 after remaining hemodynamically stable.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient reported significant improvement in nasal breathing and complete resolution of right‐sided nasal discharge and left‐sided nasal dryness by the second postoperative week. At one‐month follow‐up, nasal endoscopic examination demonstrated maintained patency between the choana and nasopharynx without evidence of restenosis, granulation tissue, or synechiae. The patient was able to breathe comfortably through both nostrils and reported high satisfaction with the surgical outcome. Further follow‐up at 3, 6, and 12 months is planned to monitor for late restenosis.

Discussion

Choanal atresia is an uncommon congenital anomaly that may present with a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations depending on the laterality and severity of the obstruction. While bilateral choanal atresia is usually recognized immediately after birth due to respiratory distress, unilateral cases frequently remain undiagnosed for many years [1, 3]. The present case illustrates the potential for delayed diagnosis, as the patient presented in adulthood with longstanding nasal symptoms of 12 years' duration. This delay is consistent with previously published adult cases. For example, a 45‐year‐old woman with unilateral choanal atresia was misdiagnosed as having chronic sinusitis for over 20 years before endoscopic repair [8]. Similarly, a 34‐year‐old man presented with refractory unilateral nasal obstruction that had been treated unsuccessfully for allergic rhinitis for 15 years [9]. In another case series, the average age at diagnosis for unilateral choanal atresia was 22.4 years, with delays ranging from 5 to 31 years [10].

Delayed diagnosis in unilateral choanal atresia occurs primarily because the contralateral nasal cavity provides sufficient airflow, allowing patients to adapt to chronic obstruction. Consequently, symptoms such as persistent unilateral nasal obstruction, nasal discharge, and recurrent sinusitis are often attributed to more common conditions such as deviated nasal septum, allergic rhinitis, or chronic rhinosinusitis [5, 6]. This overlap in clinical presentation highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion when evaluating patients with long‐standing unilateral nasal symptoms that do not respond to conventional medical therapy.

Radiological imaging plays a crucial role in confirming the diagnosis and guiding surgical management. Computed tomography of the paranasal sinuses remains the imaging modality of choice because it clearly demonstrates the anatomical configuration of the atretic plate and associated sinonasal abnormalities [7]. In a study of 22 patients with choanal atresia, CT accurately identified the type (bony vs. membranous) and thickness of the atretic plate in all cases, which directly influenced surgical approach [11]. Diagnostic nasal endoscopy further aids in direct visualization of the posterior nasal cavity and facilitates surgical planning [12]. In the present case, both CT imaging and nasal endoscopy were essential for establishing the diagnosis and determining the appropriate surgical approach.

Surgical repair remains the definitive treatment for choanal atresia. The use of postoperative stenting remains controversial. Some surgeons advocate stent placement to maintain patency and reduce the risk of restenosis, while others report similar outcomes without stenting [13]. Potential complications associated with stents include infection, granulation tissue formation, mucosal injury, and patient discomfort. A meta‐analysis by Strychowsky et al. demonstrated comparable success rates between stented and non‐stented repairs (success rates 86% vs. 84%, respectively), suggesting that the decision should be individualized based on intraoperative findings and surgeon preference [13]. Adjunctive therapies such as topical mitomycin‐C have also been explored as a strategy to reduce restenosis by inhibiting fibroblast proliferation and scar formation [14, 15]. However, the current evidence regarding its routine use remains inconclusive, and further studies are required to determine its long‐term benefits.

Historically, transpalatal approaches were commonly used because they provided wide exposure of the atretic plate; however, these procedures were associated with increased operative morbidity, palatal fistulas, and longer recovery times [16]. Over the past two decades, the endoscopic transnasal approach has become the preferred surgical technique due to improved visualization, reduced tissue trauma, and favorable postoperative outcomes [12, 16]. Several studies have reported high success rates (90%–95%) with endoscopic techniques, particularly when combined with careful postoperative monitoring. Predictive factors for success after endoscopic repair include the thickness of the atretic plate (< 4 mm associated with better outcomes), use of mucosal preservation techniques, and the presence of associated anomalies [17]. Compared with traditional approaches, endoscopic repair offers superior outcomes with less morbidity [18].

This case also highlights important considerations in surgical planning. Adult patients with unilateral choanal atresia generally have well‐developed sinonasal anatomy, which can facilitate endoscopic access compared with neonates. However, associated anatomical variations such as septal deviation may influence surgical management. In our patient, concurrent septoplasty was performed to optimize surgical access and improve postoperative nasal airflow. A similar combined approach has been reported in 30% of adult unilateral choanal atresia cases in a recent case series [10].

Nevertheless, this report has certain limitations inherent to single case studies. The findings cannot be generalized to all patients with choanal atresia, and long‐term outcomes beyond the early postoperative period were not assessed. Future studies involving larger patient populations and longer follow‐up are needed to better evaluate surgical outcomes and recurrence rates, which have been reported to range from 5% to 15% after endoscopic repair [13, 17].

Overall, this case emphasizes the importance of considering congenital structural anomalies in adults presenting with persistent unilateral nasal obstruction. Early recognition with nasal endoscopy and appropriate imaging allows timely surgical intervention and favorable clinical outcomes.
