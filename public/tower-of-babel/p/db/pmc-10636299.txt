The ties that bind. Disordered linkers underpin carboxysome construction
Authors: Sacha B. Pulsford, Nghiem D. Nguyen, Benedict M. Long
Published: 2023

Carboxysomes are critically important cellular compartments where as much as 25% of global CO2 assimilation occurs (1, 2). These proteinaceous “organelles” operate within the bacterial CO2 concentrating mechanism (CCM) to promote highly efficient carbon fixation by increasing the local CO2 concentration at the site of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco), the prime enzyme for CO2 capture into the biosphere (3). In these systems, cellular HCO3− is first accumulated via membrane-bound HCO3− transporters, before diffusing into carboxysomes. Here, a specialised carbonic anhydrase (CA), colocalised with Rubisco, rapidly converts HCO3− to CO2, achieving near saturation of Rubisco sites (Fig. 1A). This maximises carboxylase activity while competitively inhibiting off-target oxygenation reactions. Notably, a complete absence of cytosolic CAs is essential for the activity of this CCM to prevent dissipation of the elevated HCO3− pool. There are two convergent carboxysome types (α and β) in bacterial lineages (1). Both are highly complicated megacomplexes (~100 to 1,000 MDa) containing sets of subcomplexes that are coordinated to enable finely tuned function. Each carboxysome type encapsulates FormI Rubisco enzymes (FormIA in α-carboxysomes and FormIB in β-carboxysomes) but very different CA enzymes. While some detail about CA incorporation is known within the β-type (4–6), little is understood of the processes in the α-type. In a recent report, Blikstad et al. (7) provide fine detail of the mechanism that leads to inclusion of the CA found in α-carboxysomes (CsoSCA) of the model proteobacterium Halothiobacillus neapolitanus, revealing unexpected protein interactions.

(A) The bacterial CCM utilizes membrane-bound pumps to elevate cytoplasmic bicarbonate (HCO3−) concentrations that diffuses into carboxysomes. These house a specialized carboxysomal CA (CsoSCA) that raises CO2 concentrations around the primary cargo enzyme Rubisco to maximize carboxylation. The linker protein CsoS2 plays a role in connecting the luminal cargo to the protein shell (8). (B) Blikstad et al. (7) reveal how CsoSCA is encapsulated into carboxysomes, interlinking to form complexes with Rubisco via a SLiM that interacts with a cleft between Rubisco large subunits, at a similar location to a SLiM in CsoS2 (PDB IDs: 6uew, 7snv).

CsoSCA was initially identified as a minor shell component (CsoS = Carboxysome Shell) of α-carboxysomes. This was based on immuno-gold localization of the protein to the periphery of the carboxysome in transmission electron-microscope preparations (9), and its association with the precipitable “shell” fraction upon freeze-fracture (10). Though CsoSCA was found to have CA activity relatively early in its discovery, its sequence-level divergence meant accurate classification as a novel sub-family of the β-CA class only occurred after structural characterisation of the core catalytic domain (9–11). Redox sensitivity has been documented in some isoforms (10), with recent studies suggesting an oxidation requirement and dependence on RuBP (the organic substrate for Rubisco) for function in a cyanobacterial form (12).

In a previous α-carboxysome interaction study there was no observed link between CsoSCA and any other carboxysome protein, obscuring the encapsulation mechanism of this key enzyme (13). However, in that study, protein interactions were assessed on a single-partner polypeptide basis, and not in the presence of complete carboxysome protein subcomplexes. In contrast, Blikstad et al. (7) used biolayer interferometry on intact carboxysome components to determine CsoSCA interacting partners. The Rubisco holoenzyme was the lone protein that bound CsoSCA, with binding dependent on the CsoSCA’s unique disordered N-terminal domain (NTD).

The criticality of carboxysome function in CCMs is emphasised by the fact that many genetic lesions in carboxysome genes lead to high CO2-requiring (HCR) phenotypes in the organisms they support (1). Blikstad et al. (7) show that deletion of the csoSCA gene of H. neapolitanus leads to an HCR phenotype that can then be rescued by expression of the full-length protein. They also show that expression of truncated forms of CsoSCA missing elements of the NTD leads to a loss of cell growth at air, consistent with NTD coordinating encapsulation during biogenesis. Blikstad et al. (7) then confirm the NTD role in CsoSCA recruitment by using the region to target NTD-fluorescent protein fusions to carboxysomes in vivo.

The results from Blikstad et al. add to a growing set of discoveries in which Rubisco-partner binding sits at the core of Rubisco compartment construction, and possible evolutionary trajectories delineating CCM function and emergence.

To derive greater detail of the Rubisco–CsoSCA interaction, Blikstad et al. (7) use cryo-EM to solve a 1.98 Å structure of an NTD peptide bound to the Rubisco holoenzyme. CsoSCA NTD binds to the cleft between Rubisco large subunits (Fig. 1B) primarily through hydrogen bonds via a short linear motif (SLiM). This solves a recent conundrum in which attempts to build a hybrid α-β carboxysome failed to recruit the α-carboxysome CA (14). In that study, the FormIB Rubisco (related to that found in C3 crop plants) from a β-carboxysome was incorporated within an α-carboxysome shell. Blikstad et al. (7) provide the underlying principles of why such an interaction would not work without detailed modification of the Rubisco–CsoSCA interface in hybrid systems.

Surprisingly, this structure reveals that CsoSCA binds the FormIA Rubisco complex at the same site as the key carboxysome scaffolding protein, CsoS2 (Fig. 1B), although via a different SLiM and binding mechanism. CsoS2 is critical for carboxysome assembly, interacting with both shell proteins and Rubisco, providing a mechanism for Rubisco encapsulation (15) and acting as a “molecular thread” to link component proteins (8). Blikstad et al. (7) suggest that the amount of CsoSCA recruited into the carboxysome is likely regulated by relative protein expression rather than by binding site competition with CsoS2. However, unlike CsoS2, the CsoSCA NTD has a very low degree of conservation across α-carboxysome forming genera. Indeed, the Rubisco-binding SLiM of CsoSCA is not conserved across CsoSCA homologues (7). That both CsoSCA and CsoS2 SLiMs can bind this pocket raises interesting questions about the biophysical properties of these interactions and the promiscuity-specificity trade-off implicit to binding Rubisco at this site.

Given the criticality of CsoSCA to CCM function, a conserved encapsulation mechanism seems parsimonious. If we assume Rubisco–CA associations are conserved despite the lack of a clear motif, further clarifying the properties that underpin this apparent promiscuity will be critical to understand this interaction—primarily through characterising Rubisco–CA complexes in related families. Intrinsically disordered proteins, like the CsoSCA NTD, evolve at different rates and under different constraints to traditional globular domains, thus elucidating their emergence and diversification is challenging (16). However, as methods develop, mining CsoSCA NTD sequences for detectable patterns will be illuminating. This will be of interest for engineering objectives and to further understand how cargo is encapsulated into carboxysomes and other bacterial microcompartments more broadly.

The revelation that CsoSCA interacts directly and solely with the carboxysome luminal cargo, Rubisco, undermines its previous characterization as a shell component. The initial association of CsoSCA with the shell allowed for modelled function of the carboxysome whereby cytoplasmic HCO3− was directly converted into CO2 upon entry to the organelle via the shell-attached CA (17). This was also consistent with existing structural models for β-carboxysomes where there were known interactions between the Rubisco–CA supercomplex and outer shell proteins (4, 6). However, these models are inconsistent with the likely rapid escape of CO2 from peripherally associated CA (CO2 would have greater opportunity to diffuse away from Rubisco before being fixed), and homogeneous arrangements of Rubisco and CA offer a better explanation of function (18). Indeed, evidence now supports this internally distributed arrangement of Rubisco and CA in β-carboxysomes (19). The mechanism of Rubisco–CA interaction described by Blikstad et al. (7) provides greater clarity of this arrangement, conceptually dissociates CsoSCA from the shell and refines our understanding of Rubisco–CA supercomplex formation within α-carboxysomes. While evidence that CsoSCA might interact with the shell cannot be rejected at this point, the data provided by Blikstad et al. (7) offer new avenues for investigation.

Blikstad et al. (7) also provide evidence that the CsoSCA of H. neapolitanus may form a homohexamer, unlike the initial crystal structure describing a dimer (11). While further confirmation is needed to support this in vivo, the result is supported by recent evidence that the homologue from the cyanobacterium Cyanobium forms hexamers in vitro (12). This hints at the potential for CsoSCA to increase its valency and opportunity to bind Rubisco through oligomerisation. This also speaks to the evolution of Rubisco–CA complexes and the question of whether they could lead, independently, to enhanced Rubisco carboxylation over the free enzyme in evolutionary trajectories (20). Simple analysis would suggest a stand-alone Rubisco–CA complex would not allow efficient CO2 capture due to the rapid loss of CA-generated CO2. However, a Rubisco–CA condensate, which this structure suggests has the required valency to form, might offer the required molecular environment to enhance CO2 localization and capture. Such a complex could comprise a key intermediary state in carboxysome and Rubisco evolution (20).

The results from Blikstad et al. (7) add to a growing set of discoveries in which Rubisco-partner binding sits at the core of Rubisco compartment construction (4–6, 15) and possible evolutionary trajectories delineating CCM function and emergence. Knowing how and where CA interacts with its binding partner also informs efforts to reconstruct this binding synthetically, as there is potential for improved fixation with just a Rubisco–CA condensate (20). The construction of α-carboxysome-based CCMs in crop plants has enormous potential to improve photosynthetic efficiency and yield (14). The findings of Blikstad et al. (7) are a key step toward our understanding of how this might be achieved.
