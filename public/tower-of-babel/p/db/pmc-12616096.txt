DIRAS3-derived cyclic peptides disrupt KRAS-RAF interaction and KRAS nanoclustering, suppressing KRAS-driven pancreatic and ovarian tumors
Authors: Joshua P. Gray, Gamze Bildik, Ha-Neul Kim, Margie N. Sutton, Jing Wang, Amarachi O. Osuji, Nefeli Batistatou, Hailing Yang, Weiqun Mao, Zhi Tan, Geneviève M.C. Gasmi-Seabrook, Junchen Liu, John F. Hancock, Christopher B. Marshall, Joshua A. Kritzer, Mitsuhiko Ikura, Robert C. Bast, Steven W. Millward, Zhen Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMutations in KRAS drive 88% of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas (PDAC) and up to 40% of low-grade serous ovarian cancers (LGSOC), making KRAS a long-standing therapeutic target. We previously showed that DIRAS3 binds RAS, forming heterodimers that disrupt RAS clustering and downstream MAPK signaling. Building on this, we developed conformationally constrained DIRAS3-derived peptides using two cyclization strategies and characterized them by NMR and biolayer interferometry. These cyclic peptides attenuate the interaction between KRAS and the BRAF RAS-binding domain, penetrate cells efficiently, and inhibit KRAS nanoclustering on the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane. Functionally, they reduce cell viability and suppress PDAC and LGSOC growth in cell culture and xenograft models. Our findings demonstrate that DIRAS3-based cyclic peptides represent a distinct strategy to directly inhibit oncogenic KRAS signaling and provide a promising framework for therapeutic development in KRAS-driven cancers.

Introduction

Mutations in the KRAS oncogene are associated with 88% of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas (PDAC), up to 40% of low-grade serous ovarian cancers, as well as a significant portion of colorectal cancers and non-small cell lung cancers.1,2,3,4 Oncogenic mutations elevate the GTP-bound, activated state of KRAS, resulting in the constitutive activation of downstream effectors. The RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating various cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, and survival.

The activation of RAS primarily takes place at the plasma membrane (PM). Mutant KRAS, particularly KRASG12D and KRASG12V, form nanoclusters at the PM, which consist of unique combinations of RAS oligomers and lipids. These RAS nanoclusters serve as focal points for effector recruitment and signaling.5,6,7,8 By concentrating multiple RAS molecules within a compact area of approximately 300 nm2 on the PM,6,9,10,11 nanoclustering enhances the likelihood of RAS-effector interactions. Recent research shows that the nanoclustering of KRAS on the inner leaflet of the PM intensifies downstream RAS signaling.12,13 Disrupting KRAS clustering not only hampers RAS signaling but also impedes cancer cell growth, underscoring the significance of RAS nanoclusters as a potential therapeutic target.

The interaction between RAF and membrane-bound, activated RAS initiates the activation of the RAS-MAPK signaling pathway.14,15 The activation of RAF kinase results in the phosphorylation of MEK kinase, which goes on to phosphorylate ERK kinase. Ultimately, activated ERK kinases are transported into the nucleus, where they phosphorylate various substrates, including transcription factors. Mutations in RAS that result in the constant activation of the MAPK pathway are prevalent in human cancers. Strategies that aim to prevent RAS-mediated RAF activation are being developed to treat RAS-driven cancers.16

DIRAS3, an endogenous RAS inhibitor, is a 26 KDa small GTPase protein that shares 50–60% homology with the classical RAS family members (H-, N-, and K-RAS).17 Unlike classical RAS proteins, DIRAS3 possesses a distinct 34 amino acid N-terminal extension, which undergoes myristoylation and is required for its inhibition of RAS function.18 Similar to RAS, DIRAS3 undergoes prenylation at a C-terminal CAAX site, exhibits high-affinity binding to GTP, displays modest intrinsic GTPase activity, and relies on membrane association for its biological activity.19 Our previous study demonstrated that DIRAS3 directly binds to RAS, forms DIRAS3-RAS heterodimers, and disrupts RAS clustering and Raf kinase activity.20 The DIRAS3-RAS interaction blocks downstream MAPK signaling and inhibits cancer cell growth in PDAC and ovarian cancer cell lines driven by different KRAS mutations.21

Despite recent advancements in developing small molecules and synthetic peptides to target RAS directly,22,23 along with the emergence of acquired resistance, the challenge of producing high-affinity binders with promising cellular activity across the broad spectrum of mutant KRAS-driven cancers persists. Consequently, there is an urgent need for next-generation agents capable of blockading aberrant KRAS activity in human tumors. In the present study, we further investigated the interaction between DIRAS3 and KRAS, as well as the role of DIRAS3 in inhibiting mutant KRAS. To achieve this, we designed a cyclic peptide based on a sequence spanning parts of α5 and the hypervariable region of DIRAS3, leveraging knowledge-based hot spot prediction.24 Our findings reveal that conformationally constrained, DIRAS3-derived peptides bind KRAS with mid-micromolar affinity and effectively inhibit its interaction with RAF in cell culture. Furthermore, these cyclic peptides demonstrate remarkable cytosolic access, enabling the inhibition of KRAS homodimer formation and disruption of nanoclustering in cell culture models. This results in the significant inhibition of phospho-ERK, reduced cell viability, and suppression of tumor growth in xenografts of human pancreatic cancer.

Results

The DIRAS3 stapled peptide binds KRAS(G12V) in both GDP- and GTP-bound forms

Previous studies by our research group indicated that DIRAS3 disrupts KRAS clustering via direct interactions involving the α5 and hypervariable regions of KRAS.20 To further delineate the precise DIRAS3/KRAS binding sequence, we employed a knowledge-based hot spot prediction method to generate a linear peptide sequence (194 MLLNYKKKPT 203) representing the junction of DIRAS3 α5 and hypervariable regions24 (Figures 1A and S1). As Leu-195 and Pro-202 residues were not predicted to directly contribute to the binding interaction between DIRAS3 and KRAS, we substituted these with L-cysteine residues (Compound 1, Figure 1B) to permit cyclization through the chemical crosslinking of the thiol side chains (Compounds 2 and 3, Figures 1C and 1D). The N-terminal methionine (M194) was replaced by the structural analog L-norleucine to eliminate the potential for the oxidation of the side chain thioether. An all-D-isomer version of Compound 2 was synthesized as a control peptide for cell- and animal-based studies. An allyl-capped variant (Compound 4, Figure 1E) was synthesized as a non-cyclized linear control for nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy studies. The variant cyclized with a bis-phenyl linker (Compound 2) was obtained in high yield under standard conditions and was used in both cell culture and xenograft models.25 However, this analog and its inactive all-D control showed poor solubility at mid-micromolar concentrations. Consequently, we synthesized a second variant (Compound 3, Figure 1D) with a decane crosslink between the cysteine side chains. Compared to the bis-phenyl variant, this compound was significantly more challenging to produce (∼6% yield) but showed much improved aqueous solubility. Similar patterns were observed in the NMR titrations of the 15N-labeled KRAS(G12V)-GDP (BMRB: 27718) with the DIRAS3 stapled peptide (Compound 3).26 The intensity of cross-peaks corresponding to specific residues (e.g., Q25, D33, E37, S39, D57, S74, and K169) of KRAS(G12V)-GDP progressively decreased upon the addition of Compound 3, but not Compound 4 (titrated up to a molar ratio of 1:8). Four of the residues (Q25, D33, E37, S39) exhibiting significant peak broadening upon Compound 3 treatment are in the KRAS Switch I region, with two (D57, T74) in the nearby Switch II region, and one (K169) in the hypervariable region (Figures 3A–3D). Full-length DIRAS3 was previously shown to bind to both KRAS and HRAS.20 To investigate whether Compound 3 is selective for the KRAS isoform or potentially has broader activity against other isoforms, we examined 1H-15N HSQC spectra of an activated mutant of a third RAS isoform, NRAS(Q61K)-GTP, in the presence and absence of Compound 3 (Figure S6). Compound 3 induced several CSPs in the NRAS(Q61K)-GTP spectrum, indicating that binding occurs, and further, the pattern of CSPs strongly resembles those observed in KRAS(G12V)-GTP, supporting that it binds both isoforms through the same mode. Given that Compound 3 is derived from DIRAS3 (α5 and HVR), we investigated the binding of purified DIRAS3 protein containing this region to 15N-KRAS(G12V)-GDP using NMR and compared the spectral changes to those induced by Compound 3. For this comparison, we used a previously designed truncated mutant DIRAS3 construct (C75S/C80S, residues 35–207; hereafter referred to as DIRAS3-ΔNC), comprising the GTPase domain and part of the hypervariable region, extending slightly beyond the peptide sequence represented by Compound 3 (Figure S7A). The mutation of two cysteine residues to serine and truncations improved the stability and solubility of DIRAS3.27 The HSQC spectra revealed a broadening of the same core residues that exhibited significant peak broadening upon Compound 3 incorporation (Q25, D33, E37, S39, D57, S74, and K169) as well as some additional residues (G12, D30, D54, E62 and L133), most of which map to the same KRAS surface (Figure S7B–S7F). This indicates that Compound 3 and DIRAS3-ΔNC bind to KRAS(G12V) in a similar manner, with a potentially larger interaction interface involved for the protein. These data also suggest that cyclization dramatically enhances peptide binding, which is consistent with a reduction in the conformational entropy of the peptide.28,29

Next, we employed a biolayer interferometry (BLI) assay to assess the relative binding affinity between KRAS(G12V) and Compounds 3 and 4. In this assay, HIS-tagged KRAS(G12V) loaded with GMPPNP (non-hydrolyzable GTP analog) was immobilized on HIS1K biosensors, and sensorgrams were generated by exposing these biosensors to increasing concentrations (ranging from 6.2 μM to 100 μM) of either Compound 3 or Compound 4 (Figures S8A and S8B). Our analysis yielded a dissociation constant (KD) value of 80 μM for the cyclized Compound 3, whereas no binding was detected for the linear Compound 4 (Figure S8C). Under the same conditions, the inactive form of HIS-KRAS(G12V)-GDP exhibited a similar KD value (100 μM) to Compound 3 (Figure S8D). This finding is in line with the NMR results, indicating that Compound 3 binds to KRAS in solution in a manner that is dependent on cyclization, regardless of nucleotide-bound state.

DIRAS3 stapled peptide (compound 3) disrupts the interaction between KRAS(G12V) and BRAF-RBD

Mapping the residues of KRAS(G12V)-GTP that exhibited chemical shift perturbations upon titration with Compound 3 (highlighted in red) suggests a binding site near Switch I (Figure 4A), which may overlap with the RBD-binding site based on a crystal structure of BRAF-RBD in a complex with KRAS(G12V) loaded with GMPPNP (PDB: 6XHA) (Figure 4A). AlphaFold 330 was used to predict structural models of the DIRAS3 peptide interaction with KRAS. The KRAS4b and DIRAS3-derived peptide (194–203) sequences were submitted to AlphaFold 3 (with GTP and Mg2+), which predicted models in which the DIRAS3 peptide interacts with KRAS Switch I with some antiparallel beta-strand properties (Figure 4A). The peptide extends the KRAS beta sheet in a binding mode resembling KRAS interaction with RBD. All five models generated by AlphaFold 3 converged and were consistent with the KRAS CSPs induced by the peptide (which were not submitted as restraints). AlphaFold 3 is not enabled for the submission of a stapled peptide. Thus, we investigated whether the binding of Compound 3 to KRAS(G12V)-GMPPNP impacts its interactions with effector proteins. To address this question, we conducted BLI experiments in which GST-fused BRAF-RBD was immobilized on a biosensor, and the binding of KRAS(G12V)-GMPPNP was assessed in the presence of increasing concentrations of Compound 3 (Figure 4B). To assess the consistency of the binding signals, the GST-tagged BRAF-RBD immobilized biosensor was immersed in four different wells, each containing the same concentration of KRAS(G12V)-GMPPNP (5 μM), and reproducible binding responses were obtained for all four wells. Interestingly, the addition of Compound 3 to the wells decreased these binding signals in a dose-dependent manner. In contrast, the linear Compound 4 showed no significant effect on the interaction between KRAS(G12V)-GMPPNP and the RBD (Figures 4C and 4D), which is consistent with the NMR and BLI studies that did not detect an interaction between Compound 4 and KRAS(G12V). These findings indicate that the DIRAS3 stapled peptide (Compound 3) reduces the binding of BRAF-RBD to KRAS(G12V). KRAS-binding cyclic peptides exhibit rapid penetration into cancer cells, effectively inhibiting mutant KRAS(G12V) clustering

To assess the impact of cyclization on membrane permeability and cytosolic access, we employed the chloroalkane penetration assay (CAPA).29 In this assay, cells expressing cytosolic HaloTag protein are pulsed with a chloroalkane-tagged molecule (ct-molecule) (Figure S9) followed by a chase with chloroalkane-tagged dye (ct-dye). HaloTag forms an irreversible covalent bond with the chloroalkane tag, so the extent to which the ct-molecule penetrates into the cytosol during the pulse step can be measured as a decrease in the amount of ct-dye retained in the chase step. CAPA is typically performed using serial dilutions of the ct-molecule in the pulse step, so a curve can be fit to the data and a CP50 value can be calculated, representing the concentration ct-molecule that can block 50% of cytosolic HaloTag. CAPA analysis revealed that Compound 2 and its all-D analog exhibited greater cytosolic penetration compared to Compound 1 (linear control) (Figure 5A). This enhancement is consistent with previous studies where cyclization imparted enhanced cell permeability relative to a linear sequence.28,29 The CP50 values were 2.0 and 5.3 μM for Compound 2 and its all-D analog, respectively, indicating that both compounds access the cytosol at comparable efficiencies (Figure 5B). While not as penetrant as the poly-arginine control (CP50: 0.03 μM), a low micromolar CP50 suggests sufficient intracellular delivery at pharmacokinetically achievable concentrations, demonstrating comparable cell penetration to Tat (CP50: 2.5).31 The cytosolic penetration of Compound 2 was confirmed through immunofluorescence experiments, which revealed strong cytoplasmic staining along with a punctate pattern (Figure S10A). This pattern suggests an endosomal uptake mechanism consistent with known modes of cell penetration for cyclic peptides. Chloroalkane-tagged versions of Compounds 2 and 3 were found to be stable in HeLa cell lysates for up to 24 h, in contrast to chloroalkane-tagged Compound 4 (an allyl-capped version of Compound 1), which was rapidly degraded within 4 h (Figure S10B). In addition to confirming that the cytosolic uptake of Compound 2 is not an artifact of degradation, these data suggest that cyclization dramatically enhances metabolic stability relative to the linear precursor. It is important to note that while CAPA is a powerful tool for assessing cytosolic penetration, the addition of the chloroalkane tag itself can influence permeability depending on the chemical nature and size of the molecule being tested. As previously reported by the Kritzer group,29,32 the tag has minimal effect on the penetration properties of larger, cell-penetrant peptides, whereas it can significantly alter permeability for smaller compounds that rely on passive diffusion, as demonstrated in Huh et al.33 Given that our compound is a macrocyclic peptide, the addition of the chloroalkane tag is not expected to substantially impact its intrinsic permeability. Our recent study has highlighted that full-length DIRAS3 binds directly to KRAS, forms DIRAS3-KRAS heterodimers, and disrupts KRAS clustering. This disruption leads to the inhibition of downstream MAPK signaling, ultimately reducing cancer cell growth.20 To further validate the importance of DIRAS3 α5 and the hypervariable region in inhibiting KRAS, we employed the ReBiL system, a recombinase-enhanced bimolecular luciferase complementation platform (Figure S11). This system enables the detection of weak protein-protein interactions (PPI) in living cells34 and was utilized to detect interactions between DIRAS3 peptides and KRAS.

As the linear version of the DIRAS3 peptide showed reduced cell penetration and proteolytic instability (Figures 5 and S10B), we selected the D-form of Compound 2 (Compound 2-D) as a negative control for cell-based experiments. Our findings revealed that 25–50 μM Compound 2, but not Compound 2-D, reduced the bioluminescence generated by KRAS multimers by approximately 50% (Figures 6A and 6B). Although the tested concentrations do not fully capture the dose-response relationship since it is not possible to define the curve with only two concentrations, these results provide proof of effect for Compound 2, demonstrating its biological activity. Furthermore, we evaluated gold-labeled nanoclustering on the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane in HeLa expressing GFP-KRASG12V treated with 50 μM Compound 2 and Compound 2-D, using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) coupled with spatial statistics of univariate K-function, expressed as L(r)-r. The results demonstrated a striking difference in the spatial distribution of GFP-KRASG12V-labeled gold nanoparticles analyzed with and without the cyclic peptide. Specifically, the presence of Compound 2, but not Compound 2-D, markedly disrupted the plasma membrane localization, quantified by the gold labeling density, and clustering of GFP-KRASG12, quantified by the maximal value (Lmax) of the L(r)-r function (Figures 6C–6E). Treatment with DIRAS3-derived cyclic peptide effectively inhibits mutant KRAS-mediated MEK/ERK signaling and suppresses the growth of pancreatic and ovarian cancer cells and a PDAC xenograft

Compound 2 efficiently disrupted KRAS multimers and nanoclustering, prompting an investigation into whether downstream KRAS signaling is affected. To assess this, the phosphorylation of ERK (p-ERK) was examined by Western blot analysis in the AsPC-1 pancreatic cancer cell line (harboring a KRASG12D mutation) and the HCC5075 low-grade ovarian cancer cell line (harboring a KRASG12V mutation) following treatment with Compound 2 and its inactive all-D control peptide. Compound 2 treatment led to a dose-dependent inhibition of p-ERK in both cell lines, with a significant decrease observed at concentrations as low as 10 μM (Figures 7A and 7B). Additionally, we evaluated whether DIRAS3 peptides could inhibit the growth of pancreatic and ovarian cancer with KRAS mutations. Treatment with Compound 2 significantly inhibited growth in both the KRASG12D mutant AsPC-1 cells and the KRASG12V mutant HCC5075 cells, while Compound 2-D showed minimal effect in either cell line (Figure 7C). Moreover, when assessing the effect of Compound 2 on tumor cell growth in vivo using a KRASG12D mutant pancreatic cancer xenograft model, we found that the mean tumor volume in the Compound 2 treatment group (574 ± 386 mm3) was significantly lower than that in either the Compound 2-D control group (1182 ± 773 mm3, Tukey’s HSD p < 0.0001) or the saline control group (1322 ± 497 mm3, Tukey’s HSD p < 0.0001) (Figures 7D and S12). Discussion

DIRAS3, an endogenous physiological RAS inhibitor, significantly impedes mutant KRAS signaling in pancreatic and low-grade ovarian cancers. Our study identified a DIRAS3-derived cyclic peptide (Compound 3) that binds the switch I region of KRAS with modest affinity and reduces BRAF-RBD effector binding in vitro. A more synthetically tractable variant (Compound 2) was shown to readily access the cytosol and disrupt nanoclustering on the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane. This resulted in the robust inhibition of KRAS signaling, reduced cell viability, and increased cell death in pancreatic cancer cells and xenografts expressing mutant KRAS. The originally proposed binding mode of the stapled peptide as a helix docking on the α4/α5 surface suggested that it would impair nanoclustering by spoiling the reported KRAS α4/α5 “dimerization” interface; however, the experimentally determined switch I binding site is remote from this α4/α5 interface. We previously showed that mutant KRAS has a second “self-association” interface near switch I.35 KRAS GTPase domains require more than one self-association interface to form higher-order “oligomers;” thus, we proposed that the second interface mediates assembly of KRAS “dimers” into nanoclusters. While it has been argued that the driving force for the formation of KRAS nanoclusters is HVR-lipid interactions, GTPase domains within nanoclusters interact with each other, and docking of molecules that spoil these interactions can disrupt nanoclustering.36 The mechanism underlying the inhibitory effect of Compound 2 on nanocluster formation may involve disrupting self-association through the second interface near switch I. Importantly, KRAS self-association, particularly through the second interface, is transient and of low affinity; thus, the modest affinity of the stapled peptides could be sufficient to interfere with nanoclustering. Our findings suggest that the development of DIRAS3 stapled peptides could offer a distinct therapeutic approach to target mutant KRAS-driven cancers, not only in PDAC but also in various other cancer types such as lung and colon cancers, where DIRAS3 is downregulated.3,37

The larger size of peptides relative to small molecules allows them to adapt to the large, shallow surfaces that mediate protein-protein interactions (PPIs). Unfortunately, the size and chemical complexity of linear peptides limit access to the cytosol, where many therapeutically relevant targets reside. Moreover, linear peptides are readily degraded by intracellular and extracellular proteases, further reducing their utility for cancer therapy. Covalent cyclization (rather than redox-labile disulfide bonds) provides a relatively straightforward strategy for converting linear peptide candidates to lead compounds for therapeutic development.38,39,40 While this strategy has enjoyed broad success, it also carries the risk of disrupting the favorable conformation(s) of the linear peptide. In this study, we observe that full-length DIRAS3 and a cyclic peptide derived from the α5 and hypervariable regions of DIRAS3 bind to mutant KRAS with approximately the same affinity and binding mode. This is notable given the relatively flexible aliphatic linker chosen for cyclization (Compound 3). Our original model of the DIRAS3 was helical (Figure S1), although the Alphafold model of DIRAS3 predicts that the peptide comprises the last turn of helix 5 followed by an intrinsically disordered region. Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy of Compounds 3 and 4 is more reminiscent of a random coil than a helical structure (Figure S13). CD spectra of the stapled versus linear peptides (Compounds 3 and 4, respectively) exhibit differences in the position of their minima, indicating a structural transition upon cyclization, although it is difficult to confidently assign a defined secondary structure to the cyclic compound. This, combined with the poor binding of the linear compound, suggests that the conformational constraint supplied by the decane linker is sufficient to recapitulate the structure of the peptide in the context of full-length DIRAS3, although additional structural studies are required to validate this conclusion.

Many endogenous biochemical signaling proteins (e.g., SH3 domains), interact with their binding partners with modest (low micromolar) affinities but high specificity.41 This allows for a dynamic yet selective response to a given stimulus. Our observations on the binding between full-length DIRAS3 and KRAS (Figure S1) indicate that the affinity of this interaction is relatively modest, suggesting that DIRAS3 may act to fine-tune KRAS signaling by disrupting KRAS membrane nanoclusters and impeding KRAS-RAF interactions. This regulatory role appears to be spatially enhanced, as the interaction between DIRAS3 and KRAS is likely promoted when both are confined to a two-dimensional membrane surface.

There have been many attempts to develop ligands that selectively bind mutant KRAS or GTP-bound KRAS vs. GDP-bound KRAS.22,42,43,44,45,46,47,48 In this work, we developed a cyclic peptide based on an endogenous RAS-binding motif that binds to multiple RAS isoforms and does not appear to distinguish between GTP- and GDP-bound states. It has a slightly higher affinity for GTP-loaded KRAS, which is likely mediated by the nucleotide-dependent differences in the structure and dynamics of SwI that enable KRAS-GTP interactions with multiple effector proteins in slightly different conformations, which may be favourable for interaction with the peptide. The binding affinity of this peptide is modest (∼80 μM for KRAS-GTP), yet it effectively inhibits KRAS multimerization and downstream signaling at 25–50 μM in cellulo and at 15 m.p.k in vivo.

Although precisely relating CP50 and intracellular concentration is challenging, previous work by Deprey & Kritzer32 suggests that one can calculate the upper bound of the intracellular concentration at a given exogenous concentration using a simple equation relating the CP50, the cell volume, and the cell number. In the case of Compound 2 (CP50 ∼2 μM), this calculation yields an approximate intracellular concentration of 250 μM when the compound is administered at 50 μM, which would be sufficient to bind ∼75% of intracellular KRAS (simple one-site binding model). This may overestimate KRAS occupancy since Compound 2, unlike its chloroalkane-tagged counterpart, has no intrinsic intracellular trapping mechanism. This being said, we do observe ∼75% reduction in pERK/ERK in AsPC-1 and HCC5075 cells (Figure 7B) and ∼50% reduction in KRAS dimerization in U20S cells (Figure 6B) after treatment with 50 μM Compound 2. Quantitatively similar effects on cell viability (Figure 7C) and KRAS clustering (Figures 6D and 6E) are also observed, consistent with this rough estimate of intracellular receptor occupancy. Ultimately, KRAS inhibitor potency can be viewed as a function of affinity, stability, and cytosolic access. For macrocyclic DIRAS3-derived peptides, inhibitor potency appears to be driven less by KRAS affinity and more by target access and metabolic stability (Figure S10B).

Plasma membrane localization and clustering of KRAS are critical for its biological functions.6,49,50 RAS clustering has emerged as an area of interest both as a mechanism of RAS function and as a potential target for anticancer therapies. While RAS isoforms share a highly conserved G-domain, their C-terminal hypervariable regions (HVRs) exhibit substantial divergence. The HVR undergoes modifications that facilitate membrane anchoring, enabling KRAS-membrane interactions, formation of nanoclusters, and activation of downstream signaling pathways. Through ReBiL and TEM techniques, we demonstrated that a cyclic DIRAS3 peptide interacts with KRAS, disrupting its clustering in intact cells. Thus, the observed inhibition of MAPK signaling by the DIRAS3-derived peptide likely involves additional effects beyond its direct binding to KRAS and blocking RAF binding. The switch I binding site of the peptide overlaps not only the RAF RBD binding site, but also the secondary self-association interface that supports assembly of higher oligomers of mutant KRAS. By interfering with KRAS nanoclustering, the DIRAS3 peptide could prevent multiple KRAS molecules from assembling and forming active signaling complexes in cancer cells. KRAS self-association is weak and transient, likely rendering it susceptible to interference by the DIRAS3 peptide, despite its modest affinity for KRAS. Considering the higher affinities of RAF RBDs for KRAS, disrupting nanoclusters may be the more relevant of these two potential DIRAS3 peptide mechanisms of action.

In summary, our study underscores the potential of the DIRAS3-derived cyclic peptide as a therapeutic candidate for inhibiting KRAS signaling. The switch I binding site is 100% conserved amongst the three RAS isoforms, and we showed that Compound 3 also interacts with an activated NRAS mutant, while we previously demonstrated that full-length DIRAS3 also interacts with mutant HRAS.20 The Compound 3 peptide thus may have potential as a “pan-RAS” inhibitor, which requires further investigation since the divergent HVR regions of the three RAS isoforms exhibit distinct modes of interaction with phospholipids, and thus form nanoclusters differently. The use of an endogenous RAS inhibitor to design macrocyclic RAS inhibitors represents a promising strategy for the development of targeted KRAS inhibitors for clinical translation.

Limitations of the study

Our findings demonstrate that DIRAS3-derived cyclic peptides can access the cytosol, disrupt KRAS nanoclustering, and suppress oncogenic signaling; however, several limitations remain. The modest binding affinity suggests that inhibitor potency is driven more by target access and metabolic stability than by affinity alone, underscoring the need for additional structural studies and peptide optimization. Although activity was observed against KRASG12D and KRASG12V, efficacy across other RAS isoforms, mutations, and tumor contexts has not yet been systematically assessed. Finally, a comprehensive evaluation of pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, delivery strategies, and potential resistance mechanisms will be essential to guide further preclinical development and clinical translation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture and maintenance

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell line AsPC-1 with KRASG12D mutation and HeLa cells (RRID:CVCL_0030) were obtained from ATCC. The low-grade serous ovarian cancer cell line HCC5075, harboring a KRASG12V mutation, was provided by Dr. Adi F. Gazdar of UT Southwestern Medical Center (Dallas, TX). U2OS cells were generated and obtained from Dr. Geoffrey Wahl, The Salk Institute for Biological Studies (La Jolla, California). The cell lines were authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination every three months to ensure the integrity and purity of the cultures. These cells were grown in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin. The cells were cultured in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2.

Generation of human PDAC xenografts in mice

Six-week-old male athymic nude mice (nu/nu, Envigo Biosciences), weighing 18–22 g, were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in individually ventilated cages with a 12-h light/dark cycle, controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and humidity (55 ± 10%), and provided ad libitum access to sterilized chow and water. Mice were injected subcutaneously on the right flank with 1.5 million AsPC-1 pancreatic cancer cells harboring a KRASG12D mutation. Once tumors became palpable, animals were randomized into three groups (n = 10 per group) and treated intraperitoneally with 15 mg/kg Compound 2, the Compound 2-D negative control peptide, or saline vehicle. Treatment continued for four weeks. Tumor volume was measured every three days using calipers. Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism (RRID:SCR_002798) using two-way ANOVA to assess the independent and interactive effects of peptide treatment on tumor growth. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test to identify pairwise differences between treatment groups. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center under protocol number 00001195-RN03.

Method details

Hotspot residue prediction using computational approaches

Hotspot residues are referred to as amino acids that play a critical role in the binding free energy. Identifying these key residues involved in DIRAS3-KRAS interaction is critical to the follow-up peptide design and synthesis. Our approach involved utilizing the KFC web server53 to forecast the key residues contributing to the DIRAS3-KRAS interaction. Specifically, residues of DIRAS3 (amino acids 1–229) and KRAS (amino acids 1–169) were used as input for the prediction. Homology modeling of the DIRAS3 protein was built using Modeller v9.1754 based on the crystal structure DIRAS1 (PDB ID: 2GF0). The basic steps include the following: First, BLAST55 was used to find appropriate templates for the DIRAS3 protein; then, sequence alignment was performed using MOE56 software, and homology models were built and refined using Modeller; finally, the generated models were evaluated using Ramachandran Plot and PDBsum. The best model was selected for the following studies. Protein-protein docking was performed using the ZDOCK57 web server. Residues 151–165 of KRAS were set as constraints. The residues with the highest scores in the predictions were subjected to further visual and analytical scrutiny.

Peptide synthesis – General procedure

Peptides were synthesized using manual and automated solid-phase peptide synthesis (Figures 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5). In a typical synthesis, Rink Resin SS 100–200 (0.300 g, 0.225 mmol, Advanced Chemtech) was swollen in DMF for 1 day, washed twice with DMF, and treated twice with 20% piperidine (2 × 10 min) to remove the first Fmoc protecting group. Amino acid couplings were carried out with 3 equivalents of Fmoc-protected amino acid, 2.85 equivalents of N,N,N′,N′-Tetramethyl-O-(1H-benzotriazol-1-yl)uronium hexafluorophosphate (HBTU, Oakwood Products, Inc.), and 3 equivalents of N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIEA) in 3 mL DMF. The coupling reaction was allowed to proceed at room temperature for 30 min, followed by resin filtration. A second coupling reaction (double coupling) was carried out, followed by capping with 200 μL of acetic anhydride and 200 μL DIEA. The resin was treated twice with 20% piperidine (2 × 10 min) and subsequent amino acids were installed as described above. Acetic anhydride capping was only performed after the first amino acid. At the conclusion of synthesis, the resin was washed five times with DMF, five times with DCM, and dried under vacuum.

Chloroalkane-tagged peptides were synthesized as above. After deprotection of the final Fmoc group, 100 mg resin was washed five times in DMF and treated with 4.1 eq 4-2-2-(6-chlorohexyl)oxy)ethoxy)ethyl)amino)-4-oxobutanoic acid (Halo-PEG(2)-Suc, Iris Biotech, 100 mg), 4.1 eq. PyClock (171 mg), and 10 equivalents of DIEA (132 μL) DIEA in 3 mL DMF. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 1 h, washed five times with DMF, five times with DCM, and dried under vacuum.

Peptides were deprotected and cleaved from the resin with 6 mL Cleavage Mixture B (95% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid, 2% (v/v) water, 1% (v/v) triisopropylsilane, and 2.5% 3,6-dioxa-1,8 octanethiol (DODT)) at room temperature for 3 h, precipitated with cold diethyl ether, and dried under vacuum. Crude products were purified by reverse phase preparative HPLC (Luna Phenomenex, 5 μm C18(2), 250 mm × 21.2 mm LC column) using gradient elution (0–40% Buffer B over 60 min; Buffer A dH2O + 0.1% (v/v) TFA, Buffer B CH3CN + 0.1% (v/v) TFA). Appropriate fractions were combined and lyophilized, yielding a white solid. Purified products were confirmed by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry.

Recombinant protein expression and purification

KRAS(G12V/C118S) variant

Expression and purification of recombinant KRAS variant (G12V/C118S, residues 1–173; hereafter referred to as KRAS(G12V) were performed as previously described with minor modifications.51 For nucleotide exchange, a 10-fold excess of GMPPNP was added to purified KRAS (G12V) in the presence of 10 mM EDTA and calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase (CIP). After treatment with 20 mM MgCl2, gel filtration chromatography (Superdex-75) was then performed to remove excess MgCl2, CIP, and unbound GMPPNP. 15N-labeled KRAS(G12V) was produced by culturing E. coli in M9 minimal medium containing 15NH4Cl (0.4 g/L) as the sole nitrogen source.

DIRAS3 (C75S/C80S, residues 35–227) variant

Expression and purification of truncated human DIRAS3 protein (C75S/C80S, residues 35–227; hereafter referred to as DIRAS3-ΔNC) were conducted as described in our previous study.27 In brief, DIRAS3-ΔNC expression was induced by 0.05 mM IPTG when OD600 reached approximately 0.8. Resuspended cell lysates in 50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 10% Glycerol, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1% NP40, 20 mM Imidazole, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, pH 8.0 with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche EDTA-free) were loaded onto Ni-NTA columns and then bound proteins were eluted with a stepwise imidazole gradient (50, 100, 150, and 500 mM). After TEV digestion to remove the histidine tag, gel filtration chromatography (HiLoad 26/600 Superdex 75 column) was used for further purification.

GST-tagged BRAF-RBD and GST protein

GST-tagged BRAF-RBD (residues 150–233) was expressed and purified as described previously.52 In brief, GST-tagged BRAF-RBD (residues 150–233) was subcloned into pGEX-4T2 vector and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) Codon plus. Recombinant GST-RBD was purified using Glutathione Sepharose 4B (GE Healthcare) followed by gel filtration chromatography. The GST proteins were removed by treatment with thrombin and separated by gel filtration chromatography (HiLoad 26/600 Superdex 75 column).

NMR spectroscopy

NMR data were acquired at 298 K on a Bruker 800 MHz U2 NMR spectrometer equipped with a NEO console and 5 mm TXO cryoprobe. Purified 15N-labeled KRAS(G12V) was prepared in 20 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM TCEP at pH 7.4, supplemented with 10% (v/v) D2O. All NMR spectra were processed using the NMRPipe/NMRDraw software58 and were analyzed using the NMRFAM-SPARKY software.59 Non-labeled DIRAS3-ΔNC and DIRAS3 peptides (Compound 3 and Compound 4) were dissolved in the same buffer as KRAS(G12V). 15N-labeled KRAS(G12V) was titrated with DIRAS3-ΔNC (molar ratios 1:1; 1:2), Compound 3 (molar ratios 1:4; 1:8) and Compound 4 (molar ratios 1:4; 1:8). The peak intensities at the titration endpoints (1:8 M ratio) were normalized to the signal from reference peak (V29) and depicted as ‘intensity loss’ in Figures 3C and 3D. The formula Y = 1-([Ibound(X)/Ifree(X)]/[Ibound(V29)/Ifree(V29)]) was used for the intensity plot, where ‘Ibound’ represents the peak intensity corresponding to each individual residue ‘X’ in the presence of DIRAS3 peptides. The CSP plots were calculated by applying the formula: CSP = √([ΔH)]2 + 0.1[(ΔN)]2).

Bio-layer interferometry (BLI) binding assay

The binding signals between His-tagged KRAS(G12V)-GMPPNP and DIRAS3 peptides (Compound 3 or Compound 4) were obtained using an Octet RED-384 biolayer interferometry instrument equipped with the Octet Data Acquisition 9.0.0.37. To measure binding affinity, His-tagged KRAS(G12V)-GMPPNP (10 μg/mL) was immobilized on the anti-penta-HIS(HIS1K) biosensor and then immersed in wells containing increasing concentrations (6.2–100 μM) of DIRAS3 peptides (Compound 3 and Compound 4). FortéBio Data analysis software (Sartorius) was used for data processing and analysis. Reference well values were subtracted from the raw data, and the y axes were aligned to a baseline. The data were fit to a 1:1 binding model using steady-state analysis.

BLI competition assay

GST-tagged BRAF-RBD (residues 150–233) at a concentration of 10 μg/mL was immobilized to anti-GST-conjugated biosensors (Sartorius). The biosensors were repeatedly immersed in four different wells, each containing a fixed concentration (5 μM) of KRAS(G12V)-GMPPNP. For comparison under the same conditions, DIRAS3 peptides (Compound 3 and Compound 4) were added in increasing concentrations (10, 20, 40, and 80 μM). BLI signals were obtained by subtracting the background signals observed for GST-tagged BRAF-RBD and DIRAS3 peptides in the absence of KRAS(G12V)-GMPPNP.

All BLI experiments were conducted in 96-well plates at 25 °C with agitation (1000 rpm) in a solution containing 20 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 2 mM TCEP pH 7.4 supplemented with 0.02% BSA and 0.01% Tween 20 to minimize nonspecific binding.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy

CD spectra were recorded using a Jasco J-815 CD spectrometer with a 1 mm path-length quartz cuvette. Stapled and non-stapled linear peptides were diluted to a concentration of 0.34 mg/mL in buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM TCEP at pH 7.4. Far-UV CD wavelength scans were measured by averaging three individual spectra recorded between 200 and 260 nm, with 1 nm spectral bandwidth, a response time of 8 s, and a scanning speed of 20 nm min−1.

Chloroalkane penetration assay for peptide cell permeability

To perform quantitative measurement of α5C and α5G cellular penetration by chloroalkane penetration assay (CAPA), chloroalkane azides were conjugated to the side-chain alkyne of the C-terminal Pra residue using copper-catalyzed click chemistry. Reactions were performed by combining 3 μmol purified peptide, 1.2 equivalents FAM-azide, 6-isomer (50 mg/mL in DMSO, Lumiprobe), 50 μL click solution (16 mg/mL CuSO4·2H2O and 30 mg/mL L-ascorbic acid in dH2O), 5 μL of tris-(benzyltriazolylmethyl) amine (TBTA, 100 mg/mL in DMF), and 150 μL dH2O. The reaction mixture was protected from light and rotated at room temperature 2 h and directly injected onto preparative scale reverse phase HPLC for purification (Luna Phenomenex, 5 μm C18(2), 250 mm × 21.2 mm LC column) using gradient elution (5–95% Buffer B over 45 min; Buffer A dH2O + 0.1% (v/v) TFA, Buffer B CH3CN + 0.1% (v/v) TFA). Fractions containing the product were identified by UV absorbance at 220 nm and the purified product was confirmed by electrospray ionization mass spectroscopy. Appropriate fractions were pooled and lyophilized, yielding a white solid.

On the day of the experiment, the media was aspirated, and cells were treated with fresh media (as described above) and serial dilutions of ct-peptides. ReBiL 2.0 split luciferase assay of KRAS clustering

Cells U2OS cells (RRID:CVCL_0042) with nl-HA-KRAS_G12D cl-HA-KRAS) were seeded in 96-well plates with black walls and clear bottoms at 40,000 cells/well. Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates with black walls and left to attach overnight at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and 95% air. The next day, the growth media were replaced with fresh media containing serial concentrations of the compounds to be tested, and the cells were further incubated for 72 h 100 μL CellTiter-Glo 2.0 reagent was added to each well. The plate was shaken for 2 min to cause cell lysis and then incubated at room temperature for 10 min to stabilize the luminescent signal before measuring the luminescence using a BioTek multi-plate reader. Control wells containing medium without cells were used to determine the background luminescence.

Immunoblotting

To extract protein from the cells, the wells were washed with cold PBS and treated with SDS lysis buffer for 1 min at room temperature. The lysis buffer contained Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), SDS, glycerol, β-mercaptoethanol, and bromophenol blue. The lysates were heated at 99°C for 5 min and then separated using SDS-PAGE with a 5–12% polyacrylamide gel. The separated proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes and subjected to immunoblot analysis with the specified antibodies. The protein bands were visualized using an ECL-enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (SuperSignal West Pico PLUS, Thermo Scientific). The intensity of the bands in the western blots was quantified using the ImageJ program.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The statistical analyses for the study were conducted using GraphPad Prism software. To determine the significance of differences between groups, t-tests or two-way ANOVA were performed. All experiments were repeated at least three times, with samples measured in technical triplicate.
