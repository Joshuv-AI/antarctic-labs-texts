Clinical Characteristics, Treatment Patterns, and Drug-Resistant Epilepsy Among Emirati Adults with Epilepsy
Authors: Mahra Alhammadi, Bayena Khamis Alblooshi, Amal Ahmed, Mohammad Alshaer, Raya Almazrouei, Saif Al-Shamsi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDetailed clinical descriptions of treated adult epilepsy in the Arab region remain scarce. We examined epilepsy type, antiseizure medication (ASM) use, comorbidities, and drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) in Emirati adults with epilepsy in the United Arab Emirates (UAE).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of Emirati adults aged 18–65 years attending neurology clinics at a tertiary center in Al Ain between January 2019 and January 2024. Patients were screened from neurology clinic visits, and those with at least one G40.909 coded encounter underwent chart review. Epilepsy type was classified using 2017 International League Against Epilepsy frameworks. DRE was defined as failure of at least two appropriately chosen and tolerated ASMs. Clinical and treatment data were extracted from electronic medical records.ResultsThe cohort included 204 adults [median age 34 years (26.0–44.2); 55.9% male]. Epilepsy type was specified in 178/204 patients; among whom generalized epilepsy was more common than focal epilepsy (66.9% vs. 33.1%). Monotherapy was the most common recorded treatment pattern (66.7%), and levetiracetam was the most frequently prescribed ASM (50.0%). Dyslipidemia (38.7%) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (12.7%) were the most common comorbidities. DRE was identified in 36/150 patients (24.0%) with available prior ASM failure data and in 36/204 patients (17.6%) in the full cohort.ConclusionsThis study adds data on adult epilepsy from an underrepresented Emirati population. Most patients were treated with ASM monotherapy, and levetiracetam was the most commonly prescribed drug. Cardiometabolic comorbidities were common. DRE was seen in nearly one in four patients with available prior ASM failure data, while the lower estimate in the full cohort was at least one in six patients. These findings highlight the need for better clinical documentation and for larger multicenter studies in the UAE and the wider region.Keywords: Epilepsy, Drug-resistant epilepsy, Antiseizure medications, Clinical epidemiology, Comorbidities, United Arab Emirates

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Epilepsy is a common neurological condition, but detailed clinical data on treated adult epilepsy in the Arab region remain limited.Drug-resistant epilepsy, defined as ongoing seizures despite trials of at least two appropriate antiseizure medicines, remains an important unmet need in routine care.This study described epilepsy types, treatment patterns, comorbidities, and the frequency of drug-resistant epilepsy among Emirati adults with epilepsy attending a tertiary center in the United Arab Emirates. What was learned from the study? Among 204 adults (median age 34 years; 55.9% male), epilepsy type was specified in 178; among these patients, generalized epilepsy was more common than focal epilepsy (66.9% vs. 33.1%). Monotherapy was the most common recorded treatment pattern (66.7%), most often with levetiracetam (50.0% of the full cohort).Drug-resistant epilepsy was present in 36/150 patients (24.0%) with enough information on previous treatment failure, and in 36/204 patients (17.6%) in the full study group. It was seen in patients taking one antiseizure medicine and in those taking more than one, because drug-resistant epilepsy is defined by failure of previous treatments, not by the number of medicines a patient is taking now.Cardiometabolic comorbidities were common, particularly dyslipidemia (38.7%) and type 2 diabetes (12.7%), supporting the need for more integrated epilepsy and chronic disease care in the United Arab Emirates.

Introduction

Worldwide, epilepsy is a major cause of disability and premature death [1] and contributes substantially to healthcare costs and loss of productivity [2]. It is a neurological disorder characterized by a persistent tendency to have epileptic seizures due to abnormal neuronal activity [3]. Although seizures occur intermittently, epilepsy often requires ongoing follow-up and adjustment of treatment [3].

Estimates of epilepsy incidence and prevalence differ widely between countries and across studies. Some of this variation reflects real differences in population characteristics and causes of epilepsy, but differences in how epilepsy is defined and recorded also contribute [4, 5]. Hospital- or record-based studies and community surveys often yield different estimates, reflecting variation in access to care and in who is identified as having epilepsy [5]. Stigma can further limit disclosure and delay presentation, especially in relation to marriage, where fear of rejection may lead people to hide their diagnosis [6, 7]. As a result, population prevalence figures alone do not show how many patients are actually receiving treatment, which antiseizure medications (ASMs) they receive, or how they use health services.

From a clinical perspective, treated epilepsy cohorts provide information that prevalence estimates alone cannot. They show which ASMs are being used, how often polytherapy is needed, how seizure and epilepsy types are distributed, and how many patients remain uncontrolled despite treatment. Long-term cohort studies show that seizure freedom is most often achieved with the first appropriately chosen ASM, with the chance of control falling after each additional regimen, while a subgroup remains uncontrolled despite adequate treatment [8, 9]. The International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) defines drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) as failure of adequate trials of two appropriate and tolerated ASM schedules, whether as monotherapy or in combination, to achieve sustained seizure freedom [10]. Meta-analyses suggest that DRE affects a substantial proportion of treated patients, although reported proportions vary by study design and care setting [11]. These patients have higher mortality and more comorbid conditions than those with drug-responsive epilepsy [12], which makes it important to identify and quantify drug resistance in routine care.

Electronic medical record-based cohorts complement population-based surveys by showing how epilepsy is managed in everyday practice. They allow researchers to examine the sequence in which drugs are tried, how long treatments are continued, how often polytherapy is used, and which comorbidities are documented in clinical care. Multicountry European cohorts using electronic records have also reported differences in patient characteristics, ASM use, healthcare use, and seizure-related injuries, and have been used to compare focal drug-resistant epilepsy across health systems [13, 14]. Together, these findings suggest that differences between countries may reflect how care is delivered and recorded, as well as differences in the underlying disease.

Comparison between cohorts also depends on how seizures and epilepsies are classified. The 2017 ILAE framework uses seizure type and epilepsy type as key descriptors and provides a common language for clinical records and epidemiological reports [15]. Many studies still rely on older classification systems or incomplete records, which makes it difficult to compare reported seizure types and treatment patterns across settings [4, 5, 15]. Comorbidities are likewise central to the epilepsy profile. Population-based studies and systematic reviews show higher rates of psychiatric disorders in people with epilepsy than in the general population [16, 17], and medical comorbidities can affect ASM selection, tolerability, adherence, and longer-term risk [18–20].

Despite the growing international literature, region-specific data remain limited, particularly in the Arab world. A recent systematic review found incidence or prevalence estimates for only some Arab countries and reported wide variation in methods and reporting, with few studies providing detailed descriptions of treated adult epilepsy [21]. Other regional work has focused on psychosocial aspects and stigma, which can influence disclosure, care-seeking, and how epilepsy is documented in clinical records [22]. These factors are likely to affect which patients are captured in routine clinical datasets.

Detailed clinical descriptions of treated epilepsy in Arab healthcare settings therefore remain scarce. In this study, we describe the clinical profile of Emirati adults with epilepsy receiving care at a tertiary neurology center in Al Ain, United Arab Emirates (UAE). Using ILAE classification frameworks, we report epilepsy types, patterns of ASM use (monotherapy and polytherapy), the burden of DRE, and recorded psychiatric and medical comorbidities. We limited the study to Emirati adults because comparable data in this population remain limited. This allowed us to describe a more clearly defined cohort while adding clinically relevant data that may help inform future research on outcomes and service planning.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study of Emirati adults with epilepsy attending neurology clinics at Tawam Hospital, a tertiary care center in Al Ain, UAE. The study period was from 1 January 2019 to 31 January 2024. All eligible clinic visits during this period were screened, and each patient was included once. The index date was defined as the first neurology visit within the study window that met the inclusion criteria.

Participants

We screened Emirati adults aged 18–65 years who attended neurology clinics during the study period. Patients were eligible if they had a neurology clinic encounter carrying International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) code G40.909 at the index visit. Final inclusion required confirmation of epilepsy by chart review. We limited the study to Emirati adults to define a more stable cohort with more complete follow-up, as non-Emirati patients may receive care across different providers or leave the country, which can lead to missing data and loss to follow-up in retrospective studies.

We excluded patients whose seizures were clearly non-epileptic or acute symptomatic, including seizures attributed to metabolic derangements, acute medical illness, alcohol or drug use, or other provoked events. We also excluded patients with psychogenic non-epileptic seizures, progressive encephalopathy, known progressive central nervous system (CNS) disease or lesions (e.g. primary or metastatic brain tumors, active CNS infection, demyelinating disease), congenital structural brain malformations, and primary metabolic disorders. Ethical Approval

This study involved human participants and was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of Tawam Hospital (approval number MF2058-2024-1115). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of Tawam Hospital and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because the study used retrospective, de-identified data obtained from routine clinical care. Patient consent for publication was not applicable.

Variables

Data were extracted manually from the hospital’s electronic medical record by the study investigators. For each patient, we recorded demographic information including age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) at the index visit. Epilepsy-related variables included epilepsy type, electroencephalography (EEG) findings, and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings.

Treatment-related data comprised the ASMs recorded at the time of review, the number of ASMs used concurrently (monotherapy vs. polytherapy), and the number of failed ASMs when this was documented. Family and social history variables included family history of epilepsy in first-degree relatives, reported parental consanguinity, smoking status, alcohol use, and recreational drug use. We also recorded documented history of head trauma and psychiatric and medical comorbidities, including mood disorders, autism spectrum disorder, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, hypertension, arrhythmia, obstructive sleep apnea, autoimmune disease, chronic headache, asthma, and insomnia, based on the electronic problem list and clinic notes.

Because epilepsy-related documentation was not standardized in the medical record, epilepsy type was recoded for analysis into three categories: focal epilepsy, generalized epilepsy, or unknown. Focal epilepsy was assigned when the clinical history or EEG suggested focal onset, generalized epilepsy when the available clinical description and EEG findings supported generalized onset, and unknown when available information was insufficient to make this distinction. Classification was based on retrospective review of the clinical history, EEG, and imaging findings, using 2017 ILAE classification frameworks [15, 23]. EEG and MRI were categorized as normal or abnormal; any reported abnormal finding in the clinical report was coded as abnormal.

Definitions

DRE was defined according to the ILAE consensus proposal as failure of adequate trials of two appropriately chosen and tolerated ASM schedules, whether as monotherapies or in combination, to achieve sustained seizure freedom [10]. For the purposes of this study, drug resistance was coded as present, absent, or unknown based on review of the clinical notes and medication history. The names of previously failed ASMs and the reasons for failure were not consistently recorded in the medical records.

Mood disorders were defined as any documented diagnosis of major depressive disorder or bipolar disorder. Type 2 diabetes mellitus was considered present if it was clinically documented in the chart or if glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was ≥ 6.5% when available. Dyslipidemia was considered present if it was clinically documented or if low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol was ≥ 3.0 mmol/L when available. HbA1c and LDL values were extracted from available laboratory records. Status epilepticus, CNS infection, prior brain surgery, and other relevant neurological or medical history variables were recorded as present if specifically documented in the chart. Sleep-related variables, including insomnia and obstructive sleep apnea, were recorded when specifically documented in the chart.

Statistical Analysis

The analysis was conducted on adults with a confirmed diagnosis of epilepsy (n = 204). Missing values were not imputed. The sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients seen during the study period.

Continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR) because distributions were non-normal on inspection. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages.

Comparisons of clinical and demographic characteristics by epilepsy type were restricted to patients classified as having focal or generalized epilepsy (n = 178). For continuous variables, group comparisons between focal and generalized epilepsy used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For categorical variables, Fisher’s exact test was used.

ASM use was described in two ways. First, patients were categorized as receiving monotherapy, polytherapy, or no ASM recorded at the time of data extraction, and proportions in each category were calculated using the full cohort as denominator. Second, individual ASMs were tabulated as the number and percentage of patients receiving each drug in the full cohort. For descriptive clarity, the frequencies of individual ASMs were also expressed among treated patients.

Selected descriptive analyses were also summarized separately for male and female patients. The prevalence of DRE was reported in two ways: first, as a proportion among patients with available data on previously failed ASMs and, second, as a proportion of the overall cohort. The proportion in the overall cohort should be interpreted as a lower estimate when data on previously failed ASMs were missing.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 204 adults with epilepsy were included in the analysis. The median age was 34 years (IQR 26.0–44.2), and 114/204 (55.9%) were male and 90/204 (44.1%) were female. The median BMI was 27.7 kg/m2 (IQR 24.5–32.9). Consanguinity status was documented in only 11 patients, of whom 7 (63.6%) had documented parental consanguinity. A family history of epilepsy was available for 189 patients, of whom 70 (37.0%) had a positive history.

History of mood disorders was reported in 35/198 (17.7%) patients, and autism was documented in 2/204 (1.0%). Smoking was reported in 36/195 (18.5%), drug or other substance use in 6/204 (2.9%), and alcohol use in 8/199 (4.0%). Other comorbidities included chronic headaches in 18/204 (8.8%), asthma in 15/204 (7.4%), and insomnia in 22/204 (10.8%). Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of Emirati adults with epilepsy, we described epilepsy type, ASM use, comorbidities, and the burden of DRE. Among the 178 patients with a specified epilepsy type, generalized epilepsy was more common than focal epilepsy. Most patients were treated with monotherapy, and levetiracetam was the most commonly prescribed ASM. DRE was seen in nearly one in four patients with available prior ASM failure data, with a lower full cohort estimate of about one in six. Dyslipidemia and type 2 diabetes mellitus were the most frequent medical comorbidities, and abnormal EEG findings were also common.

Antiseizure Medication Patterns and Drug-Resistant Epilepsy

Most patients in our cohort were treated with ASM monotherapy (66.7%), while 17.2% were on polytherapy and 16.2% had no ASM recorded at the time of assessment. This pattern is broadly consistent with current treatment approaches, which generally favor starting treatment with a single drug and switching to another monotherapy or adding a second drug only if seizures remain uncontrolled [18]. However, the category “no ASM recorded” should not be interpreted as a single untreated group because the medical records did not reliably capture why no current ASM was listed. Some patients may have been off treatment, while others may have been receiving treatment that was not fully reflected in the medication list, and in some cases the medication documentation may have been incomplete at the time of review. For comparison, a previous study from Sheikh Khalifa Medical City (SKMC) likewise reported frequent use of monotherapy (87/160, 54.4%), although the proportion was lower than in our cohort [24], which may reflect differences in patient characteristics and referral patterns.

Among treated patients, levetiracetam was the most commonly used ASM (59.6%), followed by valproate (16.4%), carbamazepine (15.2%), and lamotrigine (11.1%). The predominance of levetiracetam is similar to findings from recent Saudi data and large United States prescribing datasets, including cohorts of older adults [25, 26]. By contrast, Egyptian and Iranian studies describe valproate as the most frequently prescribed drug, often as monotherapy [27, 28]. This may reflect differences in prescribing practice, the patients included, and access to different drugs, but we could not assess this directly from our data. Global utilization data from 73 countries show rising use of newer ASMs such as levetiracetam and lamotrigine over the past decade, while valproate remains the most widely used agent overall, especially in lower-income regions where its cost and broad-spectrum efficacy are major advantages [29]. Our findings fit with this shift towards newer ASMs in more affluent countries, while still showing an important ongoing role for valproate. However, our medical records did not consistently contain information on the names, sequence, or reasons for prior ASM failures, so we could not determine which ASMs, including valproate in patients with generalized epilepsy, had been tried before DRE classification.

DRE was identified in 36/150 patients (24.0%) with available prior ASM failure data and in 36/204 patients (17.6%) in the full cohort. Since prior ASM failure data were missing for 54 patients, the full-cohort proportion is a lower estimate. This frequency is at the lower end of the range reported internationally [11] and somewhat lower than some regional estimates. For example, studies from Saudi Arabia have reported rates of medically resistant epilepsy of up to 30% of patients [30]. Differences in how cohorts are defined (incident vs. prevalent cases), length of follow-up and how strictly the ILAE criteria for drug resistance are applied [10] are likely to explain part of this variation. Among patients with available prior ASM failure data, DRE was present in 16/102 (15.7%) patients on monotherapy, 9/26 (34.6%) on polytherapy, and 11/22 (50.0%) patients with no ASM recorded. This is not inconsistent with the ILAE definition, which is based on prior treatment failure rather than the number of ASMs currently prescribed [10]. A patient could therefore still meet DRE criteria while receiving monotherapy at the time of review. However, because the medical record did not reliably record the sequence of prior ASM changes or the reasons for stopping them, these findings should be interpreted descriptively. Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of adults seen in tertiary care in Al Ain had DRE, supporting the need for clear referral pathways to advanced epilepsy care.

The higher proportion of generalized epilepsy in this cohort, including among patients with DRE and a defined epilepsy type, was unexpected because focal structural epilepsy is more common in many tertiary cohorts. Several factors may explain this pattern. First, patients were initially identified through neurology clinic encounters using a single code, so some patients recorded under other epilepsy subcodes may not have been captured. Second, we excluded patients with progressive CNS disease and congenital structural malformations, which may have reduced the number of structural focal epilepsies in the cohort. In addition, epilepsy type was assigned retrospectively from the available clinical record, which limited more detailed characterization. This finding should therefore be interpreted with caution and should not be assumed to reflect the wider epilepsy population in the UAE.

Comorbidities and EEG Abnormalities

Beyond treatment patterns, dyslipidemia (38.7%) was the most frequent medical comorbidity in this cohort, followed by type 2 diabetes mellitus (12.7%). This pattern is consistent with reports of substantial cardiometabolic comorbidity in adults with epilepsy and supports consideration of cardiovascular risk in epilepsy clinics [19, 31].

Obstructive sleep apnea was documented in 1.5% of the cohort and only among patients with focal epilepsy. Although this difference reached statistical significance, the absolute number of cases was small. Meta-analyses suggest that obstructive sleep apnea is more common in people with epilepsy than in the general population and is seen in both focal and generalized epilepsies [32], so our pattern likely reflects under-recognition and selective referral for sleep assessment rather than a true restriction to focal epilepsy. Some ASMs are associated with weight gain [33], which may worsen obstructive sleep apnea or increase its risk [34]. Retrospective data, for example, have linked levetiracetam and valproate to weight gain in routine practice [33], with possible implications for both obstructive sleep apnea and cardiometabolic health.

Further comorbidities in our sample included chronic headache (8.8%), asthma (7.4%), and insomnia (10.8%). These conditions may interact with epilepsy care; for example, sleep disturbance can lower seizure threshold [35] and chronic pain and respiratory conditions are both common comorbidities in patients with epilepsy, adding to overall symptom burden and healthcare use [36].

Abnormal EEG findings were more frequent than normal recordings in this cohort (55.1% vs. 44.9%). Abnormal EEG findings were also more common in focal than in generalized epilepsy (66.7% vs. 52.3%), although this difference was not statistically significant. An Egyptian hospital-based study likewise reported a high proportion of abnormal EEGs (77.5% vs. 22.5% normal); however, in that cohort, abnormalities were more frequent in generalized epilepsy [27]. These differences may reflect variation in referral patterns, the timing of EEG relative to seizures, and the classification of EEG findings. Furthermore, in our study, the EEG documentation in the medical record was limited. Because of this, we could not always tell whether abnormal EEGs in the generalized epilepsy group reflected clearly focal findings or less specific abnormalities. This may have affected how these findings were interpreted in some cases.

Sex Distribution and Stigma

In addition to these clinical characteristics, our cohort showed a modest male predominance (55.9%). In the descriptive analyses stratified by sex, monotherapy use was similar in male and female patients, while DRE was more common in male patients. Given the sample size and retrospective design, these differences should not be overinterpreted. The overall sex distribution in our cohort is similar to that reported in several studies from the region. A Saudi cohort found that males made up 54.5% of people with epilepsy [37], and a systematic review of Arab studies reported a male predominance in seven of eight included cohorts [21]. In contrast, a study from SKMC in Abu Dhabi reported a higher proportion of female adult patients with epilepsy (56%) [24]. These differences are unlikely to be explained by biology alone and probably reflect differences in referral patterns and who presents for care.

Stigma and gender roles may contribute to under-identification of women with epilepsy in routine practice. In India, women with epilepsy have been considered unsuitable for marriage, leading some to hide their diagnosis [6]. Regional literature indicates that stigma and misconceptions about epilepsy are still common in many Arab countries, including the UAE [22]. In a survey from Sharjah, many respondents expressed reluctance or uncertainty about marriage to a person with epilepsy, highlighting the persistence of epilepsy-related stigma in the UAE [38]. Such attitudes may discourage disclosure and delay seeking help, particularly among women, and could contribute to the male predominance reported in many treated cohorts. Our data cannot explain these mechanisms directly, but they suggest that gender patterns should be interpreted in their sociocultural context.

Consanguinity and Family History of Epilepsy

Family history of epilepsy was better documented and was present in 37% of patients with available data. This is higher than in several regional reports: a systematic review from Saudi Arabia found family history rates between 6% and 24.9% [39], and a hospital-based study from Egypt reported a rate of 7% [27]. The higher proportion in our cohort may reflect a combination of a high background rate of consanguinity in the Emirati population [40] and more systematic recording of family history in our clinic. Given the multifactorial nature of epilepsy [21], these findings suggest that family history should be recorded more consistently in routine practice to better understand genetic influences on epilepsy in the Arab population.

Consanguinity is common in many Gulf and neighboring countries and is shaped by factors such as tribe, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and urbanization [40]. Population-based and review data indicate that consanguineous marriage remains common in the UAE and in several other Arab populations [40, 41]. In our cohort, however, consanguinity status was recorded for only 11 patients, 7 of whom had documented consanguineous parentage. This very small number means we cannot draw reliable conclusions about the true prevalence of consanguinity in our sample or its relationship with epilepsy characteristics. We did not find a significant difference in recorded consanguinity between focal and generalized epilepsy (p = 0.491), but consanguinity was documented in too few patients to draw firm conclusions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary center in Al Ain, and was intentionally restricted to Emirati adults, so the findings are not generalizable to all adults with epilepsy in the UAE. In addition, because initial screening relied on a single ICD-10-CM code (G40.909), some patients recorded under other epilepsy subcodes may not have been captured. Second, key variables such as consanguinity were often not documented by clinic staff in the electronic medical records. As a result, only 11 patients had consanguinity recorded, so we cannot draw conclusions about its role in this cohort. This highlights the need for more systematic recording of such information in routine clinical notes. Furthermore, prior ASM failure data were unavailable for 54 patients. Seizure data over time and the names, sequence, and reasons for failed ASM trials were also not documented in enough detail for this analysis. These gaps limit DRE classification and mean that the full-cohort DRE proportion should be interpreted as a lower estimate. Third, epilepsy classification was retrospective and simplified to focal, generalized, or unknown. In some cases, the clinical records did not contain enough detail from the history, EEG, and imaging to tell whether the epilepsy was focal or generalized. We therefore could not determine whether those cases would have been classified differently if more complete clinical information had been available. Fourth, we excluded patients with acute symptomatic seizures and seizures related to brain tumors, congenital anomalies, alcohol or drug use. This improved the homogeneity of the cohort but further reduced numbers and may limit generalizability to broader seizure populations. Finally, the retrospective, largely cross-sectional design does not allow us to examine long-term relationships between comorbidities, treatment choices, and seizure control.

Despite these limitations, this study provides clinical data on adult epilepsy from a region where such evidence remains limited, using ILAE concepts applied to electronic health record data. It also adds to prior regional work, which has focused more on prevalence, stigma, or health-related quality of life than on treatment patterns and DRE.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort of 204 Emirati adults with epilepsy seen at a tertiary center in Al Ain, most patients were treated with ASM monotherapy, and levetiracetam was the most commonly prescribed drug. Among patients with a specified epilepsy type, generalized epilepsy was more frequent than focal epilepsy. Cardiometabolic comorbidities, particularly dyslipidemia and type 2 diabetes, were prevalent. DRE was seen in nearly one in four patients with available prior ASM failure data, while the lower estimate in the full cohort was about one in six.

This study adds data on adult epilepsy from an underrepresented Emirati population seen at a tertiary center. The findings should be interpreted with caution because of the retrospective single-center design, but they also highlight the need for better epilepsy documentation and for larger multicenter studies in the UAE and the wider region.
