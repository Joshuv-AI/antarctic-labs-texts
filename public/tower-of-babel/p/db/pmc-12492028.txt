Domestic animals and hygiene on infants' risk of contact and exposure to animal faeces in urban neighbourhoods in Kenya: A mixed methods cohort study
Authors: Innocent K. Tumwebaze, Mark Krysan, Phylis J. Busienei, Sheillah Simiyu, Blessing Mberu, Daniel K. Sewell, Kelly K. Baker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Contact with animals or their faeces could expose infants to zoonotic pathogens associated with diarrheal infections in infants. This study investigated the risk of infants' exposure to domestic animals and their faeces in low- and middle-income urban neighbourhoods in Kenya. A total of 275 caregivers with infants aged 0–12 months from low- income and middle-income neighbourhoods in Nairobi and Kisumu were enrolled in a cohort prospective study and completed the survey. Of these, 136 belonged to low-income and 139 to middle-income neighbourhoods. To ensure balanced recruitment of infants in each neighbourhood, community health promoters who provided a listing of caregivers and their infants aged 0–12 months to form the sampling frame. Recruitment was based on the target for each infant age cluster. Negative Binomial, Zero-Inflated Poisson (ZIP), and Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB) regression models were used to test for the rate of infant contact (per hour) with animals and animal faeces and logistic regression for infant exposure to surfaces contaminated with animal faeces. Bayes factors were used for model selection. Overall, 27.9 % of the studied low-income neighbourhood households and 30.9 % of those in middle-income neighbourhoods owned domestic animals. Infants in households who owned cats had a 109 % increase (95 %CI = 59 %, 173 %) in contact compared to non-cat owning households. There was a 22 % decrease (95 %CI = 7 %, 42 %) in infant contact with non-feline animals compared to households that did not own them. The rate of contact with animals, animal faeces, or surfaces polluted by animal faeces increased with age. Full penning of household owned animals reduced the rate of infant contact with animal faeces by 93 % (95 %CI = 16 %, 99.7 %), compared to no penning, and reduced the odds of infant contact with a contaminated surface by 97 % (95 %CI = 42 %–99.9 %), compared to no penning. Self-reported animal faeces disposal practices were not significantly associated with a reduced rate of infant contact with animal faeces or touching surfaces contaminated by animal faeces. The study findings infants in households that owned cats have an increased contact with them while full penning of domestic animals is associated with reduced risk of exposure to animal faeces or contaminated surfaces.

Introduction

Zoonotic pathogens are a growing public health concern [1], contributing to the burden of diarrheal diseases especially in children under five years [2,3]. Exposure to diarrheal and febrile pathogens such as Campylobacter, non-typhoidal Salmonella, Cryptosporidium, and Lassa virus that are spread by animal faeces contribute to one million annual deaths [2].

In low and lower-middle income countries (LMICs) like Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) diarrhea accounts for over 90 % of the mortality in children, especially from poor households [[4], [5], [6]]. Evidence also shows that within this age group, children under two years are more vulnerable to diarrheal sequalae such as malnutrition and growth deficiencies [7]. The common faecal-oral pathways to zoonotic transmissions in children may be through direct or indirect contact with animals, their faeces or the environment that has been contaminated by faeces [4,[8], [9], [10]]. Young children in households keep animals at high risk of pathogen exposure due to their mouthing tendencies that lead to ingestion of contaminated soils and faeces [[11], [12], [13]]. Children also have heightened risk of infection due to their under-developed immune system and high behavioural hand-to-mouth contact tendencies [14].

Most developing country cities also have low-income urban settlements with high human and animal population densities, which can increase children's vulnerability to zoonotic hygiene risk exposures [14,15]. The problem of zoonotic hygiene and disease exposure risk, particularly for infants under one year of age, has not, however, been extensively studied [3,16]. Evidence from this study could contribute to informing public health initiatives to minimize infant exposure to contact with animals, their faeces or contaminated surfaces as safeguards to zoonotic infections. Whilst some WASH interventions, like handwashing, may limit animal to human transmissions, but do not address all pathways, such as those through direct animal contact or contact with contaminated soils and surfaces in shared living environments [13,17,18]. Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) services have mainly focused on control of human excreta exposure pathways, without addressing other sources like from domestic animals [6,16,19]. Thus, there is growing attention to look at public health promotion beyond WASH to a holistic one health approach that also includes animals in the framework [19].

Like any other LMIC, diarrhea is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in under-five children in Kenya especially among children in urban's low income communities [20]. Evidence from previous studies points to environmental exposures such as animals and/or their excreta as a contributor to the prevalence of diarrhea and other enteric infections in children [17,[21], [22], [23]]. In one study conducted among peri-urban infants in Kenya, cohabitation with animals was strongly associated with pathogen codetection in infants [17]. Relatedly, animal faeces may play a greater role in neighbourhood-level pathogen diversity in soil and surface water than with human faeces [24]. This study assesses the risks of infants' zoonotic exposures due to direct contact with animals or their faeces and indirect exposure via contact with faecal contaminated surfaces using data collected from an epidemiological cohort study on urban societal development and the ecology of enteric disease transmission among infants, domestic animals and the environment [25]. It also evaluates the potential for existing animal hygiene practices to moderate those exposure risks.

Methods

This was a mixed methods cohort prospective study conducted between November 2021 – February 2024. It is part of the parent PATHOME study that investigated the effect of urban societal developments on the ecology of enteric disease transmission among infants, domestic animals and the environment in low-and middle-income neighbourhoods in Kenya [25] The focus of this manuscript is on the risk of infant contact with animals and exposure to their faeces in the studied neighbourhoods.

Study population

This study was conducted in low- and middle-income neighbourhoods in Nairobi (Kibera and Jericho) and Kisumu (Nyalenda B, and Arina, Kibuye, Nubian, Brilliant and Shauli Moyo). Nairobi, Kenya's capital city, had a population of 4,396,828 people while Kisumu, the third largest city after Nairobi and Mombasa, had a population of 1,155,551 people as per the 2019 population census [26]. Low-income slums like Kibera and Nyalenda B are home to 60 % of the urban population in Kenya [27]. The middle-income neighbourhoods represent a rapidly growing population group in Kenya and provide variation in the level of societal development compared to low-income neighbourhood [25]. Low-income neighbourhoods are often more populated and deprived in societal developments and access to basic services [28,29]. The socio-economic status (SES) of study neighbourhoods were verified through qualitative assessments prior to their inclusion as PATHOME study communities regarding their improvements in services such as drainage, cleanliness, road networks and housing [25]. Households with infants 0–12 months were considered eligible for enrolment. Methods for recruitment, enrolment, retention, and data protections are described elsewhere (Clinicaltrial.gov ID NCT05322655 [25].

Study design and data

This study applied a mixed methods approach that involved a 14-day data collection process on infant health outcomes and integrated exposure pathways to pathogen contaminants. In this paper we used data collected through (1) a household survey questionnaire administered to caregivers of infants in the study households at enrolment, (2) 10 h of structured observation data of each infant, and (3) observation of domestic animal faeces found within the household premises. A proof of birth registration card was needed to confirm eligibility for inclusion and the infant needed not to have any form of disabilities [25]. During the household survey, data was collected on domestic animal (including type and number of animals) ownership, where animals were kept during both day and night, whether animals from neighbouring households come into their premises, and how animal faeces were managed. Structured observations focused on infant behaviours such as child touching of animals or their faeces and mouthing of soils or surfaces that could be contaminated with animals or their faeces. Observations also focused on the presence of animals or their faeces during any of the days within the 14-day follow-up period for each of the study households. The parent study also involved collection of samples for animal faeces to test for enteric pathogens of interest [25].

Data processing and analysis

Data cleaning was conducted using R version 4.4.2 [30]. We considered three outcome variables. First, we used the structured observation data to obtain for each infant the number of times of touching an animal. Second, we used structured observation data to measure the count for each infant of the total number of times that an infant directly touched animal faeces. Finally, we linked structured observation data to data on animal faeces observed on household surfaces to create a binary indicator of whether an infant touched/mouthed surfaces where an animal faeces sample was observed and collected from the household's floor or compound. Infants who did not touch surfaces or who touched surfaces lacking observable animal faeces served as the reference group.

The independent variables used in the analysis were SES of the neighbourhood (either low- or middle-income), infant age (polychotomized into four 3-month bins), household animal ownership (excluding cats), household cat ownership, presence of neighbourhood animals in study households' private domain, household animal penning practices, and household animal faeces disposal practices. Household animals and cat ownership were binary indicators. Cats were analyzed separately from other domestic animals due to observations of highly different roles and interactions with humans during data collection and there was insufficient statistical power to analyse these species separately. Prior to building models, we examined data distributions on numbers of households owning different species and counts of animal species, animal faeces, or contaminated environment touch and mouthing exposures by species. Due to absence of animal ownership in three of the four neighbourhoods for poultry, dogs, and ruminants and low frequency for some exposures, model convergence could not be achieved using individual species categories. Cats contact was frequent enough that it could be analyzed alone, grouping avians, ruminants, dogs as a non-feline category.

The presence of neighbouring animals in the private domain was a binary variable, indicating if the household reported that animals from neighbouring households entered their premises. The penning variable was a categorical variable created from responses to the questionnaire about penning with three levels: no penning, partial penning, and full penning. A household fully pens if they indicate every animal (except cats) they own is penned at all times of the day, partially if at least one animal is penned sometimes day or night, and no penning if they do not pen any animals. Faecal disposal was a binary variable indicating if animal faeces were disposed of or left on the ground.

To analyze the rate at which infants touched animals, we used the best fit of zero-inflated Poisson and zero-inflated negative binomial regression models. Zero-inflated models were chosen due to the high number of infants who were not exposed to animals. An offset of total observation time in hours was used along with the following covariates: neighbourhoods' SES, infant age, animal ownership, cat ownership, neighbouring households' animal presence, and an interaction term of animal ownership and penning behaviour as covariates. We modelled the outcome of having observed child touch animal faeces using a binomial regression with a complementary log-log link function. The same offset was included and covariates as the models for animal touching rates. In addition, we included an interaction term for animal ownership and faecal disposal habits as well as neighbourhood animal presence and faecal disposal habits. By using the complementary log-log link, we were able to naturally incorporate time as an offset into the model, allowing us to interpret results in terms of a rate of touches per hour. Logistic regression was used to model exposure to surfaces contaminated by faeces. We included the same covariates, including interaction terms, as was used for the model describing infants touching animal faeces. Since assessing neighbourhood SES contribution to infant-animal interactions was one of our study goals, it is treated as a covariate in the model. Adding household income would result in multicollinearity with neighbourhood SES, adding extra variation to interval estimates, and was therefore not included in models. For each of these analyses, we made Bayesian inference using the brms R package (brms version 2.22.0, R version 4.4.2) [31]. We tested the likelihood of city/SES and age on each of the outcome variables using the Bayes Factor.

Household owned domestic animals and those from neighbouring households.

A total of 120 out of 275 infants (43.6 %) in the study were observed to touch one or more types of domestic animals over the observation period at a median rate of about 1 touch per hour. The count comprised the total number of times that an infant either touched or mouthed any animal (Supplemental Appendix Fig. 1). There was no discernible difference in odds of infant contact with animals in households who owned poultry, dogs, or ruminants versus those who did not own them, or in households who had free-roaming neighbourhood animals enter the household versus those who did not (Table 4). However, infants whose household owned cats had a 109 % increase (95 %CI: 59 %, 173 %) in contact than households that did not cats. Consistent with observations of fewer households with animals in Nairobi, infant risk of contact with animals and the rate of contact per hour was significantly lower in Nairobi's low-and middle-income neighbourhood than in Kisumu's low-income neighbourhood. While middle-income households in Kisumu owned more total animals than their low-income neighbourhood, the odds of infant-animal contact and the rate of contact per hour were significantly lower for middle-income infants, albeit these income-based differences were smaller than observed city-level comparisons. Infants aged over 3 months had a higher risk of animal contact and a higher rate of contact per hour than infants under 3 months. We found that both fully penning and partially penning of animals did not result in a decreased rate of infant contact with poultry, ruminants, or dogs. Discussion

The goal of this study was to establish the influence of domestic animal and hygiene practices on infants' (0–12 months) risk of contact with animals and their faeces in low- and middle-income urban slum neighbourhoods in Kenya. Specifically, we evaluated the chance of infants contacting animals, animal faeces, and surfaces visibly contaminated by animal faeces. We also examined the effect animal penning and faeces on the rate of infant contact with animals, their faeces, or surfaces contaminated by animal faeces.Hypothesis 1The presence of domestic animals in low-and middle-income neighbourhoods, whether owned by the household or free-roaming, increases the risk of infant exposure to animals, animal faeces, or surfaces contaminated by animal faeces.

The presence of domestic animals in low-and middle-income neighbourhoods, whether owned by the household or free-roaming, increases the risk of infant exposure to animals, animal faeces, or surfaces contaminated by animal faeces.

This study revealed infants in low- and middle-income neighbourhoods have frequent contact with domestic animals such as cats, especially in Kisumu where animal ownership is more common. We also observed infant exposure to animal faeces and surfaces where animal faeces were present. However, we did not find animal ownership or their presence to be associated with infant exposure. In both cities, low-income neighbourhoods had the higher numbers of domestic animals free roaming. Previous studies have used ownership of animals or their presence in private domains as a proxy indicator for direct contact with humans or contact with animal faeces to explain their effect on health outcomes [3,17,[32], [33], [34]]. For instance, in a study that was conducted in Kisumu's peri-urban areas, human contact with animals was defined as having direct interaction with animals, animal waste or sharing the same physical environment [32]. Evidence disentangling animal ownership from animal exposures, especially on a species-level basis, are absent. We found that the risk of direct infant contact with animals among households does not depend upon ownership of domesticated animals, except for cats. Ownership of domestic cats in low- and middle-income neighbourhoods increased the frequency of infant contact with animals. This could heighten an infant's risk of exposure to pathogens of feline origin [35]. The dual role of cats as companions (especially kittens) and pest control necessitates close contact with humans. This could explain why the odds of touching cats by infants in household owning cats were over two-times more common than that of touching other animals.

Although ownership of cats increased infant-animal contact, it did not increase infant contact with animal faeces, nor did ownership of non-feline species. This may be related to cats' behavioural tendencies of covering up their faeces after defecation. It may also be that their faeces are easily cleaned, especially from households. Previous studies that have also reported not finding association between household animal ownership and infant exposure to pathogens through contact with animal faeces [2,3,17,36,37]. Most households had free-roaming neighbourhood animals which may defecate in public spaces rather than in their premises if most of the time they are in the public domain. While free-roaming animals entering private premises or compounds was a widespread problem, it also did not increase direct infant-animal contact, or with animal faeces. It is possible that people who are used to free-roaming animals on their property take responsiveness in scooping animal faeces out of their environment. This could have led to us under-observing how often free-roaming animals defecate in private households.

Likewise, this study found no discernible differences in the likelihood of infant contact with surfaces contaminated by animal faeces among households that owned cats or other animals compared to those that did not own them. The presence of animals from neighbouring households also did not present a greater likelihood of infant contact with surfaces contaminated with animal faeces. In contrast, a study by Ercumen and colleagues (2017) on animal faeces' contribution to domestic faecal contamination found that most compounds of the studied population had animal faeces which was associated with high levels of faecal contamination of soil and water sources [38]. Similarly, studies conducted in Kenya's urban slum communities found that the presence of domestic animals was associated with faecal and pathogenic contamination of surfaces such as housing floors and the domestic environment like soil as well as water sources [15,17,32,39].

Finally, our data showed that the increase in age (months) over infancy played a major role in animal contact with animal faeces and contaminated surfaces while living in a middle-income neighbourhood with lower animal ownership plays a major role in decreasing contact with faeces or faeces contaminated surfaces.Hypothesis 2Penning household animals decreases the risk of infants' exposure to animals, their faeces, or contaminated surfaces.

Penning household animals decreases the risk of infants' exposure to animals, their faeces, or contaminated surfaces.

This study did not find that the penning of poultry, pigs, dogs, or ruminants was associated with a reduced rate of infant contact with animals. However, full penning of animals was significantly associated with a reduced the rate of infant contact with animal faeces or surfaces contaminated by animal faeces compared to households that do not pen their animals. One reason for no association with animal contact could be that penned animals are kept in one place which could be within the reach of infants if not restricted, especially among older infants who may want to interact with them. However, this is an area that would warrant deeper exploration given that penning is also promoted as a protective intervention from infant contact with animals.

The risks associated with infants living in households with animals and their exposure to pathogens and related disease outcomes is greatly documented [2,3,17,23,38,40]. For instance, in a study previously conducted in Kisumu peri-urban areas, infants who slept in households with animals had 1.54-fold higher odds of pathogen count than infants who did not in households with animals [17]. However, whilst penning of poultry, dogs, pigs, or ruminants was not associated with a reduced rate of infant contact with animals, full penning of animals was associated with a reduced rate of infant contact with animal faeces and the likelihood of contact with surfaces contaminated by animal faeces. Previous studies have suggested that hygiene interventions such as animal penning or having playpens for infants could protect them from the risk of contact with animal faeces and ensure playing in clean spaces [13,15,41]. We provide novel evidence supporting the premise that penning of domestic animals could protect infants from the risk of exposure to animal faeces.Hypothesis 3Disposal of animal faeces decreases the risk of infants' exposure to animal faeces.

Disposal of animal faeces decreases the risk of infants' exposure to animal faeces.

This study did not find that animal faecal disposal of practices among households reduced the risk of infant exposure to animal faeces compared to households that reported leaving animal faeces on the ground. Disposal of animal faeces meant it was cleaned from the household or compound premises to other locations rather than leaving the faeces on the ground. Whereas most households in Nairobi's low- and middle-income neighbourhoods had high faecal disposal practices compared to Kisumu, animal faeces were mainly disposed of in rubbish piles within the compound. Animal faecal disposal in rubbish piles within the compound, which is a common practice in such neighbourhoods lacking solid waste removal services, can leave the environment polluted. Moreover, there are no clear policies or guidelines towards management and disposal of animal faeces in such congested slum neighbourhoods. Some households also reported disposal of animal faeces in water drains or solid waste situated outside their compounds, evidenced most in Kisumu's middle and low-income neighbourhoods. Thus, existing disposal practices may not lead to safeguarding infants from exposure if not safely contained from the reach of children. Public health guidelines are important to guide community hygiene practices to achieve safe containment of animal faeces in low and middle-income neighbourhoods.

The study findings have also shown that household hygiene practices towards disposal of animal faeces may vary from one city to the other, which could be related to the city variations in policy, infrastructural development of the cities, and spacing. For example, neighbours in Nairobi were more congested compared to those in Kusumu. Previous studies underscore the importance of proper removal of animal faeces from domestic environments as a safeguard to prevention of zoonotic pathogen transmission through the oral-faecal route [3,21]. Inadequacy in disposal of animal faeces has been reported in some studies as linked to the perceived belief that animal faeces are not disgusting compared to human faeces [3,21]. The perceived belief could be a potential barrier to hygiene practice and effective intervention designs. Addressing such beliefs through presentation of evidence on pathogens contained in animal faeces as part of awareness creation is important to develop prioritization in management practices.

This study has some limitations. Household animal penning and faeces disposal practices were self-reported, which could have been influenced by courtesy bias. Observation of animal faeces and infant contact with animals, faeces, and their surfaces occurred over approximately 5 h on two different days. Important animal and animal faeces exposure behaviours among infants, as well as caregiver hygiene behaviours, may be common during times of the day when observations could not be performed. This could result in over/or underestimates of animal-infant contacts. Our study focused upon domestic animals belonging to urban households that could be managed with intervenable penning or hygiene practices. Faeces from rodents and wild animals also pose a health risk to humans but require different intervention strategies. In this study, all domestic animals other than cats were aggregated into a single exposure variable rather than examining them individually as cats. The reason for not disaggregating animal species other than cats was due to limited numbers of households with other species or limited counts of animal, animal faeces, or contaminated environment exposures and thus, insufficient statistical power to have them separately considered. This may obscure species-specific differences in risk and the potential for unmeasured confounding related to household animal ownership and infant exposure behaviours.

In this study, caregiver education and income were not included in the regressed models because our primary goal was to understand whether infant-animal exposures differ between lesser and more developed neighbourhoods. As shown in Table 1, the distribution of households by income level was highly similar between households in low-income neighbourhoods and between middle-income neighbourhoods. Thus, including household income in models would result in multicollinearity with neighbourhood SES, adding extra variation to the interval estimates. The absence of higher income households in the low-income neighbourhoods would further result in non-convergence issues when analyzing neighbourhood and household SES at the same time. We opted to keep neighbourhood SES as our covariate of choice to address primary study hypotheses. Regarding maternal education, there was also not enough information to achieve model convergence and thus the reason for its exclusion. Within-neighbourhood variation in household assets and education may also have contributed to differences in child exposures within the neighbourhood. However, the Kisumu low-income neighbourhood with the highest risk of animal contact among infants did not have the highest number of households earning less than 10,000 shillings or caregivers with a primary education. Similarly, the Nairobi MIC neighbourhood with the lowest risk of infant exposure to animal faeces or contaminated environments did not have the most households in higher income or higher education brackets. This could indicate other neighbourhood factors are more important than household SES and education in assessing risks from animals, or that income and general education level are imperfect measures of household SES and hygiene knowledge. Finally, contact with animals and their faeces does not always pose a health risk. However, exposure to some pathogens can still be asymptomatic or subclinical yet relevant to long-term health.

Analysis of enteric pathogens in animals, the environment, and children to understand relationships between animals, hygiene behaviours, and health was beyond the scope of this manuscript, but will be addressed in future PATHOME studies. Examining the relationship between animal hygiene indicators was an important step to proceed with further enteric disease modelling, but humans and animals transmit many pathogens between each other beyond enteric pathogens. The generalizable observations of human-animal hygiene interactions from this study can inform research on other disease ecologies and pathogen species. This study suggests that penning of animals could be a potential hygiene intervention area to limit infant exposures to animal faeces or animals from contaminating surfaces that infants may always be placed. However, contextual factors such as availability of space where animals can be penned as well as their safety concerns may have an influencing on animal penning behaviours.

Conclusion

This study has shown that infants between 6 months and one year are more susceptible to the risk of contact with animals, their faeces, or contaminated surfaces with animal faeces. Middle-income neighbourhood status decreased the chances of animal contact, except for cats, and infant touching of surfaces contaminated by animal faeces, but this varied by city. The full penning of animals significantly reduced the rate of contact with animal faeces and the likelihood of contact with surfaces contaminated by animal faeces. Future research should investigate enteric pathogens originating from contact with animals, their faeces or polluted surfaces with animal faeces that could be sources of infection in infants in such low- and middle-income urban communities and the effect of hygiene interventions. The behaviour and hygienic practices of caregivers to prevent their infants from contact with animals, their faeces and contaminated surfaces could protect them from getting infections. Lastly, the findings point to the need for integration of zoonotic hygiene into existing public health initiatives to provide guidance to safe animal domestication practices to reduce risk of infant exposures to disease transmissions.
