Effects of glutamate supplementation on feed consumption, intestinal histology, growth performance, muscle mass, intestinal barrier, and antioxidant-immune genes in the juvenile giant freshwater prawn, Macrobrachium rosenbergii
Authors: Thiraphon Deethaisong, Pannasa Manjit, Warinthip Vetkama, Ruchanok Tinikul, Prasert Sobhon, Yotsawan Tinikul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Glu-supplemented feed promoted food consumption and feeding behaviors in juvenile stage of M. rosenbergii.•Glu-supplemented feed improved intestinal histology and enhanced digestive enzyme activities.•Glu-supplemented feed increased growth performance.•Glu-supplemented feed upregulated the expression of muscle-growth- and antioxidant-immune-associated genes.Keywords: Digestibility, Feeding stimulant, Glutamate supplement, Growth promoter, Intestinal health, Juvenile prawn, Macrobrachium rosenbergii

Introduction

The giant freshwater prawn, Macrobrachium rosenbergii, is indigenous and widely cultured in tropical Asia, including China, India, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia, and Thailand. The prawns have commercial significance for domestic consumption and as a high-value export commodity (FAO, 2025). Nevertheless, freshwater prawn culturing in Thailand currently faces significant challenges, notably the high-density captive breeding system which increases stress and retards growth of juvenile prawns. Stress leads to diminished appetite, decreased food intake, stunted growth, heightened vulnerability to diseases and elevated mortality rates. Feeding is an important process for acquiring nutrients essential to energy production and growth in juvenile prawns. Numerous factors influence feeding, including food type, temperature, light, hormones, and neurotransmitters (Tierney, 2020; Volkoff, 2016; Volkoff & Rønnestad, 2020). These factors interact through complex regulatory mechanisms that control digestive function for nutrient absorption, energy balance, and growth. Increasing feeding rates can also lead to higher growth, improved health, as well as enhanced production and economic value of cultured prawns.

Glutamate (Glu) is a neurotransmitter and a versatile non-essential amino acid. Glu in intestinal cells stimulates neural networks, consequently influencing brain regions through nutrient sensing. This indicates that Glu is a significant regulator of food consumption, digestion, and nutrient absorption (Deethaisong et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2024). Currently, most research on the roles of Glu and its derivatives in feeding and growth has focused on fish; however, there is limited documentation available regarding its effects in crustaceans. In the Japanese yellow tail fish (Seriola quinqueradiata), MSG, a sodium derivative of glutamic acid, elevated food consumption and appetite-related peptides, including neuropeptide Y (NPY) expression, suggesting a correlation between Glu and NPY in controlling feeding (Senzui et al., 2020). In adult female M. rosenbergii, Glu promoted food intake in this adult prawn (Deethaisong et al., 2025). In tests with various chemoattractants for the slipper lobster (Thenus orientalis), l-Glu showed the highest average score among the monosodium salt forms (Teoh et al., 2023). Juvenile Chinese mitten crabs (Eriocheir sinensis) were fed diets with 0–2 % Glu supplementation, comprising low protein (30 %) and normal protein (35 %) levels, for a feeding period of 8 weeks. The WG and SGR showed slight improvements when their diets were supplemented with 1 % and 2 % Glu, with a protein content of 30 % (Zheng et al., 2024). In E. sinensis, 0.59 % MSG supplementation significantly improved the WG, SGR, and feed utilization. Additionally, moderate dietary MSG improved the morphology of the mid-intestine and substantially increased the appetite-promoting genes in the brain, including agouti-related protein (agrp) and ghrelin (Wang et al., 2024).

Apart from the role of Glu in feeding and growth, Glu modulates immunity in certain crustacean species. In the white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei), shrimp fed with a diet augmented with specific amounts of Glu exhibit increased resistance to Vibrio species and an enhanced flavor profile (Yamamoto & Inui-Yamamoto, 2023). The incorporation of dietary Glu at concentrations of 0.50 % or higher appears to exceed the metabolic capacity of L. vannamei, resulting in DNA damage. Additionally, improvements in resistance to Vibrio diseases and fillet quality at a lower dose of 0.25 % suggest that shrimp had a significantly lower tolerance for Glu supplementation compared to fish (Ma et al., 2025). At a protein concentration of 30 %, the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) substantially enhanced with the augmentation of Glu levels, suggesting that dietary Glu enhanced the antioxidant capacity under low protein conditions (Zheng et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the effects and underlying regulatory mechanisms of Glu on feed consumption, digestibility, growth, appetite-related peptides, and antioxidant–immune gene expression in juvenile freshwater prawn, M. rosenbergii, remain unclear. Given its physiological importance, Glu may serve as a key regulator of feeding, growth, and immune responses in juvenile prawns.

The aim of the study is to investigate the effects of Glu on feed consumption, digestive function, intestinal histomorphology, growth performance, intestinal barrier integrity, and antioxidant–immune gene expression in the juvenile stage of prawn M. rosenbergii. Additionally, the relationship between this neurotransmitter and expression of appetite-controlling neuropeptide F (NPF), an ortholog to the vertebrate NPY was examined. Our findings provide insights into the effects and regulatory mechanisms of Glu on feeding, growth performance, muscle mass, and antioxidant-immune genes in juvenile prawns. This essential foundational knowledge may assist in the development of formulated feed additives to enhance feeding efficiency, stimulate growth, reduce feed costs, and ultimately increase juvenile prawn production in aquaculture.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

Juvenile freshwater prawns (M. rosenbergii) (weighing about 3–4 g BW) were obtained from a farm in Samut Prakan Province, Thailand. The animals were cultured in 1.50-m diameter fiberglass containers. Dissolved oxygen remained above 5 mg/L and pH levels were kept between 7 and 8.5. The water temperature was maintained between 28 °C and 30 °C, salinity ranged from 1 to 3 ppt, ammonia–nitrogen levels were below 0.1 mg/L, and nitrite–nitrogen levels were below 0.5 mg/L. Mineral levels were maintained at 600 ppm for 6 weeks and increased to 800 ppm by the final week of the study, with ∼30 % of the water replaced every week. The mineral was composed of potassium, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, sodium, chloride, sulfate, and bicarbonate. Hardness was 50 – 200 mg/L, and alkalinity ranged from 80 – 150 mg/L. The photoperiod was maintained at a 12-h light–dark cycle. Shelter was provided by providing cages in each container. The animals were acclimated for at least two weeks before the commencement of the experiments.

Effects of Glu supplementation on feeding and digestibility

Juvenile prawns were divided into control and Glu-supplemented groups (n = at least 60 juveniles per group), as follows: individuals in the G1 control group (Ctrl) were fed commercial feeds without supplements, while those in the G2 and G3 groups received commercial feeds supplemented with Glu (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at doses of 55 and 550 mg/kg feed, respectively. In this study, a preliminary screening was conducted to determine an appropriate dose range for evaluating feed intake, growth performance, and survival. The two doses used were selected based on the results of this screening, as well as applied from previous studies demonstrating their potential to stimulate physiological processes in the freshwater prawn, M. rosenbergii (Deethaisong et al., 2025; Tinikul et al., 2023). Therefore, the selected doses of 55 and 550 mg/kg are considered suitable for promoting feed intake, growth, and immune function in juvenile prawns. The commercial feeds (1.5 %–2 % of BW) were obtained from Charoen Pokphand Group Public Company Limited (C.P. Group), Thailand. These feeds contain ingredients with proximate compositions as follows: (1) 36 % crude protein, achieved using a mixture of fish meal, squid meal or squid liver powder, and soybean meal or steamed soybeans; (2) a carbohydrate fraction composed of wheat flour, rice flour, and corn; (3) a lipid fraction derived from marine fish oil; and (4) micronutrients, including vitamins and minerals. The final composition also included 4 % crude fat, 5 % fiber, and 14 % moisture. The pellets were freshly prepared every week, and they were immersed in distilled water mixed with Glu for at least 5 min and dried for 10 min, and kept at −20 °C until use. These procedures were performed based on the description by Kiris et al., 2004 and Christie et al. (2011), with some modifications. Juveniles were fed at 9:00 a.m. and 6:00 pm. during the experiments.

Feed conversion ratio (FCR), and food efficiency rate (FER) were assessed after feeding with supplemented diets. Pellets were provided to juveniles at 9:00 a.m., and after 2 h, any uneaten food was collected with a fishing net, dried, and weighed to a constant weight. Each experiment was performed in triplicate to guarantee accuracy and reliability. Feeding parameters were calculated as described by Javahery et al. (2019), Soares et al. (2005), and Deethaisong et al. (2025), with minor modifications.

The feed performance indicators for juvenile prawns, encompassing gastric emptying (GE), midgut index (MGI), hepatosomatic index (HSI), digestive enzyme activity, and feeding behaviors, were carefully assessed and recorded at 12-week intervals. Juvenile prawns from each culture tank were gently handled during assessments of all indicators to minimize potential stress on the animals. To assess GE, MGI, and HSI, juveniles (n = a minimum of 15 juveniles per group) were subjected to a 20-h starvation period, commencing after 9:00 pm. This deprivation phase was designed to promote intestinal evacuation and presumably, to enhance appetite. Following the food deprivation period, juveniles were administered pellets for a duration of 2 h. At least 2 h was allowed for food to pass through the foregut (FG) and midgut (MG). The FG, MG, hindgut (HG), and hepatopancreas (HP) were carefully dissected out.

The indicators provided by Santos and Pontes (2016) and Tierney et al. (2020) were employed to assess and record feeding behaviors every three weeks, with minor adjustments. Evaluations and recordings were performed between 2:00 pm. and 3:00 pm. The feeding behaviors for the Glu-supplemented groups and the control groups were assessed during weeks 3–12. Three feeding behaviors were identified: foraging (searching-for-food behavior), approaching food (moving-toward-food behavior), and competing for food (fighting-for-food behavior).

Analysis of digestive enzyme activity after Glu supplementation

Juvenile prawns were fed with Glu-supplemented diets at 6:00 pm. every three weeks. Gut samples were taken from all Glu-supplemented and control groups (a minimum of 120 animals per group) at 10:00 pm. and preserved at −20 °C for subsequent analysis. The assessment of digestive enzyme activity was conducted according to the prior description. The activities of digestive enzymes were quantified in units per milligram of protein (U/mg) (Furné et al., 2005; Vetkama et al., 2026).

Evaluation of growth performance after Glu supplementation

The growth performance was assessed weekly from weeks 1 to 12, using a protocol adapted from previous studies (Christie et al., 2011). All groups were fed at 9:00 a.m. and allowed to consume feed for 2 h. Subsequently, growth performance metrics (n = 120 juveniles per group, 30 juveniles per tank with four replicates) were calculated using established equations (Javahery et al., 2019).

Examination of changes in gut and muscle histomorphology and histochemistry after Glu supplementation

The intestines and muscles of juveniles from a minimum of 15 prawns/group were dissected out after administration of the Glu-supplemented diets at weeks 3, 6, 9, and 12. The tissues of digestive organs, abdominal muscles (AMs), and pleopod muscles (PLMs) were immersed in 4 % paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M PBS. The samples were then sectioned into 4 µm-thick and further performed with H & E method. ImageJ software (version 1.54d, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA; available on the internet: http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij/) was used to evaluate the number and diameter of epithelial (Ep) cells, the height and width of villi, and the depth of crypts. The methodologies employed were based on those described (Guo et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2023). Villi and Ep cells were quantified in a minimum of 25 prawns. At least 30 Ep cells were identified in the FG areas. The MG was quantified from ≥10 prawns at each villus, while the HG was assessed for villi and gland cells. In the HG glands, at least 25 acini and 25 villi were evaluated. For histochemical analyses, the 5 µm sections were treated with a series of ethanol. The PAS and AB (Abcam, USA) stains were employed to visualize glycogen and glycoproteins in the sections of the gut tissues. In addition to the measurement of gut histological parameters, mean fiber diameter and fiber cross-sectional area of the AMs and PLMs were quantified.

Analysis of Glu supplementation on NPF and genes related to muscle growth and antioxidant-immunity

Juveniles were sampled at 3-week intervals, with 30 individuals collected per group. Following Glu supplementation, the mRNA expression levels of MrNPF, MrTOR, eIF4B, and LIM in muscle tissue, antioxidant- and immune genes in guts, were assessed using RT-qPCR. Briefly, RNA was isolated using the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). High-quality RNA was brought for cDNA synthesis with iScript Reverse Transcription. Subsequently, qPCR was performed using KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Master Mix (KAPA Biosystems, USA) and gene-specific primers (Table 1) on a CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR (Bio-Rad). The thermal cycling of qPCR conditions were: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 3 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 65 °C for 5 s. Each qPCR experiment included a negative control lacking cDNA. A calculation of relative gene expression was conducted according to the 2−ΔΔCt method, with elongation factor-1α gene serving as an internal control (Vetkama et al., 2026).

Analysis of NPF expression and MUC thickness in the gut after Glu supplementation

The expression of MrNPF-immunoreactivity (-ir) and MUCs-like-ir was evaluated following supplementation with Glu (n = at least 15 animals per group). Briefly, the gut samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde and cut at the thickness of 5 μm. Sections were incubated with primary antisera: rabbit anti-MUC2 (1:500, Abcam); goat anti-MUC19 (1:400, Abcam); mouse anti-E-cadherin (1:400, Santa Cruz Biotechnology); or rabbit anti-MrNPF (1:400) (Vetkama et al., 2025). Sections were immersed with secondary antibodies—Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit, Alexa Fluor 568 goat anti-mouse IgG, or Alexa Fluor 647 donkey anti-goat (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA)—each diluted 1:500 for 2 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich), diluted 1:1000. The images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope. The thickness assessment of MUCs-like-ir layers was conducted as reported (Liu et al., 2020), with minor modifications, while the measurement of intensities was made utilizing ImageJ software (version 1.54d, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA; available on the internet: http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij/)

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed for statistical significance using SPSS Statistics 27.0.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), employing a one-way analysis of variance and Duncan’s multiple range test. A probability value <0.05 (P < 0.05) indicates a statistically significant difference.

Results

Feed consumption

At weeks 6 and 10, the juvenile prawns fed with a supplemented higher dose (550 mg/kg feed) of Glu showed a reduced FCR, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 1A). Both doses of Glu-supplemented feed demonstrated a significantly elevated FER percentage at weeks 4, 10, and 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 1B). Nevertheless, only a high dose of Glu exhibited markedly higher FER at week 6 (P < 0.05; Fig. 1B). Every week, a high-dose Glu-supplement diets demonstrated significantly decreased mean GE scores (P < 0.05; Fig. 1C). Both doses of Glu supplementation exhibited considerably lower MGI scores at weeks 9 and 12; however, the groups fed with a high dose of Glu-supplemented diets also showed markedly decreased MGI at week 6, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 1D).

Feeding behaviors

For feeding behavioral analysis, Glu-supplemented groups promoted searching-for-food behavior at week 9, while those receiving a low dose of Glu supplementation, showed markedly enhanced searching-for-food behavior at weeks 9 and 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 1E). Both doses of Glu-supplemented meal markedly enhanced fighting-for-food behavior activity at weeks 9 and 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 1E). Notably, most juvenile prawns receiving Glu supplementation exhibited faster feed consumption and greater aggression than the control groups, suggesting enhanced feeding motivation, competitive interactions for food, and foraging efficiency. Glu-induced fighting behavior may also be explained by its role in promoting rapid energy, thereby increasing short-term energy availability and facilitating rapid movements associated with aggressive interactions. The prawns fed with the supplemented feed with only a high dose of Glu showed an elevated HSI value at week 6, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 1F).

Digestive enzyme activity

Amylase activity in the FG was promoted following a high dose of Glu supplementation at week 3. However, a low dose of Glu-supplemented feed increased amylase activity in the FG at week 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A). In the MG and HG, both doses of Glu-supplemented diets led to a significant enhancement in amylase activity at week 3, while a low-dose Glu-supplemented meal stimulated amylase activity at week 12. In the HP, both doses of Glu supplementation markedly increased amylase levels at week 9, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A).

Asterisks indicate significant differences (P < 0.05), compared with the control groups.

Protease activity was significantly increased in the FG in juveniles fed a high-dose Glu-supplemented diet at weeks 3 and 6, whereas in those fed with a low-dose Glu-supplemented diet at weeks 6 and 12. Furthermore, both doses of Glu-supplemented diets considerably increased protease activity in the MG and HG at week 6, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 2B). In the HP, groups fed with a high-dose Glu-supplemented diet exhibited a notable enhancement in protease activity at weeks 6, 9, and 12, while those fed diets supplemented with a low dose of Glu promoted this enzyme activity at weeks 3 and 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 2B).

Additionally, lipase activity in the FG was markedly raised in prawns fed diets supplemented with a low dose of Glu at weeks 9 and 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 2C), while in those that received meals supplemented with a low dose of Glu, lipase activity in the MG and HG was promoted at weeks 3 and 9. In the HP, both doses of Glu supplementation considerably increased lipase activity at weeks 9 and 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 2C).

Gut histology and histomorphology

The EpH in the ESO and CD was greater in groups fed with a low dose of Glu-supplemented diets at week 6, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 3A-C, Z). The EpH, VW, and VH in the FG regions were considerably larger with all doses of Glu-supplemented meals at week 12 (P < 0.05; Fig. 3D-F, Z). In the MG, the EpH was markedly increased in groups that received a high dose of Glu-supplemented diets, exclusively at week 6 (P < 0.05; Fig. 3G-I, A’).

Growth performance

The sizes of prawns supplemented with two doses of Glu were larger than those of the control groups during weeks 6, 9 and 12 (Fig. 4D-L). It is noteworthy that juvenile prawns receiving the higher dose of Glu (550 mg/kg feed) exhibited a gradual increase in growth rate from week 6 onward, compared with the control group. WG was markedly elevated in juvenile groups that received meals enriched with a high dose of Glu throughout weeks 8, 10, and 12, whereas WG was increased in prawns fed diets with a low dose of Glu at weeks 8 and 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 4M). In addition, GR was promoted in prawns fed diets supplemented with both doses of Glu at weeks 10 and 12, compared with the control groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 4N).

Muscle histomorphometry

At week 6, the diameters of the AMs and PLMs were notably larger in groups receiving a high dose of Glu, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 5A-C, G-I, M). At week 12, the diameter of the AMs was markedly increased in prawns fed diets mixed with a low dose of Glu (P < 0.05; Fig. 5D-F, M), while the PLM diameters were increased in prawns fed diets supplemented with a high dose of Glu at week 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 5J-L, M). The fiber areas of the AMs and PLMs in all doses of Glu-supplemented juveniles were substantially greater at week 6, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 5A-C, G-I, N). Additionally, the fiber areas of the AMs showed a substantial increase in prawns fed diets supplemented with a low- and high-dose Glu at week 12 (P < 0.05; Fig. 5D-F, N). Nevertheless, the fiber areas of the PLMs were markedly greater at week 12 in prawns fed diets supplemented with only a high dose of Glu, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 5J-L, N).

Muscle growth-associated genes

High expression of MrTOR, LIM, and elF4B transcripts in the AMs were observed in prawns fed diets enriched with both doses of Glu during weeks 3 to 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 5O, Q, S). Similarly, muscle growth-stimulating genes in the PLMs subjected to diets supplemented with both doses of Glu were also significantly upregulated from weeks 3 to 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 5P, R, T).

Expression of appetite-related gene in guts and muscles

The MrNPF transcript in the FG was markedly upregulated in groups receiving both doses of Glu supplements at weeks 3, 6, and 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 6A-B, I-J, U). The MG showed a notable increase in MrNPF mRNA levels in groups given diets with a low dose of Glu at weeks 6 and 9 (Fig. 6C-D, K-L, V), whereas those fed with a low-dose Glu-supplemented diet exhibited increased levels in the HG during week 9 (P < 0.05; Fig. 6E-F, M-N, W). In the HP, the MrNPF mRNA levels were increased in groups fed with a high dose of Glu-supplemented meal at weeks 3 to 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 6G-H, O-P, X). However, a significant increase in the MrNPF mRNA level in the HP was seen in prawns on diets supplemented with a low dose of Glu at weeks 6 and 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 6X). In the context of the fluorescence intensity, MrNPF-immunoreactivity (-ir) in the FG, MG, and HG had a significant enhancement in prawns fed diets with a high dose of Glu-supplemented diets at weeks 6 and 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 6Y). Furthermore, the levels of the MrNPF transcript in the AMs showed a notable increase following diet supplementation with a high dose of Glu at week 12 (P < 0.05; Fig. 6Q-T, Z). Glu supplements also significantly enhanced MrNPF-ir intensity in the AMs at week 12, compared with the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 6A′).

Discussion

Our findings show that feed consumption, digestive enzyme activities, and gut histochemistry of juvenile M. rosenbergii were significantly improved by supplementing feeds with Glu. Glu increased the growth performance by promoting WG, GR, AGD, and SGR, enhancing the expression of genes related to muscle growth, and improving muscle histology. Furthermore, Glu upregulated the antioxidant and immune-related genes. Our results clearly suggest that Glu stimulated food intake, promoted growth performance, and maintained the health status in the juvenile stage of M. rosenbergii.

The present work showed that Glu promoted feed consumption, improved intestinal histology, promoted the activities of digestive enzymes, and increased HSI in juvenile prawns, M. rosenbergii. In juvenile rainbow trout (O. mykiss), the supplementation of dietary Glu promoted the digestion of fatty acids, suggesting that Glu improved nutrient digestibility (Belghit et al., 2023). In grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella), elevated Glu levels significantly increased the HSI, trypsin activity in the HP and intestine, lipase activity in the proximal and mid-intestine, and the height of intestinal folds in the proximal and mid-intestine. This suggests that dietary Glu positively affected intestinal morphology to increase absorptive capacity (Zhao et al., 2015). In little yellow croaker (Larimichthys polyactis), the incorporation of 0.4 %–1.2 % Glu in the feed markedly increased muscle thickness, villus height, and the activities of digestive enzymes. The findings indicate that Glu can improve intestine digestive capacity, stimulate intestinal glutathione synthesis to mitigate intestinal damage and preserve intestinal health (Liu et al., 2025). Our findings in juvenile prawn, M. rosenbergii indicated that both doses of Glu-supplemented prawns resulted in increased FER during weeks 4 to 12. Furthermore, a high-dose Glu-supplemented diet led to significant reductions in GE and MGI values at weeks 6, 10, and 12, while these values decreased in those fed a low-dose Glu-supplemented meal during weeks 10 and 12. The findings were supported by enhanced activities of digestive enzymes, an increase in HSI, and improvements in gut histology following Glu supplementation. This ultimately resulted in better stimulation of feeding activity, improved feed conversion, enhanced digestive efficiency, and increased nutrient absorption in juvenile prawns. Our results are consistent with those found in L. polyactis, but our applied doses were much lower than those used in this fish (Liu et al., 2025). Notably, in the present study, a low-dose Glu-supplemented diet (55 mg/kg feed) significantly enhanced feeding rates by approximately 50 %, 50 %, and 35 % at weeks 4, 10, and 12, respectively. In comparison, a high-dose Glu-supplemented diet (550 mg/kg feed) resulted in feeding rates of approximately 50 %, 60 %, and 60 % at the corresponding time points, compared with the control groups. Collectively, these findings suggest that a higher dose of Glu may be more effective in improving feeding performance and may have practical applications in aquaculture for juvenile M. rosenbergii.

Supplementation with Glu showed higher growth rates, including WG, GR, and SGR, as well as larger prawn sizes at weeks 8 to 12 in juvenile M. rosenbergii. In C. carpio, adding 10 and 20 g/kg of Glu to a low-phosphorus diet significantly enhanced WGR and SGR, suggesting that Glu can improve growth performance (Li et al., 2020). In juvenile yellow croaker (L. polyactis), diets supplemented with 0.4 %–1.2 % Glu significantly increased SGR (Liu et al., 2025). In largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides), diets containing up to approximately 15 % Glu enhanced final body weight and WG (Zhao et al., 2025). In E. sinensis, the WG and SGR were not substantially influenced by dietary Glu (Zheng et al., 2024). In L. vannamei, Glu supplementation exceeding 0.25 % increased muscle Glu content, which may influence on their growth (Ma et al., 2025). In vertebrates, the regulation of muscle growth is governed by mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and muscle LIM, which are muscle growth-related genes. mTOR serves as a vital regulator of skeletal muscle protein synthesis, governing numerous phases of myogenesis and growth (Dai et al., 2024). The activation of the complex results in the phosphorylation of two critical substrate groups: eIF4E binding proteins and ribosomal S6 kinases, which facilitate growth and other cellular anabolic functions (Bond, 2016). In this study, our findings demonstrated that a high dose of Glu-supplemented diet was capable of significantly promoting growth performance by enhancing WG, GR, ADG, and SGR from weeks 8 to 12 in juvenile prawns, while a low dose of Glu showed similar growth-promoting effects at slightly later time from weeks 10 to 12. The findings in juvenile M. rosenbergii are consistent with the growth-promoting effects of Glu reported in L. vannamei (Ma et al., 2025) and L. polyactis (Liu et al., 2025), but differ from those observed in E. sinensis (Zheng et al., 2024). These results suggest that the effects of Glu on growth may be both dose- and species-specific manner.

Considering the doses used in this study, both Glu-supplemented juvenile prawns exhibited increased body size and muscle mass at weeks 8 and 12. This was supported by larger muscle fiber diameters and increased fiber density, along with the upregulation of genes associated with muscle growth. Collectively, these findings suggest that Glu may serve as an effective growth promoter in juvenile prawns. Notably, the SR of juvenile prawns supplemented with a low dose of Glu was higher than that of the control group at weeks 2 and 6, whereas those fed a high-dose Glu-supplemented diet showed improved SR at weeks 2, 8, and 12. From an economic perspective, Glu is a cost-effective and readily available additive (approximately 0.3 USD/g), supporting its potential application in aquaculture. Taken together, we propose that Glu supplementation at 550 mg/kg feed represents the most effective dose for promoting growth and may be a practical option for aquaculture applications in juvenile prawns. Nevertheless, considering the potential adverse effects of Glu at higher doses (e.g., metabolic burden), the doses used in this study were selected based on preliminary trials and previous literature. No evidence of negative effects on survival or general health was observed under the experimental conditions. Comprehensive safety evaluations, particularly at higher dose levels, are still required, as well as detailed physiological and metabolic assessments.

In invertebrates, NPF, a homolog of vertebrate NPY, regulates appetite, feeding, growth, and reproduction in various insects and crustacean species (Fadda et al., 2019; Vetkama et al., 2024, 2026). In Mandarin fish (Siniperca chuatsi), l-Glu modulates NPY via the mGluR4–Ca2+–ERK1/2 signaling pathway. To further investigate the involvement of ERK1/2 in NPY regulation by l-Glu, researchers employed the ERK1/2 inhibitor U0126, which abolished the L‑Glu‑induced increase in NPY mRNA expression, suggesting that l-Glu may modulate NPY expression through ERK1/2 signaling pathways (Duan et al., 2024). In yellowtail (S. quinqueradiata), umami substances, including MSG, influenced food intake and increased NPY expression, suggesting a potential association with Glu (Senzui et al., 2020). Our investigation revealed that supplementation with both doses of Glu significantly increased MrNPF mRNA and protein levels in the gut and associated AM tissues at weeks 3 and 12. These tissues play key roles in controlling the feeding apparatus, muscle activity, food transport, and nutrient absorption in crustaceans. Collectively, these findings suggest a functional interaction between Glu and NPF in regulating feeding activity in the juvenile prawn, M. rosenbergii. These results are also consistent with previous studies in fish, which reported an orexigenic effect associated with elevated NPY/NPF mRNA levels following Glu supplementation (Duan et al., 2024). In addition, our findings provide clear evidence of a synergistic interaction between Glu and NPF, extending from the juvenile stage (as shown in the present study) to the adult stage (Deethaisong et al., 2025). This interaction suggests that Glu, as a neurotransmitter, works in concert with NPF to regulate gut and muscle cell functions, thereby promoting feeding activity and muscle growth during the juvenile stage of M. rosenbergii. However, the precise molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways underlying the interaction between these two molecules in the regulation of feeding remain to be elucidated.

The juvenile prawns fed diets enriched with both doses of Glu markedly increased MUC levels and mucus layer thickness, which were corroborated by the greater number of cells positively stained for PAS/AB. MUCs are highly glycosylated proteins that constitute the primary structure of the intestinal mucus layer. This layer in the gut parts functions as a dynamic and selective barrier that captures and neutralizes pathogens while maintaining a symbiotic relationship with beneficial microorganisms, which is essential for immunity. Glu has an ability to augment gut integrity and improve mucosal barrier function via the synthesis of mucin (Jiao et al., 2015; Święch et al., 2022). The enhanced quantity and dispersion of goblet cells in the intestines of juveniles Morone saxatilis ♀ × Morone chrysops ♂, suggesting that Glu supplementation could influence mucosal immunity, as these cells serve as characteristic morphological indicators of an active mucosal immune response (Hissen et al., 2023). In the present study, we observed the upregulation of MUC mRNA and proteins, along with increased mucin thickness in the digestive organs of juvenile prawns through weeks 3 to 12, indicating that Glu supplementation potentially enhanced gut protection by fortifying the intestinal barrier integrity and maintaining gut health during the juvenile prawn growth.

Furthermore, juvenile prawns fed diets containing two doses of Glu supplements exhibited increased expression of antioxidant and immune genes in the gut parts, including SOD, HSP70, ALF2, and HEMO1 mRNA, from weeks 3 to 12. Antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD, are essential for neutralizing surplus reactive oxygen species and protecting cells from oxidative harm (Guo et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2025). In crustaceans, HSP70 serves as a vital molecular chaperone that helps maintain cellular protein homeostasis and functions as a key component of the innate immune system, especially in response to various environmental and pathological stresses (Junprung et al., 2021). ALF2 is essential for the innate immune system, primarily functioning as an antimicrobial agent. Additionally, ALF2 acts as a vital molecule in eliminating pathogens and modulating the host immune response (Li & Li, 2020). In arthropods and mollusks, HEMO1 is a protein mostly present in the hemolymph, crucial for oxygen transport and the immunological response. Previous studies have indicated the involvement of Glu in antioxidant immunity in fish and crustacean species. In yellow croaker (L. polyactis), the addition of 0.4 %–1.2 % Glu in the diet significantly improved serum activity and antioxidant enzymes, including glutathione peroxidase and glutathione synthetase, indicating that the Glu can mitigate gut damage and improve gut immunity (Liu et al., 2025). In grass carp (C. idella), dietary Glu supplementation enhanced scavenging of superoxide anion and hydroxyl radicals and elevated glutathione reductase and SOD activities, suggesting that Glu increased intestinal antioxidant capacity and immunity in this species (Zhoa et al., 2015). In juvenile E. sinensis, supplementing the 0 %–2 % Glu and 30 % protein markedly elevated the activity of SOD and antioxidant capacity, suggesting that Glu can ameliorate antioxidant capacity in a dose-specific manner or nutritional factors (Zheng et al., 2024). In the present study, significant enhancements in the expression levels of SOD, HSP70, ALF2, and HEMO1 genes were observed across various digestive organs of prawns fed diets supplemented with Glu from weeks 6 to 12. These positive findings are consistent with results reported for E. sinensis (Zheng et al., 2024). Notably, Glu supplementation in our study significantly upregulated antioxidant–immune-related genes even at much lower doses than those used for E. sinensis (Zheng et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings suggest that Glu supplementation supports the maintenance of intestinal health in juvenile M. rosenbergii.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that dietary Glu supplementation enhanced feed intake, improved intestinal histology, promoted growth performance, and upregulated muscle growth–related genes in juvenile M. rosenbergii. In addition, Glu strengthened the intestinal barrier and increased the expression of antioxidant- and immune-related genes. Overall, supplementation at 550 mg/kg feed was the most effective dose within the tested range for improving feed intake, growth efficiency, and health status. However, further studies using a wider range of dosages are needed to establish a clear dose–response relationship and determine the optimal Glu level.
