A dosimetric comparison of brachytherapy sources for endometrial cancer: an electronic brachytherapy and an iridium-192 source with multichannel cylinders and a three-dimensional technique
Authors: Wenhui Wang, Bei Wang, Lang Yu, Hongnan Zhen, Yue Zhang, Siqi Feng, Zhou Chen, Yuan Zhang, Jie Qiu, Fuquan Zhang, Ke Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeIr192 vaginal brachytherapy (IBT) is commonly used for patients with postoperative endometrial cancer (EC). We devised a novel multichannel vaginal applicator that could be equipped with an electronic brachytherapy (EBT) device. We aimed to explore the differences in physical parameters between the EBT and IBT.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study included 20 EC patients who received adjuvant IBT from March 1, 2023, to May 1, 2023. Multichannel vaginal cylinders were used, and three-dimensional plans were generated. We designed an electronic multichannel vaginal applicator model and simulated a three-dimensional EBT plan. In order to ensure comparability, D90 of the CTV for the EBT plan was normalized to be equivalent to that of the IBT plan for the same patient.ResultsTwenty EBT plans were compared with 20 IBT plans. Results showed, the mean D90 value of clinical target volume (CTV) was 536.1 cGy for both treatment plans. For the mean dose of CTV, the EBT was significantly greater (738.3 vs. 684.3 cGy, p = 0.000). There was no significant difference in CTV coverage between the EBT and IBT plans. For high-dose areas (V200% and V150%), the EBTs were significantly greater. There were no significant differences in the maximum doses to the vaginal mucosa between the EBT and IBT, whether at the apex or in the middle segment. For the bladder and rectum, both the low-dose area and high-dose area were significantly lower in the EBT plans. For the conformity index, there was no significant difference between the EBT and IBT plans. For the dose homogeneity index, the EBT value was lower.ConclusionIn conclusion, under the premise of a three-dimensional brachytherapy plan, for patients receiving multichannel vaginal applicator brachytherapy, compared with IBT, EBT could reduce the dose to the surrounding organs at risk while maintaining the dose in the target area.

Background

Endometrial cancer (EC) is the second most common gynecological tumor in China. Surgery is the main treatment method, and for postoperative early-stage EC patients with risk factors, adjuvant vaginal brachytherapy can reduce the recurrence rate [1]. Currently, postoperative radiotherapy usually relies on iridium-192 high-dose-rate brachytherapy (IBT). However, the risk of source blockage and radiation exposure may occur during transportation and treatment. A decrease in radio-source activity over time can result in high economic costs. For the above reasons, radiation sources are difficult to obtain, which leads to the inability of postoperative EC patients to receive standard treatment, resulting in a high recurrence rate. In addition, due to the risk of radiation exposure from radioactive sources, shielding devices are needed. Therefore, how to reduce radiation exposure risks while maintaining clinical efficacy has become an urgent problem.

Electronic brachytherapy (EBT) is a new type of radiotherapy technique that utilizes an electronic source to generate low-energy X-rays. It was initially used for intraoperative radiotherapy (IORT) for breast cancer patients. Later, with the production of various applicators, it began to be applied in other tumors. The X-ray brachytherapy device used in this study (Xoft® Axxent® electronic brachytherapy system) has a variety of applicator systems, including vaginal, cervical, balloon, and surface applicators. Currently, it has been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of various diseases, such as postoperative vaginal brachytherapy for endometrial cancer. The electronic brachytherapy device uses a 50-kV X-ray, which is characterized by a rapid decrease in dose with distance. Previous physical dosimetric studies [2, 3] have shown that, compared to Ir-192 vaginal brachytherapy, EBT provides equivalent target volume coverage while simultaneously reducing the doses to the organs at risk (OAR). Clinical studies [4–6] have demonstrated that Xoft has excellent clinical treatment efficacy and low toxicity.

In terms of the types of vaginal applicators used, the single-channel form was the only type available for electronic brachytherapy machines and was used in previous studies for postoperative vaginal stump irradiation among endometrial cancer patients. However, dosimetric studies have shown that compared to single-channel applicators, multichannel applicators can significantly reduce the dose for organs at risk (OARs) while achieving equal target volume coverage [7]. Moreover, multichannel applicators have better conformity [8]. In addition, radiotherapy treatment plans made in previous studies were two-dimensional, which means that in terms of the area irradiated, there was no adduction in the rectum or bladder and no dilation in the thick mucosa at the top of the vagina due to surgical resection and suture methods. The irradiated area did not reflect the real target volume. Therefore, this study retrospectively analyzed postoperative EC patients who received Ir-192 high-dose-rate (HDR) vaginal brachytherapy at our institution. All patients were treated with multichannel cylinders and underwent a three-dimensional (3D) treatment plan. We designed a multichannel applicator that could be applied in Xoft electronic brachytherapy. The 3D treatment plan of the EBT was simulated, and its physical dosimetric characteristics were compared with those of the IBT plan.

Methods and materials

Patients and treatment plans

This was a retrospective research. A total of 20 consecutive postoperative EC patients treated at our institution from March 1, 2023 to May 1, 2023 were included in this research. The inclusion criteria was: radical surgery; postoperative Ir192 high-dose rate vaginal brachytherapy alone; with the dose of 30 Gy in 6 fractions;30 mm diameter vaginal applicator. Patients with the following clinical scenarios were excluded: palliative resection; previous radiotherapy; other brachytherapy prescription doses; individualized vaginal applicator; missing data. During the CT scan, a 3 cm diameter multichannel cylinder was inserted into the patient’s vagina (Fig. 1a). CT scans with 2.5 mm slice thickness were performed for all patients at the status of a filling bladder and an empty rectum before the treatment plan. The bladder, rectum, sigmoid colon and small intestines were contoured and evaluated. The clinical target volume (CTV) was defined as a 5 mm expansion of the vaginal applicator and an extension of 3–5 cm in length. The irradiation length of CTV depended on the risk factors. If there were fewer risk factors, such as low grade (G1-2) or superficial muscle invasion, the irradiated length would be 3 cm. If the pathology demonstrated a large amount of lympho-vascular space invasion, or the surgical removal of the vagina was too short, the irradiation length should be extended to 5 cm. The surrounding OARs, such as the rectum, bladder, sigmoid colon and small intestines, were excluded, and thick mucosal areas, such as the top of the vaginal stump, were included. The prescription dose was 30 Gy in 6 fractions. A three-dimensional treatment plan was made through an Oncentra treatment planning system (TPS) before the first treatment, and the subsequent 5 fractions were generated according to this plan. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital (N0. I-23PJ1425).

When designing the multichannel applicator, in order to ensure the same degree of dilation of the vaginal mucosa, we defined the simulated applicator diameter as 3 cm, which was the same as the Ir192 vaginal applicator. In addition, considering the diameter (5 mm) of Xoft source pipe and the adjustability of the dose distribution, we simulated a multichannel (2-3-2 layout) model (Fig. 1b). Three channels were arranged every 7.5 mm in the midline. Two channels were arranged in the upper row, with their centers located at 1/3 of the horizontal midline and 6 mm vertical away from it. The same layout was applied to arrange the third row of channels. Afterwards, we generated a new 3D plan using an electronic brachytherapy source. Therefore, we compared 20 IBT plans with 20 EBT plans.

We exported patients’ IBT DICOM files from the Oncentra TPS. The CT and Rt structure files were imported into BrachyCare TPS (V1.1.0). A multichannel (2-3-2 layout) applicator was used to simulate the EBT dose distribution based on the AAPM TG43 model. The electronic brachytherapy plans according to the prescription requirements at our institution were made and exported as Rt Dose files. The DICOM files (including CT, RT structure and RT dose files) for IBT and EBT were imported into the RayStation TPS. Physical dosimetric comparisons were performed on the RayStation TPS while ensuring that the D90 of the CTV (dose of 90% of the target volume) for the EBT plan was normalized to be equivalent to that of the IBT plan for the same patient.

Dosimetric assessment

All the treatment plans were evaluated for the following dose parameters: V200%, V150%, V100%, V95%, V90% (which were the percentages of CTV receiving 200%, 150%, 100%, 95% and 90% of the prescription dose, respectively), D98, D90 (which were the doses for 98% and 90% of the target volume, respectively) and Dmean (which was the mean dose for the target volume) for CTV. The surface dose for the vaginal surface was evaluated for the top and middle of the target volume. For the bladder and rectum, V35%, V50% (the percentage of the bladder or rectum receiving 35% and 50% of the prescription dose, respectively), D2 cc, D1 cc and D0.1 cc (the highest dose to 2 cc, 1 cc and 0.1 cc of the bladder or rectum, respectively) were evaluated. For the sigmoid and small intestine, D2 cc (which was the highest dose to a 2 cc of sigmoid or small intestine) was evaluated.

The following parameters were evaluated: quality indices, conformity index (COIN), dose homogeneity index (DHI), dose nonuniformity ratio (DNR) and overdose index (OI). The COIN and DHI were defined as the ratio of the volume receiving at least 95% or 100–150% of the prescribed dose to the total volume of CTV (COIN = V95%/VCTV, DHI = V100 − 150%/VCTV). The DNR and OI were defined as the ratio of the volume receiving ≥ 150% or ≥ 200% of the prescribed dose to the volume receiving ≥ 100% of the prescribed dose (DNR = V150%/V100%, OI = V200%/V100%).

Statistical analysis

SPSS statistical software (version 25.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) was used to perform the data analysis. The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov method was used to test for normality. Student’s t test was used to assess normally distributed variables. The Mann‒Whitney U test was used to assess non-normally distributed variables. The Levene method was used to test the homogeneity of variance. The Cohen’s d analysis was used to calculate effect size. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

CTV doses

Baseline clinical characteristics for the included patients were showed in Table 1. For the 20 patients, according to the simulated electronic internal irradiation plan, the average treatment time was 154.1 s (range, 22.1 to 172.1 s). Table 2 shows the dose coverage for the CTV. Comparisons between physical parameters among 20 EBT plans and 20 IBT plans were conducted. The mean D90 was 536.1 cGy for both treatment plans. The target coverage of the two radiotherapy methods was similar. There were no significant differences in the V100%, V95%, V90% or D98 between the EBT and IBT plans. However, the high-dose area of EBT was wider. For V200%, the dose for EBT was almost three times greater than that for IBT. For V150%, the EBT value was still significantly greater than the IBT value. In addition, the EBT was significantly better than the IBT in terms of the mean dose to the CTV. The highest dose within the target area was significantly greater for EBT than for IBT.

For the surface dose of the vaginal mucosa, we evaluated the Dmax of the vaginal stump apex mucosa and CTV midpoint surfaces. For EBT, the surface dose (top) ranged from 665 cGy to 1874 cGy, while for IBT, it ranged from 616 cGy to 1470 cGy. The surface dose (middle) ranged from 489 cGy to 824 cGy for the EBT and from 488 cGy to 796 cGy for the IBT. The median surface dose (top) was slightly greater for the EBT than for the IBT (961.5 vs. 844.0 cGy, p = 0.079). However, neither the top nor middle surface doses of EBT were significantly different from those of the IBT plans.

We could see that the DVH curve of the EBT plan for all the OARs lies below and to the left of the DVH curves of the IBT plan and dominates at lower doses, whereas for higher doses, the DVH curves for these two plans converged. Normal tissue doses

Table 3 shows the parameters of the normal tissues. For normal tissues, the V35% and V50% values depicted the lower dose areas, while D2 cc represented the higher dose regions. For the bladder, the V35%, V50% and D2 cc were significantly lower for the EBT plan than for the IBT plan. The dose at the lower dose regions was almost only half the value of the IBT parameter. For the rectum, we reached the same conclusion: the V35%, V50% and D2 cc were significantly lower for the EBT plan. For the sigmoid colon and small intestines, due to their distance from the target volume, only the high-volume area doses (namely, D2 cc) were evaluated. For the sigmoid colon, the EBT was almost half of the IBT parameter. In the small intestine, the value was lower, although the difference was not significant.

Discussion

For postoperative EC patients with risk factors, vaginal irradiation alone is the standard adjuvant treatment [1, 9–11]. At present, Ir192 source brachytherapy is the main radiotherapy method. However, IBTs require radioactive sources, a high-dose-rate after-loader unit and a shielded room. The high economic costs make the application of IBT difficult. As a result, many patients who need postoperative brachytherapy cannot receive effective treatment. An electronic brachytherapy system uses an electron source to generate X-rays. EBT is characterized by a high dose rate and low-energy X-ray, and it does not require a special shield for the treatment room, thus reducing the restrictions on its use. Currently, Xoft enables IORT for breast cancer patients and after-load brachytherapy for patients with gynecological tumors, such as endometrial cancer.

For postoperative EC patients who need brachytherapy, the current EBT vaginal applicators are single-channel cylinders. However, studies [8] have shown that compared with single-channel applicator, multichannel applicator has better conformal properties and better coverage of the target volume as it could both modify position of the source residence pipeline and the residence time which could be translated into physical advantages. Physicians can assess physical parameters more accurately through three-dimensional brachytherapy plans for the target area and OARs, as physical plans are made on CT images. In this research, we designed a multichannel applicator model based on the diameter of the Xoft source pipe and applicator used during the IBT at our institution. All patients were treated with a 3D Ir-192 brachytherapy plan. The electronic 3D brachytherapy plans were simulated to assess physical parameter differences.

A series of studies have been conducted on postoperative endometrial cancer patients receiving EBT. Dickler et al [2] retrospectively analyzed 11 postoperative EC patients and reported that the EBT provided equivalent target volume coverage to that of the IBT. The V35% and V50% of the bladder and rectum were significantly lower for patients receiving EBT than for those receiving IBT. Morbit et al. [3] reported that the V150% and V200% of the target volume were significantly greater for the EBT than for the IBT. For the bladder and rectum, although the V35% and V50% were significantly lower among the EBTs, no significant difference was detected in the high-dose area (namely, D2 cc) of the rectum. However, in the above studies, a single-channel vaginal applicator and two-dimensional treatment plan were used. In this study, a multichannel applicator was designed, and a 3D plan was generated. We reported that the EBT achieved the same coverage in the target area as did the IBT, although the average dose in the target area was greater. In addition, EBT performed significantly better than IBT in both the low-dose (V35% and V50%) and high-dose (D2 cc) regions for the organs at risk. For both the sigmoid colon and small intestines, the high-dose area from the EBT plan was lower than that from the IBT plan. As the small intestines were farther from the CTV than the sigmoid colon in most cases, there were no significant differences in D2 cc for the small intestines.

The reason for the higher high-dose regions (V200% and V150%) for EBT plan was mainly due to the physical characteristics of low-energy X-rays. As the distance from the radiation source increased, dose of low-energy X-rays decreased faster than that of Ir192 [12]. Therefore, when reaching the same value of D90 for CTV, the higher high-dose regions (V200% and V150%) and the vaginal surface dose were relatively high for EBT plan. Morbit et al. demonstrated [3] that the dose to the vaginal mucosa was 15 − 22% greater in the EBT plan than in the Ir192 plan. Low-energy photons are known to have greater radiobiological effectiveness (RBE) [13–15]. Rava et al. [12] analyzed 5 postoperative EC patients and used an RBE of 1.5 for biological effective dose (BED) calculations. Compared with the IBT, the accumulated physical dose of EBT on the mid-vaginal mucosal surface increased by 28.4% (36.6 vs. 28.5 Gy, p < 0.05). However, compared to the IBT plan, the BED [10] was increased by 79.1% (55.6 vs. 99.6 Gy, p < 0.05), and the BED [3] was increased by 71.5% (118.8 vs. 203.7 Gy, p < 0.05) for the EBT plan. The authors concluded that the physical dose might underestimate the biological effects of radiation on the vaginal mucosa when EBT is used. However, it is worth studying whether such vaginal doses can translate to clinical toxicity. Sarria et al [16] conducted a retrospective study on 29 patients with endometrial cancer who underwent EBT. The median follow-up time was 47 months. In the treatment group with external irradiation combined with brachytherapy (n = 8), there was 1 case (12.5%) of grade 3 vaginal toxicity. In the brachytherapy alone group (n = 21), no grade 3 vaginal toxicity occurred. Lozares-Cordero et al. [17] conducted an analysis on 94 EC patients (37 patients in EBT alone group; 57 patients in EBRT combined with EBT group). He demonstrated there was no grade 3 or above acute vaginal mucositis. The PORTEC-2 trial revealed that there was only 1 case (< 1%) of grade 3 vaginal mucosa atrophy in the pelvic external irradiation group and 4 cases (4%) in the Ir192 brachytherapy group. Therefore, it could be inferred from the above studies that higher vaginal mucosal surface doses did not seem to translate into increased toxicity in patients receiving EBT alone. In this study, there was no significant difference in the dose to the vaginal surface in the middle of the target area between the two radiation methods. For the apex of the vagina, although not significantly different, the dose from the EBT plan was slightly greater than that from the IBT plan. The reason was that for postoperative EC patients, differences in surgical suture methods and vaginal healing procedures resulted in excessive thickness of the mucosa at the top of the vagina for some patients. The low-energy X-ray dose decreased rapidly. Therefore, to cover the target area as much as possible, the surface dose to the vaginal apex mucosa was high. In summary, the conclusions of previous studies and this study have raised the question of how safe a low-energy X-ray dose is for accessing the apical mucosa of the vagina when used clinically. In addition, for patients with thick mucosal membranes at the top of the vagina, whether the radiotherapy dose needs to be adjusted, for example, by reducing the single doses while simultaneously increasing the number of fractions per treatment, is unclear. More clinical studies are needed to explore all of the above issues.

Grade 3 or higher late adverse effects were closely related to the quality of life for patients. In the ICRU89 brachytherapy reports [18], the recommended dose limits to avoid grade 3 or higher late adverse reactions were as follows, for bladder D2cc ≤ 80 Gy, for rectum D2cc ≤ 70 Gy and for sigmoid, D2cc ≤ 70 Gy. Similar dose limitations were also mentioned in the Timmerman report [19]. For patients who only underwent vaginal irradiation, due to the low prescription dose, the dose received by the organs at risk was much lower than the maximum tolerated dose. In 2022, we reported radiation toxicities of endometrial cancer patients treated from 13 institutions [20]. The rate of ≥ grade 3 late cystitis and proctitis was less than 1%. However, the rate of grade 1–2 late proctitis was 10.3%, and grade 1–2 late cystitis was 4.0%. We could conclude that decreased dose to organs at risk might have an effect on the grade1-2 rectum and bladder adverse effects. However, further studies were needed to confirm this conclusion. For the management of the adverse effects, we recommended vaginal dilation for 1–2 years to prevent vaginal stenosis. Supportive medical treatment was applied to the required patients for the management of proctitis and cystitis, including anti-diarrheal, anti-inflammatory drugs, hydration, et al.

Information was scarce and heterogeneous on correlation between quality indices (such as CI, DHI, DNR and OI) and radiobiological outcomes. In the physical plan of external intensity-modulated radiation therapy, we pursued high CI and DHI to avoid overdose in the target area. The reason was that there were often organs at risk such as bowels and spinal cord inside of target areas. Excessive overdose areas within the target area could lead to high adverse reactions. However, in brachytherapy, we usually placed the radiation source through the center of the tumor (such as radical radiotherapy for cervical cancer patients) or through areas with suspected residual tumors (such as vaginal brachytherapy for postoperative endometrial cancer patients) where the OARs were far around the target areas. There was a consensus that high CI was positively correlated with high TCP. However, researches on the relation between DHI, DNR, OR and radiobiological outcomes were rare and heterogeneous. In a report on dosimetric studies of cervical cancer, Kaur et al. [21] reported tumor control probability (TCP) increased with increased CI, DNR and OI values while TCP decreased with an increased DHI. DHI was a good indicator of the pattern of dose distribution in a target volume. However, there are no studies till date suggesting plans with a high DHI being associated with a better clinical outcome, as compared to such plans with inferior DHI [22]. Especially in case of brachytherapy where the dose distribution was highly heterogeneous. This article also showed that with an increase in OI and DNR values, TCP increases, but simultaneously, complications to OARs also increases. In this research, the CI values were similar for both EBT and IBT plans while the DHI value was lower and DNR, OI values were higher in the EBT plan than that of the IBT plan which seemed good according to the researches mentioned above. However, the significance of these parameters still needs to be confirmed in clinical applications.

Previous clinical studies with single-channel applicators and two-dimensional plans [4–6, 16] demonstrated that EBT had good clinical effects and low toxicity. Dickler et al. [4] conducted a multi-institutional prospective study to explore the toxicity of EBT. The study included 15 patients with postoperative endometrial cancer. Among them, 10 patients received EBT only, and 5 patients were treated with pelvic external radiation combined with EBT. After 3 months of follow-up, a total of six grade 1–2 events were reported, and no grade 3 or above events occurred. Dooley et al. [5] conducted a multi-institutional retrospective analysis of 41 EC patients (EBT alone, n = 16) and EBT combined with pelvic external radiation, n = 25), with a median follow-up of 3.8 months. There was only one grade 3 adverse event in the combined group, and there was no recurrence or metastasis. Kamrava et al. [6] reviewed patients treated with EBT from February 2009 to November 2010 and reported no grade 2 or higher toxicity in five patients who received EBT alone after a median follow-up of 20.5 months. Due to the short follow-up time of the above clinical studies, the American Brachytherapy Society reported recommendations for EBT treatment that it could be performed on the basis of safety and efficiency evaluations in 2019 [23]. Afterwards, in a study published in 2020, Sarria et al. [16] reviewed 29 EC patients who received EBT from a single center. Among them, 21 patients received EBT alone, and 8 patients received EBT with EBRT. The median follow-up time was 47 months. Two cases of grade 3 toxicity occurred, both in the combined group. No cases of grade 3 or above cystitis or proctitis occurred, and no local recurrence was found. The study concluded that EBT had a low grade 3 event rate and a high local control rate. In addition, the absence of grade 3 or higher bladder and rectum toxicity was also remarkable. In summary, electronic brachytherapy has the characteristics of high efficiency and low toxicity in the application of postoperative brachytherapy for endometrial cancer patients. However, more studies and explorations are needed.

The limitations of this study were as follows. Firstly, as a retrospective study, there might be selective bias in enrollment. Secondly, only 20 patients were included in each of the groups. The sample size was too small. Thirdly, due to different source diameters, the multichannel models used were different between groups. The above reasons might reduce the statistical effect thus affecting the reliability of the research. Besides, this research only analyzed physical parameters and clinical data was lacking. Whether the physical advantages of the EBT could be translated into improved clinical outcomes and decreased rectum and bladder adverse reactions, and whether its higher vaginal surface dose would be translated into increased vaginal toxicity were unknown. Our future research directions were as follows. Firstly, we will transform this simulated multichannel vaginal applicator into a product. Secondly, we will conduct prospective clinical trials in the next stage. We will enroll more patients receiving EBT with this multichannel vaginal applicator and its original single-channel applicator. Physical parameters will be compared and long-term clinical data regarding outcomes and adverse effects will be recorded. Anyway, for the first time, this study designed a novel multichannel vaginal applicator that could be equipped with an Xoft electronic brachytherapy system. The three-dimensional brachytherapy plan was simulated. We found that this approach could reduce the dose to the surrounding organs at risk while maintaining the dose in the target area. The findings of this study will provide a new option for patients who undergo vaginal brachytherapy for gynecological tumors.

Conclusions

In conclusion, under the premise of a three-dimensional brachytherapy plan, compared with IBT, EBT achieved the same target coverage (V100%, V95%, V90% or D98), while the high dose (V200% and V150%) and mean dose in the target area were greater. The doses for OARs (bladder and rectum) were lower for EBT in both the low-dose area (V35% and V50%) and the high-dose area (D2 cc). For the apical mucosa, the EBT was slightly better than the IBT, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. Further clinical trials were needed to explore the clinical significance of these above parameters.
