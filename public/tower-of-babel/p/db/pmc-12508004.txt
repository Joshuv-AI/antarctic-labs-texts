Multi-omics profiling reveals key microbial and metabolic variations in ischemic stroke: insights into gut and oral microbiota
Authors: Lihe Yao, Lei Li, Jun Chen, Yuan Ma, Adel I. Alalawy, Yisha Jiang, Wei Zhang, Youquan Gu, Yaming Xi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dysbiosis of oral and gut microbiota may influence ischemic stroke (IS) pathogenesis through immune and metabolic mechanisms. This study investigated microbial translocation between oral and gut niches and its functional metabolic impact in IS patients. A total of 57 individuals were analyzed, including 25 controls (FC/KC) and 32 IS patients (FS/KS). Samples were obtained from the gut and oral subgingival plaque. The microbiome analysis was performed using the PacBio platform 16 S rRNA full-length gene sequencing, while non-targeted metabolomics was used to assess intestinal metabolites. The oral cavity of IS patients harbored abundant periodontal pathogens, including Porphyromonas and Fusobacterium. Bacterial genera (such as Streptococcus, Neisseria, Ligilactobacillus, Leptotrichia, Blautia, Veillonella, and Capnocytophaga) significantly overlapped between the oral and gut niches in IS patients, suggesting mutual transfer. IS patients exhibited increased diversity and dysbiosis in their gut microbiota, characterized by a deficiency of short-chain fatty acids (SCFA)-producing bacteria (Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium), and an enrichment of opportunistic pathogens (Streptococcus and Ligilactobacillus). Metabolomic analysis revealed concomitant disturbances in microbial metabolism, marked by decreased SCFAs (butyrate) and increased levels of specific amino acids (L-tryptophan). These metabolic changes were correlated with the microbial shifts. IS is associated with distinct dysbiosis in both the oral and gut ecosystems, which are interconnected through bacterial translocation. The metabolic disruption may link microbial dysbiosis to stroke pathogenesis through the oral-gut axis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00203-025-04494-x.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) occurs due to temporary or permanent interruption in blood flow to the brain (Qaja and Tadi 2024). This condition can result in significant brain injury, leading to lasting neurological deficits, dementia, or even death. Stroke is the second leading cause of death and third in disability worldwide (Saini et al. 2021). IS accounts for (85%) while hemorrhagic (15%), approximately 24.9 million cases reported annually, imposing a serious burden on socioeconomic and medical conditions (Benjamin et al. 2018). Approximately 90% of stroke cases have been reported to be associated with behavioral factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and atherosclerosis. Microbial dysbiosis also have been shown to play a significant role in the pathogenesis of ischemic stroke through signal transduction, immune regulation, and metabolic function (Benesch et al. 2021), altering the body’s response to ischemic events, impacting the overall prognosis of the stroke (Honarpisheh et al. 2022).

Microbiota-gut-brain axis (MGBA) is a bidirectional communication network between gut microbes and the brain (Khan et al. 2024). Gut microbes and their metabolites regulate the transmission of brain neurotransmitters (such as serotonin and catecholamines) through neural, immune, humoral, and endocrine pathways (Sorboni et al. 2022). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is an immunogenic endotoxin produced by gram-negative bacteria (such as Yersinia pestis, Klebsiella pneumonia, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Bifidobacterium species, Chlamydia trachomatis and Francisella tularensis). LPS can directly cause neuroinflammation or peripheral immune cells to migrate into the brain (Kalyan et al. 2022). Intestinal dysbiosis alters immune homeostasis in the small intestine leading to an increase in regulatory T cells and a reduction in IL-17(+) γδT cells. These cells then migrate from the small intestine to the brain, where they cause infarct damage (Benakis et al. 2016). IS can lead to impaired intestinal barrier function and increases microbial metabolites, such as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), which directly regulate the hyperreactivity of platelets in the body, accelerating the formation of thrombi (Zhu et al. 2016). On the other hand, these substances are translocated into the bloodstream, which subsequently accelerates the deterioration of symptoms, leading to poor prognosis for stroke patients (Zhu et al. 2016; Swidsinski et al. 2012).

Recent studies have identified the presence of periodontal pathogens in cerebral aneurysms samples and thrombi from patients experiencing acute ischemic stroke (Patrakka et al. 2019; Pyysalo et al. 2016). Porphyromonas gingivalis and Streptococcus sanguinis have been detected in these clinical specimens, suggesting a potential link between oral health and cerebrovascular events. This association highlights the role of oral microbial infections in the pathogenesis of ischemic stroke, as these pathogens may contribute to inflammatory processes and thrombus formation (Haraszthy et al. 2000). Epidemiological studies further support this link, showing that a higher number of dental caries, periodontal disease, or more tooth loss is associated with a higher risk of IS, while regular dental care may reduce it (Sen et al. 2018).

The oral and gastrointestinal tracts are intimately connected, forming an “oral-gut axis” that facilitates the translocation of oral microbes to the intestine. Recent studies utilizing high-throughput sequencing technology have demonstrated the biological homology between oral and gut microbiota. The oral microbiome can colonize the intestine, leading to intestinal microbial dysbiosis (Mo et al. 2022), indicating the importance of the oral-gut axis in the diagnosis and treatment of diseases (Park et al. 2021). Studies have shown that Porphyromonas gingivalis entering the circulation system could be captured and engulfed by neutrophils, leading to their activation (Freiherr Von Seckendorff et al. 2024), and the systemic inflammatory burden elevated stroke susceptibility (Palm et al. 2014). Oral microbiota not only invade the trigeminal nervous system and blood to promote the production of peptide β-amyloid (Sureda et al. 2020), but also directly or indirectly affect the behavior of glial cells through the gut microbiota and its metabolites (Margolis et al. 2021). However, the specific patterns of oral and gut microbial co-dysbiosis in IS patients, the extent of oral-to-gut translocation, and the functional metabolic consequences of these changes remain poorly understood. Therefore, this study employed 16 S rRNA full-length sequencing to characterize the oral and gut microbial communities and the overlap and potential translocation of bacteria. Evaluate the alterations in fecal metabolites to elucidate the potential mechanisms linking the oral-gut-microbial axis to ischemic stroke pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Study design and specimen collection

This is an observational study. This project was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Lanzhou University (LDYYLL2022-513). Clinical trial numbers are not applicable. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the institution. Patient recruitment was conducted in the neurology inpatient department between January, 2023 and May, 2024, and all participants were aged between 20 and 76 years.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Over 18 years of age; (2) Have new ischemic stroke diagnosed by head computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) within 3 days of onset; (3) Have two major ischemic stroke subtypes; large-artery atherosclerosis (LAA) and small-artery occlusion (SAO) according to the Trial of ORG 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) classification; (4) Have not received probiotics or antibiotics within one month prior to enrollment.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Have cardioembolism (CE), stroke of other determined etiology (SOE), and stroke of undetermined etiology (SUE); (2) Have severe heart, liver, or kidney-related diseases; (3) Have diagnosed intestinal or metabolic disorders; (4) Users of proton pump inhibitors, such as omeprazole.

A questionnaire was completed for each individual at the time of samples collection to record demographic and other relevant information such as smoking, drinking and clinical history, and oral examination results were recorded. Assess risk by using the national institutes of health stroke scale (NIHSS). Periodontitis is defined as ≥ 4 teeth with ≥ 1 site having probing depth of ≥ 4 mm, clinical attachment loss of ≥ 1 mm, and bleeding on probing.

To specifically collect subgingival plaque, a sterile Gracey curette was inserted into the periodontal pocket until resistance was felt at the base of the pocket (approximately 4–6 mm depth) on the lingual side of the mandibular first molars. The curette was then gently scraped against the root surface to dislodge the subgingival plaque (Kim et al. 2023). The collected plaque was then placed in a sterile cryopreservation tube and stored in a -80 °C refrigerator for further analysis. 5 g of stool sample was collected from each participant approximately within 48 h after admission using a stool collection container with a tightly screwed lid and sealed with parafilm. Blood parameters and C-reactive protein (CRP) were analyzed using an automatic blood analyzer system (Sysmex XN-20; Kobe, Japan). Biochemical parameters were analyzed using a fully automatic biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter AU5800, California, USA).

DNA extraction and 16 S rRNA sequencing

The full-length (V1-V9) bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequences were amplified using barcoded 27F (5’- AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG-3’) and 1492R (5’- RGYTA CCTTGTTACGACTT-3’) primers. Both forward and reverse 16 S primers were tailed with sample-specific PacBio barcode sequences to allow for multiplexed sequencing. The amplification of DNA was performed using KOD One PCR Master Mix (TOYOBOLife Science, Osaka, Japan) with the following protocol; initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 25 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1.5 min, and a final step at 72 °C for 2 min. Subsequently, the PCR amplicons were purified using VAHTSTM DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) to remove residual primers and nucleotides. The concentration of purified DNA was quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit and Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Oregon, USA). SMRTbell libraries were prepared from the amplified DNA by SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Pacific Biosciences, California, USA).

LC/MS non-targeted metabolomics analysis

Weigh 110 mg of sample tissue, add 1000 µL of extract containing internal standard, vortex mix for 30 s, and ultrasonicate for 10 min (ice water bath). The LC/MS system used for metabolomics analysis consisted of a Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled to a Waters Xevo G2-XS QTOF high-resolution mass spectrometer. The chromatographic column being an Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm 2.1*100 mm) (Waters, Massachusetts, USA). In mass spectrometry analysis, a data acquisition cycle includes the simultaneous collection of both low and high-collision energy data using a dual-channel setup. The low collision energy is set at 2 V while the high collision energy ranges from 10 to 40 V. The scanning frequency is set at 0.2 s per mass spectrum to accurately capture the desired data distribution. The raw data collected using MassLynx (v4.2) were processed using Progenesis QI software (v2.0), including peak extraction and peak alignment, based on the online METLIN database and Biomark’s self-built library for identification, and at the same time, theoretical fragment identification and mass deviation all are within 100 ppm.

Statistical analysis

Demographic data were compared between groups using SPSS software (v27.0). Comparisons were conducted using a variety of statistical methods, including two independent-sample t-test, the Chi-Square Test, and Fisher’s exact test. The bioinformatics analysis of this study was performed with the aid of the BMKCloud (http://www.biocloud.net/). The qualified sequences with more than 97% similarity thresholds were allocated to one operational taxonomic unit (OTU) using USEARCH software (v10.0). R software (v3.6.1) was used to draw a Venn diagram and bubble chart. Alpha diversity was assessed to identify the complexity of species diversity of each sample utilizing QIIME2 software (v2023.5). Beta diversity calculations were analyzed by Non-MetricMulti-Dimensional Scaling (NMDS). Wilcoxon test was used to compare bacterial abundance and diversity. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was applied to evaluate the differentially abundant taxa. Random forest analysis was performed to compare the microbiome characteristics.

After normalizing the original peak area information with the total peak area, the follow-up analysis was performed. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to judge the repeatability of the samples within the group and the quality control samples. The ropls package (v1.6.2) was used to perform Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) modelling, and 200 times permutation tests were performed to verify the reliability of the model. Identified compounds were annotated and mapped to pathways using the KEGG database. Metabolites with the VIP value > 1 as well as Fold Change (FC) > 1 were further applied to a t-test (P < 0.05) to measure the significance of each metabolite. Enrichment analysis of KEGG pathways was performed using a hypergeometric distribution test. Correlations between microbial taxa and metabolic profiles were assessed using Spearman rank correlation. To account for multiple hypothesis testing, false discovery rate (FDR) control was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Adjusted Q-values are reported alongside raw P-values for all relevant analyses, including microbial differential abundance, metabolite features, correlation analyses, and KEGG enrichment.

To further investigate whether these factors were independently associated with IS after controlling for potential confounders, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed (Table S1). After adjustment, the associations of hyperhomocysteinemia, CRP, and HCY levels with IS were attenuated and no longer reached statistical significance (P > 0.05). This attenuation suggests that the impact of these traditional risk factors on ischemic stroke risk may not be direct and could potentially be mediated or confounded by other mechanisms, such as the alterations in oral and gut microbiota, which is the focus of this study. Additionally, likely due to the limited sample size of this pilot study, the model yielded wide confidence intervals. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution and also require verification in a larger cohort.

Composition and diversity of gut and oral microbiota

The number of shared OTUs in the intestinal and oral microorganisms of IS patients was 10,763 (Fig. 1A), which was significantly higher than the number of shared OTUs in the healthy controls, which was only 3,591 (Fig. 1B). Among the top ten genera, Streptococcus, Neisseria, Ligilactobacillus, Leptotrichia, Blautia, Veillonella, and Capnocytophaga were observed to be shared genera in both the intestine and the oral cavity (Fig. 1C-D). The content of Streptococcus, Neisseria, Leptotrichia, Veillonella, and Capnocytophaga in the FS group was significantly lower than that in the KS group, while the change in the FC/KC was the opposite. Similarly, the content of Ligilactobacillus and Blautia in the FS group was significantly higher than that in the KS group; conversely, the change in the FC/KC group displayed opposite levels.

A total of 27,727 OTUs were identified based on the conventional criterion of 97% similarity in 57 fecal samples. The number of unique OTUs in the FS group was 16,114, which was much higher than that in the FC group (Fig. 1E). The top 10 genera of the intestinal microbiota include Escherichia,_Shigella, Blautia, Bacteroides, Ligilactobacillus, Streptococcus, Bifidobacterium, Ruminococcus, Faecalibacterium, Subdoligranulum, and Romboutsia (Fig. 1G). In the 57 oral samples, a total of 8,900 OTUs were shared between the KS group and KC group (Fig. 1F). Among the top ten species, only the Neisseria was found in the KS group at higher contents than in the KC group (Fig. 1H).

In terms of α diversity, the richness and evenness of intestinal microbial flora between the two groups were statistically significant(Shannon index P < 0.05, ACE index P < 0.05)(Fig. 2A-B), and the diversity of the FS group was higher than that of the FC group. The similarity of bacterial community structure between FS and FC groups was evaluated by NMDS, which showed a clear separation between the gut microbiome composition of FS and FC groups characterized by Wilcoxon test P < 0.05 (Fig. 2C). The KS group was significantly higher than the KC group in oral microbial diversity (shannon index, P < 0.05; ACE index, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D-E). Similarly, the community structures of the two groups were clearly separated as analyzed with Wilcoxon test P < 0.05 (Fig. 2F).

Identification of biomarkers in patients with ischemic stroke (IS)

Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was used to explore the changes between the oral and intestinal microbiota under different physical states (P < 0.05, LDA > 4) (Fig. 3A-B). At the genus level, the oral and intestinal biomarkers of IS patients were similar to those of healthy controls, with eight overlapping bacterial genera respectively, including Capnocytophaga, Neisseria, Fusobacterium, Selenomonas, Streptococcus, Veillonella, Prevotella, and Leptotrichia in the oral cavity and Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcus, Dorea, Romboutsia, Bifidobacterium, Blautia, Escherichia,_Shigella, and Subdoligranulum in the gut, which was similar to previous findings (Tan et al. 2021; Liao et al. 2022). On the other hand, each group has unique enriched bacterial genus in oral cavity and gut. The unique flora enriched in the KS group was Aggregatibacter, and in the FS group was Agathobacter, Eubacterium, Streptococcus, Limosilactobacillus, and Ligilactobacillus (Fig. 3A). Lachnoanaerobaculum and Campylobacter were unique flora enriched in the KC group, and Bacteroides, Holdemanella and Akkermansia were unique flora enriched in the FC group (Fig. 3B).

Similarly, at the genus level, Subdoligranulum, Streptococcus, and Limosilactobacillus were enriched in the FS group when compared with the FC group (Fig. 3C). Meanwhile, Bacteroides, a type of bacteria that produces short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), was lacking in the FS group. In the oral microbiota, the biomarkers in the KS group were Neisseria and Massilia (Fig. 3D). The ANOVA test was used to screen out the significant bacteria with a relative abundance greater than 0.1% (Fig. 3E), and it was found that the Bacteroides (P < 0.001, Q < 0.001), Parabacteroides (P < 0.001, Q = 0.004), Roseburia (P < 0.001, Q = 0.007) and Holdemanella (P < 0.001, Q = 0.008) in the FC group was significantly higher than that in the FS group. These are all beneficial intestinal bacteria that generate SCFAs, can improve metabolism, and reduce inflammation (Cui et al. 2022; Romaní-Pérez et al. 2021; Tamanai-Shacoori et al. 2017). The Lachnoanaerobaculum, is also involved in the production of beneficial compounds such as butyrate, acetate, and glucosidase, in the oral specimens of the KC group was significantly higher than that in the FC group (Fig. 3F) (P < 0.001, Q = 0.002) (Hedberg et al. 2012). Porphyromonas, a periodontal pathogen, was significantly higher in the KS group compared to the KC group (P = 0.004, Q = 0.049).

The correlation between intestinal and oral microbiota

The intestinal and oral microbiota of patients with IS interact synergistically, exhibiting a positive correlation (Fig. 4A). The presence of harmful bacteria may lead to the activation of some opportunistic pathogens, resulting in their own virulence enhancement and changes in the host immune status (Tay et al. 2016). There was a positive correlation between periodontal disease-related bacterial genera with the KC group, such as Neisseria and Aggregatibacter, Prevotella and Selenomonas. The core intestinal bacteria genus Lachnoclostridium is negatively correlated with a variety of opportunistic pathogens, such as Capnocytophaga, Fusobacterium, and Campylobacter (Fig. 4B). These were a normal part of bacterial colonization in the human body.

We further used Source Tracker to determine the contribution of oral microbes to the gut in stroke patients, where 61.85% of the gut microbiota came from the oral cavity (Fig. 4C), and 58.35% of the gut microbiota in healthy controls came from the oral cavity (Fig. 4D). There was no significant difference between the two groups (Wilcoxon test, P = 0.193) (Fig. 4E), which again verified the influence of oral microbes on the structure of gut microbiota.

Association between microorganisms and clinical factors

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to explore the correlations between the microbiome and blood indicators and Periodontitis. The results showed that among the top ten species abundance in intestinal, the bacteria enriched in the FS group were positively correlated with the risk factor. A trend of positive association was observed between Ligilactobacillus and Hyperhomocysteinemia (P = 0.026, Q = 0.129), Bifidobacterium was positively associated with Hyperhomocysteinemia (P = 0.011, Q = 0.120), Hyperlipidaemia (P = 0.029, Q = 0.195) and CRP (P = 0.010, Q = 0.103). Streptococcus was positively correlated with periodontitis (P = 0.022, Q = 0.037), and Ruminococcus was positively associated with WBC (P = 0.021, Q = 0.120). Blautia and Romboutsia were positively associated with folic acid (P = 0.008, Q = 0.051; P = 0.010, Q = 0.361). By contrast, the beneficial intestinal bacterial were negatively correlated with risk factors, such as Faecalibacterium was negatively correlated with uric acid (P = 0.039, Q = 0.389), and Bacteroides was negatively correlated with Hyperlipidaemia (P = 0.049, Q = 0.425) (Fig. 5A). Among the top ten oral bacteria, Campylobacter was positively correlated with glycated haemoglobin (P = 0.048, Q = 0.619), folic acid (P = 0.03, Q = 0.619), and brushing time (P = 0.02, Q = 0.234) (Fig. 5B).

Therefore, we screened the bacteria enriched in the oral flora of patients with ischemic stroke and analyzed their correlation with risk factors. A relatively small proportion of oral bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium (HCY: P = 0.022, Q = 0.036; Period: P = 0.003, Q = 0.046), Pseudomonas (HCY: P < 0.001, Q = 0.005; Period: P < 0.001, Q = 0.002), Ruminococcus (HCY: P < 0.001, Q = 0.017; Period: P < 0.001, Q = 0.017), Massilia (HCY: P < 0.001, Q = 0.006; Period: P < 0.001, Q = 0.003), Bacillus (HCY: P = 0.002, Q = 0.033; Period: P < 0.001, Q = 0.008), Rhodanobacter (HCY: P = 0.003, Q = 0.046; Period: P < 0.001, Q = 0.007) and Sphingomonas (HCY: P = 0.002, Q = 0.028; Period: P < 0.001, Q = 0.005), have been found to have a direct positively correlation with both homocysteine levels and periodontal disease (Fig. 5C).

Our analysis of a correlation network diagram revealed several key insights: Periodontitis emerged as a central point, showing a notable positive link with alpha diversity indices within the FS group. Specifically, it exhibited the strongest associations with the Chao1 index (R = 0.397, P = 0.001, Q = 0.03) and the ACE index (R = 0.317, P = 0.001, Q = 0.03), implying that those with periodontitis tend to possess greater gut microbial species richness. Conversely, hyperhomocysteinemia displayed an inverse relationship with microbial richness, as indicated by the Chao1 index (R = 0.093, P = 0.007, Q = 0.06) and ACE index (R = 0.101, P = 0.001, Q = 0.075), suggesting that elevated homocysteine might contribute to reduced gut microbial diversity. Furthermore, frequent toothbrushing (FTB) was significantly and positively related to both Chao1 and ACE indices (R = 0.183, P = 0.003, Q = 0.045; R = 0.178, P = 0.002, Q = 0.04), reinforcing the connection between oral hygiene practices, microbial richness, and clinical outcomes (Fig. 5E).

In the KS group, the study of the oral microbiota in cerebral infarction patients and their clinical phenotypes revealed that periodontitis had a strong positive correlation with both microbial richness indices (Chao1: R = 0.429, P = 0.001, Q = 0.02; ACE: R = 0.429, P = 0.001, Q = 0.02), thereby indicating a close relationship between periodontitis and significantly elevated oral microbial species richness (Fig. 5F).

Correlation between Microbiome and metabolite

Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between differentially abundant metabolites and microbial genera. The beneficial genus Bacteroides showed a positive correlation with butyric acid (P = 0.042, Q = 0.429). In contrast, bacterial genera enriched in the FS group, including Sphingomonas, Rhodanobacter, Massilia, TM7a and Rhizorhapis exhibited negative correlations with short-chain fatty acids but significant positive correlations with multiple amino acids and cadaverine. Specifically, these genera exhibited significant positive associations with L-Aspartic Acid, L-Tryptophan, L-Histidine, L-Asparagine, and cadaverine, with consistently strong P-values (all P < 0.01) though varying FDR-corrected Q-values (ranging from < 0.001 to 0.213) (Fig. 8A). For example, Massilia correlated with L-Tryptophan (P < 0.001, Q = 0.036), L-Aspartic Acid (P < 0.001, Q = 0.006), L-Histidine (P < 0.001, Q = 0.033), L-Asparagine (P < 0.001, Q = 0.036), and cadaverine (P = 0.002, Q = 0.043). Similar trends were observed for the other genera (Table S2). These results suggest a notable link between disrupted amino acid metabolism and gut microbiota composition in the FS group.

In a separate analysis, we also explored correlations between oral microbiota and fecal metabolites (Fig. 8B). There was a significant positive correlation between oral pathogen, Porphyromonas, with the fecal amino acids, L-Aspartic Acid (P < 0.001, Q < 0.001) and L-Histidine (P < 0.001, Q < 0.001). Although not all correlations remained significant after strict FDR correction, the overarching pattern strongly suggests a link between these microbes and amino acid metabolism dysregulation (Fig. 8B, Table S2). It is important to note that our metabolomic analysis was performed on fecal samples, while the microbiome analysis included both oral and gut sites. Therefore, the correlations between oral microbiota and fecal metabolites (Fig. 8B) should be interpreted with caution, as they may not reflect direct local interactions but rather point to systemic metabolic alterations or shared influences.

Discussion

In this study, the association among oral health, gut microbiota, metabolites and ischemic stroke was further examined and verified (Roth et al. 2020). While our initial univariate analysis identified elevated HCY and CRP in IS patients, a multivariate model adjusting for multiple covariates did not retain these as independent predictors, a finding limited by sample size and requiring further investigation. The primary and more robust highlight of this study point to the significant associations between IS and distinct alterations in the oral-gut microbiome axis and associated metabolic pathways.

The oral microbial signature we observed in IS patients, characterized by an increase in Porphyromonas and Fusobacterium, is highly consistent with the classic “red complex” and associated pathogens that define periodontal dysbiosis (Socransky and Haffajee 2005). This suggests that these individuals in our cohort had a concomitant state of periodontal dysbiosis.

The mouth serves as a gateway to the gut. Oral pathogens are not only implicated in oral diseases (caries and periodontitis) (Baker et al. 2023), but are also associated with an increased risk of systemic diseases (diabetes, cancer, autoimmune diseases and cardiovascular disease) (Graves et al. 2019; Yamazaki 2023). Even with the protective roles of gastric acid, the intestinal barrier, bile, and pancreatic secretions, the mouth and intestine still share bacterial genera (Segata et al. 2012; Kitamoto et al. 2020). We observed that the intestinal and oral flora in IS patients showed overlapping bacterial genera, including Streptococcus, Neisseria, Ligilactobacillus, Leptotrichia, Blautia, Veillonella, and Capnocytophaga, which further confirms the concept of the “oral-gut axis” (Lam et al. 2023; Kunath et al. 2024). The abnormal presence of oral bacteria within the intestine is recognized as a primary factor in disturbing gut homeostasis, potentially inciting an inflammatory response and fostering the transition of the intestinal microenvironment from a healthy to a diseased condition (Segata et al. 2012; Kitamoto et al. 2020). In the oral cavity, the AgI/II protein family plays a crucial role in the adhesion and virulence of Streptococcus, promoting biofilm formation, platelet aggregation, tissue invasion, and host immune interactions (Abranches et al. 2018). In the gut, Streptococcus inhibit inflammatory responses by activating the NF-κB pathway in intestinal epithelial cells and reducing IL-8 production after colonization, thereby maintaining intestinal homeostasis (Kaci et al. 2011, 2014). Ligilactobacillus also has a multifaceted role; it’s frequently employed as a probiotic to boost intestinal metabolic and immune functions (Miri et al. 2024). Yet, its proliferation is sometimes detrimental, Ligilactobacillus levels are elevated in a hyperlipidemia model compared to controls (Selmin et al. 2021), owing to its significant resistance to unconjugated bile acids, which inhibits bile acid-susceptible microorganisms and leads to its increased abundance (Zhou et al. 2023). Thus, it is our conjecture that the heightened level of L. lactis found in the intestines of cerebral infarction patients might signify either a compensatory function or just a quick reaction to external environmental alterations (Chuandong et al. 2024).

A finding of our study was the overlap and potential translocation of oral pathogens, such as Porphyromonas, Veillonella, and Neisseria, within the gut ecosystem of IS patients, which may be a mechanism linking periodontal disease to cerebral infarction. Porphyromonas colonization of the intestine has been linked to loss of intestinal surface epithelium, destruction of crypt structures, infiltration of inflammatory cells, and enhanced expression of flavin monooxygenase 3 (FMO3), which promotes the conversion of TMA to TMAO, potentially exacerbating intestinal inflammation (Xiao et al. 2021). Simultaneously, Porphyromonas induced an increase in branched-chain amino acids levels, activated the AKT/STAT3/NF-κB signaling pathway, and exacerbated microglia-induced neuroinflammation (Shen et al. 2023). Furthermore, Porphyromonas has been directly detected in human atherosclerotic plaques, indicating its direct involvement in thrombosis (Haraszthy et al. 2000). Clinical studies have shown a positive correlation between the abundance of Veillonella and systemic inflammation levels, stroke severity, and adverse outcomes (He et al. 2024). During inflammation in the body, nitrate is a hallmark metabolite of inflammation, and nitrate respiration promotes the growth of Veillonella through the narGHJI operon, thereby increasing its ability to compete and establish in the intestinal environment (Rojas-Tapias et al. 2022). Consequently, we propose that the migration of these “shared genera” to the intestine might establish a pro-inflammatory feedback mechanism, thereby elevating stroke risk. This inflammation, in turn, modifies the intestinal milieu, facilitating the colonization of oral microbes.

Metabolomic analysis revealed significant alterations in intestinal metabolites after metablic analysis, particularly involving short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) and amino acid metabolism. Concentrations of beneficial SCFAs, such as butyrate and isovaleric acid, were significantly reduced, a finding strongly correlated with decreased abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria including Bacteroides, Roseburia, Lachnospiraceae, and Faecalibacterium (Chen et al. 2019; Pryde et al. 2002). Butyrate exerts multiple beneficial effects, including enhancing oxidative stress resistance, acting as a histone deacetylase inhibitor to influence gene expression (Bourassa et al. 2016), and promoting an anti-inflammatory environment by stimulating IL-10 production while weakening the neutrophil extracellular trap (NETs) and improving intestinal inflammation (Li et al. 2021).

Tryptophan levels were elevated and positively correlated with multiple bacteria, such as Sphingomonas, Rhodanobacter, Massilia, TM7a, Rhizorhapis and Subdoligranulum. Tryptophan, a microbial-derived metabolite, is produced by various intestinal bacteria including Escherichia coli, Bacillus, Clostridium, and Shigella (Chappell et al. 2016). Although tryptophan metabolism can yield antioxidant serotonin via tryptophan hydroxylase (Song et al. 2017), its degradation by mitochondrial enzyme monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A) generates reactive oxygen species that disrupt blood-brain barrier integrity and promote apoptosis (Nocito et al. 2007). Additionally, tryptophan activates aryl hydrocarbon receptors, exacerbating oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and vasogenic edema, thereby potentiating brain injury (Sadler et al. 2020).

In conclusion, our research provides strong evidence for a “mouth-gut axis” whereby ectopic colonization by periodontal pathogens triggers chronic intestinal inflammation, disrupts the integrity of the epithelial barrier, and leads to increased proinflammatory cytokines, which are drivers of atherosclerosis. Metabolomics findings of decreased SCFAs and increased tryptophan further support this mechanism. This understanding suggests that interventions targeting the oral–gut axis such as improved oral hygiene or microbiome modulation, could offer a novel approach to reduce systemic inflammation and lower the risk of stroke.

Limitation

Despite these findings, our study has several limitations. First, although our sample size (n = 57) is appropriate for an exploratory multi-omics pilot study, a larger cohort would provide greater statistical power to detect subtler associations and strengthen the generalizability of our findings. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm our results. Second, major confounders such as recent antibiotic use and proton pump inhibitors were controlled through the exclusion criteria. However, other potential confounding factors known to influence the microbiome, such as detailed dietary composition, physical activity, and undiagnosed systemic conditions, were not fully assessed. The influence of these factors cannot be completely ruled out and should be more rigorously controlled in future longitudinal studies. Third, our study is uses of a conventional 97% similarity OTU clustering approach for bioinformatic analysis. While this method is well-established and allows for direct comparison with many previous studies, newer techniques such as Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) offer higher resolution and reduced bioinformatic noise. Future studies could benefit from employing ASV-based pipelines (DADA2 or Deblur) to validate and refine our observations at an even finer taxonomic level. Finally, we did not perform metabolomics profiling on oral samples. Consequently, the functional implications of the oral dysbiosis we observed are inferred from fecal metabolome data or existing literature, rather than being directly measured from the oral environment. Future studies incorporating plaque metabolomics would be invaluable to directly link the oral microbiome with its local metabolic output and further elucidate the oral-gut-metabolite axis in IS.

Conclusion

This study highlights variations in the gut and oral microbiota of ischemic stroke patients and reveals a complex relationship between these microbial communities and their metabolites. The findings indicate that the FS group exhibited increased microbial diversity, specifically, bacteria that produce tryptophan increased, but lacked beneficial short-chain fatty acid-producing bacteria, which may contribute to dysbiosis. The intestinal and oral flora in IS patients showed overlapping bacterial genera, potentially contributing to microbial dysbiosis. These results underscore the importance of the oral-gut axis in stroke pathology and suggest that targeting microbial dysbiosis may offer new avenues for preventive and therapeutic strategies in ischemic stroke management.
