Cystic glioblastoma is associated with alterations of KDR and the Rb pathway: a single-center retrospective analysis
Authors: Johannes Werner, Sahar Alizada, Cheuk Hong Leung, Heather Lin, Ahmed Msherghi, Erick Campbell, Chetna Wathoo, Chibawanye I. Ene, Maria A. Gubbiotti, Vinay Puduvalli, Max Wintermark, Nazanin K. Majd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common malignant primary brain tumor in adults. Cystic GBM is a phenotypic subset requiring distinct clinical management. This study aimed to comprehensively assess the genomic landscape of cystic GBM. GBM, IDH wild-type patients with available next-generation sequencing and presurgical brain MRI data were evaluated for cysts by neuroimaging specialists and a senior neuroradiologist. Patients with incomplete radiological or histopathological data, treatment-related cysts, or indeterminate cyst status were excluded. We used logistic regression to assess the association of cystic GBM with demographic variables and the top 24 altered genes. We employed Cox regression to analyze the association of cystic GBM with survival. Among 374 eligible patients, we identified 111 (30%) with cystic GBM and 263 (70%) with non-cystic GBM. Multivariable logistic regression revealed the association of cystic GBM with alterations of the KDR (OR 8.75 [1.77–43.3], p = 0.038), RB1 (OR 5.12 [2.58–10.1], p = 0.008), TP53 (OR 2.72 [1.67–4.43], p = 0.008), CDK4 (OR 2.36 [1.27–4.37], p = 0.036) genes, as well as wild type EGFR (OR 0.33 [0.20–0.53], p = 0.008). Cystic GBM was not associated with overall survival (HR 0.82 [0.61–1.11], p = 0.205). Cystic GBM is marked by alterations of the Rb and p53 pathways as well as KDR alterations. This provides new insights into the pathogenesis of cystic GBM. Several alterations associated with cystic GBM, including CDK4 and KDR, are targets of small-molecule inhibitors. Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common malignant primary brain tumor in adults [2, 31]. With a median survival of 10–15 months upon diagnosis, the prognosis of GBM is dismal, resulting in a major unmet need for improving diagnostic and therapeutic approaches [11, 38]. Traditionally, the diagnosis of GBM was based on histological criteria, but this view was complemented by molecular features in the 2021 WHO classification. GBM is characterized by a characteristic molecular profile, with mandatory absence of IDH1/2 mutations, common alterations like TERT promoter and EGFR mutations, and copy number changes of chromosomes 7 and 10 [25]. Despite the presence of targetable gene alterations in GBM, this has not been translated into a major therapeutic benefit [8]. Further exploration of gene alterations in GBM subtypes may provide insights to improve therapeutic outcomes in GBM.

Common radiographic features of GBM are contrast uptake, mass effect exerted on the surrounding brain tissue, central necrosis, and surrounding vasogenic edema. Besides, GBM is characterized by considerable phenotypic heterogeneity, rendering initial diagnosis difficult [35]. Up to 20% of GBMs display cystic components, forming a distinct phenotypic subtype [7, 18, 27, 37, 44]. In cross-sectional imaging, cystic GBM presents similarly to brain abscesses, complicating initial differential diagnosis [42]. Furthermore, cystic GBM requires specific surgical management, including decompression of cysts during resection [26]. While several studies reported improved survival of patients with cystic GBM, this effect was not replicated by other investigations [18, 26, 27, 37, 43]. The exact prognostic value of cystic GBM, therefore, remains unclear. Whether the distinct phenotype of cystic GBM is also representative of a specific genotype remains poorly understood. Previous studies found no association of cystic glioma with MGMT promoter hypermethylation (MGMTp) and IDH mutations [7, 47]. Several studies investigating the specific radiological features of BRAF mutant GBM found a high prevalence of cysts in these tumors [15, 22, 30]. A recent study reported the enrichment of PDGFRA, KIT, and RB1 alterations and the reduced incidence of CDKN2A alterations in a cohort of 17 cystic GBM patients [43]. We aim to complement this by comprehensively assessing the genomic landscape of a large cohort of cystic- and non-cystic GBM. This could reveal further genomic drivers of cystic GBM and improve the molecular diagnosis of GBM.

Methods

Study cohort

We retrospectively assessed a cohort of 938 patients with diffuse gliomas and available next-generation sequencing (NGS) of tumor samples who presented at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center between May 2012 and September 2024. We excluded patients with IDH1/2 mutations and WHO grade 1–3 tumors, resulting in a cohort of 587 GBM, IDH wild-type. Using a natural language processing (NLP) algorithm, we searched the electronic medical records (EMR; brain MRI reports, surgical notes, and healthcare provider notes) of these patients for the terms “cyst” and “cystic”. From this screening, we obtained 201 potential cases with cystic GBM and 386 potential control cases. Using the EMR, we manually confirmed whether the hit referred to an actual tumor cyst. Patients were excluded if the term “cyst” referred to other cysts than their brain tumor (n = 95) or the tumor cyst was secondary to surgery or biopsy of the glioma (n = 19). In both groups, patients with insufficient radiological and/or histopathological data were excluded (n = 51). To verify the cyst status, the 422 pre-selected cases (78 cystic + 344 non-cystic), were independently assessed by two neuroimaging specialists, and the assessment was approved by a senior neuroradiologist with 27 years of experience. Patients who were excluded prior to this selection step were not evaluated radiologically. During this assessment, 10 non-cystic GBM cases were identified in the cystic GBM cohort, and 52 cystic GBM cases were identified in the control cohort. These cases were switched to the respective groups. Furthermore, 48 patients with indeterminate cyst status were excluded (9 cases from the primary case cohort and 39 from the primary control cohort). Finally, 111 cystic GBM cases and 263 non-cystic GBM cases were identified.

Case definition

Definitions of cystic GBM vary throughout the literature. Previous studies defined cystic GBM as GBM with the presence of well-defined spherical or ovoid homogenous T1-hypointense lesions with an enhancing rim [5, 7, 21, 34], while some studies required a cyst volume of ≥ 50% of the whole tumor volume [18, 26, 27, 44]. Guided by these criteria, we defined cystic GBM as GBM harboring at least one well-circumscribed, homogeneous, fluid-filled lesion that was T1-hypointense, T2-hyperintense, and T2-FLAIR hypointense (consistent with simple fluid signal), showing no internal enhancement or solid/nodular components and bordered by a thin (typically < 2 mm), smooth, regular enhancing wall along ≥ 75% of its circumference, without irregular thickening, nodular enhancement, or heterogeneous debris within the cavity. Morphological differentiation criteria are summarized in Table S1. We did not use a cyst-to-tumor volume threshold.

Analysis of genomic alterations

For the patients in our cohort, different clinical NGS panels were used to assess gene alterations. For each patient, the status of specific genes was determined as wild-type, altered, or not reported. For the genes significantly associated with cystic GBM, we analyzed the specific alteration type. For patients who underwent multiple biopsies with NGS testing, we only used data from the initial biopsy or surgery, as this is the closest approximation to the initial, pre-treated cystic tumor. To obtain a consensus gene panel, we selected genes for which the status was reported in ≥ 75% of the study cohort and altered in ≥ 2% of the study cohort.

Statistical methods

Univariable logistic regression was used to assess the association of cystic GBM with specific sociodemographic and genomic variables. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed for each gene, including clinical variables associated with cystic GBM, at a significance level of 0.1. Correction for multiple testing was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) method. Furthermore, we constructed a multivariable logistic regression model using genes associated with cystic GBM by p < 0.05 and sociodemographic variables associated with p < 0.1 as control variables, followed by backward elimination to identify independent variables associated with cystic GBM. To assess overall survival, the time from initial tissue sampling to death or last follow-up was measured. The distribution of overall survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. The mortality risk was compared between groups using the Cox proportional hazards model. For all statistical tests, p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and performed using SAS 9.4 and R 4.5.1.

Results

Patient characteristics and sociodemographic factors associated with cystic GBM

From the initial cohort of 938 glioma patients, we identified 374 eligible patients for analysis. Among those, we identified 111 (29.7%) cases of cystic GBM and 263 (70.3%) cases of non-cystic GBM (Fig. 1, Fig. S1). Representative images of cystic and non-cystic GBM are shown in Fig. 2. Patients in the final cohort had a mean age of 59.3 years, 63.1% were male, and 87.5% of patients were white (Table 1). Among patients with available MGMT promoter (MGMTp) methylation status, 41.0% had MGMTp methylation (Table 1). We assessed the association of cystic GBM with sociodemographic and clinical variables using univariate logistic regression. This analysis showed that the sociodemographic factors, Age, Gender, Race, and Ethnicity as well as MGMTp methylation were not significantly associated with cystic GBM (Table S2). In GBM, genomic alterations frequently occur in a co-dependent manner, which could possibly influence the results of our analysis [28]. Therefore, we assessed the co-occurrence of genomic alterations of the Rb (RB1, CDK4) and p53 pathways (TP53, MDM2) and EGFR alterations in our cohort. This analysis revealed that alterations of CDK4 and MDM2 frequently co-occurred, with MDM2 alterations present in 60.8% of CDK4-altered tumors, but only in 2.5% of CDK4 wild-type tumors (Table S5, Fig. S2A). Similarly, alterations of RB1 were present in 34.0% of TP53-altered tumors, but only in 3.0% of tumors with wild-type TP53 (Table S5, Fig. S2A). On the other hand, alterations of RB1 and TP53 rarely occurred in CDK4 or MDM2-altered tumors, respectively (Table S5, Fig. S2B). Furthermore, RB1 and TP53 were more frequently altered in EGFR wild-type tumors (RB1, 17.3%; TP53, 38.9%) than in EGFR altered tumors (RB1, 4.8%; TP53, 15.1%) (Table S5, Fig. S2C). These results suggest that alterations of the Rb and p53 pathways frequently co-occur and are negatively associated with EGFR alterations, and that most tumors only harbor alterations of one Rb or p53 pathway component. Since this analysis only assessed the binary alteration status of a gene irrespective of the detailed alteration type, we analyzed the distribution of alteration types across the genes significantly associated with cystic GBM. This analysis revealed that in the overall cohort, among patients with EGFR alterations 89.9% had an amplification and 42.8% had a mutation (Table S6). Among patients with KDR alterations, 77.8% had an amplification and 22.2% had a mutation (Table S6). In patients with EGFR and KDR alterations, the distribution of alteration types did not differ significantly between patients with cystic and non-cystic GBM (Table S6). Both patients with a KDR mutation had cystic GBM (Table S6). All patients with RB1 and TP53 alterations had a mutation, whereas all patients with CDK4 alterations had amplifications (Table S6).

Finally, we assessed the association of select gene co-alterations with cystic GBM. This analysis demonstrated that the co-alterations of CDK4 and MDM2, as well as TP53 and RB1, were significantly associated with cystic GBM (Table S7). Strikingly, only 22.7% of tumors with wild-type RB1 and TP53, but 66.7% of RB1-TP53 co-altered tumors displayed a cystic phenotype. Similarly, 27.1% of CDK4 and MDM2 wild-type tumors, but 45.2% of CDK4-MDM2 co-altered GBMs were cystic. Furthermore, the alteration of either RB1 or CDK4 strongly increased the likelihood of cystic GBM. 22.1% of tumors with wild-type RB1 and CDK4 were cystic, while in tumors with single RB1 or CDK4 alterations, 61.9% and 42.9% were cystic, respectively (Table S7). Similarly, 20.0% tumors with wild-type TP53 and MDM2 were cystic, while 45.7% and 44.7% of tumors with single TP53 or MDM2 alterations were cystic (Table S7). Together, this suggests that alterations of the Rb and p53 pathway, as well as KDR alterations, are strongly associated with cystic GBM.

Cystic GBM is not associated with overall survival

To assess the association of cystic GBM with survival, we used a Cox proportional hazards model. This analysis demonstrated that cystic GBM was not significantly associated with overall survival in our cohort (HR 0.82 [95% CI 0.61,1.11]) (Fig. 4, Table S8). Age > 60 years (HR 1.35 [95% CI 1.02, 1.78]) and absence of MGMTp methylation (HR 2.47 [95% CI 1.64, 3.72]) were significantly associated with reduced OS (Table S8). Gender, race, and ethnicity were not significantly associated with OS (Table S8). Among the 23 most frequently altered genes, only alterations of NOTCH1 were significantly associated with OS (Table S9). Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively explored the sociodemographic and genomic factors associated with cystic GBM in a large retrospective cohort. We confirmed the association of RB1 mutations with cystic GBM, which was recently reported in a cohort of 17 cystic GBMs [43]. Furthermore, we found that the co-alterations of RB1 and TP53 and CDK4 and MDM2, were associated with cystic GBM. Loss of RB1 and amplification of CDK4 result in an impaired function of the Rb pathway, leading to an acceleration of the cell cycle and a reduction of apoptosis [6, 17]. Alterations of TP53 and MDM2 both impair the physiological function of the p53 protein, resulting in increased cell cycle progression and resistance to oncogenic stress [6]. Although exhibiting various distinct biological functions, the Rb and p53 pathways are tightly interconnected both act tumor-suppressive by restricting cell cycle progression and mediating apoptosis in homeostasis [9]. Our results suggest that GBM with disturbed Rb and p53 pathway activity is more prone to cyst formation. Interestingly, recent studies have highlighted that GBMs with co-alteration of RB1 and TP53 often harbor a primitive neuronal component upon histopathological examination, and that these tumors display a distinct DNA methylation profile [4, 32, 41]. Although we did not assess histomorphology and DNA methylation in our cohort, our findings are consistent with distinct morphological properties of RB1/TP53-altered GBM. Future studies should further assess whether cystic GBM is also associated with a histopathological primitive neuronal component.

Targeting the oncogenic effects of Rb and p53 pathway alterations has been explored in several cancers, including GBM. For instance, CDK4/6 inhibitors have demonstrated strong clinical efficacy in hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer [17]. However, in GBM, CDK4/6 inhibitors did not result in an improvement of progression-free survival or overall survival despite efficacy in preclinical models [29, 46]. On the other hand, emerging data from Phase I trials suggest that MDM2 inhibitors, including Navtemadlin and Idasanutlin, may elicit pharmacodynamic and clinical responses in GBM patients with wild-type TP53 [33, 36, 46]. As RB1 and TP53 alterations, which confer resistance to inhibitors of CDK4/6 and MDM2, respectively, are enriched in cystic GBM, patients with cystic GBM may be less susceptible to future therapeutic approaches with these agents.

Furthermore, we demonstrated that cystic GBM is negatively associated with EGFR alterations including amplification and mutations. Despite the lack of efficacy of initial approaches to EGFR inhibition in GBM [20], several current trials investigate novel EGFR inhibitors in GBM harboring EGFR alterations (NCT05256290, NCT0708964, NCT01520870). In patients with cystic GBM, these agents may display a lower level of efficacy due to the lower presence of EGFR alterations.

Additionally, we showed that alterations of KDR, which encodes Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) receptor 2, are strongly enriched in cystic GBM. Approaches targeting KDR alterations using the small molecule Apatinib have shown promising results in GBM [19, 23, 48]. Besides, our results complement previous research implying VEGF signaling and angiogenesis in glioma cyst formation. For instance, cyst formation in glioma was linked to breakdown of the blood–brain-barrier and a disbalance between edema generation and resorption [24]. In the process of edema formation, tight junction and endothelial proteins as well as VEGF-signaling and Aquaporin related proteins were implied [1]. Cytokine measurements in glioma cyst fluid revealed elevated levels of VEGFA, and cystic gliomas showed high expression of angiogenetic modulators [12, 13, 16, 39, 40]. This suggests a vital role of dysregulated angiogenesis in glioma cyst formation. In line with this hypothesis, preclinical evidence showed that impaired Rb function is associated with a dysregulation of angiogenesis and increased expression of angiogenetic factors [14, 45], potentially linking of Rb pathway alterations and cyst formation. Future research should elucidate this potential mechanism and investigate whether patients with cystic GBM benefit from KDR inhibition with Apatinib or VEGF-directed therapy with Bevacizumab, which is already approved for the treatment of GBM. Besides treatment targeting VEGF, Avapritinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting PDGFRA and c-KIT, is currently evaluated in a clinical trial including high-grade gliomas (NCT04771520). KDR is often co-amplified with PDGFRA and KIT, and cystic GBM was shown to be associated with alterations in these genes, although this association was not significant when adjusting for multiple testing [43]. Therefore, cystic GBM could inform molecular testing and targeted treatment for these alterations.

Previous research investigating the prognostic effect of cystic GBM yielded conflicting results [18, 26, 27, 44]. Here, we complement these results showing that in a large cohort of 374 patients, there is no significant difference in overall survival from initial diagnosis between patients with cystic and non-cystic GBM, even when adjusting for important prognostic variables. Further research is required to identify potential morphological or radiographic features of cystic GBM potentially associated with survival.

While our study relies on a comprehensive dataset of clinical and genomic data, there are several limitations. This study is a single institutional study and lacks a validation dataset. Moreover, our approach using NLP identification of cases with cystic GBM based on EMR harbors a source of selection bias. Cases with cystic GBM in which the radiology reports did not explicitly mention the presence of cysts may have been overseen by this approach, hindering a truly comprehensive assessment of all cystic GBMs in the original cohort. On the other hand, our study may overestimate the prevalence of cystic features in GBM due to the pre-defined exclusion criteria. The retrospective nature of our study prevents any causal inference between genomic or sociodemographic variables and cyst presence. Furthermore, intratumoral heterogeneity may result in an incomplete capture of genomic alterations present in a tumor and therefore compromise our analysis. Moreover, we classified genes in altered or non-altered regardless of the underlying specific type of alteration, potentially leading to false positive or false negative results. Besides, we only included IDH1/2 wild-type patients into the study, obscuring potential associations of cystic with these gene alterations. With < 2%, alterations of CDKN2A, which were reported to be negatively associated with cystic GBM [43], occur less frequently in our dataset than described in literature [3, 10], potentially confounding the results. For survival analysis, control patients were not matched to the cystic GBM cases, potentially confounding the results by sociodemographic factors, although no significant effects of sociodemographic and genomic covariates with OS were found. Furthermore, the OS analysis did not incorporate several important prognostic factors such as extent of surgical resection or KPS performance status, which may obscure relevant OS effects of cystic GBM. Finally, although providing a comprehensive assessment of genomic correlates of cystic GBM, other relevant biological data such as histopathology or DNA methylation data were not assessed in this study.

Conclusion

Alterations of the Rb and p53 pathways, as well as alterations of KDR, are enriched in a cohort of 111 patients with cystic GBM. This provides new insights into cystic GBM pathophysiology and may help guide molecular testing and targeted treatment strategies. The enrichment of KDR alterations, which result in increased angiogenic signaling, is in line with studies reporting a crucial role of VEGF signaling in glioma cyst formation. Further studies are required to investigate whether VEGF-directed treatment proves particularly effective in cystic GBM patients.
