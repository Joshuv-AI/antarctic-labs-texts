Brain abscess management: A decade of experience
Authors: Vartika Gupta, Pankaj Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Brain abscess remains a significant neurosurgical challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, despite advances in neuroimaging, microbiological diagnostics, and surgical techniques. Optimal management requires a combination of timely diagnosis, appropriate antimicrobial therapy, and judicious surgical intervention.Methods: This retrospective single-center study analyzed 59 patients who underwent surgical management for intracranial brain abscesses over 10 years (January 2015–December 2024). Demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, predisposing factors, radiological features, microbiological profile, surgical modality, and outcomes were evaluated. Neurological status at admission was assessed using the Glasgow Coma Scale, and outcomes were measured at 6 months using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS). Outcomes were categorized as favorable (GOS 4–5) or unfavorable (GOS 1–3).Results: The majority of patients were male (72.9%), with a mean age higher in the unfavorable outcome group. Single abscesses were observed in 48 patients (81.4%). The frontal lobe was the most common location (30.5%), followed by the temporal (23.7%) and cerebellar (13.6%) lobes. Headache (72.9%), fever (52.5%), seizures (50.8%), and focal neurological deficits (44.1%) were the predominant presenting symptoms. No identifiable predisposing factor was found in 50.8% of cases, whereas post-traumatic etiology was the most common identifiable cause (16.9%). Microbiological cultures were positive in 27 patients (45.8%), with Streptococcus species being the most frequently isolated organisms; cultures were negative in 54.2% of cases. Image-guided aspiration was performed in 57.6% of patients, whereas craniotomy and excision were required in 37.3%. Favorable outcomes were achieved in 53 patients (89.8%), with an overall mortality rate of 10.2%. Poor outcomes were significantly associated with immunosuppression and the presence of multiple abscesses.Conclusion: A combined medical and surgical approach yields favorable outcomes in the majority of patients with brain abscess. Image-guided aspiration is an effective first-line surgical modality in appropriately selected cases, whereas craniotomy and excision remain essential for complex or refractory lesions. Early diagnosis, prompt antimicrobial therapy, and timely surgical intervention are critical determinants of outcome.

INTRODUCTION

Brain abscesses constitute approximately 2% of all intracranial space-occupying lesions in high-income countries, whereas their incidence increases to nearly 8% in low- and middle-income nations.[4,8,21]

At present, image-guided aspiration and craniotomy with excision remain the two primary surgical treatment modalities for brain abscesses.[19,25,28] Image-guided aspiration is generally preferred because it is minimally invasive and is associated with a lower risk of postoperative neurological morbidity compared with open excision.[14,25] Despite advances in neurosurgical techniques—including stereotactic aspiration, improved microbiological culture methods, expanded antimicrobial therapy, and modern noninvasive neuroimaging—brain abscesses continue to represent a significant public health challenge, particularly in developing countries.[4,5,10,12,16,19,28] However, excision remains important for multiloculated abscesses, fungal infections, and lesions refractory to aspiration.[19,28] The microbiological spectrum of brain abscess has evolved over time, with Streptococcus species, anaerobic bacteria, and opportunistic pathogens frequently implicated,[1,8,9,11] particularly in immunocompromised individuals.[2,32] Improvements in neuroimaging, stereotactic techniques, microbiological culture methods, and antimicrobial therapy have significantly improved diagnostic accuracy and treatment outcomes.[3,6,7,13,16]

This study is based on our 10-year single-center experience involving 59 patients who underwent surgical management for brain abscesses. We evaluated demographic characteristics, predisposing risk factors, microbiological spectrum, treatment strategies, and prognostic indicators to identify factors influencing disease presentation and clinical outcomes [Table 1].

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient cohort

Brain abscess was defined as a focal intracerebral infection characterized by a ring-enhancing lesion with central necrosis on contrast-enhanced computed tomography/magnetic resonance imaging (CT/MRI), consistent with a capsule-forming suppurative process. Subdural empyema, epidural empyema, and isolated cerebritis without capsule formation were excluded from this study.

This retrospective study included 59 patients with surgically managed intracranial brain abscesses treated at the Department of Neurosurgery, Mahatma Gandhi Hospital, over 10 years from January 2015 to December 2024. Patient data were obtained from institutional medical records.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Mahatma Gandhi Hospital. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived.

The variables analyzed included demographic characteristics, neurological status at admission as assessed by the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), clinical presentation, predisposing risk factors, anatomical location of the abscess, number of lesions, surgical modality employed, microbiological culture results, and neurological outcome. Neurological outcomes were categorized as favorable or unfavorable, and their associations with clinical, radiological, and treatment-related factors were evaluated. Radiology

All patients underwent preoperative CT and MRI of the brain. CT was useful for early detection and precise localization of the abscess, assessment of number, size, and stage of the lesion, and identification of associated findings such as hydrocephalus, raised intracranial pressure, perilesional edema, subdural empyema, and ventriculitis, thereby aiding surgical planning. CT also played an important role in assessing treatment adequacy and in sequential follow-up.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain (plain and contrast-enhanced) was the imaging modality of choice, as it provided superior delineation of lesion extent, capsule characteristics, surrounding edema, and involvement of adjacent structures.[24]

Patient evaluation

Any clinical condition or event directly related to the development of a brain abscess was considered a predisposing factor. Routes of infection were categorized as contiguous spread, post-neurosurgical procedures, open head injury, hematogenous spread, or unknown. Neurological status at admission was assessed using the GCS.

All patients underwent routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, serum biochemistry, blood cultures, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and C-reactive protein (CRP). Microbiological analysis of all intracranial samples formed the basis of etiological diagnosis. Pus aspirates obtained intraoperatively were promptly transported to the microbiology laboratory of Mahatma Gandhi Hospital and processed according to standard protocols for aerobic, anaerobic, and fungal cultures, microscopy, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Clinical outcome was assessed at 6 months postoperatively during outpatient follow-up using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS). A GOS score of 4 or 5 was considered a favorable outcome, whereas a score of 1–3 was categorized an unfavorable outcome.

Medical management

Antibiotic therapy

Empirical intravenous antibiotics were initiated immediately upon radiological diagnosis, before surgical intervention, except in cases where emergent decompression was required. Intraoperative samples were obtained before irrigation to avoid culture contamination.

Empirical intravenous antibiotic therapy consisted of ceftriaxone and metronidazole, selected based on the presumed portal of entry and anatomical location of the abscess. Antibiotic regimens were modified 3–5 days later according to culture and sensitivity results when available. Intravenous antibiotics were continued for a total duration of 6 weeks, guided by serial neuroimaging findings and clinical response. All antimicrobial therapy was administered under the supervision of infectious disease specialists.

Steroid therapy

Patients with significant mass effect and perilesional edema received low-dose corticosteroid therapy (intravenous dexamethasone 4 mg every 8 h). Steroids were tapered and discontinued as soon as clinical stability was achieved.

Antiepileptic therapy

All patients received antiepileptic drugs for seizure prophylaxis, which were continued for a minimum duration of 2 years.

Surgical management

Image-guided aspiration was performed using CT-based frameless neuronavigation. Image-guided aspiration was performed for abscesses larger than 2.5 cm, those producing significant mass effect with features of impending herniation, lesions in proximity to the ventricular system, involvement of eloquent cortical areas, deep-seated locations (including the basal ganglia or brainstem), progressive enlargement despite medical therapy, or neurological deterioration. Aspiration was avoided in cases of cerebritis without radiological evidence of necrosis or capsule formation. When post-aspiration imaging demonstrated inadequate reduction or increase in abscess size, repeat aspiration was performed. During the procedure, the abscess cavity was completely evacuated and irrigated with gentamicin-mixed saline until the effluent was clear.

Craniotomy and excision were reserved for patients with poor response to repeated aspirations (more than three attempts) and medical therapy. Additional indications for excision included:

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median (range), and categorical variables as number (percentage). Categorical variables between the two outcome groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software.

RESULTS

A total of 59 patients with intracranial abscesses were included in the study and treated surgically. Single abscesses were observed in 48 patients (81.4%), whereas 11 patients (18.6%) had multiple abscesses. Patient age ranged from 1.8 to 76 years. There was a clear male predominance, with 43 males (72.9%) and 16 females (27.1%), resulting in a male-to-female ratio of approximately 2.7:1.

The most common clinical manifestations were headache, fever, and focal neurological deficits. Headache was the most frequent symptom, present in 43 patients (72.9%). Fever was observed in 31 patients (52.5%), and seizures occurred in 30 patients (50.8%). Focal neurological deficits were noted in 26 patients (44.1%), while nausea and vomiting were reported in 11 patients (18.6%). Visual disturbances were documented in three patients with occipital lobe abscesses, and aphasia was observed in three patients.

At admission, 52 patients (88.1%) had a GCS score of 14–15, while 7 patients (11.9%) had a GCS score ≤13. Admission GCS did not demonstrate a significant association with clinical outcome. Similarly, presenting symptoms did not correlate with GOS scores at 6-month follow-up.

Predisposing factors varied across the cohort. No identifiable risk factor was found in 30 patients (50.8%). Among identifiable causes, post-traumatic abscesses were the most common, seen in 10 patients (16.9%). Postoperative abscesses occurred in 6 patients (10.2%), chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM) in 6 patients (10.2%), congenital heart disease in 4 patients (6.8%), and immunosuppression in 3 patients (5.1%) [Table 3].

No documented cases of paranasal sinus–related or odontogenic infections were identified in this cohort. Despite the predominance of frontal lobe abscesses (n = 18, 30.5%), no clear sinus or dental source was recorded in the medical records. These cases were categorized as cryptogenic.

The frontal lobe was the most commonly involved site, affected in 18 patients (30.5%), followed by the temporal lobe in 14 patients (23.7%) and the cerebellum in 8 patients (13.6%) [Table 2].

Contiguous spread accounted for 6 cases (10.2%) and had a direct influence on abscess location [Table 3]. Otogenic infections were predominantly associated with temporal (4/6) and cerebellar abscesses (2/6). Among frontal lobe abscesses, no identifiable predisposing factor was found in 10 of 30 patients (33.3%).

Image-guided aspiration was the most frequently employed surgical modality, performed in 34 patients (57.6%). Craniotomy and excision were required in 22 patients (37.3%), whereas 3 patients (5.1%) underwent excision with concomitant radical mastoidectomy [Table 2]. All immunosuppressed patients had a history of renal transplantation.

Laboratory parameters demonstrated limited diagnostic or prognostic significance [Table 4]. Leukocytosis was observed in 15 patients (25.4%), elevated ESR in 15 patients (25.4%), and normal CRP levels in 29 patients (49.2%).

At 6-month follow-up, 53 patients (89.8%) achieved a favorable outcome (GOS 4–5), whereas 6 patients (10.2%) had an unfavorable outcome (GOS 1–3). On univariate analysis, multiple abscesses (P < 0.001) and immunosuppression (P = 0.006) were significantly associated with unfavorable outcome. Admission GCS did not demonstrate a statistically significant association (P = 0.18). The overall mortality rate was 10.2% (n = 6). Deaths occurred in the early postoperative period, most commonly due to multisystem organ failure in immunocompromised patients and severe ventriculitis or deep-seated abscesses in patients presenting with poor neurological status.

Postoperative seizures were the most common complication and were managed with antiepileptic therapy. After discharge, patients were followed in the outpatient clinic. Long-term follow-up was defined as clinical and radiological assessment beyond 6 months postoperatively. Follow-up data beyond 6 months were available for 53 patients (89.8%). All surviving patients had a minimum follow-up of 6 months, as per study protocol. Six patients (10.2%) died in the early postoperative period and therefore were not available for long-term follow-up.

Microbiological analysis revealed positive cultures in 27 patients (45.8%), whereas no growth was observed in 32 patients (54.2%) [Table 5].

DISCUSSION

In the present study of 59 patients with intracranial abscesses, the frontal lobe was the most commonly involved region (30.5%), followed by the temporal (23.7%) and cerebellar (13.6%) lobes. This anatomical distribution is consistent with most published series, in which frontal and temporal lobes predominate because of their proximity to paranasal sinuses, middle ear structures, and common routes of hematogenous spread. Frontal lobe abscesses are frequently associated with paranasal sinus infections and odontogenic sources due to direct contiguous spread from adjacent anatomical structures. Recent literature has increasingly highlighted odontogenic infections as an underrecognized source of intracranial abscess, particularly affecting the frontal and temporal lobes. Although no documented sinus or dental sources were identified in our cohort, the high proportion of cryptogenic frontal abscesses suggests that occult sinus or odontogenic infections may have been underdiagnosed in this retrospective series. A large meta-analysis analyzing more than 9,000 patients with brain abscess, reported a similar predominance of frontal and temporal lobe involvement and emphasized that neurological status at presentation remains one of the strongest predictors of outcome.[21,30] Seizures were observed in approximately half of the patients (50.8%), whereas headache (72.9%), fever (52.5%), and focal neurological deficits (44.1%) were frequent presenting symptoms. Despite these common manifestations, the classical triad of headache, fever, and focal neurological deficit was present in only a minority of cases, as has been reported in previous studies.[16,30]

A clear male predominance was observed in our cohort, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 2.7:1. Similar sex distributions have been reported worldwide, suggesting a higher susceptibility among males, possibly related to occupational exposure, trauma, and higher prevalence of risk factors.[15,27] However, some studies have reported a female predominance, highlighting regional and population-based variability. Given the single-center nature and limited sample size of the present study, definitive conclusions regarding sex as a prognostic factor cannot be drawn, and larger multicenter studies are required.

Although patients older than 40 years constituted a substantial proportion of the cohort, age-related susceptibility to brain abscess remains inconsistent across published literature. While some authors report a higher incidence in older patients, others describe predominance in younger age groups. These discrepancies suggest that age alone is unlikely to be a primary determinant and that underlying comorbidities and predisposing conditions may play a more significant role in disease development.

Single abscesses were observed in the majority of patients (81.4%), with multiple abscesses present in 18.6% of cases.[21,30] This distribution closely mirrors that reported in large contemporary series. The frontal lobe remained the most frequently affected site regardless of the number of abscesses, whereas temporal and cerebellar involvement was more often associated with contiguous otogenic infections. Odontogenic infections are increasingly recognized as an important source of intracranial abscess, particularly involving the frontal and temporal lobes, as highlighted in a recent literature.[10,12]

Predisposing factors were identified in approximately half of the patients, whereas no clear etiology was found in the remaining 50.8%. Post-traumatic abscesses were the most common identifiable cause (16.9%), followed by postoperative infections and CSOM (each 10.2%). Similar proportions of cryptogenic abscesses have been reported previously, underscoring the challenges in identifying a definitive source despite detailed clinical and radiological evaluation.[17,18] Contiguous spread accounted for a smaller proportion of cases (10.2%) but demonstrated a strong association with abscess location, particularly the temporal and cerebellar lobes in otogenic infections.

Although no cases of documented paranasal sinusitis or odontogenic infection were identified, the high proportion of cryptogenic frontal lobe abscesses suggests that occult sinus or dental sources may have been under-recognized in this retrospective review. Although, contiguous infections such as chronic otitis media, sinusitis, and dental infections remain important etiological factors in previously reported studies.[23,29] Systematic otorhinolaryngological and dental evaluation was not uniformly performed, representing a limitation of this study.

Microbiological growth was obtained in 27 patients (45.8%), whereas cultures were negative in 32 patients (54.2%). Streptococcus species were the most frequently isolated organisms, consistent with prior reports.[11,22,26] Tubercular and fungal abscesses constituted a small but clinically significant subset. The relatively high rate of culture-negative abscesses may be attributed to prior antibiotic exposure, delayed transport of specimens, or limitations of routine culture techniques, particularly for anaerobic organisms.[9,26] Recent advances in molecular diagnostic techniques have markedly improved pathogen detection in culture-negative brain abscesses. For example, a large retrospective cohort study demonstrated that metagenomic next-generation sequencing significantly increased overall pathogen identification rates in both pus and cerebrospinal fluid samples compared with conventional culture methods, particularly revealing a broader spectrum of anaerobic and fastidious organisms that routine cultures often miss. These findings reinforce the need for empirical broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy when cultures are negative. Advanced molecular diagnostic techniques, including 16S rRNA gene sequencing and metagenomic analysis, have shown promise in improving pathogen detection in culture-negative cases and may enhance future diagnostic accuracy.

Image-guided aspiration was the most commonly employed surgical modality in our cohort (57.6%), whereas craniotomy and excision were required in 37.3% of patients. Image-guided aspiration is widely considered a first-line approach in many centers. Contemporary evidence does not conclusively favor excision over aspiration with respect to overall clinical outcomes.[14,20,25] In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis comparing aspiration versus excision in intraparenchymal abscesses, both techniques yielded similar overall outcomes, although excision may be associated with a lower re-operation rate in select cases without strong contraindications to either modality. This supports the continued use of image-guided aspiration as a safe and effective initial strategy, with excision reserved for multiloculated, refractory, or fungal abscesses, or when anatomical considerations preclude safe aspiration. Radical mastoidectomy combined with excision was performed in a small subset of patients with otogenic cerebellar abscesses. Overall outcomes were favorable, with 89.8% of patients achieving good recovery (GOS 4–5). The overall mortality rate was 10.2%, which is comparable to rates reported in contemporary series in the modern imaging era. Recent clinical studies describe similar mortality and outcome profiles in patients with brain abscess since the introduction of CT and MRI, highlighting continued improvements in diagnosis, surgical intervention, and multidisciplinary care. In our cohort, multiple abscesses and immunosuppression were significantly associated with unfavorable outcomes. Patients presenting with multiple lesions had a markedly higher risk of poor outcome, likely reflecting greater disease burden and systemic dissemination. Similarly, immunosuppressed patients—particularly renal transplant recipients—demonstrated worse prognosis, consistent with previously published literature.

Although neurological status at presentation has been identified as a strong predictor in several large series, admission GCS did not reach statistical significance in our study. This may be related to the relatively small number of patients presenting with severe neurological impairment.

Admission GCS scores were satisfactory (14–15) in the majority of patients (88.1%) and did not independently predict outcome in this cohort. This finding may reflect the influence of other factors such as immunosuppression, abscess location, and systemic complications. While neurological status at presentation has been identified as a strong prognostic indicator in several studies, our data suggest that outcome is multifactorial and cannot be reliably predicted by GCS alone.

Postoperative seizures were the most common complication and were effectively managed with antiepileptic therapy. No clinical or radiological evidence of recurrence was observed among patients available for follow-up, supporting the effectiveness of the combined surgical and medical management strategy employed in this study.

Fungal abscesses, although uncommon, have been reported even in immunocompetent individuals, as described by Wilson and Kennedy.[31]

CONCLUSION

Brain abscesses were more commonly observed in males, with the frontal lobe being the most frequently involved site. Headache, fever, seizures, and focal neurological deficits were the predominant presenting symptoms. Streptococcus species constituted the most commonly isolated pathogens, although microbiological growth was not obtained in a substantial proportion of cases.

All patients require prompt initiation of intravenous antimicrobial therapy. Image-guided aspiration is an effective first-line surgical option for abscesses ≥2.5 cm in diameter, lesions associated with neurological deterioration, deep-seated or eloquent-area involvement, or failure of medical management. Craniotomy and excision should be considered for multiloculated, thick-walled, recurrent, fungal, or post-traumatic abscesses, and in cases showing inadequate response to aspiration [Figure 1]. Combined medical and surgical treatment remains the cornerstone of management.[19,28]
