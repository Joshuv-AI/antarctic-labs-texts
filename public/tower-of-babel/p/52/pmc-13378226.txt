Multifunctional films incorporating carbon quantum dots derived from lactic acid bacteria for extending the shelf life of mangoes
Authors: Devadharshini Chelladurai, Ramalakshmi Alaguthevar, Balakrishnan Murugesan, Karthikeyan Subburamu, Kavitha P. S.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing demand for sustainable and active food packaging materials to extend the shelf life of perishable products has driven the exploration of bio-derived nanomaterials. This study explores the development of biogenic carbon quantum dots (CQDs) derived from lactic acid bacteria (LAB) metabolites as a sustainable approach for active food packaging. CQDs were synthesized via a hydrothermal process and incorporated into carrageenan-based films to develop multifunctional LAB-M/CQD films. The films exhibited significant antibacterial activity, with inhibition zones of 4.14 cm for Escherichia coli, 2.78 cm for Bacillus cereus, and 2.61 cm for Staphylococcus aureus. The antioxidant activity, determined using the DPPH assay, reached a maximum of 97.8% in films containing 3% CQDs, showing a concentration-dependent increase. Mechanical analysis revealed enhanced stiffness, with Young’s modulus increasing from 4.36 to 30.97 MPa and tensile strength improving from 1.09 MPa (control) to 1.86 MPa at 3% CQD. However, elongation at break decreased from 17.24% to 6.57%, indicating reduced flexibility. The 3% CQD film demonstrated an optimal balance between strength and functional performance. Barrier analysis showed a reduction in water vapour transmission rate (WVTR) from 506.84 to 446.57 g/m2·h and corresponding water vapour permeability (WVP) from 4.00 × 10−⁶ to 3.91 × 10−⁶ g·m/m2·h·Pa with increasing CQD concentration. Shelf-life studies on mangoes demonstrated that LAB-M/CQD films effectively delayed ripening. Fruits packaged with 3% CQD films showed improved stability in pH and titratable acidity and reduced ethylene production over 21 days compared to controls. This study highlights the potential of LAB-derived CQD-incorporated films as sustainable, active packaging materials for extending the shelf life of perishable foods.

Introduction

Fruit consumption is crucial for human health since it contains a high concentration of vitamins, minerals, antioxidants, and dietary fibre, all of which play important roles in disease prevention and overall wellness. Fruit-rich diets have been linked to a lower risk of chronic illnesses like cardiovascular disease, certain types of cancer, and metabolic disorders [53, 54]. Fruits are highly perishable due to their elevated water content, enzymatic activity, and vulnerability to microbial growth. This necessitates effective preservation strategies to maintain quality, reduce spoilage, and extend shelf life from farm to table. This perishability is a key concern in the fresh produce market, as spoiling causes significant economic losses for both producers and consumers [19].

Consumers today are more aware of fruit packaging. Its importance extends beyond preservation to include food safety, sustainability, and convenience. According to surveys, more than 70% of consumers prefer visual freshness and packaging integrity when buying fresh food [9, 22, 38]. Effective packaging is thus essential for increasing shelf life, preserving nutrient quality, and avoiding food waste. Traditional packaging methods have disadvantages, such as the environmental impact of plastic waste and their limited efficiency against specific spoilage factors [24, 45]. In the pursuit of sustainable and effective packaging solutions, natural and biodegradable materials have received more attention. Carbon Quantum Dots (CQDs) possess distinctive properties that address these gaps, being non-toxic, eco-friendly, and highly biocompatible, which makes them ideal for food packaging applications. CQDs are carbon-based nanoparticles (< 10 nm) that have emerged as a cutting-edge innovation due to their exceptional water solubility, low toxicity, strong photoluminescence, and high surface-area-to-volume ratio [14–16]. These features allow CQDs to play multiple functions in food preservation, such as antibacterial activity, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and fluorescence-based sensing [14–16]. The antibacterial effect of carbon quantum dots has been partially attributed to reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation under ambient or light-assisted conditions, which promotes oxidative stress and membrane damage in microorganisms [13].

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB), a gram-positive microbe, are gaining popularity for their multifunctional properties in food preservation and bioproduction. LAB can limit pathogenic growth by producing bacteriocins, organic acids, hydrogen peroxide and enzymes, forming a natural barrier to food spoilage. Lactic acid bacteria (LABs) can serve as effective and practical biological preservatives and control agents. LABs classified as Qualified Presumption of Safety (QPS)-approved microorganisms are suitable for food production as additives, preservatives, and fermentation starters [29, 48]. Scientists have developed an interest in the Weissella genus during the past 20 years [18]. The genus Weissella was established in 1993 following the reclassification of Lactobacillus and Leuconostoc species [18]. As relatively recent members of the LAB family [2], Weissella species are gram-positive, catalase-negative, and non-spore-forming bacteria. They produce lactic acid and carbon dioxide by metabolizing glucose through the heterolactic fermentation pathway. Recently, certain polysaccharides derived from Weissella species have been functionally characterized for applications in the food industry [25, 30]. Aside from their biological activities, LABs have distinct potential as precursors for nanomaterial synthesis, suggesting a convergence of microbiology and nanotechnology. These metabolites have functional groups and are high in carbon, such as carboxyl and hydroxyl, which are required for CQD production. Compared to traditional precursors such as graphite or chemical polymers, LAB metabolites provide advantages over hazardous synthetic predecessors. LAB metabolites are produced from renewable sources, which reduces the reliance on non-renewable or toxic materials utilised in traditional CQD production methods (such as arc discharge or chemical oxidation) [33]. This is consistent with global initiatives towards greener technologies.

The Neelam (Mangifera indica L. cv. Neelam) mango variety, primarily grown in Southern India, is renowned for its rich flavour, aroma, and nutritional value. Like many tropical fruits, it is highly perishable, with a short post-harvest shelf life under ambient conditions, limiting its potential. Mangoes produced in India account for over 45% of total global tropical fruit production, with an annual production of over 22.5 million tonnes [4]. However, the Neelam cultivar suffers severe post-harvest losses, estimated at 15—20%, due to fast ripening and microbial destruction, resulting in huge economic and nutritional waste. Because of its rapid climacteric ripening and documented susceptibility to postharvest microbial spoilage under ambient conditions, the Neelam cultivar is a sensitive and appropriate model for assessing the efficacy of antimicrobial packaging systems designed to extend fruit shelf life [46]. Postharvest spoiling of mangoes and other climacteric fruits is mostly caused by fungal pathogens such as Colletotrichum gloeosporioides and Lasiodiplodia theobromae, as well as bacterial contamination from harvesting and handling. Bacterial species such as Bacillus and Staphylococcus are regularly identified from fresh fruit surfaces and postharvest habitats, contributing to microbial deterioration under ambient storage settings. Escherichia coli is a typical indicator of sanitary quality in fresh vegetables [47]. Bacillus cereus, Staphylococcus aureus, and Escherichia coli were chosen as typical Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria to test the antibacterial efficiency of LAB metabolite-derived CQD-incorporated packing sheets for mango preservation.

Biopolymer-based films and coatings, composed of proteins, polysaccharides, and lipids, have gained significant attention over the last decade. Packaging film manufacturers utilize various polysaccharides, such as gums, cellulose, pectin, chitosan, starch derivatives, and carrageenan [36, 44]. Carrageenan with high hydroxyl content has exceptional gelling and film-forming properties, making it a potential Packaging material. Carrageenan-based composite films often include functional ingredients such as polyphenols, nanomaterials, and enzymes [23].

This study is a pioneering investigation into Lactic Acid Bacteria metabolites -derived CQDs fabricated in biopolymer packaging film, examining their synthesis, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties, and other characterization. Previous research has reported the inclusion of CQDs into antimicrobial food packaging materials; however, studies evaluating biogenically produced CQDs in packaging films under real-world fruit storage conditions are limited. Previous research has also documented carbon quantum dots produced from LAB or biogenic sources, mostly for antibacterial or sensing purposes in the laboratory. However, the use of LAB-derived extracellular metabolites as CQD precursors, followed by integration into packing films and validation via fruit shelf-life experiments, remains scarcely explored.

The functional film's efficacy was evaluated, demonstrating a sustainable approach designed to extend the shelf life of the Neelam mango variety. It intends to create a foundation for the future of, biodegradable packaging materials that integrate food safety, sustainability, nanotechnology, and microbiology in novel ways. In comparison with traditionally produced carbon quantum dots, LAB metabolite-derived CQDs provide comparable antibacterial and functional performance while offering advantages such as biogenic origin, decreased chemical synthesis requirements, and better appropriateness for food-contact applications.

Experimental materials and methods

Source of lactic acid bacteria

The Lactic acid bacterium Weissella cibaria strain M1RC1 (accession number—OR220340.1) was obtained from the Food Microbiology Laboratory of the Department of Food Process Engineering, Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU), Coimbatore.

Analysis of lactic acid bacteria by GC–MS analysis

The isolates of Weissella cibaria were cultured in MRS broth at 37 °C for 24 h. Microbial cells were separated from the culture medium by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. The pH of the collected supernatant was adjusted to acidic conditions (pH 2.0) to facilitate the extraction of extracellular metabolites. The bacterial suspension was used to identify carbon-rich metabolites using Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis as described by Rajaofera et al., [39]. The gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis was performed using a Perkin Elmer Clarus SQ 8 C system. Carbon-dense metabolites were analyzed in the Metscape for the pathway-based analysis. Metscape, a Cytoscape plugin, was used for pathway-based analysis of the detected carbon-dense metabolites. The network was built using data from the KEGG database, incorporating interactions between compounds, reactions, enzymes, and genes to illustrate metabolic connectivity.

Synthesis of carbon quantum dots from lactic acid bacteria

Through a one-step hydrothermal method, CQDs were synthesized using the aqueous cell-free supernatant (CFS) of Weissella cibaria. [28]. The probiotic bacterium W. cibaria was sub-cultured in De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) broth and incubated under anaerobic conditions at 37 ± 1 °C for 48 hours. The bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 minutes to obtain the CFS, which was then incubated in a stainless-steel chamber at 200 °C for 24 hours. The resulting dark brown solution, termed Lactic Acid Bacteria Metabolites Derived CQDs (LAB-M/CQDs), was filtered through sterile syringe filters (0.42 μm and 0.22 μm) to eliminate larger particles. The filtrate was freeze-dried to form a powdered CQD, which was then stored in aseptic conditions at 4 °C in the refrigerator for further testing. The J/MRS broth (only MRS broth without the bacteria) was also utilized as a control for developing CQDs (J/CQDs) using the same process described above.

Characterization of synthesized CQDs

The UV–visible absorption and transmittance of the CQDs' aqueous solution were measured after centrifuging it at 10,000 rpm for 20 min. The measurements were performed using a double-beam spectrophotometer (UV 1800- Shimadzu) with a 1 nm resolution over a wavelength range of 190 to 800 nm. Particle Size Analysis SA (Malvern Panalytical Zetasizer Ultra) and TEM (JEOL JEM-2100) were employed to analyse the synthesised CQDs' size, zeta potential, and morphology. The Raman spectrometer R-3000-QETM was used to analyse the structural properties of CQDs.

Fabrication of functional films

The synthesized CQDs were incorporated into a carrageenan-based functional film following the method described by Khan et al. [26]. A total of 2 g of carrageenan (Carg) was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water and stirred at 85 °C for 1 h. Glycerol, at 30 wt% of Carg, was added as a plasticiser and stirred for an additional 2 h. LAB-M/CQD concentrations of 1 wt%, 2 wt%, and 3 wt% were introduced into the mixture and stirred at 50 °C for 2 h. The resulting film solutions were cast onto a Teflon-coated glass plate (24 cm × 30 cm) and allowed to harden at room temperature for 48 h. After drying, the films were removed and stored at 25 °C and 50% relative humidity for at least 48 h. A neat carrageenan film without LAB-M/CQD (N-Carg) was also prepared using the same process. The finalised multifunctional films were designated as Carg/CQD 1% (LAB-M/CQD1%), Carg/CQD 2% (LAB-M/CQD 2%), Carg/CQD 3% (LAB-M/CQD3%), and N-Carg.

LAB-M/CQD-based functional films characterization

Antimicrobial activity

The antibacterial activity of the films was evaluated using the agar well diffusion method against Escherichia coli (Gram-negative), Bacillus cereus (Gram-positive), and Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive) on solid media. An inoculum containing 6 log CFU/mL was evenly spread onto agar plates using a sterile swab. Wells measuring 8 mm in diameter were punched into the agar, filled with 100 μL of each film solution, and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. The diameter of the inhibition zones was recorded [41]. Additionally, the antibacterial efficacy of the films against E. coli, B. cereus, and S. aureus was determined using the total viable colony count method, following the procedure described by Ezati et al. [14–16].

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant properties of the functional films were assessed using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging method, as outlined by Ezati et al. [15]. A methanolic solution of DPPH was prepared, and approximately 100 mg of each film sample was added to 20 mL of the DPPH solution. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 360 min, and the absorbance was measured at 517 nm and the experiments were performed in triplicate. Structural, morphological, and mechanical properties of functional film

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction (XRD) were used to examine the structural and morphological features of the functional films containing CQD. The surface morphology of the films was investigated by SEM analysis (FEI, Czech Republic Model: Quanta 250), with samples sputter-coated with a thin coating of gold under a high vacuum before imaging. FTIR spectroscopy (Jasco FT/IR-6800) was utilized to analyse the functional compounds and interactions between CQDs and the biopolymer matrix at wavelengths of 4000-450 cm−1. The film's crystalline characteristics were analyzed using XRD (Rigaku Ultima IV X-ray Diffractometer). XRD was recorded in the 2θ range of 5° to 80°, and the degree of crystallinity was determined to examine the influence of CQD inclusion on the structural properties of the functional film. All measurements were conducted at a controlled room temperature of 25 ± 2 °C. Mechanical properties such as elongation at break (EAB) and tensile strength (TS) were tested using an Instron Model 3366. The results were used to determine the impact of CQDs on the mechanical attributes of the films.

Water vapour transmission rate

The water vapour transmission rate (WVTR) of the films was measured following the method described by [20]. Film samples were cut into uniform squares (30 × 30 mm2) and carefully placed over the opening of pre-weighed (IBW) glass bottles filled with 15 mL of ultrapure water. The mouth of each bottle was sealed tightly with Teflon tape to prevent vapour leakage [6].

The sealed bottles were incubated at a controlled temperature of 40 °C for 24 h to simulate conditions that promote water vapour diffusion. After incubation, the bottles were reweighed to determine the final bottle weight (FBW), and the weight difference before and after incubation was used to calculate the amount of water vapour transmitted through the film.

The WVTR was calculated using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{W}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{R} \left(\frac{\mathrm{g}}{{\mathrm{m}}^{2}}/\mathrm{h}\right)=\frac{IBW-FBW}{AMB\times T}$$\end{document}where AMB is the area of the exposed film in square meters, and T is the period in hours.

This value quantifies the film's ability to act as a moisture barrier, which is essential for packaging applications, particularly for moisture-sensitive products like fresh fruits and vegetables [37].

Film thickness was taken into consideration when calculating water vapor permeability (WVP) using the following relation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{W}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{P}=\frac{WVTR\times L}{\Delta P}$$\end{document}where ΔP is the water vapor pressure differential across the film, and L is the film thickness (m). The saturated water vapour pressure at 40 °C (7380 Pa) was used to determine the water vapour pressure differential (ΔP), taking into account the presence of distilled water inside the test cell (≈100% relative humidity) and dry exterior conditions as specified by the experimental setup. WVP values were given in g·m/m2·h·Pa [27].

Oxygen transmission rate

The oxygen transmission rate (OTR) was determined using a gas permeability tester (following ASTM D3985). Film samples (30 × 30 mm2) were mounted in an OTR cell, where one side was exposed to pure oxygen, and the other side was filled with nitrogen. The device measured the amount of oxygen that permeated through the film over time under standard conditions (23 °C and 0% relative humidity).

OTR was calculated as the amount of oxygen transmitted per unit area and unit time, expressed in OTR was expressed as the volume of oxygen transmitted per unit area per unit time, in cm3/m2·day. The oxygen barrier properties of the LAB-M/CQD films were compared with the control carrageenan film, and the results provided insights into the effectiveness of the films in controlling oxygen transmission, which is critical for preventing oxidation and extending the shelf life of packaged products [51].

Packaging applications

Mango fruit of the Neelam Mangifera indica L.cv. Neelam) variety was used to assess the film's effectiveness in determining fruit shelf life. Mangoes were packed with LAB-M/CQD functional film in varying concentrations and unpacked fruit as a control. Fruits were packed in thin films (carrageenan film or CQD-incorporated films) and stored at 25 ± 2 °C for 21 days. Changes in the appearance of each group were recorded at 7-day intervals using a digital camera (Samsung Galaxy A34 5G). The pH (digital pH meter, Toshcon LH-14) and titratable acidity of the samples were measured at regular intervals to evaluate changes in chemical composition during storage.

Biodegradability assessment

Film samples (30 × 30 mm2) were weighed (initial weight, IW) and buried at a depth of 5 cm in garden soil maintained at 25 ± 2 °C. Soil moisture was controlled by the periodic addition of 5 mL of water to maintain consistent humidity. After 7 days, samples were carefully retrieved using forceps, and adhering soil particles were gently removed using tissue paper without washing, to avoid structural damage. The samples were then dried at 40 °C until constant weight and conditioned in a desiccator for 24 h prior to final weighing. Titratable acidity and ethylene scavenging property

Mango pulp (~ 7 g) was homogenised in distilled water to determine titratable acidity. The filtrate was then titrated with 0.1 N NaOH using phenolphthalein as an indicator until a persistent pale pink endpoint was reached [5]. A gas chromatograph (PerkinElmer Clarus SQ8) fitted with a Porapak Q column and a flame ionization detector (FID) was used to measure the amount of ethylene produced. Mango samples were placed in airtight containers, and a gas-tight syringe was used to extract 1 mL of headspace gas, which was then injected into the GC apparatus. The carrier gas was nitrogen. Using external calibration with standard ethylene gas, the ethylene concentration was measured and reported in parts per million [10]. Every measurement was done in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Film properties were assessed using independently prepared films, each tested in duplicate. Statistical significance (p < 0.05) of mean property values was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0.1 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results and discussion

Carbon-dense metabolites of Weissella cibaria

The metabolites produced by the isolate ME 26 showcase a diverse range of chemical structures and functional groups. Among the first 15 compounds, cholestan-3—ol, 2-methylene-, is notable for having the highest number of carbon atoms (27), indicating a complex structure typical of steroid-like compounds. Other compounds such as benzoic acid derivatives also exhibit significant carbon content. The presence of benzoic acid, 6-chloro-3-[5-(3-pyridylcarbonylhydrazonomethyl) fur-2—yl] suggests that the isolate may produce metabolites with complex aromatic structures. The diversity in carbon content, ranging from smaller molecules like diglycolic acid with 4 carbons to larger ones like naphthalenediol with 17 carbons, highlights the metabolic versatility. This versatility may enable the bacteria to thrive in varied environments and contribute to their role in fermentation and preservation processes. Additionally, the presence of functional groups such as hydroxyl, nitro, and amine indicates potential interactions with biological systems, paving the way for applications in food preservation, probiotic formulations, and even therapeutic uses against certain pathogens. Characterization of synthesized LAB-M/CQDs

Optical, morphological, and structural properties

The synthesized LAB-M/CQDs demonstrated significant optical characteristics under different lighting conditions, including natural and ultraviolet light. The solution appeared brownish in natural light, indicating carbonization, and displayed a bright blue fluorescence at 365 nm in UV light, indicating a high fluorescence quantum yield (Fig. 1. (c), inset). The UV–Vis spectra revealed distinct absorption peaks, especially in the UV region, associated with n-π* transitions of surface functional groups such as C = O and C-O (Abdella & Malla, 2023). This fluorescence activity is comparable to the quantum confinement effect in carbon quantum dots, where functional groups improve photoluminescence by altering electron transitions. According to Baker and Baker [8], the synthesis conditions and the type of precursors used were crucial in determining fluorescence efficiency.

The LAB-M/CQDs had a spherical shape, with an average particle diameter of 8.4 nm, a uniform size distribution with prominent monodispersity, and a zeta potential of around −17.5 mV (Fig. 2a, b). CQDs have a homogenous shape and little aggregation. The measured zeta potential reflects the particles' surface electrical charge and is an important predictor of dispersion stability, as greater absolute values induce electrostatic repulsion and improve colloidal stability [14–16]. The observed zeta potential indicates moderate colloidal stability, and no visible aggregation or sedimentation was observed during the experimental period relevant to film fabrication and antimicrobial evaluation; however, the current study did not assess long-term colloidal stability beyond this timeframe. The Raman spectrum of LAB-M/CQDs showed distinct D and G bands, indicating the presence of disordered and graphitic domains within the carbon structure (Fig. 2 d). The D-band at ~ 1350 cm⁻1, with an intensity of around 850 a.u., indicates unstructured sp2 carbon atoms. The G-band, detected at ~ 1600 cm⁻1 with an intensity of roughly 1600 a.u., is linked to the in-plane stretching vibration (E₂g mode) of crystalline graphitic carbon. The ratio of the D-band intensity to the G-band intensity (I₈₅₀/I₁₆₀₀ ≈ 0.53) suggests a moderate degree of structural disturbance in the LAB-M/CQDs, emphasizing the interaction between graphitic areas and defects.

The ID/IG ratio is a critical statistic for determining the crystallinity and relative abundance of carbon domains. The Raman profile obtained here indicates that LAB-M/CQDs have a balanced carbon framework, with enough graphitic areas to maintain structural stability and defects that may improve functional aspects like fluorescence and surface reactivity [7].

Multifunctional film characterization

Surface morphology and optical properties

SEM examination is required to investigate the surface morphology and microstructural characteristics of packaging films. It gives extensive information about the film's homogeneity, texture, and dispersion of integrated materials. SEM pictures of the pure carrageenan (Carg) film have a smooth, uniform, and void-free surface. The concentration of LAB-M/CQDs in the Carrageenan-based film increases gradually, as does surface roughness and particle aggregation. This has an impact on the overall texture and distribution of particles in the matrix, going from a smooth surface (control) to a more granular and aggregated structure at 3% LAB-M/CQD (Fig. 3 B). The films showed a homogeneous, dense microstructure with no apparent phase separation, indicating good compatibility and strong intramolecular interactions with the fillers and the polymer matrix [42]. The relatively uniform surface indicates that the material was properly treated in vast regions, which is significant for features such as barrier performance in food packaging and protective coatings. Increasing CQD concentration altered film morphology, suggesting that higher particle loadings influence both the functional and structural features of the films [26].Fig. 3A Photographic images and (B) SEM image of LAB-M/CQD Carrageenan based Packaging film and control (carg)

The addition of CQDs at various concentrations has a considerable impact on the films' optical and colour properties. These changes are critical for evaluating the films' visual appearance, as optical transparency and colour variations are important aspects of consumer acceptance and functional uses of active packaging materials (Fig. 3 A). The lightness (L*) value, which measures film brightness, dropped as the CQD concentration increased (Table 1). The control film has a high L* value (90.3 ± 0.769), indicating a dazzling and practically colourless look. The inclusion of CQDs resulted in a considerable decline in L* value (50.6 ± 0.441 for the 3% CQD film), indicating a loss in brightness. This drop is due to the darker hue imparted by CQDs (Ezati et al., 2021). The redness (a* value) of the films, which represents the red-green axis, increased significantly after CQD addition. The control film had a mild a* value of 4.13 ± 0.0333, whereas the 3% CQD film had a higher a* value of 7.27 ± 0.0667, indicating a shift towards a reddish hue. Marković et al. (2020) found that adding pigments or nanomaterials increased redness due to their particular chromophore characteristics. Similarly, the yellowness value (b*), which represents the yellow-blue axis, increased significantly. The 3% CQD film significantly increased its b* value to 25.6 ± 0.208, compared to the control film's lowest value of 0.567 ± 0.0333. The observed alterations in the films' optical and colour properties suggest that CQD inclusion has the potential to produce visually differentiated and useful active packaging films. These changes help to differentiate products and increase consumer appeal while keeping functional features. The FTIR spectrum of LAB-M/CQD-integrated films revealed the successful incorporation of functional groups associated with LAB-M/CQDs, confirming their interaction with the film matrix (Fig. 4a). The spectrum revealed peaks at ~ 2926 cm⁻1 and ~ 1238 cm⁻1, representing symmetric and asymmetric stretching of C-H and CH₂ groups, respectively, indicating the existence of methyl and methylene functionalities. The XRD diffractograms of the LAB-M/CQD films were analysed to determine their crystalline shape and structural alterations after introducing LAB-M/CQDs at different concentrations (1%, 2%, and 3%) into the biopolymer matrix (Fig. 4b). The control film (without LAB-M/CQDs) showed a broad diffraction peak around 2θ ~ 20°, demonstrating the amorphous state of the biopolymer [3]. Higher concentrations of LAB-M/CQDs resulted in additional peaks at 2θ values. Films containing 3% LAB-M/CQDs had sharper peaks, indicating better crystalline order than the control film. These peaks most likely correspond to the characteristic planes of the incorporated LAB-M/CQDs, indicating that these quantum dots were successfully integrated into the biopolymer matrix. The observed improvement in crystallinity with increasing LAB-M/CQD concentrations is most likely owing to the nucleation impact of carbon quantum dots, which promotes a more organised polymer matrix. Antimicrobial and antioxidant properties

The antibacterial efficiency of packaging films containing LAB-M-derived Carbon Quantum Dots (LAB-M/CQDs) was tested against three foodborne pathogens using the agar well diffusion method: Escherichia coli (Gram-negative), Bacillus cereus, and Staphylococcus aureus (both Gram-positive) (Fig. 5A). The results showed that films containing LAB-M/CQDs efficiently inhibited bacterial growth, with the inhibition zone diameter increasing as the CQD concentration in the film increased. Among the films examined, the one containing 3% LAB-M/CQDs had the most antibacterial efficacy, providing the biggest inhibition zones for all three infections. The inhibitory zones were measured as 4.14 ± 0.03 cm for E. coli, 2.78 ± 0.03 cm for B. cereus, and 2.61 ± 0.015 cm for S. aureus. Higher CQD concentrations improve antibacterial effectiveness due to a greater surface area, ROS production, and stronger contact with bacterial cell walls. The inhibitory pattern reveals that E. coli was more vulnerable to the LAB-M/CQD films than Gram-positive bacteria. This could be owing to changes in bacterial cell wall architecture; Gram-negative bacteria have a thinner peptidoglycan layer, rendering them more vulnerable to harm from nanoscale materials like CQDs. LAB-M/CQDs' antibacterial effect is linked to various mechanisms, including their tiny size, surface functions, and generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The CQDs' nanoscale size allows them to break through and destroy the bacterial cell wall. Surface functional groups including hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl groups improve ionic interactions with the membranes of bacteria, resulting in mechanical damage and leaking of internal contents [12, 49].

Furthermore, LAB-M/CQDs are known to produce ROS, including hydroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen, and superoxide anions, which cause oxidative stress in bacterial cells. This oxidative stress impairs cellular homeostasis, causing lipid peroxidation, protein denaturation, and nucleic acid breakage, which subsequently leads to the death of cells [43, 50]. Furthermore, LAB-derived metabolites found in CQDs may operate synergistically, increasing ROS generation and antibacterial effectiveness. The enhanced effectiveness of films containing 3% LAB-M/CQDs may be due to the greater availability of active sites for ROS production and greater contact with bacterial cell walls. This finding is consistent with publications that show metal-doped quantum dots exhibit improved antibacterial action due to faster electron transfer pathways, which magnify ROS production [40, 56].

The antioxidant capabilities of films containing carbon quantum dots (CQDs) generated from lactic acid bacteria (LAB) metabolites were studied utilising the DPPH assay. This assay relies on the scavenging of DPPH free radicals, characterized by their distinct purple colour, which fades to pale yellow or becomes colourless when reduced by antioxidants. A statistically significant, concentration-dependent increase in antioxidant activity was observed with higher LAB-M/CQD content in the films (p < 0.05). The maximum activity was achieved in films containing 3 wt% LAB-M/CQDs, exhibiting a DPPH radical scavenging efficiency of 97.8% (Fig. 5B). The improved antioxidant activity of LAB-M/CQDs is primarily attributed to their surface chemistry. The CQD surface features hydroxyl (-OH), carboxyl (-COOH), and amine (-NH2) groups, which neutralize free radicals effectively through hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) mechanisms. These functional groups provide hydrogen atoms to free radicals like DPPH•, thus reducing their activity and stabilising the system. CQDs' nanometric size allows for uniform dispersion within the polymeric film matrix and effective interaction with free radicals [34]. Similar findings have been documented for hydrophilic carbon dot-based systems, where surface functionalisation has a direct impact on antioxidant effectiveness [17]. The exceptional antioxidant activity of LAB-M/CQD-incorporated films highlights their potential effectiveness in active packaging applications, particularly for foods that are susceptible to radical deterioration.

Barrier properties of LAB-M/CQD film

Water vapour transmission rate (WVTR) is an important factor for assessing the moisture shield property of food packaging materials. Lower WVTR values indicate lower moisture intrusion, crucial for prolonging food's shelf life by lowering water activity-induced spoiling. Melanie et al., [35] found that WVTR values decreased as the concentration of arrowroot starch increased in carrageenan-based biofilms, with the greatest concentration of starch of 80% having the strongest moisture barrier qualities. Reduced WVTR in polybutylene succinate (PBS) film was observed by Winotapun et al. [52] on the addition of butyrate adipate terephthalate (PBAT) to films. In this research, the average WVTR significantly reduced with the inclusion of LAB-M/CQD, with the control film's average WVTR being around 506.84 g/m2·h and 446.57 g/m2·h, for 3% LAB-M/CQD inclusion samples. This shows that the film's capacity to block water vapour motion across the film is enhanced by increasing the concentration of LAB-M/CQDs, with the 3% sample exhibiting the highest performance (Fig. 7A) (p < 0.05). This indicates that adding LAB-M/CQDs considerably improves the packing film's water vapour barrier qualities, indicating the film's potential to be used in food packaging applications. Shelf life improvement test on Mango

Over a 21-day storage period, the effect of LAB-M/CQD-based films on mango ripening was evaluated using visual inspection and biochemical assays such as pH, titratable acidity, and ethylene generation. The films, made with different concentrations of LAB-M/CQDs (1%, 2%, and 3%), were compared to a control (non-packaged mangoes) and a carrageenan-only (Carg) film. Visual studies of mango ripening, together with changes in pH and titratable acidity (Fig. 6 c & d), demonstrated that LAB-M/CQD films effectively delayed ripening as compared to unpackaged and control mangoes (Carg film). On the seventh day, non-packaged mangoes showed significant yellowing, indicating advanced ripening, but mangoes packaged with LAB-M/CQD films preserved their original appearance. Mangoes wrapped in 1% and 2% LAB-M/CQD films began to yellow on the 14th day; however, those wrapped in 3% LAB-M/CQD films showed slight changes to yellow on the 21 st day (Fig. 9). Non-packaged mangoes showed the greatest decline in pH and titratable acidity, with pH dropping from 4.5 to 4.0 and acidity falling from 4.5% to 3.5% over 21 days. Mangoes wrapped with 3% LAB-M/CQD films, on the other hand, displayed the highest level of stability, with pH rising from 4.0 to 5.5 and acidity remaining constant. These findings indicate that the LAB-M/CQD films, especially at higher concentrations, successfully inhibited the metabolic processes related to ripening, resulting in a longer shelf life [14–16].Fig. 9Appearance change of mango packaged in LAB-M/CQD carrageenan-based films during storage at room temperature on the 1 st, 7th, 14th, and 21 st day

The ethylene scavenging properties of LAB-M/CQD films were essential in lowering ethylene production, a crucial hormone involved in fruit ripening. Similar effects have been reported in research utilising nanocomposite films to regulate ethylene levels in perishable produce [11].

Conclusion

Carbon Quantum Dots were synthesized hydrothermally using microbial metabolites of Lactic acid bacteria. This study reinforces the multifunctionality of CQDs as potent nanomaterials capable of good Antioxidant and antimicrobial Properties which can enhance food safety and the continuous supply of produce in the supply chain. The findings of this study show that LAB-M/CQD films, especially at higher concentrations, are a promising alternative for increasing mango shelf life by delaying ripening while retaining fruit quality. Their ability to stabilise pH, titrate acidity, and reduce ethylene production demonstrates their promise as active packaging materials for post-harvest management. Future research on LAB metabolite-derived carbon quantum dot-based packaging films should look beyond short-term antibacterial effectiveness and consider variables important for practical food packaging applications. These include a rigorous assessment of barrier and optical qualities, such as moisture sensitivity and UV–visible light shielding, under actual storage environments. Furthermore, more research is needed on the long-term stability of CQDs in polymer matrices, notably in terms of aggregation, migration, and antimicrobial functionality retention. From an application standpoint, problems such as scalable and reproducible synthesis of metabolite-derived CQDs, uniformity of physicochemical attributes, and compliance with food-contact regulatory requirements must be overcome before industrial implementation can begin.

Though lactic acid bacteria metabolites are generally deemed safe, their conversion into carbon quantum dots (CQDs) demands cytotoxicity testing to maintain safety and trust among consumers. These investigations are critical in assessing potential dangers, such as the leaching of CQDs or other components during storage, especially in food packaging. Ensuring the safety of these compounds by cytotoxicity testing will foster trust between customers and regulatory organizations.
