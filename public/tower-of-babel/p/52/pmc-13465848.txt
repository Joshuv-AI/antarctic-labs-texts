Barriers and facilitators to dissertation publication among emergency medicine postgraduates in Tanzania: a 12-year analysis
Authors: Humphrey G. Medarakini, Said S. Kilindimo, Hendry R. Sawe, Chris A. Rees, Alphonce N. Simbila, Evelyne G. Mapunda, Emmanuel W. Mmbando, Brittany L. Murray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostgraduate dissertations offer valuable sources of scientific output given their rigorous development and execution, yet unpublished results may be common. This gap is particularly concerning in sub-Saharan Africa. We sought to describe the frequency of publication, and perceptions of barriers and facilitators to publication of postgraduate dissertations results in peer-reviewed journals.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey between 1st December, 2025 and 28th February 2026 among graduates of the Emergency Medicine (EM) residency program at Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences in Dar Es Salaam, Tanzania. All eligible EM graduates from 2013 to 2024 were invited to participate. Data were collected using a structured web-based questionnaire administered through Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) and distributed via email and a WhatsApp group that is professionally managed by the Emergency Medicine Association of Tanzania and includes all program graduates. Weekly reminders were sent to non-respondents to enhance response rates. The survey collected information on demographics, dissertation publication status, perceived barriers and facilitators to publication, and recommendations for improving dissertation publication. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data.ResultsOut of 97 eligible graduates, 69 (71.1%) completed the survey. Respondents reported that most studies used a cohort (n = 31, 45%) or cross-sectional design (n = 25, 36%). 24 (34.8%) of the 69 respondents had published their dissertation in a peer-reviewed journal. Among 24 published dissertations, 17 (70.8%) had been presented in ≥ 1 scientific conference. Among 32 respondents who reported general barriers to publication, the most common barriers were limited time due to clinical duties (n = 19, 59.4%), high publication costs (n = 15, 46.9%), lack of institutional support (n = 15, 46.9%), insufficient research skills training (n = 13, 40.6%), lack of motivation (n = 13, 40.6%) and lack of mentorship (n = 11, 34.4%). Among the 69 respondents, research skills training (n = 61, 88.4%), supportive supervision and mentorship (n = 56, 81.2%), manuscript writing workshops (n = 48, 69.9%), and financial support for publication cost (n = 41, 54.4%) were reported as facilitators for successful publication.ConclusionIn the largest EM residency program in East Africa, approximately one in three postgraduate dissertations are not published in peer-reviewed journals. Addressing barriers to publication may improve the translation of postgraduate research into published evidence and bolster the regional data available in published medical literature.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09642-5.

Background

Globally, the rate at which postgraduate medical dissertations are eventually published in peer-reviewed journals varies considerably [1–3], with rates as high as 45–66% in high-income countries (HICs) [4, 5] and < 30% in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [6–9]. Failure to publish limits the dissemination of context-specific findings, impedes evidence-based policy and practice, and risks wasting academic investment [10].

Multiple studies have identified a range of barriers that may hinder the successful publication of postgraduate research, including limited academic writing skills, inadequate research training, inadequate departmental research culture, competing clinical responsibilities, limited time for scholarly work, financial constraints, and insufficient mentorship [1, 11–16]. These barriers may be particularly pronounced in resource-constrained settings [15, 17–20]. However, there are also factors shown to facilitate the successful publication of postgraduate research, such as strong departmental research culture, structured research curricula, mentorship programs, and collaboration with experienced researchers [21–26]. Interventions such as structured writing workshops, research mentorship programs, and institutional incentives for scholarly work have been recommended as strategies to support research dissemination among postgraduate trainees [23, 24]. However, much of the prior work on barriers and facilitators to the publication of results from dissertations has been done in HICs, with fewer reports from LMICs where disease burden is higher and there is a relative paucity of context-specific evidence to inform clinical care.

Emergency medicine (EM) residency training has increased in Sub-Saharan Africa (sSA) over the last decade. Postgraduate research dissertations form a core component of most programs’ curricula in sSA where medical residency programs are typically MMed degrees [27]. Despite the substantial effort and rigorous review involved in developing postgraduate dissertations, the literature remains limited, particularly in sSA, on the frequency of publication of the results of dissertations, as well as perceived barriers that prevent dissertations from ultimately being published in peer-reviewed journals [7]. Therefore, this study aims to assess the publication rate of EM postgraduate dissertations at Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences (MUHAS), the first and largest EM residency program in East Africa, and to identify perceived barriers and facilitators for successful dissertation publication. These findings may help inform interventions to improve scientific dissemination and support clinical and policy decision-making in sSA related to EM postgraduate education.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional survey study between 1 st December 2025 and 28th February 2026 to evaluate the publication status of EM postgraduate dissertations and perceived barriers and facilitators to publication of such dissertations among graduates of an EM residency program in Tanzania.

Study setting and population

The study was conducted at MUHAS, a public health sciences university located in Dar Es Salaam, Tanzania. MUHAS provides undergraduate and postgraduate training in multiple health disciplines and is affiliated with several teaching hospitals in Tanzania.

The EM residency program at MUHAS is the first and largest in East Africa, and has trained the majority of EM physicians in Tanzania. It was launched in 2010 and is a three-year residency. As part of the program requirements, residents must complete and submit the results of their dissertation prior to graduation. Successful defense of their postgraduate dissertation is a requirement for graduation. Both local and international experts serve on the dissertation review panel.

The study population included all EM physicians who graduated from the MUHAS-EM residency program between December 2013 (the first class of graduates) and December 2024. Graduates from 2025 were not included in the main survey because we aimed to assess publication rates among graduates with some time following graduation to not over-inflate rates of non-publication. All eligible graduates (n = 97) from 2013 to 2024 were invited to participate. Graduates who provided informed consent were included. Individuals who declined participation or had incomplete or irretrievable data were excluded.

Data collection

A novel structured questionnaire (supplementary file 1) was developed. The content was informed by the study objectives and findings from previous literature on postgraduate research publication, barriers to publication, and factors leading to successful publication. The draft questionnaire was reviewed by EM experts to assess its content relevance, clarity, and completeness. Afterwards, it was pilot tested with EM residents from the 2025 cohort who were not part of the main study to ensure clarity and relevance. The questionnaire included seven sections, including multiple-choice, Likert-scale items, and several optional free text response fields that allowed participants to provide additional responses. The survey assessed demographics, dissertation details, publication outcomes, and perceived barriers and facilitators to publication. The final version gathered information on participants' demographics, dissertation characteristics, publication status, perceived barriers and facilitators for publication, and suggestions for improving dissertation publication rates. Participants were then asked to identify perceived barriers and facilitators to dissertation publication from predefined response options, with the option to select multiple responses and provide free text additions. In the context of this study, publication was defined as a dissertation that resulted in a peer-reviewed journal article published in a scientific journal, regardless of the graduate’s authorship position. For consistency throughout this study, we used the term “published dissertations” to refer to dissertations that resulted in a peer reviewed journal publication and “unpublished dissertations” to refer to dissertations that had not resulted in a peer reviewed journal publication at the time of survey. Participants were then asked to identify perceived barriers and facilitators to dissertation publication from predefined response options, with the option to select multiple responses.

Eligible participants were identified using departmental records. Contact information, including email addresses and phone numbers, was obtained from the departmental databases and verified for accuracy. When contact details were missing or outdated, additional information was obtained from colleagues or through a professionally managed WhatsApp group owned by the Emergency Medicine Association of Tanzania that includes all program graduates.

Participants received a link to a web-based survey administered through REDCap. Participation was voluntary and electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before accessing the questionnaire. The survey invitation was distributed through personal email and via the professional WhatsApp group. The WhatsApp group therefore served two purposes: facilitating verification of graduate contact information and providing a platform for survey distribution. The survey remained open for three months. Reminder messages and emails were sent weekly to non–respondents to augment response rates. Responses were accepted up to two weeks after the initial survey period closed. All study data were stored securely using password-protected REDCap databases and encrypted storage systems.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 27. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize variables. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were summarized using means with standard deviations and medians with interquartile range as appropriate.

Results

Of 97 eligible graduates, 69 (71.1%) completed the survey (Fig. 1). Among the 69 participants, 42 (60.9%) were male, and 27 (39.1%) were female (Table 1). The largest proportion of respondents were those who graduated between 2022 and 2024, (n = 28, 40.6%). Most respondents completed their undergraduate medical training at public universities (n = 37, 53.6%). Prior to enrollment in the EM residency program, 67 participants (97.1%) were employed. The median duration of clinical work prior to residency was 3 years (IQR 2–4). Postgraduate research training and institutional support during residency

During residency, most respondents reported receiving training in research methodology (n = 61, 88.4%) and biostatistics (n = 52, 75.5%), while fewer reported training in scientific writing (n = 31, 44.9%), journal selection and submission (n = 14, 20.3%), or responding to reviewers (n = 10, 14.5%). Regarding institutional support, 44 (63.4%) reported access to mentorship program, 37 (53.6%) to a medical library, and 32 (46.4%) to statistical software, whereas only 13 (18.8%) reported receiving institutional research funding, and 11 (15.9%) reported access to scientific writing workshops (Supplemental Table 1).

Characteristics of postgraduate dissertations

Among the respondents, 24 (34.8%) had published their postgraduate dissertations in a peer-reviewed journal and 45 (65.2%) had not (Fig. 1). Across the 69 dissertations analyzed, cohort studies were the most common study design among both published (n = 10, 41.7%) and unpublished (n = 21, 46.7%), followed by cross-sectional studies (n = 9, 37.5% versus n = 16, 35.6%). Conference presentations were more frequent in published dissertations than in unpublished dissertations. Among published dissertations, 17 (70.8%) had been presented at least in one scientific conference compared with 14 (31.1%) unpublished dissertations. Overall, 24 of 69 dissertations (34.8%) were published in peer-reviewed journals. The median time from graduation to publication was 1.5 years (IQR 1–2). Most published dissertations listed the graduate as the first author (n = 21, 87.5%) and two (8.3%) had co-first authors with the graduate occupying one of those authorship positions. Seven publications (29.2%) listed the graduates as corresponding authors. All published dissertations appeared in international journals and were open access. Most journals were indexed (n = 22, 91.7%) in major databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, with impact factors ranging from 2.1 to 4.0 (Supplemental Table 2). Among the 45 unpublished dissertations, only seven (15.6%) had been submitted to a journal at least once (Supplemental Table 3).

Personal barriers were reported by 27 (39.1%) respondents (Table 3). The most frequently reported personal barriers was a perceived lack of motivation by the graduate (n = 18, 66.7%), time constraints (n = 13, 48.1%), and financial constraints (n = 10, 37.0%). Manuscript writing-related barriers were also common, including difficulty in understanding the peer-review process (n = 32, 46.4%), formatting manuscripts according to journal guidelines (n = 31, 44.9%), manuscript preparation (n = 27, 39.1%), and limited funding to support publication or data analysis (n = 24, 34.8%).

Facilitators and strategies that will support successful postgraduate dissertations publications

Respondents’ recommendation to enhance successful postgraduate dissertation publications

All respondents (n = 69), regardless of publication status, identified several strategies to improve publication rates for postgraduate dissertations (Table 5). The most frequently suggested interventions were strengthened research skills training (n = 61, 88.4%), strengthened mentorship programs (n = 56, 81.2%), improved manuscript writing workshops (n = 48, 69.6%), training on journal selection (n = 46, 66.7%), financial support for publication fees (n = 41, 54.4%), training on peer review process (n = 40, 58.0%), and internal support on manuscript review before journal submission (n = 39, 56.5%). Most participants (n = 62, 89.9%) indicated interest in participating in future mentorship or training programs related to research publication. Likewise, they recommended several strategies to improve dissertation publication (Supplemental Table 4).

Discussion

Postgraduate dissertations represent a valuable source of context-specific evidence; however, their impact is limited when findings remain unpublished. The rate of publication in EM programs across HICs has a wide range, [28] and in LMICs is largely unreported. Despite the rapid expansion of the specialty in the region, to our knowledge, this is the first study examining dissertation publication outcomes among EM residency graduates in sSA. We found that only one-third of the dissertations were published in peer-reviewed journals. Key barriers to publication included limited time due to clinical duties, high publication costs, lack of institutional support, inadequate research skills training, lack of personal motivation and limited mentorship. Conversely, research training, supportive supervision and mentorship, manuscript writing workshops and financial support for publication cost were key facilitators towards publication.

Despite the publication rate in the current study being lower than that observed in some HICs [4, 5], this rate is higher than reported rates in other specialties in LMICs which have shown ranges from 14 to 20% [7–9]. However, the comparison with previous studies should be interpreted cautiously. Many prior studies use much broader definitions of publication outcome and include conference abstracts, presentations to difference audiences, and letters to editors alongside full-text manuscripts. In addition, other studies often examined heterogenous postgraduate populations across multiple specialties rather than a single residency program, programs with varying research requirements, and broader timeframes of the cohort involved. Our study focused specifically on dissertations produced within an EM residency program, where research activities are embedded within a structured postgraduate curriculum, with a consistent exposure to research methodology, supervision, and academic expectations which may have contributed to relatively different publication rates observed.

Importantly, all published dissertations in our study appeared in international, open-access journals, and the majority were indexed in major databases. These metrics allow published results to achieve meaningful visibility and contribute to the global EM literature. This is relevant in the sSA context, where locally generated EM evidence is underrepresented in global literature [29]. The relatively short median time from graduation to publication in our study (1.5 years) also suggests that graduates who pursued publication were able to complete the process within a reasonable timeframe, consistent with previous studies examining the trajectory from dissertation completion to publication [2, 3].

Our study identified institutional, personal, and manuscript-related barriers that may hinder the successful publication of postgraduate dissertations. Out of the 32 respondents who reported general barriers to publication, the majority (n = 19, 59.4%), cited clinical duties as the main obstacle. This is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that clinical workload during and after residency is a major constraint on scholarly productivity [15, 16]. In many training environments, particularly in LMICs, residents and early career physicians often face substantial clinical responsibilities that compete with the time required for manuscript preparation and journal submission [7]. Addressing this barrier may require the introduction of protected academic time, structured research blocks, or post-residency support mechanisms by mentors to facilitate manuscript development and submission.

Another major challenge in publishing identified in our study is article processing charges [25, 26], which are often beyond reach for most scholars based in LMICs. Among 32 respondents who identified barriers to publication, nearly half (n = 15, 46.9%) reported that the high cost of publication and limited institutional support hindered their ability to publish their work. Publication fees associated with open-access journals, costs related to statistical support, and limited availability of research funding can discourage early-career researchers from submitting manuscripts for publication. Lack of training in research skills and lack of mentorship as obstacles to publication were each reported by more one-third of participants who identified barriers to publication. This is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that mentorship plays a critical role in supporting research productivity and academic development [21, 22]. Mentors provide guidance on refining research questions, navigating the peer review process, formatting manuscripts according to journal guidelines, preparing manuscripts for submission, and selecting appropriate journals, all of which were among the frequent manuscript-related challenges reported in the current study. Among respondents who reported personal barriers to publication (n = 27), in the current study, lack of motivation to pursue publication, competing personal responsibilities, and career priorities can hinder the completion of manuscripts following dissertation completion consistent with previous studies [3]. This may also be an area where mentorship could assist graduates in motivation towards publication. Protected time either during or after residency, and institutional support may also help graduates overcome these barriers and successfully disseminate their research findings.

Despite the barriers described above, our study also identified facilitators that supported successful dissertation publication. Among graduates who successfully published their dissertations (n = 24), supportive mentorship and supervision were the most frequently cited, consistent with previous studies [19, 22]. Graduates who successfully published their work frequently reported receiving active encouragement and guidance from supervisors or experienced collaborators, which significantly enhanced their research productivity. Collaboration with experienced co-authors also emerged as an important facilitator. Collaborative research networks may provide access to methodological expertise, statistical support, and experience navigating the journal submission process. Such a collaborative environment may improve scholarly productivity and improve the quality of academic outputs within training programs [21]. Research training during residency was another important facilitator to publication of dissertation results. Out of the 69 respondents, most participants reported receiving training in research methodology and biostatistics; however, fewer reported receiving formal instruction in scientific writing, journal selection, or responding to peer review. Although all participants graduated from the same residency program, there was variability in the survey responses. This could have been due changes in research support resources over the 12 years study period (i.e., curriculum delivery, research training and structure), as well as differences in participant recall and interpretation of training experiences. Expanding training in these areas may therefore represent an important opportunity to strengthen the conversion of postgraduate research into publishable manuscripts. Previous studies have demonstrated that structured research curricula and longitudinal research training programs can significantly improve scholarly productivity among residents [23, 24].

Limited human resources often limit the availability of programmatic research support in LMIC residency programs. Virtual engagement with mentors abroad and engagement in virtual research training can have an important role. Emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) tools, including large language models may provide new opportunities to aid in the transition of postgraduate dissertations into peer reviewed publications. Manuscript editing and grammar formatting may be particularly helpful for postgraduates especially those who do not speak English as their first language. However, the use of AI in academic publishing should be accompanied by ethical considerations and adherence to journal policies regarding transparency and authorship.

It is important to note that our study focused on the publication of dissertations in peer reviewed journals. We acknowledge the distinction between the educational purpose of postgraduate dissertations and the dissemination role of peer reviewed publications. All of our program graduates successfully completed their dissertations. While completion of a dissertation is intended to develop research skills and scholarly competencies, publication facilitates broader dissemination of findings and contributes to the evidence base informing clinical practice and policy. Though other methods of dissemination of dissertation research results, such as conference presentations, educational activities, quality improvement initiatives, or policy discussions can be important and lead to practice change and inform policies, the publication of a manuscript in a peer reviewed journal can ensure broader visibility, scientific scrutiny through peer review, and accessibility of locally generated evidence. This accessibility and dissemination of research results is of utmost importance in LMICs.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of self-reported data on perceived barriers and facilitators introduces the potential for recall bias, particularly among graduates who completed residency several years before the survey. Second, although the response rate was relatively high, non-response bias may still be present. Third, the study was conducted within a single EM residency program in Tanzania, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other training programs or medical specialties. However, the EM program represents the largest EM training program in the region. In addition, this was a cross-sectional study and focused primarily on descriptive analysis hence did not assess causal relationships between identified barriers or facilitators and publication outcomes. Finally, this study focused on peer reviewed journal publication as the primary measure of research dissemination and did not assess other dissemination pathways such as policy reports, guidelines development, educational integration, or implementation into clinical practice.

Conclusion

The majority of postgraduate dissertations from an EM program in Tanzania were not published in peer reviewed journals which limits the dissemination of their results. Identified barriers to publishing dissertations include competing clinical demands, financial constraints, limited mentorship, and gaps in research training and manuscript preparation. Facilitators to publication included strong mentorship, research training, and institutional support. Addressing barriers and strengthening facilitators to dissertation publication is an opportunity to improve the number of postgraduate dissertations that become peer-reviewed publications and add to context-specific evidence that can improve emergency care in LMIC settings.
