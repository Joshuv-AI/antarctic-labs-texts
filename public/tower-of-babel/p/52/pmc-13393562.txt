Associations of NLR, MLR, and (neutrophil + monocyte)/lymphocyte ratio with 30-day mortality in diabetic ICU patients with heart failure: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jiazheng Chen, Yuxin Liang, Jiabei Wu, Hongli Li, Xiaoyang Wang, Feitong Qin, Zhijun Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure (HF) is a common complication in diabetes mellitus (DM), affecting approximately 40% of HF patients. While inflammation plays a crucial role in both conditions, the prognostic value of inflammatory markers in patients with both DM and HF remains unestablished.ObjectiveTo examine the associations of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and (neutrophil + monocyte)/lymphocyte ratio (NMLR) with 30-day mortality in ICU patients with both DM and HF.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 1318 patients with both HF and DM from the MIMIC-IV database. NLR, MLR, and NMLR were calculated from complete blood counts within 24 h of ICU admission. Multivariate Cox regression, restricted cubic spline analysis, and ROC curves assessed predictive performance.ResultsAmong 1318 patients, 239 died within 30 days. The deceased group had significantly higher median values of NLR (10.9 vs. 6.5), MLR (0.9 vs. 0.4), and NMLR (12.1 vs. 7.0) compared to survivors (all P < 0.001). After adjustment, each one-unit increase in MLR was associated with 11% increased mortality risk (HR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.04–1.19, P = 0.002), while NMLR and NLR showed 1% increases, respectively (NMLR: HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1–1.01; NLR: HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1–1.01; both P < 0.001). MLR demonstrated the highest discriminative capacity (AUC = 0.672), followed by NMLR (AUC = 0.658) and NLR (AUC = 0.650).ConclusionNLR, MLR, and NMLR are independent predictors of 30-day mortality in ICU patients with DM and HF, with MLR showing superior discriminative capacity.Keywords: Heart failure, Diabetes mellitus, Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, Monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio, Mortality

Introduction

DM is a chronic disease marked by elevated blood glucose levels and metabolic disturbances [1]. According to projections from the International Diabetes Federation, the prevalence of diabetes is expected to increase substantially, reaching 643 million cases by 2030 and 783 million cases by 2045 [2]. Epidemiological studies have identified a significant association between Heart failure (HF) and Diabetes mellitus (DM) [3], [4], [5]. Patients with DM face a higher risk of developing HF, along with increased mortality [6], Notably, among patients with HF, 40% have DM [7]. Generally, type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a risk factor for cardiovascular events such as HF [8], and HF is the most common cardiovascular complication in DM [9]. DM and HF share common pathophysiological mechanisms, illustrating the importance of considering their synergistic effect in clinical diagnosis and treatment [8], [9]. Although growing research has focused on the influence of inflammatory responses on cardiovascular health and DM, the complex underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated, and a more effective prognostic model is still needed for predicting outcomes in patients with T2D who also have HF.

Research has emphasized that DM, as comorbidity, influences HF progression through mechanisms closely related to oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [10], [11]. The chronic inflammatory state induced by T2D may result in cardiac remodeling, ultimately culminating in HF [12]. In addition, pro-inflammatory cytokines may cause endothelial dysfunction and reduced vascular compliance, leading to concentric left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy and decreased LV compliance [13]. Emerging inflammatory markers such as the Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR), the Monocyte-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (MLR), and the (Neutrophil + Monocyte)/Lymphocyte Ratio (NMLR) integrate counts of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes into a single variable reflecting an inflammatory state. Recent studies indicate that NMLR plays an important role in prognostic assessments of sepsis, acute myocardial infarction, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [14], [15], [16], [17]; NLR is associated with mortality risk in various cancers, HF, atrial fibrillation, and acute respiratory failure [18], [19], [20], [21]; MLR is considered relevant to the mortality risk of coronary artery disease, HF, myocardial infarction, and chronic kidney disease [22], [23], [24], [25]. Additionally, some studies have shown that elevated NLR is associated with poor glycemic control in patients with T2D [26], and both NLR and MLR are associated with insulin resistance in T2D [27].

Despite the close relationship between DM and HF, no studies have explored the prognostic value of NLR, MLR, and NMLR specifically in critically ill patients with both DM and HF. Therefore, this study aims to examine the association between NLR, MLR, and NMLR levels and 30-day mortality in a large US cohort of critically ill adults with coexisting HF and DM.

Methods

Database

The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV (version 3.1) database was used in this study. It is a large-scale, free, open-access critical care database [28], jointly developed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC). This comprehensive database contains detailed clinical information on 524,000 hospital admissions of more than 257,000 unique patients from 2008 to 2019 at BIDMC [29]. MIMIC-IV includes extensive clinical data collected during routine hospital care, such as vital signs, laboratory test results, medication information, clinical notes, imaging reports, and demographic data. All protected health information was strictly de-identified by the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) standards. After completing the requisite training on human research, investigators were granted access to the database, with one author obtaining the relevant certification (Name ID: 13642570, Certificate No.: 64860643). This study strictly followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study population

The patient selection process is shown in Fig. 1. In this retrospective study, an initial total of 94,458 patients were identified from the MIMIC-IV database. The following exclusion criteria were then applied sequentially: (1) exclusion of patients with repeated hospital admissions; (2) exclusion of patients with multiple ICU admissions, retaining only the first ICU admission record for each patient to avoid data duplication; and (3) selection of patients with both T2D mellitus (T2D) and HF, followed by exclusion of patients missing laboratory data (including monocyte, lymphocyte, or neutrophil counts). After applying all inclusion and exclusion criteria, 1318 patients remained for the final analysis.

Flowchart of the study cohort.

Extraction of exposure factors and relevant covariates

The main exposure variables were NMLR, NLR, and MLR calculated from the first complete blood count obtained within 24 h after ICU admission. The neutrophil, lymphocyte, and monocyte counts used to calculate these ratios were measured in K/μL (thousands per microliter).

Covariates were selected based on previously published literature, clinical relevance, and our univariate analyses. Demographic characteristics consisted of age, sex, and race. Vital signs included systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, mean blood pressure, respiratory rate, heart rate, temperature, and oxygen saturation (SpO2). Laboratory parameters comprised white blood cell count, creatinine, sodium, potassium, and prothrombin time. Information on acute kidney injury within the first two days of admission was also collected. Assessed comorbidities included kidney disease, malignancy, severe liver disease, and metastatic solid tumors. Heart failure phenotype was classified based on left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) according to ESC guidelines: HFpEF (LVEF ≥50%), HFmrEF (40–49%), and HFrEF (<40%). LVEF data were available for 403 of 1318 participants (30.58%). Disease severity was evaluated using the Charlson Comorbidity Index, the Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), and the Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS). All continuous variables were measured within the first 24 h of ICU admission.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality, defined as death from any cause within 30 days following ICU admission. Mortality information was obtained from hospital records and the Social Security Death Index. For patients discharged within 30 days, follow-up continues until death or the end of the 30-day observation period.

Data analysis

All missing variables related to exposure and outcome were excluded during the sample screening process, with missing values for all covariates below 5%. We employed multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) to handle missing data. The overall proportion of missing data was low for most covariates, with the exception of LVEF (69.4%), which was handled through the imputation model rather than by casewise deletion. For each cohort, we constructed separate MICE models to account for potential between-cohort differences in data distributions: predictive mean matching was used for continuous variables and logistic regression for binary variables, with all analytical variables incorporated to preserve relational consistency across the imputation models. Five imputed datasets were generated per cohort, and results from multivariable regression analyses were pooled using Rubin's rules, ensuring that the final estimates adequately reflect variation arising from both the imputation process itself and between-cohort differences. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (http://www.R-project.org, R Foundation) and Free Statistics V2.1. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are shown as frequency or percentage. Between-group comparisons were carried out using the chi-square test for categorical variables, the student's t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables.

The analysis proceeded in three consecutive steps. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout.

First, univariate and multivariable Cox regression models were constructed using a stepwise adjustment strategy. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for demographic variables. Model 3 additionally adjusted for vital signs and blood biochemical indicators. Model 4 further added comorbidities. The stability of the effect estimates was assessed across these models.

Second, a generalized additive model with restricted cubic splines (RCS) was used to identify threshold effects and visualize possible nonlinear relationships between the ICU admission inflammatory markers (NMLR, NLR, and MLR) and 30-day mortality. The predictive performance of NMLR, NLR, and MLR for 30-day mortality in ICU patients with both DM and HF was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and optimal cutoff values were determined. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and cumulative hazard curves were plotted to compare 30-day survival among patients with different levels of these markers, and differences were evaluated using the log-rank test.

Third, in a sensitivity analysis, continuous variables were converted into categorical variables for multivariate Cox regression analyses, and the P for trend was calculated to test the main results and assess potential nonlinear relationships. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Fourth, Subgroup analyses stratified by heart failure phenotypes (HFpEF/HFmrEF/HFrEF), age (<65 vs ≥65 years), renal disease, malignant cancer, severe liver disease, metastatic solid tumor, and OASIS score assessed effect modification of inflammatory indices on 30-day mortality. Separate multivariable Cox models were fitted per subgroup with inflammatory indices as continuous predictors, and multiplicative interaction terms were tested using Wald tests.

Fifth, Incremental predictive value was evaluated using logistic regression by comparing a baseline model (SAPS II alone) with models incorporating each inflammatory marker (NLR, MLR, NMLR) separately. Discriminative improvement was assessed via ΔAUC using the DeLong test, while reclassification performance was evaluated using categorical and continuous NRI and IDI.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

As shown in Table 1, the study included a total of 1318 patients, of whom 824 were male (62.5%). The mean (± standard deviation) age was 71.7 ± 11.0 years. Of the participants, 774 (58.7%) were White. The outcome included 1079 survivors and 239 deaths. There were significant differences in inflammatory markers between the two groups. Compared with survivors, the median values of NLR (10.9 vs. 6.5), MLR (0.9 vs. 0.4), and NMLR (12.1 vs. 7.0) were all significantly higher in the deceased group (all P < 0.001). When stratified by tertiles, the deceased group had a significantly higher proportion in the highest tertile of these inflammatory markers (NLR: 51%, MLR: 53.1%, NMLR: 51.9%; all P < 0.001). The most common comorbidity was acute kidney injury (77.9%), followed by renal disease (48.8%) and myocardial infarction (45.4%). Moreover, some other variables also showed statistically significant differences between the two groups, including SBP, DBP, MBP, respiratory rate, heart rate, temperature, glucose, SpO₂, WBC, creatinine, sodium, potassium, PT, AKI within 2 days, renal disease, malignant cancer, severe liver disease, metastatic solid tumor, Charlson Comorbidity Index, APACHE III, SAPS II, and OASIS.

Associations of NLR, MLR, and NMLR with all-cause mortality in patients with DM and HF

Multivariate Cox regression analysis (Table 2) assessed the independent associations of NMLR, MLR, and NLR with 30-day mortality across four progressively adjusted models. In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), all three markers analyzed as continuous variables were significantly associated with mortality (NMLR: HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1–1.01; MLR: HR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.04–1.19; NLR: HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1–1.01; all P for trend <0.001). When analyzed by tertiles, compared with the lowest tertile (T1), the highest tertile (T3) of each marker was associated with a significantly increased risk of mortality (NMLR: HR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.38–2.8, P < 0.001; MLR: HR = 2.69, 95% CI: 1.82–3.99, P < 0.001; NLR: HR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.26–2.54, P = 0.001). The associations were consistent across less adjusted models (Models 1–3). These findings indicate that higher levels of NMLR, MLR, and NLR are independently associated with increased 30-day mortality.

Restricted cubic spline analysis

After adjusting for all confounding factors, the Fig. 2 RCS analysis indicates a significant association between inflammatory markers and mortality risk. All three markers showed a consistent positive correlation with mortality risk, although the patterns of their associations differed. (Fig. 2A) NMLR (overall P < 0.001, nonlinear P = 0.366) and (Fig. 2B) NLR (overall P = 0.004, nonlinearity P = 0.459) both demonstrated a significant linear correlation with mortality risk, with reference points at 7.571 and 6.981, respectively. Above these thresholds, the risk ratio rose steadily as NMLR and NLR values increased. In contrast, (Fig. 2C) MLR showed a significant nonlinear correlation with mortality risk (overall P < 0.001, nonlinearity P = 0.009), with a reference point of 0.5137. At lower MLR values, the risk increased more rapidly, while at higher levels, a plateau effect was observed, indicating the presence of a threshold effect in the relationship between MLR and mortality risk.

Restricted cubic spline analysis shows the relationship between (A) NMLR, (B) NLR, and (C) MLR and mortality risk. The red solid lines represent adjusted hazard ratios, and pink shaded areas indicate 95% confidence intervals. Blue histograms display the distribution of inflammatory marker values in the study population. Dashed lines indicate reference points (NMLR = 7.571, NLR = 6.981, MLR = 0.5137). All RCS analyses were set to display results for up to 99.9% of exposure data. Overall P values indicate the significance of the relationship as a whole, while non-linear P values were used to test the null hypothesis of linearity in the relationship.

Discriminative ability of inflammatory markers for predicting all-cause mortality

The Fig. 3 ROC curve analysis showed that the predictive abilities of different inflammatory markers and individual cell counts varied. MLR displayed the highest discriminative capacity, with an AUC of 0.672, followed by NMLR (AUC = 0.658) and NLR (AUC = 0.650). Single-cell components had relatively lower predictive power. Composite ratios (MLR, NMLR, and NLR) demonstrated superior discriminative ability compared with individual cell counts, suggesting that these combined markers may better reflect the underlying inflammatory status. All markers performed significantly better than random chance (AUC > 0.5, P < 0.001), although overall predictive accuracy remained moderate. No marker reached a level that would support standalone clinical risk stratification. Table 3 reports the optimal cutoff values derived from the ROC curves (NLR = 7.866, MLR = 0.497, NMLR = 8.602), along with sensitivity, specificity, and 95% confidence intervals for the AUCs. These cutoffs are exploratory and require external prospective validation before clinical application.

In a 30-day survival analysis depicted in Fig. 4, all three inflammatory markers demonstrated significant prognostic value. Using the aforementioned ROC-based optimal cutoff values, patients were classified into high NLR (> 7.866) and low NLR (≤ 7.866) groups, high MLR (> 0.497) and low MLR (≤ 0.497) groups, and high NMLR (> 8.602) and low NMLR (≤ 8.602) groups. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted for each inflammatory biomarker (Fig. 4A for NLR, Fig. 4B for MLR, Fig. 4C for NMLR), and the cumulative incidence of 30-day mortality was compared between high and low groups (Fig. 4D for NLR, Fig. 4E for MLR, Fig. 4F for NMLR). Based on the log-rank test, patients in the higher inflammatory marker groups had significantly lower survival rates than those in the lower marker groups (all P < 0.001).

Heart failure phenotype distribution and subgroup analyses

HF phenotype-stratified subgroup analyses (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5) showed directionally consistent associations with no significant interaction by phenotype. Given this limited sample size, these phenotype-specific findings are exploratory and should be interpreted with caution, as the analysis is likely underpowered to detect modest effect modification.

Subgroup analyses stratified by age, renal disease, malignant cancer, severe liver disease, metastatic solid tumor, and OASIS score were performed (Supplementary Table 6). NLR and NMLR showed consistent associations with 30-day mortality across all subgroups, with no significant interactions (all P > 0.05) except for a weak OASIS interaction (P = 0.006) where hazard ratios remained close to 1.0. For MLR, significant interactions were observed for renal disease (P < 0.001) and OASIS score (P < 0.001): MLR was strongly associated with mortality in patients without renal disease (HR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.47–1.97) compared with those with renal disease (HR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.07–1.23), and the association was stronger in the low-risk OASIS group (HR = 1.90, 95% CI: 1.56–2.32) than in the high-risk group (HR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.08–1.22). No other subgroups (age, malignant cancer, severe liver disease, metastatic tumor) showed significant interactions for MLR. The direction of association remained positive across all subgroups, and the primary conclusions were unchanged. These exploratory findings suggest that MLR's prognostic value may be more pronounced in patients without renal disease or with lower illness severity, and require external validation.

Incremental predictive value

To assess the incremental predictive value of each inflammatory marker beyond illness severity alone, we used a baseline model that included only SAPS II. We then added each marker (NLR, MLR, NMLR) to this baseline model and calculated ΔAUC (DeLong test), NRI, and IDI. The baseline AUC was 76.39% (95% CI: 73.16%–79.61%) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Adding MLR increased the AUC to 78.52% (ΔAUC = 0.0213, P ≤ 0.001); adding NLR increased the AUC to 77.91% (ΔAUC = 0.0152, P = 0.001); adding NMLR increased the AUC to 78.01% (ΔAUC = 0.0163, P < 0.001). The changes in AUC were modest, with overlapping confidence intervals. NRI and IDI (Supplementary Table 3) were statistically significant but modest. Overall, the markers provide limited incremental value and are not sufficient for standalone risk stratification. These analyses evaluate the incremental value relative to the SAPS II model alone, rather than relative to a fully optimized clinical model.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study found that NLR, MLR, and NMLR were significantly associated with mortality risk in HF and DM patients after confounder adjustment. NLR and NMLR showed independent linear associations with mortality, whereas MLR demonstrated a nonlinear relationship.

Previous studies have reported associations of NLR, MLR, or NMLR with mortality in various diseases. In a heart failure (HF) cohort, only NLR was an independent predictor of long-term all-cause mortality [30]. A study by Yu Wang et al. identified NMLR as an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality in acute myocardial infarction [16]. Similarly, Yanyan Ma et al. found that elevated admission NMLR independently predicted in-hospital mortality in HF patients [31]. In sepsis, higher ICU admission NMLR levels were a risk factor for 30-day mortality [14], and also associated with 28-day mortality [15]. Additionally, Yuer Li et al. reported that admission NMLR predicted 5-year mortality in COPD patients [17]. Notably, unlike most previous studies that covered various conditions, ours is the only one focusing on ICU patients with concurrent HF and diabetes, and the first to examine the association of NMLR with 30-day mortality in this dual-diagnosis population.

NLR, MLR, and NMLR were significantly correlated with 30-day mortality in U.S. patients with CHF and DM. ROC curve analysis showed that all three inflammatory indices had modest predictive value for 30-day mortality, with MLR demonstrating the highest discriminative capacity among the three markers. K-M survival analysis confirmed that higher levels of each marker were associated with higher mortality. These indices are easily obtained from routine blood tests, are cost-effective, and could be combined with other markers to improve risk stratification in HF patients with DM.

The mechanisms linking elevated inflammatory indices to adverse outcomes remain unclear but may involve inflammatory cascades [3], [32]. In DM, visceral adipocytes secrete cytokines, and hyperglycemia can induce myocardial cell cytokine secretion, attracting monocytes/lymphocytes and causing chronic inflammation. Pathways such as RAAS [33], AGEs, and DAMPs can promote myocardial inflammation [10], [34]. In HFpEF, DM induces a systemic proinflammatory state, leading to endothelial dysfunction, reduced NO bioavailability, and PKG inhibition, ultimately causing fibrosis and hypertrophy [35]. Thus, patients with DM and HF experience persistent systemic inflammation, which has been associated with increased mortality in HF [36].

Immune-inflammatory activation releases mediators that promote inflammation and fibrosis [37], [38]. Inflammation is linked to abnormal neutrophil and lymphocyte counts [39]. High neutrophil and low lymphocyte counts indicate severe myocardial injury and higher mortality risk [40], [41]. (1) Neutrophil-mediated immunothrombosis is a key HF mechanism [42]. Neutrophils aggregate around infarcted tissue, phagocytose necrotic cells, release inflammatory mediators, and activate downstream signaling [43], [44], [45]. They recruit monocytes and induce phenotypic changes [46]. Elevated neutrophils reflect worse systemic inflammation, more severe myocardial injury, poorer LVEF, and higher mortality [40]. Neutrophils also release peroxidases [47] and cytokines that induce lymphocyte apoptosis [48]. Notably, neutrophils release more cytokines in DM and HFpEF patients, which may explain worse outcomes [49]. (2) Monocytes contribute to myocardial damage, hypertrophy, fibrosis, and apoptosis [50]. Higher monocyte counts are associated with cardiac remodeling and carotid dilation [51]. Activated monocytes release inflammatory cytokines [52] that may induce lymphocyte apoptosis [48], [53]. (3) Lymphocyte reduction indicates severe stress, higher myocardial oxygen consumption, and greater HF risk [40]. Low lymphocyte counts are also linked to increased death and rehospitalization in HF [37]. Overall, the association between high NLR, MLR, NMLR and adverse outcomes in HF patients with DM may be explained by neutrophil- and monocyte-driven inflammatory cytokine release that induces lymphocyte apoptosis.

This study has several strengths. First, evidence on NLR, MLR, and NMLR in ICU patients with concurrent heart failure and type 2 diabetes is limited. We provide additional evidence on their association with 30-day mortality in this dual-diagnosis subgroup, identifying linear associations for NLR/NMLR and nonlinear (L-shaped) association for MLR. Given the modest discriminative performance (AUC 0.65–0.67), these markers are not ready for standalone clinical use but may supplement existing risk scores. Second, we performed multivariable Cox regression and sensitivity analyses to address confounding. Third, the large sample from MIMIC-IV enhances reliability.

Limitations include potential residual confounding (e.g., diabetes duration, glycemic control), and the fact that the study was based on a single database (MIMIC-IV), which may limit generalizability. In addition, LVEF-based HF phenotype data were available in only 403 participants (30.58%). Consequently, the phenotype-stratified analyses should be considered exploratory and likely underpowered; therefore, the lack of a statistically significant interaction should not be interpreted as strong evidence against phenotype-specific associations. External validation was not performed due to the lack of an appropriate independent cohort with sufficient sample size after definition harmonization. Therefore, the optimal cutoff values reported in this study are dataset-specific and require external validation before any clinical application. Moreover, our analyses evaluated the incremental value of these biomarkers relative to SAPS II alone, rather than against a fully optimized clinical model; thus, the observed improvements in risk discrimination should be interpreted with caution and do not necessarily reflect superiority over a comprehensive clinical risk assessment. Future prospective, multicenter studies with systematic phenotype ascertainment and adequate sample sizes are needed to confirm our results.

Conclusion

In this study, higher admission NLR, MLR, and NMLR levels were potentially associated with 30-day mortality in ICU patients with T2D and HF. The proposed cutoff values are exploratory and warrant further validation in larger external cohorts. Given the modest discriminative performance, these markers should not be used in isolation for clinical decision-making but may offer complementary information. Further prospective studies are needed to confirm these associations.
