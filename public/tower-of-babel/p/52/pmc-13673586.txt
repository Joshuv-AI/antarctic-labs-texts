Background

Mondor’s disease describes sclerosing thrombophlebitis occurring in subcutaneous veins and its occurrence has been reported in anatomical areas such as the penis, axilla, and more commonly the breast and anterio-lateral chest wall [1, 2]. Mondor’s disease of the breast is a rare benign clinical condition with the incidence rate of symptomatic cases estimated to be less than 1.00% with just about 400 reported cases in literature so far [1–3]. A few African studies have previously reported Mondor’s disease. A Tunisian study reported three cases in Caucasian African patients [3]. Over a 4-year period in a breast clinic at a tertiary health facility in Ghana, about 0.94% of 748 patients who self-referred to the clinic with breast complaints were diagnosed with Mondor's disease of the breast [4]. Mondor’s disease of the breast as a clinical entity was first extensively reported on through a published case series in 1939 by Henri Mondor – a French surgeon – after whom the eponym is derived [1, 3, 5]. In the breast, the thrombophlebitis commonly affects the lateral thoracic, thoraco-epigastric and the superior epigastric veins leading to the classic finding of a palpable cord (sometimes termed as the Mondor’s cord) in the outer quadrants of the breast [1, 2, 6, 7]. Mondor’s disease of the breast is hardly seen in the upper inner quadrant of the breast [1, 8]. Mondor’s disease of the breast typically evolves from an acute phase with thrombosis of the affected vein which is mainly characterized by breast pain over several weeks to recanalization of the vein leading to resolution of pain and the Mondor’s cord [1, 2, 5].

On physical examination, the classic sign of Mondor’s disease of the breast is the presence of a tender, palpable cord in the affected quadrant of the breast which may be associated with skin induration, localized erythema or mild edema [1–3, 5]. Most published clinical images of Mondor’s disease of the breast depict patients with lighter skin tones (Fitzpatrick types I–III), in whom erythema, induration, and overlying inflammatory changes are more readily appreciable. In contrast, in individuals with darker skin tones (Fitzpatrick types IV–VI), subtle inflammatory skin changes such as erythema and early peau d’orange may be less visually conspicuous due to increased epidermal melanin content masking underlying vascular congestion and dermal edema. Seeman and co-authors noted the need for increased lighting and skin retraction to enhance observation of a nonerythematous Mondor’s cord in a patient with type V Fitzpatrick skin tone [6]. This may lead to under-recognition or delayed identification of superficial thrombophlebitis on clinical examination.

Though the exact pathophysiology of Mondor’s disease still remains unexplained, certain contributory risk factors have been explored in literature. These include mechanical factors such as direct trauma, breast surgery or procedures; hypercoagulable factors such as antiphospholipid syndrome, Factor V Leiden mutation, and breast malignancy; and inflammatory factors, such as infections or chronic inflammatory conditions [1, 2, 5, 9]. In a majority of Mondor’s disease cases however, no single risk factor can be identified and cases remain idiopathic [1, 3, 5]. It is however recommended to investigate patients with recurrent thrombophlebitis for hypercoagulable factors particularly occult malignancy [5].

Sickle cell disease is well known to be a prothrombotic state with increased occurrence of both arterial and venous thromboembolic events in persons living with the disease beginning as early as childhood [10, 11]. This prothrombotic tendency exists even at steady state hemoglobin levels and in the absence of acute sickle cell crisis [10, 12]. Previous associations between Mondor’s disease and sickle cell disease have not yet been established in literature. A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar using the terms "Mondor’s disease" AND "sickle cell disease" AND "thrombophlebitis" with no previously reported cases identified as of [March,2026].

We report a case of Mondor’s disease of the breast in a Ghanaian female patient living with sickle cell disease.

Case presentation

A 42-year old Ghanaian female living with sickle cell disease (genotype SS) reported to the outpatient department with a week’s history of bilateral breast pain and lumps in her left breast. This was her first presentation to a health facility with the problem. Breast pain was of new onset, initially severe with a pain score of 8 on the numerical pain scale. It was worse in the left breast and most marked parasternally at the outset. The patient had also felt lumps in her left breast, she initially felt two lumps but as at presentation, she could only feel one lump with minimal pain.

She did not report nipple discharge, weight loss, back pain, difficulty in breathing and symptoms of anemia. Menarche was attained at age 17, she had 3 children and had breastfed 2 of them exclusively. History was negative for oral contraceptive use, smoking, alcohol use, prior breast trauma or procedures such as biopsies. There was no personal or family history of breast disease. She had had a cholecystectomy and previous surgical management of a pterygium. With regards to her sickle cell disease, the patient rarely had acute pain episodes or any of the other crises. She was on 500 mg of oral hydroxyurea daily.

Physical examination revealed pendulous symmetrical breasts with a palpable thickened cord and mild peau d’orange change of the overlying skin in the upper inner quadrant of the left breast. The patient had type VI Fitzpatrick skin tone, no skin induration or erythema was observed on inspection, peau d’orange change required very close observation and gentle skin retraction before being noted. The right breast had no masses or a palpable cord. Both breasts at the time of examination were nontender with no axillary lymphadenopathy noted clinically.

The patient was recommended for a mammogram of both breasts based on her age and the peau d’orange skin change noted which raised concerns for a possible inflammatory breast cancer though the short history was atypical. Mammography of both breasts was normal with no abnormality found. Complementary ultrasounds of her breasts were conducted thereafter. Ultrasound of the left breast showed a tubular, hypoechoic structure located within the subcutaneous plane, following the course of a superficial vein. The structure was noncompressible consistent with a thrombosed vein. No internal color Doppler flow was detected on color Doppler imaging. Surrounding soft tissues demonstrated minimal perifocal edema without evidence of drainable fluid collection. There was a focal area of skin thickening. The right breast showed no abnormalities on ultrasound. However, multiple benign axillary lymph nodes were also found to be present bilaterally on ultrasound with the largest measuring 0.70 cm in the shortest axis. Imaging was consistent with a diagnosis of Mondor’s disease of the breast. The patient was counseled on the condition and reassured after diagnosis was established. Watchful waiting for resolution of the cord was then instituted. Follow up at three months revealed complete resolution of the cord and no breast complaints by the patient.

Discussion

With the incidence rate of symptomatic disease estimated to be less than 1.00%, Mondor’s disease is a rare differential of breast pain [2, 5].

Though previous associations between Mondor’s disease and sickle cell disease have not been established, the background of sickle cell disease in this patient may have been the predisposing factor for Mondor’s though it could have also been idiopathic and unrelated to her underlying sickle cell disease. This may be explored further in future studies.

In Mondor’s disease, ultrasonography is the primary imaging modality for confirming the diagnosis following clinical suspicion [2, 5]. Ultrasound features of the thrombosed vein include the presence of a noncompressible tubular structure of varying echogenicity (usually hypoechoic or anechoic) with absent flow on color Doppler [1, 2, 5, 9]. Associated soft tissue findings can also be visualized on ultrasound when present. Mammography has limited diagnostic power in Mondor’s disease as it can give a report of normal or inconclusive findings [1, 2]. As was seen in this patient, her mammogram did not pick up any evident abnormality though the thrombus was still present and was visualized on ultrasound. Mammogram is however still valuable in ruling out breast malignancy especially when there is a strong suspicion of such associated with thrombophlebitis [5, 9].

This case is a rare occurrence even among the uncommon, seeing as the thrombophlebitis affected a vein in the upper inner quadrant of the breast which is very unusual in Mondor’s disease.

In this patient with Fitzpatrick type VI skin tone, the peau d’orange change required careful inspection under adequate lighting and gentle skin traction to appreciate subtle textural differences. The absence of visible erythema despite later confirmed superficial thrombosis highlights the importance of tactile examination and a low threshold for ultrasonographic evaluation in patients with darker skin tones presenting with localized breast pain and palpable cords. Given that diagnostic algorithms for breast pathology often emphasize visible inflammatory changes, clinicians should be aware that reliance on visual erythema alone may be insufficient in patients with increased skin pigmentation.

Most images available in literature on patients with Mondor’s disease of the breast are from patients with lighter skin tones (Fitzpatrick skin tone I – III) [6]. The patient had type VI Fitzpatrick skin tone but unfortunately an image of the affected breast could not be obtained during the time of examination due to nonconsent. Increased representation of dermatologic and breast pathology images in diverse skin tones remains important to reduce potential diagnostic bias and improve equity in clinical assessment.

Conclusion

Mondor’s disease of the breast, diagnosed mainly with ultrasonography, though a rare cause of breast pain, still needs heightened awareness among clinicians. Most cases are idiopathic with patients going on to full recovery with resolution of the Mondor’s cord and pain. Greater representation of benign and malignant breast pathologies in darker skin tones within medical literature may enhance clinician awareness and reduce potential disparities in early detection.
