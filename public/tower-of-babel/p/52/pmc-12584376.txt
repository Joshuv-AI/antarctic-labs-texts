The emergence of Mpox in Ethiopia: implications for public health action
Authors: Alfoalem Araba Abiye, Hana Shafi Amde, Mirgissa Kaba
Published: 2025

Introduction

On 25 May 2025, Ethiopia reported its first confirmed case of Mpox (clade Ib), involving an infant with a recent history of exposure linked to travel in Kenya [1], Preliminary epidemiological investigations linked the Moyale cluster to cross-border movement, as the father had travelled to Kenya shortly before symptom onset [2, 3]. As of June 13, 2025, 18 Mpox cases had been confirmed across seven regions of Ethiopia, with one death (for a case fatality rate at 5.6%) [4]. These cases occurred against a backdrop of a surging Mpox outbreak across Africa. In 2024 alone thousands of cases and hundreds of deaths were recorded in multiple countries, prompting WHO to declare the African Mpox resurgence as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern [5] and Africa CDC to declare it a “Public Health Emergency of Continental Security” in August 2024 [6–8]. Data from Africa CDC shows that Mpox case count tripled in early 2025 compared to late 2024 [9, 10]. This commentary analysed the Ethiopian context of Mpox emergence, reviews relevant lessons from Nigeria, DRC and Ghana, and makes actionable recommendations. We emphasized the need to strengthen surveillance, diagnostic capacity, health workforce, and risk communication to curb transmission pathways [6, 10].

Background and global trends

Mpox is a zoonotic orthopoxvirus endemic in parts of West and Central Africa [11]. In 2017, Nigeria experienced a Mpox outbreak involving more than 200 confirmed cases across multiple states, making it one of the largest recorded outbreaks in recent history [12], and in 2022 a new multi-country outbreak (mostly clade II) spread internationally via travel and sexual networks [13]. Meanwhile, a separate clade-I outbreak escalated in Central Africa since 2023 [14]. WHO reported that by mid-2024 “the number of cases reported so far this year has exceeded last year’s total, with more than 15,600 cases and 537 deaths” in Africa [5] para. 2). The novel clade 1b variant spreading in and around the DRC including Burundi, Kenya, Rwanda, and Uganda was cited as particularly concerning [5, 7, 8]. Africa CDC similarly noted that suspected Mpox cases in 2024 on the continent ( > 17,000) far surpassed totals in 2022 (7,146) and 2023 (14,957) [6]. By July 2024, Africa CDC reported approximately 37,600 cases and 1,451 deaths from Mpox across 15 African Union countries. Notably, 15,000 new cases were recorded in 2023 alone. [15]. DRC has been the epicenter of the Mpox outbreak, accounting for approximately 96% of all cases and 97% of all deaths reported in 2024, but as of July 20, 2025, 24 countries in Africa have reported a total of 28,152 confirmed Mpox cases, including 133 deaths (case fatality rate: 0.5%). Additionally, 21 countries have reported ongoing active transmission in the past six weeks [16]. Africa CDC reported 37,600 cases and 1,451 deaths across 15 AU countries by July 2024, whereas WHO reported 28,152 cases and 133 deaths across 24 African countries as of July 2025, reflecting both differences in reporting periods and coverage.

This resurgence prompted coordinated response. WHO declared the African Mpox outbreak a PHEIC on 14 August 2024 [5], and Africa CDC issued an August 2024 declaration, enabling resource mobilization, risk communication, surveillance and laboratory strengthening via a One Health approach [6]. Gavi and partners set up vaccine stockpiles and prioritized support for DRC and neighbors [7]. Vaccine access has been a challenge: Nigeria obtained 10,000 donated Jynneos doses (to vaccinate health workers and at-risk groups) in August [17], while Africa CDC estimated approximately 10 million doses are needed continent-wide. In Ethiopia’s region, Kenya confirmed Mpox cases in July 2024 (its first) [18], and Somalia has had Mpox reports. These trends underscored that Mpox is no longer confined to its historical foci: it is evolving (with new clades) and expanding geographically and demographically. Control thus requires vigilant surveillance, communication, and clinical management across Africa [5, 6].

In response to the emergence of Mpox in Ethiopia, the Ministry of Health (MoH), in collaboration with the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI), activated national emergency response mechanisms. These include enhanced surveillance, contact tracing, and the deployment of rapid response teams to investigate and contain cases. Laboratory capacity is being reinforced to enable timely diagnosis, while public awareness campaigns have been launched to educate communities about symptoms, transmission routes, and prevention strategies. The MoH and EPHI are also working closely with international partners, notably the WHO and Africa CDC, to coordinate strategic responses and mobilize technical and material support [19].

National situation in Ethiopia

Ethiopia did not report confirmed Mpox cases until May 2025. However, in anticipation of regional spread, the Ministry of Health stepped up preparedness since mid-2024. In August 2024 it instituted “stronger than usual” health screenings at all border crossings (especially in Oromia, Somali and Southern regions) and at Addis Ababa’s international airport. A Public Health Disaster Coordination and Response Center was established to lead Mpox preparedness. These measures were announced immediately after Africa CDC’s August 2024 PHECS declaration, reflecting Ethiopia’s high alert status [6, 19].

On 25 May 2025, public health authorities confirmed Ethiopia’s first Mpox cases: a 21-day-old infant and the infant’s mother, both in stable condition and quarantined, in Moyale, a key trade hub on the Ethio-Kenyan border. Initial investigations suggest cross-border transmission where the infant’s father had recently travelled to Kenya, where Mpox cases had been reported earlier [1]. Subsequently, measures were taken to trace contacts to quarantine them. The Ministry and EPHI emphasized coordinated contact tracing, urged exposed persons or anyone with symptoms to seek care promptly, and assured the public the situation was contained. With only this localized cluster confirmed so far, the government’s focus is on rapid containment [19].

These developments come at a time when Ethiopia is engaged in other public health emergencies (e.g. COVID-19, cholera, polio campaigns), which, while straining resources, may also contribute to overall readiness by fostering adaptive infrastructure and cross-program collaboration [20]. Yet, Ethiopia’s Expanded Program on Immunization and polio surveillance networks can potentially be leveraged. Experiences from WHO assisted redeployment of polio experts for Mpox response in DRC [21] is useful experience to emulate. Evidently, this first case of Mpox in Ethiopia, which was not on the national radar, highlights challenges in ensuring that existing surveillance, diagnostic, and clinical systems are adequately tested and responsive for rapidly scaling up diagnostic and treatment capacity. The government’s experience to date (border screening and rapid case confirmation) showed commitment, but key challenges remain in fully operationalizing Mpox response capacities. These challenges include:

Surveillance and early detection

Ethiopia’s routine disease surveillance (IDSR) does not yet include Mpox-specific algorithms. In remote or resource-limited areas (like Moyale), healthcare workers may not recognize Mpox symptoms promptly. Enhancing event-based surveillance and training clinicians on the Mpox case definition needed to be in place [22]. Community health extension workers and local informants should be enlisted to report unexplained rash illness. Cross-border coordination is critical with Kenyan and Somali health authorities to monitor population movement and share information on cases, following models of ongoing collaboration such as between Uganda and Rwanda [5].

Diagnostic capacity

Confirming Mpox requires PCR testing (for monkeypox virus DNA), but Ethiopia’s laboratory network has few molecular labs in remote areas around the borders. Building in-country diagnostic capacity was key: the Public Health Institute needed to acquire reagents and train staff for Mpox PCR and sequencing (to determine the clade). In the interim, Ethiopia may need to send samples to WHO regional labs. The DRC experience shows how crucial lab support is: WHO sent > 200,000 vaccine doses and strengthened DRC’s cold chain and surveillance to support targeted response [21]. Ethiopia should similarly ensure specimen transport, cold chain, and lab networks are ready to test suspected cases rapidly.

Healthcare workforce

Ethiopia has one of the world’s lowest doctor-to-population ratios (only ~0.03 physicians per 1,000 people) [23], though mid- and lower-level cadres are more numerous. The sudden Mpox response will add workload to already stretched clinicians, nurses and public health officers. Rapid training of health workers on Mpox case management (including infection control) is needed, along with sufficient PPE and support for isolation of cases. Ethiopia’s experience with COVID-19 showed that worker shortages and turnover can hinder outbreak control [24]; thus retaining and protecting staff is crucial. Mobilizing health extension workers and community health volunteers as contact tracers or risk communicators (under supervision) can partly fill gaps, as Ethiopia did for Ebola preparedness.

Vaccine and therapeutics

No Mpox-specific vaccine exists, but smallpox vaccines (JYNNEOS/MVA-BN) are believed to be effective for Mpox [25]. Ethiopia currently has no Mpox vaccine stock. Nigeria’s example suggests early engagement with donors where Nigeria secured 10,000 JYNNEOS doses from the U.S. in [17]. Ethiopia should urgently request WHO/Gavi-facilitated donations (potentially through COVAX mechanisms being mobilized for Mpox) [7] or through bilateral channels. Considering Ethiopia’s limited cold chain, any received doses must be prioritized (for health workers and high-risk contacts). Similarly, therapeutic antivirals (e.g. tecovirimat) are virtually unavailable in Africa; Ethiopia should consult WHO for possible access to investigational stockpiles. Until vaccines arrive, Ethiopia should prepare protocols for ring vaccination (targeted immunization around detected cases) rather than mass vaccination, in line with global guidance.

Socio-cultural factors: stigma and misinformation

Stigma surrounding Mpox has been a problem elsewhere. In Nigeria, sensationalized media reports fuelled fear and anxiety [26]. Mpox has been erroneously associated with certain populations (e.g. MSM) or with conspiracy theories. In Ethiopia, unchecked rumours could lead to discrimination against patients or xenophobia towards border communities. The Ministry must deliver clear, factual messages that anyone can contract Mpox through close contact, and emphasize compassionate care. WHO stresses the need for “inclusive messaging” and avoiding stigmatizing language [27]. Risk communication should use Ethiopia’s multiple languages and community channels. Learning from Nigeria’s 2017 outbreak, journalists should be engaged and trained so media coverage is accurate and calm [26]. Community leaders and the influential network of health extension workers can help dispel myths and encourage early care-seeking. Ethiopia should also leverage social media monitoring (as done in other outbreaks) to detect and counter misinformation quickly.

Coordination and resources

Ethiopia’s public health response will involve multiple ministries (Health, Transport, and Interior) and partners (WHO, UNICEF, Africa CDC). Given limited budgets, domestic and international funding must be mobilized. Africa CDC’s call for collective action [6] is relevant: Ethiopia should request technical support (from Africa CDC’s health institutes) for training and surveillance, and financial/commodity aid via the AU and WHO. Sectoral coordination, engaging not just government sectors but also academic and research institutions is essential to support a multisector response. Cross-border coordination bodies (e.g. IGAD Health Sector) should be activated to align regional strategies. Moreover, academic institutions should be supported to conduct studies, including molecular analyses, to determine whether circulating Mpox strains in Ethiopia may differ from those elsewhere. Importantly, Ethiopia must avoid knee-jerk border closures or travel bans (Africa CDC advised against such measures that could hamper essential travel) [6]; instead focus on targeted screening and advisories.

Evidence from national and African studies

In generalized settings, models predict that Mpox spreads more easily where smallpox-derived immunity has waned. For example, Chen et al. estimate R₀≈2.8 for DRC’s 2022 outbreak, implying > 60% vaccine coverage would be needed to interrupt transmission in the absence of prior immunity [28]. In practice, limited supplies mean targeted strategies outperform untargeted campaigns. Modelling of the recent outbreak found that isolating cases and ringvaccinating contacts (with tracing) curbed spread more efficiently than broad highrisk vaccination alone [29]. Similarly, prioritizing vulnerable subgroups can magnify impact: a dynamic model in the DRC showed that vaccinating children in endemic provinces (e.g. 80% of < 15 yrs) could cut cases by > 50% and deaths by ~70% with far fewer doses than universal coverage [30]. Fractional-dose regimens may further extend scarce stock: one model found that if fractional (dosesparce) vaccines retain ~40–50% of full efficacy, vaccinating more people with smaller doses could avert substantially more infections than full-dose use when supply is very limited [31].

For Ethiopia, which has virtually no residual smallpox immunity and constrained resources, these findings suggest a focus on highly efficient strategies, e.g. rapid identification/isolation of cases, ring vaccination of contacts, and prioritizing high-risk or younger groups (possibly using fractional dosing) to maximize the benefit of any available vaccine supply.

Recent Ethiopian data confirm critical knowledge, attitude, and practice gaps among healthcare workers and the general public regarding Mpox. Studies in Addis Ababa, Injibara, and Amhara revealed that most healthcare workers lack confidence in Mpox diagnosis and treatment, with little experience in case definition and treatment guidelines [32, 33]. Lack of training, Mpox case exposure, and poor availability of diagnostic centers were all causally associated with these gaps. Community surveys also revealed poor knowledge of Mpox symptoms and transmission among the community, warranting the undertaking of pro-active health communication campaigns to improve awareness, facilitate early care-seeking, and address misinformation [32].

Vaccine uptake and preparedness also indicate the challenges facing Ethiopia in Mpox control. National health worker surveys were quoted as having as low as 28.2% acceptance to receive the Mpox vaccine, whereas in risk groups, the acceptability rates had been up to 75% [34]. These findings highlight targeted immunization campaigns among targeted interventions of frontline health workers and risk groups. Evaluations of Ethiopia’s disease surveillance system indicated lacunae in early detection, in-country diagnostic capacity, and response coordination to outbreaks [35]. Collectively, these researches highlight that beyond the establishment of surveillance, laboratory, and workforce capacity, Ethiopia also must implement culturally adapted risk communication and early vaccination strategies. Leveraging existing platforms such as polio and COVID-19 response networks may enhance preparedness and facilitate rapid containment of emerging Mpox clusters [36].

Policy recommendations and conclusion

Ethiopia’s confirmation of Mpox cases demands an urgent and coordinated response. Mpox should be fully integrated into the IDSR, with trained health workers, including those serving mobile and pastoralist communities, equipped to recognize and report suspected cases. Rapid response teams must be ready to investigate and contain outbreaks, while laboratory capacity is decentralized through regional PCR testing and linked to sequencing centers for timely confirmation and viral tracking.

Preparedness also requires strengthening the health workforce through Mpox-specific training, infection prevention resources, and psychosocial support. A clear vaccine strategy is vital, beginning with requests for doses through WHO and Gavi, supported by cold-chain readiness and a ring vaccination approach targeting case contacts and frontline workers. Parallel to these measures, robust community engagement campaigns should counter misinformation and stigma, using trusted local leaders and culturally tailored messaging to build public trust and encourage prevention and care-seeking.

Finally, Ethiopia must pursue strong regional coordination with Kenya, Somalia, and IGAD partners to harmonize case definitions, share data, and align border health measures. Integrating Mpox response within existing outbreak platforms such as polio and COVID-19 programs will optimize scarce resources and build on existing systems. With decisive leadership, global solidarity, and a sustained One Health perspective, Ethiopia can not only contain Mpox but also strengthen long-term preparedness for future zoonotic threats in an era of climate change and increased human-animal interactions.
