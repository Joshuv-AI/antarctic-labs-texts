Clinical features of patients who needed more than one-time photorefractive keratectomy (PRK), a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ulfat Anjum, Hesam Hashemian, Masoud Khorrami-Nejad, Reza Yousefi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose To evaluate the clinical features of patients who had undergone photorefractive keratectomy (PRK) more than once. Identifying these pre-operative parameters can help in assessing patients at higher risk of repeat PRK. MethodA historical cohort study was carried out at Farabi Eye Hospital between 2015 to 2022. The data were obtained from electronic records, refined, and divided into two groups based on PRK outcome: cases that underwent a second round of PRK and controls that underwent PRK only once. Only patients with at least 12 months postoperative follow-up were included. The baseline preoperative data, including subjective & objective refraction, spherical equivalent, anterior and posterior keratometry, and corneal pachymetry at the apex & thinnest location, were collected. The PRK surgery was performed using Schwind Amaris 1050 RS by Sirius CSO, and the anterior segment measurements were taken using Pentacam® HR. ResultsForty eight patients were included, with 17 in the case group who underwent a second round of PRK and 31 in the control group who had a single PRK. The mean ages of the control and case patients were 32.1±7.8 and 31.00±8.62 years, respectively. A statistically significant difference was observed between the control and case groups in the mean subjective sphere (control -3.17±2.77 vs. case 0.07±2.50,P<.001), subjective astigmatism (control -0.90±0.71 vs. case -2.06±1.26,P<.001*), subjective spherical equivalent (SE) (control -3.62±2.77 vs. case-0.96±2.73, P<.001), objective refraction sphere (control -2.75±2.94 vs. case 0.57±3.00, P<.001), objective astigmatic refraction (control -0.91±0.75 vs. case -1.97±1.28, P = .003), and SE of objective refraction (control-3.20±2.30 vs. case -0.41±3.05, P<.001). There were significant differences in anterior flat keratometry (K1) (control 43.93±1.17 vs. case 42.75±1.64, P<0.05) and anterior corneal astigmatism (control 1.03±0.57 vs. case 1.82±1.25, P<0.05) between patients who needed more than one PRK surgery versus those who successfully managed with PRK once.ConclusionPatients who needed more than one PRK surgery had significantly lower sphere and SE, greater astigmatism, lower anterior flat-K, decreased corneal thickness, and greater anterior corneal astigmatism than patients who needed only one PRK surgery.

Introduction

The most common cause of visual impairment in the world is uncorrected refractive error [1]. All types of refractive errors, including myopia, hyperopia, and astigmatism, are mainly treated by glasses, contact lenses, and intraocular lens implantation (IOL), where IOL was used to correct refractive errors before corneal refractive surgeries became known [2]. Nowadays, with the advent of laser technology in refractive surgeries, there are more viable, permanent and practical solutions available to reduce dependence on glasses or contact lenses. Photorefractive keratectomy (PRK) is a corneal ablative procedure for correcting refractive errors by ablating the corneal stroma, and it was the first refractive surgery to receive Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval in 1995 [3–5]. With advancements in technology, different refractive keratectomy procedures have evolved, offering safer and painless options, reducing the number of PRK procedures; however, the use of PRK is still considered a procedure of choice in many cases worldwide [6] with the long-term stable results [7, 8]. PRK has remained an effective procedure, especially for thin corneas, surface irregularities, and those contraindicated for other refractive procedures [9, 10]. PRK is also considered a viable option for patients with myopia of up to −12 diopters (D), astigmatism of up to 6 D, and hyperopia of up to 5 D [11]. Although outcomes are better and more consistent in low to moderate refractive errors [12].

Not all patients achieve optimal vision with a single PRK. Repeat refractive surgeries are typically performed to refine vision and patient satisfaction. The average retreatment rate across studies is approximately 6–7%, with recent estimates indicating that approximately 3.8–20.8% of patients who undergo excimer laser myopia correction ultimately require a secondary “enhancement” procedure [13]. It is anticipated that 10–20% of patients following primary PRK would experience residual myopia to a degree that prompts them to seek its elimination [14–17]. The PRK enhancement surgeries are carried out within the first 12 to 24 months following the initial surgery [18]. A study revealed that significant changes in the spherical equivalent can be experienced after 1 year postoperatively in myopia [19]. But there are very limited studies on the hyperopic regression and efficacy of PRK in hyperopia. Studies have shown stable results in lower hyperopes, although the data on moderate to higher hyperopes is limited [20]. The outcomes of post-refractive surgery retreatments have primarily been examined in situations of repeat PRK following prior LASIK [16]. But only a few examples of repeat PRK are available following primary PRK [15]. The studies have shown that when examining the long-term effects, the efficacy of PRK may decrease after 15 years [21].

Corneal indices are primarily taken into account when planning a refractive surgery and are measured by various analyzers, with Pentacam ® HR (Oculus Inc., Germany) considered among the most accurate instruments [22]. The instrument has a high degree of data accuracy, reliability, short duration, and acceptability by patients [23, 24]. There is evidence that corneal topographic parameters correlate with the PRK stability. Naderi et al. found that eyes with higher simulated keratometric astigmatism and higher preoperative corneal irregularity (> 5 mm topographic irregularity index) had significantly higher odds of refractive regression after PRK. Some research indicated that baseline keratometry and central thickness are not accurate indicators of recurrent PRK [25]. However, more research into certain corneal anterior and posterior surface measures is required to further explore the difference between eyes that achieve the desired correction in one PRK versus those that later require a second PRK.

Understanding the risk of requiring more than one PRK in a patient’s profile is crucial as it adds cost, recovery time and yields less predictable outcomes. Excimer laser refractive surgery predictability is known to be influenced by several parameters, including higher preoperative astigmatism, hyperopia, myopia, and older preoperative age are predisposing factors for retreatment surgeries [26, 27]. However, it is established that preoperative keratometric values are less likely to predict the need for enhancement [28].

The present study aimed to understand the baseline clinical features of patients needing PRK more than once. To the best of our knowledge, this was the first study to compare the refractive and keratometry features of patients who required PRK more than once versus those who were successfully managed with one-time PRK. Clinical characteristics provided us with data for establishing patient profiles, raising awareness among refractive surgery candidates, and providing us with directions for advancing PRK surgery that need to be considered.

Methodology

This was a historical cohort study performed on patients aged 18 to 60 years who underwent primarily PRK at Farabi Eye Hospital affiliated with the Tehran University of Medical Sciences. All official approvals from the Ethical Review Board of Tehran University of Medical Sciences were obtained and the personal information of the patients was kept confidential. Patients who underwent PRK procedures between January 2015 and September 2022 were selected as the sample population. We collected preoperative data at baseline from the hospital’s electronic records and excluded patients who had any type of refractive surgery other than PRK, any history of corneal ectasia, uveitis, or other corneal disorders or trauma, a history of any ocular surgery except PRK, or less than 1 year of follow-up to ensure regaining refractive stability. The range of follow-up was not recorded either.

The data were divided into two groups. Patients were assigned to the case group if they had already undergone a second PRK, and their preoperative data before undergoing the first PRK was taken as baseline and recorded. The other group that remained stable after the first PRK surgery served as the control group. The reason for undergoing a second round of PRK in the case group was not recorded either. The PRK surgery was performed using Schwind Amaris 1050 RS by Sirius CSO. A Snellen E-Chart was used to measure uncorrected and corrected distance visual acuity. We changed “Snellen acuity” to “logMAR acuity”. Each patient underwent subjective refraction and retinoscopy before surgery to determine the full refractive correction. Pentacam HR (Oculus Inc., Germany) was used for corneal surface indices, including anterior and posterior keratometry for PRK patients. We gathered information for both groups at baseline before going to PRK surgery, such as uncorrected distance visual acuity (DVA), corrected distance visual acuity, degree of spherical refractive error, cylindrical refractive error, and spherical equivalent of the right eye, separately. We collected corneal surface data in D as the flat K, steep K, mean K (Km) of both surfaces and the Kmax. The corneal thickness (CT) at the pupil centre (µm), pachy apex (µm), and thinnest point (µm) were also obtained for both groups. For statistical autonomy and to prevent inter-eye correlation, only right-eye data was used for interpretation. All collected data from both groups were then analysed using SPSS 26 (IBM, Inc., Chicago, USA). The normality of the data was first checked by using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The mean and standard deviation (± SD) values were reported for continuous data such as age and spherical errors. A paired sample t-test was used to compare the corneal parameters, such as flat K, steep K of both surfaces, corneal pachymetry, corneal thinnest location, visual acuity and mean spherical equivalent, between the two groups. Statistical tests were performed at a level of 5% error. Independent sample t-tests were used to compare the case and control groups. We also evaluated the case group before undergoing the 2nd surgery and compared it with the other groups. One-way ANOVA was used to compare three groups, and a P value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Post hoc least significant difference tests were used to compare differences between groups.

Results

A total of 48 patients were found to fulfill our criteria. These participants were divided into two groups: 31 who underwent one-time refractive surgery only (the control group) and 17 who underwent more than one PRK procedure. In the case group, 11 patients were female, and 6 were male; in the control group, 19 patients were female, and 12 were male. The mean age of the control group patients was 32.10 ± 7.83 years, whereas the mean age at the time of the first surgery in the case group was 31.00 ± 8.62 years, which was not a significant difference (P < 0.001) as shown in Table 1.

Subjective and objective refractive parameters showed a statistically significant difference between the case undergoing repeat PRK versus the control group, with a treatment of one successful PRK. The mean subjective sphere of the control group was more myopic (−3.17 ± 2.77 D) than that of the case group, which was nearly emmetropic on average (+ 0.07 ± 2.50 D, p < 0.001). Similarly, patients in the control group had higher levels of myopia on average, as shown by the subjective spherical equivalent being significantly more negative in the control group (−3.62 ± 2.77 D) than in the case group (−0.96 ± 2.73 D, p < 0.001). The average subjective astigmatism was found without much difference between the groups (control: −0.90 ± 0.71 D vs. case: −0.96 ± 2.73 D), but the maximum myopic astigmatism was − 4.50D in the case group and − 2.50D in the control. All the objective refraction components followed a similar pattern, confirming the subjective refraction components as shown in Table 1. After initial PRK, subgroup analysis in the case group before undergoing subsequent PRK revealed a subjective spherical equivalent of 0.06 ± 2.01 with a maximum myopic shift of −2.75D to hyperopia of + 3.75D. It was further confirmed by the objective analysis of the subgroup with a higher astigmatism of −3.50 D, myopic objective spherical equivalent of −2.88 D, and hyperopic objective spherical equivalent of + 1.50 D.

K1, Km and astigmatism in the control group were significantly different from those in the case group (K1 control 43.93 ± 1.17, case 42.75 ± 1.64 P < 0.05*), (Km control 44.43 ± 1.19, case 43.51 ± 1.48, P < 0.05*), and (astigmatism control 1.03 ± 0.57, case 1.82 ± 1.25, P < 0.05*), respectively. Discussion

PRK is still a widely accepted ophthalmic refractive procedure. There is plenty of literature on the need for PRK after the initial LASIK surgery, but less data is available regarding the clinical profile of patients who needed a repeat PRK after the initial PRK. To bridge this gap, our study aimed to find and compare the clinical refractive and keratometry features of patients who underwent more than one-time PRK versus those of patients who were treated with only one successful PRK. We included myopic, hyperopic and astigmatism patients. The follow-up duration was 1 year in our study after initial PRK and proceeding to subsequent PRK to regain the corneal biomechanics and stabilize the refractive power as supported by Hjortdal et al. [18]. Although the corneal biomechanics never return to the pre-operative state.

Our study found that patients who underwent a second round of PRK had less preoperative mean spherical equivalent (−0.96 ± 2.73 D) than the control group (−3.62 ± 2.77 D) at baseline. The mean spherical equivalent showed a less myopic, more hyperopic refractive error in a repeat PRK group, while a single PRK group largely comprised myopic patients. Our study is well supported by a previous large-scale analysis by Mimouni et al. where the authors found that hyperopic patients with less mean myopic refraction undergo retreatment [26]. By multiple logistic regression in the same analysis, the author confirmed that the presence of hyperopia is the independent predictor for repeated surgery. In contrast to our study, a higher myopic spherical refractive error > 5.0 D was considered a risk factor for retreatment [25]. The findings from our case group were less predictable because our case group received less myopia and higher astigmatism treatment at the baseline, despite having a low mean sphere.

Astigmatism is a standout challenge in refractive surgery. In our study, the case group had a higher degree of astigmatism (maximum range > 4.00D) than its control group (maximum range < 3.0D) at baseline. Greater cylindrical refractive error is considered a reason of repeating the refractive surgery. Our findings are supported by earlier research by Russell Pokroy et al. and Mimouni et al., which showed that a higher degree of astigmatism raises the risk of retreatment procedures [29, 30]. This enforces the importance of considering the amount of cylindrical refractive error before planning PRK surgery for astigmatic patients.

Our study found no relationship between the age of the patients who underwent one PRK in the control group and that of the patients who underwent repeat surgery in the case group. The minimum age was 21 years in both groups, while the maximum age was 50 years in the case group and 54 years in the control group. A post hoc test also revealed no significant direct or indirect differences. Our study contradicts the findings of a previous study by Mimouni M et al. that older preoperative age increases the risk of retreatment surgery [26]. Although few other studies support our finding that there is no correlation between age and retreatment surgery [31–33]. Other factors including optical zone diameter, ocular fixation stability, room temperature, and surgeon experiences should be considered as reported a cause of retreatment surgeries [31].

Corneal shape profile has been recognised as a risk factor for complications in corneal refractive surgery. Our repeat PRK patients’ profile showed a baseline flatter anterior and posterior corneal parameters in both vertical and horizontal meridians compared to those with a single successful PRK. A flatter cornea has less optical power; therefore, to achieve emmetropia, a larger ablation area might be necessary, which could lead to undercorrection or residual refractive error. Our findings support those of Varssano et al., indicating that a flat meridian results in lower postoperative corrected distance visual acuity [28]. This is further supported by Mostafa et al., who reported that LASIK eyes with preoperative K < 43.5 D tended to have greater undercorrection compared to those with steeper corneas [34]. Baseline flat keratometry should be considered a risk factor for complications in PRK surgery, as it serves as a potential indicator for retreatment.

There is less literature on corneal pachymetry profile for PRK. The suitability of a candidate for PRK treatments to correct ametropia is influenced by corneal pachymetry; lower CT are thought to increase the risk of ectasias after the procedures, and there is less literature on the corneal pachymetry profile for PRK. In our study, the central corneal thickness (CCT) at the apex and maximal CT were greater in the one-time successful PRK group than in the repeat treatment cases. CCT at apex ranged from 473 μm to 560 μm with a mean CCT of 516.23 ± 27.42 in the case group at the baseline, while it was 506 μm to 543 μm with a mean of 524.22 ± 42.66 μm. Other investigations on various ethnic backgrounds and populations suggest no variations and span similar to the CCT value in our studies [35–38]. The eyes with thicker corneas may be able to handle more ablation and get to the goal of emmetropic refraction more consistently. A case-control study by Mohammadi et al. did not show that preoperative pachymetry predicts retreatment [25]. Nevertheless, our data indicate that even minor variations in central thickness can be significant. It is worth noting that the mean of corneal apex pachymetry for repeat PRK surgery was 467.43 ± 39.62 μm, with the range from the lower bound of 440 μm to the upper bound of 503 μm. It extends our previous study findings that PRK is safe and successful even in corneas with a pachymetry of < 500 μm [39]. Our pachymetry data can serve as a baseline for the retreatment in the future. Although the cases of repeat PRK is declining, other factors like optical zone diameter, ocular fixation stability, room temperature, and surgeon experiences should be considered to.

Due to the retrospective nature of our study, we were not able to fully match the groups. Patients with incomplete follow-up and missing preoperative keratometry were excluded from the analysis. No imputation techniques were applied; only complete-case data were analysed. We didn’t stratify refractive error subtypes due to limited sample size and included both myopic and hyperopic patients. The opposing mathematical expressions (+/-) might cancel each other effect during statistical analysis, yielding lower mean SE. We suggest future research with larger cohort for a separate evaluation of refractive subtypes to improve interpretability. Additionally, future research utilizing a large sample size of Pentacam keratometry indices involving high-order aberrations, corneal asymmetry, ocular fixation stability may provide more robust identifications of features that highten the risk of requiring more than one PRK.

In conclusion, we found that a high degree of preoperative astigmatism can lead patients to undergo another PRK. Age and high spherical equivalent were not found to be statistically significant, suggesting that other factors for retreatment should be considered when screening patients for PRK. Flatter keratometry in the case group than in the control group was detected via both anterior and posterior surface keratometry. Corneal pachymetry and thickness were greater in patients who underwent one-time PRK than in patients in the case group.
