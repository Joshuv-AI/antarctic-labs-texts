Association of social participation with progression and reversion of intrinsic capacity in older adults: based on multistate model
Authors: Guilan Xie, Chiara Natalie Focacci, Jiajia Li, Ruiqi Wang, Gong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the link of social participation with progression and reversion of intrinsic capacity (IC).DesignA prospective cohort study.Setting and participants2955 adults aged 60 years and older in China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study were included in this study.MeasurementsIC was measured by locomotion, vitality, cognition, psychological capacity, and sensory (vision and hearing), and was further divided into intact IC and impaired IC. Social participation was measured by the frequencies of six types of social activities and classified as low, moderate, and high levels by tertiles. Multistate Markov model was employed to investigate the associations of social participation with transitions of intact IC, impaired IC, and death.ResultsThose with moderate or high social participation had higher likelihoods of residing in intact IC and reversion from impaired IC to intact IC, while had lower probabilities of progression from intact IC to impaired IC and from impaired IC to death than those with low social participation over three-year period. Moderate social participation (HR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.39, 0.98) and high social participation (HR: 0.61, 95% CI: 0.39, 0.96) were related to reduced probabilities for progression from impaired IC to death. High social participation was also related to a 22% reduction of probability for progression from intact IC to impaired IC (HR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.62, 0.98). Those with moderate or high social participation had longer total life expectancy and life expectancy of intact IC than those with low social participation.ConclusionSocial participation could slow down the IC declines, mitigate mortality, and prolong life expectancy. The findings provide evidence to call for all sectors to embed social participation into healthcare and pension systems to promote healthy, active, and successful ageing, and ultimately support the achievement of universal health coverage.Keywords: Social participation, Intrinsic capacity, Life expectancy, Multistate model

Introduction

Intrinsic capacity (IC) refers to the composite of all the physical and mental capacities of an individual [1], which is one of the important concepts of healthy ageing. Impaired IC is related to increased risks of hospital admission, disability, and mortality, which overwhelm individuals, families, and healthcare systems [2]. There is a declining trend of IC with the rise of age. However, heterogeneity of individual characteristics and environment contributes to the difference in trajectories of IC. To help older adults remain healthy and active, WHO put forward the concept of active ageing, which sought to optimize opportunities for health, participation and security in older adults [3]. Previous evidence suggested that social participation was linked to health outcomes by promoting emotion, cognition, resilience, social support, social connection, and better access to healthcare services [4,5]. Social participation is also beneficial to facilitate social inclusion, mitigate health inequality, and promote well-being. As an important constituent of active ageing, social participation is called for to be embedded in health systems to achieve universal health coverage in 2030 [6,7]. Aligned with the 2030 Agenda, promoting social participation of older adults has been explicitly proposed as one of the goals of developing the care service systems for older adults in China [8].

Impaired IC might be reversible if supported with good healthcare, friendly environments, and sufficient social support. As one of the critical and modifiable components of social determinants, social participation has aroused public concern. Studies in Japan [9], Singapore [10], and Mexico [11] observed the positive association between social participation and IC. A systematic review summarized the social determinants of IC and found that more social participation was related to better IC [12]. However, most studies were based on the cross-sectional design and focused on the single-point and static measurement of IC, overlooking the longitudinal changes of IC. The intricate development of IC includes forward and backward transitions, while previous studies only took the progression of IC into consideration, ignoring the reversion of IC and failing to comprehensively elucidate the dynamic relationship between social participation and development of IC. Moreover, evidence of social participation with IC from China is still limited. To address the aforementioned gaps, the objective of this study was to investigate the link of social participation with progression and reversion of IC in Chinese older adults based on a longitudinal prospective cohort study.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was based on China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), which collected the health and family information of those aged 45 years and above. The baseline survey (Wave 1) of CHARLS began in 2011, and probability-proportional-to-size sampling method was used to choose participants, covering 450 villages/communities, 150 counties/districts, 28 provinces, and 17708 participants from 10257 households [13,14]. Follow-up surveys of CHARLS were conducted every two to three years. Nowadays, CHARLS has conducted six waves of surveys. Because only Wave 1 (2011–2012), Wave 2 (2013–2014), and Wave 3 (2015–2016) conducted physical examinations for participants, this study abstracted the data of Waves 1–3 to assess IC. Those who were recruited and aged 60 years old and above in Wave 1 were included, and those with missing data of IC in Wave 1 (N = 3512), with incomplete data of IC both in Wave 2 and Wave 3 (N = 821), or with missing information of social participation (N = 2) were excluded (Fig. 1). Finally, there were 2955 older adults included in this study. Comparison of the characteristics between the included and excluded participants was showed in Table S1.

Flowchart of participants’ selection.

Outcomes: states of IC

IC was measured by locomotion, vitality, cognition, psychological capacity, and sensory (vision and hearing) [15]. Each subdomain was scored 1 point if it was intact function, while it was scored 0 point if it was impaired function. The total score of IC ranged from 0 to 6 points. Those with 6 points were classified as intact IC, and those with less than 6 points were classified as impaired IC [16].

Locomotion

Locomotion was measured by five times sit-to-stand test (SST) independently. Participants crossed their arms over their chest and sat up straight as fast as possible five times without stopping and without using their hands to support themselves [13]. Those with an average time of five times SST equal to or less than 14 s were with intact locomotion (coded as 1), while those with an average time of five times SST more than 14 s were with impaired locomotion (coded as 0) [15].

Vitality

Vitality was measured by body mass index (BMI), which was calculated on the basis of weight and height. Participants took off their shoes and stood on the calibrated weighing scale to measure weight [13]. Participants took off their shoes, placed their heels together, and spread their toes at a 60-degree angle to measure height [13]. Those with a BMI equal to or higher than 18.5 kg/m2 were with intact vitality (coded as 1), while those with a BMI less than 18.5 kg/m2 were defined as underweight (impaired vitality, coded as 0) [17].

Cognition

Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status (TICS), word recall, and figure drawing were leveraged to assess cognitive function. Participants were asked for year, month, day, day of the week, and season to assess orientation, with one point for each correct item [18]. Five times of serial subtraction of 7 from 100 were used to assess computation, with one point for each correct operation [18]. In word recall, interviewers read 10 words to participants, and participants were asked to recall the aforementioned 10 words immediately and four to ten minutes later. One point was assigned if participants answered one word correctly, and the average point of immediate and delayed recall ranged from 0 to 10 points [19]. For figure drawing, participants were asked to draw two overlapped pentagons. If participants drew the picture successfully, they were scored 1 point, otherwise, they were scored 0 point [19]. The score range of cognitive capacity was 0–21 points. Participants were further divided into subgroups of age by 5-year intervals. The cognitive scores below one standard deviation (SD) of the corresponding age subgroup were classified as impaired cognition (coded as 0), otherwise they had intact cognition (coded as 1) [20,21].

Psychological capacity

The Centre for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) was applied to measure psychological capacity. Participants were asked how they felt and behaved during the last week. CESD-10 measured depression with ten items, among which two items were reverse-scored [19]. The frequency of each item was scored 0−3. The score range of CESD-10 was 0–30 points. Those with equal to or more than 10 points were considered as depressed (impaired psychological capacity, coded as 0), otherwise they had intact psychological capacity (coded as 1) [22].

Vision

Participants were asked how good their eyesight was for seeing things at a distance or up close. If participants responded they had excellent, very good, good, or fair eyesight for seeing things both at a distance and up close, they had intact vision (coded as 1). If participants responded they had poor eyesight for seeing things either at a distance or up close, they had impaired vision (coded as 0) [16].

Hearing

Participants were asked how good their hearing was. If participants responded they had excellent, very good, good, or fair hearing, they had intact hearing (coded as 1). If participants responded they had poor hearing, they had impaired hearing (coded as 0) [16].

Exposure: social participation

The type and frequency of social activity were considered at the same time to measure social participation. There were six types of social activities that required face-to-face interaction with others in the last month, covering (1) interacted with friends, (2) played Ma-jong/chess/cards or went to community club, (3) went to a sport, social, or other kind of club, (4) took part in a community-related organization, (5) did voluntary or charity work, and (6) attended an educational or training course. The frequency of each social activity included none = 0, not regularly = 1, almost every week = 2, and almost daily = 3. Social participation index was calculated by summing up the frequencies of six types of social activities with reference to a previous study [23]. Social participation index was further categorized into low, moderate, and high levels by tertiles [23].

Covariates

Factors related to IC and social participation were considered as covariates, containing age, gender, residence, educational level, current smoking status, current drinking status, multimorbidity, and per capita consumption expenditure at baseline. For educational level, those without formal education were classified as illiterate, otherwise they were classified as literate. Multimorbidity refers to the coexistence of ≥2 kinds of chronic diseases. With reference to the previous study [24], consumption expenditure was used to measure economic status because it could measure the long-term resources better and with less measurement error than income. Per capita consumption expenditure was divided into low and high levels by median.

Statistical analysis

Participants were grouped into IC states at baseline, and independent samples t-test and Pearson’s chi-square test were adopted to the distribution of IC in different groups. Missing information of covariates was imputed with multiple imputation by chained equations. This study did not include the survey weight when fitting the regression models. Multistate Markov model was exerted to verify the association of social participation with progression and reversion of IC, which was based on the assumption that the future state only depended on the current state and with no relationship to historical states. A three-state model (intact IC, impaired IC, and death) was conducted to assess the transitions of IC and examine how social participation influenced different IC state transitions simultaneously. In the multistate model, intact IC and impaired IC were considered as transient states, and death was the absorbing state. Three-year transition probabilities, hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. To better quantify the effects of social participation on IC, the total life expectancy and life expectancy of different IC states for each subpopulation at ages 65, 70, and 75 were estimated.

Several sensitive analyses were conducted. First, associations of social participation with transitions of each subdomain of IC (locomotion, vitality, cognition, psychological capacity, vision, and hearing) were investigated. Second, social participation index was used as a continuous variable to explore the association between social participation index and total score of IC in Wave 1 based on ordered logistic regression. In the ordered logistic regression, number of chronic diseases was used to measure chronic diseases status rather than multimorbidity.

The msm and elect packages of R 4.2.2 were applied to construct multistate model and estimate life expectancy. Two-tailed P-value lower than 0.05 was statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics

Of the 2955 older adults, 31.71% had intact IC and 68.29% had impaired IC at baseline, 34.85%, 60.61%, and 4.54% were in the states of intact IC, impaired IC, and death in Wave 2, and 33.39%, 60.26%, and 6.35% were in the states of intact IC, impaired IC, and death in Wave 3, respectively (Table S2-S3). Compared with those with intact IC, those with impaired IC were more likely to be older, female, rural dwellers, illiterate, non-drinkers, with multimorbidity, with low consumption expenditure, and with low social participation (Table 1).

Transition probabilities

Those with moderate or high social participation had higher likelihoods of residing in intact IC (0.449 and 0.517) and reversion from impaired IC to intact IC (0.276 and 0.283), while had lower probabilities of progression from intact IC to impaired IC (0.497 and 0.439) and from impaired IC to death (0.056 and 0.073) than those with low social participation over three-year period (Fig. 2).

Three-year transition probabilities of IC among all participants (A), those with low (B), moderate (C), and high social participation (D).

Effects of social participation on transitions of IC

Moderate social participation was related to a 38% reduction of probability for progression from impaired IC to death (HR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.39, 0.98). However, the significant effects of moderate social participation on the transitions from intact IC to impaired IC, from intact IC to death, and from impaired IC to intact IC were not observed. High social participation was related to 22% and 39% reductions of probabilities for progression from intact IC to impaired IC (HR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.62, 0.98) and from impaired IC to death (HR: 0.61, 95% CI: 0.39, 0.96), respectively. However, the significant effects of high social participation on the transitions from intact IC to death and from impaired IC to intact IC were not observed (Fig. 3).

Effects of social participation on transitions of IC.

Life expectancy of different IC states

Overall, those with moderate or high social participation had longer life expectancy of intact IC and total life expectancy at ages 65, 70, and 75 years than those with low social participation. Life expectancy of intact IC in female was shorter than that in male, while total life expectancy in female was longer than male. Those who were urban dwellers, literate, non-smoker, non-drinkers, without multimorbidity, with high consumption expenditure, and with high social participation had longer life expectancy of intact IC and total life expectancy than those who were rural dwellers, illiterate, smokers, drinkers, with multimorbidity, with low consumption expenditure, and with low social participation (Fig. 4, Table S4-S6).

Sensitive analysis

Those with moderate or high social participation had decreased likelihoods of progression of vitality, cognition, psychological capacity, and hearing decline, and those with moderate social participation had increased likelihood of reversion from impaired locomotion to intact locomotion (Table S7-S24). Social participation index was the protective factor for the reduction of total score of IC (Table S25-S26).

Discussion

High social participation was related to lower IC transition probabilities, and had protective effects on the progression from impaired IC to death. Those with moderate or high social participation had longer life expectancy of intact IC and total life expectancy than those with low social participation.

This study indicated that moderate or high social participation was the protective factor for the progression of IC decline. Emerging literature has provided evidence for the beneficial effects of social participation on the therapy intervention and rehabilitation of early dementia, stroke, and arthritis [[25], [26], [27]]. Social participation can influence IC through direct and indirect pathways. For the direct ways, chronic inflammation is one of the potential biological mechanisms related to IC decline [28]. A study in France indicated that plasma inflammation-related biomarkers were related to lower levels and faster decline rates of IC [29]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines can influence muscle atrophy by activating a series of signaling pathways, destroying the balance between muscle protein synthesis and degradation, increasing skeletal muscle proteolysis, and changing the metabolic state of other tissues or cells [28,30]. Chronic inflammation happens in both the peripheral and the central auditory system, which contributes to the development of hearing loss [31]. For the indirect pathways, task-oriented social participation can exercise several cognitive domains, which are helpful to sustain cognitive function and further improve the resilience of cognitive decline [32,33]. The 1970 British Cohort Study reported that life-course social participation encouraged people to conduct physical activities in middle life, which were beneficial to sustain the functions of muscles and joints and keep active [34]. One cross-sectional study in the US also indicated that social participation was related to improved gait speed, which is the surrogate indicator of overall health and reflects both physical health and social health [35].

Older people are more susceptible to social isolation due to the loss of work-related social connections after retirement, falling behind the rapidly updating digital technology, and the subjective and objective functional decline. Social participation is conducive to improving the social contact and networks of older adults with other people, accumulating social capital, and getting access to emotional, financial, and care support from friends, communities, and societies [36,37]. In addition, social participation provides opportunities to gain a sense of fulfilment by active and engaged activities, which are helpful to reduce the risks of mental health problems. It can empower older adults and affirm their social values. When older adults take part in social activities, they perceive a positive social role and evaluate their health status in an active perspective, which is beneficial to increase psychological resilience [38,39]. In addition, those with social participation might have higher self-efficacy and exert subjective initiative, they can fully use social support, resources, and strategies to improve or sustain their health status and slow down the IC decline. Social participation can also promote appetite by avoiding negative emotions, improving healthy literacy, and keeping healthy lifestyles under mutual peer effects, such as carrying balanced dietary habits and increasing physical activities.

This study illustrated that women had longer total life expectancy than men, while had shorter life expectancy of intact IC. Previous studies also indicated that social participation could extend life expectancy through physiological, behavioral, and psychological pathways [36,40,41]. The gender paradox in life expectancy is ascribed to that women are more susceptible to chronic diseases while men are more likely to develop acute fatal diseases [42]. In addition, socioeconomic inequalities such as educational attainment, occupation, income, and access to healthcare resources lead to health inequalities between different genders [41,43]. We also found that urban dwellers had longer total life expectancy and life expectancy of intact IC, and shorter life expectancy of impaired IC than rural dwellers, which was due to the more abundant healthcare resources, higher level of healthcare technologies, and more comprehensive social security system in urban areas than rural areas [44]. Those with high consumption expenditure might have more affluent food, balanced diets, and healthier behaviors. Like other studies [[45], [46], [47]], this study indicated that those who were literate, non-smokers, non-drinkers, and without multimorbidity had longer total life expectancy and life expectancy of intact IC.

IC reshapes the care of older adults from a disease-focused to a function-focused perspective. The relationship between IC and disease management should be balanced. As a critical pillar of healthy ageing, social participation has been called for being embed social participation in health systems and amplifying its impact in health reform to advance universal health coverage [6,7]. WHO outlines the resolutions to improve social participation by bringing people’s voices into health policy-making and recommends the guidelines of Integrated Care for Older People to conduct older person-centred and integrated interventions to manage IC declines [15,48,49]. China is dedicated to boosting the social participation of older adults by a basket of measures, such as building age-friendly environments, providing fields and room for leisure activities, and organizing voluntary activities in communities. Two studies in China have found the positive effects of consistent and moderate internet use on preserving IC through the mediator of social participation [50,51]. Internet-related services are helpful to transcend geographical constraints, compensate for the shrinking social circle with age, and mitigate the time and resources used in social connections. The strengthening, systematic, and sustainable capacity of society and communities is still needed to deepen their involvement in health facility decision-making and facilitate effective participatory processes.

There are some strengths. First, multistate Markov model is adopted to elucidate the linkages among social participation and different transitions of IC simultaneously. The identification of the dynamic progression of IC is helpful to prevent, delay, or reverse the IC deterioration and prolong life expectancy of intact IC, which is conducive to improving older adults’ life satisfaction, quality of life, and well-being. Second, this study gives a nuanced picture of the linkages between social participation and dynamic trajectories of IC, supporting the highlights of encouraging social participation to promote universal health coverage. However, there are still some limitations. First, there is a lack of exact information on death dates and IC declines, which could lead to measurement bias. Second, there might be selection bias for those with better health status and health potential are more likely to be recruited, which might distort the findings. Third, there might be reverse causation between social participation and IC and the follow-up time is short, which might not fully mirror the long-term effects of social participation on the trajectories of IC. Randomized controlled trial and longitudinal studies with long follow-up time are warranted to illustrate the temporal associations of social participation with IC and potential mechanisms.

Conclusions

In conclusion, social participation could slow down the IC decline, mitigate mortality, and prolong life expectancy. The findings provide evidence to call for all sectors to embed social participation into healthcare and pension systems to promote healthy, active, and successful ageing, and ultimately support the achievement of universal health coverage.
