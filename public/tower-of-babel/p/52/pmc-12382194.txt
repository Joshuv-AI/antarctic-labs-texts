Waiting time and 30-day mortality association in elderly patients having hip fracture surgery
Authors: Min-zhe Xu, Ke Lu, Yao-wei Ye, Si-ming Xu, Qin Shi, Ya-qin Gong, Chong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe current study aims to investigate the possible independent association between 30-day mortality rate and the length of waiting time in older people who underwent underwent surgery for hip fractures.MethodsThis is a retrospective cohort study. Data were collected from 818 individuals aged 65 years and above who underwent surgery for hip fractures between January 2017 and July 2022 at the Affiliated Kunshan Hospital of Jiangsu University. The exposure variable was the length of waiting time, while the outcome variable was the 30-day mortality rate following hip fracture surgery. Potential covariates were analyzed including month of admission to hospital, week of admission to hospital, hip fracture category, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, age, surgery, gender, operation time and laboratory data. The association was finally analyzed using logistic regression models.ResultsOur findings revealed a positive association between 30-day mortality rate and waiting time in older people who underwent surgery for hip fractures. The obtained results showed an increase in the 30-day mortality by 13.6% (OR, 1.136; 95% CI, 1.027 to 1.256; P-value = 0.0136) when there was an addition of 10 h of waiting time after the adjustment of the covariates. The results showed a nonlinear relationship of the 30-day mortality and waiting time, while the inflection point for the waiting time in the smoothed curve was observed at 42. 428 h. Furthermore, when waiting time was < 42. 428 h, we did not detect an increase in the probability of 30-day mortality. However, a waiting time of more than 42. 428 h may increase the risk of 30-day mortality.ConclusionsOur study provides evidence of a clear positive association between waiting time before hip fracture surgery and 30-day mortality risk in older individuals. The waiting time of 42. 428 h may represent a threshold for higher risk of mortality.Keywords: Elderly, Hip fracture surgery, Waiting time, 30-day mortality, Threshold effect

Introduction

Hip fractures present a significant challenge for both patients and the healthcare system, with an increasing prevalence and severe social impact on older patients [1]; [2]. Consequently, hip fractures in older patients have become a critical issue for orthopedic surgeons [3–5]. It is generally known that patients who underwent hip fractures show high mortality and morbidity rates. According to sources, older patients have a 30-day death rate of around 8% and a 1-year mortality rate of up to 25% [6–8]. Numerous variables have been identified as risk factors for higher mortality, including older age, low body mass index (BMI) and concomitant pathologies [9].

Due to the increasing number of patients who require urgent hip fracture surgery [10]the involvement of hospital executives, allied staff, and numerous surgical and medical specialists has become essential [11]. These patients may be medically complex and often require surgery outside regular working hours [12]. Despite the efforts to clarify whether it is more beneficial for the patient to receive the surgery earlier [13]; [14]there is still a controversy over how long patients should wait before receiving surgery. In countries like Australia [15] and the United Kingdom [16], specialists recommend waiting time for surgery of less than 36 h. However, according to the United States [17] and the State of Israel [18] guidelines, surgery should be conducted within 48 h. Likewise, Chinese guidelines are in unison with the above guidelines concerning the 48-hour time interval. Inconsistent policy and guideline adherence result from uneven empirical evidence concerning the time-to-surgery criterion. Additionally, authors in other studies have arbitrarily divided the waiting times into early and delayed groups [19–21] and measured them imprecisely in days [22]; [23]. Thus, this approach reduces the statistical power to detect differences, as such categorization reduces the granularity of the data. This simplification diminishes the ability to detect subtle yet clinically important differences in outcomes, as key variations within the waiting time intervals are effectively averaged out. Furthermore, the lack of precision in measurement introduces potential misclassification bias, which can obscure true associations between waiting time and patient outcomes. More importantly, there is a lack of relevant evidence-based medicine data in China. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the association between waiting time and 30-day mortality rate in older patients who have undergone hip fracture surgery.

Materials and methods

Research design and participants

This is a retrospective cohort study on the analysis of the retrieved data from the database of our tertiary referral center’s prospectively gathered database (January 2017–July 2022). The eligible participants received hip fracture surgery in Affiliated Kunshan Hospital of Jiangsu University (AKHJU) from the 1 st of January 2017 until the 31 st of July 2022. After receiving hip fracture surgery, a 30-day follow-up was conducted (until the 31 st of August 2022). In total, 957 patients who underwent surgery for hip fractures were evaluated for inclusion in this study (Figure. 1). The exclusion criteria were patients under 65 years old, delay of hip fracture surgery of more than 10 days (Delays beyond 10 days were excluded based on clinical relevance and supporting literature, as prolonged preoperative delays are associated with poorer outcomes. Specifically, such delays increase the risk of pneumonia, pressure ulcers, and thromboembolic events due to immobilization, elevate the likelihood of underlying medical complications requiring stabilization, and lead to progressive deconditioning and functional decline. These concerns are supported by the literature, including the study by Pincus et al. [4]), exhibition of extreme values or incomplete data (Extreme values were defined as those beyond 3 standard deviations from the mean or clinically implausible [e.g., negative waiting times], and their exclusion was pre-specified in the analysis plan), and not being clinically representative of the majority of hip fractures (e.g., pathological, periprosthetic, or high-energy trauma fractures). 818 patients matched the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study participants were identified through the Regional Health Registration Platform (RHRP) of Kunshan City and the Population Death Registration System (PDRS) of Jiangsu Province. Follow-up data were collected by linking the RHRP and PDRS to the AKHJU FF Registration System (FFRS) using patient or unique hospital identifiers, along with information on diagnosis, and date of admission, surgery, and discharge. Affiliated Kunshan Hospital of Jiangsu University granted approval for this investigation in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration (approval No. 2020-03-046-K01). The data were initially collected for auditing purposes to enhance the quality of patient care. All analyses were conducted by medical staff who were blinded to the patients’ identities, ensuring objectivity in the findings. The investigation was observational in nature, and all data were anonymized to protect patient confidentiality. Informed consent for the use of anonymized clinical data in research was obtained prospectively at the time of admission. The Institutional Review Board waived the requirement for additional consent for this retrospective analysis, as it involved no additional risk and used de-identified data.

Study flow chart. Extreme values were defined as those beyond 3 standard deviations from the mean or clinically implausible (e.g., negative waiting times), and their exclusion was pre-specified in the analysis plan. Excluded patients had clinically unrepresentative of the majority of hip fractures (e.g., pathological, periprosthetic, or high-energy trauma fractures), which require different surgical and postoperative management

Exposure variable

The primary exposure variable was the waiting time for surgery, measured from the patient’s arrival at the emergency room until the operation (the collected data were in minutes). First, we converted the unit of waiting time from minutes to hours. Then it was analyzed as a continuous variable in hours.

Outcome variable

The main outcome of this study was the 30-day mortality rate following hip fracture surgery. A 30-day observation window is commonly used in the literature to assess postoperative outcomes [14]; [24]. The mortality information was collected from the PDRS of Jiangsu Province.

Covariate analyses

The analyzed potential covariates in this study were as follows: gender, age, BMI, ASA score (1 to 2/3 to 4), platelet count, hemoglobin, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), operation time, hip fracture category (intertrochanteric and subtrochanteric fractures of the femur/femoral neck fracture), surgery (artificial femoral head replacement/total hip arthroplasty/closed reduction and internal fixation/open reduction and internal fixation), hypertension (no/yes), diabetes (no/yes), week of admission to hospital and month of admission to hospital. In addition, prior to surgery, all laboratory data were evaluated, and patients were instructed to fast. Neutrophil, lymphocyte, and monocyte counts were included as covariates to adjust for systemic inflammation, which may influence postoperative outcomes but were not assumed to directly affect surgery timing.

Statistics

While categorical data were reported as frequencies, continuous demographic, laboratory, and clinical data were presented as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians (Q1-Q3) (in percentages). The participants were divided into three tertiles depending on the waiting time. Namely, Tertile1 had a waiting time interval of 3.48–58.40 h; Tertile 2, with an interval of 58.89–90.67 h; and Tertile 3, with an interval of 90.90–235.03 h. All three tertiles showed their respective mean and SD. Mann–Whitney U tests were used to evaluate nonnormally distributed data, while independent two-tailed t-tests were employed to compare normally distributed data. Chi-square tests were used to assess differences between categorical data, which are expressed as frequency (%). A Fisher exact test was utilized in place of the chi-squared test if its assumptions were compromised.

By accounting for the covariances’ impact, we evaluated the associations between waiting time and 30-day mortality rate using logistic regression models. The results of the unadjusted (Model 1) and the fully adjusted analysis (Model 2/3) were calculated. Covariance collinearity was initially identified by employing the variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis. The following criteria were then used to determine whether to adjust covariance as the criteria were: (1) The covariate is added to the basic model or removed from the full model, and the matching odds ratio (OR) is changed by at least 10%; (2) Univariate model with a covariate P-value of less than 0.1 or Criteria 1 [25]. Thus, regarding the fully adjusted models, Model 2 and Model 3 were produced using Criteria 1 and 2, respectively.

To find non-linear relationships, a generalized additive model (GAM) was also utilized. In the case of a non-linear relationship, a two-piecewise linear regression model was used to determine the threshold effect concerning the curve’s smoothing. When a distinct ratio was discernible in the smoothing curve, the recursive method automatically determined the turning point at which to adopt the maximum likelihood model [26].

All statistical analyses were done using the R software version 3.6.3 (http://www.r-project.org) and Empower Stats (www.empowerstats.com, X&Y solutions, Inc., Boston, MA, USA). A P-value of 0.05 or lower was chosen as the significance threshold.

Exploration of the association between waiting time and 30-day mortality

The results summarized in Table 3, demonstrated an independent positive relationship between waiting time and 30-day mortality in all three models. We employed a two-level adjustment based on the covariance analysis: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for gender, age, BMI, ASA score, month of admission to hospital, and week of admission to hospital; Model 3 was adjusted for Model 2 plus hip fracture category, surgery, and AST. Our findings showed that there was a significant positive association between waiting time and 30-day mortality in Model 1 (OR, 1.008; 95% CI, 1.000 to 1.017; P-value = 0.0479), Mode 2 (OR, 1.012; 95% CI, 1.002 to 1.022; P-value = 0.0154) and Model 3 (OR, 1.013; 95% CI, 1.003, 1.023; P-value = 0.0136). These results can be further interpreted as: a 10-hour delay in waiting time was associated with an 8.7% (OR, 1.087; 95% CI, 1.001 to 1.180, P-value = 0.0479) increase in the odds of 30-day mortality in Model 1 or a 12.8% (OR, 1.128; 95% CI, 1.023 to 1.242, P-value = 0.0154) increase in Model 2 or a 13.6% (OR, 1.136; 95% CI, 1.027 to 1.256, P-value = 0.0136) increase in Model 3.

Spline smoothing plot and threshold analysis

We investigated the nonlinear relationship between waiting time, regarded as a continuous variable, and 30-day mortality (Fig. 2). After accounting for model 3, we identified a nonlinear relationship. We discovered that the inflection point on the two-piecewise linear regression model was at 42.428 h (P-value for likelihood ratio tests = 0.022). On the left side of the inflection point, there was no evidence of an association (OR, inf.; 95% CI, 0.000 to inf., P-value = 0.9863), and on the right side of the inflection point, there might be a positive relationship (OR, 1.009; 95% CI, 0.998 to 1.020, P-value = 0.1242) (Table 4). There was no increase in the probability of the 30-day mortality rate when the waiting time was < 42.428 h. Moreover, our findings suggest that the risks of the 30-day mortality rate may increase by 0.009 units per hour with a waiting time exceeding 42.428 h.

Discussion

Herein, we aimed to: (1) identify a statistically significant relationship between waiting time and 30-day mortality; (2) find the best range of time for performing hip fracture surgery before the chance of 30-day mortality rate employing population-based waiting-time data. Based on a retrospective cohort study on 818 older people who underwent surgery for hip fractures, we found a positive association between the length of waiting time and the 30-day mortality rate following surgery. Moreover, the adjusted model suggested a non-linear relationship between waiting time and 30-day mortality for subjects with hip fractures, with an inflection point at 42.428 h of waiting time. Because mortality may rise when surgery is delayed beyond this point, a waiting time of 42.428 h may serve as a threshold indicating higher risk. In short, a patient’s operation may be postponed for up to 42.428 h without an increase in the 30-day mortality rate. The study provided valuable evidence-based medicine information for the management of elderly Chinese patients with hip fractures during the perioperative period.

Hip fractures are associated with a poor prognosis if not treated promptly. Cardiovascular, pulmonary, thrombotic, infectious, and bleeding problems are possible outcomes in those patients, which might be fatal. Thus, timely surgery remains the mainstay of treatment for hip fractures [27]; [28]. One study has also shown that early operation can alleviate patients’ pain, shorten hospital stays, and reduce mortality [5]. Chinese guidelines recommend that there should be no more than 48 h between admission and surgery, which is primarily based on foreign studies rather than domestic research in this area. To the best of our knowledge, for the first time in China, the study has analyzed time as a continuous variable in hours and established a time-to-surgery threshold linked to a higher death rate following hip fracture. The 42.428-hour waiting time in this study can serve as a reference for a threshold defining high risk in older Chinese people. Another research on Canadian adults showed that postponing surgery increases complications, which ultimately raises the 30-day mortality rate [4]. Additionally, one study reported three essential reasons for postponing hip fracture operations: organizational (51%), medicinal (44%), and anesthetic (4%) [29]. Findings have demonstrated that lack of an available operating room for planned surgery was the most frequent reason for the delay (60.7%), followed by acute medical reasons (33.1%), which included the necessity to stop the therapy with antiplatelet drugs (16.8%). Third was reported the requirement for an echocardiogram or other examinations (7.8%) and clinical instability (8.5%) [30]. Elderly patients with hip fractures are older and manifest a variety of underlying diseases, with 95% of patients in the study having at least one major preoperative comorbidity [31]. A thorough preoperative examination is essential, but it can delay surgical intervention. Guidelines increasingly advocate for early surgery for hip fractures, but compliance with these guidelines varies significantly across hospitals. For example, in the period between the 1 st of December 2009 and the 31 st of December 2011, only 8% of the admitted patients at Ji Shui Tan Hospital (JSTH), China’s best tertiary orthopaedic care center, received surgery within 48 h of the admission, compared to 83% of the patients in the UK [32]. Similarly, in the case of hip fractures abroad, a 2-day surgical delay was also common [33]. For instance, in Spain, only 40% of surgeries were completed in fewer than 48 h, with a mean surgical delay being substantially worse, around 75.7 h [34].

Uri et al. recently published intriguing clinical research in Israel in which the average preoperative waiting time was decreased from 77 to 28 h. This was done by putting in place a reimbursement system that encourages meeting a 48-hour deadline for operation [35]. According to Giannoulis et al., the implementation of fast-track treatment pathways, which emphasize timely surgical intervention within the first 36 h, multidisciplinary collaboration, early medical optimization, and comprehensive geriatric assessments, may significantly lower the 30-day mortality rate of elderly patients undergoing surgery for hip fractures [7]. Several studies in Norway and Israel have proposed a comprehensive orthopedic-geriatric care modality for older patients with hip fractures since it is more resource- and cost-efficient and increases the quality-adjusted life years (QALY) [36–38]. Furthermore, individuals with hip fractures treated at a co-managed Geriatric Fracture Center (GFC) in Germany had shorter hospital stays and faster recovery after surgery [39]. Notably, different nations have different healthcare systems. Nonetheless, relevant public health organizations must be mindful of the surgery problem related to its postponement and ensure sufficient facilities for the prompt management of these older, frequently frail patients. Patients who decide not to have an operation should also be informed of any risks that might result from a procedure delay greater than the threshold of waiting time.

The study had several advantages. Firstly, instead of arbitrarily categorizing patients into early and delayed surgery groups, we used precise waiting time data gathered in minutes to objectively determine a threshold for increased risk [19–21]; [40]. Consequently, we have suggested a more accurate waiting time than other existing studies. Secondly, we gathered enough variables in this study, including a significant amount of laboratory data, allowing us to fully adjust the models. Lastly, we examined the relationship between waiting time and 30-day mortality using a linear and a nonlinear regression model.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, while we observed a significant association between waiting time and 30-day mortality in elderly hip fracture patients, the retrospective design precludes causal inference. Second, although we adjusted for ASA score as a composite measure of comorbidity burden, the lack of detailed comorbidity data (e.g., specific conditions such as cancer, cardiovascular diseases, hypocalcemia, or osteoporosis) and clinically relevant biomarkers (e.g., serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen) may affect the external validity of our results, particularly for healthier elderly individuals. This gap underscores the need for future prospective studies to incorporate comprehensive health assessments. Third, and critically, data on perioperative complications (e.g., pneumonia, sepsis), detailed hospital stay duration, and granular cause-specific mortality (e.g., cardiovascular events, thromboembolic complications) were unavailable. These omissions constrain mechanistic exploration and limit our ability to distinguish direct versus indirect effects of waiting time. Furthermore, while primary analysis identified significant temporal trends, it did not systematically account for contributors to surgical delays (e.g., preoperative optimization delays, operating room scheduling conflicts) or institutional-level covariates (e.g., annual surgical volume, bed capacity, specialist staffing). Finally, the single-center design and moderate sample size (n = 818) may constrain the generalizability of our conclusions across diverse racial/ethnic populations and healthcare settings. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger cohorts should incorporate: (1) comprehensive comorbidity profiling, (2) standardized documentation of perioperative complications and hospital course, and (3) cause-specific mortality data to better elucidate the pathways linking surgical timing to outcomes. Such studies could also benefit from formal mediation analyses to quantify the contribution of intermediate factors like complications.

Conclusions

We discovered an increased risk of 30-day mortality rate in the elderly, associated with a longer delay before the operation of hip fractures. Therefore, A waiting time of 42.428 h may serve as a reference for a threshold indicating elevated mortality. Herein, this study identifies key factors associated with mortality risk in elderly Chinese patients with hip fractures, offering insights for targeted interventions. Further research is needed to confirm the results of this study through a prospective study, which will ensure their validity and applicability.
