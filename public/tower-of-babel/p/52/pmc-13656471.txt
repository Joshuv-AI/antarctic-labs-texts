Mortality predictors in cancer patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation for aortic stenosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Azin Alizadehasl, Yasamin Afsari Zonooz, Golnar Mortaz Hejri, Hoda Hakimian, Alia Bahramnejad, Ehsan Amini-Salehi, Seyyed Mohammad Hashemi, Roozbeh Narimani-Javid, Seyedeh Fatemeh Hosseini Jebelli, Azam Yalameh Aliabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe coexistence of cancer and aortic stenosis is increasingly common in elderly patients, presenting unique challenges for management. Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) offers a less invasive alternative to surgical valve replacement, but the impact of various clinical factors on post-TAVI mortality in cancer patients remains unclear.ObjectiveTo systematically review and meta-analyze the predictors of mortality in cancer patients undergoing TAVI for aortic stenosis.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted in Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase up to May 2025. Studies assessing mortality predictors in cancer patients post-TAVI were included. Data extraction and quality assessment were performed independently by two reviewers. Pooled hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using random-effects models. Sensitivity analyses addressed within-study correlation assumptions, and heterogeneity was assessed using I² and τ² statistics.ResultsFive high-quality case-control studies, encompassing over 7,000 patients, were included. Advanced NYHA class (III/IV), prior myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, COPD, diabetes mellitus, and higher STS scores were robustly associated with increased mortality. Active cancer conferred a significantly higher mortality risk (HR 1.64, 95% CI: 1.17–2.30), while preserved left ventricular ejection fraction and renal function were linked to improved survival. Most predictors remained stable across sensitivity analyses, although the impact of cancer status varied with correlation assumptions.ConclusionBoth traditional cardiovascular and cancer-specific factors significantly influence mortality in cancer patients undergoing TAVI. These findings underscore the need for individualized, multidisciplinary risk assessment and highlight the importance of further research to refine patient selection and optimize outcomes in this growing patient population.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40959-026-00527-6.

Introduction

Even though significant improvements in the management of chronic diseases, cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and cancer are still two of the most pressing international health issues that lead to an enormous global burden even in most developed countries [1, 2], cardiovascular diseases involve a wide range of conditions such as valve diseases which hold significant clinical importance, among them aortic stenosis is one of the most prevalent in the general population [3].

Cancer and aortic stenosis can frequently coexist since their prevalence is higher in elderly people [1, 4]. Accordingly, there are multiple mechanisms contributing to this, such as an inflammatory state, key risk factors, and valve-toxic effects of therapies, particularly chest radiotherapy [2]. Regarding this, a standard evaluation before transaortic valve implantation (TAVI) consists of chest, abdomen, and pelvis CT scans [5].

Surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) has traditionally been the gold standard treatment for aortic stenosis, but certain morbidities can impact the feasibility of this strategy (SARI). Notably, the presence of active malignancies in patients is considered to be a major contraindication for SAVR in clinical practice. Approximately, TAVI is known to be a less invasive operation than SAVR for aortic stenosis with a shorter hospitalization period and faster recovery, which can be beneficial for elderly patients with intermediate to high operative risk and may better align with their overall clinical management [1, 6, 7]. Despite its minimally invasive nature, TAVI is not without risks, and it can lead to several complications that have a crucial impact on patients’ outcomes. This demonstrates the need for meticulous patient selection, taking into account each patient’s unique comorbidities and clinical characteristics and the safety of the procedure [8].

The presence of concurrent malignancy appears to significantly reduce the survival rate of patients undergoing TAVI. This raises an ongoing discussion regarding the appropriateness of this advanced and expensive treatment for aortic stenosis in these cases [9]. However, some studies have shown that the prognosis of cancer patients with concurrent, untreated AS is worse than that of treated patients [7]. The mortality rate can be affected by different variables such as patient sex, age, BMI, comorbidities, etc., which have been reviewed in various studies [10].

This meta-analysis aims to thoroughly analyze and review the variables influencing mortality rates among cancer patients undergoing TAVI for the treatment of aortic stenosis. To the best of our knowledge, there is a notable absence of a comprehensive investigation examining the impact of different factors on post-TAVI mortality in cancer patients, indicating the need for more exploration of this issue.

Methods

The current systematic review and meta-analysis were carried out per the 2020 PRISMA guidelines [11]. To reduce publication bias, two researchers independently conducted all stages of article search, evaluation, selection, and data extraction, resolving any discrepancies through discussion until a consensus was reached.

Search strategy and selection criteria

To identify the documents related to the research question (How do different variables affect the mortality of cancer patients after TAVI operation?), the main international databases, including Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase, were searched. The keywords included Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement, Neoplasms, Cancer, Malignancy, Hazard ratio, and related or associated factors. To develop the search strategy in databases, related and validated keywords were used using MeSH and combined with OR and AND operators. The search strategy had no time limit to retrieve all relevant documents by May 21 st, 2025 [11]. The complete search strategy has been provided in Appendix 1. All studies were imported into EndNote 21, where duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were reviewed against inclusion and exclusion criteria, resulting in the exclusion of irrelevant studies. Full texts were examined, and those not aligned with research objectives were discarded. Only articles meeting all inclusion criteria were selected for qualitative assessment.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria comprised original scientific research articles and studies that investigated the impact of various factors on the mortality of cancer patients undergoing TAVI. These studies needed to compare two groups of patients—those with cancer and those without—while presenting the full article text and excluding any involvement of other diseases. The exclusion criteria ruled out studies that did not report effect sizes, such as Hazard ratios. Additionally, case reports, case series, conference abstracts, letters to the editor, qualitative studies, dissertations, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and animal studies were also excluded.

Assessment of risk of bias and data extraction

Quality evaluation of eligible studies was conducted by two researchers using a critical appraisal checklist developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute. (2020) https://jbi.global/critical-appraisal-tools.

The checklist comprised ten points for Case-Control [12] and covered various aspects such as the title, abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, and other pertinent information. Whenever there was any discrepancy, the researchers came to a consensus. Each item was assigned a score accordingly. Furthermore, the first author’s surname, year of publication, country of publication, study design, reported effect size, 95% CI, and corresponding p-value, and demographic information such as mean age and sex for both groups, were extracted for all eligible studies.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

After extracting crude effect sizes from the studies, variables were standardized based on their names. Only standardized variables with at least one estimate from a minimum of two independent studies were included in the analysis. A univariate meta-analysis was first performed, pooling HRs for each variable across studies using a random-effects model to address anticipated heterogeneity. Subsequently, a multivariate meta-analysis was conducted to account for potential correlations between variables within the same study, incorporating varying within-study correlation coefficients (ρ). To rigorously evaluate the impact of these correlation assumptions on the stability of HR estimates, a comprehensive sensitivity analysis was implemented by iteratively refitting the multivariate model while systematically varying ρ from 0.0 to 0.9 in increments of 0.1. For each ρ value, the variance-covariance matrix was constructed with the specified correlation to assess the consistency of HR estimates for all variables across correlation scenarios. This approach enabled detailed quantification of the robustness of pooled effect estimates in the context of uncertainty surrounding within-study correlations. Heterogeneity across variables was assessed using two standard metrics: the I² statistic, which quantifies the proportion of total variability due to between-study heterogeneity, and τ² (tau-squared), which represents the between-study variance. These metrics were visualized for each variable to provide a comparative overview of heterogeneity patterns. All statistical analyses were performed in R (v. 4.4) via RStudio (Version 2025.09.2 + 418, Posit Software, PBC) [13] using the packages metafor [14], ggplot2 [15], and ggpubr [16].

Results

Study selection

A systematic search across three databases yielded 319 articles. Additionally, 4 studies were added by backward citation searching. Following duplicate removal, 213 articles underwent title and abstract screening. Subsequently, 23 articles were selected for full-text review, resulting in five articles for quality assessment. Ultimately, five articles were included in the final analysis. Figure 1 provides a detailed diagram of the study collection process.

Nature of the Selected Variables

From 5 different studies, we obtained 47 distinct variables. Among them, 22 variables were reported in more than one study and were subsequently selected for the analysis. The variables with the highest frequency of reporting were the age, the presence of cancer, either historically or currently active, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and Diabetes mellitus (DM). Other frequently reported variables included atrial fibrillation, Body Mass Index (BMI), and history of stroke or cerebrovascular accident (CVA), each reported by four studies. Some variables, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, pulmonary artery pressure (PAP), and STS score, were reported by only two studies. Characteristics and Reporting of Variables of Studies

Quality appraisal

Using the JBI checklist, reviewers identified all of the studies as having high quality (slightly biased). The visualization of the risk of bias was performed with the ROBVIS tool [19]. The complete results of quality appraisal and traffic light plots are provided in Appendix 2.

Heterogeneity

The heterogeneity assessment revealed variable patterns of between-study variability across the analyzed covariates. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the majority of variables demonstrated low to moderate heterogeneity, with I² values generally below 60%. Specifically, variables such as age, diabetes mellitus (DM), and previous interventions (PCI, CABG) exhibited negligible between-study variance (τ close to 1 on the hazard ratio scale) and very low I², indicating consistent effect estimates across studies.

Fig. 2Comparison of Heterogeneity Across Variables

Comparison of Heterogeneity Across Variables

Conversely, certain biomarkers and clinical indicators, including C-reactive protein (CRP), myocardial infarction (MI), and cancer, showed relatively higher heterogeneity, with I² values approaching or exceeding 80%, suggesting greater variability in effect sizes between studies for these variables. Notably, MI and cancer displayed the largest τ values on the HR scale, approximately 1.96 and 1.46, respectively, indicating that true effect sizes may vary by up to ± 96% and ± 46% around the pooled estimates. The horizontal line at τ = 1 in Fig. 2 marks the threshold of no heterogeneity.

Overall, these findings support the robustness of pooled hazard ratios for most variables despite some heterogeneity, which was accounted for through the use of random-effects modeling and sensitivity analyses across correlation assumptions.

Univariate (linear) meta-analysis & pooled HR variables

Among the different variables, designation as NYHA1 class III or IV, was associated with the highest HR: 2.009 [95% CI: 1.563— 2.581], p: 0.007, with I2: 0.00%, followed by Myocardial infarction HR: 2.004 [95% CI: 0.89 — 4.53], p: 0.007, with I2: 0.00%, and Atrial fibrillation HR: 1.667 [95% CI: 1.249 — 2.225], p: 0.011, with I2: 51.42%. A subgroup analysis was conducted for the cancer cohort, separating patients into ‘Active cancer’ and ‘History of cancer’ groups. The active cancer group demonstrated a higher hazard ratio (HR = 1.637; 95% CI = 1.166–2.298). These results are illustrated in Fig. 2. In addition, Kojima et al. [10] reported an HR of 5.16 (95% CI 2.52–10.6, p < 0.001) for metastasis; however, as this was the only available estimate, a pooled analysis was not feasible. On the other side of the spectrum, hypertension, higher hemoglobin, lower body mass index, higher LVEF2, and higher eGFR3 were associated with lower mortality, with HRs of 0.846, 0.964, 0.975, 0.983, and 0.987, respectively. Among them, the results for lower LVEF and eGFR were statistically significant. Some variables were associated with slightly higher mortality rates for cancer patients, such as older age, higher pulmonary artery pressure, and higher C-reactive protein levels, with HRs of 1.012, 1.012, and 1.005, respectively. The complete result for each variable is shown in Fig. 3.

Multivariate (multilinear) & sensitivity analysis

Multivariate meta-analyses were conducted under a series of within-study correlation assumptions, with ρ values ranging from 0.0 to 0.9, to rigorously assess the stability and robustness of predictor effects on mortality risk. Across all correlation scenarios, several key variables—including NYHA class III/IV, myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), previous coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), male sex, diabetes mellitus, and the STS score—consistently demonstrated statistically significant associations with increased mortality risk (p < 0.05), underscoring their robust prognostic relevance.

At the lowest correlation setting (ρ = 0.0), cancer exhibited a slightly elevated hazard ratio (HR 1.452; 95% CI: 1.291–1.633) compared to peripheral artery disease (HR 1.444; 95% CI: 1.200–1.738). However, starting from ρ = 0.1 and persisting through ρ = 0.9, peripheral artery disease consistently displayed a higher HR than cancer, reflecting a stable reversal in relative risk magnitude with increasing correlation assumptions. Notably, the HR estimate for cancer exhibited a gradual downward trend as ρ increased, as illustrated in Fig. 4, suggesting sensitivity of this predictor to the assumed correlation structure.

Biancari et al. [9] used the Finnish nationwide FinnValve registry and included 2,130 patients treated between January 2008 and October 2017. Cancer history was present in 19.6% of patients, with 5.3% classified as having active malignancy according to the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) criteria, which include recent diagnosis within 6 months, active treatment, recurrent, metastatic, or inoperable disease. Among the active cancer subgroup, 29.2% were diagnosed within 6 months before TAVI, 46% received active antineoplastic therapy (biological, hormonal, or chemotherapy), 24.8% had recurrent disease, and 25.7% had metastatic disease. The most frequent cancer types included breast (30.7%), prostate (23.0%), colon (8.6%), and lymphoma (8.4%). No detailed staging was reported. Importantly, blood malignancies were associated with significantly increased all-cause and cancer-related mortality, while breast and prostate cancers did not increase mortality risk. The study reported a mean follow-up of 2.1 years and highlighted the limitations of retrospective design and tumor heterogeneity.

Kojima et al. [10] reported from a Japanese multicenter registry including 1,114 TAVI patients enrolled between 2010 and 2019. Active cancer was present in 5.6%, with 1.5% metastatic disease. Active cancer was defined as treatment ongoing or planned concurrent with TAVI, or completed within one year prior. Cancer types were diverse, including breast, colon, gastric, prostate, and lung cancers, among others. Treatment timing varied, with 31% receiving treatment before TAVI, 48% after, and 21% both before and after. Radical and palliative therapies were distinguished, with half of those receiving radical therapy treated post-TAVI. Median follow-up was 19 months. Mortality analyses showed 24% of active cancer patients died during follow-up, with malignant tumors accounting for 73% of cancer deaths. Metastatic cancer patients had a shorter median survival (16.5 months) than non-metastatic patients (22.2 months). Study limitations included a small cancer cohort size and a lack of subgroup prognostic analysis.

Mangner et al. [17] analyzed 1,821 patients at a single center in Germany from 2006 to 2014. Active cancer disease (ACD) was present in 5.4%, with additional patients having historical cancer disease (HCD). Cancer staging was classified based on tumor size, nodal involvement, and metastasis, including refractory or relapsing disease. Cancer management strategies included watch-and-wait, ongoing treatment, palliative care, or TAVI before cancer therapy. Active cancer patients had significantly higher 1-year mortality (37.4%) compared to HCD and controls. The study highlighted heterogeneous cancer types, with prostate and breast cancers most common by sex. Follow-up included 30-day and 12-month outcomes. Limitations included the single-center design and limited active cancers.

Tabata et al. [1] from a German single center included 1,568 patients treated between 2008 and 2018. Active malignancy was present in 2.9%, and a history of cancer in 16.1%. Active cancer was defined as current or planned cancer treatment post-TAVI. Cancer types were diverse, with prostate, breast, colorectal, and hematologic malignancies common. Detailed staging showed 4.4% stage I, 17.8% stage II, 20.0% stage III, and 28.9% stage IV among active cancer patients. Treatment details included surgery (71.8%), chemotherapy (29.0%), and radiation (30.3%). Five-year mortality was higher in cancer patients (69.2%) versus non-cancer patients (50.2%). The study acknowledged limitations, including incomplete cancer subtype analysis and limited hemodynamic data.

Trimaille et al. [18] analyzed 1,125 patients from a French tertiary center (2010–2019), with 7.8% active malignancy. Active cancer was adjudicated by a multidisciplinary team, excluding non-melanoma skin cancer. Hematologic cancers (36.4%), breast and prostate cancers (each 14.8%), and digestive malignancies were prevalent. Cancer staging showed 18.2% stage I, 40.9% stage II, 26.1% stage III, and 14.8% stage IV. Treatment goals were mostly curative (79.5%), with surgery, radiotherapy, and antineoplastic therapies reported. Antithrombotic management included single and dual antiplatelet therapy and anticoagulation. Active cancer was associated with higher 1-year mortality (27.3% vs. 13.9%), driven mainly by non-cardiovascular causes. Limitations included small cancer subgroups and a retrospective design.

Discussion

The intersection of cancer and cardiovascular disease presents unique challenges for invasive interventions like transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI). This meta-analysis of 7,758 patients identified both traditional cardiovascular and cancer-specific factors as independent predictors of mortality. Advanced heart failure (NYHA class III/IV), prior myocardial infarction, and atrial fibrillation posed the highest risks, consistent with broader TAVI literature [1, 17, 20]. Conversely, preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and higher estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were linked to improved survival, underscoring the critical role of baseline cardiac and renal reserve.

A pivotal finding of this study is that active malignancy acts as a cardiovascular risk equivalent, increasing mortality risk by 64% (pooled HR: 1.637) compared to a history of cancer [9, 10, 17, 18]. The pathophysiology linking active cancer to poor TAVI outcomes is multifactorial and synergistic. Tumors induce a pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic state (elevating CRP and D-dimer levels), which accelerates endothelial dysfunction and post-procedural thrombotic risk [21–26]. Furthermore, cancer-associated cachexia and sarcopenia severely diminish physiological reserve, impairing the patient’s ability to recover from procedural hemodynamic shifts [27, 28]. The timing of oncologic therapy is equally critical; chemotherapy or radiation within 90 days of TAVI significantly amplifies short-term mortality due to overlapping immunosuppression, cytopenias, and vascular toxicity [10, 12, 29–32]. Notably, mortality risk is heterogeneous across cancer types: while localized breast and prostate cancers did not significantly increase mortality, hematologic malignancies were linked to substantially worse outcomes due to their systemic nature and profound impact on hematologic parameters [9]. These findings underscore the necessity of integrating detailed oncologic staging and treatment timelines into standard pre-TAVI risk stratification.

The risk of mortality is profoundly exacerbated by metastatic disease, which demonstrated a stark hazard ratio of 5.16 [10]. This exponential risk increase likely reflects multiple mechanisms. Secretion of bone morphogenetic proteins (BMP-2/4) by metastatic cells promotes bioprosthetic valve calcification through SMAD pathway activation [23]. Cancer-associated muscle wasting reduces physiological reserve for post-TAVI rehabilitation, exacerbating deconditioning cycles [29]. Advanced metastasis depletes CD8 + T-cell populations, increasing susceptibility to opportunistic infections from implanted devices [24].

Beyond oncologic status, traditional comorbidities significantly compound procedural risk. COPD and peripheral artery disease exacerbate the hemodynamic burden of aortic stenosis and increase susceptibility to post-TAVI respiratory and vascular complications [17]. Similarly, atrial fibrillation increases mortality risk by 67%, presenting unique anticoagulation challenges in thrombocytopenic cancer patients [33, 34]. Procedurally, transfemoral access was associated with lower mortality compared to transapical approaches, highlighting the importance of minimizing surgical trauma in this immunocompromised and frail population [17, 35].

Age and sex emerged as significant predictors of mortality. Older age was associated with higher mortality, consistent with previous studies that have shown age-related declines in physiological reserve and increased susceptibility to complications [9]. Male sex was also linked to higher mortality, which may reflect differences in comorbidity profiles and disease progression between genders.

Diabetes mellitus, while a predictor of mortality, showed a relatively lower hazard ratio compared to other comorbidities. This may reflect advancements in diabetes management and its relatively less direct impact on cardiovascular outcomes compared to conditions like atrial fibrillation and peripheral artery disease.

Lower left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were associated with higher mortality, highlighting the importance of cardiac and renal function in determining outcomes. Elevated pulmonary artery pressure and C-reactive protein levels also predicted worse outcomes, reflecting the role of pulmonary hypertension and systemic inflammation in the pathophysiology of AS and cancer.

Variables such as EuroSCORE II, STS score, and previous coronary interventions (CABG, PCI) were significant predictors of mortality. These findings underscore the importance of procedural risk assessment and the need for tailored approaches to minimize complications.

Our analysis confirmed that transfemoral (TF) access is associated with lower mortality compared to transapical (TA) approaches, consistent with findings from Mangner et al. [17]. The main benefit of TF-TAVI is lower surgical trauma, which is particularly important in immunocompromised patients with cancer. To further contextualize these results, we incorporated insights from the recent review by Faggiano et al. [2], which provides a detailed comparison of TAVI access strategies. This study highlights the superior safety profile of TF access, particularly in patients with advanced comorbidities, while acknowledging the technical challenges posed by peripheral artery disease [35]. Balloon-expandable valves were associated with reduced complications (e.g., paravalvular leakage ≥ 2: HR: 1.02 [1.01–1.03]), while self-expandable valves exhibited comparable long-term outcomes [18].

Our rigorous multivariate sensitivity analysis, which accounted for within-study correlations, confirmed that the prognostic impact of active cancer, NYHA class, and key comorbidities remains robust and independent of confounding variables. This methodological approach mitigates the risk of overestimating single variables due to hidden confounding, reinforcing the validity of these predictors for future risk stratification models [36, 37].

These findings mandate a paradigm shift toward highly individualized, multidisciplinary pre-TAVI risk assessment. Heart teams must integrate cancer stage, disease activity, and recent treatment timelines into decision-making. For patients with widespread metastatic disease or those receiving cytotoxic chemotherapy within 30 days, TAVI should be carefully weighed against palliative or symptom-directed care, unless cardiovascular symptoms are refractory and life expectancy exceeds 12 months. Elective TAVI should ideally be scheduled during oncologic “treatment windows” (e.g., > 4 weeks post-chemotherapy) to mitigate infectious and hemorrhagic complications, with transfemoral access prioritized to reduce procedural trauma. Post-TAVI surveillance protocols should be intensified for patients with recent chemotherapy or hematologic cancers due to their heightened risk of late complications such as valve thrombosis and infective endocarditis. Scheduled echocardiographic evaluations at 3, 6, and 12 months post-procedure, combined with laboratory monitoring of inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP) and coagulation profiles, can facilitate early detection and intervention. Integration of cardio-oncology clinics into follow-up care can ensure timely management of overlapping cardiovascular and oncologic issues.

This study has limitations inherent to meta-analyses of retrospective data, including substantial heterogeneity in cancer definitions, staging reporting, and treatment protocols across the included studies. The relatively small number of patients with specific cancer subtypes limits the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Additionally, this meta-analysis was not prospectively registered on PROSPERO, as its guidelines exclude the registration of completed reviews. Future research must prioritize prospective, multicenter cardio-oncology registries with standardized reporting of cancer histology, stage, and treatment modalities. Investigating advanced imaging biomarkers and geriatric frailty metrics could further refine personalized risk prediction and optimize shared decision-making in this complex patient population.

Future research should prioritize prospective studies with standardized definitions and protocols to validate and build upon the findings presented here. Dedicated cardio-oncology trials are needed to systematically monitor the impact of anthracyclines and checkpoint inhibitors on TAVI outcomes, as these increasingly used cancer therapies may have important implications for valvular interventions. Advanced imaging biomarkers, particularly computed tomography-derived myocardial extracellular volume measurements, warrant investigation as potential tools to predict post-TAVI recovery and guide patient selection. Integration of geriatric assessment tools, such as frailty metrics including grip strength, alongside cancer staging information, could enhance personalized risk prediction and improve treatment decision-making. Finally, standardized reporting requirements that mandate the inclusion of specific cancer characteristics—including histology, stage, and treatment modality—in TAVI registries would facilitate more robust analyses. Multicenter collaborations represent an essential strategy to overcome current sample size constraints while maintaining data quality and integrity across contributing institutions.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal that both traditional cardiovascular risk factors, such as advanced NYHA class, prior myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, COPD, diabetes mellitus, and elevated STS scores, and cancer-specific factors, particularly active malignancy, are independently associated with increased mortality following TAVI. Conversely, left ventricular ejection fraction and HIGHER eGFR, as well as hypertension, were associated with improved survival. These results highlight the need for personalized care pathways that integrate oncologic, cardiovascular, and procedural considerations to optimize outcomes in this heterogeneous patient population.
