Epidemiology of alcohol use in Sri Lanka: Findings of the SLHAS national survey
Authors: Bilesha Perera, Ravindra P Rannan-Eliya, Nilmini Wijemunige, Sarath Samarage, HMM Herath, Wasantha Jayawardene, Mohammad R Torabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReliable epidemiological evidence of alcohol use in Sri Lanka is limited. This study addressed this gap using nationally representative data.MethodsData from a 2018/2019 national study of non-institutionalized adults in Sri Lanka were used. Weighted data were analysed using descriptive and logistic regression techniques.ResultsAmong 6473 adults (mean age 43.9 (SD = 16.7); 52.5 % women) surveyed, 53.1 % (95 %CI; 51.9 %, 54.3 %) of men and 2.0 % (95 % CI; 1.9 %, 2.1 %) of women were current (yearly) alcohol users. Per capita consumption of alcohol was 3.12 l for men and 0.028 l for women. Predictors of alcohol use in men included being aged 30–39 years, Sinhalese ethnicity, secondary education, currently married, and being in the middle wealth quintile and in women, being aged 30–39 years, Indian Tamil ethnicity, tertiary education, being a widow or separated, and being in the highest wealth quintile were the significant predictors of current alcohol use. Hazardous drinking (≥21 units per week) was observed only in men: 14.1 %. In men, smoking, elevated blood pressure and low self-rated health were related to higher likelihood of alcohol use. Alcohol use showed protective effects on type 2 diabetes and depression in men.ConclusionsPrevalence, distribution and associated factors of alcohol use vary by demographic and health characteristics of Sri Lankan adults. Preventive strategies should be based on population risk stratification of alcohol use.Keywords: Alcohol use, Hazardous drinking, Prevalence, Associated factors, Sri Lanka

Introduction

Alcohol use causes substantial premature deaths and disabilities worldwide, with individuals in low- and middle income countries (LMICs) disproportionately affected [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. In 2019, it accounted for 2.6 million deaths, or 4.7 % of all global deaths. In 2021, high alcohol use was the 10th leading risk factor for loss of disability-adjusted life year (DALY) in people, accounting for 2.5 % of the global DALY burden. Additionally, alcohol use causes a range of socio-economic and environmental harms. Violence, property damage, absenteeism in work, and social misconduct are more common in heavy alcohol users and in communities where alcohol use is common [2,5].

A systematic review by Manthey et al. (2021) of 29 studies in high-income countries (HICs) found that alcohol use costs an average of 2.6 % of gross domestic product (GDP), compared with overall health expenditures of 14.0 % of GDP, making the cost of alcohol use about 19 % relative to health expenditure. A 2015 study in Sri Lanka, a LMIC in South Asia, estimated alcohol use costs at 1.1 % of GDP, 27.5 % of health expenditure [7]. That year, Sri Lanka generated USD 780 million in excise tax revenue from alcohol, but the estimated total economic cost of alcohol was USD 886 million. These findings highlight the fact that the socio-economic burden of alcohol use is severe in LMICs, and diminishes economic and social wellbeing of people living in those countries. Alcohol use also exacerbates disparities across income levels, age, gender, and urban and rural residences [2,7,8], reinforcing health and economic inequalities within and between countries. It also hinders progress toward several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDGs 1, 3, 5 and 10 which aim to reduce poverty, improve health, promote gender equality, and reduce inequalities [[2], [3], [4],8].

Sri Lanka, with a population of 22.2 million and a GDP per capita of USD 3374 in the year 2022 [9,10], has the highest Human Development Index (0.782) and highest mean years of schooling (11.2 years) in South Asian region. The sex ratio of the country (men per 100 women) was 93.9, and life expectancies at birth were 78.6 years and 72.0 years for women and men respectively. The literacy rate was 93.1 %. Sri Lanka is in the late stages of both demographic and epidemiological transitions. Thus, Sri Lanka is having impressive population health indicators despite being a LMIC. However, the country's population is rapidly aging, and non-communicable diseases (NCDs) account for about 81 % of deaths. There is a decreasing trend in production of legal alcoholic beverages, from 51.7 million litres of hard liquor in 2015 to 26.5 million litres in 2022, a 51.3 % drop [11]. Per capita consumption of alcohol, estimated as the sales of legal alcohol, has fallen from 4.3 l in 2016 to 2.8 in 2019 [2]. But alcohol revenues of the government have steadily increased from Sri Lankan rupees (LKR) 66.0 billion in government excise tax on liquor in 2016 to LKR 165.2 billion in 2022: a 250 % increase [11]. Although price increases of legal alcoholic beverages are probably the major reason for the drop of legal liquor consumption and increase in excise tax, illegal low cost liquor production has increased over the last few decades [12,13] and has probably led to rising alcohol consumption in the country. An estimated 65 % of the total alcohol market in Sri Lanka is believed to be illicit today [12] and evidence suggests that the alcohol consumption rates are on upward trend in the country [[12], [13], [14]]. Given the lack of reliable and valid estimates of illegal liquor production and consumption, per capita consumption statistics should be interpreted cautiously. There is a paucity of reliable and valid information of epidemiological underpinnings of alcohol use in Sri Lanka: nationally representative samples were not utilized in estimating prevalence rates and associated factors. The situation has hindered the development of effective alcohol health policies in the country, and prevented international comparisons that would support strategies to attain 2030 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). To bridge this knowledge gap, this study used baseline data from the Sri Lanka Health and Aging study (SLHAS), a national longitudinal health survey [15] to identify prevalence, patterns and associated factors of alcohol use among adults in Sri Lanka. Such information would be useful to mitigate adverse consequences of alcohol use in the country and South Asia.

Methods

Study design and setting

This analysis used data from the first wave of the Sri Lanka Health and Aging Study (SLHAS) conducted from mid-November 2018 to mid-November 2019. The SLHAS is a national longitudinal cohort study approved by the Ministry of Health (MOH), Sri Lanka [15]. Stratified, multistage probability sampling was used to recruit a nationally representative sample of the non-institutionalized adult (≥ 18 years) population. Stratification involved two steps. In the first, all grama niladhari divisions (GNDs) (N = 14,104), which are the smallest administrative unit in Sri Lanka and primary sampling units (PSUs) in the SLHAS, were categorized by district (N = 25) and sector of residence (urban, rural, estate, rural/estate) into 57 preliminary strata. Then, PSUs (GNDs) within each preliminary stratum were further stratified into equally sized population quantiles after having ranked them using an index of area socioeconomic status (SES), generating 157 strata. Within strata, individuals were sampled using multistage, probability cluster sampling. In the first stage, a minimum of two PSUs were selected from each stratum using probability proportionate to the size of their adult population, with large PSUs picked with certainty as an implicit additional layer of stratification. At the second stage, four to six widely spaced households were identified in each PSU by systematically sampling the electoral register, or in rural areas where households were not distributed by street by using satellite maps showing the distribution of buildings to pick dispersed geo-locations.

Recruitment teams visited these households or the household nearest to the sampled geo-location, and then additional households at pre-set inter-household intervals of two to four by walking in a predefined track, with larger intervals in more densely populated or urban PSUs. If they gained entry, recruiters enumerated all household residents using a computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) application running on iPad computer tablets. If the household gave consent, the CAPI software randomly selected one adult (≥18 years) using weighted probabilities that targeted a final equal distribution of respondents by sex and by age up to 69 years with oversampling of those aged ≥70 years. Privacy and confidentiality were strictly maintained during data collection. All participants gave informed written consent. An interviewer administered pre-tested questionnaire was used to collect data. The study protocol excluded pregnant women, and adults unable to give informed consent, and if the selected individual declined participation, the whole household was excluded, with the CAPI system preventing recruiters from selecting another individual. Selected individuals were invited to attend a field clinic near their residence, where they were interviewed to assess their health and collect other individual and household information including substance use related data. Individuals with mobility limitations were interviewed at home. The Sri Lanka Medical Association Ethical Review Committee (ERC/18–022) approved the study.

Measures and procedures

Participant information about personal and household characteristics was collected and medical records and medications were reviewed. Height was measured using a seca 240 cm height measure (Seca, Hamburg, Germany) to the nearest 0.1 cm. Weight was measured using an OMRON BF511 Body Composition Monitor to the nearest 0.1 kg. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kilogram) divided by the square of height (m2). BMI categories are defined using the WHO Asian BMI categories (underweight <18.5; normal 18.5–22.9; overweight 23.0–24.9; obese ≥25 kg/m2).

Field staff assessed whether participants had been previously diagnosed with diabetes, and whether they had hypertension (Details published in previous studies; Rannan-Eliya et al., 2022, Rannan-Eliya et al., 2023). They also assessed self-reported health (SRH) using a single item measure based on a respondent's response to the question “In general would you say your health is?” with the following response options: excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor. Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ9) was used to measure level of depression in the respondents (15).

For alcohol use, participants were asked whether they had consumed at least ‘one drink’ of any form of alcoholic beverage (‘arrack’, ‘kassippu’, whisky, gin, brandy, beer, toddy, wine or other alcoholic beverages) at least once during the previous year. Those answering “no” were categorized as abstainers or former users, and those answering “yes” as current (yearly) users. Current users were further asked to describe their consumption frequency, categorized as daily, 4 or more times a week, 2–3 times a week, 2–4 times a month, once a month, once a year or more, but not monthly, or less than once a year. Weekly alcohol consumption was calculated in units (1 unit = 8 g of alcohol) based on the average alcohol content of different alcoholic beverages: one drink (25–30 ml) of arrack/whisky, 50 ml of illicit alcohol, half a pint of beer/toddy and a small glass of wine (175 ml) were each considered to be equivalent to one unit (11,21). Hazardous drinking was defined as consuming ≥21 units/week of alcohol for men and ≥ 14 units/week for women [18,19].

Data were analysed on the basis of self-reported ethnicity, as ethnicity is primarily a social construct. Sinhalese are the predominant ethnic group in Sri Lanka, followed by Sri Lankan Tamils and Sri Lankan Muslims. Plantation Indian Tamils (descended from indentured labour brought by the British from what is now Tamil Nadu and other southern Indian states to work in the plantation sector during the 19th century), and Burghers and Malays (descendants of Dutch and Indonesian/ Malaysian migrants) are other minority groups (classified as “Other”). Most Sinhalese (70 %) are Buddhists, while the majority (80 %) of both Sri Lankan and Indian Tamils follow Hinduism. Almost all Muslims (99 %) follow Islam.

Urban-rural classifications used in Sri Lanka are based on administrative boundaries, which often bear little relation to urbanicity, and so we did not use these for analysis. Income levels were categorized based on monthly family income (LKR). Educational level was divided into six categories based on number of years of full time education (school or higher education). To facilitate consideration of socioeconomic status (SES), the SLHAS generates a socioeconomic status (SES) ranking of all participant households based on principal components analysis of a range of household characteristics. Combined with population weights, this was used to classify participants into quintiles of equal population size when needed.

Prevalence and risk factor estimation

The data were weighted to ensure representative of Sri Lanka's adult population. Non-response weights were generated by modelling the propensity to participate as a function of characteristics recorded during recruitment. These were then adjusted to the age–sex population structure of strata, districts, and provinces to generate post stratification design weights. Finally, weights were calibrated in successive steps using iterative proportional fitting (IPF) to match the strata, age–sex, ethnic and sector structure of the population at district, province, and national levels. For this study, the base weights for the eligible sample were again calibrated using IPF at increasing levels of aggregation from stratum to national level to match the 2019 population totals for strata, age–sex groups, sectors, and ethnicity. For age-standardized estimates, we recalibrated these to match the WHO standard population [20]. We estimated prevalence using these final weights, accounting for the clustered sampling design with a finite population correction and estimating variances using Taylor linearization. When making prevalence estimates at provincial and district levels, the weighting procedure described above was modified to generate province-specific and district-specific weights by matching to the age, sex, and ethnic profiles at the province or district level. Logistic regression was used to analyse the relationship between the predictor variables age, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, BMI, Self-reported health (SRH), depression, and response variables current alcohol use and hazardous alcohol use.

Analyses were performed using Stata 17.0 (Stata, College Station, Texas, USA) and SPSS 25. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines in reporting. The final SLHAS Wave 1 sample is well balanced with the national adult population in terms of gender, age, geographical distribution and area socioeconomic status. Less than 5 % of the observations were missing in a few variables. The means of the observed values were used to impute the missing values. The final sample was well-balanced by gender, age sector, socio-economic status, and ethnicity. The sampling and weighting procedures are detailed elsewhere [15,16].

Results

Of 10,689 sampled households, 10,247 were contacted, and 10,062 agreed to participate, with 6627 selected adults attending a field clinic, and 41 completing home interviews, giving an effective response rate of 65.0 %. Medical Officer of Health (MOH) Offices located in the survey areas were utilized for data collection. Some of the participants who agreed to participate in the research did not show up at the data collecting centres on their given date, probably due to personal issues. However, substantial selection biases were not observed. This participation rate is comparable to or higher than similar large-scale population surveys that involve extensive interview and examination component [17]. Response rates were higher in women (69 %), adults aged ≥45 years (74 %), rural residents (70 %). Alcohol use data were collected from 6559 participants, with 86 respondents (1.3 %) excluded due to missing or incomplete data on alcohol use. This left 6473 participants for analysis. Weighted percentages of the sample characteristics are given in Table 1. Of the total, 52.5 % were women, 25.2 % were young adults (aged 18–29 years) and 19.8 % were older adults (aged 60+ years). The majority were Sinhalese (75.6 %) followed by 12.0 % of Sri Lankan Tamils and 9.0 % of Muslims. The sample closely represents the sex, age and ethnic distributions of the Sri Lankan population.

The prevalence of current alcohol use (any form of alcohol use in the past year) was 26.3 % (95 % confidence interval, CI: 25.5 %, 27.0 %), significantly higher in men (53.1 %; 95 % CI: 51.9 %, 54.3 %) than women (2.0 %; 95 % CI: 1.9 %, 2.1 %). Among current users, men consumed a mean of 12.26 alcohol units/week (95 % CI: 11.94, 12.57) compared to 0.30 units/week (95 % CI: 0.29, 0.32) for women. Two hundred and seventy five of the total of 1644 current male alcohol users was hazardous drinkers. The prevalence of hazardous drinking in current male alcohol users was 14.1 % (95 % CI: 14.1 %, 14.1 %), with male hazardous drinkers consuming 62.56 units/week (95 % CI: 61.21, 63.91). Per capita consumption of alcohol (in litres) was 1.48, and in men it was 3.12 and in women it was 0.028.

Alcohol use peaked among middle-aged (30–49 years of age) adults (Table 2). Overall, among middle aged people, 61.8 % (95 % CI: 60.8 %, 61.9 %) of men and 2.5 % (95 % CI: 2.4 %, 2.6 %) of women were current alcohol users. The lowest prevalence rates were in older adults (60+ years) with a sharp decrease with increasing age. The prevalence rate in young adults (aged 18–29) was 48.7 % (95 % CI: 48.6 %, 49.8 %) and 2.2 % (95 % CI: 2.2 %, 2.3 %) for men and women respectively. A high prevalence rate of current alcohol use was observed among women aged 30–39 years (3.2 %, 95 % CI: 2.9 %, 3.5 %). Among current male users, the prevalence of hazardous drinking was highest among those aged 80+ years (24.0 %, 95 % CI: 23.4 %, 24.7 %) followed by 50–59 years (20.4 %, 95 % CI: 20.3 %, 20.5 %) and 30–39 years (15.8 %, 95 % CI: 15.7 %, 15.9 %) (Table 2). Hazardous drinking rates were comparatively low among those men in the age groups 40–49 years (12.9 %, 95 % CI: 12.8 %, 13.0 %) and 60–69 years (11.1 %, 95 % CI: 11.0 %, 11.2 %). None of the women surveyed reported hazardous drinking behaviour.

In men, the prevalence of current alcohol use was relatively higher (about 50 % of the sample subjects were current alcohol users) among those with secondary school education (grade 6 up to passed A/L) compared to others. Among women, however, a noteworthy overall higher prevalence of current alcohol use was observed among those who have tertiary level of educational qualifications (3.8 %0.95 % CI: 3.6 %, 4.0 %). In men, hazardous drinking was more prevalent among those who have had no education (26.9 %, 95 % CI: 26.6 %, 27.2 %) and among those who studied up to primary level (26.2 %, 95 % CI: 26.0 %, 26.3 %) (Table 3). In men, people in middle wealth quartile have the highest prevalence rate (57.6 %; 95 % CI: 57.5 %, 57.7 %). In women, the prevalence rate was highest in the richest wealth quintile (4.2 %; 95 % CI: 4.1 %, 4.2 %). In men, the prevalence of hazardous drinking in current drinkers was highest among those in the poorest wealth quintile (21.3 %, 95 % CI: 21.2 %, 21.4 %) (Table 3).

Those who were currently married had a higher likelihood of being current alcohol users (55.1 %, 95 % CI: 54.7 %, 55.5 %) compared to those who were not married (46.7 %, 95 % CI: 46.6 %, 46.8 %) and ‘others’ (46.8 %, 95 % CI: 46.5 %, 47.0 %) where the ‘Other’ category included separated, widowed, divorced and those who are cohabitating (Table 2).

Results indicate that gender is the strongest determinant of alcohol use in Sri Lankan adults, with only 2 % of women reporting current alcohol use. Consequently, factors associated with alcohol use were analysed only for men (Table 4). In adjusted analysis, men aged 30–59 years had higher odds, while those aged 60+ years had lower odds, of being current alcohol users compared to men aged 18–29 years (Table 3). However, hazardous drinking was more prevalent in older age groups: men aged 80+ years are having about 3.6 times higher odds of hazardous drinking compared to young adults (18–29 years). Smoking was strongly positively associated with current and hazardous drinking behaviour of men. Smokers were having 5.8 times higher odds of being a current alcohol users than non-smokers, and among current users, smokers were having 2.2 higher odds of being hazardous drinkers.

Compared to non-hypertensives, hypertensives were having 1.12 times higher odds of being current alcohol users and 1.19 times higher odds of being hazardous drinkers. Having diabetes was associated with lower likelihood of being an alcohol user (OR = 0.83, (95 % CI: 0.82, 0.83). Obese, overweight and underweight men had lower odds of being current alcohol users than men with normal BMI. Among current male alcohol users, the likelihood of being a hazardous drinker was higher in those obese and under-weight. It should be noted here however, that a significant percentage of heavy drinkers underreport their amount of consumption, so they are likely to be misclassified as moderate or mild drinkers, leading to overestimation of health consequences of moderate and mild drinking.

Those reporting lower SRH had higher odds of consuming alcohol, with hazardous drinkers having higher odds to report poor SRH. Hazardous drinking was also strongly associated with moderate and severe depression, though current alcohol users showed a lower likelihood of depression. Results indicated that those who have moderate and severe depression had higher odds than those with ‘no depression’ of current drinking. However, among current alcohol users, those who were identified as having severe, moderate or mild depression had lower odds of hazardous drinking than those with ‘no depression’.

Discussion

This study analysed data from a representative sample of 6473 Sri Lankan adults aged 18+ years, providing reliable, nationally representative estimates of alcohol use, unlike previous studies limited to either specific locations or population groups.

The prevalence of current alcohol use and the amount of alcohol use were higher in men than women. The WHO estimates that in 2019, alcohol per capita consumption in Sri Lanka was 4.8 l for men and 1.0 l for women [2]. In our study, self-reported alcohol per capita consumption was 3.12 l for men and 0.028 l for women; lower than the WHO estimates. Given the facts that about half of the Sri Lankan population are women, and less than 2 % of adult women consume alcohol; only in light amounts in an infrequent manner, it is very unlikely that per capita alcohol consumption of women is 1.0 l as estimated by the WHO. Thus, our estimates are likely to be more reliable, at least in relation to gender differences. In 2019, estimated global alcohol per capita consumption was 8.2 l for men and 2.2 l for women. In the same year, 52 % of men and 35 % of women globally were current alcohol users, a 17 % gender gap. In Sri Lanka, this gender gap of alcohol use was substantially higher at 51.1 %, which would be the highest in South Asia, indicating that in Sri Lanka alcohol use is predominantly a male behaviour as has been seen in many Asian cultures [21,22]. In Sri Lanka, alcohol use by men is more culturally normalized [13,14,22,23] due to several reasons. Historically, the majority of women were confined to household roles, while men, being more socially mobile, used alcohol to build connections, and to signal social status and economic power. Conversely, drinking by women remains stigmatized and drinking publicly is considered culturally inappropriate for women. A study conducted in 2002 in Sri Lanka observed that religious reasons (83 %) and family and social influence (83 %) were the major reasons for women's abstinence, while economic (64.5 %) and health (70 %) reasons were less important [24], emphasising the religio-cultural context of women's alcohol use in the country. However, societal changes, including westernization and women entering the paid workforce, have led to gradual social acceptance of drinking by women, particularly in work-related events [22,23]. Although men consume more alcohol, gender differences in alcohol use have narrowed globally; exposing women to increasing alcohol-related risks. Sri Lanka is no exception.

The prevalence of current alcohol use was found to be higher in middle age (30–49 years of age) people. In South Asian countries alcohol use behaviour among old age people is remarkably low [2,5,13] in contrast to that of developed counties [2,6]. This is probably because alcohol purchasing power diminishes with aging because the majority of older adults in South Asia do not have a pensionable job or social security benefits [25]. In addition, number of social events and contacts that older adults have had in the past that accompany alcohol, decline with aging, limiting their access to social drinking. Therefore, economic vulnerability and reduced social interactions with increasing age may constrain drinking behaviour. Heavy alcohol use is supposed to decline with aging when people mature and take on more responsibilities [3,26], however, our results did not show such a trend. In men, about one fourth of adults aged 80+ years reported hazardous drinking, a pattern that contrasts with studies in other countries [26,27]. Possible reasons include the availability of cheap illegal alcoholic beverages in many parts of the country [12,14] and physical ability of those who survive to 70 years of age to engage in heavy alcohol consumption. Co-occurring use of other substances such as tobacco and cannabis may also contribute to continued alcohol use behaviour [13,28].

In this study, higher prevalence rates were observed among participants from Sinhalese and Tamil ethnic backgrounds where the majority (> 70 %) were either Buddhists or Hindus [9,10]. The majority of Sinhalese people practice Buddhism and the majority of Tamil people practice Hinduism. Both religions discourage alcohol use, emphasising the severe adverse consequences related to alcohol use behaviours, but both permit followers to make their own individual choices. Conversely, Islam religion totally prohibits the drinking of alcohol, while Roman Catholic and other Christian faiths allow mild or moderate drinking behaviour while condemning harmful drinking. The prevalence rate of alcohol among Muslims who practice Islam religion was very low in our study. These results align with the general pattern in South Asia in 2019, where the highest adult alcohol per capita consumption rates were in India (4.9) where the majority were Hindus and in Sri Lanka (2.8) where the majority were Buddhists. The consumption was very low (about 0.1) in Bangladesh and in Pakistan, where the majority are Muslim [2]. Cultural, religious and social norms grossly affect alcohol use, but it should be emphasized that cultural and ethnic norms vary by context and place, and alcohol use patterns are mixed even within the same ethnic group in a country [12,13,22]. Further, urbanization, westernization and acculturation are normalizing alcohol use, particularly in highly populated city areas. Our results indicate higher use of alcohol in districts where the population density is high such as Colombo and Gampaha. Alcohol use is also high in the ‘other’ ethnic category, which comprises descendants of Dutch, Portuguese and Malay immigrants, and where the majority are Roman Catholics or other Christians, most of whom live in the highly populated west and south-west coastal areas.

Political and economic forces that promote availability are challenging traditional belief systems in Sri Lanka that discouraged alcohol use. This is a trend in countries like Thailand, where the majority are Buddhists and have more conservative attitudes toward alcohol use, but a large tourism industry and recent constitutional reforms, such as a national policy liberalizing production and distribution of alcohol, have contributed to increased alcohol use, and notably among women, undermining previously dominant anti-alcohol cultural and religious values. The alcohol use prevalence rates in Thailand were 59.0 % and 31.0 % for men and women respectively in 2020 [29]. Cultural and religious values and norms in Sri Lanka face similar influences today, as society becomes more affluent and ‘westernised’, and the government is eager to generate income from tourism. Although it is a general belief that low income people use alcohol to alleviate their sufferings and to increase solidarity, in the real world such an expectation is only a myth [30]. Aggressive alcohol marketing strategies depicts alcohol as a symbol of attainment of higher social status in the society. The poor often get trapped in these myths proliferated by the alcohol industry. The ultimate outcome would be the deterioration of health and economic standards of the poor due to wastage of their income for these ‘non-essential’ expenses. Affluent people have more control over their alcohol use behaviour due to social support networks and peer pressure. Qualitative research on these trends is needed to understand the underlying dynamics.

Geographical variations in alcohol use need to be considered seriously in developing effective alcohol control policies. Alcohol use was higher in central hill country of Sri Lanka (Kandy, Matale, Nuwara-Eliya and Badulla districts), likely due to its colder climate which is causally associated with higher alcohol intake [31], and the greater concentration of Indian Tamils, whose alcohol prevalence rates were comparatively higher, living in the plantation sector that is mostly located in these areas. Among women, the highest prevalence rate of alcohol use was reported by Indian Tamils. This ethnic group may encounter various forms of discriminatory treatment and such negative life experiences may have contributed for them to use alcohol to elicit stress and negative emotions in them [32,33]. In districts such as Jaffna, Trincomalee, Puttalam, and Batticaloa where the majority were either Sri Lankan Tamils or Muslims, alcohol prevalence rates were comparatively low. The impact of ethno-religious factors thus interacts with other social factors in Sri Lanka for alcohol motives, and this should be considered in alcohol prevention activities.

Among men, those with secondary education and in the middle wealth quintile, and in women, those with tertiary level education and in the richest wealth quintile were most likely to use alcohol. Since level of education and income are positively correlated, this finding aligns with and is consistent with the literature [13,34]. Individuals with higher education, particularly women, have more opportunities to consume alcohol than those with less education. Educated individuals tend to have more disposable income for alcohol use, greater exposure to social events that complement alcohol, and are more likely to postpone childbearing and related responsibilities. Further, educated women are more likely to work in male-dominated workplaces where the drinking culture is normal and are more likely to use alcohol as a social symbol [14,22]. Since affordability is a key driver of alcohol consumption in countries like Sri Lanka, people in the poorest wealth category tend to drink less, but our results do not totally support less use in the poor. The availability of cheap illicit alcoholic beverages and preparation of homemade alcoholic beverages have boosted drinking behaviour in poorer Sri Lankans [13,14,22]. Illicit alcohol is common in many LMICs, and in conjunction with oftentimes weak alcohol policies, the control of such alcohol beverages is extremely difficult. Adults in the poorest wealth category reported the highest rates of hazardous drinking. Heavy drinking disproportionately harms the poor, increasing their risk of dying or becoming ill due to alcohol consumption.

Among men, alcohol users were more likely to be smokers than others. This is consistent with previous research demonstrating a clear positive relationship between smoking and alcohol use [28,35]. In districts where the majority were Tamil, like N'Eliya, Kilinochchi and, Jaffna smoking prevalence rates were comparatively higher, probably due to production and use of local, cheap smoking products and cultivation of tobacco in such areas. Smoking and alcohol may increase likelihood of using illegal drugs [2,13]. Given that illegal drug smuggling and use has become a serious public health threat in Sri Lanka, research on the concurrent use of tobacco, alcohol and illegal drugs should be a future public health research priority. We find that alcohol consumption is associated with higher prevalence of hypertension in Sri Lankan men, consistent with similar studies from other countries [2,36]. Since evidence does not support the protective effect of moderate alcohol intake against cardiovascular disease [37], total absenteeism rather than harm reduction might be a more effective strategy for improving health in Sri Lanka. In male alcohol users, alcohol use was negatively associated with diabetes. The literature suggests alcohol has a net protective effect on diabetes risk, but the evidence is inconclusive [1,2,37].

Obese, overweight and malnourished men were less likely to be current alcohol users, but hazardous drinking was more common in obese and underweight subjects. While the relationship between alcohol consumption and obesity remains inconclusive, malnourishment and hazardous drinking are prevalent among the poorest Sri Lankans. Studies suggested that alcohol intake was associated with the increased odds of overweight and that heavy alcohol drinking is positively associated with odds of overweight and abdominal obesity compared to non- or light alcohol drinking [38]. Since alcohol's role in promoting obesity or underweight is a product of many biological and psycho-social factors, further investigations are however, warranted to identify cause-effect relationship.

Those with worse self-rated health (SRH) were more likely to use alcohol, and among men, hazardous drinkers were more likely to report low SRH. A U-shape association was observed between alcohol consumption trajectories and SRH [39]. In men, hazardous drinkers were more likely than other drinkers to have moderate or severe levels of depression. However, current users were less likely to have depression in this sample of adult men. Our observations are consistent with other studies finding that reduced risks of dementia and mental disorders among mild and moderate drinkers, while hazardous drinking exacerbates psychological problems [3,4,40].

Sri Lanka lacks a dedicated national alcohol policy. While the National Authority on Tobacco and Alcohol (NATA) has been established in Sri Lanka by the National Authority on Tobacco and Alcohol Act, No. 27 of 2006 [9,12], gaps remain. The 2016 National Policy on Alcohol Control outlined ten priority areas for alcohol control, including marketing restrictions, but health warning labels and effective controls on illegal liquor production remain lacking. Further, conflicting objectives between NATA and the Department of Excise hinder progress. Whilst NATA aims to reduce harm, the Excise Department prioritizes revenue generation, as evidenced by a 62 % increase in issuance of liquor licenses from 2012 to 2019 [11]. Issuance of soft alcohol licenses in the recent past also seek to boost tourism. These actions in fact promote overall alcohol use in the country.

This study has several limitations. The data collected were based on a self-reporting, thus recall bias and inaccuracy is possible. Underreporting of liquor consumption due to social desirability bias, especially in women, may have affected the estimates. However, collecting alcohol consumption data using severity and frequency of alcohol use was a strength. Further, the nationally representativeness of the sample and the incorporation of potential confounders in the analysis were strengths.

Conclusions

Although alcohol use is continues to be a predominantly male behaviour in Sri Lanka, a considerable proportion of women, specifically among rich and educated, were alcohol users. Middle-aged and low- and middle-income categories individuals were more likely to use alcohol and to experience alcohol related negative consequences. Marginalised groups, such as estate workers and widows, were at higher risk of alcohol use. Increased availability and affordability of alcohol might endanger existing cultural and social norms that favour abstinence of alcohol use, leading to harm to health and wellbeing of the population. Hazardous drinking, which is confined to men at present, disproportionately harms poorer Sri Lankans. Effective alcohol control policies need to be developed based on scientific evidence considering gender, age, ethnicity and income variations of alcohol users in the country, and settings and contexts that drinkers use alcohol. Chronic disease burden, deterioration of quality of life of Sri Lankans, and draining of resources and money for alcohol prevention and to provide health care facilities for the victims should be advocated by health and economic authorities in country. Alcohol industry is strongly linked with tourist industry in the country, and therefore harm reduction approaches rather than total absenteeism should be advocated by health and education authorities as a realistic goal to promote population health. Enhanced coordination between stakeholders, the alcohol industry, tourist industry and the government is essential to plan preventive strategies without harming various trade and development agreements the Sri Lanka has with other countries. Such strategies should contribute to promote population health and regional economy in South Asia so that to achieve sustainable development goals by 2030.
