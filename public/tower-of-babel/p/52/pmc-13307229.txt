The 1965 “Gloria Films,” Then and Now
Authors: Jack Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEverett Shostrom's 1965 Three Approaches to Psychotherapy films were created and used as demonstrations of how to do psychotherapy. After a brief account of how they came about, precises of the three films are presented to highlight major differences among them. These are followed by descriptions of post‐filming reactions and interactions of the participants—Gloria Szymanski, Carl Rogers, Fritz Perls, and Albert Ellis. Consideration then is given to the possible impact of the “Gloria Films” on Americans' acceptance of psychotherapy as a pathway to self‐improvement that competed with more longstanding views of psychotherapy as applied to the alleviation of serious mental illness. It is also argued that more recently, the films have been used to demonstrate how not to do psychotherapy and to reveal how psychotherapy can exploit as well as help. This work is a close study of the films that attempts to explain their enduring relevance and impact, while also providing an example of how changing historical, biographical, and sociocultural contexts must be considered in any commendations or criticisms concerning applied psychological practices such as psychotherapy. Particular attention is paid to Gloria Szymanski as central to the films' enduring impact.

In 1964, psychologist and psychotherapist Everett Shostrom produced what have become known as the famous “Gloria Films” that recorded an initial psychotherapeutic interview of a 31‐year‐old woman named Gloria Szymanski with each of three significantly older, famous male psychotherapists—Carl Rogers, Fritz Perls, and Albert Ellis. Intended as demonstrations of different forms of psychotherapy for student and professional psychotherapists, the films that were released in 1965 helped to train and inspire at least two generations of mental health workers during the last decades of the twentieth century. These films have stimulated many discussions, analyses, and controversies since their release. This study of the films considers the historical, sociocultural, and biographical contexts within which they were created and have been used, praised, and criticized. Not only have the “Gloria Films” served instructional purposes envisioned by Shostrom, but they also have influenced how psychotherapy and mental health have become staples in the everyday lives of many Americans, how services and institutions dedicated to mental health and wellbeing have developed and proliferated, and how psychotherapy has been used and perhaps abused. What follows is a study of the films, their creation, and their enduring impact and relevance. It is also an example of how changing historical, biographical, and sociocultural contexts must be considered in any commendations or criticisms concerning applied psychological practices such as psychotherapy. It is not intended to be a wider history of psychotherapy, such as those already available (e.g., Cushman 1995; Foschi and Innamorati 2023; Norcross et al. (2011).

Production and Distribution of the “Gloria Films”

In describing how the idea of producing Three Approaches to Psychotherapy (TAP) came about, Everett Shostrom explained that:As a professor, I was constantly looking for ways to provide my students with direct access to what I believed were new ideas and revolutionary concepts in the field of psychotherapy. … Ideally, I would have wanted such persons as Carl Rogers, Frederick (Fritz) Perls, and Albert Ellis to be available in person to my students.(quoted in Weinrach 1990, p. 91)

As a professor, I was constantly looking for ways to provide my students with direct access to what I believed were new ideas and revolutionary concepts in the field of psychotherapy. … Ideally, I would have wanted such persons as Carl Rogers, Frederick (Fritz) Perls, and Albert Ellis to be available in person to my students.

To make a lesser version of his ideal available to not just his own but potentially to all students of psychotherapy, Shostrom took advantage of the presence of Rogers, Perls, and Ellis at the 1964 Convention of the American Psychological Association in Los Angeles (September 4th to 9th) to record the films. Shostrom had gotten to know Rogers during the summer of 1948, which he spent studying client‐centered therapy at the University of Chicago. He had met Fritz Perls, then a fellow Californian, through attending meetings of the Southern California Psychological Association and had participated in a series of weekly group therapy sessions with him that had begun more than a year before the taping of TAP. Shostrom first met Ellis when he gave a workshop at Shostrom's Institute for Therapeutic Psychology. At that time, Shostrom had volunteered to be the client in a demonstration of rational‐emotive therapy conducted by Ellis.

A little‐known fact about Three Approaches to Psychotherapy is that the client in these films, Gloria Szymanski, was a real‐life client of Everett Shostrom, and her daughter Pamela was a real‐life client of Shostrom's wife, child psychologist Miriam Shostrom (Burry 2008, pp. 52–54). When asked, during a 1990 interview with psychologist Stephen Weinrach why he had chosen Gloria as the client in the films, Everett Shostrom replied that Gloria “had been a patient of mine in both individual and group psychotherapy,” and that he “chose her because she was verbally spontaneous, bright, and had unresolved issues to discuss,” going on to say that “She had sufficient ego strength to cope with such a venture” (quoted in Weinrach 1990, p. 92). Shostrom also volunteered that he did “not believe that Gloria came back into therapy with me after the filming.” Weinrach then asked: “Why did Gloria threaten to sue you about the showing of TAP” (p. 93)? In response, Shostrom stated that “A community college in Northern California showed the film on public television for a telecourse in psychology,” which “evidently upset Gloria as it did me. … Although Gloria had signed a release that we could use the films for any purpose whatsoever.” Shostrom said that he “had not anticipated that 1 day public television stations would broadcast college courses. We just had not foreseen such a technological change in the educational process” (p. 93). Perhaps so, but in Gloria's daughter Pamela Burry 2008 book Living with the Gloria Films: A Daughter's Memory, Pamela describes how after receiving a telephone call from a co‐worker of her mother, she “stood behind our enormous couch and watched my mother, on our very own console TV, get slammed by Dr. Perls” (Burry 2008, p. 59). Pamela also describes having in her possession “a photograph of a cinema marquee in California … that announces that Three Approaches to Psychotherapy will be the next attraction” (p. 60). Because the threatened suit was never filed, there is no way to determine what its legal status might have been. However, by present standards of adequate informed consent and recruitment to participate in psychological inquiry, Shostrom's 1964 agreements with Gloria certainly would be seen as insufficient to protect her (cf., Moon 2009).

After apparently acceding to Shostrom's request that she participate in the filming of TAP, Gloria was asked by Shostrom to select a topic she would like to talk about. She chose to speak to each therapist concerning her unease about dating other men following her divorce from Pamela's father, Bill, in 1964. She did this with Rogers and Ellis, but she did not mention this concern in her session with Fritz Perls. The films were shot in a single day, using the facilities of Psychological and Educational Films located in Southern California. Part of the production studio was made to resemble a therapist's office. For Gloria, the experience was like running a psychotherapeutic marathon through three vastly different therapeutic sessions during which she interacted with Rogers, then Perls, and finally with Ellis.

It is worth noting that Shostrom, during his 1948 summer with Rogers at the University of Chicago, had developed a keen interest in the recording of psychotherapy sessions. During his academic career at the Ohio State University, the University of Chicago, and the University of Wisconsin, Rogers and his colleagues in the Counseling Centers of those institutions had undertaken extensive studies of processes and outcomes of psychotherapy that made use of recordings of their and others' counseling and psychotherapeutic interventions with university. students and others (cf., Kirschenbaum 2007).1 In his interview with Weinrach, Shostrom described his lifelong habit and preference for wanting to “see ideas visually,” inclinations he attributes to being born and raised within “a family of artisans [and] sign painters” (1990, p. 91). Shostrom then tells Weinrach that when he was at the University of Chicago in the summer of 1948, he had recorded Rogers “on a wire‐recorder … to share with my students, but that prized recording was accidentally ruined.” He adds that “I felt the loss deeply and believe that my desire to have a ‘permanent’ record of Carl led me to want to do the films that became Three Approaches to Psychotherapy” (p. 91). It is possible that the subsequent popularity of the “Gloria Films” owes much to the power of visual learning, not just for Shostrom, but for many of us. It is also possible that Shostrom, as a Californian who was active in the 1960s upsurge of interest in psychotherapy and the then popular human potential movement recognized a unique opportunity to contribute to and benefit from what he anticipated was likely to be a very popular undertaking that would advance both his professional and personal interests. Within the confines of his own then state‐of‐the‐art recording studio, Shostrom was able to ensure that these films were carefully recoded, stored, and made ready for distribution.

Gloria's daughter Pamela expresses her constant amazement at the sustained interest Shostrom's three films have generated. “Who, I have wondered, would continue to be interested in this small matter concerning psychological teaching films in which celebrated founders of various therapeutic fields … practiced their techniques on the same client in order to—what exactly—see what happens” (Burry 2008, p. 44)? Pamela finds this especially surprising given the “very little time” Gloria spent with each therapist, “particularly if one expects any unfolding” of her mother's story (Burry 2008, p. 45).

Unfolding, as it turns out, is not what was sought. Demonstration, really, is what was sought, and eventually Gloria's response was equally sought. … Who elicited the ‘big’ response; who brought forth defenses, coyness, aggression, tears? Which interview produced anger, or sadness, or confusion? Certainly, only one of the filmed sessions produced anything faintly resembling love. (Burry 2008, pp. 44‐45)

The absence of a sense of what Pamela calls an “unfolding” of her mother's story, together with her disappointment and anger over the way in which Everett Shostrom was able to distribute the films he made without the explicit consent of Pamela and her family, appear to have figured large in Pamela's decision to write and eventually publish her book “Living with ‘The Gloria Films: A daughter's memory” in 2008. In the Acknowledgments at the beginning of that book, she writes, “Many unwritten books track us in our imagination. … This book tracked me in real life. … It wasn't until I began the project that it became clear to me that the ability to liberate oneself from the past lies in the doing (p. i).

Précises of the Three Films

Each of the films that make up Three Approaches to Psychotherapy is approximately an hour in length. Evert Shostrom introduces each film by stressing the novelty of these productions in offering the viewing public a glimpse of what really happens in psychotherapy and inviting them to share in this therapeutic adventure. Each film then begins with a brief introduction by one of the psychotherapists about the kind of therapy he has developed. This is followed by an approximately half‐hour therapy session during which the therapist interacts with Gloria. Each film ends with the therapist's analysis of the session and its effectiveness as experienced by the therapist.

In the brief precises that follow, I have selected a few excerpts from transcriptions I made of each of the Three Approaches to Psychotherapy (Shostrom 1965) films that I think will give the reader a sense of how the theories and practices of each of the three psychotherapists were applied in their psychotherapeutic interactions with Gloria. I have retained the use of the word “patient” by Perls, as well as by Ellis, and Ellis' use of male pronouns. These films are historiographically important for this study because their content provides an evidential basis against which the interpretations offered herein can be considered by readers. Those who wish to compare my précises to the three films in their entirety may do so, as they are readily available through a number of online sites.2

Gloria and Carl Rogers

Carl Rogers begins his pre‐therapy remarks by clarifying that he has never met Gloria before and hints that what is about to occur will be an adventure for him as well. He stresses that it is the attitudes that the therapist exudes in his interactions with the client that concern him most. Chief amongst these is the therapist's ability “to be real” during the therapeutic encounter in ways that do not impose on the client. Ideally, such genuineness is coupled with a spontaneous “prizing” of the client “as a person,” which allows the therapist to encounter the client's world of experience while simultaneously accepting her ability to explore and take responsibility for herself—to find her own meanings and ways forward.

Rogers begins the actual therapy session by introducing himself, sympathetically noting the tremor in Gloria's return greeting, and indicating that he would be happy to know what concerns her. Gloria explains that she is recently divorced, is adjusting to her single life, and worried about how her children, particularly her 9‐year‐old daughter Pamela, with whom she tries to be honest, are reacting to her relationships with men. Gloria confesses that she recently lied to Pammy when her daughter asked: “if I had ever made love to a man since I left her Daddy”? In response to Gloria's expression of dissatisfaction with herself and desire to be told what to do in such situations, Rogers says that “I sure wish I could give you the answer as to what you should tell her.” From there, the first part of the session unfolds with Gloria talking about oscillating between being ashamed of “my shady side,” yet wanting to be honest with her children—she desperately wants to be a good mother but also wants to have relationships with men that she worries will negatively affect her relationships with her children. As these themes are elaborated, Rogers listens attentively, while communicating that he “couldn't possibly answer … for you. But I sure as anything will try to help you work toward your own answer.” Gloria continues to talk about conflicts and guilt she experiences between her felt need to be honest and a good reliable mother, while also enjoying her new single life and adult relationships. She states that “I want to be a good mother so bad, and I feel like I am a good mother but then … The minute I think I'm not being real good to the children or giving them enough time, then I start feeling guilty. … I am becoming more and more aware of what a perfectionist I am.” At this point, Rogers reflects Gloria's feelings but offers a plausible extension to them by saying “I guess I hear it a little differently … what you want is to seem perfect, but … it's … a matter of great importance to you to be a good mother and you want to seem to be a good mother, even if some of your actual feelings differ from that.” Gloria responds by saying “I don't feel like I am saying that. … I want to approve of me always, but my actions won't let me.” Rogers replies with “You sound as though your actions were kind of outside of you.” Gloria then describes the kind of man she would like to be with, but explains that her physical desires sometimes take precedence, and she then feels guilty that she “resents the children” and thinks “why should they stop me from doing what I want, and it is really not that bad.” Rogers' response is to say “But I guess I heard you saying too, that it isn't only the children, but you don't like it as well. … And sometimes you feel like blaming them for the feelings you have. … Why should they cut you out from a normal sex life? You feel that at times you're acting in ways that are not in accord with your own inner standards.”

Shortly thereafter, Gloria returns to her earlier demand by saying “I want you to give me a direct answer. Will it hurt my children if I tell them I lied to them”? Eventually, Rogers asks Gloria “What is it you wish I would say to you”? Gloria responds by saying “I wish you would say to me to be honest and take the risk that Pammy's going to accept me … but I don't want the responsibility that it would upset her.” Somewhat later, Rogers says “I guess that's what I meant when I said life is risky. … To take the responsibility for being the person you would like to be with her is a hell of a responsibility.” Gloria later asks “How do I know when I am following my true feelings”? Rogers’ eventual response is that “But you're saying too that you know perfectly well the feeling within yourself that occurs when you are really doing something that's right for you.” Toward the end of their conversation, Gloria talks about her past and continuing frustrations of not being listened to by her father and feeling as if her ex‐husband experiences only the positive side of having fun with his kids and little of the down sides of taking real responsibility for them. At one point, Rogers responds by referring to such difficulties as “that big lump inside of hurt … You feel permanently cheated.” Toward the end of their session, Gloria says to Rogers “I'd like you to be my father,” to which he responds by saying “You look to me like a pretty nice daughter.”

In his post‐session assessment of his interview with Gloria, Rogers says that despite initially feeling the artificialness of the setting, he soon forgot about everything else and focused on what Gloria was saying, adding that he thought Gloria also was able to focus on their conversation. Early on, Rogers said that Gloria talked about her concerns and was looking for some valuation of her experiences and actions. Whereas, by the end of the session, she was experiencing “in the moment” and expressing her upsets directly, with more awareness of them—going from the “there and then” to the “here and now.” Rogers noted that it would have been possible for him to have interpreted Gloria's comments about him in terms of “transference and counter transference” but to have done so would have taken the focus away from the “here and now” and the “I‐Thou” experience of their encountering each other. He said he felt “very good about the interview” and had genuine regret that it could not continue.

Gloria and Fritz Perls

In his pre‐interview remarks, Fritz Perls gives a thumbnail sketch of his gestalt therapy. He begins by stressing that this is an approach that attempts to get hold of the obvious and surface features of the situation in which we find ourselves. In contrast to depth psychology, he understands “any retreat into the future or past” by the patient as resisting the ongoing encounter between the therapist and patient. The overall aim of such therapy is to help patients recover their lost potentials as human beings by “integrating polarities”—for example, becoming aware of the difference between “game playing” and “authentic, genuine, confident behavior” and moving toward the latter. In the “safe emergency of the therapeutic situation” the therapist helps the patient take risks that transform their attempts to manipulate their environments and others for support into the development of greater self‐support that recognizes and relies on their own resources. The ideal therapeutic encounter helps patients to stand on their own feet emotionally and intellectually so that their need for “therapy will collapse” and they will “wake up from the nightmare of [their] existence.”

The therapeutic technique is not to explain things to patients but to give them opportunities to understand and develop themselves. This is accomplished by assisting patients to encounter and confront themselves. Patients' “lost potential” is recovered as they assimilate their projections by “acting out the erring parts” of themselves. Often, such a recovery of lost potential is aided by focusing on patients' nonverbal actions and expressions that the gestalt therapist believes to be less subject to self‐deception.

Perls, who is smoking a cigarette, begins his session with Gloria by asking her what she is going to do with their half an hour together. Gloria responds by lighting a cigarette of her own, saying “I don't know, but I'm scared.” Perls then asks “You say you're scared but you're smiling. How can you be scared and smiling at the same time”? Gloria then says, “And I'm suspicious of you. I think you understand, and you know that when I get scared, I laugh to cover up.” A little later, Gloria elaborates by saying “I'm afraid you're going to get me in a corner and I'm afraid of it. I want you to be more on my side.” After a few related exchanges, Perls says “Where would you like to go? Can you describe the corner you'd like to go to”? Gloria responds by pointing to a corner of the room, saying “It's back in the corner where I'm completely protected.” A bit later, Perls adds “Well, you made your way to this corner and you're perfectly safe now. What would you do in this corner”? After saying she'd “just sit” in the corner and being asked by Perls how long she would sit there, Gloria adds “As you're saying this, this reminds me of when I was a little girl. Later, Gloria adds “I feel like you're telling me the only way you respect me as a human being is if I'm aggressive and forceful and strong. … I'd be scared to death to cry in front of you. I feel that you don't accept my weak side.” Perls then asks Gloria if she realizes that her eyes are moist. She admits that she feels “choky.” Perls asks Gloria if she would “jump on me if I would cry.” When she says no, he adds “But I would jump on you if you would cry. Are you sure of this”? After a pause, Gloria says “I'm not sure of it.” Perls exclaims “Ah, now we're getting somewhere. First, you want to be close to me. Then, you're afraid to be too close to me.” Gloria acknowledges this by saying “That is what I'm saying.” Toward the end of the interview, Perls tells Gloria that she has “two poles of existence now. Either hide away in a corner or be so close that you meld into one with the other person. You travel between two extremes.” The session ends with an unfinished fantasy role‐play exploring Gloria's ambivalence toward Perls by fantasizing about caring for each other as babies:Perls: “Now if I would cry, what would you do with me”? You'd feel more on my level. I'd like that. I wouldn't have to feel so dumb with you.” Perls: “And the other way around, you would have to be my baby. You could cry. You'd like to play the baby and be comforted—poor thing, poor thing.”

Perls then ends the session as time runs out by saying “Well, I'll tell you what Gloria, I think we came to a nice closure and you gained a little bit of understanding. I think we finish this situation. Alright”? Gloria says “Alright” as they both rise.

In his post‐interview assessment, Perls says that he thought the demonstration was consistent with his theoretical approach. He summarized what had transpired by describing how “the avoidance of the genuine encounter manifested in three ways.” Gloria took control by putting on a phony mask by pretending to be frightened at the same time as indicating that she “had me figured out.” She then withdrew from the encounter by “hiding in a corner” and playing for time. Nonetheless, Gloria was “capable of identifying herself with several fantasies she had projected onto me,” especially in her “initial denial for a need to be respected.” “She wanted to play the role of the lonely child and wanted to be hugged and comforted. But here too the assimilation of her projection began to work and she began to experience holding me like a baby.” Perls goes on to describe his emphasis on the inconsistency between Gloria's verbal and nonverbal behavior (e.g., saying she was frightened while she was smiling). He ends by saying that her “embarrassment was protected by her brazenness and anger,” and that “to get to her existential embarrassment we would have to work through and eliminate her phoniness—the ease with which she can superficially switch … assume any role that is required for a specific situation.”

Gloria and Albert Ellis

The third and final film in Three Approaches to Psychotherapy is a demonstration of Albert Ellis' rational‐emotive therapy (RET). In his introduction to this form of psychotherapy, Ellis begins by saying that RET is based on some basic hypotheses, the most important being that the past is not crucial in a person's life. Of course, the past does affect a person, but the person affects himself much more than his past affects him. It is he who continues to dwell on and indoctrinate himself with events and ideas that he has drawn from his past life. So, RET focuses on the present, not the past. When people talk to themselves in self‐defeating ways, they “literally create their negative feelings and emotions and the behavior that follows.” Ellis stresses that much of one's self‐talk is sane in that a person is liable to tell himself that he does not like and regrets what he has done, but often such sane self‐talk is followed by insane self‐talk that includes the person concluding that because he has done things he regrets. I “am a louse, I am worthless, I am a no goodnik.” Over time, this insane self‐talk produces “his anxiety, and through his anxiety, his depression, his guilt, and his other forms of self‐defeatism.” It's not what happens to us; it's our view and continual repetition of what upsets us that creates our difficulties. In rational‐emotive therapy, Ellis shows the patient that whatever he thinks upsets him (usually something he has done or others have done to him), it is what he keeps telling himself about what occurred that really upsets him.

At the beginning of their therapeutic demonstration, Ellis rises from his chair, and shakes hands with Gloria while saying “Hello Gloria, I'm Dr. Ellis. Would you like to be seated please.” Gloria returns his greeting while settling into her place and smiling at him. Ellis begins the session with “Well, would you like to tell me what's bothering you most”? Gloria responds by saying “I think the things I'd like to talk to you about are adjusting to my single life. Mostly men, I guess.” Gloria goes on to say that she has read Ellis' book The Intelligent Woman's Guide to Man‐Hunting and says that she was impressed by it and agrees with it, but that she has problems with shyness when meeting the kinds of men she would like to meet. She concludes by saying, “I don't know if it's something about me or what, but I really do want to meet this kind of man.” After reflecting on some of what Gloria has said, Ellis suggests that they focus on Gloria's shyness by seeing “what you are telling yourself to create this. You meet this man and you feel shy and embarrassed.” Gloria replies “Yes, but I don't usually show that. I usually act flip, right back. I act as the other men act to me as a matter of fact. I act flip. I don't seem near as intelligent. I act like a typical dumb blonde. I just don't feel myself with them. I'm more un‐at‐ease.” Ellis responds by referring to his book, saying that having read it, Gloria probably knows that he believes that people typically only get negative emotions like shyness and shame by telling themselves something in “simple exclamatory sentences.” He then suggests that they see “if we can figure out what you're telling yourself.” He goes on to set a scenario in which Gloria encounters a man who interests her. “What are you telling yourself before you get flip”? Gloria replies that “I know what it is. I'm telling myself that I don't stand up to his expectations. I'm not quite enough for him. He's superior to me. Although I want this type of man, I'm afraid that I won't have enough to attract him.” Ellis reflects what Gloria has said, then adds that she also is telling herself that if what she has said is true, then (stressing each word) “that would be awful”! Gloria immediately reacts by smiling and sayingWell, not so extreme as that because I've thought about that too. It's usually, “I've missed my chance again,” because … if I want to, I can show the very best of myself, because I have self‐confidence and I have enough to offer. … But when I get afraid like that, I show all the bad qualities. I'm flip and then I'm so much on the defensive that I can't show my good qualities and I'm like “I missed my chance again.” There was a good opportunity to be close to this man and I loused it up again.

I'm flip and then I'm so much on the defensive that I can't show my good qualities and I'm like “I missed my chance again.” There was a good opportunity to be close to this man and I loused it up again.

Ellis accepts and elaborates what Gloria has said, then suggests that “there must be something very bad in your missing your chance again.” Gloria elaborates further by saying “Am I never going to get that kind of man that I would enjoy? I only seem to get the other ones.” Ellis nods and adds that “then that would be awful.” Gloria says that she “doesn't like thinking of herself that way,” that she wants to put herself “on a higher standard. I don't want just to be an average Jane Doe.”

At this point, Ellis suggests a kind of thought experiment, saying “Okay, but for the sake of argument, for the moment, let's just assume that that were so—that you were just another Jane Doe. Now, would that be so terrible”? When Gloria doesn't respond immediately, Ellis continues with “Would you get an emotion like shyness, embarrassment, or shame by thinking I'm going to end up like Jane Doe”? Gloria says, “I don't know,” leading Ellis to elaborate and express his opinion that he does not think just being an average Jane Doe would be sufficient to elicit such emotions. He ends by saying that, at some level, Gloria must be saying that it would be “bad, terrible” if she were just another Jane Doe. The two of them go back and forth discussing this possibility, with Ellis pushing Gloria to consider that she is “generalizing” and “catastrophizing” what it would be like to be a Jane Doe and Gloria countering that if she were just a Jane Doe, she would never get what she wants. As this goes on, Ellis wonders aloud if by saying that she would never get what she wants Gloria is casting a vote of “non‐confidence” in herself—someone who will never ever get anything she wants and “never be a happy human being.” A bit later, Gloria opens her purse and reaches for a cigarette, saying “Okay, even if it wasn't a catastrophe, I don't like the way I am living right now.” Ellis continues to suggest that Gloria is still catastrophizing at some level, insisting that she is saying that she will never get what she wants and that would be unbearable. He interprets her upset, shame, and anxiety as evidence of this. Gloria continues to reject this conclusion as too extreme, saying that she would just like to feel as if she is able to make some progress in her relationships with men and toward the life she wants. She tells Ellis that she is hoping that he will be able to help her make this kind progress. In response, Ellis says that he is trying to do exactly that. He then summarizes much of what has already been said between the two of them and reasserts his view that Gloria is creating anxiety and fear about her relationships with men and her life more generally by overgeneralizing, catastrophizing, and blaming herself for this state of affairs, rather than simply taking each possible relationship as a possibility that may or may not work out, nothing more.

In his commentary on his interview with Gloria, Ellis begins by saying that he admires Gloria as an intelligent, courageous person. He says that he thought their session provided a good demonstration of a typical session of rational‐emotive therapy. He states that he was able to quickly determine that Gloria was blaming herself far too much for imperfect behavior. He elaborates by saying that perfectionism is often a primary factor in emotional disturbances such as anxiety and self‐condemnation. Gloria was “beating herself up” for experiencing these kinds of feelings. Although he was vigorously attacking Gloria's philosophies and ideas, Ellis felt that she did not experience any of this as attacking her personally and thought he had given her several ideas about what she could do in the future. Ellis was attacking her negative evaluations of herself to show her how she might go about doing the same. Ellis concludes his thoughts about the brief therapy session with Gloria by saying that what RET tries to do is to give patients catalytic ideas and strategies that they can use to combat their negative thinking and acting in their daily lives. This is something that they must learn to do and continue to do for the rest of their lives if therapy is to be effective.

Gloria's Comments and Interactions With the Three Therapists After the Filming

In the final part of Three Approaches to Psychotherapy, Shostrom interviews Gloria. He begins by acknowledging that Gloria has really been “put through the mill” today and requests that she give her reactions to each of the three therapists she has talked with. In response, Gloria says that she “was the most relaxed and the most comfortable with Dr. Rogers” and that she “enjoyed talking with him” and “felt free” with him. She then says that she was “afraid of Dr. Perls” and wasn't “as comfortable with him” and that with Dr. Ellis she “couldn't keep up.” She “had to think more with him … I didn't feel as sharp with him. It took me a while to sink into what he was saying.” Taking her cue from Shostrom's next query about experiencing herself differently with “each of these guys,” Gloria nods and says “Yes, very much … I was my more loveable, soft, and caring self with Dr. Rogers … I even felt more free and openly about sex” which surprised her. She then says that she felt “more cold with Dr. Ellis. I didn't have enough feeling. I was so busy trying to think with him that I didn't have enough feeling there. In contrast, Gloria says that “the biggest amount of emotions came up with me with Dr. Perls.” Shostrom then leads Gloria to comment further by saying that “You have some negative feeling for your experience with both Perls and Ellis, and yet … they were dealing with parts of you that you didn't have to deal with, with Dr. Rogers.” Gloria nods her agreement, saying that “if I was just dealing with Dr. Rogers only it would be harder for my anger and my ‘spit fire’ self to come out. So, I don't think it would be as full balanced therapy, for me anyway.” Gloria adds that “with Dr. Perls, I want to get in there and fight. So, with Dr. Perls and Dr. Rogers, they're almost a perfect combination for me. … I felt unfinished, especially with Dr. Perls. I wanted more.” Gloria adds that she also “felt unfinished too with Dr. Ellis because it couldn't sink in enough. I would have liked more time to appreciate more what he had to tell me.” Gloria then volunteers an unsolicited opinion, saying that:If I were to go into therapy, especially being brand new, I would like someone like Dr. Rogers because it wouldn't frighten me so. But I think at the stage that I'm in right now that Dr. Perls could be the most valuable to me. So, he isn't quite as calming but I think I could get a lot from him, although I'd like to battle with him too, but I think I could get a lot from him.

As it turned out, it was Carl Rogers who not only helped Gloria to be comfortable during his interactions with her as part of the 1964 filming of Three Approaches to Psychotherapy, but who had by far the greatest involvement and impact on Gloria's post‐filming life. A year after the filming, Gloria enrolled in a weekend conference Rogers was giving at the Western Behavioral Sciences Institute in La Jolla, California, where he, at the time, was a distinguished resident fellow. Aware that she would be attending, Rogers wrote to her, saying that he would be using the film of their demonstration of client‐centered psychotherapy during the WBSI weekend conference. “If you attend, I would feel that I should mention that fact since otherwise people would feel possibly deceived if they were talking about you and the film and then suddenly discovered that you were there” (quoted in Burry 2008, p. 78). Rogers added that “I want you to know that I would be perfectly comfortable to have you present while the film is shown and discussed.” In fact, Rogers used their demonstration film in many subsequent workshops in America and abroad. In a letter to Gloria sent from Europe in May of 1966, Rogers tells Gloria that after watching their film, “people have been deeply moved by our contact and others have felt—especially the French—that it could not possibly have been fulfilling or lasting”! He ends the letter by writing “I hope you will continue to find it easier to be you” (quoted in Burry 2008, p. 96).

Carl and his wife, Helen, exchanged letters with Gloria every few years. On December 12, 1970, in a joint letter to Gloria, the Rogers expressed their sorrow over “the tragic news” about Gloria's son Skip contracting childhood leukemia and congratulated Gloria on training to be a nurse (Burry 2008, pp. 96–97). On August 19, 1972, Carl wrote to Gloria a letter of condolence after Skip's death: “I am sure he continues to live on in your life and that of others. … I won't soon forget your sentence, ‘I had no idea there could be so much living in dying.’ … Your spirit and that of Skip shines through the letter” (pp. 98‐99). Rogers and Gloria exchanged several more letters about Gloria's career as a nurse, their mutual love of gardening and music, and eventually Gloria's own diagnosis of cancer. Gloria's final letter to Carl in 1979 ended with “I'll sign off now, with love and beautiful wishes for you. Please know that you are one dear person to me and I hold you in my heart” to which Rogers responded with “I hope you know that you are a beautiful person. … I will think of you often [and] appreciate more than I can say the time you took to tell me of your whole situation … With affectionate and enduring caring … Carl (pp. 130–131). In this final letter to Gloria, Rogers adds a postscript, writing that in his most recent workshops in the East, “people spoke to me about how much the ‘Gloria interviews’ helped them in their understanding and growing and improving their work” (p. 131).3 Both Gloria and Helen Rogers died in 1979.

Compared to her ongoing exchanges of letters with the Rogers, Gloria's post‐filming interactions with Fritz Perls and Albert Ellis were very brief and few. The most outlandish interaction that Gloria had with any of the three psychotherapists occurred immediately following the ending of the filming of Three Approaches to Psychotherapy. When writing about her experiences on that day, shortly before her death in 1979, Gloria had completely discarded her belief that she might benefit from any further interactions with Fritz Perls. “What I needed most at that point in my life was permission to be me. Instead, I found myself with Perls in a vicious circle of game playing … I was feeling small, belittled, unimportant, confused” (quoted in Dolliver et al. 1980, p. 141). Gloria then adds thatAfter a full day of filming, when saying good‐byes to the doctors and filming crew, Dr. Perls, who was standing beside me smoking a cigarette, chatting with Dr. Ellis … made a motion to me with his hands as if to say “Hold out your hand in cup‐like form—palm up … [and] he flicked his cigarette ashes in my hand.”(p. 141)

There is no mention of this demeaning interaction in Perl's autobiography (Perls 1969) or in Clarkson and Mackewn's (1993) or Martin Shepard's (1975) intellectual and personal biographies of Perls. However, Dolliver (1981, p. 42) suggests that Perls' manipulative interactions with his clients might issue from his “overemphasis on the issues of autonomy and self‐support which … reflected his own unresolved problems of dependency.” In his biography of Perls, Martin Shepard calls this problem Fritz's “Neediness Paradox.” According to Shepard, Perls believed that people should rely on themselves and not blame or ask others for help. In both his therapy and his life, Perls regarded blaming others for one's problems as a kind of projection that prevented one from taking ownership of and responsibility for one's actions and difficulties. Fritz's “neediness paradox” arose from the fact that when Perls himself “wanted” something from others, he “wouldn't ask,” but would condemn “the wantingness of others.” The paradox was that in doing so, Fritz was projecting his own weakness and wanting onto others and then treating them with contempt and sometimes cruelty for their weaknesses and neediness, all while doing nothing to take responsibility for his own actions (cf. Shepard 1975, pp. 120–123). “Fritz's lessons of ‘be true to yourself,’ ‘live in the present,’ and ‘reown your projections’ flowed directly from his own difficulties in achieving these same ends” (Shepard 1975, p. 169). This being so, perhaps Fritz Perl's treatment of Gloria Szymanski during and immediately following the filming of Three Approaches to Psychotherapy, might be explained, at least in part, by Shepard's description of Fritz's Paradox.4

Interestingly, Carl Rogers' biographer Howard Kirschenbaum (2007) maintains that Rogers' emphasis on therapeutic conditions such as congruence (authenticity), unconditional positive regard, and empathy reflects Rogers' struggle to overcome his unresolved problems of finding his own voice and being genuinely attuned to his clients and others (also see Martin 2025). Similar life‐work paradoxical connections can be found in Albert Ellis’ posthumous autobiography (Ellis 2010) that directly link challenges and emotional roadblocks in his personal life to the therapeutic system (RET) he developed to help others, even as he constantly had to remind himself to control his own irrational tendencies. Ellis even went so far as to say that “a psychotherapist's methods largely originated in his own experiences as much or more than they did in his professional training and his experiences with clients” (p. 15). As psychobiographer James Anderson has noted: “Perceptive observers have long noticed that there is an intimate connection between the personal life of psychologists … and the psychological ideas and theories they create” (Anderson 2005, p. 203).

Also in his autobiography “All Out,” Ellis (2010, p. 555) briefly recalls some of his experiences during the filming of Three Approaches to Psychotherapy. He describes Perls as being “very nasty to Gloria” and says that “she didn't like him at all.” On the other hand, “Rogers was very nice and accepting and fatherly … and she liked him and corresponded with him later.” Ellis also says that Gloria “corresponded with me” and when she “got married a few years later … said that it was partly a result of my session with her. … So she appreciated Carl Rogers and me, but not Fritz Perls” (p. 555). Interestingly, in her 2008 book, Living with the Gloria films: A Daughter's Memory, Gloria's daughter Pamela reinterprets what her mother and family had come to call “the Fritz episode.” “After the filming, Papa Gestalt stood in the foyer, smoked his cigarette, and behaved badly, and in so doing, he handed my mother the opportunity to speak for herself” (Burry 2008, p. 67). Pamela came to this conclusion when her family, led by her stepfather Joe, attempted to sue Everett Shostrom for distributing and showing the Three Approaches to Psychotherapy films to audiences and in ways to which Gloria had not agreed. Although the lawsuit never materialized, Pamela maintained that both it and her mother's telling of “the Fritz episode” showed that “Gloria was looking for redress. She was ready to stand up for herself” (Burry 2008, p. 68). In this, Gloria was not alone. The 1960s and 1970s, the last decades of Gloria's life, witnessed major and lasting changes in American sociopolitical culture that included psychotherapy and mental health more generally.

The “Gloria Films” and the Beginning of Therapeutic Culture

It is now more than 60 years since the “Gloria Films” were produced, distributed, and shown. Although there have been many empirical and interpretive studies of these films, psychological researchers have tended to code and interpret interactive, narrative, and transactional processes and patterns evident in the films themselves (e.g., Kiesler and Goldston 1988; Moreira et al. 2011) rather than focus on the broader historical contexts and impact of the films. The mid 1960s to the 1980s saw many alterations to the sociocultural fabric of the United States. These included notable changes to the civil rights, social participation, vocational opportunities, and health of women, minorities, and other marginalized persons. Although perhaps not as well noted and publicized as changes in some other areas of American society and culture, transitions in attitudes toward and treatment of those suffering mental health concerns and difficulties were especially dramatic during this time. Massive deinstitutionalization and shuttering of the country's large mental health facilities began in the 1960s in response to increased public concerns that included anti‐psychiatry and patient rights movements. Accompanying these calls for change at the institutional level were significant alterations in the attitudes of many Americans, including those experiencing psychological distress, toward mental health and treatment (cf. Murray 2022). The combination of deinstitutionalization and destigmatizing constituted a significantly altered ethos of acceptance and concern for Americans' mental health and created new ideas and contexts for mental health treatment and care. Gone were most of the large psychiatric asylums administered by medical superintendents and psychiatrists. In their place were many smaller community and privately funded treatment centers increasingly featuring the services of social workers, psychiatric nurses, psychologists, and psychotherapists (for a critical historical analysis of deinstitutionalization and its consequences, see Grob 2008). Physically invasive shock treatments, constraints, and surgeries became much less common, and in many cases nonexistent (Shorter and Healy 2012). In this altered sociocultural and institutional context, various approaches to psychotherapy, such as those featured in the Gloria films grew by leaps and bounds in popularity. This growth was aided by new philosophies, theories, and practices of caring for oneself and others nurtured within the alternative lifestyles of 1960s and 1970s America, aspects of which gradually became integrated more broadly into American culture. In this changing sociocultural context, the “Gloria Films” sometimes have been understood as partially fueling and certainly contributing to what became, by the end of the twentieth century, an increasingly multifaceted therapeutic culture for many Americans. During the first decades of the twenty‐first century, this is a cultural development that has drawn increasing attention, concern, and, more recently, highly divisive commentary from many social scientists and the American public at large.

For many contemporaries, “being in therapy” has become the status quo of progressive Americans. Currently, there is a wide range of psychotherapies available to those with the inclination and resources to access them. These include behavioral, cognitive‐behavioral, dialectical behavioral, humanistic, psychodynamic, existential, and emotion‐focused therapies, as well as newly minted mindfulness, neurologically informed, and ever‐changing psychopharmaceutical treatments (see DeLeon et al. 2011 for an overview of psychotherapeutic growth and change from 1960 to 2010). In general, contemporary psychotherapies tend to be integrative, goal‐focused, future‐oriented, and collaborative. This contemporary psychotherapeutic zeitgeist tends to attract individuals more interested in self‐development than in alleviating suffering due to severe mental health disorders. Mental health consumerism for personal growth, life coaching, and actualizing individual potential now competes with more traditional forms of psychotherapy targeted at relieving depression, anxiety, stress, fears, self‐defeating ideation, and more serious forms of mental illness, such as psychotic, bipolar, post‐traumatic stress, and obsessive‐compulsive disorders. Many more contemporary clients resemble Gloria much more than the institutionalized patients who populated America's mental health asylums and large institutions prior to and during the 1960s. In many ways, the Gloria Films of 1965 anticipated a major change in the delivery and clientele of mental health services in and beyond the United States.

These films can be seen as contributing to a transformation in our understanding of and attitudes toward psychotherapy as the province of authoritarian psychiatric experts working with “mad” people in oppressive institutional settings or conducting lengthy psychoanalyses of a small but well‐to‐do clientele. In their place were well‐intended, approachable, and caring psychotherapists helping people struggling with every‐day life and its common anxieties, challenges, and inevitable setbacks in non‐institutional contexts. Where the former institutions and authorities helped mainstream American society to ignore and avoid contact with sick people or enabled elite access to specialized psychoanalytic expertise, the new therapeutic culture seems more likely to accept and celebrate our individual and collective struggles to cope with our lives as an inevitable part of daily existence. Of the three forms of psychotherapy demonstrated in the Gloria films, it is Carl Rogers' person‐centered therapy that many historically minded psychologists believe has been most responsible for this shift (cf., Kirschenbaum 2007). It is also Carl Rogers, who several historians of psychology believe helped to create, through his emphasis on individual self‐actualization, a therapeutic milieu that continues, long after his death in 1985, to privilege the wellbeing of individuals over their responsibilities to others (e.g., Cushman 1995). More recent commentators like Christina Hoff Sommers and Salley Satel (2005) go much further and claim that “Rogers believed that society corrupts and demoralizes people, imposing codes of conduct that foster self‐loathing, fear, aggression, and sickness.” Consequently, “we all are ‘unactualized,’ deeply alienated, and in serious need of therapeutic help” (p. 71). Sommers and Satel also argue that “therapism” is “at odds with” cherished, long‐standing, and invaluable commitments to “The American Creed [and its] paramount values of self‐reliance, stoicism, courage in the face of adversity, and the valorization of excellence” (p. 218).

In her book Please Touch: A Guided Tour of the Human Potential Movement, Jane Howard (1970) captures the therapeutic ethos that began to envelop Americans involved in psychotherapeutic encounter groups grounded in the humanistic psychology of the 1960s—“you don't have to be sick to get better,” “you needn't stop growing just because you are chronologically an adult,” and “the best place to achieve growing is amongst other people also engaged in growing” (p. 4). In short, mental health is a never‐ending journey of self‐discovery toward self‐understanding and self‐actualization. Importantly, such a journey through life was no longer restricted to philosophers, theologians, psychologists, and other elite segments of the American public. At places like the Esalen Institute in Big Sur, California, where Fritz Perls lived and reigned as therapeutic guru, such experiences were available to all, including Gloria Szymanski, whose psychotherapist, Everett Shostrom, led groups and taught at Esalen. Clearly, judging from her early session remarks to Rogers, Perls, and Ellis, Gloria was familiar with, if not an expert regarding, their different approaches to and theories of psychotherapy. Yet, there could be little question that it was the therapists who controlled the unfolding of the therapeutic demonstrations in Three Approaches to Psychotherapy. Gloria's treatment at times during these sessions, especially by Perls, reflects what by today's standards would be considered by many to be sexist to the point of being better classified as mistreatment. Ellis' occasional pedantic lecturing might also be seen as conveying attitudes and assumptions typical of men of his generation. The pervasive sexism of the 1960s is also evidenced in remarks made by classmates of Gloria's daughter Pamela while watching the Three Approaches to Psychotherapy films in one of her college classes—comments such as “What is it with this woman?” (Burry 2008, p. 81), as if to blame Gloria for her therapists' questionable or inappropriate comments and actions.

Although the psychotherapeutic approaches of Rogers, Perls, and Ellis are very different, each in its own way envisions particular roles for the therapist and the client. Of the three, Rogers' approach seems to be the most respectful of the personhood of the client. The therapist is considered to be a supportive facilitator or helper, while the client's role is one of self‐explorer and active agent. In contrast, Perl's approach casts the psychotherapist as a director and the client as an actor in a kind of psychotherapeutic theater. Against both of these positionings, Ellis's therapist operates as a trainer or coach with the expectation that the client will fit into the role of learner and follower.5 There is some flexibility in the ways in which clients position themselves within these various roles. For example, Gloria behaves somewhat differently with each of her three therapists, but for the most part, it is the therapist who runs the show. In Three Approaches to Psychotherapy, all the psychotherapists are men, with Gloria as their female client.

In her Play, The Patient Gloria, Gina Moxley (2019) examines and critiques the behavioral and sexual censoring of women that she perceives in Three Approaches to Psychotherapy. In Moxley's play, Gloria is portrayed as intelligent and willing to express with sincerity her desires and reactions, despite the often disapproving and/or intimidating presence of her psychotherapists. To Moxley, Gloria feels shame and guilt, and occasionally reacts in a girlish manner, because of her society's gender‐unbalanced pressures concerning motherhood and sexuality. The central message of Moxley's play is that none of her three therapists understands their complicity in creating the very problems from which they are now attempting to help Gloria escape. To Moxley, Gloria has legitimate issues she would like to discuss, but her therapists shirk any responsibility for the situation in which Gloria finds herself. She also seems to suggest that the therapists are more intent on seducing than helping her. For example, Perls dismisses Gloria's verbal calls for help while he carefully examines her bodily postures and movements. Moxley's play stands as testimony to why the Gloria films still matter 60 years after they were released much more widely than Gloria apparently had authorized (cf., Burry 2008).

There are many contemporary calls for psychologists to attend more carefully to the historical and sociocultural contexts within which people develop and important aspects of their psychological lives are constituted. One of the earliest examples that speaks directly to psychotherapists is that issued by Philip Cushman (1995) in his book Constructing the Self, Constructing America: A Cultural History of Psychotherapy. Cushman defines what he calls “decontextualized approaches to psychotherapy” as “approaches that imply that therapy is a healing technology that transcends the politics and culture of its era” (1995, p. 7). If psychotherapists ignore the “values and politics of their respective eras,” they cannot free themselves or their clients from “the socioeconomic structures … and the power relations of ethnicity, race, class, and gender … without simultaneously reproducing them” (p. 7). This is precisely why Moxley accuses Gloria's three psychotherapists of complicity in creating problems for Gloria rather than helping her. Cushman wants psychotherapists to attend to the sociocultural contexts in which they and their clients are embedded so that they will be able to recognize that decontextualized psychotherapies, even if “effective in producing behavior change or emotional experiences in the short run” may exacerbate “the very causes of the ills they treat by implicitly valorizing and reproducing the isolated, empty individual” (p. 7). This is an individual committed to self‐actualization without awareness of life contexts that promote conformity “to the requirements of our consumerist, stratified clearing” (p. 356). Such a person will remain ignorant of a kind of selfhood and self‐fulfillment that might:shape a new configuration of the self, one that leads to a citizenship based on realistic mutual regard and a moral commitment to economic justice and the well‐being of all citizens, rather than a citizenship vulnerable to manufactured hates based on blood, race, and gender.(p. 356)

shape a new configuration of the self, one that leads to a citizenship based on realistic mutual regard and a moral commitment to economic justice and the well‐being of all citizens, rather than a citizenship vulnerable to manufactured hates based on blood, race, and gender.

Cushman, unlike Sommers and Satel (2005), does not believe that psychotherapy and therapeutic culture necessarily erode Americans' self‐reliance, courage, or excellence, but that such classic American characteristics cannot be nurtured within sociocultural, including psychotherapeutic, contexts empty of the understanding and exercise of fundamental virtues of good citizenship that include concern and care for others and awareness of their struggles and strengths. Others, like Bauman (2001) and Beck (1992), agree, claiming that self‐concerned individualism has grown to such an extent that our capacity for collective resistance to the economic greed and moral lapses of neoliberal and late capital sociopolitical forces has been whittled away.

In this context, it is interesting to consider briefly the relationship between contemporary therapeutic culture and the human potential movement of the 1960s and 1970s. In her book Encountering America: Humanistic Psychology, Sixties Culture and the Shaping of the Modern Self, Jessica Grogan (2013) decries what she sees as the decline of humanistic psychology, under the negative influence of the excesses of the encounter group movement, especially as realized in places like Esalen. She also suggests that the humanistic psychology of the 1960s paid insufficient attention to American racism and sexism. However, what was true of the 1960s in this regard certainly does not remain true of most contemporary humanistic psychology. Nonetheless, if one distinguishes between humanistic psychology and the 1960s human potential movement, where the former remains influential, the latter seems to have languished. Is it possible that contemporary therapeutic culture has replaced some of what the human potential movement of over 50 years ago attempted? Such speculation would require much more intensive investigation than is possible here, but a relevant detailed study might prove to be a valuable addition to the history of humanistic psychology and psychotherapy.

Why the “Gloria Films” Matter, Then and Now

In her review of Gloria's daughter Pamela Burry's book Living with “The Gloria Films”: A Daughter's Memory, Moon (2009, p. 287) writes that this book “is a poetic rendering of the issues of confidentiality, meaningful consent, release of information, research in the area of psychotherapy, [and] case presentation.” She notes that Pamela's book is “a fascinating mix of California subdivision and the ‘Age of Aquarius’ counterculture in the 1960s and 1970s … in its evocative descriptions of family life, parent‐child relationships, and experience of illness and tragedy” (p. 287). At the time of their release, the “Gloria Films” depicted a typical middle‐class woman concerned about her ability to secure a good life and close, meaningful relationships with her children, a partner, and friends. The very ordinariness of Gloria's life and concerns indirectly told viewers of the films that psychotherapy was a potential source of help in living lives they desired. One did not need to be seriously mentally disturbed to benefit from psychotherapy. Moreover, there were different forms of psychotherapy that might prove to be good matches with one's concerns, goals, and preferences. With this growing diversity and popularity, potential and actual psychotherapy clients like Gloria were relatively unconcerned about being stigmatized as mentally unstable. In fact, entering psychotherapy signaled a way of strengthening oneself and one's resolve. For many viewers, it was Gloria, not any of the therapists, who was the real star of Three Approaches to Psychotherapy.

Yet, upon closer examination, both those familiar and unfamiliar with psychotherapy could not help but notice that, despite her courage, intelligence, resolve, and overall sanity, it was not Gloria who determined much of what happened during her therapy sessions, but her psychotherapists. The facts that Gloria, even after being demeaned but Fritz Perls in her therapy session with him and afterwards, still thought she could potentially benefit from further interactions with him and that it wasn't until several years later that she realized just how wrong she was in this regard, speak volumes about twentieth‐century life and gender relationships during the 1960s and 1970s, even as American society was experiencing major sociopolitical transformation and change.

Interpretations of the Gloria Films and 1960s American culture by scholars and artists like Jane Howard and Gina Moxley alert us to perils as well as promises of modes of psychotherapy and therapeutic ways of life. As Hannah Arendt (1958) made clear many years ago, once something is turned loose in the world, there is no telling where it might go. One implication of Arendt's prescient observation is that life circumstances are always subject to change and reinterpretation. But this does not mean that we cannot recognize and act in ways that are likely to benefit ourselves and others. In Arendt's words:faith and hope in human affairs come from the fact that new people are continually coming into the world, each of them unique, each capable of new initiatives that may interrupt or divert the chains of events set in motion by previous actions. … in human affairs, it is actually quite reasonable to expect the unexpected, and that new beginnings cannot be ruled out even when society seems locked in stagnation or set on an inexorable course.(1958, xvii)

What the Gloria Films succeeded in doing in 1965 and continue to do in 2026 is remind us of the everchanging landscapes of human life and existence and invite us to participate in them by developing ourselves and our abilities to engage in personal and collective agency to improve those frontiers for all of us.6 Despite her therapists exercising control over the way in which Gloria was positioned in her interactions with Rogers, Perls, and Ellis, Shostrom's post‐filming interview with her (probably quite unintentionally on his part) allowed her to judge and adjudicate each of her three therapists. Almost all who have viewed the films recall that Gloria liked Rogers, had difficulty “following” Ellis, and thought she, as a client with some experience of psychotherapy, might benefit most from future therapy sessions with Perls. Yes, she later changed her mind about Perls and had post‐filming contact with Rogers and his wife that she came to cherish. However, none of this changes the fact that she, an ordinary woman who proved to be not so ordinary, held and played a trump card when it came to evaluating her interlocutors. With this in mind, it is possible to think quite differently about Gloria and come to see her as not only starring in, but also controlling much of what the history of these films might tell us about the unexpected twists and turns of psychotherapy and the human condition more generally. It is interesting to note that such an interpretation seems quite feasible today, but would probably have been considered highly unlikely in the 1960s. In retrospect, it seems appropriate that the Three Approaches to Psychotherapy films have become known and will go down in history as The Gloria Films.
