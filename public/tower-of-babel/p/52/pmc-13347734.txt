Radiological Evaluation of Distal Trochanteric Transfer in Patients with Proximal Femoral Growth Disturbance
Authors: Saltuk Bugra Tekin, Mehmet Burak Yalcin, Ata Mert Civilibal, Gazi Zorer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesProximal femoral growth disturbance (PFGD), resulting from conditions such as Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease and developmental dysplasia of the hip, causes femoral head deformity and trochanteric overgrowth. This study evaluates the effectiveness of distal trochanteric transfer (DTT) in anatomical restoration using objective radiological parameters.MethodsPatients with unilateral PFGD who presented with pain, abductor muscle weakness, and Trendelenburg gait and subsequently underwent DTT were retrospectively included in this study. All patients had skeletal growth potential, confirmed by the presence of open growth plates. Patients who had standard anteroposterior pelvic radiographs and anteroposterior and lateral hip radiographs were included, as these imaging modalities were required to evaluate the articular trochanteric distance (ATD), central trochanteric distance (CTD), trochanteric offset (TO), and lever arm ratio (LAR).ResultsThe study cohort consisted of 13 patients (11 females, 2 males). The mean age at surgery was 10.54±2.11 years, and the mean follow-up period was 7.62±5.25 years. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was significant for 9 of the 12 total measurements, with three parameters demonstrating statistically non-significant values. Preoperatively, the pathological hips showed a significantly reduced mean ATD of −2.00±8.57 mm compared with the normal hips (17.38±5.58 mm; p=0.001). Postoperative ATD significantly improved to 15.62±6.36 mm (p=0.001). Similarly, the significantly reduced preoperative TO (18.69±6.84 mm) was restored to 24.85±7.03 mm postoperatively (p=0.008). Furthermore, the postoperative LAR (2.83±0.48) was significantly higher than both the preoperative value (2.21±0.46; p=0.012) and the normal hip value (p=0.001). No significant difference was observed for CTD.ConclusionDTT effectively achieves trochanteric distalization and lateralization, leading to restoration of the abductor muscle mechanism. Our analysis confirms that both ATD and TO are reliable radiographic parameters, as demonstrated by their strong ICC values, for evaluating immediate anatomical results. However, CTD is age-dependent, and both CTD and LAR are inherently influenced by the underlying femoral head anatomy. Future studies are needed to correlate these anatomical improvements with long-term clinical outcomes.

Proximal femoral growth disturbance (PFGD) is caused by septic arthritis of the hip, Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease (LCP), slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE), and developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) in childhood.[1] These developmental problems lead to significant anatomical and biomechanical changes, including shortening of the femoral neck and alterations in its shape, despite normal development of the greater trochanter.[2] These changes impair the abductor lever arm, leading to hip pain during ambulation, Trendelenburg gait, and trochanteric impingement (gear-stick sign). Furthermore, these altered joint biomechanics may accelerate progression toward avascular necrosis (AVN) and osteoarthritis.[3,4]

The literature describes various surgical strategies aimed at improving abductor mechanics, delaying degenerative changes such as AVN and osteoarthritis, and restoring near-normal hip anatomy, which may also facilitate future total hip arthroplasty if required. Despite numerous surgical options, the optimal method for assessing anatomical correction and biomechanical restoration remains a subject of debate.[3,5–8]

Among the several surgical options available, lateral and distal transfer of the greater trochanter (DTT) is frequently chosen for its efficacy in re-establishing a functional abductor mechanism and normalizing anatomical parameters.[3,9] Several studies have reported favorable radiological and biomechanical outcomes following DTT.[10,11] The existing literature demonstrates that trochanteric advancement or transfer surgery can have both positive and negative effects on biomechanics, radiological parameters, and clinical outcomes in both the short and long term.[3,9,11–13]

Radiographic parameters such as the articular trochanteric distance (ATD), central trochanteric distance (CTD), trochanteric offset (TO), and lever arm ratio (LAR) are frequently used to assess trochanteric position and abductor biomechanics. Although these measurements are widely reported in the literature, their reliability, age dependency, and sensitivity to underlying femoral head morphology vary considerably. We hypothesized that distal trochanteric transfer would result in measurable improvements in selected radiographic parameters; however, we anticipated that not all measurements would demonstrate consistent reliability or anatomical relevance in this patient population.

Methods

Study Design and Ethics Approval

Ethical approval for the research was received from the local ethics committee (Date: 25.01.2024; Decision no: 117, Memorial Bahcelievler Hospital). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Following ethics committee approval, we retrospectively reviewed pediatric patients who had undergone trochanteric advancement and lateralization procedures. All patients or their legal guardians provided written informed consent. Patients diagnosed with unilateral DDH or LCP were included in the study. Despite differing etiologies, proximal femoral growth disturbance results in a comparable pattern of trochanteric overgrowth; therefore, patients were analyzed together to evaluate radiographic changes after trochanteric transfer. Surgical indication was established for patients presenting with Trendelenburg gait, abductor weakness, and hip pain during ambulation. These patients underwent surgery between 2005 and 2023, with a minimum follow-up of two years. Bilateral involvement, a history of previous hip or pelvic surgery, inconsistent follow-up, and metabolic bone diseases were defined as exclusion criteria. Among the 20 patients initially identified, 13 met the inclusion criteria. Seven patients were excluded due to incomplete follow-up and insufficient archival radiographic records, which precluded reliable measurements. The final study group included 11 females and 2 males.

Surgical Procedure and Postoperative Care

A standardized surgical technique was employed by a single senior surgeon (G.Z.) across all cases. The operation was performed through a lateral approach, using fluoroscopic monitoring for guidance. The trochanter was separated at its base by making a cut parallel to the superior contour of the femoral neck with an oscillating saw. The gluteal musculature was then released from its distal soft-tissue insertions.

To accommodate the repositioned trochanter and prevent excessive lateral projection, a narrow section of bone was excised from the posterolateral femoral surface, exposing cancellous tissue. The goal of repositioning was to achieve optimal abductor mechanics by placing the transferred trochanter in the most anatomically correct position, guided intraoperatively by comparison with the contralateral unaffected hip. Avoiding lateral overhang is essential, as it may lead to irritation beneath the fascia lata, causing pain and potential bursitis.

Fixation of the trochanter was achieved with cancellous screws with washers, usually two, to provide rotational stability and enable early partial weight-bearing. Postoperatively, patients were immobilized with a pelvipedal splint, discharged within one to two days, and transitioned through stages of weight-bearing over two months, with regular follow-up examinations extending to annual review.

Radiologic Assessment

All radiographic parameters, including ATD, CTD, TO, and LAR, were measured on standardized anteroposterior pelvic and anteroposterior-lateral hip radiographs. The ATD was measured as the vertical height between two parallel horizontal lines: one tangent to the most cephalic aspect of the femoral head and the other passing through the apex of the greater trochanter. To evaluate the center of rotation, the CTD was determined as the vertical distance between the femoral head’s center of rotation and the tip of the greater trochanter. For both ATD and CTD, measurements were recorded as negative values if the greater trochanteric tip was positioned superior to the respective femoral head landmarks, namely the most cephalic portion or center of rotation, and as positive values if it was positioned inferiorly. Additionally, TO was quantified as the horizontal distance between two parallel vertical lines, one passing through the femoral head’s center of rotation and the other through the tip of the greater trochanter. The LAR was defined as the ratio of the distance between the center of gravity, projected as the pelvic midline passing through the pubic symphysis, and the femoral head center to the abductor lever arm, defined as the perpendicular distance from the femoral head center to the line of action of the abductor muscles (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). To minimize measurement bias and ensure reproducibility, all measurements were conducted independently in a double-blind fashion by three orthopedic specialists using Mose concentric circles to determine the femoral head center. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which demonstrated high consistency across all measurements. Consequently, the data from the most experienced senior surgeon were used for the final statistical analysis. Specific definitions and ICC values are detailed in Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 22.0 (IBM, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). All data were considered non-parametric due to the small sample size. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed for preoperative and postoperative comparisons within the same pathological hip, while the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare values between normal and pathological hips. The consistency among the measurements of the three different surgeons was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and ICC values above 0.8 were considered to indicate high reliability. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05. A post-hoc power analysis was conducted based on a two-group comparison using the Mann-Whitney U test and assuming a large effect size (d=0.80) and an alpha level of 0.05. This analysis determined that the achieved statistical power for the study was 0.58.

Results

In this study, the mean age of the patients was 10.54±2.11 years, with an age range of 8 to 15 years. Although the longest follow-up period was 20 years, all patients were followed up for at least two years, and the mean follow-up duration was 7.62±5.25 years. Of the patients, 11 (84.6%) were female and 2 (15.4%) were male. Ten patients were diagnosed with DDH, and three were diagnosed with LCP. The number of right- and left-sided pathological hips was nearly equal (Table 2). No postoperative complications were observed during the follow-up period. (Supplementary Table 1).

The post-hoc power analysis yielded a value of 0.58, indicating that the study was underpowered for some comparisons. Although clinically relevant improvements were observed in several parameters, the changes in CTD did not reach statistical significance (p>0.05). Given the limited power, these specific findings should be interpreted with caution, as they may represent a type II error rather than a lack of surgical effect on these radiographic relationships.

Discussion

The primary goal of DTT is to restore hip anatomy and function following PFGD. Our investigation, which examined radiological outcomes across a spectrum of underlying pediatric hip pathologies, confirms the anatomical efficacy of DTT. Specifically, ATD and TO demonstrated successful correction toward normal values and exhibited high ICCs, validating their use as the most robust anatomical indicators following DTT surgery. The utility of CTD was deemed less reliable due to its inherent age dependency and poor ICC results. The unexpected significant increase in postoperative LAR, coupled with its limited reproducibility, highlights the complex biomechanical interplay between the trochanter and residual femoral head deformity.

In this study, surgeries were performed during the peak growth period in nearly all patients, with a mean patient age of 10.54 years. Although the proximal femoral epiphysis was damaged in many patients, greater trochanteric apophysodesis (GTA) and transfer are still recommended during the peak growth period, based on the assumption that the remaining growth potential of the proximal femoral epiphysis will help restore normal hip anatomy.[14] The mean follow-up period was 7.62 years, with a minimum of two years, which was deemed sufficient to assess surgical outcomes. In the literature, follow-up periods have been reported to be similar to those in our study.[9,11–13] Although the study group was heterogeneous, including both DDH and LCP, this approach is consistent with the existing literature on proximal femoral realignment. Recent studies, such as series reporting Morscher osteotomies for five different etiologies, have similarly pooled patients to evaluate surgical outcomes for shared morphological deformities.[15] We believe that focusing on the common mechanical endpoint, namely trochanteric overgrowth, provides clinically relevant data for hip preservation surgeons.

ATD has been described as a reliable measurement method for evaluating greater trochanter surgery.[16] In our study, pathological hips demonstrated significantly reduced ATD compared with normal hips preoperatively. Following distal trochanteric transfer, ATD values increased and approached those of the normal hips. This finding suggests that distal trochanteric transfer effectively corrects trochanteric height at the radiographic level. In contrast, previous studies evaluating alternative trochanteric procedures have reported less consistent restoration of ATD, indicating that the extent and direction of trochanteric relocation play a critical role in achieving anatomical correction.[13] Although significant improvement in ATD after distal trochanteric transfer has been demonstrated in the literature, the association between radiographic correction and clinical outcomes remains a subject of ongoing debate.[8,12,13]

CTD is also used to evaluate trochanteric height in relation to the femoral head. In the present study, CTD values in pathological hips did not differ significantly from those of normal hips, either before or after surgery. This finding may be explained by the age-dependent nature of CTD, as values tend to be positive in early childhood and gradually shift toward negative values with skeletal maturation.[17,18] Given the age range of our patient cohort, the absence of a significant difference in CTD may reflect physiological variation rather than a lack of surgical effect. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, although others have described postoperative normalization of CTD values. These discrepancies suggest that CTD should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in pediatric populations in which growth-related changes may influence measurement.[18–20]

DTT aims to displace the greater trochanter laterally and distally to enhance the mechanical strength of the abductor musculature. Although lateral translation has a substantial effect on abductor force generation, distal displacement is generally regarded as having a more pronounced biomechanical impact.[10] In patients undergoing isolated trochanteric distalization, the resultant joint force has been reported to concentrate over a smaller articular surface area, potentially altering load distribution within the hip joint and thereby increasing the risk of osteoarthritis development.[21] Our results demonstrated improvement in trochanteric offset following distal trochanteric transfer, with radiographic findings indicating that the intended distal and lateral repositioning of the greater trochanter was achieved, as reflected by changes in ATD measurements. Although various studies in the literature have reported successful radiological outcomes, not all clinical results have shown the same degree of improvement.[8,10–12]

The LAR is a commonly used biomechanical index in orthopedics to estimate load transmission across the femoral head during the stance phase of gait.[22,23] In the present study, postoperative LAR values were significantly higher than those of both preoperative and normal hips. Although a decrease in LAR might theoretically be expected after distalization and lateralization of the greater trochanter, our results demonstrated an increase in this parameter. This finding suggests that LAR may be influenced by factors beyond trochanteric position alone. In hips with proximal femoral growth disturbance, residual femoral head deformity and altered proximal femoral geometry may affect the spatial relationship between the femoral head and pelvic reference points used for LAR calculation. In addition, chronic abductor insufficiency and long-standing gait adaptations may lead to functional pelvic obliquity, potentially altering the body-weight moment arm despite apparently standardized radiographic positioning. Furthermore, LAR represents a geometric measurement based on bony landmarks and does not account for changes in abductor muscle orientation or effective force transmission, which may persist despite anatomical correction. Therefore, an increased postoperative LAR may reflect measurement limitations and underlying anatomical variation rather than a direct indicator of biomechanical change.[24]

The high ICC values observed for ATD and TO confirm their validity as reliable and precise radiographic indicators for assessing anatomical success following DTT. Our study demonstrates that DTT surgery is effective in restoring abductor power by successfully correcting these parameters, which are directly influenced by trochanteric lateralization and distalization. Conversely, the limited correction observed in CTD and LAR can be largely attributed to the underlying fixed structural deformity of the femoral head; this suggests that standard radiographic reference points may lack sufficient accuracy in complex cases. Therefore, advanced imaging modalities, such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which provide clearer visualization of the femoral head’s complex morphology, are warranted to improve the reliability of results, particularly when assessing parameters such as LAR.

Regarding parameters that showed no statistically significant difference, particularly CTD, future research should explore the utility of established clinical measurements and novel radiographic techniques to fully contextualize the long-term functional impact of trochanteric repositioning. In our study, the strict inclusion criteria, specifically selecting patients with proximal femoral epiphyseal disturbance who still retained growth potential and ensuring a minimum two-year follow-up, naturally limited the sample size. This resulted in a statistical power of 0.58, which may have affected our ability to detect minor radiographic changes. However, we believe that this specific cohort provides valuable insights despite these statistical limitations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design and relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the heterogeneity of the underlying etiologies reflects the clinical spectrum of proximal femoral growth disturbance but may introduce variability in radiographic measurements. Another limitation is the relatively high loss to follow-up, which is largely related to the retrospective design and extended follow-up period. In some cases, archived radiographic records were incomplete or of inadequate quality for reliable measurements, which may have introduced potential selection bias. The absence of standardized clinical outcome measures precludes direct correlation between radiographic changes and functional improvement.

Conclusion

Distal trochanteric transfer provides consistent radiographic improvement in parameters directly reflecting trochanteric repositioning, particularly ATD and TO. These findings support the role of DTT as an effective technique for correcting trochanteric height and lateralization in hips affected by proximal femoral growth disturbance.

In contrast, parameters such as CTD and LAR appear to be influenced by residual femoral head deformity and altered proximal femoral geometry, limiting their reliability as isolated indicators of surgical success. This observation underscores the importance of cautious interpretation when assessing complex radiographic parameters and morphological relationships in structurally abnormal hips.

Future studies incorporating advanced imaging techniques and standardized clinical outcome measures are needed to clarify the relationship between radiographic changes and overall hip morphology following trochanteric repositioning in this patient population.
