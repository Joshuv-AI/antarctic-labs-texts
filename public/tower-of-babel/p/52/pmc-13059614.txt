Introduction

ISarc of the CC and GV are extremely rare primary tumors of the cardiac chambers (CC), aortic root (AO) and pulmonary artery (PA). Sarcomas reported in the CC and GV include ISarc, angiosarcoma, synovial sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, undifferentiated sarcoma, myxofibrosarcoma, and leiomyosarcomas 1. ISarc are typically composed of variably atypical spindle cells with some tumors demonstrating epithelioid cells 2. Identifying MDM2 alterations is helpful in supporting a diagnosis of ISarc when examining a tumor in the CC or GV. A study of 100 primary cardiac sarcomas demonstrated alterations in MDM2, CDK4, HMGA2, DDIT3, GLI, KIT, PDGFRA and EGFR 1.

ISarc are rarely resectable and are associated with rapid progression and poor clinical outcome. We queried whether comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) could uncover new routes to targeted therapies for this aggressive form of malignancy.

Materials and methods

Approval for this study, including a waiver of informed consent and a HIPAA waiver of authorization, was obtained from the Western Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. 20152817). The histology of 27 cases of ISarc were centrally reviewed, and inclusion of cases in this study required direct association with the intimal surfaces of the CC and GV with supporting routine histological features and immunohistochemical staining patterns. DNA and RNA extracted from ISarc cases were both sequenced using a hybrid-capture based CGP, whole exome sequencing (WES). This is Targeted hybrid-capture based sequencing using a panel of 324 genes to study all classes of genomic alterations (GA) and determine MSI status, TMB level, HRD score, genomic ancestry, cosmic trinucleotide signature (Signature) and germline status 3–8. PD-L1 expression was measured by IHC (Dako 22C3 using the tumor proportional score (TPS) scoring system. Germline status was predicted using the SGZ algorithm applied to the somatic sequencing data as previously published.

Results

The 27 ISarc cases included 15 CC and 12 GV (11 pulmonary artery ISarc and 1 aortic root ISarc). There were 24 (88.9%) histologic grade 3 and 3 (11.1%) grade 2 tumors using FNCLCC (Table 1). Median ages for all ISarc ranged from 45 to 46 years and were similar in both groups. The CC ISarc patients tended to be female when compared with the GV pts (73% vs. 50%; P=0.26). GA potentially impacting targeted therapy selection in clinical trials for rare tumors included amplifications of MDM2 in 66.7% of CC ISarc and 50% of GV ISarc; PDGFRA in 53.3% CC ISarc and 41.7% of GV ISarc; CDK4 in 60.0% of CC and 33.3% of GV ISarcs; and KIT in 40.0% of CC and 33.3% of GV ISarcs that were not significant. Homozygous deletions in CDKN2A in 26.7% in CC vs. 66.7% of GV was near significant (p = 0.055) and significantly more frequent point mutations in PIK3CA in CC at 20% vs. 0% in GV (p=0.007) and ERBB3 in 13.3% of CV and 8.3% of GV which was not significant. EUR ancestry was present in slightly more than 50% of ISarc cases and similar in both sub-groups. 4 ISarc cases could be classified as Signature and included 3 MMR and 1 APOBEC case. There were no (0%) MSI-high cases in either ISarc group. Median TMB was similar and ranged from 4.3 to 4.7 mutations/Mb with 1 (6.7%) CC ISarc having a TMB of ≥ 10 mut/Mb. In 1 case, the aortic root GV ISarc, germline mutations were found in the VHL and KEAP1 genes. 1/5 (20%) tested ISarc cases stained for low level PD-L1 expression. Discussion

The intimal sarcomas (ISarc) of the cardiac chambers (CC) of the heart and of the great vessels (GV) are rare but aggressive malignancies that often give a significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. These tumors are genomically characterized by complex karyotypes with heterogenous alterations limiting treatment options. Research in sarcomas is challenging; the low prevalence makes it difficult to have enough evidence for treatment efficacy and the wide variety of subtypes exhibit different clinical characteristics. Research in ISarc has even more barriers secondary to limited reports and prevalence, limiting the evidence for treatment options.

Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) can offer critical insight into the molecular alterations of these tumors, identifying recurrent mutations that may serve as diagnostic markers or even possible therapeutic targets. Previous analyses have shown that CGP and targeted tumor sequencing strategies identified common genetic altered pathways that lead to implementable clinical actions in about one-third of patients with rare and poor-prognosis cancers 9, 10. Accurate tumor classification is crucial to ensure optimal treatment selection, which involves molecular profiling in modern oncology. This is significant since despite having a low prevalence in individual populations, registries have identified around 20% of patients diagnosed with a rare cancer in the United States 11. CGP offers genetic profiling of these tumors, which opens new therapeutic targets. We performed a CGP analysis in our cohort.

Genetic alterations

Our findings revealed that the most recurrent genomic alterations in our cohort were mainly in MDM2, PDGFRA, CDK4, and C-KIT. There was no difference in the prevalence of these genetic alterations when comparing GV tumors vs. CC tumors. Notably, CDKN2A homozygous deletions were more prevalent in GV tumors (66.7%) than in CC tumors (26.7%), approaching statistical significance (p = 0.055). Additionally, point mutations in PIK3CA were significantly more frequent in CC tumors (20%) versus GV tumors (0%, p = 0.007), indicating a potentially targetable oncogenic pathway enriched in CC ISarc. There is limited previous data on the molecular alterations regarding these specific sarcoma subtypes. Our results align with previous studies that have identified overexpression and amplification of the region 12q12-15, which involves MDM2 and CDK4 in intimal sarcomas in some cohorts 1, 12. Palbociclib may be useful in some sarcomas with a high prevalence of MDM2 amplification, although there is no data on ISarc 13. Other genetic alterations like PDGFRA, also found in our cohort, have previously been identified to play a role in tumorigenesis and therapeutic resistance, via co-amplification. This creates a window for new therapies. Pazopanib, a C-KIT and PDGFRA inhibitor, has been studied in this subset of patients 14.

Many of the genomic alterations identified in intimal sarcomas overlap with variants reported across other soft tissue sarcoma subtypes, particularly those involving cell-cycle regulation, receptor tyrosine signaling, and chromatin remodeling pathways2, 4. Recurrent alterations such as MDM2 amplification, CDK4 amplification, and abnormalities in TP53 and PI3K/AKT/mTOR–related genes have been described in multiple sarcoma entities, supporting shared oncogenic mechanisms6, 7. However, the high frequency and co-occurrence of these alterations in intimal sarcomas, especially MDM2 and CDK4 amplification, appear more characteristic of this disease and may help distinguish it from other sarcoma subtypes, suggesting a partially overlapping but distinct genomic landscape.

Several ongoing clinical trials have arisen from genetic profiling. PAZNTIS trial (NCT02180867) is a randomized phase II/III trial studying pazopanib, with radiation therapy for non-rhabdomyosarcoma soft tissue sarcomas. NRSTS2021 is a current ongoing trial evaluating maintenance pazopanib with other adjuvant therapies in the same population. ImmunoSarc trial (NCT03277924) is a phase I-II, single-arm, non-randomized, open-label, multicenter, international clinical trial evaluating sunitinib and/or nivolumab plus chemotherapy, which included ISarc in their third cohort. These efforts reflect a growing shift toward genomically informed, precision oncology strategies.

Immunological alterations

There are only three approved predictive biomarkers for immunological alterations warranting immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) therapy in tumors: programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), microsatellite instability (MSI), and tumor mutational burden (TMB) 15. In our cohort, no MSI-high cases were identified in either CC or GV ISarc tumors, indicating that mismatch repair alterations were not identified. This follows the limited records available. Sarcomas are tumors that typically do not have a high profile of MSI. Mismatch repair deficiencies have been found in around 2% of sarcomas and specifically MSI-high in around 0.2% [16]. The MSI profile varies depending on the subtype of the sarcoma, commonly seeing in stromal sarcoma and leiomyosarcoma 17.

TMB was low and comparable between subtypes, ranging from 4.3 to 4.7 mutations/Mb, with only one CC case (6.7%) exceeding the threshold of 10 mutations/Mb. Previous reports indicate that sarcomas are tumors with a low somatic mutation burden, with lower TMB 18. There is limited data using TMB as a predictive biomarker of ICI response in sarcomas, with some studies showing a lack of significant association 19. The SARC028 trial has shown a 23% objective response to ICI schemes in their final results 20, 21, which opens new areas for research to explain this mechanism.

PD-L1 expression was detected at low levels in only 20% of the tested ISarc cases, indicating limited expression across the cohort. Records of PD-L1 expression vary depending on the sarcoma subtype; meta-analyses have shown significant variability 22. Previous data concluded that sarcomas have low immunoreactivity to PD-L1 [23]. Since ICI therapies’ responses do not correlate with the immunologic profile, previous studies have shown that ICI therapy can be indicated without biomarker requirement in several tumors 24. PD-L1 expression alone may not serve as a reliable biomarker for treatment stratification in these tumors, reinforcing the need for further biomarker assessment, which should be explored in future research.

Specific cases

Two specific cases from our cohort need their own discussion on how genetic analysis can guide treatment in these tumors. A 44-year-old woman with CC sarcoma, was partially resected and relapsed in the brain. After receiving systemic chemotherapy with gemcitabine, docetaxel, and olaratumab, CGP identified microsatellite stable tumor with low TMB of 7 mutations/Mb. The genetic profile in Fig. 3 revealed amplifications of CDK4, KIT, PDGFRA, MDM2, and CCNE1, along with short variant mutations in CPS1, FGF14, LRP1B, NTRK3, and PELN. Notably, a BCOR rearrangement was also detected. BCOR genetic alterations have been associated with small round blue cell sarcomas (Fig. 4), a histologic pattern seen in the patient’s brain metastasis 25. Oncoprint is seen in Fig. 5. This case exhibits the potential of CGP to reveal diagnostic molecular features and suggests possible lineage-specific targets even in histologically complex or metastatic presentations.

Oncoprint for case 1

Another remarkable example found in our cohort include a 59-year-old male with pulmonary artery GV sarcoma (Fig. 6) which exhibited immunopositivity for vimentin, desmin, myogenin, and focal staining for CD117 and cytokeratin. MDM2 amplification was identified through both next-generation sequencing and FISH. CGP further uncovered a non-germline NF1 inactivating frameshift mutation (L1187fs*17). NF1 encodes neurofibromin, a GTPase-activating protein that negatively regulates Ras signaling; thus, this loss-of-function mutation is predicted to activate the PIK3CA/MTOR/AKT pathway. This case is notable for its deviation from the common pattern seen in GV tumors in our cohort, where short variant mutations were infrequent and copy number alterations predominated. Oncoprint is seen in Fig. 7. The presence of a functional NF1 mutation highlights a rare but potentially target within GV sarcomas, emphasizing the importance of CGP in these rare tumors. Conclusions

CGP of ISarc cases reveals that these tumors do feature a modest number of GA potentially associated with benefit for targeted therapies, but low and absent biomarkers predictive of immunotherapy benefit. Further study of ISarc in the setting of rare tumor clinical trials appears warranted.
