Launching a Neonatal Resuscitation Escape Room to Combat High Infant Mortality Rates
Authors: Ellen Marie Campos, Kathryn L. Dambrino, Jennifer Sternberg, Taryn McCoy, Shelby L. Garner, Kendell Natt, Lyn S. Prater, Weiming Ke, Leena Lourduswamy, Sheela Victor, Vernonsiya Kirubha, Beth Hallmark, Julie Honey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:In South India, infant mortality rates are high, and nurses require advanced training to respond to infants in infant distress. Evidence shows that improved nurse competency in neonatal resuscitation (NNR) and the ability of students to recognize and respond to distressed infants have led to decreased infant mortality rates.Purpose:The purpose of this quality improvement project was to design and implement a simulated escape room to improve NNR outcomes among undergraduate students in South India.Methods:This quality improvement project used a quantitative descriptive design. Undergraduate nursing students from 6 schools in South India completed an asynchronous maternal–fetal nursing course followed by an in-person simulated NNR escape room. Two quantitative surveys were used for evaluation: the facilitator survey assessed students’ completion of NNR skills during the escape room, and the student survey measured perceptions of the escape room experience.Results:There was a measurable increase in knowledge retention on the infant distress portion of the posttest following participation in the escape room. Most student groups successfully completed the NNR sequence with minimal assistance, demonstrating improved NNR competency. Students reported that the activity enhanced their teamwork and collaboration skills in a clinical context. Both students and facilitators described the escape room as an enjoyable, engaging, and psychologically safe learning experience.Implications for Practice and Research:This article provides an overview of the quality improvement project in which an NNR escape room was implemented as an innovative learning activity for nursing students in South India.

BACKGROUND

In Karnataka, the largest southern Indian state, the infant mortality rate is 21 infant deaths per 1000 live births.1 The World Health Organization’s objective is to reduce infant deaths to 12 or fewer per 1000 live births by 2030.2 According to the American Academy of Pediatrics, approximately 30% of intrapartum-related deaths could be prevented if proper neonatal resuscitation (NNR) education were provided to clinicians working in low-resource healthcare settings.3 To address this educational gap and improve neonatal survival within the first month of life across developing countries, the American Academy of Pediatrics launched the Helping Babies Breathe (HBB) program in 2010.4

South India, home to more than 250 million people, faces significant disparities in access to healthcare, particularly maternal and neonatal healthcare services. Many communities are rural or semiurban, with limited healthcare infrastructure and shortages of trained neonatal care personnel.1-3 Nursing education programs in this region also vary in quality and clinical exposure, affecting students’ preparedness to manage high-acuity situations such as NNR. Strengthening the clinical competency of nursing students through innovative, simulation-based education is therefore essential to improving neonatal outcomes and reducing preventable infant mortality in this setting.

Increasing autonomy among nurses is essential for improving neonatal outcomes, particularly in resuscitation scenarios where timely, independent decision-making can determine survival. In South India, nurses often serve as the primary providers during deliveries, especially in rural and low-resource settings. Strengthening autonomy through simulation and online learning equips nurses with the confidence and competence to perform NNR effectively. The HBB program emphasizes hands-on, practical training for clinicians, enabling them to recognize and respond to infants in infant distress.4 Training programs like this can help improve NNR skills among healthcare providers, which in turn can reduce neonatal mortality rates. In Tanzania, healthcare professionals who completed the HBB course demonstrated a 47% reduction in neonatal deaths within the first 24 hours after birth.3 The HBB program emphasizes hands-on, practical training for clinicians, enabling them to recognize and respond to infants in infant distress.4 Evidence supports that innovative, simulation-based learning modalities improve critical thinking, self-efficacy, and team-based communication, all of which are essential for NNR practice.5-7 Incorporating online learning and simulation strategies, such as an NNR escape room, promotes self-directed learning and enhances clinical judgment, bridging the gap between knowledge acquisition and autonomous practice in clinical settings.

For decades, nurses in India struggled to find structured nursing roles that valued their clinical expertise.8 During the 19th and 20th centuries, nursing education in India was structured to prepare nurses to assist physicians.9 In recent years, the scope of nursing practice has changed, and nurse educators are beginning to educate nursing students to handle an elevated level of responsibility, involving increased autonomy and decision-making capabilities.9

The Saving Lives at Birth program was developed to strengthen NNR knowledge and skills among undergraduate nursing students in South India. Designed as a simulation-based and active learning initiative, the program aims to enhance students’ preparedness for real-world neonatal emergencies. Unlike existing programs such as HBB, Saving Lives at Birth provides comprehensive NNR education tailored for nursing students and applicable to both inpatient and outpatient settings, an essential component of nursing practice in India.9 The project utilized a constructivist learning approach, emphasizing teamwork and problem-solving as students applied concepts from the online modules within the simulated escape room. During the simulation, students practiced the NNR sequence, which includes warming and drying the infant, assessing airway and breathing, providing positive pressure ventilation, and performing coordinated chest compressions as indicated.

Online learning expanded rapidly during the COVID-19 pandemic and has remained an integral component of nursing and higher education since 2020. As a result, there is substantial evidence regarding the strengths and limitations of online instruction.5-7,10 Research indicates that many health science students feel underprepared for clinical practice when education is delivered solely online.5-7,10 While students appreciate the flexibility of self-paced learning, they often report reduced engagement with faculty and peers, negatively affecting knowledge retention.5-7,10 Incorporating experiential and active learning strategies into nursing curricula enhances knowledge retention and supports clinical competency development.

Despite ongoing efforts to improve neonatal outcomes, a gap remains in the practical training and readiness of undergraduate nursing students to perform effective NNR in South India.9 Strengthening educational strategies that promote active, hands-on learning are essential to address this gap. Therefore, the aim of this quality improvement project was to design and implement a simulation-based NNR escape room to enhance clinical competency and teamwork among undergraduate nursing students in South India.

Current Literature on Simulation-Based Escape Rooms

Simulation-based escape rooms have been increasingly adopted in nursing education over the past decade. An escape room is a live-action, team-based game in which participants encounter obstacles to complete a defined mission within a limited time.11 Game-based learning is an evidence-based approach for teaching hands-on clinical skills, fostering critical thinking, problem-solving, and team collaboration.12-15 Research demonstrates that escape rooms enable clinicians and nursing students to practice clinical reasoning, apply knowledge in a low-anxiety environment, and develop skills that enhance retention and performance.7,16, 25

The use of simulation escape rooms aligns with Constructivist Learning Theory, which emphasizes active, hands-on, and collaborative engagement with content.16 In nursing education, escape rooms provide students with opportunities to work in teams to solve clinical scenarios, practice decision-making, and apply knowledge from prior coursework.5,6,10 Psychological safety is central to this approach, allowing students to participate, ask questions, and share ideas without fear of embarrassment or negative consequences.17

Evidence suggests that escape rooms can improve teamwork, engagement, and confidence in clinical skills, which informed the design of the NNR escape room implemented in this project.12, 25 Incorporating these findings ensured that students not only applied knowledge from the Saving Lives at Birth course but also developed the autonomy and collaborative skills necessary for effective neonatal care in South India.

PURPOSE

The purpose of this quality improvement project was to design and implement a simulation-based NNR escape room to enhance clinical competency and team-based skills among undergraduate nursing students in South India. This project is a quality improvement initiative as it seeks to enhance the quality of care by implementing an intervention within a clinician training program. While planning and implementing the activities included in the NNR escape room project, the team worked to ensure that the activities within the escape room allowed nursing students to practice resuscitation in a psychologically safe, controlled environment, improving their comfort and skill performance in the clinical setting. The project’s overall purpose was to provide an interactive, hands-on learning experience in which students could practice NNR skills in a controlled, psychologically safe environment, bridging the gap between didactic knowledge and clinical application.

AIM

The Saving Lives at Birth simulation-based escape room was implemented as a quality improvement initiative to enhance nursing students’ knowledge and clinical competency in NNR. The activity provided a hands-on, team-based learning experience that bridged the gap between theoretical instruction and clinical practice. Grounded in a constructivist framework, the escape room promoted critical thinking, communication, and teamwork in a psychologically safe, controlled environment.

OBJECTIVES

To achieve this aim, 3 project objectives were established. The first objective was to develop and implement a simulation-based escape room aligned with the Saving Lives at Birth online course content. Second, provide structured, team-based opportunities for students to apply NNR knowledge and skills. Third, foster critical thinking, problem-solving, and collaborative decision-making during clinical scenarios in a psychologically safe learning environment.

METHODS

Setting

The project was conducted at the Rebekah Ann Naylor School of Nursing in Bengaluru, India, with participation from 5 additional nursing schools also located in Southern India (see Table 4 for students’ demographics information). All 6 schools follow a standardized undergraduate nursing curriculum aligned with national guidelines, and students participating in this project had completed similar maternal/fetal nursing courses prior to engaging in the Saving Lives at Birth online modules. Students were at the same point in their undergraduate programs (in their final year), ensuring comparable baseline knowledge and clinical exposure. This structure allowed for consistent implementation of the escape room intervention across all participating schools.

Design and Intervention

In June 2023, an online course, Saving Lives at Birth, adapted from HBB material, was launched at 6 nursing schools across Southern India to improve nursing students’ competency in neonatal care and resuscitation. Although learning outcomes were overall positive, posttest scores from the online course related to infant distress indicated that students needed additional education on identifying an infant in distress and opportunities to demonstrate the correct order of NNR (see Table 1). Additionally, because students engaged in independent online learning, they had not yet experienced working as a team to perform NNR, as is typically required in hospital settings.

A collaborative team from the US and India designed and implemented the NNR escape room. Team members included nurse educators, nurse practitioners, and students from Baylor University, Belmont University, and the Rebekah Ann Naylor School of Nursing in Bengaluru, India. The escape room was a novel activity for the Indian faculty. Team members from the US provided an initial presentation on the educational effectiveness of escape rooms during a team meeting in Bangalore in January 2024. After approval from the Indian team, both teams collaboratively developed and launched the escape room over 6 months. The activity was designed to mirror typical hospital environments in India while also simulating the conditions of nonhospital births in rural villages and urban slums, where resources and rapid team-based neonatal care are critical. Equipment and supplies reflected those commonly available in local healthcare settings.

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1. For a paired t-test, the sample size required to detect a significant difference between the pre- and posttests was 44, with a desired power of .90, a significance level of .05, and a medium effect size of d = .50. To account for attrition, 50 to 55 students were recruited from each of the 6 schools (N = 310), allowing the researchers to test differences both within and across schools.

After completing the online Saving Lives at Birth course in March 2024, students from all 6 schools attended a day of simulation-based learning at the Rebekah Ann Naylor School of Nursing from March to May 2024. During this experience, students participated in 2 maternal–fetal health simulations and the NNR escape room activity. The escape room was facilitated collaboratively by Rebekah Ann Naylor School of Nursing faculty and senior-level students, with US team members available for consultation and periodic monitoring of data entry for accuracy.

An average of 6 students per group completed the escape room activity. Students progressed through the NNR sequence while solving riddles to unlock boxes containing supplies needed for NNR, such as stethoscopes, blankets, or bag-valve masks. The final task required performing the full NNR sequence on an infant manikin, including warming, airway management, positive pressure ventilation, and chest compressions (see Figure 1). Facilitators observed performance, provided 1 allowable clue per team, and documented completion data. Structured pre-briefing and debriefing sessions promoted psychological safety and reflective learning.

Student Evaluation Tool:

Student Evaluation Tool:

Students completed a postactivity form consisting of 5-point Likert-type scale items (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) that measured perceived learning, confidence, engagement, teamwork, and the cultural relevance of the activity.

Although these were 2 separate instruments, data from both were analyzed together as a single, integrated measure to reflect the overall educational impact of the escape room. Facilitator checklists provided objective indicators of skill performance and teamwork, while student feedback offered insight into perceived learning, confidence, and engagement.

In addition to the facilitator and student evaluation tools, posttest results were analyzed to assess knowledge retention related to infant distress and appropriate NNR interventions. Findings demonstrated improvement in posttest scores following the escape room, indicating enhanced understanding and application of NNR principles.

A total of 53 student groups (N = 310) participated in the project. Because the escape room emphasized team-based performance, data were analyzed at the group level to reflect coordinated resuscitation practice rather than individual actions.

Analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 20. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize quantitative results. Pre- and posttest scores were compared using an independent sample t-test. Facilitator survey data were analyzed to determine the percentage of student groups that successfully completed each step of the NNR sequence during the escape room. Student survey data were analyzed to summarize perceptions of the escape room experience using Likert-scale responses.

The escape room and foundational HBB material were vital components of this project. They not only replicated hospital settings in India but also exposed students to realistic scenarios encountered during nonhospital births in rural or urban low-resource settings, enhancing both practical skills and contextual preparedness.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical considerations were an essential component of this quality improvement project. Before beginning the Saving Lives at Birth course’s online portion, students were told they were taking part in a quality improvement project initiative. As a result, students were informed that if they agreed to take the course, their personal identifying data would be protected and not shared at any time. Students were not required to share their names on the evaluation forms or the pre-/posttests. Additionally, all student responses were stored on locked computers, with only direct grant team access to the information. This quality improvement project was deemed to be exempt by the International Review Board in the US (Ref 1483) and was approved by an Ethics Review Board in India, presenting no more than minimal risk to project participants.

RESULTS

The NNR escape room led to notable improvements in students’ knowledge, teamwork, and clinical performance. Analysis of pre- and posttest scores showed a clear increase in understanding related to infant distress and NNR procedures. As shown in Table 1, the mean pretest score was 5.54 (SD = 3.31) and the posttest mean increased to 7.05 (SD = 3.74), a statistically significant improvement (t = −3.582, P < .001). Although not all 310 participants completed both assessments, results from both paired and independent t-tests confirmed consistent and significant knowledge gains across groups.

Students also demonstrated stronger application of NNR skills as they moved through the escape room. To progress to the next challenge, teams had to complete each step of the NNR sequence with 1 or fewer clues. As shown in Table 2, most student groups successfully completed each step, suggesting that the activity helped reinforce proper resuscitation techniques and encouraged critical thinking under pressure.

Feedback from students reflected similar enthusiasm. On the postactivity evaluation, which used a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), nearly all participants reported that the escape room positively influenced their teamwork and collaboration. A total of 95.8% agreed or strongly agreed that “working on a team during the escape room helped me develop team-based skills and collaborate positively with my peers.” Students also viewed the experience as engaging and supportive, with 99.3% agreeing that it was enjoyable, 99.7% indicating that it felt psychologically safe, and 96.4% agreeing that the instructions were easy to follow (Table 3).

Student comments during follow-up interviews provided further insight into their experience. One student shared, “The escape room was so well thought out and detailed—it was different from any other learning experience I’ve participated in.” Another noted, “It was a good experience, and I was able to practice providing critical care for infants.” These reflections suggest that the activity offered a safe yet challenging space for students to practice essential skills while building confidence in their abilities.

Facilitators also responded positively to the activity. All reported that leading the escape room was an engaging and rewarding experience. On average, groups completed the activity in 30.31 minutes, including setup, which met the project goal of 35 minutes or less per session.

Overall, results from both students and facilitators highlight the success of the NNR escape room as an educational tool. The significant improvement in posttest scores, high rate of skill completion, and overwhelmingly positive evaluations indicate that this active learning approach effectively enhanced students’ knowledge, teamwork, and confidence in NNR.

DISCUSSION IMPLICATIONS FOR PRACTICE AND RESEARCH

Summary

Of the total number of students completing the NNR escape room, 77.5% did so with minimal assistance, demonstrating proficiency and skill in NNR upon completion of the activity. The primary aim was for 90% of students to complete the NNR sequence successfully; while that goal was not fully met, the majority of students were ultimately able to complete each step of the sequence (see Table 1). The most challenging step was Step 4, providing 5 initial breaths and reassessing the airway completed successfully by 77.5% of groups. Facilitators observed that students often struggled to maintain an effective seal and assess chest rise, reflecting a common challenge in novice resuscitation training. The most successfully completed step was Step 6, performing 2-finger-to-thumb cardiopulmonary resuscitation, with 98.1% of groups demonstrating accurate technique.

Students’ ability to progress through the escape room sequence provided a tangible measure of their competency in performing NNR. The overall results indicate that students performed well on both foundational and advanced skills, though continued practice in ventilation and reassessment is needed. These findings are consistent with literature suggesting that simulation-based escape rooms promote engagement, teamwork, and clinical skill development among nursing students.12

The evaluation data and posttest results support that the NNR escape room was a positive and effective learning experience. Although improvements in knowledge and performance cannot be attributed solely to the escape room due to subsequent simulation experiences, the activity clearly enhanced student confidence and competence in NNR. Students also rated the experience highly, with over 99% agreeing it was enjoyable and psychologically safe, and 96.4% agreeing that instructions were easy to understand.

The escape room offered an interactive and psychologically safe environment for students to practice critical NNR skills and collaborate as a clinical care team. Implementing similar escape rooms in additional nursing programs could further strengthen hands-on learning and team-based clinical preparedness, especially in resource-limited or high-risk neonatal care settings. These findings suggest that practicing nurses could also benefit from similar simulation-based, gamified learning experiences to reinforce rapid clinical decision-making and improve confidence during high-risk, low-frequency neonatal emergencies. By integrating escape-room models into annual competencies or continuing education, health systems may enhance team communication and readiness for real-world NNRs.

Interpretation

The data from this quality improvement project provide evidence that escape rooms can positively affect student learning and team collaboration. Moreover, 77.5% of students could complete all parts of the resuscitation sequence during the escape room activity. Additionally, 95.8% of students voiced that working on a collaborative team was a helpful experience, and the escape room enhanced their understanding of how to resuscitate an infant. The timed environment that students were in while completing the escape room intentionally mimicked the high-adrenaline environment that nurses are in when an infant is unable to breathe and needs resuscitation.

Strengths and Limitations

This project’s strengths include its large sample size of 310 undergraduate nursing students from 6 nursing programs in South India, providing diverse perspectives and helping ensure anonymity in feedback. The escape room offered a structured, psychologically safe, team-based learning environment that allowed students to practice NNR skills in a controlled setting.

Limitations include that participants were undergraduate nursing students, many of whom may not pursue neonatal or NICU specialization, limiting generalizability to practicing clinicians. The escape room was implemented in a single geographic region, and replication in other schools and countries is needed to evaluate its broader effectiveness. While facilitator observations indicated improved understanding of NNR, direct measurement of clinical outcomes such as effective resuscitation or neonatal mortality was not feasible. Not all students shared their demographical information prior to beginning the exam. Additionally, not all students completed the fetal distress pre- and posttest, and for those who did, it followed both the escape room and maternal–fetal simulations, making it difficult to isolate the escape room’s impact. The pre- and posttest lacked established reliability and validity, and group collaboration may have influenced individual performance results.

CONCLUSIONS

Testing the escape room teaching modality to educate nursing students on NNR represents an innovative approach to enhance nursing education. While this project demonstrated improved student knowledge, teamwork, and skill performance in a controlled, psychologically safe environment, it was not designed to measure changes in infant mortality. The escape room can be adapted and implemented in other nursing programs in India and globally to evaluate its effectiveness as an active learning strategy. Future research should explore its impact on clinical performance and broader healthcare outcomes (see Supplemental Digital Content 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/ANC/A399 and Supplemental Digital Content 2, available at: http://links.lww.com/ANC/A400).
