Associations between Prenatal Perceived Stress and Child Autism-Related Traits in the ECHO Cohort
Authors: Luke P. Grosvenor, Monica McGrath, Jasmine Douglas, Jennifer L. Ames, Ndidiamaka Amutah-Onukagha, Lyndsay A. Avalos, Brennan H. Baker, Patricia A. Brennan, Neika Christalin, Assiamira Ferrara, Kendria Kelly-Taylor, Kristen Lyall, Ruby H. N. Nguyen, Rebecca J. Schmidt, Lisa A. Croen, ECHO Cohort Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine associations of prenatal perceived stress with child autism-related traits and to evaluate effect modification by both child sex and co-occurring prenatal depressive symptoms.Study designThe sample was drawn from Environmental Influences on Child Health Outcomes cohort sites with data on prenatal perceived stress (Perceived Stress Scale [PSS]) and autism-related traits (Social Responsiveness Scale, second Edition [SRS-2]). We used linear and logistic regression models to determine associations between PSS and either SRS-2 T-scores or odds of moderate-to-severe autism-related traits (SRS-2 T-score ≥65) and evaluated effect modification by sex and prenatal depressive symptoms.ResultsAmong 4115 mother–child pairs, higher PSS was associated with increased severity of autism-related traits (β = 0.15 [95% CI = 0.13–0.18]) and odds of moderate-to-severe traits (OR = 1.04 [1.04–1.05]). There was evidence for sex-by-exposure interaction for continuous (βMales = 0.18 [0.14–0.22]; βFemales = 0.13 [0.10–0.16], Pinteraction = 0.03) but not dichotomous trait severity (ORMales = 1.05 [1.03–1.06]; ORFemales = 1.04 [1.03–1.05], Pinteraction = 0.52). Associations did not differ by prenatal depressive symptoms (low: n = 2,538, b = 0.14 [0.11–0.18]; high: n = 889, b = 0.10 [0.04–0.15]; Pinteraction = 0.66).ConclusionsWe found small yet significant associations between prenatal stress and autism-related traits. Limited evidence for effect modification by sex or prenatal depressive symptoms suggests prenatal perceived stress influences autism-related traits after accounting for these factors.

Stress is a complex psychosocial construct measured in terms of physical strain, socioeconomic status, or psychological perception.1 Prenatal maternal stress can have lasting impacts on child health and has been associated with poorer executive and cognitive abilities2,3, more internalizing and externalizing behaviors4,5, increased likelihood of autism spectrum disorder (ASD)6,7, and severity of autism-related traits (ARTs).8,9 Studies of prenatal stress and child neurodevelopmental outcomes have primarily measured stress using the self-reported number of adverse life events or biological markers. For example, extreme weather10, death of a first-degree relative6, and legal troubles11 occurring during pregnancy have been associated with increased likelihood of ASD. Measures of prenatal perceived stress, such as the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)12, can instead capture meaningful variation related to appraisal of stressful events. However, only one study has examined prenatal perceived stress as a risk factor for ASD.13

Dysregulation of the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis is one proposed mechanism underlying associations between prenatal perceived stress and child neurodevelopmental outcomes. Stress exposure causes spikes in secretion of the adrenal glucocorticoids cortisol and cortisone14, which play critical roles in supporting neurodevelopmental processes (eg, neurogenesis, synaptogenesis15,16) and have been associated with increased likelihood of neurodevelopmental disorders.17 Another potential mechanism is maternal immune dysregulation18, in which chronic prenatal stress causes sustained cytokine and chemokine release, in turn increasing likelihood of neurodevelopmental disorders.19,20 Epigenetic modifications have also been associated with prenatal perceived stress and likely play a mediating role in associations with child outcomes21, for example by altering expression of the glucocorticoid receptor gene NR3C122 or serotonin transporter gene SERT.23

Sex differences in associations of prenatal exposures with neurodevelopmental disorders are of interest given strong evidence for an elevated male-to-female sex prevalence ratio in ASD.24 Biological hypotheses underlying this include the female protective effect25,26 and differences in fetal microglial activation and neuroinflammation.27,28 A recent study also reported higher autism heritability in males than females29, suggesting that nongenetic factors—including prenatal exposures such as stress—may influence the likelihood of neurodevelopmental outcomes in a sex-dependent manner.

Co-occurring maternal psychosocial exposures may also influence associations between prenatal perceived stress and ARTs. Prenatal depression and anxiety commonly co-occur with stress30 and have been associated with increased likelihood of neurodevelopmental disorders31,32 and severity of ARTs.33,34 However, no studies to date have investigated joint effects of multiple prenatal psychosocial exposures (eg, stress and depression symptoms) on ARTs, which could inform prenatal mental health screening and behavioral interventions aimed at improving child outcomes.

Prior studies of associations between prenatal stress and ARTs8,9 have been limited by relatively small sample sizes (largest n = 164), precluding evaluation of effect modifiers, and by a reliance solely on reported prenatal stressful life events as opposed to mothers’ perceived stress levels. The present study addresses these gaps using data from a large, population-based sample to examine associations of prenatal perceived stress with ARTs and evaluate effect modification by child sex and co-occurring prenatal depressive symptoms.

Methods

Data were used from the Environmental Influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) cohort, an NIH-funded consortium of sites across the US described previously.35–38 The study was approved by cohort-specific institutional review boards and/or the single ECHO institutional review board and written informed consent obtained for participation in both the ECHO protocol and specific cohorts.

Biological mother–child pairs met the following inclusion criteria: (1) mother reported on perceived stress during pregnancy, (2) pregnancy was of a single gestation, and (3) child had ART outcome data collected at ages 2.5–18 years old. For mothers with more than one child, we randomly selected one for inclusion in analyses. Sites with <20 eligible participants were excluded (Figure S1, available at www.jpeds.com). For analyses of effect modification by depressive symptoms, mothers also had to have completed the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) depressive symptom scale during pregnancy.

Prenatal Perceived Stress

Perceived stress was self-reported using Cohen’s PSS12 at any time during pregnancy. Three versions were collected as follows: the 4-item (PSS-4, n = 1093 [27%]), 10-item (PSS-10), n = 2786 [68%]), and 14-item (PSS-14), n = 236 [6%]). All items regarding ability to control one’s stress were scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from never (0) to very often (4). Item response theory was used to calculate scores using all available data and scores were harmonized to the perceived stress NIH Toolbox T-score (mean = 50; SD = 10). For mothers with multiple PSS measurements (n = 1398), we selected the earliest administered as repeated measures were correlated in our sample (r = 0.83). We modeled perceived stress as both a continuous and categorical variable with the following T-score cutoffs used to indicate severity: low (PSS <50), moderate (PSS ≥50 and < 60), and high (PSS ≥60).

Child Quantitative ARTs

The Social Responsiveness Scale, second Edition (SRS-2) was used to assess the presence and severity of social impairment and ARTs.39 The SRS-2 is a validated questionnaire with items reported on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (never true) to 3 (almost always true).40 We used the 65-item (long) and 16-item (short) parent-reported questionnaires; scores from the different versions are comparable.41 The school-aged form was used for children 4–18 years of age, and the preschool form for ages 2.5–4.5 years. Child ARTs were measured both as continuous SRS-2 T-scores (mean of 50 and SD of 10) and categorized at a cut-point of <65 (typical development) vs ≥65 (moderate-to-severe ARTs). Numerous validation studies have found strong agreement between this cut-off and ASD diagnosis.40,42 If more than one SRS-2 form type was completed (n = 71), we analyzed the school-age data.

Prenatal Depressive Symptoms

Prenatal depressive symptoms were assessed using the PROMIS v1.0 – Depression 8a (PDEP) T-score.43 Other instruments, including the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale, and Patient Health Questionnaire, were converted to the PROMIS T-score using crosswalk tables to facilitate the merging of data across sites.44,45 Standardized PROMIS T-scores have a mean of 50 and SD of 10 and the following cut-offs were used to indicate depressive symptom severity: T-score £55 = Normal, >55 = Mild, Moderate, or Severe.46 If multiple questionnaires were completed during pregnancy, the first was selected. The PDEP was administered at the same timepoint in pregnancy as the PSS for over 88% of mothers, and the mean difference in time for those measured before (5%) or after (7%) was 54 days.

Covariates

Covariates were ascertained from self-report and medical record abstraction data and included maternal education (high school/general educational development/equivalent or less, some college, Associate’s degree, or trade school, Bachelor’s degree, or graduate degree), race and ethnicity (self-reported and categorized as American Indian/Alaska Native American, Asian, Black, Multiple Races, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, Other Race, and White) and age at delivery (years). Child characteristics included age at SRS-2 administration (years), birth year (4 categories: 2003–2010, 2011–2014, 2015–2018, and 2019–2021), biological sex (male or female), race and ethnicity, and SRS-2 form type. Covariates with less than 20% missing data were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations47, and regression estimates pooled using Rubin’s rule.48

Statistical Analysis

We computed frequencies, percentages, means, and SDs of covariates and other variables of interest overall and stratified by categorical PSS. We used generalized estimating equations (GEEs) to model associations between PSS (continuous and categorical) and SRS-2 (continuous and categorical) T-scores, including cohort site as a cluster variable, robust standard errors, and an exchangeable correlation matrix. We produced a directed acyclic graph (DAG) using dagitty49 to inform selection of covariates for adjustment in GEE models (Figure 1) based on published associations and those observed in our data. Race and ethnicity were used only to describe the social composition of the sample. The final adjustment set included maternal age, education, child sex, birth year, age at SRS-2 administration, and SRS-2 form type.

We evaluated effect modification by child sex with an interaction term between continuous PSS T-scores and sex and sex-specific effect estimates among stratified samples. Effect modification by prenatal depressive symptoms was evaluated among the subsample of mother-child pairs with PDEP measurements also with an interaction term between continuous PSS T-scores and continuous PDEP T-scores and by comparing estimates from subgroups stratified by PDEP severity.

Sensitivity Analyses

We repeated analyses in the main sample after excluding 2 cohort sites that enrolled high familial-likelihood ASD families. To examine effects of individual cohort sites, we performed a leave-one-out analysis where we excluded one site at a time, refit adjusted models for the remaining sites and compared coefficients across resulting subgroups. We also conducted stratified analyses to evaluate differences by SRS-2 form type (preschool or school-age). Analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.250 (GEE analyses using the geeglm function from the “geekpack” package51–53 and multiple imputations using the pool function from the “mice” package47).

Results

The final sample included 4115 mother-child pairs from 20 ECHO sites (Figure S1, available at www.jpeds.com). Demographic information is reported in Table I. Compared with low prenatal stress, those with high stress were younger at delivery (29.0 [5.9] vs 30.3 [5.3] years), reported higher ratings of prenatal depressive symptoms (PDEP T-score mean [SD] = 57.7 [7.3] vs 46.8 [7.7]), and were less likely to have completed at least a bachelor’s degree (38% vs 59%). Comparing children of mothers in the high (PSS ≥60) vs low (PSS <50) stress groups, the age at SRS-2 administration was similar (mean [SD] = 6.6 [3.] vs 6.0 [3.1] years) and SRS-2 T-scores were higher (mean [SD] = 52.0 [10.2] vs 47.9 [8.0]). SRS-2 T-scores were lower for preschool compared with school-age SRS-2 forms (mean [SD] = 46.2 [8.0] vs 50.7 [8.7], respectively).

Associations of Prenatal Perceived Stress with ARTs

Table II shows associations between continuous and categorical prenatal perceived stress ratings and ART severity. After adjusting for all covariates, higher continuous prenatal perceived stress ratings were associated with small yet significant increases in child ART severity (β = 0.15 [95% CI, 0.13–0.18]). Categorical prenatal perceived stress was also associated with increased ART severity, with children born to mothers who reported either moderate or high prenatal perceived stress having significantly higher continuous SRS-2 T-scores than those in the low stress group (βModerate = 2.30 [1.50–3.10]; βHigh = 3.35 [2.57–4.13]) (Figure 2). Models of dichotomous SRS-2 T-scores produced consistent results: odds of children expressing at least moderate ARTs (SRS-2 T-score ≥65) increased significantly, by 5%, with each unit increase in continuous PSS T-score.

Associations between continuous PSS T-scores and continuous SRS-2 T-scores were significantly elevated for both males and females and higher in males, though the associations remained small in both sexes (βMales = 0.18 [95% CI, 0.14–0.22]; βFemales = 0.13 [0.10–0.16], interaction P value = .04). When modeling SRS-2 as a dichotomous outcome, there were no differences by sex in the odds of children expressing at least moderate ARTs (ORMales = 1.05 [1.03–1.06]; ORFemales = 1.04 [1.03–1.05], interaction P value = .58).

Effect Modification by Prenatal Depressive Symptoms

The subsample with PDEP T-score data included 3427 individuals across 18 cohorts with similar distributions of sociodemographic characteristics compared with the main sample. There was a significant moderate correlation between continuous PSS T-scores and continuous PDEP T-scores (r = 0.46, P value < .01 Crude and adjusted associations between continuous or categorical prenatal PSS T-scores and continuous child SRS-2 T-scores were similar to those from the main analyses. After stratifying the subsample into low (n = 2538) and high (n = 889) PDEP subgroups, associations between continuous PSS and continuous SRS-2 T-scores were attenuated but remained statistically significant and were similar to associations among the full subsample (n = 3427) (Table III). There was no evidence for effect modification by prenatal depressive symptoms from the fully adjusted model including an interaction term for PSS and PDEP (interaction P value = .66).

Sensitivity Analyses

After removing the 2 high familial-likelihood ASD sites, adjusted associations between PSS T-scores and SRS-2 T-scores (β = 0.15 [95% CI, 0.12–0.17]) were materially identical to those from the main analyses (β = 0.15 [0.13–0.18]). Results from leave-one-out analyses were also very similar, indicating no disproportional effects of individual sites on observed associations (Figure S4, available at www.jpeds.com). There were no differences in associations after stratifying by preschool and school-age SRS-2 form types as indicated by comparable effect estimates and overlapping confidence intervals (preschool: n = 1,529, β = 0.13 [0.10–0.17]; school-age: n = 2,586, β = 0.16 [0.13–0.19])

Discussion

Among a large, diverse sample of mother-child pairs from ECHO Cohort sites around the US, higher prenatal perceived stress was associated with small yet statistically significant increases in ART severity and increased likelihood of children expressing at least moderate trait severity. Although statistical evidence indicated slightly stronger associations among male compared with female children, estimated effects among each sex remained small and clinically nonsignificant. Prenatal depressive symptoms played little to no role in the relationship between prenatal stress and ARTs in our sample, highlighting the importance of disentangling contributions of co-occurring psychosocial exposures.

Past research on the relationship between prenatal stress and ASD or sub-clinical ARTs has focused primarily on acute stressors, such as natural disasters54,55 and legal troubles11, or cumulative number of adverse events8,56, with only one recent study examining associations between prenatal perceived stress and risk of ASD diagnosis.13 Among a sample of 261 mother-child pairs from a high familial-likelihood ASD cohort, the authors measured stress throughout pregnancy using both the PSS and urinary cortisol and found modest associations with increased ASD diagnosis risk for only higher PSS scores.13 We uniquely examined influences of prenatal perceived stress on child ART severity. Use of the standardized PSS may have enabled us to capture meaningful population-level variation in the severity attached to the perception of stressful experiences that self-reported counts of adverse events or biological measures (eg, saliva or blood cortisol levels) may fail to capture. The aforementioned recent study of perceived stress and ASD diagnosis risk found that the PSS had better predictive properties than urinary cortisol13, and other studies have reported mixed findings on the correlation between perceived stress and cortisol levels in hair and blood.57,58

We found small, statistically significant associations between continuous measures of prenatal perceived stress and ARTs. When modeling prenatal stress as a categorical exposure; however, moderate and high levels of prenatal perceived stress were associated with potentially clinically meaningful changes in ART severity. Exposure to high compared with low or no prenatal stress was associated with an over three-point increase in SRS-2 T-scores. This change could represent the difference between moderate and severe ARTs and, on the population level, an increase in the proportion of children above this cutoff. Behavioral interventions for prenatal stress could theoretically be informed by the PSS categories used here, with possible benefits for child neurodevelopmental outcomes. Our findings should be replicated and considered in the context of other child health outcomes prior to informing any clinical recommendations.

We found statistical evidence for elevated associations in males compared with females; however, sex-specific estimates remained very small in both sexes, with overlapping CIs. A previous ECHO study of associations between continuous prenatal depressive symptoms and ARTs measured with the SRS-2 also reported no differences by child sex, though point estimates were slightly elevated in boys.33 Of the handful of past studies that reported on sex differences related to prenatal stress, one found associations between third trimester cortisol levels and severity of autism-related traits at age 3 in boys only, but no differences at age 5 years.59 Others reported no differences in associations between stressful life events or perceived stress and likelihood of ASD6,11,13 or autism-related trait severity.9 Taken together, these findings suggest that the mechanisms underlying associations between prenatal stress and autism-related traits may not differ by fetal sex.

We did not find evidence for effect modification by prenatal depressive symptoms. Disentangling contributions of prenatal stress and depressive symptoms presents a considerable challenge, as it is not always clear whether they represent distinct constructs or should be considered confounders, mediators, or moderators (PSS and PDEP T-scores were significantly correlated in our sample, though only moderately [r = 0.46]). The lack of effect modification by prenatal depressive symptoms suggests a potentially independent association between prenatal perceived stress and child ARTs.

Study limitations include use of self- and maternal-reported measures of stress and ARTs. Although this may have introduced recall or social desirability biases, the use of self- and maternal-reported measures presents advantages compared with objective or biological measures of stress by capturing the appraisal of stressful events rather than occurrence alone. We did not examine effect modification by gestational timing of stress because the PSS asks about stress during the past month, making it challenging to define timing windows. Past studies found that elevated stress earlier in pregnancy may be particularly salient for child emotional and behavioral outcomes60, whereas others have suggested stressors occurring later in pregnancy are most relevant to autism risk given overlap with neuroanatomical changes to the cerebellum.61 We measured PDEP at the same time as PSS for the majority (88%) but not all mothers, which could have biased associations if PDEP lay on a causal pathway between prenatal stress and ARTs. We were unable to account for maternal psychiatric history such as diagnosis of depression or anxiety because of high missingness for these variables in ECHO. Lastly, there is potential for differences in measurement of autism-related traits across wide age ranges, but sensitivity analyses conducted only in school-age children were comparable with primary findings, suggesting any potential measurement differences across ages did not influence findings.

Study strengths include the use of a geographically as well as racially and ethnically broad, representative ECHO dataset.35,36 The large sample size enabled us to detect small effects as well as to investigate sex differences and perform multiple sensitivity analyses. Investigation of the moderating role of co-occurring prenatal depressive symptoms sheds light on the synergistic relationship with prenatal perceived stress and provides a more complete picture of the prenatal psychosocial environment than previous studies of stress or depression alone.

This study provides evidence for small yet statistically significant associations between prenatal perceived stress and ARTs that persisted after accounting for co-occurring prenatal depressive symptoms and did not differ by child sex. Further research, for example to evaluate differences by gestational timing of stress or social disparities (eg, perceived discrimination), is needed to advance understanding of the relationship between prenatal psychosocial exposures and neurodevelopmental outcomes.
