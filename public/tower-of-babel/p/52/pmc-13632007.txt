A bilingual cardiometabolic screening workflow during pediatric well-child visits: early experience from a hispanic-serving primary care clinic
Authors: Rayma Florez Marcano
Published: 2026

Introduction

Pediatric cardiometabolic risk factors, including excess weight, elevated blood pressure, dyslipidemia, physical inactivity, and impaired glucose metabolism, often begin during childhood and contribute to cardiovascular disease later in life [1]. Although well-child visits provide an important opportunity for systematic cardiometabolic prevention, comprehensive risk assessment is not consistently incorporated into routine pediatric primary care [2].

Implementation of structured prevention is particularly challenging in community clinics serving predominantly Spanish-speaking families, where limited time, staffing constraints, language barriers, and incomplete follow-up may hinder delivery of recommended preventive services. These real-world challenges may reduce opportunities for early identification and management of cardiometabolic risk factors.

The objective of this study was to describe the implementation of the Community Pediatric Cardiometabolic Prevention Model (CPCPM) during routine well-child visits and to summarize early cardiometabolic risk findings in a Hispanic-serving primary care clinic.

Methods

This descriptive, retrospective, single-center study was conducted at Guadalupe Medical Center, a community pediatric primary care clinic serving a predominantly Hispanic population in Dallas, Texas. The CPCPM was implemented between November 2023 and May 2025. During the study period, the clinic conducted approximately 10,000 pediatric visits, including approximately 2000 preventive visits performed by the investigator using the CPCPM workflow. A consecutive registry of the first 250 eligible children aged 2–17 years attending routine well-child visits was included in the analysis.

Universal screening included height, weight, body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, family history of hypertension and diabetes, physical activity assessment, and cardiovascular examination. BMI categories were defined according to CDC BMI-for-age percentiles [3], blood pressure according to the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics Clinical Practice Guideline [4], and elevated HbA1c according to American Diabetes Association criteria [5]. Sedentary behavior was defined as caregiver report of no regular physical activity outside the school curriculum, and abnormal lipid profiles according to age-specific pediatric reference values.

Selective evaluation included lipid profile testing, HbA1c measurement, electrocardiography, and specialist referral when clinically indicated. Lipid testing followed current American Academy of Pediatrics recommendations [6]; however, completion depended on insurance coverage because some caregivers declined testing when out-of-pocket payment was required. HbA1c testing was obtained only in children with overweight, obesity, or additional cardiometabolic risk factors, while electrocardiography was reserved for cardiovascular symptoms or abnormal physical examination findings. Children with excess weight received individualized nutrition and physical activity counseling, with follow-up recommended at four and six months. Referral to a registered dietitian or pediatric cardiologist was made when clinically indicated. Bilingual education included counseling in the family's preferred language and standardized Spanish-language educational materials.

Data were analyzed descriptively and are presented as frequencies and percentages.

Results

A consecutive registry of 250 children aged 2–17 years was included in the analysis. The study population comprised 121 (48.4%) girls and 129 (51.6%) boys. Most children came from Spanish-speaking households (175, 70.0%), followed by bilingual (67, 26.8%) and English-speaking households (8, 3.2%). A family history of hypertension and diabetes was reported in 90 (36.0%) and 19 (7.6%) children, respectively.

Universal screening identified a substantial burden of cardiometabolic risk factors. Overall, 113 children (45.2%) had excess weight, including 67 (26.8%) with overweight, 33 (13.2%) with obesity, and 13 (5.2%) with severe obesity. Elevated or hypertensive-range blood pressure values were recorded in 58 children (23.2%), including 28 (11.2%) with elevated blood pressure and 30 (12.0%) with blood pressure values in the hypertensive range; these measurements were used for risk identification and follow-up planning rather than to establish a diagnosis of hypertension. Sedentary behavior was reported in 119 children (47.6%).

Lipid profile testing was completed in 106 of 250 children (42.4%), of whom 32 (30.2%) had abnormal results. HbA1c testing was performed in 49 of 250 children (19.6%), with elevated values identified in 3 (6.1%).

Children continued routine care within primary care. Additional management included nutrition referral, repeat blood pressure or lipid evaluation, and pediatric cardiology or other specialty referral when clinically indicated.

Discussion

This descriptive implementation experience showed that a structured bilingual cardiometabolic screening workflow was incorporated into routine pediatric well-child visits, identifying a meaningful burden of excess weight, elevated blood pressure, sedentary behavior, and abnormal lipid findings in a predominantly Hispanic population. Rather than evaluating the effectiveness of an intervention, this report describes how systematic cardiometabolic screening was integrated into routine pediatric care and how risk identification supported individualized counseling, selective laboratory evaluation, and follow-up planning.

Low completion of lipid testing, largely related to insurance coverage and caregiver acceptance when out-of-pocket payment was required, highlights the practical challenges of translating pediatric prevention recommendations into routine clinical practice. The bilingual component included standardized Spanish-language educational materials and counseling delivered in the family's preferred language; however, its impact on caregiver understanding, satisfaction, or adherence was not evaluated.

This study is limited by its single-center, retrospective design, absence of a comparison group, incomplete laboratory testing, lack of longitudinal outcomes, and the absence of formal assessment of workflow time burden or implementation fidelity.

Conclusion

This descriptive implementation experience showed that routine well-child visits provide an opportunity to systematically identify cardiometabolic risk factors in children within a Hispanic-serving primary care clinic. The findings also highlight real-world barriers to completing laboratory evaluation and follow-up, underscoring the challenges of translating pediatric prevention recommendations into routine clinical practice. Further multicenter studies are needed to evaluate the reproducibility and longitudinal impact of this workflow.
