A quantitative model of enzyme-free copying of RNA with dimers
Authors: Ludwig Burger, Franziska Welsch, Eric Kervio, Marc Henker, Gabrielle Leveau, Ulrich Gerland, Clemens Richert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe transfer of genetic information from one RNA strand to a daughter strand in the absence of polymerases could have initiated prebiotic evolution. This reaction, driven by base pairing and chemical reactivity alone, is slow and low yielding. The molecular basis of its inefficiency has remained unclear. We conducted a systems chemistry analysis of the most effective enzyme-free RNA copying system to date. This system uses the strongly pairing dimers (CC, CG, GC, and GG) and in situ activation. Rate constants for activation were obtained from nuclear magnetic resonance-monitored model reactions, effective dissociation constants in the sub-millimolar range were obtained from inhibitor assays, and rates of phosphodiester formation for bound and activated dimers were determined by fitting extension kinetics, with the latter ranging from 1.4–16 × 10−3 h−1. Using a kinetic model that incorporates all experimentally determined parameters, we simulated primer extension. This identified phosphodiester formation on templates as the rate-limiting step. Our model shows copying of up to 12 template bases through a combination of primer extension, dimer–dimer coupling, and fragment ligation steps. The fluxes through reaction channels provide an unprecedented view of genetic copying in a protein-free RNA system. Our analysis identifies the remaining bottlenecks of enzyme-free pathways and provides the basis for rationally searching for more efficient self-replicating systems.

Introduction

Transmitting genetic information from one generation to the next is an indispensable part of life. How this process occurred before protocells had developed a genetic machinery is unclear. There is reason to believe that the first genetic material was RNA [1–3]. So, the question of the origin of the transfer of genetic information may be reduced to the question of the replication of RNA sequences. How could the information encoded in a sequence of oligoribonucleotide bases undergo readout before encoded polymerases could catalyze the process [4]? How did molecular recognition and phosphodiester formation occur, and what reaction conditions supported the processes? Experimental work has provided at least partial answers to these questions, and a combination of simulation and experiments may improve our understanding of such processes further [5–8].

Before genetic copying could set in, the strands to be copied must have come into being. Fortunately, significant experimental evidence supports the notion that RNA can form spontaneously from ribonucleotides in enzyme-free media. For example, it was shown that strands can form through abiotic oligomerization of chemically activated ribonucleotides, either with the help of clay minerals as catalysts [9–12] or in homogeneous aqueous solution with in situ activation [13, 14]. Once strands exist, copying may set in through the template-directed formation or extension of (oligo)nucleotides bound on a template. Early work focused on homopolymeric sequences, particularly poly(C) as a template for the oligomerization of GMP [15, 16], which produces long strands via reactions studied on a quantitative level. Unfortunately, their homopolymeric nature makes them useless for encoding genetic information. The advent of routine solid-phase synthesis of oligonucleotides made assays producing defined sequences at a specific locus of a template possible. One form of these assays uses self-templating hairpins [17]; the more common version uses an RNA primer bound to a template [18].

Primer extension in the absence of polymerases has been demonstrated for RNA in a number of different ways [19], but many extension reactions were found to be inefficient, with just a few nucleotides being added until the copying activity ended because the activated nucleotides were depleted [20]. There have been recent findings, e.g. for prolinyl nucleotides, which allow incorporation of all four bases (A/C/G/U) [21] without the need for immobilization of strands [22], but overall, yields were modest, even after long reaction times. So, inefficiency remains a major issue in the field. Unless yields of individual primer extension steps are high, it is difficult to see how the genetic information of sequences long enough to act as even a primitive gene can be transmitted effectively [23].

One approach that has proven fruitful in the quest to solve the copying problem is to employ a combination of mononucleotides and short oligomers as building blocks in enzyme-free primer extension [24]. Mixtures of chain lengths had been discussed early on [22] and seem reasonable, assuming that a distribution of chain lengths is formed in the oligomerization reactions that produce the templates. Recently, we employed dimers or combinations of monomers and dimers as building blocks for copying of RNA [25]. With this, and a focus on the strongly pairing bases (G and C), up to 12 bases were copied from the template in one run when in situ activation with 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) is combined with organocatalysis by N-ethylimidazole (EtIm). Monomers can contribute to primer extension under those reaction conditions, but do not outcompete dimers [25]. Trimers also react. What factors in the activation and ligation chemistry made this approach more successful than earlier attempts was unclear, though, motivating us to initiate the study on which we report here: a study undertaken to get a quantitative understanding of the processes underlying high-yielding dimer-based copying (Fig. 1).

Steps of the enzyme-free extension of an RNA primer by a dinucleotide, as directed by an RNA template. The non-covalent binding step occurs with the strength expressed in the dissociation constant (Kd). The 5′-terminal phosphate of the dinucleotide can be chemically activated with the help of a condensing agent and a leaving group or organocatalyst, and the activated form can then react with the 3′-terminus of the primer to give the extended primer shown on the right-hand side.

One aspect of the activation chemistry that was reasonably clear was that in situ activation reduces the likelihood of competitive inhibition by spent monomers [22, 26] that increasingly block the extension sites for primer extension as assays progress and hydrolysis reduces the concentration of preactivated species. Another aspect that was reasonably well understood was that organocatalysis by 1-alkylimidazoles makes phosphodiester formation more productive, as it improves the ratio between extension and hydrolysis reactions [27]. But this combination of factors had been known since 2015 and had not produced long copying reads. Unless a model was developed that quantitatively simulates (and predicts) the molecular processes occurring when long RNA reads with dimers were taking place, it would be difficult to progress further.

The study whose results are presented here involved determining rate constants for each of the reactions critical for phosphodiester formation in aqueous condensation buffer. Further, binding constants were determined for each of the dimers required to copy sequences with the strongly pairing bases (G and C). This proved particularly difficult for self-pairing sequences, where other binding equilibria than building block–template pairing come into play. Furthermore, we determined rates for cyclization of dimers, another phenomenon competing with the desirable process of primer extension, and the rate constants of primer extension by EDC-activated dinucleotides. The latter rate constants allowed us to quantify the relative contribution of EDC-activated and EtIm-activated dinucleotides to primer extension to accurately pinpoint the organocatalytic effect of EtIm. These experimental constants then enabled us to simulate successful copying, and to eventually quantitatively predict how other sequences will fare in enzyme-free copying. Besides a better understanding of the copying process, there were also new qualitative insights, as we found chain extension to occur by both direct primer extension and by ligations within non-covalent helices formed by dimers and the template.

Materials and methods

General

Reagents and anhydrous solvents were acquired from Sigma/Aldrich/Fluka (Deisenhofen, Germany), Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), VWR Chemicals (Darmstadt, Germany), or Acros/Thermo Fisher and were used as received. Templates and primers were from Biomers (Ulm, Germany) and were converted to their sodium salt form via cation exchange. Therefore, a cartridge (Waters; Sep Pak VAC C18 3 cc, 500 mg) was treated with acetonitrile (2 mL), washed with water (10 mL, HPLC grade) and NaCl aqueous solution (10 mL, 1 M). Each oligonucleotide was dissolved in aqueous NaCl (0.5 mL, 1 M) and applied to the cartridge, followed by washing with NaCl solution (10 mL, 1 M) and water (10 mL). Elution was achieved with a mixture of acetonitrile in water [20%, (v/v)]. Pure fractions were pooled and lyophilized to dryness. The quantification of oligonucleotides was performed using an ND-1000 spectrophotometer from NanoDrop Technologies (Wilmington, USA), with measurements at 260 nm. The synthesis of 5′-phosphorylated dimers was performed according to a procedure previously described [28] on an H-2 synthesizer (K&L Labs, Schaafheim, Germany), using the protocols recommended by the manufacturer. The C18 cartridges employed for purification were rinsed with acetonitrile (2 mL), and equilibrated with water (10 mL) prior to use. The sample was applied as aqueous solution (500 µL), followed by washing with water (10 mL), and eluting with an acetonitrile gradient in water (2%, 5% and then 10%). Pure fractions were combined and lyophilized. Quantification was via UV absorbance, and purity was checked via MALDI-TOF MS.

Oxyazabenzotriazole activation of dinucleotides

The following protocol for the conversion of a 5′-phosphorylated dinucleotide to its oxyazabenzotriazole (OAt) ester [29, 30] is for the activation of CG to OAt-CG and is representative. The OAt group is a good leaving group in phosphodiester-forming reactions [29]. The OAt-activated dimers were used to study dimer binding, not for unraveling the in situ activation chemistry. In the event, the reaction producing the title compounds was carried out in nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) tubes to allow for monitoring the conversion to the product. In the event, 1-hydroxy-7-azabenzotriazol (HOAt, 10.2 mg, 75 μmol, 5 eq) and 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCl, 15.8 mg, 75 μmol, 5 eq) were dissolved in a solution of NaOH (1 M, 50 μl) in D2O (950 μl) to obtain a solution at a pH value above 5. The dinucleotide CG (3 μmol, 1 eq) was quickly dissolved in the activation mixture (200 μl) at r.t., and reaction progress was monitored by 1H and 31P NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. S1). After full conversion, the OAt-activated dinucleotide was precipitated out of a solution of sodium perchlorate (1 mg, 6.8 mM) in ice-cold acetone/diethyl ether (4/1, v/v, 1.2 mL). The precipitate was harvested through centrifugation and washed four times with cold mixtures of acetone/diethyl ether (4/1, v/v, 1 mL). The solid was then dissolved in water (1 mL) and lyophilized. For purification, a C18 cartridge (SepPak® Vac C18 3 cc/500mg) was rinsed with acetonitrile (2 mL), water (10 mL), and NaCl solution (1 M, 10 mL). The activated dinucleotide was taken up in NaCl solution (1 M, 1 mL) and loaded on the cartridge. The loaded cartridge was washed with NaCl (1 M, 10 mL) and water (10 mL). The product was eluted with an acetonitrile gradient 2%–10% in water and collected in the cold. Product-containing fractions were detected by UV/Vis absorption and MALDI-TOF MS, combined, and lyophilized.

NMR spectroscopy

Samples were prepared in 3 × 100 mm glass microtubes from Hilgenberg (Malsfeld, Germany). Spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance III system (700 MHz for ¹H, 284 MHz for ³¹P) or on a Bruker Avance III HD-NanoBay spectrometer (400 MHz for ¹H, 162 MHz for ³¹P). Coupling constants are reported in Hertz (Hz), and chemical shifts in delta (δ) parts per million, referenced to either the solvent peak or an internal standard.

MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry

Spectra were measured on a microflex LRF spectrometer from Bruker Daltonics (Bremen, Germany) or an AXIMA Confidence spectrometer (Shimadzu Deutschland, Duisburg, Germany). A 0.5 μl aliquot of the sample was applied to the target plate, followed by drying and mixing with 0.5 μl of matrix solution on the target. For mass ranges above 1 kDa, a matrix consisting of 2,4,6-trihydroxyacetophenone (0.3 M in ethanol) and diammonium citrate (0.1 M in water) at a 2:1 (v/v) ratio was used. For measurements below 1 kDa, 9-aminoacridine (60 mM in methanol) served as a matrix. Aliquots of assay solutions were diluted with water (1:5 or 1:50), treated with 2–5 mg of cation-exchange resin (DOWEX 50WX8, NH4+ form), and incubated for 10 min. Oligonucleotides were measured under conditions for quantitative MALDI-TOF MS [31].

Rate constants for in situ activation

The following protocol is for AMP, EDC, and 1-ethylimidazole (EtIm) and is representative. A stock solution was prepared containing 3-(N-morpholino)propanesulfonic acid (MOPS) (1.25 M), EtIm (187.5 mM), MgCl2 (100 mM), and D2O (50 μl) at pH 6. To 450 μl of this solution, AMP (7 mg, 0.02 mmol) and EDC·HCl (77 mg, 400 μmol) were added. The mixture was then diluted with H2O to a final volume of 500 μl, resulting in final concentrations of MOPS (1 M), EtIm (100 mM), MgCl2 (80 mM), AMP (40 mM), and EDC (800 mM). The reaction was monitored over 24 h by ¹H and ³¹P NMR spectroscopy. The methyl resonances of EtIm, EDC, and 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)urea (EDU), the product of EDC when it acts as a condensing agent, were integrated, and the combined signal of EDC and EDU was normalized to 0.8 M for quantification. Similarly, the phosphorus signals were integrated and normalized to 40 mM to obtain the concentrations of AMP, EDC-AMP, EtIm-AMP, and AppA. Due to partial overlap between the AppA and EtIm-AMP peaks, the deconvolution tool of the Bruker software of the spectrometer was employed to quantify these two species.

Ligation assay and quantitative analysis

The following protocol is for the ligation of CG, as shown in Fig. 5, and is a modified version of that reported by Sosson et al. [28]. Stock solutions of primer, helper strand UCGU (simulating neighboring dimers), and the template were combined to obtain final strand concentrations of 30, 60, and 45 μM, respectively. The mixture was heated to 80°C for 5 min, allowed to cool to 25°C, and then cooled to 4 °C, followed by the addition of stock solutions of dimer CG to give a final concentration of 2 mM. Then, a buffer solution was added to give 0.15 M 1-ethylimidazole, 0.08 M MgCl2, and 0.5 M MOPS, followed by a freshly prepared solution of EDC hydrochloride to yield a final carbodiimide concentration of 0.8 M. The reaction mixture was then adjusted to a total volume of 10 μl with water and brought to pH 6.0 with HCl (1 M). The solution was vortexed and kept at 4°C. Reaction progress was analyzed via MALDI-TOF MS, and the progress of cyclization was detected in the low-mass range of MALDI-TOF mass spectra acquired with 9-aminoacridine as matrix, as described above.

Rate constants for primer extension by dinucleotides

Rate constants for the template-directed extension by any of the four dinucleotides (CC, CG, GC, GG) in reaction buffer were obtained from MALDI-TOF MS-monitored assays under two different conditions: (i) with EDC alone and (ii) with EDC and EtIm as organocatalyst. The kinetic model (vide infra) was fitted to the experimental data to extract rate constants for cyclization and template-directed ligation. In this analysis, the rate constants for the in situ activation chemistry (Table 1) were treated as fixed parameters. The rate constants for phosphodiester bond formation between primer and EDC-activated dinucleotide (kcov, EDC) or EtIm-activated dinucleotide (kcov, EtIm), as well as the rate constants for cyclization (kcyc, EDC and kcyc, EtIm), were treated as adjustable parameters. For any given set of these parameters, the kinetic model simulates the time evolution of both cyclic dinucleotide and extended primer concentrations under either assay condition (EDC only and EDC with EtIm). Weighted sum of squared deviations between the simulated and experimentally measured concentrations was computed. The best-fit rate constants, i.e. those minimizing the total discrepancy across both products and both conditions, are reported in Table 2. Further details on the fitting procedure are detailed in the Supplementary data.

Inhibitor assays

Ligation assays were performed as described above, except that the pH was adjusted to pH 8.9 in aqueous HEPES buffer (200 mM), and the final assay conditions were as follows for template (40 μM), primer (30 μM), and helper (40 μM) in HEPES buffer (200 mM) containing NaCl (400 mM), MgCl2 (80 mM), and the OAt-ester (2 mM) of the respective dinucleotide. Different concentrations of unactivated 5′-phosphorylated dinucleotides were added as inhibitors. The assays were performed in a final volume of 5 μl in polypropylene vessels, and aliquot analyzed by MALDI-TOF MS as previously described [25] (Supplementary Fig. S2). The mathematical treatment of experimental data to determine the dissociation constants (Supplementary Fig. S3) was also adapted from a method described previously [26].

Simulation—primer extension by a single dinucleotide

The kinetic model describing the template-directed extension of a primer by a single dinucleotide simulates the time evolution of all relevant chemical species under the assay conditions, including in situ activation of 5′-phosphates (Fig. 2). It is based on mass action kinetics, where each reaction rate is proportional to the product of the reactant concentrations and a rate constant (e.g. ). Activation is assumed to have sequence-independent rate constants (Table 1). Dimer cyclization and covalent bond formation with the primer are treated as sequence-dependent. The model also includes the formation of G-quadruplexes from GG dimers (rate constant ), as well as the reversible association of a dimer with the template–primer complex. Hybridization is restricted to a single site downstream of the primer, and the primer remains hybridized to the template at all times. Hybridization and dehybridization are assumed to be fast relative to the chemical reactions () [32], allowing for an adiabatic approximation: at each time point, (de)hybridization is in equilibrium, and the slower chemical reactions occur based on these equilibrium concentrations. The equilibrium is computed analytically by combining the law of mass action with mass conservation constraints. To obtain fluxes, chemical rate equations are integrated numerically using the Runge–Kutta method of order 5(4) (RK45), as implemented in the “SciPy” package [33]. Each time step requires two substeps: (i) computation of the fast (de)hybridization equilibrium and (ii) integration of the reaction progress, using the equilibrium concentrations. This is repeated iteratively until the final simulation time is reached. Further details on the simulation study are presented in the Supplementary Data and the Supplementary Figures.

Simulation—primer extension by multiple dinucleotides

We expanded the kinetic model to describe the incorporation of up to six consecutive dinucleotides (12 bases in total). The extended model accounts for hybridization of multiple dinucleotides or longer oligomers to the template up to 12 nucleotides downstream of the primer. Each oligomer can exist in various chemical states (unactivated, EDC-activated, EtIm-activated, or covalently linked), giving rise to 32 560 distinct template–primer–oligomer complexes (see Supplementary data for more details on how the complexes are enumerated). Dimers and oligomers are allowed to undergo chemical reactions while hybridized to the template. These include activation by EDC, conversion to EtIm amidate, deactivation/hydrolysis, covalent ligation to the 3′-terminus of the primer (primer extension), and ligation between adjacent hybridized dimers to form longer oligonucleotides. For example, a tetranucleotide can be formed via ligation of two adjacent dimers, and even longer oligomers (up to 12 nucleotides) are formed via successive template-directed ligations. We note that non-templated ligation reactions are assumed to only produce tetramers, as the formation of longer products in solution is assumed negligible due to the low educt concentrations. As in the single extension model, hybridization and dissociation are fast compared to chemical reactions, allowing for an adiabatic approximation. For each oligomer species (characterized by sequence, length, and activation state), total concentration is conserved across free and template-bound forms. Dissociation constants involving longer oligomers were estimated using a nearest-neighbor-like approximation, with the binding energy of a longer oligomer being approximated as the sum of the binding energies of its constituent dimers. As in the single-extension model, the full system of chemical rate equations is integrated numerically using the Runge–Kutta method (RK45) [33], and each time step consists of two substeps: (i) computation of the hybridization equilibrium via numerical root finding (Levenberg–Marquardt method as implemented in the “SciPy” package [33]) and (ii) numerical integration of the chemical reactions using the concentrations from the first step.

Results

In order to simulate the processes leading to successful copying with dimers on a quantitative level, rate constants for the individual steps were measured. Primer extension is a template-directed reaction, and one set of constants required to simulate it are the binding or dissociation constants (Kd). These had to be determined for the hybridization of each of the four C/G-based dimers (CC, CG, GC, and GG) to their cognate primer–template complexes. The other set of constants required are rate constants for the chemical steps involved in the activation of the 5′-phosphates and coupling of their activated form to the 3′-diol to give the phosphodiester. The latter steps are more complex for copying occurring under in situ activation than for reactions with preactivated building blocks. This is particularly true in the current case, the most effective form of enzyme-free RNA copying, using in situ activation [28, 34], as it involves organocatalysis or organocapture [27] by N-alkylimidazoles. Less effective forms, using preactivated building blocks, have been studied on a quantitative level before [26, 35].

Quantitative analysis of activation chemistry

The activation chemistry involves labile intermediates, such as isoureas formed between phosphates and carbodiimides, which are not detectable by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, since they are hydrolyzing during sample preparation and loading. This is why we chose to study the activation chemistry in a model system monitored by NMR spectroscopy as a non-invasive technique. The model system consisted of a mononucleotide, namely, AMP, and all components of the condensation buffer optimized for copying with dimers [28]. We assumed that the activation steps would be largely independent of whether the 5′-phosphate is part of a template-bound dimer or a mononucleotide in solution, as the phosphate points into solvent in either case. The full primer extension system is unsuitable for monitoring by NMR, containing too many nucleotides, resulting in crowded spectra in which low-level intermediates are all but impossible to detect. Figure 2 gives an overview of the chemical steps considered, and selected NMR spectra employed to detect species observable by NMR are shown in Fig. 3. Additional spectra and fits to the data can be found in Supplementary Figs S9–S18.

Determining rate constants for reactions involved in the in situ activation chemistry. (A) Reactions monitored. (B) Overlay of 31P-NMR spectra for the activation of AMP with EDC in the presence of EtIm with peaks labeled as indicated in Fig. 2.

The monitoring of AMP under buffer conditions included observation of phosphodiester formation so that the rate constant of the untemplated reaction (kAA) could be used in the simulations for the background oligomerizations in the carbodiimide-containing medium. Measurements in the 31P and 1H channels used a methodology similar to that of Tremmel et al. and the nucleotide employed in that study [27], but the current set of assays was performed under mild acidic conditions and had a different focus, chemically. Reaction mixtures of increasing complexity were monitored, starting with the hydrolysis of the carbodiimide (kh0). Attack of AMP on EDC produces an isourea (EDC-AMP, with kE as rate constant), which, in turn, either dissociates again, hydrolyzes to AMP and urea EDU, or reacts in productive fashion. Two pathways toward a phosphodiester (AA) were considered. Either a diol of another AMP molecule attacks directly, or an organocatalytic intermediate forms by reacting with EtIm. The former reaction is very slow in the absence of a template, the latter is more efficient, and in the absence of a good nucleophile, significant concentrations of EtIm-AMP were found to build up. Other competing reactions that were included in our analysis involve pyrophosphate formation (AppA with kEPP or kAppAvia the organocatalytic path). Besides hydrolysis steps, reversible attack of ethylimidazole on EDC to give EtIm-EDC, a latent form of EDC, was also included in the reaction scheme. We note that, unlike the 2-aminoimidazole activation chemistry [20], EtIm-based primer extension does not involve imidazolium-bridged dinucleotides.

The full set of rate constants obtained are listed in Table 1. They were used without further modification for the simulations of primer extension by dimers, based on the assumption that the steps involved are independent of whether the phosphate being activated was attached to a mono-, di-, or oligonucleotide. Covalent bond formation in template-directed fashion and cyclization were determined independently for each dimer, however, using MALDI-TOF-based assays (Fig. 5 for CG and Supplementary Figs S19 and S20 for CC, GC, and GG), in order to properly treat the template effect for each of the building blocks (vide infra).

Binding of dimers to template

The greatest challenge in the experimental part of our study was to obtain reliable binding constants (or dissociation constants, Kd) for the complexes of CG, GC, and GG with the respective primer–template duplexes. The self-complementary dimers engage in self-pairing, and extensive attempts to determine binding by the established NMR titration method [35] were unsuccessful because three different equilibria compete in the same solution for CG and GC, namely, self-pairing of the dimers, self-pairing of the overhangs of the hairpins employed as primer–template models, and the interspecies pairing between dimers and hairpins. All attempts to deconvolute the complicated datasets that were further plagued by line broadening were unsuccessful, yielding questionable Kd values only. Likewise, titration attempts with GG were complicated by the propensity of this dimer to assemble into higher-order structures of the G-quartet/G quadruplex type.

Therefore, we decided to obtain effective dissociation constants (Kdeff) via assays in the presence of increasing concentrations of unactivated dimer as inhibitor (Fig. 4). Here, preactivated dimers were allowed to react with the appropriate primer–template combination for each dimer, and the solutions were spiked with the inhibitors. The strength of binding of the unactivated dimers manifests itself in the inhibition of the extension reaction, as unactivated dimers can occupy the extension site, blocking it without reacting. This methodology has previously been described for monomers [35]. For GG, a partial partitioning into quadruplex structures was assumed, which are unable to bind to templates and are thus unproductive. Data from this phase of our project are shown in Fig. 4B and Table 2.

Rates of primer extension and cyclization of dimers

Another rate that could not be determined in the AMP-based system was cyclization of dimers to 3′-5′-linked cyclic dinucleotides. The intramolecular attack of the 3′-hydroxy group on the activated phosphate is insignificant for mononucleotides. It also depends on the binding status (template bound or free in solution) for dimers. Accordingly, the rate of cyclization was determined by analyzing mass spectra from samples of extension assays for individual dimers/templates with a suitable matrix, as shown in Fig. 5C. In the high-mass region, primer extension itself was measured in the presence of a tetramer helper strand that simulates the neighboring dinucleotides when studying multiple extension systems. All MALDI spectra were measured under conditions that allow for quantitative detection [31].

To analyze primer extension by the individual dimers quantitatively, we devised a detailed kinetic model, as described in the “Materials and methods” section. The model accounts for all aforementioned chemical reactions, i.e. (i) the in situ activation of the dinucleotide by EDC and EtIm, (ii) inactivation of activated dinucleotide via hydrolysis, cyclization, and non-templated oligomerization, and (iii) the extension of the primer by activated dinucleotides. The dinucleotides can be either in solution or bound to the template–primer complex, and the equilibrium of association and dissociation is assumed to be fast, as compared to any chemical reaction in the system. For GG, we account for the formation of quadruplexes, which reduce the concentration of available dinucleotides. Making use of the experimentally determined rate constants (Table 1), we fitted the kinetic model to experimental data for primer extension, as shown in Fig. 6 for CG and in Supplementary data for CC, GC, and GG. Simulation of extension by a single dimer

Integrating the coupled differential equations of the model then allowed for the calculation of fluxes through the individual reaction channels, providing a unique look at the reaction network underlying a single extension step and its bottlenecks. Figure 7 shows a flux map obtained for a reaction time of 432 h for dimer CG and the template and primer of Fig. 5A. Overall, the covalent bond formation between primer and dinucleotide appears to be the limiting step across all dinucleotides studied. Specifically, for the dinucleotide to extend the primer, the dimer hybridizes to the template, gets activated by EDC, with the isourea rapidly being displaced by the organocatalyst EtIm. The EtIm-activated dinucleotide then forms the diester bond with the primer in ∼30% of all activation events for CG or, more likely, loses its leaving group again via hydrolysis (∼70% of all activation events for CG). So, despite the reduced hydrolysis rate of the EtIm leaving group, as compared to the isourea, primer extension is limited by the slow formation of the phosphodiester bond. From the fluxes, we estimate that one CG on the template typically needs to go through about three cycles of reactivation (ratio between 0.11 mM/18 days and 0.03 mM/18 days) until it has successfully extended the primer. Even more cycles are necessary in the case of the less reactive GC and CC dinucleotides (∼27 cycles for CC, 10 cycles for GC). Unlike in a system with preactivated nucleotides, in situ activation allows such repeated activation of the dinucleotide, this allowing for higher yields.

Simulation of multiple extensions

In order to assess whether the kinetic parameters obtained explain the high yield observed in multistep primer extension of Fig. 8A [25], we extended our kinetic model, as described in the “Materials and methods” section. We now considered the full length of the template downstream of the primer binding site, allowing up to six dinucleotides to hybridize simultaneously. For simplicity, we assumed that only dinucleotides that are complementary to the template hybridize. As in the single-extension model, dinucleotides can undergo activation or deactivation in solution or while being bound to the template. An activated dinucleotide adjacent to the primer can extend the latter. Further, two adjacent dinucleotides may ligate if the downstream dinucleotide is activated and so do longer oligonucleotides. Using the parameters of Tables 1 and 2, this allowed us to compute the concentration of extended primer (extended by up to 12 nt) as a function of time. We find that the final product distribution predicted by the model agrees reasonably well with the experimental data from quantitative MALDI-TOF MS and HPLC (Fig. 8B), validating our model. The overview of what ligations occur during an assay is best seen in the flux map of Fig. 8C.

Interestingly, the concentrations of partially extended products do not follow a simple exponential distribution, but each extension step has a different rate and hence concentration at a given time point. Strands of intermediate length ending in GG (P+4, P+8, P+12) tend to have lower concentrations than products ending in CG. The kinetic model suggests that this effect can be attributed to the incorporation of GG acting as a bottleneck in the copying. As GG is likely to be partially partitioning into quadruplexes, little GG is available for primer extension, and the corresponding fluxes (P+2 + GG → P+4, P+6 + GG → P+8, and P+10 + GG → P+12) are far smaller than if GG was fully available. The system compensates for this by forming the same products via primer extension with longer oligonucleotides, e.g. two tetramers forming octamers, which circumvents the slow incorporation of the GG dimer. However, this effect is not strong enough to mitigate the bias against GG-terminating products entirely. More generally, Fig. 8C shows that the full-length product is not only produced by adding one dinucleotide after another sequentially, but also to a substantial part by extending the primer by oligonucleotides that form via ligation of shorter oligonucleotides on the template. Thus, copying proceeds as a “parallelized” process with multiple alternative pathways toward the full copy, and not by sequential addition of dimers only.

Discussion

Inefficiency has plagued enzyme- and ribozyme-free copying of RNA for as long as the phenomenon has been known [17, 23, 24]. Several factors had previously been discussed that may be the underlying cause of stalling before the full length of templates had been written in the form of a complementary strand. Among them was weak base pairing, particularly of U and A, the bases with just two hydrogen bonds. Further, slow reactions due to the poor nucleophilicity of the primer terminus and the modest leaving group properties of imidazolides and other organic leaving groups were mentioned in the literature, requiring transitions to more activated forms that may be of too low a concentration to achieve rapid phosphodiester formation. Finally, inhibition by spent (unactivated) building blocks was known to induce stalling, and so was the structural change caused by misincorporations [5]. It was unclear what the limiting factor was and why the dimer-based copying studied here was more successful than the monomer-based assays known from the literature and our own work.

The motivation of the current study was to get a grip on these issues by obtaining a quantitative, systems chemistry picture of what processes occur when a longer stretch of template sequence is copied with double-digit overall yield [25]. This is what our results provide. The picture is interesting, resolving several of the issues that had remained open.

On a chemical level, our results show that imidazolium organocatalysis is more significant, but not the only reaction pathway. The imidazolium phosphates have a more favorable reactivity profile than the isoureas in solution, giving seven-fold faster untemplated coupling, but an unchanged rate of hydrolysis (Table 1). On the template, the difference between reactivity of isoureas and imidazolium forms of dinucleotides is smaller (less than a factor of two), with the former being more reactive in three out of four cases (Table 2). The isourea does not contribute significantly to extension, though, as it is rapidly converted to the imidazolium phosphate. Further, the main side reaction for dimers is cyclization to 5′-3′-cyclic dinucleotides (Fig. 5). These cyclic molecules, which have interesting roles in extant biology [36], are unlikely to bind with both nucleobases to the template, so that their formation lowers the concentration of available dimers, but does most probably not cause significant inhibition. This is consistent with the finding that dimers are more successful in copying than monomers [25, 35].

Another surprise was how strongly the dimers bind. The technical difficulties of obtaining binding or dissociation constants, due to complex self-pairing equilibria and portioning into higher-order structures, like quadruplexes, have been mentioned above. This is why the effective dissociation constants, obtained by inhibitor assays (Fig. 4), with spiking under assay conditions and measuring the decrease in primer extension, are invaluable (Table 2). They confirm that the most strongly stacking GG dimers hybridize very strongly, mirroring the preference for G found in monomer-based assays [9] and the order of Kd values determined for monomers [35]. All four dimers (CC, CG, GC, and GG) form complexes with the template–primer duplex with sub-millimolar dissociation constants. Because the dimers were employed at 2 mM concentration each, we assume that the template is almost completely covered with dimers and oligomers, resulting in a non-covalent duplex as relevant species. In other words, binding is not the bottleneck of copying with C/G dimers.

Not entirely unexpected, but now visible in stark numerical format, phosphodiester formation on the template is the bottleneck of enzyme-free copying, even for dimers (Fig. 7). It was known that RNA ligations are less efficient than DNA ligations [37], but rate constants were rare. Differences in conformation and conformational flexibility are likely causes of this phenomenon.

Perhaps the most unexpected result from the simulation was that the system can partially compensate for difficult extension steps by first performing dimer–dimer couplings (which appear to be less problematic, probably due to greater conformational flexibility, facilitating placing the leaving group in an apical position through Berry pseudorotation), followed by extension of the primer by a tetramer. Ligation events appear to take place all along the non-covalent helix of dimers and oligomers on the template, not just via consecutive steps of adding one dimer at a time.

Conclusions

The results presented here provide an unparalleled molecular view of how the most effective enzyme- and ribosome-free copying of mixed RNA sequences occurs. Dimers with G and C as nucleobases bind so strongly that a non-covalent helix is likely to exist as soon as the rapid hybridization equilibrium has established itself. Then, activation/deactivation cycles occur that occasionally produce ligation events at different positions in the non-covalent assembly. The final product forms through different combinations of ligation events. The covalent steps are the bottleneck of the system, and the main reaction path is via the imidazolium phosphates as activated species, quickly produced from the initial isoureas. Cyclization of dimers and eventual loss of the carbodiimide fuel still limit the overall yield, but without significant inhibition. So, the dimer/in situ activation system is high yielding because it avoids inhibition and uses simultaneous ligations at different loci, overcoming several limitations of monomer-based systems. We assume that our quantitative data provide opportunities to do more extensive modeling both for further optimization and for searches in sequence space. A study on these topics is underway in our laboratories.
