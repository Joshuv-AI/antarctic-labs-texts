Recent advances in pulmonary fibrosis: from lung surfactant to the immune connection
Authors: Paula Losada-Oliva, Chiara Autilio, Barbara Olmeda, Teresa Carbone, Michele Gilio, Vito Pafundi, Elena Aloisio, Mauro Panteghini, Jesus Perez-Gil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) encompasses a heterogeneous group of progressive interstitial lung diseases characterized by aberrant wound healing, extracellular matrix accumulation, and irreversible architectural remodeling of the lung. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the clinical spectrum, epidemiology, and genetic predisposition underlying PF, followed by an in-depth analysis of key molecular and cellular mechanisms driving fibrogenesis, including epithelial injury, dysregulated repair, profibrotic signaling pathways, and immune-mediated processes. Particular emphasis is placed on the role of lung surfactant dysfunction in fibrosis development, highlighting alterations in surfactant composition, metabolism, and associated genetic variants that contribute to epithelial stress and disease progression. The review also evaluates established and emerging circulating biomarkers, including serum proteins linked to epithelial damage, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune activation, with attention to their diagnostic and prognostic utility. Finally, current and evolving therapeutic strategies are discussed in the context of progressive fibrosing phenotypes. Despite significant advances in understanding PF pathobiology, substantial gaps remain in early detection, disease stratification, and targeted treatment, underscoring the urgent need for improved translational approaches and precision medicine strategies.

Introduction

Breathing is essential to life, facilitating gas exchange and preventing alveolar collapse due to the activity of a lipid-protein mixture, namely lung surfactant (LS), which steadily maintains low surface tension at the respiratory air–liquid interface [1].

Compression-expansion breathing dynamics move into the airways thousands of liters of air per day that continuously expose the lung to the environment, exposing it to microorganisms and pollutants that ultimately activate the immune system. This may predispose lungs to progressive diseases such as Interstitial Lung Diseases (ILDs), mainly Pulmonary Fibrosis (PF), which are characterized by inflammation and fibrosis and involve epithelial and endothelial cells along with several inflammatory mediators. Together with the environment, the resulting dysregulated wound-healing response is also influenced by genetic and aging-related factors, which contribute to epithelial cell damage and abnormal activation [2].

Given the role of LS in sustaining alveolar stability, alterations in its composition and/or surface-active properties – whether a cause or a consequence of the fibrotic processes triggered by the environmental exposure or other endogenous phenomena—may have profound implications for PF. However, to date, the precise mechanism by which LS is involved in the pathogenesis of lung fibrosis is still under debate.

Moreover, the involvement of autoantibodies in the fibrotic process is gaining increasing attention. B cells and lymphoid aggregates are present in the fibrotic area, influencing cytokines and cell–cell interactions [3]. This may perpetuate a cycle of injury and repair, promoting the fibrotic phenomenon [4]. Several mechanisms have been proposed on the role of autoantibodies in fibrosis, starting from a direct pathogenic effect mediated by lung self-protein recognition, which overcomes the immune self-tolerance, ending with autoantibodies as secondary drivers whose activity contributes to the ongoing tissue damage [5, 6]. Interestingly, targeted antigens and the autoantibody-mediated mechanisms differ widely across disease subtypes [7].

Based on these considerations, in the present review, we will provide an overview of PF, focusing on pathogenesis and the molecular mechanisms underlying the fibrotic process, involving or independent of the adaptive immune system. The role of LS is also discussed, as well as the diagnostic tools that help clinicians elucidate the nature of the ILDs, centering on autoantibody profiles.

From epidemiology to genetic factors of Pulmonary Fibrosis

Interstitial Lung Diseases (ILDs) are a heterogeneous group of diseases encompassing all conditions involving lung inflammation and/or scarring. Within ILDs, the complex disease known as PF is composed of more than 200 irreversible and progressive lung disorders. PF results from various underlying molecular mechanisms, characterized by the replacement of normal tissue and deposition of excessive scar tissue in the lungs. This fibrotic remodeling disrupts the efficient gas exchange dynamics and oxygen diffusion between the alveoli and the pulmonary vasculature. Consequently, low oxygen levels can cause individuals to exhibit clinical manifestations, including shortness of breath, dry cough, and supplemental oxygen dependency, and, if the condition worsens, eventually lead to respiratory failure.

PF progression is highly variable depending on the type of PF and the time of diagnosis. Although at early stages symptoms can be barely noticeable or absent, as the scar tissue builds up gradually in the lungs, they can worsen and, in some cases, patients can experience acute exacerbation (28%), characterized by a sudden decline in lung function and a rapid deterioration of the clinical picture, resulting in respiratory failure.

The most common type of PF is idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), whose causes are unknown, although additional prevalent forms occur in the context of systemic diseases, predominantly systemic sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, or fibrosing variants of hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) [8]. According to the last update of the international guidelines on PF, updated in 2022, ILDs other than IPF that worsen in pulmonary fibrosis are known as Progressive Pulmonary Fibrosis (PPF) [9].

Based on the analyses from the Global Burden of Disease Study, it is anticipated that the mortality rate associated with ILDs, primarily because of PF, will undergo a twofold increase over the next 20 years [10]. Given the variability of different subtypes of PF, current literature lacks consensus on the incidence, prevalence, and survival of fibrosis, and therefore, each subtype is evaluated separately, with most studies focusing on IPF. Pathogenic mechanisms of Pulmonary Fibrosis: from IPF to the adaptive immune system

Healthy would healing and fibrogenesis

The most accepted model describing the pathogenesis of PF refers to IPF, as most studies have been focused on this lung disease since it is much more frequent when compared to other minority interstitial diseases. In healthy lungs, normal wound healing after damage to alveolar epithelial cells leads to a temporary and local reaction characterized by recruitment of inflammatory cells and activation of fibroblasts/myofibroblasts. These cells deposit extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins such as fibrillar collagen and fibronectin so that alveolar homeostasis can be restored by increasing coagulation, angiogenesis, activation of apoptosis pathways, and phagocytosis of macrophages. Besides this, alveolar epithelial type II (AT-II) cells have the capacity to proliferate and differentiate into alveolar type I (AT-I) cells, contributing to the repair and re-epithelialization of the alveolar surface after injury. These cells are the main cells producing LS, the lipoprotein complex essential for proper lung function. Furthermore, during this wound healing process, the release of chemokines is crucial, as well as transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFβ), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), as seen in Fig. 1 [54–57].Fig. 1Pathogenetic model of IPF. Myofibroblasts then secrete a significant amount of extracellular matrix (ECM), leading to ECM deposition. Beyond this physiological and desired damage response, during fibrogenesis of IPF, the continuous alveolar micro-injuries perpetuate the wound healing process, turning into a pathological scenario. [58]. Epithelial cells lose their cell-to-cell adherence by acquiring a migratory mesenchymal phenotype (through epithelial-mesenchymal transition, EMT), transforming into fibroblasts (Fig. 1). Indeed, in every tissue repair process, inflammation is the key, during which immune cells, such as macrophages, neutrophils, and eosinophils, are recruited, which release cytokines and growth factors to maintain inflammation and support wound healing. On the contrary, when the lung injury is sustained and lung fibrosis is developed, transdifferentiation from fibroblasts to myofibroblasts is enhanced. [54]. This event is displayed by changes in cell marker profiles from those typically epithelial (E-cadherin, thyroid transcription factor-1, aquaporin-5, zonula occludens-1, and cytokeratins) to a fibroblast-like one (fibronectin extra domain A, α-smooth muscle actin (ACTA2), type I and III collagen, connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), vimentin, and desmin). Besides this, many of the fibroblasts respond to the aberrant wound healing process by hyperproliferating and finally transdifferentiating into a profibrotic phenotype, known as contractile myofibroblasts. [59–63]. This process is mediated by the release of profibrotic mediators like TGFβ, integrin αVβ6, PDGF, CTGF, VEGF, Wnt, epidermal growth factor (EGF), FGF, interleukin-1 (IL1), CTGF and IGF2. The increase of these factors, together with an imbalance of oxidant-antioxidant molecules, results in the upregulation of mesenchymal genes and downregulation of the epithelial ones, leading to EMT. At the same time, the excessive ECM deposition, enriched in hyaluronan, fibronectin, and interstitial collagens, contributes to the impairment of gas exchange [64–71].

In detail, when comparing the ECM of healthy and IPF patients, the latter shows significant stiffening together with upregulated Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs) and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), especially MMP1 (in alveolar macrophages and epithelial cells) and TIMPs (in myofibroblasts). In turn, all this aberrant ECM deposition also affects the transcriptional profile of lung fibroblasts and increases the levels of ECM proteins, reinforcing fibroblast activation. [72, 73].

The generated high cellular activity demands high levels of proteins, and when the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is not able to synthesize, process, and pack enough proteins, it undergoes ER Stress (ERS). When ERS occurs, cells activate a protective mechanism, known as Unfolded Protein Response (UPR), to restore normal function by inhibiting protein translation and degradation. Besides this, cell apoptosis and EMT are induced, together with the production of fibrotic mediators such as TGFβ, PDGF (platelet-derived growth factor), CXCL12 (CXC chemokine 12), or CCL2 (chemokine CC ligand 2). In the case of IPF, ERS is persistent, as markers for UPR activation were observed in these patients, and can be synergized with inflammation and viral infection, inducing further cell damage. Furthermore, expression of glucose regulatory protein 78 (GRP79), a chaperone involved in ER homeostasis through UPR suppression, was found to be reduced in AT-II cells of individuals suffering IPF. On the other hand, in the case of IPF, subsequent to mutations in surfactant protein genes (SFTPC and SFTPA2), abnormal surfactant protein folding occurs, leading to ERS and activation of EMT [65, 74, 75].

As in the case of normal wound healing, this continued lung injury is characterized by the presence of different growth factors and mediators, such as the increase of IL1 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF) expression. Particularly, myofibroblasts are highly susceptible to CTGF, PDGF, TGFβ, and cytokines IL1β, IL6, IL13, and IL33. Furthermore, TGFβ, wingless-type MMTV integration site family member (Wnt/WNT), Sonic Hedgehog, or Notch pathways are also altered in this scenario [76–78]. Profibrotic mediators and altered signalling pathways

Different studies suggest that chemokines and cytokines may contribute to fibrosis in IPF patients; however, it is unclear exactly through which mechanisms. TGFβ is the primary profibrotic cytokine. It is part of a large family of polypeptides that modulate several functions, such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. TGFβ participates in embryonic lung development (branching morphogenesis and alveolarization), in the maintenance of organ homeostasis, and in the response to tissue injury, acting as a promoter of ECM formation and as the strongest chemotactic factor for immune cells [79, 80].

There are three isoforms of TGFβ: TGFβ1, TGFβ2, and TGFβ3. Although they are encoded by different genes, all the isoforms bind with high affinity to the three receptors, TGFR1, TGFR2, and TGFR3. The biological activity and potentially overlapped functions of the isoforms are not fully understood, with TGFβ1 being the most abundant and ubiquitous [81, 82]. All three isoforms are upregulated and activated in epithelial cells and macrophages from lung tissues of IPF patients, as well as in rodents with bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis [83]. Conversely, mice with impaired TGFβ signaling as a result of Smad deletions demonstrate resistance to pulmonary fibrosis induced by bleomycin [84, 85]. Overexpression of TGFβ in animal models is related to EMT activation (by increasing fibroblast recruitment and myofibroblast differentiation and survival), ECM excessive deposition and inhibition of its degradation by MMPs, induction of apoptosis and senescence of alveolar epithelial cells, inhibition of the synthesis of surfactant-related proteins (Club cell secretory protein (CCSP) and surfactant proteins SP-A, -B and -C) and phospholipids, increased levels of Vβ6 integrin and, lastly, overproduction of other profibrotic mediators [83, 86].

Like TGFβ, CTGF is another profibrotic cytokine that increases fibroblast proliferation and ECM deposition in the lungs, by activating downstream the MAP kinase pathway [87, 88]. CTGF has been found to be overexpressed in AT-II and fibroblasts of IPF patients[89], thus being considered a predictor of the disease. However, it is secreted and activated under stimulation of TGFβ [88, 90], whereas the overexpression of CTGF alone only leads to mild fibrosis [91].

PDGF is predominantly expressed in macrophages, platelets, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, and acts as a potent chemoattractant for mesenchymal cells [92]. This growth factor induces fibroblast proliferation and migration, as well as synthesis of ECM, being related to lung angiogenesis and pulmonary hypertension. In an early phase of IPF, its expression increases in alveolar macrophages, mononuclear phagocytes, fibroblasts, mesenchymal cells, AT-II, and endothelial and vascular smooth muscle cells[93–95]. In fact, patients with IPF show high levels of this factor in bronchoalveolar lavages, together with an abnormal expression of PDGF ligands and receptors. Increased levels have also been found upon bleomycin induction of IPF in mice [94, 96].

IGF1 is a key modulator of cell senescence, which, as mentioned before, is one of the main reasons for the impaired repairing function of AT-II cells in IPF. Macrophages, lymphocytes, and epithelial cells produce IGF1, which modulates cell migration and differentiation (especially of fibroblasts), lung re-epithelialization, and stimulates collagen synthesis by fibroblasts, thus leading to ECM formation [92]. During PF, AT-II cells release IGF1 that, upon binding to its receptor IGFR1, activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. This results in the release of CTGF, TGFβ and MMP9, leading to AT-II senescence and development of IPF. Furthermore, in IPF patients, increased levels of this growth factor have been described in macrophages and fibroblasts, together with a higher ECM fibronectin production [110–112].

FGFs play a fundamental role in cell proliferation, survival, migration, and differentiation during development and postnatal life, mediating the regulation of gene expression of several proteins, such as surfactant proteins [86]. FGF1 and 2 are related to epithelial recovery and inhibition of myofibroblast differentiation and EMT in IPF [113, 114], whereas FGF10 is involved in basal stem cell maintenance and regeneration after lung injury [115]. On the other hand, FGF9 and 18 promote survival and migration of lung fibroblasts and inhibition of myofibroblast differentiation, and they are thought to be upregulated in IPF [116]. Following lung injury, transforming growth factor α (TGFA) induces proliferation in endothelial cells, epithelial cells, and fibroblasts in PF, such as in asbestos- or bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis [117, 118].

During IPF, dysregulation of Wnt signaling components has been observed [119, 120]. There are 19 genes in the Wnt family, encoding glycoproteins that regulate embryonic development and wound healing. These proteins bind to ten different Frizzled transmembrane receptors, activating several intracellular signaling cascades [120, 121]. Wnt pathways have been described to be involved in epithelial cell proliferation, fibroblast-myofibroblast differentiation, and ECM deposition by releasing profibrotic cytokines and proteases (MMP7 and MMP9) [122, 123], as well as in stimulation of EMT and collagen production by fibroblasts [120]. Lastly, it is important to highlight the existence of a crosstalk between Wnt and TGFβ signaling pathways that is important in IPF, as it synergizes their actions in multiple scenarios [121, 124, 125].

The hedgehog pathway is a major developmental pathway regulating organ morphogenesis in various organisms, and plays a critical role in lung morphogenesis [126]. Regarding PF, increased hedgehog pathway activity has been demonstrated in IPF, involving especially the ligand SHH and its effectors. This upregulation stimulates myofibroblast differentiation and collagen production in tissue fibroblasts. In this line, higher SHH expression has also been reported in alveolar epithelial cells in the bleomycin-induced experimental model of lung fibrosis[127–129].

Notch signaling pathway is essential for the normal development and homeostasis of multiple organs, including the lung [130]. Notch genes are expressed during lung development and alveologenesis, playing an important role in cell fate decisions [131, 132]. Furthermore, the expression of Notch genes in the postnatal lung prevents airway club cells from differentiating into goblet cells, becoming crucial for wound healing and SP-C secretion [133]. Dysregulation of Notch has been reported in cancer progression, metastasis, and IPF, where it has been particularly related to myofibroblast differentiation, activation of EMT, and apoptosis resistance [132, 134]. Recent studies describe the importance of Wnt and Notch interaction in the progression from physiological alveolar repair to PF [135]. In normal alveolar repair, the expression in the endothelial tissue of atypical chemokine receptor 3 (ACKR3) (previously known as chemokine (C-X-C motif) receptor 7 or CXCR7) prevents epithelial damage by Jagged1 inhibition. However, in a PF scenario, the recruitment of macrophages expressing VEGF receptor-1 stimulates Wnt/β-catenin-dependent up-regulation of Jagged 1, which stimulates Notch signaling in fibroblasts, leading to PF [136–138].

Immune-mediated triggers

The adaptive immune response seems to be powered upon ILDs [97]. Lymph nodes from PF patients had evidence of enhanced germinal center reactions and T-cell activation, regardless of the etiology of the fibrotic process [98]. Coupled with this, the loss of self-tolerance, particularly in autoimmune-associated ILDs, leads to the activation of auto-reactive B Cells and the production of autoantibodies against lung structural proteins and cell-surface antigens. This may entail a positive feedback loop in which the activated B-cells, acting as potent antigen-presenting cells, interact with T follicular helper cells, promoting their differentiation and migration to germinal centers, where they further support B-cell antibody production. The resulting increase in antibodies, which bind self-antigens and form immune complexes, beyond triggering the antibody-dependent cellular toxicity (classically involving Natural Killer cells, which bind CD-16 receptor and lyse the antibody-coated target cells), also may activate the classical complement pathway (C1q-C4-C2-C3-C5-Membrane Attack Complex), producing C3a and C5a. The latter, through their receptors, recruit and activate macrophages and neutrophils, promoting profibrotic cytokine production (IL-6 and TGFbeta), which leads to cytotoxicity and local epithelial damage. At the same time, proliferation of fibroblasts and ECM deposition occur [99, 100]. In support of this mechanism, the fibrotic progression in animal models is reduced by the inhibition of the complement, such as by anti-C5 therapy [101].

Among the potential self-antigens involved in the adaptive immune response under ILDs, there are cytokeratins, vimentin, and periplakin [3]. Specifically, IgG autoantibodies against periplakin, a component of desmosomes in lung epithelium, were found in 40% of IPF patients and correlated with decreased pulmonary function. The correct mechanism is unknown, but a recent study shows how those autoantibodies suppress alveolar cell migration in vitro, thus interfering with lung injury repair [102]. Interestingly, unlike anti-periplakin IgG, HSPA2 (Heat Shock Protein Family A Member 2) autoantibodies are linked to a better survival of IPF patients, but the precise biochemical process is still unclear [103].

Beyond their role in antibody production and the antigen presentation, B cells may also secrete profibrotic and pro-inflammatory cytokines, reinforcing the adaptative immune activation and the profibrotic microenvironment [7]. As observed from IPF patients’ blood, under stimulation with microbial danger signals, peripheral B cells may release IL-8, IL-6, and MMP7 and promote fibroblasts migration, proliferation, and activation [104].

Moreover, certain antibodies have been shown to stimulate fibroblasts and epithelial cells directly in systemic sclerosis. This occurs through signaling related to PDGF receptor and leads to an increase in collagen expression and deposition [105].

Different autoantibodies are associated with specific autoimmune ILD phenotypes. For instance, anti-Scl-70 is related to the severity of systemic sclerosis, while anti-Ro52 is associated with a more severe form of lung disease [7, 106].

Interestingly, upon autoimmune alveolar proteinosis due to anti-GM-CSF antibodies, a fibrotic process takes place, leading to surfactant dysfunction [107]. However, evidence for a direct correlation between autoantibodies and surfactant inactivation is limited to date. This may be argued since, in immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-related pneumonitis, autoantibodies against SP-B were found and seem to correlate with the development of pneumonitis during ICI therapy [108]. Similarly, in patients with COVID-19-related acute lung injury, IgA antibodies targeting SP-B and C were identified [109].

Environmental factors: from chemical exposure to microbiome

As mentioned, the development of IPF is a multifactorial process that is still poorly understood. In fact, genetics may explain 20% of the cases, while the remainder is associated with environmental and occupational factors, especially cigarette smoking, metal dust, and lung microbiota [17, 58, 64, 139]. As a consequence, there is an open debate nowadays on whether IPF should be reclassified based on these genetic and environmental risk factors [9, 140–142].

The lungs represent the first barrier between the body and the environment, becoming thus constantly exposed to external and internal compounds. Fine particles of cigarette smoke, mainly carbon black and cadmium, induce fibroblast transdifferentiation into myofibroblasts, ECM deposition, and increased levels of ACTA2, all leading to lung fibrosis. Moreover, smoking has been related to miRNA imbalance and ERS [64, 143–145].

On the other hand, environmental chemical exposure to nanomaterials (e.g., multi-wall carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs)) and disinfectants (e.g., polyhexamethyleneguanidine (PHMG)) is recognized as a contributor to fibrogenesis. MWCNTs, PHMG, as well as chloromethylisothiazolinone/methylisothiazolinone are strongly associated with lung fibrosis in both toxicological and epidemiological studies [146–148]. In the past decades, the contribution of the microbiome has started to gain importance in PF, like in many other illnesses. The lung microbiome of IPF patients differs significantly from that of healthy subjects, with an increased bacterial burden, enrichment of potentially pathogenic taxa, and reduced microbial diversity, contributing to repetitive alveolar injury. Both in patients and experimental models, microbial dysbiosis has been associated with acute exacerbations and disease prognosis, supporting its relevance in disease progression [149–154] Additionally, specific microbial and metabolomic signatures have been proposed as potential tools to distinguish PF from healthy controls [155].

The "gut-lung axis" concept aids in understanding other PF conditions. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiome seems to be evident in SSc-PF, even in the early stages of fibrosis, acting as a potential predictor [156]. Current theory sustains that in these patients, alterations include reductions in symbiotic, anti-inflammatory bacteria and increases in potentially pathogenic taxa, with some microbial changes correlating with disease severity. Notably, distinct patterns have been observed compared to other inflammatory disorders.[154, 157–159]. It is well known how surfactant proteins SP-A and SP-D interact directly with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), opsonize microorganisms, and enhance the clearance of lung pathogens [160]. Interestingly, Sftpd + / + and Sftpd − / − knockout mice show lung microbiota differences in several biologically relevant phylogroups such as Staphylococcus, Lactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium [161]. Thus, we could speculate that upon PF-related lung dysbiosis, LS components, especially the two collectins, may be altered by the microorganism’s presence. Zhou et al. have indeed demonstrated how the overproduction of SP-A and SP-D, triggered by antibiotics-induced microbiota depletion, may enhance the resistance of mouse lung tissue to silica-induced injury [162].

Furthermore, several surfactant lipids, such as phosphatidylglycerol and phosphatidylinositol, have immunomodulatory and antiviral activity [163]. Thus, it is reasonable to assume that simultaneous changes in their level and variations in the lung microbiome upon PF may impact disease progression and severity.

Lung surfactant in pulmonary fibrosis

Lung surfactant (LS) is related to the pathophysiology of PF, as a key factor for maintaining alveolar homeostasis. LS is a lipid-protein complex composed of lipids (90%, w/w) and some specific proteins (8–10%). It is synthesized and assembled into specialized organelles, the lamellar bodies, within the AT-II cells, and once secreted, it adsorbs onto the respiratory air–liquid interface, to form films that reduce surface tension to very low values, in order to minimize the work of breathing and prevent alveolar collapse. Besides its biophysical function, surfactant also constitutes the first barrier between the host and the environment, with surfactant collectins playing a crucial role in host defense. The performance of LS is highly dependent on its composition. It primarily consists of phospholipids, with saturated (dipalmitoyl) phosphatidylcholine (DPPC) as the major surface-active lipid component, comprising approximately 40% of the total mass. Following DPPC, unsaturated phosphatidylcholines (PC) are the next most abundant phospholipids, making up around 25% of the total mass. Negatively charged phospholipids, namely phosphatidylglycerol (PG) and phosphatidylinositol (PI), are present in lesser quantities, ranging from 4 to 8% of the total mass of LS each. The hydrophobic proteins SP-B and SP-C are essential for their biophysical activity and represent around 1–2% of the total mass, whereas the hydrophilic surfactant proteins, SP-A and SP-D, constitute around 6–7%.

The metabolism of LS in the alveolar spaces must be balanced for a correct homeostasis. On the one hand, its absence or inactivation is associated with lung damage, edema, inflammation, and ARDS. On the other hand, if it is oversecreted, like in severe proteinosis patients, the alveolar spaces can be overfilled by LS, which can also cause obstruction and respiratory failure [164, 165].

Interestingly, the dysfunction of the surfactant system, especially if affecting surfactant proteins, can also contribute to the appearance of fibrosis. As indicated in other sections of this review, rare genetic variants in eight genes have been observed in familial IPF, including the ones related to surfactant proteins (SFTPC, SFTPA2) or surfactant biogenesis (ABCA3) and their processing and trafficking, and the ones linked to telomere biology.

A mutation in the SP-C gene (SFTPC) was associated with abnormal protein processing and impaired surfactant function. The patients with this mutation didn’t show respiratory impairment at birth, but later developed chronic respiratory failure [166]. In another study, mutations in the SFTPC gene were associated with familial PF, as they disrupted the assembly and secretion of the protein and contributed to lung dysfunction in children. The processing and subcellular localization of SP-C precursor were altered, and the ultrastructure of PF lungs appeared altered upon analysis by electron microscopy, with abnormal lamellar bodies [167, 168].

Moreover, mutations in the SFTPA2 gene in patients with sporadic PF resulted in misfolding and intracellular retention of the SP-A, leading to impaired surfactant function and increased lung fibrosis and lung cancer. In addition, rare variants in the lipid transporter ABCA3 were identified as also associated with PF, although the precise processes through which ABCA3 mutations increase the risk of IPF, likely through epithelial cell malfunction, are still unknown [36, 167].

Pulmonary fibrosis and lung surfactant homeostasis

The correct balance of synthesis, secretion, recycling, and clearance of LS is essential to sustain low enough surface tension at the air–liquid interface, maintaining alveolar stability and modulating the humoral and innate immunity [169]. Since AT-II cells synthesize LS, their dysfunction, as in the case of IPF, deeply affects its activity by reducing surfactant homeostasis [170]. At the same time, changes in surfactant properties lead to an increase in surface tension and subsequent atelectasis. The resulting excessive mechanical stress on alveolar walls may bring on microinjuries of lung epithelial cells. This may cause non-resolving damage and induce pro-fibrotic processes since the simultaneously dysfunctional AT-II cells fail to repair the lung tissue properly [171]. The resulting AT-II cells' apoptosis drives an increase in TGF-beta production, promoting fibroblast proliferation and differentiation into myofibroblasts. Moreover, this TGF-beta overexpression leads to alveolar matrix deposition, and as observed in vitro, surfactant replacement therapy seems to revert this phenotype [172]. To support the impairment of LS homeostasis under lung fibrosis, recent evidence in a murine model has shown that MWCNTs seem to induce lamellar body dysfunction through autophagy dysfunction, which then leads to reduced surfactant secretion, increased surface tension, decreased lung compliance, and increased elastance [173]. Moreover, the importance of proper LS composition as a result of a correct surfactant homeostasis is essential to mediate a protective effect against lung fibrosis development. Together with SP-B, SP-C plays a critical role in the proper structural organization and formation of lamellar bodies. Interestingly, the missense variant in the linker domain of SP-C pro-protein (SftpcI73T) induces AT-II cells macroautophagy in both in vitro experiments and animal models, promoting mitochondrial impairments and a fibrotic process [205]. A functional SP-A is also important to prevent lung fibrosis since its deficiency in Sftpa1 − / − mice is related to a silica-induced lung fibrosis exacerbation [206]. Similarly, exogenous SP-D administered intratracheally attenuates bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis in SP-D − / − mice [207].

Interestingly, a protective role is also played by several surfactant phospholipids. For instance, Kuronuma et al. have demonstrated that anionic phospholipids are able to suppress TLR4-related inflammation by binding the cell surface of macrophages [208]. Thus, since TLR4 together with damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) molecules and inflammatory cytokines contribute to the development of PF, changes in the proper balance of LS anionic phospholipids may contribute to its pathogenesis. Along the same line, a therapy with exogenous surfactant enriched with phosphatidylethanolamine seems to improve gas exchange and significantly reduce collagen deposition in bleomycin-treated mice [209].

Asbestosis and silicosis

Inhaling asbestos fibers (Fig. 3B) can lead to lipid peroxidation and trigger genomic alterations in pulmonary cells, as well as in phospholipase activity, alveolar CCSP, and SP-A. Inhaled fibers cause unproductive phagocytosis by macrophages and their apoptosis, with a consequent deficiency in the pathways of surfactant metabolism, towards its recycling and removal, in charge of macrophages [174]. In PF patients, an increase in secretory phospholipase A2 (sPLA2) leads to the hydrolysis of surfactant lipids into free fatty acids, an event that has been related not only to PF but also to ARDS and alveolar proteinosis. Interestingly, these pathologies are also characterized by an increase in surfactant levels, which remain higher even after the end of exposure, and higher levels of protein SP-A, suggesting the existence of possible partially overlapped pathogenic mechanisms in the three diseases, involving surfactant components. Finally, in the case of CCSP, its association with PF is not surprising, as this protein induces chemotaxis of human fibroblasts mediated by PDGF [175–179].

In the case of silicosis-induced PF (Fig. 3B), silica entering the alveolar spaces causes hypertrophic AT-II cells, which leads to an abnormal increase in surfactant production, with high levels of disaturated PC, PS, and SP-A in the alveoli of these patients. Besides this, the number of alveolar macrophages has been reported to be reduced in this type of fibrosis [180–185].

Radiation

Radiation therapy to treat cancer can cause acute or chronic lung injury, manifesting as radiation pneumonitis or pulmonary fibrosis (Fig. 3C). This injury is associated with alveolar epithelial damage, increased vascular permeability, and AT-II cell hyperplasia as part of the regenerative response. Although AT-II cells may transiently upregulate surfactant secretion, surfactant function is often impaired due to protein leakage and inflammation. These changes lead to reduced lung compliance, impaired gas exchange, and—if unresolved—can progress to irreversible fibrosis [186–188].

Antiarrhythmic drugs (Amiodarone)

Amiodarone affects surfactant production and metabolism at multiple levels, contributing to the development of PF (Fig. 3D). It inhibits PLA-A1 and A2 enzymes, leading to the accumulation of multilamellar bodies. This drug also disrupts the packing and architecture of surfactant within lamellar bodies, causing intracellular surfactant buildup. Consequently, significant phospholipid accumulation, particularly reduction in saturated species such as DPPC, and an increase in unsaturated phospholipids and PE-based plasmalogens occur. These changes impair surfactant function, increasing oxidative stress and triggering alveolar epithelial cell stress responses. This is further evidenced by elevated markers of ER and lysosomal stress, as well as the upregulation of the expression of surfactant proteins SP-B and SP-C in lung tissue but not in alveolar fluid, indicating impaired surfactant secretion [189–192]

Sarcoidosis and Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis

In the context of interstitial lung diseases such as sarcoidosis and HP, the immunomodulatory role of pulmonary surfactant is notably disrupted (Fig. 3E). As previously mentioned, in sarcoidosis and HP, alveolar phagocytic cells conglomerate into nodules called granulomas. These structures overexpress sPLA2, which contributes to surfactant degradation and, subsequently, respiratory failure. Normally, surfactant lipids, particularly PC and PG, suppress lymphocyte proliferation and maintain immune balance within the alveolar space. However, in sarcoidosis and HP, this suppressive function is lost or even reversed, leading to enhanced lymphocyte activation. The regulatory balance of sphingomyelin (SM)-ceramide pools is essential for lung homeostasis, as SM is involved in the regulation of PC synthesis and metabolism. In HP, SP-A levels in BAL fluid were found to be three times higher than normal, with strong expression in alveolar epithelial cells. Thus, the altered surfactant profile in sarcoidosis and HP reflects a critical loss of physiological control mechanisms that may contribute to disease progression and lung dysfunction [193–198].

Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis

In IPF, repetitive lung injury leads to reduced synthesis of LS and increased sPLA2 activity, resulting in elevated levels of FFA and LPL. These lipid byproducts impair epithelial regeneration, reduce cell viability, promote fibroblast recruitment, resistance to apoptosis, and fibrotic scarring. Surfactant composition is significantly altered in IPF, similar to HP and sarcoidosis, with reduced total phospholipid content, decreased levels of PC and PG, and elevated SM, which collectively indicate a loss of immunomodulatory function and may contribute to aberrant lymphocyte activation. In addition, changes in fatty acid profiles, specifically reductions in palmitic and oleic acid, are associated with impaired surfactant surface activity, potentially leading to alveolar collapse and gas exchange dysfunction. Functionally, lung surfactant in IPF patients shows a marked loss of surface tension-lowering capacity, with minimal tension values significantly higher than in healthy individuals, correlating with the degree of lung restriction. Although SP-A levels are often elevated in IPF, the significance of this increase remains unclear and may vary depending on disease stage and treatment history (Fig. 3E) [193, 196–204].

Molecular diagnosis of Pulmonary Fibrosis

Protein markers of PF

The need to identify viable and easily sampled biomarkers for the management of ILDs, and PF in particular, has been widely advocated as they could provide a less invasive alternative to surgical lung biopsy for diagnostic or prognostic purposes [210]. Currently, most of the information present in the literature regarding the use of biomarkers in PF refers specifically to IPF because of its relatively higher frequency when compared to other forms of PF or ILDs. The most studied markers are all related to various mechanisms that contribute to the pathogenesis of PF, such as alveolar epithelial cell dysfunction, extracellular matrix remodeling, fibroblastic proliferation or innate immunity dysfunctions, and it is widely known that they can be markedly elevated in patients who suffer of ILDs [211]. However, the ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT guidelines for the diagnosis of IPF strongly recommend that blood markers, even though potentially useful in disease management, by evidence currently available should not be used for differential diagnosis between IPF and other forms of ILD because of a too high risk of misdiagnosis (estimated at about a third of all diagnoses), which in turn could lead to delayed or inappropriate pharmacological treatments or performance of unnecessary invasive procedures, with consequent negative impact on patient care [9].

More promising evidence in favor of the use of biomarkers in PF is related to their prognostic value and usefulness for monitoring disease progression and response to pharmacological treatments. Krebs von den Lungen-6

KL-6 is a high molecular weight glycoprotein (> 200 kDa) expressed on the apical surface of glandular epithelial cells in the lung, stomach, pancreas, and breast. In the lung, the protein is expressed by AT-II, bronchiolar epithelial cells, and cells of the bronchial serous glands. In cases of proliferation, stimulation, or damage affecting these cells, along with an alteration in the permeability of the alveolar-capillary barrier, KL-6 leaks into the bloodstream, where its levels can be measured. KL-6 has a regulatory function on cell adhesion and a chemotactic function towards fibroblasts. Additionally, it promotes the proliferation and survival of pulmonary fibroblasts, which may explain its role in the genesis and progression of pulmonary fibrosis [212, 213].

KL-6 is one of the most extensively studied biomarkers in ILDs, and IPF in particular. This is due to the fact that, contrary to other biomarkers, automated and validated analytical methods for its determination in human samples are available. [214] KL-6 is one of the most sensitive biomarkers for the diagnosis of PF and has good power in discriminating PF patients from controls [214]. However, its use as a diagnostic marker is limited as it is not specific to any particular ILD, but is also increased in HP, PAP, SSc, RA-PF, and other connective tissue diseases [26, 212, 214–222]. The marker may also be increased in patients affected by advanced tumors, as well as many infectious pulmonary diseases [223–226]. The ethnic origin of patients is also a variable affecting basal serum levels of KL-6 [227].

The prognostic value of KL-6 is much more promising as high levels of KL-6 have been significantly correlated with a higher risk of mortality, disease progression, and risk of developing acute exacerbations [210, 212, 215, 216, 218, 228–232].

Matrix metalloproteinase 7

MMP-7 is an enzyme member of the matrix metalloproteinase family, consisting of structurally-related zinc-dependent endopeptidases. The main catalytic role of activated MMP7 is to break down the extracellular matrix by degrading macromolecules, including collagens type IV, V, IX, and X, fibronectin, and proteoglycan. MMP-7 is produced mainly by glandular epithelial cells and macrophages in various tissues, and in IPF patients, it is overexpressed in alveolar epithelial cells and alveolar macrophages. This overexpression probably contributes to the pathogenesis of PF through mechanisms such as epithelial cell migration, apoptosis, fibroblast proliferation, and neutrophil granulocyte accumulation [217, 233]. Increased levels of MMP7 have been found in serum, BAL, and lung tissue of IPF patients. When measured singularly, its diagnostic power is limited [233]. However, if used in combination with MMP1, another metalloprotease, its serum concentrations may help discriminate PF from healthy controls, IP, HP SSc-PF patients, or other ILDs [215, 217, 233–236].

Surfactant proteins

The detection of surfactant proteins in serum indicates alveolar injury. The level of SP-A in IPF patients is significantly higher when compared to other PPFs or infections, whereas SP-D levels are only able to significantly distinguish IPF from infection, but not from other ILDs [222, 237]. It is worth mentioning that surfactant proteins may also be elevated in heart failure patients and chronic smokers [238, 239] and that the ethnic origin of patients can also influence baseline levels in serum [210, 240].

Recently, pro-SP-B (SP-B precursor) has been proposed as a new PF marker, and indeed, higher levels of pro-SP-B were found in IPF patients, seemingly being able to correctly differentiate IPF patients from patients with all other pulmonary diseases [218, 228, 237, 241–244]. Furthermore, both proteins are able to predict disease progression in patients treated with anti-fibrotic drugs [245–247].

Chemokine Ligand-18

CCL-18 is a chemokine that is highly expressed in the human lung. Its production by antigen-presenting cells, primarily macrophages, is stimulated by cytokines released by T helper 2 lymphocytes and interleukin 10, while its production is inhibited by interferon-γ and lipopolysaccharide [248]. High levels have been observed in IPF macrophages, indicating their activation and also enhancing the positive feedback between them and fibroblasts, thus promoting collagen production [215–217, 228, 249]. CCL-18 has a promising diagnostic value, as its BAL values are higher in IPF than in sarcoidosis-PF and HP. However, in serum, the changes in CCL-18 levels are not specific and appear as significantly higher in several ILDs. Moreover, elevated CCL-18 levels were strongly correlated with increased mortality risk, disease progression, and risk of suffering acute exacerbations [215, 216, 228, 249–251]. The anti-fibrotic drug pirfenidone seems to induce a significant suppressive effect of CCL-18 expression in alveolar macrophages, which could indicate a possible role in monitoring the effectiveness of pharmacological treatment [252].

Interleukin-8

Interleukin-8 (IL-8) is a cytokine derived from monocytes and macrophages and has a strong chemotaxis activity, leading to the activation of neutrophils. IL8 levels in BAL are indeed correlated with neutrophils percentage and elevated IL8 serum levels are both associated with IPF progression and severity, as well as acute exacerbation outcome [215, 253–257].

Cytokeratin-18

The level of circulating cleaved fragment of cytokeratin-18 (CK-18) has been proposed to be an indicator of apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells. Its diagnostic value seems promising as serum levels in IPF patients are markedly increased when compared to healthy controls [228, 258]. Data on this marker are however limited and further study is warranted.

S100 Calcium-binding Protein A4

S100 Calcium-Binding Protein A4 (S100A4) is involved in myofibroblast differentiation, collagen production, and EMT. It has been used to identify fibroblasts in several experimental studies, but S100A4 increased levels have been also found in serum, BAL, and lung tissue of IPF patients and other ILDs such as HP, Sarcoidosis, and NISP. This suggests a promising differential diagnostic value for this biomarker [215, 259–261].

Insulin-like growth factor binding proteins

Insulin-like growth factor binding proteins, especially IGFBP-1, -2, -3, and -5 seem to be significantly higher in the serum and lung tissue of IPF patients compared to other pulmonary diseases. Moreover, IGFBP-3 induces expression and production of Tenascin (TN-C), an extracellular matrix glycoprotein with a key role in PF. Significantly higher levels of TN-C can be observed in lung tissue of patients with SSc-PF compared to patients without this complication, making this protein a possible differential diagnostic biomarker [253, 268–270].

Periostin

Periostin is a nonstructural ECM protein that is highly expressed at sites of injury, as it is involved in ECM deposition and remodeling. It is a key growth and profibrotic factor and its coding gene is highly expressed in the bronchial epithelium of IPF patients [262]. Preliminary studies have highlighted how Periostin is potentially able to predict disease progression [262, 263] and its prognostic value has also been confirmed in a more recent and extensive study [264].

Osteopontin

Osteopontin (OPN) is a glycoprotein that acts as a mediator of inflammation and wound healing, mainly expressed by alveolar macrophages. Significantly high OPN levels in BAL, serum, and lung tissue values have been observed in IPF and other PPFs, such as sarcoidosis or RA-PF. However, OPN is not specific to PF as it can be raised also in other ILDs or obstructive diseases such as COPD [216, 234, 265]. The prognostic value of OPN is also still unclear if considered singularly. However, it might be of use if determined in combination with other markers such as Periostin, ICAM-1 and MMP-7 [264].

Intercellular adhesion molecule 1

Intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) is an immunoglobulin expressed at the surface of endothelial cells that facilitates the transmigration of leucocytes from the bloodstream to tissues [266]. Elevated levels of IPF might be representative of endothelial injury[267]. Similarly to OPN, ICAM-1 might have a significant value in the prognostic evaluation of PF patients when used in combination with other biomarkers [264].

Chitinase-3-like protein 1

Chitinase-3-like protein 1 (YKL-40) regulates the proliferation of different cell types and is produced by alveolar macrophages and AT-II. Increased YKL-40 serum levels have been observed associated to several ILDs, such as asbestos-PF, HP, and polymyositis-dermatomyositis, in addition to IPF, so its role as a differential diagnostic biomarker is limited. Some studies have shown an association between this marker, both in serum and BAL, and worse prognoses in IPF and HP patients, however data are limited [216, 271–275].

Novel biomarkers of pulmonary fibrosis in rheumatological patients

In recent years, given the better knowledge of the immune-mediated mechanisms underlying the pulmonary fibrosis of connective tissue disease (CTD), novel biomarkers are emerging (summarized in Table 6). Antibodies against periplakin, an intracellular component of the desmosome, highly expressed at the bronchial and alveolar epithelium of both normal and fibrotic lung, have been identified in serum and bronchoalveolar lavage of patients affected by dermatomyositis and ILD [276]. The presence of these autoantibodies has been associated with worse outcomes [6, 102]. Further studies will confirm the role of these autoantibodies as possible biomarkers of pulmonary fibrosis in CTD patients. Additionally, experimental studies demonstrated the pro-fibrotic properties of some Ca2+-dependent phospholipid-binding proteins such as annexin A2 and V, strongly expressed in alveolar epithelium cells type II. The presence of antibodies against annexin I has been found in cases of acute exacerbations of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [277] and anti-annexin V have been detected in 75% of SSc patients presenting micro- and macrovascular damage of the pulmonary and renal arteries [278]. Regarding the axes IL-17/IL-23, lung fibroblasts of RA-ILD patients express higher level of IL-17A receptor (IL-17RA) when compared with RA patients in the absence of pulmonary involvement, while IL-23 contributes to the epithelial-mesenchymal transition in the lung of RA-ILD [279]. Recent studies on SSc patients demonstrated the role of the chemokine CCL24 as biomarker of progressive pulmonary fibrotic process. CCL24 is an eotaxin belonging to the CC chemokine subfamily, secreted by immune cells and epithelial cells with proinflammatory and profibrotic activity. Some preclinical models highlight the positive effects on lung inflammation, fibrosis and vasculopathy of the novel monoclonal antibody CM-101, able to bind and neutralize CCL24 activity [281, 283]. These preclinical findings support the role of CCL24 as a potential target of biological therapies in CTD-associated ILD [282].

Current and Emerging Therapeutic Strategies in Pulmonary Fibrosis

The management of interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) has historically relied on a conceptual distinction between inflammatory and fibrotic phenotypes. Early approaches assumed that inflammation predominated in most ILDs, supporting widespread use of corticosteroids and conventional immunosuppressive agents. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) emerged as the exception, showing poor or even harmful responses to immunosuppression. This established a therapeutic dichotomy that continues to guide clinical decision-making.

Immunomodulatory therapies remain central for connective tissue disease–associated ILDs (CTD-ILDs), hypersensitivity pneumonitis, and other inflammation-predominant patterns. However, evidence is largely limited to small trials, retrospective series, and observational cohorts. Corticosteroids are commonly used as first-line therapy despite limited randomised data. Conventional agents such as azathioprine and cyclophosphamide show variable efficacy and carry substantial toxicity, which restricts long-term use. Mycophenolate mofetil has increasingly replaced cyclophosphamide in systemic sclerosis–associated ILD, achieving similar preservation or modest improvement in forced vital capacity (FVC) while offering a more favourable safety profile. Biologic therapies, including rituximab and tocilizumab, have preserved lung function in selected ILDs, particularly systemic sclerosis, although evidence from robust randomised trials remains limited. Overall, these observations highlight the heterogeneity of treatment response and the incomplete ability of immunomodulatory therapy to prevent irreversible fibrosis [284].

In IPF, landmark trials have established antifibrotic therapy as the pharmacological standard of care. Both pirfenidone and nintedanib consistently reduce the rate of FVC decline in phase III studies. Post-hoc analyses also suggest potential survival benefits [285]. These agents maintain efficacy in patients with more advanced disease, and nintedanib has demonstrated similar effects in systemic sclerosis–associated ILD, including in patients receiving background mycophenolate therapy. Attempts to improve outcomes through combination therapy—either between antifibrotics or with agents targeting non-fibrotic pathways—have not yielded additional benefit. Likewise, no pharmacological interventions have proven effective for acute exacerbations, which remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality.

The recognition that progressive fibrosis is not limited to IPF has led to the identification of the progressive fibrosing ILD (PF-ILD) phenotype, characterised by relentless fibrosis despite conventional therapy. PF-ILD includes subsets of CTD-ILD, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, idiopathic non-specific interstitial pneumonitis, asbestosis, sarcoidosis, and unclassifiable ILDs. Clinical trials indicate that antifibrotic therapy can slow disease progression across this spectrum. In the INBUILD study, nintedanib halved the annual rate of FVC decline, showing consistent efficacy across radiological and clinical subgroups. Pirfenidone has shown encouraging trends in unclassifiable fibrotic ILDs, although limitations in trial design prevent firm conclusions. These findings support a behaviour-driven rather than strictly diagnosis-driven approach to treatment.

Emerging therapeutics seek to extend therapy beyond current antifibrotics by targeting profibrotic pathways and immune–fibrotic interactions. Pentraxin 2 (PRM-151) modulates TGF-β–mediated fibrocyte differentiation and macrophage activation [286], while pamrevlumab targets connective tissue growth factor (CTGF/CCN2) [287]. Both have shown promising phase 2 efficacy in IPF. Other approaches under investigation include integrin antagonists, prolyl-tRNA synthetase inhibitors, and senolytic therapies, with early signals of functional benefit. Parallel trials are exploring these strategies in CTD-ILDs, testing antifibrotics in combination with background immunomodulation or biologic therapy.

Looking ahead, several challenges remain. PF-ILD lacks a universally accepted definition, which hampers translational research and harmonised trial design. Biomarker discovery will be crucial to identify patients at high risk of progression before overt functional decline, enabling early and personalised interventions. Rational combination strategies that simultaneously target immune-mediated and fibrotic pathways may offer additional benefit for selected patient subgroups, but rigorous phase III evaluation is required. Overall, the evolving therapeutic landscape signals a shift towards mechanism-informed, precision medicine in fibrotic ILD, with the potential to improve outcomes in both IPF and non-IPF progressive fibrosing phenotypes.

Conclusions and perspectives

Pulmonary fibrosis is a very severe condition ending in lung tissue remodeling and a significant loss of crucial mechanical properties required for breathing. It results from complex and multifactorial contributions including altered alveolar homeostasis as a response to injury and inflammation. The complete understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying pro-fibrotic transitions requires combined detailed and multidisciplinary studies from biochemical, immunological, biophysical and physiological perspectives. Qualitative and quantitative molecular descriptors are also highly needed to detect early pro-fibrotic onsets and facilitate an efficient monitorization of the evolution of the fibrotic phenotype. Novel advanced technologies with the potential to integrate multi-omics and single-cell approaches as well as machine learning-assisted data analyses should open new views in the interpretation of these complex scenarios and on how they could be progressively implemented by smart interventions in key node processes.
