Community-based participatory research to improve resident wellness in a supportive living facility in the Northwest Territories
Authors: Bryany Denning, Barbara Broers, Pertice Moffitt, The Spruce Bough Research Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Spruce Bough is the first supportive living facility in Yellowknife, Northwest Territories, and hosts the first long-term residential managed alcohol program (MAP) in northern Canada. The program accepts individuals who have at least two co-morbid mental health, physical health, substance use, and/or mobility concerns. This study sought to hear from residents about their experiences in the program.MethodThis study utilized a community-based participatory approach including the residents of the Spruce Bough in development, design, and data analysis, with the aim of generating results that would improve the lives of residents. Resident co-researchers used inductive coding to draw themes from statements gathered through focus groups and interviews and used these to develop recommendations to improve resident wellness for Spruce Bough leadership.ResultsResidents reported that life was good at Spruce Bough, they had what they needed, and that staff were supportive and helpful. Concerns included unmet needs for special meals for individuals with specific health-related dietary needs; the disruptive behaviour of individuals drinking outside the MAP; and disrespect felt by residents through actions or comments by staff.ConclusionThe Spruce Bough supportive living model is seen as successful by residents; however, they identified potential areas of improvement. Community-based participatory research allowed residents to provide recommendations to be shared with service providers and funders for continued quality improvement. The insights provided and the interest in participation shown by residents indicate that the program would benefit from creating an ongoing mechanism to include resident feedback in decision making.Keywords: Homelessness, Canada, Community-based research, Substance use, Supportive housing

Introduction

Homelessness in the Northwest Territories is one of many interlinked outcomes of colonization in Canada [1]. The residential school system and subsequent child welfare interventions (the ‘60s scoop) created trauma, disconnection, and loss of culture for both the children neglected and abused within these systems, and the families left behind, many of whom abandoned traditional lifestyles to move to permanent settlements closer to schools or turned to alcohol to cope with the loss of children in the community [7]. The impacts have disrupted family and community relationships for generations, manifesting in various forms of social disorder including substance use disorders, mental health issues, poor health outcomes, and intimate partner violence [10]. While there has been recognition of the impacts of this legacy, and work has begun to address trauma and reclaim Indigenous identity and traditions, the impacts are still evident in existing social inequities experienced by Indigenous peoples everywhere in Canada [20].

In Yellowknife, over 90% of the homeless population identify as Indigenous [9], compared to 24% of the population overall [37]. As well, many people experiencing homelessness in Yellowknife are originally from communities outside Yellowknife, migrating to Yellowknife for a variety of reasons including lack of housing in small communities, seeking employment opportunities, accessing health and other services not available in their communities, or reuniting with family that have moved to Yellowknife or children that have been apprehended and placed in foster care in the city [7, 8]. In Yellowknife, a tight housing market with limited options for low-income tenants increases housing precarity and makes it incredibly difficult to exit homelessness, as well as to operate programming that increases the likelihood of successfully exiting homelessness [13].

The Spruce Bough is a supportive living facility located in Yellowknife, Northwest Territories, Canada, and is the only facility that presently offers permanent supportive housing in the territory. Work by the Yellowknife Women’s Society to acquire and convert the 42-bachelor suite hotel into housing for people experiencing homelessness began in 2018, and the facility opened to people experiencing homelessness at high risk of mortality or severe morbidity due to COVID-19 in May 2020 [43]. The majority of the residents of Spruce Bough are Indigenous men and women from across the NWT. Each resident has their own unit, and over the duration of this study, between 25 and 30 individuals were living at the residence at any one time. As residents are eligible only if they are both experiencing homelessness and are experiencing more than one challenge to being housed independently, which may include substance use disorder, physical disability, mental health issues, cognitive issues, or physical health issues, many individuals living at the Spruce Bough are Elders and older adults.

The programming offered at the Spruce Bough includes the first long-term residential managed alcohol program (MAP) in northern Canada. Managed Alcohol Programs (MAPs) encompass a variety of harm reduction programs with the common feature of providing beverage alcohol of some type to individuals who are enrolled in the program. Formats range from community-based, non-residential peer-led programs that run on a membership-based platform such as the Drinker’s Lounge in Downtown Eastside Vancouver [16] to hospital-based medically-monitored programs [26], but the most common are MAPs linked to supportive housing programming, designed to address the barriers to housing created by co-occurring severe alcohol use disorders (AUD) and homelessness [28]. Participation in MAPs has been shown to improve quality of life and reduce alcohol use and related harms, decreasing emergency department visits, hospital admissions, and interactions with law enforcement [23, 35].

While programs that incorporate harm reduction principles have increased in availability in the Northwest Territories over the past decade, particularly in response to the increase in opioid toxicity deaths beginning in 2016 [19], harm reduction approaches are not universally supported. Opposition to harm reduction measures generally stems from the belief that harm reduction is incompatible with abstinence-based approaches or Indigenous traditional culture [11]. However, there is growing recognition that harm reduction interventions exist along a spectrum that includes abstinence-based services [30] and that harm reduction services can be delivered in a culturally safe manner [22]. Efforts have been made by MAPs to improve cultural safety through the incorporation of cultural practices, including traditional medicines and cultural activities, into programming [24, 28], and MAPs have been shown to be acceptable to Indigenous clients in other Canadian jurisdictions and are able to incorporate Indigenous ways of knowing and healing within their program offerings [28].

The Spruce Bough is also unique in the North in that it offers permanent supportive housing. Other housing programs in the Northwest Territories are transitional in nature, with the expectation that people will develop the skills to move into independent housing at the conclusion of a certain length of stay, as set by the program. This program was developed based on the awareness that the target population for the Spruce Bough, individuals with a number of co-morbid health, mental health, substance use, or disability concerns, would be unlikely to have their needs met if they were to be housed independently, and if the program were to be time-limited, they would likely return to homelessness.

Once the COVID-19 vaccine was widely available and the virus was not considered as threatening to the health of residents, Spruce Bough continued to accept residents based on their assessed need, and to provide supportive living services to individuals who were able to stay in this program until they decided to move on, or their needs exceeded the capacity of the program, such as individuals who needed long-term care services. Residents can choose to spend time in the common area, eat meals together and participate in group recreational activities and trips out of the facility, or they can choose to stay in their room and have meals brought to them if they prefer. The availability of recreational programs varies depending on the availability of staff and volunteers, however generally there are several activities each week, including crafts and games, with outings, such as picnics, more common during (but not exclusive to) the summer months. Since the program was established, some residents have moved to other resources, and others have continued to reside at Spruce Bough for the past four years.

Experiencing homelessness has clear impacts on health and wellbeing, with increased rates of chronic disease, communicable disease, mental health issues, substance use concerns and early mortality [32]. Housing first approaches, where individuals are housed without expectation of meeting certain sobriety or stability thresholds before they can access housing, have been shown to improve health and wellbeing, showing reductions in emergency room visits, ambulance calls, and hospitalizations [3], improvements in mental health, and reductions in substance use [42]. Individuals are accepted into Spruce Bough using a housing-first approach, where there are no set requirements, such as attending treatment or achieving sobriety, before a person identified as in need of supports can be housed in the program.

As this facility was the first residential MAP in the NT, this study was intended to explore the impact of this service on the lives of service users, in a manner that was meaningful and relevant to Spruce Bough residents.

Methods

The objective of our research was to learn about the impact of living at Spruce Bough on resident health and wellness, using a community-based participatory approach. This approach involves the community of interest in the research process, from development of a research question and methods to approval of the final version of the results for publication. The approach was prioritized due to the resident populations being mostly Indigenous people from the Northwest Territories. Research focused on Indigenous peoples has historically been a harmful force that perpetuated the colonization process and laid the blame for social disruption and marginalization on Indigenous populations [35]. Researchers have been experienced as intrusive, exploitative and unethical, centering around non-Indigenous perceptions of Indigenous cultures ([2, 12], p.48) that served to reinforce negative stereotypes and justify continued oppression by using a social deficit lens [5]. Community-based participatory research (CBPR is a methodology that is intended to shift the balance of power in research relationships by equitably involving community members, sharing decision making and ownership in all aspects of the research process [18, 21]. Involving community in the research process has the potential to demonstrate the long-term gains that can result from engaging in research [40] and demystify the research process for community participants.

Involving residents in the design, data collection, and analysis process also has the potential to benefit residents by involving them in the creation of recommendations, that may contribute to shaping the policies that impact their lives. Residents exiting homelessness are likely to have experienced shelter environments that enact excessive restrictions and censor critique of shelter rules or environments, leading to confusion, frustration, and disempowerment among shelter users [14, 34]. Incorporating feedback from program users in decision-making has been shown to empower service recipients and improve trust and relationships between service recipients and staff in a variety of shelter and supportive housing settings, including those with MAPs [6, 28, 31, 38]. The opportunity for service users to engage in CBPR will help them to have a greater understanding of the research process, the benefits of its outcomes and their role in creating change for their community. This, in turn, may positively affect their wellbeing, through both tangible improvements to their living environment, and a sense of empowerment and influence over what happens in their lives.

For the purposes of this study, the community was defined as residents of the Spruce Bough facility, and any interested residents were invited to participate as researchers. Resident researchers will be referred to as co-researchers in this paper so as to distinguish them from the author, the academic researcher on the project. Residents were invited to participate through flyers posted at Spruce Bough and via word of mouth, and the resident Elder went door to door on days that research was occurring to check in with residents on whether they wanted to be included. The study took place between April and December 2024. The research process, and the roles assumed by the researcher and shared with co-researchers, are outlined in Fig. 1.

Research process outline

The researcher was granted permission from Spruce Bough leadership to hold sessions in the common room with Spruce Bough residents to discuss the planning of a research study about the impact of Spruce Bough on their lives. In Spring of 2024, a series of four sessions were held, thanks to funding from the Institute of Circumpolar Health Research. Sessions included a discussion of what research means, and how CBPR is different from traditional methods; how to develop a research question; and approaches to gathering qualitative data, including the benefits and drawbacks of focus groups, interviews, and surveys. The research question selected by the group was “how has living at Spruce Bough impacted your health and wellness.” Co-researchers expressed that they wanted the data collection methodology to include focus groups, with the option to conduct interviews if people didn’t feel comfortable speaking in front of the group, and developed, as a group, a list of questions to ask in the focus groups and interviews. Co-researchers were compensated for their time with $20 per session attended. Lunch was provided from a local restaurant, with requests taken after the first session for the meal the following week. Co-researchers in these sessions were informed that the researcher now had to seek ethical review based on the research question and methods outlined in the sessions, obtain a research license, and obtain further funding before she would be able to return. Co-researchers agreed to allow the academic co-researcher to complete these steps independently. Ethical approval was granted by the Aurora Research Institute (ACREC #23-11).

By late July 2024, the researcher was able to finalize ethical approvals, seek approval from the Yellowknife Women’s Society to proceed, and obtain funding from the Canadian Research Initiative in Substance Matters (CRISM). The Yellowknife Women’s Society also permitted an Elder they had hired on a part-time basis to support Spruce Bough residents to spend some of her time acting as a research advisor, by providing advice on how to best support resident participation. One focus group was held, followed by two interviews with residents that had mobility limitations that made it more difficult for them to attend sessions in the common room and, because of this, stated they preferred to have one-on-one interviews in their unit. Based on her observation of both the focus group and the interviews, the Elder felt that one-on-one interviews would be the best way to proceed, as it did not appear to her that there was enough trust between focus group participants for them to share their responses openly. Eleven more interviews were conducted, for a total of thirteen one-on-one interviews and one focus group with four participants. All interview and focus group participants were given a written consent form which the researcher read and reviewed with them to ensure that they understood the form and that they could refuse or withdraw at any time. Everyone who agreed to participate in an interview or focus group was compensated with $20 for their time.

Interview transcription and initial data categorization was completed by the researcher. Interviews and focus groups were audio recorded and initially transcribed using Otter.ai [27], then manually corrected by the researcher in a private home office. Otter.ai is a transcription service that follows security best practices used in several jurisdictions and aligns with Europe’s General Data Protection Regulation (Ibid) and the United States Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act [33]. Audio files were removed from the Otter.ai site following transcription, with audio recordings and final transcripts maintained on a password-protected private home computer and deleted from the portable home recording device. The researcher reviewed transcripts to ensure they were autonomized and did not contain names of staff or other residents and organized statements within the transcripts into broad categories based on the research questions, and the topics that appeared most frequently. Categories were identified as: life at Spruce Bough, changes since living at Spruce Bough, and Spruce Bough staff.

Data analysis, synthesis, generation of recommendations and final approval for publication took place in collaboration with co-researchers over multiple sessions from August to December 2024. Three residents participated in interviews but did not want to be co-researchers, ten residents participated as both interview and focus group respondents and co-researchers, and eleven residents acted only as co-researchers. To protect the confidentiality of interview participants, particularly those who agreed to be interviewed but did not want to be included as participants, co-researchers did not review the transcripts in their entirety. Instead, anonymized statements from interviews and the focus group were printed on card stock, and the statements in each category were read out loud to the group, who then had the opportunity to inductively identify themes, group these statements by theme, and discuss their reflections on what each themed group of statements was telling them. This conversation, which lasted just under one hour, is summarized in the results section. All co-researchers were compensated $20 for each session they attended, and informed that the researcher would write up the conversation and would return to ensure they had properly interpreted the co-researchers’ reflections in two weeks’ time.

Following the analysis session, the researcher summarized the session discussion, developed some initial recommendations based on co-researcher statements, and returned to Spruce Bough to discuss these findings with co-researchers. Co-researchers identified two additions they wanted to see to the outline of the discussion and elaborated on the recommendations presented to them by the researcher. The draft of this paper was then modified based on this discussion and presented back to co-researchers for review. A written summary of the results was also produced and provided to all Spruce Bough residents, with an invitation to attend a session with the Yellowknife Women’s Society Board of Directors to present the research summary. Following the presentation to the Board of Directors, the researcher returned one final time to present the draft results paper and seek approval from the co-researchers to submit it for publication.

Audio recordings and researcher notes are stored on a password-protected private home computer. All research materials will be deleted upon publication of the research results, and co-researchers will be informed when this has occurred.

Results

While there is considerable overlap between Spruce Bough residents who participated in interviews and focus groups, and residents who acted as co-researchers, there were some residents who participated in interviews and focus groups and did not act as co-researchers, and vice versa. To distinguish between insights collected in interviews and focus groups, and those contributed by co-researcher, statements collected during the interview process are noted as being made by interview respondents, and statements made during collaborative research sessions are noted as being made by co-researchers.

The demographics of participants reflected the demographics of the Spruce Bough overall, with 82% of the interview and focus group participants and 76% of co-researchers being Indigenous, all of whom had experienced past homelessness and were accepted into the Spruce Bough due to existing co-morbidities.

Safety and comfort

There was a consensus among the interview respondents and co-researchers that living at Spruce Bough was a good experience, especially compared to being on the streets. Residents had reported in their interviews that they were comfortable, that they slept better, there was food, and that they had their own space: “It helped me a lot. Yeah, helped me a lot. Yeah, I kind of think it almost saved my life” (Interview Respondent). Co-researchers talked about having everything they needed and being safe in this space.

Substance use and managed alcohol program (MAP)

Co-researchers had mixed views on substance use at the Spruce Bough. We discussed the following quotation:

As long as I stay away from people who drink, and it’s hard to do here. But yeah, you know, to keep them out of my room. – Interview Respondent

A few co-researchers reported that it was difficult for someone who was trying not to drink to avoid drinking, and one suggested the possibility that there were people within the group who didn’t want to drink, but that they drank out of habit because the option was available. One interview participant stated that he was cut off of the managed alcohol program because he decided to take a few days off drinking; as a result, people who might want to take a break from drinking are disincentivized from making attempts to cut back or try sobriety. Co-researchers agreed that it would be good to provide cannabis as an option instead of alcohol and that doing so might allow people to cut back on or quit drinking, with one stating “I decided to stay away from alcohol, that’s why I smoke marijuana.” They noted that it also improves sleep and people would eat better when using cannabis – “I get the munchies” (Session Respondent).

Interview respondents talked about things that have changed for them since coming to live at Spruce Bough, including having a safe and warm place to stay and eating more. In the analysis session, quotes were presented to the group in which interview respondents spoke about drinking less or being sober. This began a discussion about people being annoyed by other’s drinking; co-researchers said that they are able to kick people out of their space if they are bothering them, but that people knock at people’s doors and are sometimes loud or angry when they have had too much to drink.

Food and nutrition

Interview respondents mentioned issues with the food served at Spruce Bough. Two interview respondents said that they had health conditions that weren’t adequately catered to and had to purchase their own food, and others said that they didn’t feel that they were able to eat healthily. Other interview respondents mentioned not having enough snacks and options outside of mealtimes. When discussing these statements, co-researchers said that people could potentially talk to the cook and explain their needs, but it was unclear whether this would affect any change. In the follow-up discussion of these results, co-researchers mentioned a lack of traditional foods such as dryfish, moose and caribou within their diet at Spruce Bough and a hope that there could be more traditional foods included in their offerings.

Rules and autonomy

In response to an interview quote about programming, co-researchers said that there is often nothing to do. Interview respondents talked about the location of Spruce Bough, specifically about it being a bit farther from downtown than they would like and having difficulty getting transportation. While it wasn’t mentioned in the interviews, co-researchers also stated that visiting hours were too limiting, and that 8pm was too early for visiting hours to end, especially when they had family that had driven a long way to come and visit them.

Staff interactions and respect

In the interviews, many interview respondents made comments about the staff, and how they felt they were treated. All interview respondents said positive things about the Elder on staff, stating that she was kind and helpful, and this was reiterated by co-researchers. People had positive things to say about the staff, generally: “The staff (…) really like to help people, and the cooks, I really like them” (Interview Respondent). This was echoed by the co-researcher group: “most of them, they’re nice, they do their job, they like to help us.”

However, there were instances where people felt that staff lacked respect for facility residents. One interview respondent told a story of being in pain, and asking to go to the hospital, then having to advocate about the severity of the issue before an ambulance was finally called.

So the next day, [name redacted] come to me, and he said, you know, you should know when you’re going to be sick and arrange your own transportation. (Interview Respondent).

Co-researchers expressed frustration about being expected to predict emergencies, and about a lack of help with other needs, saying “they always say they’re short of staff, they can’t do things for the clients.” Interview respondents talked about feeling that some staff didn’t bother helping them or thought that helping them was beneath them, and lacked respect for their space, doing things like walking into their homes during the winter with outdoor shoes on and leaving a mess. Co-researchers echoed this, saying that they felt they should be treated as “one notch above where we are” rather than as beneath the staff who were meant to be helping them. One interview respondent stated: “Some of the staff there, there’s some, they’re not educated or trained to do this kind of work.” Co-researchers echoed interview statements that some staff acted like the Spruce Bough was a boot camp, rather than a supportive environment, and said that there was a staff member who would come in even when not working and talk down to clients, “like she owns this place” (Session Respondent). In the review session, co-researchers emphasized that they felt some rules, like not being able to get together to talk in one person’s room, were unjustified.

Recommendations

It was evident that individuals identified a clear improvement in their lives since moving into the Spruce Bough. They have their own safe space, eat better than they did while experiencing homelessness, and have regular interactions with neighbours and helpful staff.

While participants and co-researchers consider the Spruce Bough to be a successful program, they also identified areas for improvement. Co-researchers generated the following recommendations to improve wellness for Spruce Bough residents:

Review the MAP to ensure that staff are comfortable in knowing when someone may have been overserved or drinking outside the program and needs a break and have the skills to decline a dose of alcohol effectively. Incentives may be offered for skipping a dose to help people who may be drinking out of habit and interested in cutting back. Offering cannabis can be considered as an option for residents of Spruce Bough for whom cannabis use is not contra-indicated by other health conditions.

The recommendations were shared with the Board of Directors of the Yellowknife Women’s Society in the hopes that they may be implemented.

Discussion

This CBPR process resulted in community-developed, consensus-based recommendations that, if implemented, have the potential to improve the wellbeing of Spruce Bough residents. The use of resident feedback, either through informal discussions or formal processes such as regular advisory meetings, has been used to improve programming in a variety of MAP and other supportive housing programs [28].

The Board of Directors of the Yellowknife Women’s Society was receptive to the recommendations, however there are challenges to implementation that are beyond their control. The primary limitation is funding, which is a common challenge for harm reduction initiatives in Canada [17]. Cannabis substitution has been identified in other MAPs as being both acceptable to MAP participants and to result in reduced alcohol use by participants [29] and could support the stated goals of some of the participants to reduce their alcohol intake, and by extension, alcohol-related harm. Cannabis substitution was initially offered at Spruce Bough as part of the MAP during COVID-19, however once restrictions were lifted government funding was limited to only alcohol provision. Cannabis substitution would require a change in funding parameters to allow for the purchase of cannabis. Spruce Bough also provides all meals for residents, and rising food prices since the pandemic [41] in the context of already increased northern food costs is already challenging within the current budget. Engaging a nutritionist and preparing meals according to various dietary restrictions would require increased funding to cover this service, as well as additional food costs.

Another way that funding impacts the ability to implement these recommendations is in how funding levels influence employee retention and training capacity. In the Northwest Territories, there is generally a significant wage gap between workers in the non-profit sector, and those in government or private business. Low wages, combined with the high cost of living in the north, mean that many workers in the non-profit sector are working more than one job, and, regardless of whether or not they enjoy the work or feel affinity for the organization, are heavily incentivized to seek employment in another sector and leave when it is offered [36]. This makes implementing recommendations related to training more challenging, as high turnover increases training costs, and staff vacancies may reduce the ability for new staff to participate in training or require other staff to be paid overtime to cover operational requirements while others are in training. A first step to implementing the first three recommendations included in this paper will be advocating for increased program funding to support the residents of the Spruce Bough.

The one recommendation that could be implemented without funding consideration is the practice of involving residents in decision making. Past research in an Indigenous community in the United States demonstrated that the likelihood of participation in CBPR increases if the information from the study will be used to answer future new questions, address a problem in the community, or bring more money or services into the community [25]. The outcomes from implementing these recommendations for the Spruce Bough program may encourage future participation of co-researchers in other CBPR projects. Similarly, involving residents in discussions to make decisions and establish rules makes it more likely that residents will continue to engage. However, leadership and staff should be clear about the parameters of what is possible to change and what is beyond the influence of staff, because if residents feel that their feedback is not being considered or is being ignored, participation is unlikely to continue.

Participation in this research was high, with an average of fifteen participants in the four planning sessions, seventeen participants completing initial focus groups and interviews, sixteen attending the analysis session, and fourteen attending the draft paper review. This was likely attributable to the awareness that compensation was being offered, in the form of $20 per session. The compensation was set at that amount for several reasons: it is easy to distribute (bills in Canada come in $5, $10, $20 and $50 increments), it is above minimum wage in the NWT ($16.70), and it was not so high of an amount that it would be expected to cause undue influence, where people would feel compelled to participate against their will. There is considerable debate in the literature on the ethics of offering payment for participation, with some arguing that payment can jeopardize informed consent and exploit people’s vulnerability and marginalization [4], and others arguing that it helps in overcoming the power imbalance between the researcher and research participants [15] and helps avoid bias that may be introduced by those who would not otherwise participate if not compensated for their time, energy and perspectives [39]. The decision to compensate participants in this research came from the belief that, in CBPR, participants should be treated as and compensated as co-researchers, and receive a benefit in exchange for sharing their experience and expertise. While this may have reduced bias by bringing in people who otherwise would not have been interested in participating without compensation, increasing the breadth of views available, it could be considered coercive considering that nearly all residents of Spruce Bough are on a fixed income and may be experiencing health, mental health or substance use concerns that limit other work opportunities. Thus, there may have been some participants who were there only due to compensation and did not want to participate in these conversations.

To reduce the likelihood of coerced participation, research planning participants and co-researchers were compensated whether or not they participated actively in the planning, analysis and review sessions. Of those who attended one or more sessions, only about half of the participants in each session actively participated in the analysis and review. During the discussion of the results, one resident voiced that, while he felt confident enough to share his thoughts, he thought that others were holding back on sharing their opinions. They were offered the opportunity to contact the researcher, via the Elder on staff, if they wanted to share their thoughts on the quotes presented and the responses in a private setting. No one decided to reach out in this manner. It is unclear whether this is due to a lack of trust in the researcher, mistrust of the group, fear of reprisal for sharing their views, or a lack of interest in participation.

Conclusion

CBPR methods were useful for conducting wellness research and generating recommendations to improve wellbeing among residents at a supportive living facility in Yellowknife, Northwest Territories. Compensation for participation was offered at all stages of the process, which likely increased attendance; however, not all individuals attending sessions were willing to share their views in a group setting. Further research could be conducted in this setting, to see if familiarity with this process may increase the number of individuals willing to share their views going forward, particularly if the recommendations developed by the group are implemented within this setting and residents are able to witness CBPR leading to improvements in service delivery and wellness at Spruce Bough.
