Reproductive preservation in non-puerperal uterine inversion from adenomyoma: a case report
Authors: Zhen-Xuan Zhu, Chang-Zhu Pei, Ming Li, Xiao-Xing Song, Hao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-puerperal uterine inversion (NPUI) occurs extremely rarely and is typically associated with uterine tumors, but cases of NPUI arising from polypoid adenomyoma are even less common. The non-specific presentation of NPUI often leads to delayed diagnosis, and existing fertility-preserving surgeries lack tailoring to polypoid adenomyoma’s features.Case presentationA 32-year-old nulliparous Asian woman presented with heavy menstrual bleeding, a prolapsing vaginal mass, and severe anemia (Hemoglobin 32 g/L). Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed NPUI. Surgical management involved vaginal traction for precise lesion localization, Haultain’s procedure for uterine repositioning, and layered suturing for repair. Histopathology identified a polypoid adenomyoma. Follow-up demonstrated resolution of symptoms and return to normal menstruation.ConclusionThis optimized surgical strategy provides replicable details and references for the fertility-preserving management of rare NPUI.

Introduction

Non-puerperal uterine inversion (NPUI) caused by polypoid adenomyoma is extremely rare. The majority of recently reported NPUI cases involve uterine tumors, with submucosal leiomyomas as the most common etiology. NPUI secondary to polypoid adenomyomas is considerably rarer. Its rarity, combined with non-specific symptoms vaginal mass prolapse, irregular vaginal bleeding, and severe anemia, often leads to delayed or missed diagnosis, thus complicating clinical management.

Current fertility-preserving surgical methods for NPUI include the Huntington, Haultain, and Dobin procedure. The Huntington technique uses Allis clamps placed outside the constricting ring to progressively elevate the inverted uterine fundus [1]. The Haultain technique involves a vertical incision in the posterior portion of the constricting annular structure before repositioning [2]. The Dobin technique makes a similar incision in the anterior aspect [3]. Over the past decade, reports have described NPUI due to benign uterine lesions with four patients expressing a desire for conception [4–7].

We report the case of a 32-year-old nulliparous Asian woman with NPUI due to a polypoid adenomyoma. To address the unique features of this lesion while preserving fertility, we integrated the Lesion Traction Localization Technique (for precise lesion localization), Haultain’s procedure (for uterine repositioning), and segmented uterine suture technique. This case expands the clinical evidence for NPUI secondary to polypoid adenomyoma and offers practical technical guidance for abdominal repositioning with fertility-preserving strategies, in similarly rare and complex presentations.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old nulliparous Asian woman presented with a two-year history of progressively increasing menstrual volume, reaching two to three times her baseline, and prolonged menses lasting up to 12 days. She had no history of sexual activity or pelvic inflammatory disease. She arrived at the emergency department with vaginal mass prolapse that appeared after a forceful bowel movement accompanied by intermittent abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding. On examination, she showed signs of symptomatic anemia. A firm, tender 50 × 60 mm mass covered with blood clots was visible at the vaginal introitus (Fig. 1). No fallopian tube opening was visible on its surface, and the cervix was neither visible nor palpable. The patient was diagnosed with uterine inversion and severe anemia. She received blood transfusion and prophylactic antibiotics before emergency surgery. Intraoperatively, a deep central fundal depression was observed, with the fallopian tubes and ovaries protruding along its edge, confirming complete uterine inversion (Fig. 3). Vaginal traction on the mass revealed a 20 × 5 mm segment of fundal myometrium that moved synchronously with the lesion. A cold knife was used to excise the mass and a portion of the myometrium in a fusiform shape along the deepest point of inversion. The specimen showed an indistinct boundary between the mass and the surrounding myometrium (absence of a pedicle) and multiple cystic cavities filled with chocolate-like fluid (Fig. 4). During suturing, layered uterine repair was performed, using continuous sutures for the endometrium and interrupted sutures for the myometrium, with care to avoid endometrial penetration. Huntington’s procedure was attempted but failed due to a tight cervical constriction ring. Haultain’s procedure was then performed, resulting in successful uterine repositioning; the incised constriction ring was closed with interrupted figure-of-eight sutures. Histopathology confirmed polypoid adenomyoma (Fig. 5). The patient was advised to use contraceptives for one year postoperatively. Discussion

The etiology of NPUI is largely attributed to uterine tumors, with leiomyomas being the most common cause [8]. Uterine varus resulting from uterine polypoid adenomyoma is rarely reported. In the past decade, only four reported cases of NPUI secondary to benign uterine tumors involved patients with high fertility needs [4–7]. However, these cases relied on traditional surgical procedures, overlooking detailed fertility-preserving techniques and analysis of surgical adaptability in fertility protection scenarios.

Analysis of surgical adaptability for lesion resection and uterine repositioning reveals significant limitations. For NPUI caused by pedunculated submucous leiomyomas, transvaginal resection with ligation of the pedicle (followed by spontaneous uterine repositioning) is effective, but only applicable to lesions with clear boundaries and anatomical pedicles. For sessile or ill-defined lesions, transabdominal myomectomy often leads to residual tissue or excessive myometrial loss; meanwhile, hysterectomy (used in some studies for malignancy concern or operational difficulty) sacrifices fertility. For cases involving tight cervical constriction rings, the Haultain procedure (abdominal incision of the posterior uterus or constriction ring) achieves repositioning; however, it lacks dedicated lesion localization methods, relying on surgeon experience which risks myometrial damage.

Existing reports rarely describe specific methods for removing vaginal tumors. Certain myometrial masses having indistinct boundaries with the uterine muscle layer present a challenge; excising the fibroids may leave residual tissue, contradicting the principle of tumor-free resection. The identification of the lesion attachment site via synchronous myometrial movement during vaginal traction addressed the limitations of traditional pedicle-dependent or experience-based resection, ensuring complete lesion excision while minimizing myometrial loss, thus preserving fertility. The stepwise selection of repositioning procedures (Huntington’s first and then Haultain’s) aligns with the clinical principle of “conservative first, aggressive second.” This approach further clarified the adaptability of the Haultain procedure in cases with “tight cervical rings + sessile lesions,” providing a more rigorous decision-making path for similar cases.

Previous studies on fertility-friendly uterine repair have been deficient: most only mention “layered suture of uterine incisions” without specifying suture techniques for the myometrium or endometrium or analyzing fertility-related risks. The uterine incision requires layered suturing because single-layer suturing often leads to scar defects [9]. A defect in the muscular layer increases the risk of uterine rupture during delivery by fourfold [10]. Suturing techniques for the uterine myometrium include continuous, interrupted, and baseball suturing. Baseball suturing may be selected when hemorrhage within the myometrial cavity necessitates rapid hemostasis. Studies indicate that compared with interrupted suturing, continuous suturing carries a higher risk of uterine rupture and placental implantation [11]. The uterine myometrium subjected to continuous suturing for three months postoperatively may experience excessive ischemia, delayed postoperative vascularization, and persistent ischemia in certain areas even six months postoperatively [12]. Therefore, for patients with fertility needs, factors such as uterine structural integrity, myometrial blood supply, and cervical function should be prioritized when considering fertility preservation. During the closure of the uterine myometrium, interrupted suturing demonstrates greater advantages over continuous suturing, offering a valuable reference for clinical practice. However, the limited follow-up period for this case and lack of supporting data prevent confirmation that this approach yields greater benefits.

Accurate intraoperative assessment of lesion characteristics is crucial. If assessment is unclear, a pathological frozen section examination should be conducted promptly to rationally determine the surgical scope and prevent unnecessary trauma from secondary surgery. One case initially involved a hysterectomy with uterine repositioning, but a subsequent radical surgery was required after postoperative pathology revealed endometrioid adenocarcinoma [13]. In addition, discussions persist regarding treatment after uterine displacement. Several studies suggest that hanging the uterus by the round ligaments after repositioning may reduce the risk of recurrence [14]. However, the effectiveness of this method requires further confirmation in long-term follow-up studies with larger samples.

As a single-center, single-case report, this study is limited by the rarity of NPUI caused by polypoid adenomyoma. Thus, the generalizability of its conclusions requires verification with cases from more centers. Further, the 3-month postoperative follow-up only failed to provide long-term data on fertility outcomes and uterine scar stability. Meanwhile, techniques such as “lesion traction localization” rely on the surgeon’s experience and lack standardized procedures. Moreover, no comparison of the advantages and disadvantages of different surgical methods was conducted, resulting in relatively low evidence strength.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case optimizes key steps to managing NPUI due to polypoid adenomyoma, emphasizing precise lesion localization, appropriate repositioning technique selection, and meticulous uterine repair to preserve fertility. Compared with previous studies, it clarifies the surgical selection logic for NPUI lesions with different pathological features. The techniques described provide a replicable clinical approach for this rare condition and a reference for fertility-preserving management in other NPUI types.
