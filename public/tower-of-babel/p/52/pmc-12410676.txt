Effects of standardised patients (SP) combined with case-based learning (CBL) in Chinese clinical education: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zizhong Yu, Zhao Zhao, Xinyue Chen, Jiayi Shi, Yiting Liang, Xicheng Zhang, Mengjiao Qu, Meiyue Huang, Panpan Li, Dan Li, Man Li, Jinbo He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesCombining standardised patients (SP) with case-based learning (CBL) enriches the learning experience by immersing learners in diverse clinical scenarios. This meta-analysis study aims to assess the intervention effects of this teaching approach on Chinese medical students’ and resident physicians’ teaching satisfaction, theoretical knowledge achievements and clinical practice performance.DesignA systematic review and meta-analysis through Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement.Data sourcesPubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Education Resources Information Center, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Database and the VIP Database from their inception up to 30 April 2025.Eligibility criteria Included studies were randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing SP+CBL (experimental group) with lecture-based learning (LBL) (control group) among Chinese medical students and resident physicians, with outcomes including teaching satisfaction, theoretical knowledge scores and clinical practice performance. Introduction

Modern medicine prioritises skilled doctors capable of addressing patients’ needs effectively. However, traditional teaching methods tend to focus primarily on knowledge transmission, often overlooking the crucial aspect of skill development.1 The concept of standardised patients (SP) was first proposed by a physician from the University of Southern California. SP are individuals who have undergone rigorous standardised and systematic training to accurately simulate the clinical characteristics of patients, including their medical histories, physical symptoms and emotional responses. Participating in SP programmes allows learners to acquire various valuable skills, including interpersonal communication, motivational interviewing, clinical writing and interprofessional teamwork.2 3 Additionally, they are exposed to rare disease states that are infrequently encountered in real clinical settings, further enhancing their learning experience.4 Case-based learning (CBL) employs clinical cases as educational material, creating a simulated clinical learning environment for students to develop the ability to apply foundational theoretical knowledge to solve clinical problems.5

Medical students and resident physicians in China represent two distinct developmental phases within the country’s healthcare workforce continuum. (Resident physicians are defined as postgraduate trainees entering standardised residency training programmes, which typically involve a 3-year period of clinical immersion following undergraduate medical education.)5 The comprehensive competencies of these trainees, encompassing both theoretical knowledge and clinical skill proficiency, have a direct impact on medical service standards across China’s diverse regional healthcare settings.6

According to Kirkpatrick’s Four-Level Model for evaluating educational effectiveness, teaching satisfaction falls within the first level (Reaction), while theoretical knowledge and practical skills assessments belong to the second level (Learning).7 This study is grounded in the theoretical frameworks of situated learning theory,8 cognitive load theory9 and competency modelling. We hypothesise that integrating standardised SP with CBL strengthens the development and consolidation of clinical practice skills among Chinese medical students and resident physicians by reducing cognitive load through contextualised practice, while concurrently fostering their comprehensive competencies.

Notably, prior studies have primarily focused on meta-analyses of single teaching modalities or analysed the effects of the SP+CBL model solely through randomised controlled trials (RCTs),10,12 lacking a comprehensive systematic quantitative synthesis of this combined approach. While preliminary studies have demonstrated its feasibility, an integration of the effect sizes is absent, and available outcome measures are quantifiable.

Consequently, this research adopts a meta-analysis approach aligned with Cochrane recommendations. This methodology generates higher-level evidence to address critical gaps left by fragmented qualitative studies, thereby providing evidence to inform scaled implementation of SP+CBL within Chinese medical education.

Methods

General information

Conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement,13 our investigation employs the Population-Intervention-Comparison-Outcomes framework to systematically examine: Chinese medical students’ and resident physicians’ competencies, comparative effectiveness against traditional pedagogical methods and measurable impacts on learner satisfaction and performance outcomes. This study was formally registered on the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols (INPLASY), with the registration number INPLASY202560118.

P (Population):Chinese medical students and resident physicians engaged in clinical education.

I (Intervention):SP+CBL teaching approach.

C (Comparison):Lecture-based learning (LBL) teaching approach.

O (Outcomes): eaching satisfaction (binary variable): coded as 1 if very satisfied or satisfied with the teaching effectiveness, and 0 otherwise, derived from questionnaire surveys.

Theoretical knowledge achievements (continuous variable): scores obtained through standardised examinations, with a total score of 100.

Clinical practice performance (continuous variable): scores obtained through standardised Mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise (Mini-CEX)14 and Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) Multi -Station Assessment,15 with total score of 100.

The search spanned PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Education Resources Information Center, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Database and the VIP Database from their inception through 30 April 2025.

The strategy combined controlled vocabulary (eg, Medical Subject Headings) with free-text keywords using Boolean operators, with search syntax tailored to each database’s indexing protocols (online supplemental materials 1). Conducting both backward and forward citation tracking, we screened relevant studies from included references and identify subsequent works citing them, avoiding omission of classic and latest research. Systematic filters were applied to restrict results to RCTs, ensuring methodological rigour while maximising sensitivity for educational intervention studies.16

Literature screening and data extraction

Two researchers (JS, XC) independently screened the literature based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. In cases of disagreement regarding study inclusion, a third researcher (ZZ) was consulted for further review and resolution. Duplicate publications were identified and removed using NoteExpress (V. 4.1.09976).

The initial screening involved reviewing titles and abstracts, followed by a detailed examination of the full text to exclude studies that did not meet the criteria. Studies with incomplete data or information that could not be converted were excluded, resulting in the final selection.

For the selected studies, essential information was extracted, including the first author, publication year, sample sizes of the control and experimental groups, detailed intervention methods and outcome measures. If the outcome indicators were continuous variables, the means and standard deviations (SDs) were extracted. If they were binary variables, the number of events and the total sample size were extracted. The extracted data were then summarised and organised for analysis.

Quality assessment of the included studies

The Cochrane Risk-of-Bias 2.0 tool was used to evaluate methodological quality, focusing on six critical criteria, including the overall bias, selection of the reported result, measurement of the outcome, missing outcome data, deviations from intended interventions, radomisation process. Each criterion was categorised as low risk, some concerns or high risk.17 Given unavoidable subjectivity in assessment, we require assessors to provide detailed evaluation rationales and re-examine when disputes arise, to mitigate such subjectivity’s impact.

Statistical methods

The meta-analysis was conducted using Stata V.17.0 software. Based on the a priori assumption of high heterogeneity in educational intervention studies, a random effects (RE) model was prespecified for meta-analysis initially, and the model was ultimately adjusted according to the heterogeneity of results to output the final calculation results.

If pheterogeneity >0.1 and I2 <50%, it indicates that all the studies are homogeneous, and the FE model is chosen for final analysis; if pheterogeneity <0.1 and I2 ≥50%, it indicates heterogeneity among the studies, and the RE model is chosen for final analysis. For continuous variables, such as the theoretical knowledge achievements and clinical practice performance, the mean differences (MDs) were calculated, while for binary variables like teaching satisfaction, the odds ratios (ORs) were calculated. Additionally, the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each effect size. To assess the stability of observed results, the leave-one-out (LOO) method was applied to sequentially exclude each piece of literature.13 Subgroup analyses were performed to compare outcomes between medical students and resident physicians, focusing on differences in theoretical knowledge acquisition and practical skill performance across distinct stages of medical education and potential sources of heterogeneity were systematically explored to enhance interpretability. Funnel plot visual analysis and the regression-based Egger’s test for detecting small-study effects were conducted, and the RE model was employed to assess publication bias. Ultimately, the evidence certainty thresholds were determined following the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) evidence criteria: high, moderate, low and very low.18

Results

Search results

Literature screening and characteristics and detailed bias risk

A total of 84 studies were initially identified through database searches. After removing duplicates, screening titles/abstracts and full-text evaluation, 31 RCTs19,49 involving 2674 participants (1338 in the SP combined with CBL model group; 1336 in the LBL model group) were included (see PRISMA flow diagram, figure 1). All studies were published in Chinese literature. There were no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics regarding age, academic performance or gender composition ratio among all participants (p>0.05). Key extracted data included first author, group, SP features, teaching method and content summarised, frequency and duration, case characteristics, participants’ role (online supplemental materials 2). Included studies’ bias risk findings are detailed in figure 2. Due to inherent methodological limits in education (eg, difficulty fully implementing randomisation and blinding) and regional differences in evaluation criteria, bias risk is mostly of some concern.

Intervention measures details

The experimental group implemented a blended pedagogy integrating CBL with SP. Clinically relevant cases were designed to align with curricular objectives, with SP trained to authentically replicate patient symptoms, medical histories and physical signs. Students participated in small-group sessions to conduct SP-based interviews, perform physical examinations and collaborate on case analyses, followed by diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making. Competency assessments used dynamic tools such as Mini-CEX or OSCE, targeting clinical reasoning, procedural proficiency and communication skills.

The control group followed conventional teaching methodologies centred on LBL, relying on didactic lectures via slides or textbooks. Bedside teaching involved real patients but was constrained by inconsistent case availability and patient participation. Case discussions and ward rounds lacked standardised simulation, focusing instead on observational learning. Assessments prioritised theoretical knowledge retention through written examinations, supplemented by basic procedural drills using manikins.

Meta-analysis results

Teaching satisfaction

Among the eight included studies19 21 22 25 26 28 35 37 that used teaching satisfaction as the outcome indicators, 543 participants were included (273 in SP+CBL and 270 in LBL). As shown in figure 3, there was no heterogeneity among the studies (pheterogeneity=0.799, I2=0.0%). Therefore, the FE was employed for the analysis. The meta-analysis revealed that, compared with traditional teaching, the application of SP within the context of CBL significantly improved students’ and resident physcians’ satisfaction with teaching (OR 7.19, 95% CI 3.80 to 13.60, p<0.001), as shown in figure 3. The GRADE quality of evidence was rated as moderate (table 1).

Theoretical knowledge achievements

The 27 included studies20,2527 used theoretical knowledge achievements as outcome indicators. A total of 2419 participants were included (1208 in the SP+CBL group and 1211 in the LBL group). As shown in figure 4, there was significant heterogeneity among studies (pheterogeneity <0.001, I2=93.9%). Therefore, the RE mdel was adopted. Sensitivity analysis was used to investigate the origin of heterogeneity. The meta-analysis revealed that, compared with traditional teaching methods, the combination of SP and CBL resulted in significantly greater theoretical knowledge achievements (MD 5.91, 95% CI 4.63 to 7.18, p<0.001), as shown in figure 4. The GRADE evidence rating was classified as low (table 1).

Clinical practice performance

Among the 27 included studies,20,3436 38 clinical practice performance was used as the outcome indicator, with a total of 2403 participants (1198 in the SP+CBL group and 1205 in the LBL group). As shown in figure 5, there was considerable heterogeneity among the studies (pheterogeneity <0.001, I2=96.2%). Therefore, the RE model was finally adopted. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to explore the source of heterogeneity. The meta-analysis revealed that SP combined with CBL led to a notable improvement in clinical practice proficiency (MD 7.62, 95% CI 6.16 to 9.08, p<0.001), as shown in figure 5. The GRADE evidence rating was classified as low (table 1).

Sensitivity analysis

A systematic LOO sensitivity analysis, involving sequential exclusion of each individual study, revealed that the pooled effect sizes for teaching satisfaction, theoretical knowledge achievement and clinical practice performance consistently resided within their original 95% CIs, indicating the relative robustness of the study findings (online supplemental materials 3).

Subgroup analysis

In the assessment of theoretical knowledge achievements, the medical student subgroup included 21 studies20,2527 (MD 6.46, 95% CI 5.02 to 7.91, p<0.001) with considerable heterogeneity (pheterogeneity <0.001, I2=95.0%), while the resident physician subgroup included 6 studies2630 38,40 46 (MD 3.45, 95% CI 1.75 to 5.16, p<0.001) with moderate heterogeneity (pheterogeneity =0.09, I2=48.2%). For clinical practice performance, the medical student subgroup comprised 21 studies20,2527 (MD 7.50, 95% CI 5.85 to 9.15, p<0.001) with considerable heterogeneity (pheterogeneity <0.001, I2=96.9%), and the resident physician subgroup included 6 studies2630 36 38,40 (MD 8.09, 95% CI 4.88 to 11.29, p<0.001) with considerable heterogeneity (pheterogeneity <0.001, I2=86.4%) (online supplemental materials 4).

Publication bias

For two outcomes with a relatively large number of included studies, funnel plot visual analysis and Egger’s test based on the restricted maximum likelihood were performed. Visually, a certain degree of asymmetry was observed in the funnel plot (figure 6A). Egger’s test (p<0.05) confirmed the presence of a significant small-study effect in the meta-analysis of theoretical knowledge achievements, suggesting a certain degree of publication bias. Further sensitivity analysis using the ‘trim and fill’ method showed no significant changes in the combined effect size after excluding individual studies, thus validating the robustness and reliability of our results. In contrast, for clinical performance, both the visual analysis of the funnel plot (figure 6B) and Egger’s test (p=0.346) collectively confirmed that the small-study effect was not significant, with a low level of publication bias.

Discussion

The study revealed that the combined SP+CBL educational model can significantly improve teaching satisfaction, theoretical knowledge scores and clinical practice among chinese medical students and resident physicians. These findings are consistent with our expected outcomes and the conclusions of previous studies. Further subgroup analysis revealed distinct competency progression patterns across medical education stages. The medical students demonstrated a more pronounced improvement in theoretical knowledge achievement, suggesting that the early theoretical learning phase of medical education provides a structured framework conducive to systematic knowledge acquisition. In contrast, resident physicians exhibited superior performance in practical skills, indicating that the intermediate clinical practice stage enhances procedural competence through intensive patient exposure and repeated skill reinforcement.

The SP methodology addresses systemic challenges in modern healthcare education such as reduced clinical exposure opportunities, declining patient cooperation and instructor shortages by simulating authentic clinical scenarios. This approach enhances learners’ diagnostic reasoning and patient communication skills, while bridging theory-practice gaps through structured feedback mechanisms, providing a standardised pathway for low-risk competency evaluation.50 51 Complementing this, CBL redefines the instructor’s role from knowledge to learning facilitator. Through self-directed case analysis and problem-solving, CBL stimulates clinical thinking and improves knowledge retention. Despite operational complexities, such as time-intensive case preparation, its efficacy in enhancing both theoretical understanding and procedural skills has been empirically validated.52

The integration of SP and CBL demonstrates synergistic benefits: SP’s contextualised communication training combined with CBL’s analytical rigour creates an immersive learning environment that elevates comprehensive clinical competence. This hybrid model reduces faculty workload through structured protocols and increases learner satisfaction, balancing educational efficacy with resource optimisation.53 Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated that the integration of Chatbots standardised patients (CSP) with CBL methodologies addresses the limitations of traditional SP approaches by providing enhanced reproducibility, extensive coverage, standardised training protocols and personalised interactive mechanisms. This innovative hybrid model, which facilitates targeted knowledge delivery and consistent clinical scenario replication, shows significant potential for broader implementation in medical education due to its capacity to improve the efficiency of clinical reasoning skill development.12

However, according to GRADE criteria, the evidence quality was moderate to low. Although rigorous inclusion criteria and statistical methods were applied, key limitations existed, including elevated risks of unblinded educational interventions and incomplete randomisation reporting, and considerable heterogeneity in theoretical knowledge achievements (I²=93.9%) and clinical practice performance (I²=96.2%), which were attributed to inconsistent institutional assessments and cohort variability. While the heterogeneity in teaching satisfaction was low, the limited number of included studies resulted in wide CI for the results.

The inherent ethical and methodological constraints of educational RCTs inevitably compromised the methodological rigour of the included studies. We conducted subgroup analyses by categorising the study participants into two subgroups (medical students and resident physicians) to further investigate the sources of heterogeneity, the results indicated persistent substantial heterogeneity. This suggests that participant classification alone does not fundamentally account for the observed variability. Notably, no differential effectiveness was detected between the SP+CBL intervention in enhancing theoretical knowledge versus clinical skill acquisition among Chinese medical trainees.

We ultimately posit that the outcome heterogeneity likely originated from multiple interacting factors, including regional disparities in SP+CBL implementation protocols (such as variations in case complexity, standardised patient training duration, instructional frequency, learner specialties, academic seniority and residency training stages), inconsistent sample sizes across studies, baseline aptitude differences among learners and potential cross-group contamination during intervention delivery. However, insufficient data availability on critical intervention details in the included studies precluded subgroup analyses to investigate these potential factors.

Funnel plot analysis and Egger’s test for theoretical knowledge outcomes revealed evidence of publication bias, presumably stemming from the absence of negative results in small-sample studies. This may somewhat compromise the reliability of the research findings. Given that all included studies were published in Chinese, potential residual language bias may persist despite our comprehensive searches across multiple databases. Geographically, these findings are contextually constrained to clinical education environments within China’s healthcare system, thereby limiting direct extrapolation to other cultural contexts or healthcare education models. The generalisability of results remains particularly questionable for medical education systems employing substantially different pedagogical approaches or competency evaluation frameworks.

To advance this field, future research should prioritise developing standardised competency assessment frameworks and employing longitudinal designs to evaluate both skill retention and long-term clinical performance outcomes. Despite these limitations, the synthesised evidence supports SP+CBL as a transformative pedagogical strategy for enhancing clinical competencies within resource-constrained medical education systems.

Conclusions

The systematic review and meta-analysis on SP+CBL in Chinese clinical education shows it enhances knowledge retention and dynamic learning compared with traditional methods, bridging theory-practice gaps and boosting learner competence and satisfaction. Limitations include methodological constraints inherent to educational RCTs, notable outcome heterogeneity driven by sample size differences and regional variability and lack of multinational data, with evidence largely limited to the Chinese context. Future multicentre RCTs across populations are needed to confirm global applicability.
