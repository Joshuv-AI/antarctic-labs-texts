The association between dietary carbohydrate to protein ratio and risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome in Iranian adults: a cohort study
Authors: Zahra Gaeini, Sahar Mirzaei, Parvin Mirmiran, Fereidoun Azizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe dietary balance of macronutrients may play a critical role in the development of cardio-metabolic diseases. The carbohydrate-to-protein (CHO/Pr) ratio provides an index of this balance; however, its association with long-term health outcomes in Middle Eastern populations remains unclear. We investigated the relationship between dietary CHO/Pr ratio and the incidence of cardiovascular disease (CVD), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and metabolic syndrome (MetS) among adults from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS).MethodsA total of 2648 adults free of CVD, 2256 adults free of T2DM, and 1713 adults free of MetS at baseline (2006–2008) were included. Dietary intakes were assessed using a validated 168-item food frequency questionnaire. The CHO/Pr ratio was calculated and categorized into tertiles. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident cardio-metabolic outcomes were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusted for demographic, lifestyle, and dietary covariates.ResultsOver median follow-up periods of 10.6, 8.5, and 7.6 years for CVD, T2DM, and MetS, respectively, 171 CVD, 241 T2DM, and 596 MetS cases occurred. Compared with the lowest tertile, participants in the second tertile of CHO/Pr ratio had a higher risk of CVD (HR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.01–2.17), whereas no significant association was observed in the highest tertile. No significant associations were found between CHO/Pr ratio and incidence of T2DM or MetS.ConclusionsA moderate CHO/Pr ratio was associated with an increased risk of CVD, but not with T2DM or MetS. These findings highlight the potential importance of macronutrient balance in cardiovascular prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01351-5.Keywords: Carbohydrate-to-protein ratio, Cardiovascular disease, Type 2 diabetes, Metabolic syndrome

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and metabolic syndrome (MetS) remain leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and their prevalence continues to rise in low- and middle-income countries, including Iran [1]. Lifestyle-related risk factors, particularly diet, play a central role in the development and prevention of these conditions [2].

Beyond the effects of individual nutrients, the relative distribution of macronutrients in the diet may influence cardio-metabolic health. Diets high in carbohydrates, especially refined carbohydrates, have been associated with adverse metabolic outcomes and increased mortality in several large prospective studies [3]. Conversely, higher protein intake has been linked to improvements in satiety, weight management, blood glucose regulation, and lipid profiles, although findings differ depending on the source of protein (animal vs. plant) [4, 5].

The dietary carbohydrate-to-protein (CHO/Pr) ratio provides a simple metric to capture the balance between these two macronutrients. Emerging evidence suggests that this ratio may be more informative than absolute intakes alone in relation to disease risk. For example, higher CHO/Pr ratios have been associated with increased mortality in U.S. and European cohorts [6], while lower ratios, reflecting relatively higher protein intake, have been associated with favorable metabolic profiles [7]. However, evidence remains limited and inconsistent, particularly in Middle Eastern populations with distinct dietary patterns characterized by high carbohydrate consumption.

Given the high burden of CVD, T2DM, and MetS in Iran and the limited evidence regarding the role of macronutrient distribution in these outcomes, it is important to investigate the relationship between dietary CHO/Pr ratio and incident cardio-metabolic diseases in this population. Using data from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS), a large prospective cohort, we aimed to examine the association of dietary CHO/Pr ratio with the risk of CVD, T2DM, and MetS in a population of Iranian adults.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was conducted within the framework of the TLGS, an ongoing community-based prospective cohort initiated in 1999 to investigate risk factors for non-communicable diseases among residents of district 13 of Tehran, Iran [8]. For the present analysis, we used data from the third survey (2006–2008) as baseline, where detailed dietary information was collected using a validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). Participants were followed until the sixth survey (2015–2018).

We excluded individuals with missing dietary data, implausible energy intakes (< 800 or > 4200 kcal/day), those with prevalent CVD, T2DM, or MetS at baseline (depending on the outcome analysis), and participants lost to follow-up. The final analytic sample included 2648 participants for CVD, 2256 for T2DM and 1,713 for MetS incidence. The eligible participants were followed up until the end of the study. Median follow-up periods for CVD, T2DM and MetS analyses were 10.6, 8.5 and 7.6 years from the baseline examination, respectively.

Written informed consents were obtained from all participants. The study protocol was approved by the ethics research council of the Research Institute for Endocrine Sciences, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences.

Measurements

Demographic information was obtained through a structured questionnaire administered by trained interviewers. Anthropometric indices, including body weight, height, and waist circumference (WC), were measured using calibrated digital scales (Seca, Hamburg, Germany) and a non-stretch tape, respectively. Body mass index (BMI) was derived as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Systolic (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were assessed on the right arm with a standard mercury sphygmomanometer, calibrated by the Iranian Institute of Standards and Industrial Research [9]. Physical activity was evaluated using the validated modifiable activity questionnaire (MAQ) and expressed in metabolic equivalent minutes per week (MET-min/week) (Ainsworth, 1993). Scores ≤ 600 MET-min/week were classified as low physical activity, while higher scores indicated moderate-to-high activity levels. The validity and reliability of the Persian version of the MAQ have been previously established [10].

Blood samples were collected after an overnight fast between 7:00 and 9:00 AM. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and serum lipids were determined by enzymatic colorimetric assays: FPG with glucose oxidase, triglycerides (TG) with glycerol phosphate oxidase, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) with phosphotungstic acid. All analyses were performed at the TLGS research laboratory using Pars Azmoon kits (Pars Azmoon Inc., Tehran, Iran) and a Selectra 2 auto-analyzer (Vital Scientific, Spankeren, The Netherlands). Coefficients of variation for inter- and intra-assay measurements were consistently below 5% during both baseline and follow-up phases.

Dietary data were collected at baseline using a validated 168-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [11]. Participants reported the frequency and portion size of foods consumed during the previous year. Reported portion sizes were converted to grams using household measures, and average daily intakes were calculated. Nutrient and energy intakes were estimated using the USDA food composition tables, complemented with Iranian food composition data when available. Daily intakes of carbohydrate (CHO) and protein (Pr), in form of percentage of total energy intake, were determined, and the dietary carbohydrate-to-protein (CHO/Pr) ratio was calculated by dividing total carbohydrate intake (percentage of total energy intake/day) by total protein intake (percentage of total energy intake/day).

Ascertainment of outcomes

Information regarding the ascertainment of CVD outcomes has been published previously [12]. In the present study, CVD events were defined as coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke—characterized as a new neurological deficit persisting for at least 24 h—or cardiovascular mortality, including definite fatal myocardial infarction (MI), fatal CHD, and fatal stroke. CHD events encompassed definite MI, confirmed by diagnostic ECG findings and cardiac biomarkers; probable MI defined by positive ECG changes together with cardiac symptoms or signs in the absence of biomarkers, or positive ECG findings with equivocal biomarkers; as well as angiographically documented CHD. Participants with a prior history of ischemic heart disease or cerebrovascular accident were classified as having prevalent CVD.

T2DM was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, 2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL during oral glucose tolerance test, or use of glucose-lowering medications according to ADA criteria [13]. MetS was defined using the NCEP ATP III diagnostic criteria; presence of ≥ 3 of the following: Hyperglycemia (FSG ≥ 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or self-reported use of blood glucose-lowering medication). 2. Hypertriglyceridemia (serum TG ≥ 150 mg/dL (1.69 mmol/L) or use of lipid-lowering drugs). 3. Low HDL-C levels (serum HDL-C < 40 mg/dL (1.04 mmol/L) for men and < 50 mg/dL (1.29 mmol/L) for women or use of drug treatment). 4. Hypertension (SBP/DBP ≥ 130/85 mmHg or use of drug treatment for hypertension). 5. Abdominal obesity (WC ≥ 95 cm for both genders). Modified cutoff points for WC specific to Iranian adults were used [14].

Statistical analysis

The incidences of CVD, T2DM, and MetS during follow-up were treated as binary outcomes (yes/no) in all models. Dietary carbohydrate-to-protein (CHO/Pr) ratio (percentage from total energy) was categorized into tertiles, with the lowest tertile considered as the reference. Baseline characteristics were compared between participants based on the status of CVD, T2DM and MetS, expressed as mean (± SD) for continuous variables and frequency (%) for categorical variables.

Cox proportional hazards regression was applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association of CHO/Pr with incidence of CVD, T2DM, and MetS. Person-years were used as the underlying time scale. For each participant, follow-up time was calculated from baseline until either the occurrence of the event, censoring, or end of study, whichever came first. For censored or lost-to-follow-up cases, survival time was defined as the interval between the first and last available assessments. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld’s global test.

Confounders were identified through univariate analysis, with variables showing P entry < 0.20 entered into multivariable models. Potential confounders included age, sex, BMI, physical activity level, smoking status, total energy intake, and total fat intake.

To examine whether the association between CHO/Pr ratio and cardio-metabolic outcomes varied by macronutrient source and quality, we conducted pre-specified sub-analyses stratifying macronutrients by origin and degree of processing. Animal protein was defined as the sum of protein derived from red meat, processed meat, poultry, fish, eggs, and dairy products as captured by the 168-item semi-quantitative FFQ. Plant protein was defined as the sum of protein from legumes, nuts, seeds, whole grains, and vegetables reported in the same FFQ. Refined carbohydrates were operationalized as carbohydrate intake from white bread, white rice, refined pasta, sugar, sweets, and sugar-sweetened beverages. Whole-grain carbohydrates were defined as carbohydrate intake from whole-grain bread, brown rice, whole-grain cereals, and bran. For each participant, we calculated four macronutrient-quality ratios: carbohydrate-to-animal-protein ratio (total carbohydrate grams per day divided by animal protein grams per day), carbohydrate-to-plant-protein ratio (total carbohydrate divided by plant protein), refined carbohydrate-to-total-protein ratio (refined carbohydrate divided by total protein), and whole-grain carbohydrate-to-total-protein ratio (whole-grain carbohydrate divided by total protein). Each ratio was categorized into tertiles, and participants were followed for incident CVD, T2DM, and MetS. We applied the same multivariable Cox proportional-hazards models described in the primary analysis.

To formally evaluate potential non-linear dose–response relationships between the dietary CHO/Pr ratio and cardio-metabolic outcomes, we conducted restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses using Cox proportional hazards models. Knots were placed at the median values of each tertile of the CHO/Pr ratio (approximately 3.54, 4.33, and 5.12). The lowest tertile was used as the reference category. All models were adjusted for sex, age, BMI, physical activity, smoking status, total energy intake, and total fat intake (Model 2). A likelihood ratio test was used to assess departure from linearity by comparing the model with the spline terms to a model with only a linear term.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Restricted cubic spline analysis

The RCS analysis for CVD outcome revealed a statistically significant non-linear (Inverted-U shaped) association between the dietary CHO/Pr ratio and CVD risk (P for non-linearity < 0.001) (Table 3). The hazard ratio increased from the reference (T1: CHO/Pr ≈ 3.54) to a peak at approximately CHO/Pr ratio = 4.36 (HR ≈ 1.48), corresponding to the second tertile, and then declined toward the third tertile (HR = 1.09 at CHO/Pr ≈ 5.12). This pattern suggests that moderate CHO/Pr ratios may confer the highest CVD risk, while very high ratios may attenuate this association.

The RCS analysis for T2DM (Table 3) showed a borderline non-linear association between CHO/Pr ratio and T2DM risk (P for non-linearity = 0.052). The curve exhibited an inverted-U shape, with a modest peak near CHO/Pr ratio ≈ 4.1, followed by a decline at higher ratios (HR = 0.76 at T3). Although the non-linearity test did not reach conventional statistical significance, the visual trend is consistent with the tertile-based findings.

For MetS, the RCS analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant non-linear relationship (P for non-linearity = 0.765) (Table 3). The association appeared to follow a gradual monotone increasing trend across the range of CHO/Pr ratios, with HR increasing modestly from T1 (reference) to T3 (HR = 1.11), though confidence intervals included the null throughout.

Macronutrient quality and cardio-metabolic outcomes

To further elucidate the role of macronutrient quality, we examined associations between protein source-specific and carbohydrate quality-specific ratios and cardio-metabolic outcomes (Supplementary Table S1). The CHO/animal-protein ratio showed a significant inverse association with T2DM risk (HR for T3 vs. T1: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.50–1.00; P for trend = 0.049), suggesting that higher carbohydrate intake relative to animal protein may be protective against T2DM development. In contrast, no significant association was observed for the CHO/plant-protein ratio with any outcome, although a borderline positive trend was noted for CVD (P for trend = 0.089). When carbohydrate quality was considered, the refined CHO/protein ratio demonstrated a significant positive association with CVD risk (HR for T3 vs. T1: 1.62; 95% CI: 1.10–2.38; P for trend = 0.018) and a borderline significant association with MetS (HR for T3 vs. T1: 1.22; 95% CI: 1.00–1.49; P for trend = 0.052). Conversely, the whole-grain CHO/protein ratio showed no significant associations with any cardio-metabolic outcome. These findings underscore the importance of both protein source and carbohydrate quality in determining cardio-metabolic risk, with refined carbohydrate intake relative to protein emerging as a particularly important risk factor for CVD in this Iranian cohort.

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort of Iranian adults, we examined the association between dietary carbohydrate-to-protein (CHO/Pr) ratio and the incidence of CVD, T2DM and MetS. Our results showed that participants in the middle tertile of CHO/Pr ratio had a modestly higher risk of CVD compared with those in the lowest tertile, whereas no significant associations were observed with T2DM or MetS. These findings suggest that dietary balance between carbohydrate and protein may influence cardiovascular health, although the pattern was not linear across categories.

Previous studies have reported inconsistent associations between macronutrient distribution and cardio-metabolic outcomes. In the PURE study, higher carbohydrate intake was associated with increased risk of mortality and CVD, whereas higher fat intake appeared protective [3]. Similarly, Seidelmann et al. demonstrated a U-shaped relationship between carbohydrate intake and mortality, with both very high and very low intakes associated with increased risk [6]. Although these studies primarily focused on the absolute contribution of macronutrients to total energy intake, our findings support the importance of considering their relative balance, as reflected in the CHO/Pr ratio. The observed increased CVD risk in the middle, but not the highest tertile of CHO/Pr ratio, may be explained by residual confounding or by the heterogeneity of dietary patterns within tertiles. It is also possible that moderate imbalances between carbohydrate and protein exert more adverse effects than extreme values, though this requires confirmation in future research.

Regarding T2DM, evidence on the role of protein intake and carbohydrate–protein balance remains inconclusive. A recent meta-analysis indicated that higher total protein intake may reduce the risk of T2DM, particularly when protein is of plant origin [15]. Conversely, some cohort studies have suggested that high animal protein intake could increase T2DM risk [16, 17]. Also, for MetS, prior research has suggested that both high-carbohydrate and high-protein diets can influence its components, such as obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia [5, 7]. In our analysis the CHO/Pr ratio was not significantly associated with incident T2DM or MetS after multivariable adjustment. Several, likely complementary, explanations can account for these null findings. First, limited between-person variation and measurement error in exposure may attenuate true associations. FFQs, even when validated, introduce non-differential measurement error in absolute nutrient estimates; such error usually biases risk estimates toward the null, particularly when the exposure contrast is modest [18]. In our sample the mean CHO/Pr ratio clustered around ~ 4.3 (Table 1), indicating a relatively narrow distribution; when contrast across categories is small, statistical power to detect association’s declines.

Second, quality of macronutrients matters. A simple CHO/Pr ratio captures quantity balance but not carbohydrate quality (refined vs. whole-grain, glycemic index/load) nor protein source (plant vs. animal, processed vs. unprocessed). There is robust evidence that refined carbohydrates and sugar-sweetened beverages increase T2DM and MetS risk, whereas whole grains and legumes are protective [2]. Likewise, plant protein generally shows neutral or protective associations, whereas some forms of processed red meat are associated with higher risk [19, 20]. Our sub-analyses revealed that the association between CHO/Pr ratio and cardio-metabolic risk was strongly modified by macronutrient source and processing. Participants in the highest tertile of refined CHO/Pr ratio experienced a 62% higher risk of incident CVD compared with the lowest tertile (HR = 1.62, 95% CI 1.09–2.41; p for trend = 0.018), consistent with a glycemic-load mechanism linking refined carbohydrate burden to postprandial hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and endothelial dysfunction [1]. This finding aligns closely with prior evidence from the TLGS, in which participants in the highest quartile of dietary glycemic load—a marker of refined carbohydrate intake—had nearly threefold higher CVD risk (HR = 2.77, 95% CI 1.00–7.69) [2]. Similarly, a large Chinese prospective cohort reported that higher refined-grain intake was associated with an 80% increase in coronary heart disease risk (highest versus lowest quartile HR = 1.80, 95% CI 1.01–3.17) [3]. These converging results underscore the adverse cardiovascular impact of diets dominated by refined grains and added sugars, a pattern highly prevalent in contemporary Iranian dietary habits where white rice and white bread constitute the majority of daily energy intake [4].

In contrast, a higher carbohydrate-to-animal-protein ratio was associated with lower T2DM risk in the highest tertile (HR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.51–0.99; p for trend = 0.049), suggesting that reducing the proportion of animal-source protein may confer metabolic protection. This inverse association is biologically plausible given that higher animal protein intake has been linked to elevated systemic inflammation and insulin resistance through pathways involving high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, leptin, and sex hormone–binding globulin [5]. Large-scale prospective analyses from the Women’s Health Initiative and UK Biobank cohorts demonstrated that higher animal protein intake was associated with a 31% increase in T2DM risk (highest versus lowest quintile HR = 1.31, 95% CI 1.24–1.37), and that substituting 5% of energy from plant protein for animal protein reduced T2DM risk by 21% (HR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.74–0.84) [5]. These findings suggest that the metabolic harm of animal protein is partly mediated by adiposity-related inflammatory and endocrine biomarkers, and that Iranian adults consuming diets higher in carbohydrate relative to animal protein may experience lower diabetes incidence through reduced inflammatory burden.

The whole-grain CHO/Pr ratio showed neutral associations with all three outcomes, indicating that when carbohydrate intake is predominantly whole-grain in origin, the ratio to protein does not materially influence cardio-metabolic risk. This neutrality is consistent with the known benefits of whole grains—higher fiber content, lower glycemic response, and greater micronutrient density—which may offset any potential adverse effects of higher CHO/Pr ratios [6]. In the TLGS, a higher ratio of whole grains to total grains was associated with a 25% lower risk of incident MetS (highest versus lowest quartile HR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.60–0.94), supporting the protective role of whole-grain carbohydrate quality [7]. The absence of harm from higher whole-grain CHO/Pr ratios in our cohort reinforces the importance of distinguishing refined from whole-grain sources when evaluating macronutrient balance.

Taken together, these quality-stratified findings have important implications for dietary guidance in Iran, where refined grains dominate carbohydrate intake and animal-source protein consumption is rising [4]. Public health strategies should prioritize replacement of refined grains with whole-grain alternatives and encourage plant-based protein sources to mitigate cardio-metabolic risk, particularly for CVD and T2DM prevention.

Third, residual confounding and mediating pathways may explain the absence of association. Body weight and adiposity mediate much of the diet→T2DM/MetS pathway. In our models we adjusted for baseline BMI (and in some models for waist circumference). If BMI lies on the causal path (e.g., CHO/Pr → weight change → T2DM), adjustment for BMI would reduce the apparent diet effect. Conversely, not adjusting for post-baseline weight changes may leave residual confounding. Marginal structural models or formal mediation analyses can help disentangle mediation versus confounding [21].

Fourth, statistical power and event rates matter. Compared with CVD, T2DM and MetS events in a given follow-up period may have different incidences and timing; limited number of events, particularly in stratified analyses, reduces ability to detect modest associations. Moreover, if the diet effect on diabetes/MetS requires a longer latency or cumulative exposure than the follow-up available, a single baseline FFQ may not capture the etiologically relevant exposure window.

Finally, population-specific dietary patterns can modulate associations. Middle Eastern diets often have high staple carbohydrate consumption but differ in sources and co-consumed foods compared with Western cohorts (e.g., more bread, rice, legumes, and different cooking methods). Therefore, findings from Western cohorts may not generalize, and the net metabolic impact of a given CHO/Pr ratio could differ by dietary context.

Together, these points provide plausible reasons why, despite biologic plausibility, our cohort did not show significant CHO/Pr—T2DM/MetS associations. Importantly, null results do not exclude clinically meaningful effects — they highlight the need for more granular analyses (see below).

It should be noted that a ratio such as CHO/Pr condenses the relative macronutrient balance into a single index, which can be advantageous because human diets are inherently compositional; increasing the share of one macronutrient necessarily reduces the share of another (macronutrient substitution). Ratio metrics therefore reflect the substitutional nature of diet better than isolated absolute intakes. From a public-health perspective, a CHO/Pr ratio quickly signals whether a diet is carbohydrate-dominant or protein-rich and may be more interpretable for guideline translation. However, ratios have limitations. They conflate changes in both numerator and denominator and ignore the absolute energy context (e.g., a ratio of 4 can arise from low-energy or high-energy diets). More informative analytical techniques are iso-caloric substitution models (which estimate the effect of replacing X kcal from carbs with X kcal from protein) and compositional data analysis that treats macronutrients as parts of a whole. Evidence from substitution analyses indicates that replacing refined carbohydrate with protein (particularly plant protein) often associates with lower T2DM risk, whereas replacing carbohydrates with saturated animal fat may increase CVD risk [19, 22]. Thus, combining ratio-based description with substitution modeling yields a more complete picture.

Commonly recommended macronutrient distributions (e.g., carbohydrate 45–65% energy; protein 10–35% energy) correspond to CHO/Pr ratios roughly in the range ~ 1.3 to 6.5, depending on the exact percentages. Guidance from the Institute of Medicine (IOM) and national dietary guidelines (e.g., Dietary Guidelines for Americans) use Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Ranges (AMDRs): carbohydrate 45–65% of energy, protein 10–35%, fat 20–35% [23]. These AMDRs imply typical CHO/Pr ratios in ~ 2–6 for many populations; an optimal ratio is not universally specified because health effects depend on macronutrient quality and total energy. Population averages vary; many Western populations consume ~ 45–55% energy from carbohydrate and ~ 12–18% from protein (CHO/Pr ≈ 3–5). In contrast, several Asian and Middle Eastern populations have higher carbohydrate shares (often ≥ 60% energy) and lower protein percentages, yielding higher CHO/Pr ratios (≈ 4–6 or more). In our study, mean ± SD of CHO/Pr ratios were 4.32 ± 0.97, 4.35 ± 0.97, and 4.33 ± 0.98 in CVD, T2DM and MetS population, respectively. Our cohort’s mean ratios (~ 4.3) sit toward the higher end of the Western range and are consistent with other reports from the region. These differences in baseline macronutrient profiles can modify the direction and magnitude of diet–disease associations reported across cohorts.

Beyond the earlier-stated strengths (prospective design, validated FFQ, adjudicated outcomes), this study benefits from well-characterized covariates and use of established risk scores to stabilize models. Limitations include single baseline dietary assessment, potential misclassification of protein and carbohydrate sources, limited power for subgroup analyses, and the observational design that precludes causal inference. Future work should examine substitution models, repeated dietary measures, and compositional analyses to provide more mechanistic and policy-relevant insights.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings suggest that the dietary carbohydrate-to-protein ratio may be associated with CVD risk, while no significant associations were observed for T2DM and MetS. These results highlight the importance of considering macronutrient balance in dietary recommendations for cardiovascular prevention. Further studies, particularly those accounting for protein source and carbohydrate quality, are warranted to clarify these relationships.
