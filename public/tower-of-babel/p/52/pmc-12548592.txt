Pragmatic paradigm for patient-reported outcome measure selection in lymphoma clinical trials: a rapid review study
Authors: Jennifer D Guo, Abraham Hartzema, Jonathon B Cohen, Nicole Tunstall, Adel Gehchan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesLymphoma is a haematologic malignancy affecting cells of the immune system. With numerous treatment options available, clinicians and patients frequently face difficulty in selecting the most appropriate therapy. Patient-reported Outcome (PRO) offers valuable patients’ insight that may support treatment differentiation. A PRO measure (PROM) is a questionnaire or survey measuring a PRO. Despite many efforts to guide the selection of PROMs in clinical trials, choosing the appropriate ones remains a challenge. This study aims to develop a pragmatic paradigm for selecting PROMs in clinical trials involving adult patients with lymphoma through the collaboration and communication between clinical investigators involved in trials and Health Economics and Outcomes Research (HEOR) scientists specialised in research methodologies.DesignA rapid review was conducted to identify existing PROMs for adult patients with lymphoma in clinical trials and guidelines supporting PROM selection.Data sourcesPubMed, Google Scholar and websites for regulatory and health technology assessment (HTA) bodies in eight countries of interest were searched from 2009 to July 2024.Eligibility criteriaPublications with PROMs were identified for adult patients in lymphoma trials. The most relevant guidelines supporting the development of the pragmatic paradigm were selected.Data extraction and synthesisThe initial search and data extraction were conducted by one author. All authors participated in an in-depth review process.ResultsWe categorise 31 applicable PROMs for lymphoma into four distinct groups, streamlining the PROM selection process to facilitate effective communication among clinical investigators, HEOR scientists, patients and others. Additionally, a five-step pragmatic paradigm is developed for identifying appropriate PROM(s).ConclusionsThe pragmatic paradigm presents a practical approach for selecting PROM(s) in lymphoma clinical trials. An appropriate PROM should conceptually align with the treatment goals and be acceptable to regulatory and HTA bodies. Thus, lymphoma clinical trials can generate more patient-focused data, contributing to improving patients’ quality of life and advancing lymphoma care.

Introduction

Lymphoma is a broad term for heterogeneous malignancies arising from the clonal proliferation of lymphocytes. Two basic forms are Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non-HL (NHL), with rare subtypes.13 There are numerous emerging molecules and novel treatment strategies targeting various expressions of lymphoma.4 5 Clinicians and patients often face difficulty in evaluating similar treatments using traditional cancer clinical trial endpoints,6 7 which may help differentiate between treatments.2 8

Patient-reported outcome (PRO) is defined as ‘any report of the status of a patient’s health condition that comes directly from the patient, without interpretation of the patient’s response by a clinician or anyone else’.9 Practically, PROs refer to patients’ perspective on diseases and/or treatments in terms of disease symptoms, functions, quality of life (QOL)/health-related QOL (HRQOL)/health utility, adherence to/satisfaction with treatments and side effects of treatments.1014 A PRO measure (PROM) is a questionnaire or survey measuring a PRO.15 16 A PROM is suggested in a clinical trial when a concept is best known by patients or measured from the patients’ perspective.9 PRO data will inform clinicians, regulatory, health technology assessment (HTA) bodies/payers of patients’ experience with treatment(s).12 14 17

In the current patient-centric research era, the value of PROs is increasingly recognised and advocated in clinical trials.14 18 19 However, the impact of PRO data has been limited by trial design, PROM selection, data collection, analysis and reporting.20 21 Although many efforts are made to guide the appropriate PROM selection,92225 it still remains a challenge.1416 20 2629 Literature indicates that PROs are less frequently included in haematological malignancy trials (including lymphoma) compared with solid tumour trials.17 21 When clinical investigators design clinical trials, PROMs are often selected based on their recognition or previous usage.29 It is essential that clinical investigators involved in trials collaborate with Health Economics and Outcomes Research (HEOR) scientists who specialise in research methodologies.8

There is an urgent need for an actionable approach to choose the PROM(s) efficiently. This study aims to develop a pragmatic paradigm for selecting PROM(s) in clinical trials involving adult patients with lymphoma through the collaboration and communication between clinical investigators and HEOR scientists. Thereby, the paradigm will advance clinical treatment strategies to improve the QOL for patients with lymphoma.

Methods

Due to the urgent need, a rapid literature review30 was adapted. Literature related to PROs/PROMs was searched from 20099 to July 2024. We searched through databases including PubMed, Google Scholar and websites for regulatory and HTA bodies in eight countries: England, France, Germany, Spain, Italy, the USA, Canada and China. These countries are the primary pharmaceutical markets that profoundly affect global healthcare decisions, of which China is a large emerging market.

The five regulatory agencies include the European Medicines Agency for the European Union countries, Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency in the UK, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in the USA, Health Canada in Canada and the National Medical Products Administration in China. We also looked at the nine HTA or HTA-like bodies. Among these, the European countries and Canada have established formal HTA processes, while HTA-like bodies are in the USA and China. The HTA bodies of interest refer to the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence in England; the Haute Autorité de Santé in France; the Federal Joint Committee and the Institute for Quality and Efficiency in Health Care in Germany; Agencia de Evaluación de Tecnologías Sanitarias (Health Technology Assessment Agency in English) in Spain; Agenzia Italiana del Farmaco (Italian Drug Agency in English) in Italy and the Canadian Drug Agency (formerly called Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health in Canada). The HTA-like bodies include the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review and the Academy of Managed Care Pharmacy in the USA and the China National Health Development Research Center in China.

The keywords in English included various combinations of PRO, PROM, good practice, guideline, guidance, oncology, haematology/haematology, haematological/haematological malignancies and lymphoma. We started by concentrating on review-type articles. Online translational tools were employed as needed and verified by multilingual coauthors.

This study initially screened abstracts and assessed full-text articles as needed. Then, the reference lists of the selected articles were scanned for relevant sources. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed based on the authors’ knowledge and experience in the subject matter. The inclusion criteria were: (1) publications on PROs/PROMs in oncology; (2) guidelines/good practices relevant to PROs/PROMs. The exclusion criteria were: (1) non-lymphoma-relevant PROs/PROMs; (2) irrelevant to the selection guidelines of PROs/PROMs. The authors searched for the final version of guidelines to check if the selected ones were still in draft status prior to journal submission.

PROMs practical usage in clinical trials

To facilitate the selection of PROMs, it is informative to understand how they are adopted in clinical trials, including trial design, inclusion proportion, the endpoint model of a PROM and the number of PROMs used. This information was only identified in review articles for oncology trials.

Internationally open-label trials are the most used in oncology, ranging from 72.4% to 79%.21 27 31 In Europe, the EMA is more likely to accept PRO data in the product label from open-label trials, as compared with the FDA.17

The proportion of oncology trials included PROs ranges from 35% to 73%.31 32 PROMs are most commonly included as secondary or exploratory endpoints.19 28 31 When PROs are included in a trial, more than one PROM is often used.20 27 36

PRO decision team establishment to set objectives

The PRO decision team should consist of members from multidisciplinary backgrounds.2 It has at least one clinical investigator knowledgeable about lymphoma and its treatment landscape; and an HEOR scientist who is a subject matter expert familiar with the PRO concept and relevant guidelines. It is valuable to engage external key opinion leaders (KOLs) specialising in lymphoma and/or PROMs; and patients with lymphoma and/or patient advocacy groups. The team’s objective is to develop a strategy for selecting appropriate PRO(s)/PROM(s) and creating an execution plan to align with clinical trial goals, such as regulatory labelling, HTA success and/or integration into clinical practice.

PRO concept definition

To determine the PRO concept relevant to specific lymphoma, we suggest that the decision team follows the FDA conceptual framework33 composed of two models below.

The first conceptual model is to support the first two parts of the roadmap to patient-focused outcome measurement in clinical trials37: (a) understanding the disease or condition (ie, features of the target population) and (b) conceptualising treatment clinical benefits and risks.13 33 Specifically, the concept of interest (COI, how a patient feels, functions or survives) and the concept of use (COU, the context of use for a clinical trial) need to be defined.33 The second measurement model generates a score (or scores) to assess the treatment’s impact on PRO.7 33 The PRO can be measured in absolute terms or as a change from a previous measure.9

Clinically, lymphoma patients often fall into three groups: curable aggressive group, incurable aggressive group and incurable low-grade group over a long period of time. The treatment under each scenario requires weighing patients’ willingness to tolerate impaired QOL and treatment goals. For example, a patient with curable lymphoma may be willing to tolerate the transient impaired QOL for the cure, while patients with incurable low-grade lymphoma and long-term treatment may prioritise decent QOL with limited toxicity. In essence, the PRO concept relies on the severity of heterogeneous lymphoma and treatment goals.

PROM selection considerations

The PROM selection is a crucial step with five segments.

PROM psychometric attributes

An appropriate PROM needs to have three psychometric attributes: validity, reliability and responsiveness.9 The validity refers to the extent to which a PROM measures what it is supposed to measure.7 It mainly contains content validity and construct validity.9 The reliability measures the consistency or stability of a PROM, which contains test–retest (intrainterviewer), internal consistency and interinterviewer.2 9 The responsiveness (or sensitivity) is the extent to which a PROM detects changes when the COI changes.2 9 To note, a PROM is not valid or reliable on its own as it relies on the growing bodies of evidence in various conditions and clinical settings.7 23

PROM feasibility perspective

There are several feasibility considerations during implementation, such as patient/investigator burden (eg, administration burden), cultural/language adaptations and others.2 13

PROM interpretability

When choosing a PROM, interpretability is important, especially the minimal important difference (MID, also known as the minimal clinically important difference), which shows the smallest meaningful score change perceived by patients as an improvement or deterioration.7 23 Moreover, the MID separates statistical and clinical significance, as a PROM might show a statistically significant change that’s too small to be clinically relevant. The FDA encourages pharmaceutical companies to avoid proposing labelling claims based solely on statistical significance.7 9 22

Regulatory and HTA/payer perspectives

The FDA and EMA have different focuses when using PROM(s) for regulatory approval. The FDA emphasises disease/treatment-related symptoms, functions and safety.14 19 38 In contrast, the EMA-granted claims are more likely to include HRQOL or function measures.19

Accordingly, HTA bodies/payers evaluate PROM(s) differently. US payers prioritise perceived improvements in symptom severity or frequency.14 On the other hand, non-US payers emphasise the importance of HRQOL or functional status.14

Trial design elements relevant to PROM

The key elements of a trial related to PROM include the trial design, the endpoint model of a PROM, and the number of PROMs included. Both the FDA and EMA recommend a double-blind design for PRO collection as open-label designs are rarely adequate to support label claims.27 The FDA suggests that the PROM properties be well established before patient enrolment in confirmatory clinical trials. Hence, including PROM(s) in the early phase, especially for insufficiently validated ones, might be an opportunity to inform its strategic usage/selection in the confirmatory phase.11 39 40

Regarding the PROM endpoint model, it is often suggested that PROM(s) be included as primary or secondary endpoints to support regulatory labelling or meet HTA requirements.9 15 A PROM could be a primary endpoint for palliative care or rehabilitation trials.12 As an exploratory endpoint, PROM(s) could be used for economic evaluation purposes or hypothesis testing for future confirmatory studies.9 12

To meet the needs of all stakeholders in various countries, clinical trials could include one generic HRQOL, one disease-group/disease-specific HRQOL, and one symptoms/functions/safety PROM.2 15 16 38 41 The final number of PROMs included should be guided by the specific objective of incorporating PROMs in the clinical trial.

PRO consultation

To determine what matters to patients, it is essential to consult with clinicians and patients at an early stage of a trial. Moreover, consultation with regulatory and HTA bodies is pivotal to ensure the acceptance of PROM(s).9 15 27 42 Especially for rare diseases, evaluators of PROM(s) are frequently sceptical that the PROM accurately assesses what it purports to measure, or that the supporting evidence is robust enough.16

PRO fit-for-purpose finalisation

The last step of the paradigm is to finalise the fit-for-purpose PRO choice. Fit-for-purpose in the regulatory context means validity.33 The considerations are twofold: (1) describe COI and COU clearly and (2) provide sufficient evidence to support a clear rationale for using the PROM(s) and a proposed interpretation.

Discussion

Through literature review and multidisciplinary collaboration, this study offers a pragmatic paradigm for existing PROM selection in adult lymphoma clinical trials.

During the literature review, the authors discovered variability in PROM terminology. The FDA identifies three core psychometric attributes for PROMs: validity, reliability and responsiveness (ie, ability to detect meaningful change).9 In contrast, the European Network for Health Technology Assessment (EUnetHTA) builds on this foundation by also including acceptability (or feasibility).41 On reliability, the International Society for Quality of Life Research (ISOQOL) group recommends a coefficient of ≥0.70 in 2013,22 while Mckenna et al suggest a coefficient of ≥0.85 in 2021.29 Regarding responsiveness, the FDA and EUnetHTA treat it as a distinct psychometric attribute, whereas the ISOQOL group conceptualises it as a component of validity.22 Since PRO is an active research area with inconsistent terminologies, it is essential to harmonise these terms to ensure effective communication within the PRO decision team and with regulatory/HTA bodies.

Extensive PRO/PROM-relevant guidelines are identified, representing a challenge for pharmaceutical companies’ clinical research team when selecting PROM(s). After an in-depth assessment, nine guidelines are selected to support the development of the paradigm. Of these, some are specifically for guiding PROs in rare diseases, as many lymphoma subtypes are rare forms, such as mantle cell lymphoma, follicular lymphoma, etc. PROM selection for rare diseases is not much different from that for prevalent diseases but is more challenging due to the small sample size, heterogeneity, lack of natural history and short-term studies.42 43

Lymphoma is a heterogeneous disease characterised by clinical manifestations, histological features, cellular origin, genetic characteristics and prognosis.44 Literature revealed a substantial gap in PRO inclusion between haematological malignancy (44%–54.5%) and solid tumour trials (78.6%–82.5%).17 21 We encourage pharmaceutical companies to prioritise and integrate appropriate PRO(s)/PROM(s) in lymphoma clinical trials.

The strengths of the study are as follows. First, this review paper provides an efficient method for examining the literature, enabling timely decisions on PROM selection for lymphoma clinical trials. Second, it contains a more comprehensive review of existing PROMs, compared with other publications for lymphoma.2 31 32 Moreover, compared with a review article on PROM selection guidelines,25 we have identified more lymphoma-relevant guidelines, such as PRO guidelines for haematology, PROM guidance for rare disease treatments, etc.2 15 41 42 Finally, the paradigm aligns with the widely accepted FDA roadmap to patient-focused outcome measurement in clinical trials and incorporates considerations from regulatory and HTA bodies.2 33 41

On the other hand, this study has some limitations. First, according to Smela et al, ‘A rapid review is a form of knowledge synthesis that accelerates the process of conducting a traditional systematic review through streamlining or omitting a variety of methods to produce evidence in a resource-efficient manner’.45 Stevens et al mentioned distinguishing a rapid review from a systematic review in reporting.46 Our study simplified the review process compared with a systematic review, making adjustments to the full search strategy, study inclusion/exclusion criteria and quality assurance measures. Second, this study may not include all available PROMs for lymphoma trials. It could be that our search missed information and/or there were fewer reported PROMs in publications. Third, this study focuses on guidelines related to PROM selection for clinical trials only, not for clinical practice or healthcare system. Lastly, the selection of PROM(s) for lymphoma is at the PROM category level, not for the lymphoma-specific subtype. Choosing specific PROM(s) for lymphoma subtypes requires further assistance and discussion with KOLs, HEOR experts, clinicians and patients.

Conclusions

This pragmatic paradigm provides a viable and practical approach for selecting appropriate PROM(s) in lymphoma clinical trials. PROMs should conceptually align with the treatment goals. They should also be acceptable to regulators for leveraging treatment efficacy and safety; and to HTA bodies/payers for reimbursement/resource allocation decisions. By following this paradigm, lymphoma clinical trials can generate more patient-focused data to inform clinicians, update treatment guidelines and improve medical education materials, hence contributing to better patient outcomes and advancing lymphoma care.
