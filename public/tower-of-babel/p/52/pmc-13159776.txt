Was that really me? Relationships between memory and sense of self in people with maladaptive daydreaming
Authors: Ori Meidan, Nirit Soffer‐Dudek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesA cohesive sense of self depends on intact autobiographical memory, both of which are impaired in dissociative disorders. Maladaptive daydreaming (MD), a recently proposed dissociative condition, involves excessive immersion in fantastical inner worlds, often at the expense of real‐life engagement, leading to distress and functional impairment. The present study aimed to examine whether maladaptive daydreamers' persistent focus on imagined events and an idealized self, alongside reduced attention to real experiences, may create confusion, impair memory, and ultimately disrupt self‐coherence.Method N = 191 adults (88 with MD, 74% female, M age = 28.92, SD age = 10.86; 103 controls, 75% female, M age = 30.06, SD age = 10.90) completed objective memory tasks (Autobiographical memory test; Reality monitoring test), and questionnaires (Dissociative experiences scale, childhood trauma questionnaire, maladaptive daydreaming scale, self‐concept clarity, and a newly developed confabulation experiences questionnaire).ResultsAs predicted, autobiographical memory in MD was impaired (MD = 6.48 [2.93], Control = 7.46[2.65], t (189) = −2.41, p < .05, d = −.35), more confabulation experiences were reported (MD = 16.63[12.69], Control = 11.94[10.23], t (189) = 2.78, p < .01, d = .40) and self‐concept was less coherent (MD = 29.14[8.60], Control = 35.89[10.18], t (189) = −4.87, p < .001, d = −.71). Memory deficits mediated the MD–self‐concept relationship, but only through commission, rather than omission, errors.ConclusionIn sum, compulsive‐immersive daydreaming is related to forgetting and confabulation, which may disrupt one's sense of identity. These findings support the view of MD as a dissociative condition marked by a fragmented self‐concept.

Practitioner points This study demonstrates that individuals experiencing maladaptive daydreaming show diminished self‐concept and memory, similar to other psychopathological conditions.The partial mediation identified in this study suggests that the pathological processes underlying immersive daydreaming may affect both the sense of self and memory. Future research should aim to establish causal relationships.When treating individuals with maladaptive daydreaming, therapeutic approaches that focus on constructing a coherent life narrative may help strengthen a reality‐based self‐concept.

INTRODUCTION

Maladaptive daydreaming (MD) is a recently proposed clinical condition characterized by compulsive, immersive, and elaborate fantasizing that causes significant distress and functional impairment, including loneliness and shame (Soffer‐Dudek, Aquarone, & Somer, 2025; Somer, 2002). Its point prevalence is estimated at 2.5% (Soffer‐Dudek & Theodor‐Katz, 2022), and interest is growing rapidly in online communities (e.g., Reddit's MD forum with over 134,000 members; retrieved 26 October 2025). Individuals with MD report an addictive urge to engage in vivid inner worlds populated by complex, idealized characters (Somer et al., 2017; Somer, Somer, & Jopp, 2016a) and experience intense emotional reactions to imagined events (Zsila et al., 2018). They often spend many hours immersed in fantasy, neglecting real‐world relationships and goals (Soffer‐Dudek & Somer, 2018). MD episodes may also involve repetitive movements or music, which appear to enhance trance‐like immersion (Somer, Somer, & Jopp, 2016b). Individuals with MD often struggle with emotion regulation and show significant links to alexithymia, yet display heightened affective empathy (Greene et al., 2020; Renzi et al., 2025; West & Somer, 2020). This suggests that they experience strong emotions but have a hard time naming them and down‐regulating them in an adaptive way, which may perhaps fuel their addiction to generating intense emotional experiences through fantasizing. They also show high comorbidity with several psychopathological domains such as attention‐deficit disorders, anxiety disorders, obsessive‐compulsive disorder and narcissism (Renzi & Mariani, 2025; Somer et al., 2017), underscoring the phenomenon's maladaptive nature and its prevalence in clinical samples. Whereas typical daydreaming supports emotional regulation and social understanding (Giménez‐Dasí et al., 2016), in MD it becomes excessively structured, as an addictive, ineffective coping strategy resembling avoidance or rumination (Somer & Herscu, 2017).

MD is strongly associated with dissociation symptoms: affected individuals score high on dissociation scales and often report childhood trauma (Abu‐Rayya et al., 2020; Salomon‐Small et al., 2021; Somer et al., 2021; Somer, Lehrfeld, et al., 2016). Moreover, from the opposite perspective, MD prevalence among those with dissociative identity disorder (DID) and other severe dissociative conditions is estimated at 40%–50% (Ross et al., 2020; Soffer‐Dudek, Somer, et al., 2025). The empirical evidence regarding the associations between dissociation and MD, alongside theoretical accounts of the dissociative properties of MD in itself, has led around 30 trauma and dissociation experts to propose classifying MD as a dissociative disorder (Soffer‐Dudek, Aquarone, & Somer, 2025; Soffer‐Dudek & Somer, 2022). According to Soffer‐Dudek and Somer's (2022) theoretical model, an innate dissociative trait, known as ‘absorption and imaginative involvement’, predisposes individuals to immersive daydreaming, which is reinforced by temporary relief (negative reinforcement) and short‐term pleasure (positive reinforcement). The model places MD along a dissociative continuum of altered agency and control of internal characters, from common, mild phenomena (e.g., ‘not‐me’ thoughts, imaginary companions) to severe forms such as DID following early trauma, positioning MD between those extremities as a moderately severe condition.

A plethora of dissociative symptoms manifests difficulties with self‐processing and interferes with the proper integration of an experienced whole self (Carlson et al., 2009; Putnam, 1994). Research on the self focuses on two aspects: evaluation and knowledge (Kawamoto, 2020). Global self‐esteem refers to evaluation, whereas self‐concept clarity (SCC) refers to knowledge. SCC is the extent to which the contents of one's self‐concept are clearly defined, coherent and stable over time (Campbell et al., 1996). Dissociative disintegration probably involves difficulty in clearly and coherently knowing one's self, and indeed, dissociation and poor SCC are related (Chiu et al., 2017).

The sense of self is closely tied to autobiographical memory (Conway & Pleydell‐Pearce, 2000; Coutelle et al., 2020; Fuentes & Desrocher, 2012; Jiang et al., 2020), as coherent identity depends on integrating life events (Conway, 2005; Klein et al., 2004; Prebble et al., 2013). Awareness of personal past, autonoetic consciousness (Tulving, 1985), is impaired in schizophrenia, DID, and borderline personality disorder (Danion et al., 2007; Huntjens et al., 2014; Jones et al., 1999; Ricarte et al., 2014), all marked by poor SCC (Cicero et al., 2016; Dorahy et al., 2021; Raffard et al., 2010; Roepke et al., 2011; Scala et al., 2018). Post traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) shows similar memory self‐disruptions (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Burriss et al., 2008; Dorahy et al., 2015). As mentioned above, MD is closely associated with dissociative symptoms and is considered by some to represent a dissociative disorder; however, the specific domains of self‐concept and how it relates to autobiographical memory have not yet been directly empirically examined in this population. Theoretically, the extensive engagement with alternative realities and identities is likely to undermine autonoetic consciousness and autobiographical memory, and to lead to increased fragmentation in one's sense of self. Specifically, individuals with MD experience vivid, immersive fantasies and often feel detached or confused upon returning to reality (Soffer‐Dudek & Somer, 2022). Such fantasies frequently feature alternative ‘selves’ (Somer, 2002). Persistent immersion in fantasy may possibly reduce attention to, and encoding of, real‐life experiences, which would be expected to weaken autobiographical and autonoetic memory. Vivid daydreams about real people or events may also lead to confabulation, mistaking imagined for actual experiences. Indeed, some of the individuals suffering from MD attest that they may confuse between visions they imagined and those they perceived (Somer & Otgaar, 2025). These omission (amnestic) and commission (confabulatory) errors (Schacter, 2022) may perhaps undermine a coherent sense of reality and self‐identity. As disruptions in self and memory are hallmark features of the dissociative domain, validating their existence in MD would be significant to establish MD as a dissociative disorder. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to examine the cognitive underpinnings of MD. To reduce bias, we used several objective memory assessments alongside self‐reports. Notably, however, online testing, though necessary to reach the MD population, may have lowered recall accuracy or motivation. Moreover, measuring real‐life memory is difficult, as participants may unintentionally report false memories, which are often unverifiable. The dissociative nature of MD makes this problem especially relevant, as trait dissociation is related to a pattern of commission rather than omission errors (Giesbrecht et al., 2008). Indeed, Bregman‐Hai et al. (2018) found that individuals high in trait dissociation performed mental rotation faster but made more errors in working‐memory tasks, interpreted as a tendency to mentally fill in missing details.

Given the uncertainty around the accuracy of reported memories, we repeated all analyses pertaining to participants' recalled memories while controlling for confabulation tendencies. Since no suitable questionnaire existed to assess confabulation, we developed a self‐report scale for this purpose. Its validation and psychometric properties are detailed elsewhere (Meidan et al., submitted).

The following hypotheses were examined: (H1) When examining continuous relationships on the full range of the sample (including maladaptive daydreamers, normal‐range individuals, and any intermediate cases), MD, dissociation, and confabulation experiences will be positively correlated, while SCC and recall ability will be inversely related to all of the above1; (H2) When focusing on groups of those with suspected MD versus controls, participants in the MD group will have lower SCC and more memory impairments than controls; and (H3) The inverse relationship between MD group affiliation and SCC will be mediated by impairments in autobiographical memory (omission errors/amnesia and commission errors/confabulation).

METHOD

Participants and procedure

The study was approved by the Ben‐Gurion University of the Negev ethics review board, according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were English‐speaking adults recruited from two main sources. Control participants were recruited via the Prolific survey platform, while MD participants were recruited through MD‐specific online forums (e.g., Reddit) and the Consortium for Maladaptive Daydreaming Research (ICMDR) listserv or past participant lists. All respondents gave digital informed consent and completed a 30–40‐min online battery of questionnaires and tasks. MD participants took part voluntarily without compensation, reflecting their high motivation to support MD research. In contrast, control participants received £4.50 (about $5.50) via Prolific, as unpaid recruitment proved ineffective for the control group, necessitating a paid platform to ensure participation.

In the MD recruitment group, 217 originally indicated consent, but 100 dropped out in the beginning after realizing that the study was not anonymous (they had to provide an email for future correspondence for a planned second phase of the study).2 Of the 117 MD respondents who provided an email and commenced questionnaire completion, a total of 20 participants were excluded from the final dataset because they completed less than half of the study items, resulting in N = 97 suspected MD participants. In the control group, a total of 160 individuals initially applied for participation. However, 40 were excluded by Prolific either due to issues related to timeout (taking an excessive amount of time to respond after accessing the online address) or participants who visited the study page, reviewed the consent information, and subsequently closed the study page without providing any specific reasons. Thus, 120 participants were both approved by Prolific and successfully completed the entire study. An attention‐check item was introduced at the midway point. All 120 respondents successfully passed the attention‐check item, and thus none were excluded.

Thus, the final sample for this study consisted of 217 participants recruited from two groups: MD (N = 97) and Control (N = 120). Full demographic statistics are reported in Table 1.

Measures

MD

MD was assessed with the 16‐item Maladaptive Daydreaming Scale (MDS‐16) (Somer et al., 2017; Somer, Lehrfeld, et al., 2016). It is a self‐report questionnaire rated on an 11‐point Likert scale, measuring four aspects of abnormal daydreaming: yearning to daydream; resulting impairments in functioning; kinesthesia (repetitive movements and/or gestures embodying the daydream); and music, which initiates and maintains daydreaming (Soffer‐Dudek et al., 2020). Items, for example, are ‘Some people feel a need to continue a daydream that was interrupted by a real‐world event at a later point. When a real‐world event has interrupted one of your daydreams, how strong was your need or urge to return to that daydream as soon as possible?’ and ‘Some people experience difficulties in controlling or limiting their daydreaming. How difficult has it been for you to keep your daydreaming under control?’ The 16 items are averaged to obtain a total score. The MDS‐16 discriminates well between individuals with and without MD, using a cutoff score of 40 (Soffer‐Dudek, 2025). It demonstrated sound internal consistency and temporal stability (Somer, Lehrfeld, et al., 2016). In the present study, Cronbach's alpha was an excellent .96.

SCC

SCC was assessed using the 12‐item Self‐Concept Clarity Scale (SCCS) (Campbell et al., 1996), a 12‐item self‐report questionnaire that indicates the degree to which an individual's self‐beliefs are clearly defined, internally consistent, and temporally stable. Participants rate each statement (e.g., ‘My beliefs about myself often conflict with one another’ on a 5‐point Likert scale (1—strongly disagree, 5—strongly agree)). Higher values reflect greater clarity. Negatively worded items were reverse‐scored. The SCCS demonstrated excellent internal consistency in previous investigations (Campbell et al., 1996). Cronbach's alpha in the present study was .88.

Memory impairments

Autobiographical memory test—this task was used to identify omission errors (Williams & Broadbent, 1986). In this task, participants were presented with a series of emotional cues (of positive and negative valence) and were asked to generate a specific personal memory in response to each cue. To ensure the retrieval of episodic rather than semantic information, participants were explicitly instructed to describe an event that occurred at a particular time and place and lasted no longer than 24 h (e.g., a specific birthday party rather than ‘birthdays in general’). Procedure and Scoring: Participants were given a time limit of 60 s per cue to type their response. Responses were subsequently coded by the first author, who was blind to the participants' group allocation (MD vs. Control) to prevent bias. The primary outcome measure was the specificity of the retrieved memories. Responses were categorized as ‘Specific’ if they met the criteria of a unique event occurring within a single day. Reality monitoring task—the task was derived from Merckelbach and Wessel (2000) and Cohen (1981). Participants were required to either imagine or perform each of 36 actions (e.g., ‘stretch your arm’, with 18 requests for each condition). Participants were unaware that after completing other measures, they would be tested on those actions. Specifically, for each action, they were asked whether they had acted or only envisioned it, and they rated their confidence level for their response on a 3‐point scale. The score was the number of correct responses, ranging from 0 to 36. It consists of 9 items scored on a 6‐point Likert scale pertaining to one's entire life span, with endpoints of 0 (never happened to me) and 5 (happens to me often, has occurred over ten times). Typical items are ‘I have had a memory that I believed to be true but turned out to be false,’ and ‘My recollection of a recent social or family event was very different from that of others who were present at the scene.’ Cronbach's alpha was an appropriate .87, supporting this novel scale's reliability.

Dissociation

Dissociative experiences were assessed by the widely used DES‐II (Carlson & Putnam, 1993), which includes 28 items. Items, for example, are ‘Some people find that they have no memory for some important events in their lives (for example, a wedding or graduation)’ and ‘Some people sometimes find that they are approached by people that they do not know, who call them by another name or insist that they have met them before’. Respondents then rate what percentage of the time they experience each of these phenomena on an 11‐point scale (0%, 10%, 20%, etc.). The DES has good test–retest and internal reliability and well‐established construct and criterion validity (Carlson & Putnam, 1993). Cronbach's alpha was .95.

Childhood trauma

The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ; Bernstein & Fink, 1998) is a retrospective 28‐item self‐report questionnaire. Respondents are asked to indicate on a 5‐point Likert scale how often they experienced emotional, physical or sexual abuse and emotional or physical neglect during their childhood (1 = never, 5 = very often). Items, for example, are ‘When I was growing up, I was punished with a belt, a board, a cord (or some other hard object)’, and ‘When I was growing up, someone tried to touch me in a sexual way or tried to make me touch them’. It demonstrated excellent test–retest reliability as well as convergence with structured trauma interviews (Bernstein et al., 1994; Fink et al., 1995). In the present study, Cronbach's alpha was .86.

Data analysis

Before conducting analyses, missing data patterns were estimated using the missing values analysis in SPSS. Missingness on all items was between 0 and 1.4%. This low percentage of missingness suggests that nonresponse in the data can be ignored as it is unlikely to significantly influence any results (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2019). Next, we tested the variables' skewness patterns to identify possible deviation from a normal distribution. There were no deviations from normality except for the score distribution on the reality monitoring task, which exhibited somewhat negative skewness (−1.89), suggesting a ceiling effect, as most people were able to recognize most of their responses well. Thus, when analysing correlations and group differences with the reality monitoring task scores, we utilized the Spearman correlation coefficient and the Mann–Whitney U test, respectively.

The examination of continuous associations between MD severity, memory performance, and self‐concept (Aim 1) was conducted on the entire sample, utilizing the full range of variance available to maximize statistical power and avoid range truncation. However, for comparing specific group differences in memory and self‐concept (Aim 2) and testing the mediating role of memory errors (Aim 3), a subsample was utilized, excluding n = 17 participants from the control group who scored above the MDS cutoff (who thus may have suspected MD) and n = 9 participants from the MD group who did not meet the cutoff for suspected MD. This process resulted in two clinically distinct groups to facilitate a more focused comparison between them. The group variable was coded such that MD is ‘1’ (n = 88) and control is ‘0’ (n = 103). We acknowledge that removing participants who failed to meet the cutoff for their assigned group resulted in a more highly selected sample; however, this rigorous filtering was deemed necessary. As the primary aim was to examine specific theoretical mechanisms underlying a proposed disorder, it was imperative to minimize diagnostic ambiguity that could obscure genuine group differences. Furthermore, given that MD is not an extremely rare phenomenon in the general population, there is a substantial risk of ‘contamination’ within the control group by undiagnosed individuals. By excluding control participants who scored above the clinical threshold and self‐identified people with MD who scored below it, we ensured that the analysis compared a distinct clinical entity against a strictly non‐pathological baseline, thereby allowing for a precise evaluation of MD as a unique population.

We used Pearson correlation coefficients to analyse Hypothesis 1 (continuous associations) and t‐tests for independent samples for Hypothesis 2 (group differences). For testing Hypothesis 3 (mediation), we ran a bootstrapped mediation regression model with 5000 resamples (Hayes, 2009). The model included the dichotomous group affiliation variable (0 = Control, 1 = MD) as the independent variable, memory impairment as the mediator, and SCC as the dependent variable. This design was specifically selected to determine whether the diagnostic classification of MD versus control predicts lower SCC via the indirect path of memory errors.

RESULTS

Table 2 presents Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients for the study variables. As expected (H1), MD exhibited a significant inverse correlation with SCC (r = −.41, p < 0.001) and positive correlations with dissociation and childhood trauma. MD was also associated in the expected direction with two of the three memory impairment measures. Specifically, higher MD scores were associated with a higher reported tendency to confabulate and with less specific memories on the memory specificity task. However, the latter association was unstable as it did not remain statistically significant when examining confidence intervals. Interestingly, when examining 95% confidence‐interval bounds rather than the p‐value, the association that emerged between MD and the third memory measure, reality monitoring, was opposite of the hypothesized direction, such that higher MD scores were associated with better reality monitoring.

Beyond its expected inverse correlation with MD, the specific memories task yielded additional, unanticipated results. A higher number of specific memories was inversely related to SCC (r = −0.20, p < 0.01), indicating that participants recalling more specific events had lower SCC. There was also a non‐significant trend linking more specific memories with greater confabulation, contrary to expectations that different forms of poor memory would co‐occur positively. Following these results, we suspected that participants prone to confabulation could have more readily fabricated detailed but inaccurate memories when pressed for quick responses. To explore this possibility, we ran a multiple regression model controlling for confabulation. As anticipated, in that context the effect of specific memories on SCC became non‐significant (b = −.37 [−.84, .10], se = .23, t = −1.55, β = −.10, p = .12, as opposed to the effect in a simple regression when not controlling for confabulation: b = −.60 [−1.11, −.10], se = .25, t = −2.30, β = −.17, p < .05).

Next, we utilized a series of t‐tests and Mann–Whitney U tests to examine H2 (group differences).

As anticipated and in accordance with the continuous results, participants in the ‘purified’ MD group (n = 88) exhibited significantly lower SCC compared to the ‘purified’ control group (n = 103) (M MD = 29.14, SD = 8.60, M control = 35.89, SD = 10.18, t (189) = −4.87, p < .001, d = −.71). Furthermore, as hypothesized, they demonstrated a significantly lower number of specific memories than the control group (M MD = 6.48, SD = 2.93, M control = 7.46, SD = 2.65, t (189) = −2.41, p < .05, d = −.35), and reported more confabulation experiences (M MD = 16.63, SD = 12.69, M control = 11.94, SD = 10.23, t (189) = 2.78, p < .01, d = .40). Unlike the correlational analyses where reality monitoring had an effect in the unexpected direction, in the group comparison there was no significant difference between the groups in the reality monitoring score (M MD = 30.76, SD = 5.28, M control = 30.36, SD = 5.66, Z = −.09, r = .49). When examining the types of errors separately (commission and omission), there was no significant difference (commission: M MD = 2.21, SD = 2.69, M control = 2.54, SD = 3.06, t (189) = −.78, p = .21, d = −.11; omission: M MD = 2.57, SD = 2.64, M control = 3.05, SD = 3.13, t (189) = −1.12, p = .13, d = −.16). Interestingly, MD participants were significantly more confident in their reality monitoring answers than controls (M MD = 2.65, SD = .32, M control = 2.48, SD = .47, t (189) = 2.87, p < .01, d = .40).

To explore the extent to which H2 effects may stem from false memories, we also considered MD group affiliation as a dichotomous predictor in linear regression models controlling for confabulation. First, we ran simple regression models assessing the extent to which group affiliation predicts SCC and memory impairments (notably, these models are somewhat overlapping with the group differences analyses, but were necessary as a preliminary step before adding confabulation). Overall, MD significantly predicted SCC, confabulation, and specific memories (b = −6.74 [−9.47, −4.01], se = 1.38, t = −4.80, β = −.33, p < .001; b = 4.69 [1.42, 7.96], se = 1.66, t = 2.82, β = .20, p < .01; and b = −.97 [−1.77, −.17], se = .40, t = −2.41, β = −.17, p < .05, respectively). Next, confabulation was added to the regression model predicting specific memories to assess which effects are explained by confabulated memories. This proved to be important, as it generated a suppression effect. Specifically, MD seemed to predict specific memory impairment more strongly with the inclusion of this additional predictor in the regression model (b = −1.21 [−2.01, −.41], se = .40, t = −3.00, β = −.21, p < .01). Presumably, the presence of confabulated or false specific memories in the MD group created error variance which reduced the strength of the true relationship with recall difficulty.

Finally, to examine mediation (H3), we used PROCESS (Model 4) in SPSS (Hayes, 2009), computing the indirect effect for 5000 bootstrapped samples. It is important to note that this method does not produce a p‐value for the indirect effect as it relies on the Bootstrapping technique. Therefore, we present p‐values only for the total and direct effect. The models included MD group affiliation as the independent variable, memory impairment as the mediator, and SCC as the dependent variable. We used only the two memory impairment measures that had the expected relationships with MD. For a visual representation of these mediation models, see Figures 1a and 1b.

In the first model, confabulation experiences were used as the mediating memory impairment measure. The total effect of MD on SCC was statistically significant (b = −6.74, [−9.47, −4.01], se = 1.38, t = −4.87, β partial standardized = −.67, p < .001, R 2 = .11, F (1,189) = 23.78).3 As expected, the indirect effect of the MD group on SCC through confabulation was also statistically significant (β partial standardized = −.14, b = −1.45, se = .57, [−2.68, −0.41]), suggesting that participants with MD had more confabulation and that, in turn, was associated with reduced SCC. In the mediated model, the direct effect of MD on SCC remained statistically significant (b = −5.28, [−7.88, −2.69], se = 1.31, t = −4.02, β partial standardized = −.52, p < .001, R 2 = .23, F (2, 188) = 28.86), indicating that the mediation was only partial.

Next, we used the number of specific memories recalled as the mediator. As the reader may recall, the relationship of specific memories with MD was in the expected direction, but with SCC it was, surprisingly, opposite to that hypothesized. Still, we explored the mediation model as planned in advance. In accordance with the previous unexpected finding, H3 also proved to behave in an opposite manner to what was originally hypothesized. Specifically, the pattern of the indirect effect was statistically significant but positive, which was opposite to the expected direction (b = .81, se = .43, [.11, 1.77]).4 The direct relationship between MD group affiliation and SCC was negative as expected beforehand, and after adding the hypothesized mediator (reality monitoring recall), the negative effect became stronger, indicating a suppression effect once again (b = −7.56, [−10.25, −4.87], se = 1.36, t = −5.54, β = −.70, p < .001, R 2 = .16. F (2, 188) = 18.61). However, when also controlling for confabulation, the pattern of the indirect effect remained in the same direction and statistically significant (b = .72, se = .40, [.10, 1.63]), whereas the suppression effect dissipated (the direct effect was still negative but not as strong as before).

DISCUSSION

The goal of the present study was to assess the role of autobiographical memory in SCC in the context of MD. Utilizing not only online questionnaires but also objective memory tasks administered online, for the first time in MD research, enhanced the objectivity and validity of our exploration. We hypothesized that people in the MD group would show reduced SCC and increased memory impairments, and that these impairments would mediate the association between SCC and MD.

As anticipated (H1), dissociation, confabulation and MD were strongly correlated, further supporting the validity of the new confabulation questionnaire (Meidan et al., submitted). The link between confabulation and dissociation supports prior findings that such experiences typify dissociative phenomena (Giesbrecht et al., 2008; Merckelbach et al., 2000). Although confabulation has been debated within the ‘memory wars’ (Crews, 1995), trauma‐related links appear complex. Childhood trauma may encourage imaginative coping that fosters confabulation; indeed, highly imaginative individuals sometimes develop MD after early trauma (Somer et al., 2021; Somer & Herscu, 2017). Integrating confabulation research into trauma and dissociation frameworks remains essential, as confabulatory experiences reflect lived reality, central to understanding dissociation.

These findings offer insight into the potential mechanisms underlying memory errors, specifically commission errors such as confabulation, in the context of MD as a dissociative entity. Laboratory studies have shown that commission errors are the type of memory failure that is more characteristic of dissociation than omission errors (Giesbrecht et al., 2008). MD is considered to represent a dissociative disorder (Soffer‐Dudek, Somer, et al., 2025), and some people with MD report that they may confuse imagined with real events (Somer & Otgaar, 2025), but this is the first study to objectively examine memory functioning in this context. During intense MD episodes, attention to the external world is reduced, potentially increasing the risk of recalling inaccurate or false information, explaining the higher confabulation found in the MD group (H2). Indeed, dissociation is linked to inward‐focused attention (Soffer‐Dudek, 2023), while outward‐focused grounding techniques help reduce detachment (Neziroglu & Donnelly, 2010).

Also, as anticipated, MD was significantly related to poor SCC both in the continuous sample (H1) and through group comparisons (H2). Reduced SCC implies a diminished understanding of one's own traits, motivations, emotions towards others, and, essentially, a weakened grasp of one's identity. This lack of self‐comprehension may engender stress, and it comes as no surprise that lower SCC is a recurring feature across various psychopathological conditions (Cicero, 2017; Lee‐Flynn et al., 2011). This study marks the first SCC assessment in individuals with MD. Given that diminished SCC is linked to stress and psychopathology, these findings lend further support to the notion that MD is indeed a clinical or maladaptive condition.

The reduction in SCC among individuals with MD may stem from their prolonged immersion in daydreaming. In these fantasies, they often adopt an idealized self, distinct from their real‐life identity, necessitating recurring shifting between conflicting self‐concepts, which may impair SCC. MD's links to dissociation, confabulation and low SCC align it with dissociative conditions marked by disruptions in identity, memory, and perception, supporting the view of MD as a dissociative disorder (Soffer‐Dudek & Somer, 2022; Soffer‐Dudek, Somer, et al., 2025). The observed correlations suggest that MD shares core features with established dissociative disorders, including detachment from reality and a fragmented sense of self.

Another possible reason for the lower SCC in the MD group is memory impairment. As noted earlier, SCC is contingent upon memory, particularly the ability to recall formative events that shape self‐understanding. This study found that the MD group had more difficulty recalling specific autobiographical memories (H2) and reported more confabulation. Reduced access to autobiographical memories reflects a dissociative feature of MD, similar to dissociative amnesia. Ongoing fantasy immersion and lapses in executive control may make memories harder to retrieve later. Frequent confabulation further suggests a weaker grip on reality and more memory inaccuracies. Overall, the findings linking MD to both memory deficits and reduced SCC support its classification within the dissociative spectrum. However, it is important to note that while the differences between the MD and control groups were statistically significant across the memory measures, the effect sizes were generally small to moderate. This warrants a cautious interpretation of the findings: rather than indicating a profound or structural collapse of autobiographical memory systems as might be seen in severe neurological or psychotic disorders, the results suggest that MD is associated with a subtler cognitive interference. Future research is needed to determine whether these modest effects are stable features of the disorder or if they fluctuate with the severity of current daydreaming activity.

No significant group differences were found in reality monitoring for omission or commission errors. Although MD participants reported higher confidence, this did not reflect greater accuracy, consistent with previous evidence of dissociation between memory confidence and performance (Hermans et al., 2003). These findings suggest that, despite immersive fantasy, individuals with MD generally distinguish imagination from reality (Somer, 2002). Theoretically, the finding of higher confidence may be driven by the phenomenological nature of MD: the high absorption and intense visual imagery characteristic of MD may create a sense of ‘subjective vividness’. In memory research, vividness is often used as a heuristic for accuracy; thus, MD individuals might mistakenly interpret the richness of their mental experience as evidence of correctness. This illusion of competence indicates that MD may affect not only what is remembered but also how memory is monitored and evaluated. This observation opens a promising avenue for future research, suggesting that investigations into MD should move beyond performance deficits to explore alterations in metacognitive monitoring and reality testing. However, the lack of actual performance differences could alternatively be explained by a confound. Specifically, the normal performance as well as the unexpected positive correlation emerging between MD and reality monitoring when examining the association with confidence intervals may have stemmed from unequal task compliance between the groups; intrinsically motivated MD participants may have followed instructions more carefully than paid controls, potentially masking true group differences and even creating a weak opposite effect when focusing on correlations.

Finally, we found only partial support for H3, proposing that memory impairments mediate the MD–SCC relationship. Using confabulation (self‐reported commission errors) as the mediator, MD predicted higher confabulation, which in turn predicted lower SCC, indicating significant partial mediation. However, when specific memory recall (objectively assessed omission errors) was used, an unexpected negative association emerged where more memories recalled related to lower SCC, contradicting prior findings of a positive relationship (Coutelle et al., 2020; Dunkel & Lavoie, 2005; Fuentes & Desrocher, 2012). One possible explanation is that low SCC prompts people to recall autobiographical memories to resolve self‐confusion (Jiang et al., 2020). However, since SCC was measured after memory tasks and as a trait, this is unlikely. Alternatively, recalling many specific memories may make it harder to form a coherent self, as conflicting behaviours become harder to reconcile, though this is speculative and contrasts with past findings.

The discrepancy may stem from our unique sample, which included many MD participants. They may recall real behaviours accurately, yet contrast them with fantasized identities, producing low SCC despite intact memory (Chefetz et al., 2023). Memory's effect on SCC might also vary by error type, with commission errors more disruptive than omissions. Another explanation involves discrepancies between objective and subjective measures (Serra‐Blasco et al., 2019; Tsuchiyama et al., 2003). The self‐report confabulation scale showed expected results, whereas objective tasks—omission, commission and reality monitoring—did not. Self‐report tools may capture experiential aspects of memory that objective tasks miss, each reflecting different facets or stages of the same process.

A final methodological explanation for the observed pattern is that participants, particularly those with MD, may have recalled specific memories in a distorted or confabulated manner. Indeed, after controlling for confabulation, the unexpected inverse relationship between SCC and the number of specific memories was no longer statistically significant. Thus, the unusual findings may stem from difficulty distinguishing true memories from false ones in the memory task. A previous study (Huntjens et al., 2014) found that individuals with DID recalled fewer specific memories compared to healthy controls, but that study did not examine confabulation. Confabulation might differ across dissociative disorders, potentially playing a more central role in MD.

Future studies employing autobiographical memory tasks may benefit from including our confabulation scale (Meidan et al., submitted) as a control, to the extent that it may produce statistical suppression by removing error variance associated with false memories and thus increasing the size of the effects of interest, as occurred in this study. However, further studies are needed to examine if this pattern will indeed recur.

This study offers important insights into the specific challenges faced by individuals with MD, providing direction for tailored treatment approaches. It underscores the potential impact of prolonged daydreaming on memory and SCC, suggesting that interventions should focus on integrating past experiences into a coherent life narrative to generate a stable self‐concept. Methods like mindfulness and self‐monitoring have shown promise in reducing MD symptoms (Herscu et al., 2023). These practices, and perhaps additional ones that remain unexplored in the field of MD, such as a narrative approach, may support a more unified understanding of one's environment and internal experiences, fostering self‐knowledge and a clearer sense of identity. Mindfulness, in particular, involves non‐judgmental awareness of thoughts, emotions, and sensations, and has been associated with improved SCC, which in turn supports psychological well‐being (Hanley & Garland, 2017). Given its known effects on enhancing cognitive functioning and reducing mind‐wandering (Mrazek et al., 2013; Rahl et al., 2017), mindfulness may benefit individuals with MD partly by strengthening SCC.

This study's cross‐sectional design limits causal conclusions. Although daydreaming may impair memory and lower SCC, the reverse effect of low SCC prompting daydreaming of multiple selves is also plausible. Future work should test participants before and after daydreaming sessions to clarify directionality. The online format may have reduced task validity without experimenter supervision, and control participants from Prolific differed in incentives and possibly motivation. Given that paid survey users often show elevated psychopathology (Ophir et al., 2020), this group may not represent a truly healthy sample. Nonetheless, these limitations are balanced by notable strengths: a unique semi‐clinical MD sample, combined objective and self‐report methods, and comprehensive assessment of omission and commission errors. Overall, findings partly support the hypothesis that excessive daydreaming disrupts SCC through memory impairment.

A notable limitation of the current study concerns the use of the CEQ. While this instrument served as a central variable in our mediation analysis, its formal psychometric validation has not yet been published. Utilizing a measure that is still under development constitutes a methodological vulnerability of the present study. The CEQ was developed when planning this study due to the absence of established self‐report tools suitable for capturing this specific phenomenology in the context of MD; however, future research is necessary to further validate the scale's psychometric properties and therefore confirm the robustness of the relationships observed in this study. Finally, we must acknowledge the substantial dropout rate (~50%) observed when participants were asked to provide email addresses. This attrition introduces potential selection bias: individuals who chose to continue may possess distinct traits, such as lower social anxiety or reduced shame regarding their condition, compared to those who withdrew. Consequently, our sample may underrepresent the more avoidant or secretive segments of the MD population, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings.

Future research should replicate these findings, clarifying the relationship between SCC and memory, particularly concerning omission errors. Higher‐resolution methodologies, such as experience‐sampling, may prove beneficial in exploring how immersive daydreaming episodes influence short‐term memory. While future longitudinal research is required to definitively establish causal pathways, these findings hold immediate relevance for clinical practice. They suggest that treatment for MD should extend beyond symptom management to address the structural integrity of the self. Specifically, therapeutic approaches that focus on constructing a coherent, integrated life narrative may prove vital in strengthening a reality‐based self‐concept, thereby reducing the psychological reliance on the alternative identities found in fantasy.
