Developing items for inclusion in a new pain-specific patient-reported outcome measure for pelvic floor surgery: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Sheymonti S Hoque, Susannah Ahern, Helen E O’Connell, Rasa Ruseckaite
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen undergoing pelvic floor procedures (PFPs) to treat their pelvic floor disorder (PFD) may experience pain, affecting their health-related quality of life. Current patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) assess pain but are not specific to PFD post-surgery. This study was part of developing a new pain-specific PROM for women following PFP, aimed to (1) refine an existing conceptual framework to better understand post- post-PFP in women, (2) draft potential items, and (3) develop a consensus set of items for the new measure to ensure content validity.MethodsA mixed-methods approach was used to address the aim in three phases: (1) refining an existing conceptual framework for post-PFP pain based on feedback from semi-structured interviews with 16 women, (2) drafting potential items based on the revised framework, and (3) conducting an online two-round Delphi survey with 17 experts ranked to rank, review items, then produce a consensus set.ResultsThe interviews conducted identified key attributes of pain that need to be addressed in the new pain instrument for PFP. From the interviews, 114 items were drafted under eight framework domains, and then presented to a panel in the Delphi survey. After two Delphi rounds, 35 items were finalised, with 11 equally merged or deleted, resulting in a final set of 35 items.ConclusionThis is the first study to develop a set of items for the new pain measure based on the experiences and expertise of women and clinicians. Inclusion of the items can offer valuable insight into pain after PFP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-025-02429-0.Keywords: Pain, Patient-reported outcome measures, Pelvic floor disorders, Pelvic floor procedure, Item generation, Health-related quality of life

Introduction

Pelvic floor procedures (PFPs) to treat pelvic floor disorders (PFDs) like stress urinary incontinence (SUI) and pelvic organ prolapse (POP) often utilise pelvic mesh to support the pelvic floor and alleviate symptoms in women [1]. PFDs are commonly associated with risk factors including age, obesity, parity, and childbirth-related injury [2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, the global age-standardised incidence rate for POP in 2019 was 316.19 per 100,000 population [3]. In Australia, approximately one in two women are affected by PFDs, and as of September 2023, 436 women had undergone a PFP, 78% of which were SUI-related procedures [3].

While pelvic floor surgery offer benefits, some women report complications, most notably pain [4, 5]. Pain is associated with reduced health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [5, 6]. Reported pain rates vary. One study found up to 30% of patients experienced pain following mesh surgery [6]while another reported a 4.3% incidence of de novo pain [7]. A recent systematic review estimated chronic pain incidence at 6.7% post-POP procedures and 0.6% post-SUI procedures involving mesh [8].

Etiology of pain is multifactorial and may involve mesh erosion, exposure, inflammation, or chronic tissue irritation [9]. Importantly, pain may not emerge until years after mesh insertion, and patients may not always associate it with prior surgery [10]. Addressing pain associated with PFP is, therefore a critical aspect of patient care and long-term management [11].

Pain can be assessed using patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), which collect health information directly from patients [12]. PROMs are particularly relevant for evaluating pain as an HRQoL outcome among women after pelvic floor surgery [13]. Existing PROMs such as the McGill Pain Questionnaire, Visual Analogue Scale, and Pain Catastrophizing Scale, are limited in this context [13, 14]. They do not specifically capture pain following PFPs or address its multidimensional aspects, including precise location and impact on daily functioning [14]. Currently, no PROM exists that is specific to measuring pain related to PFP in women [14]. To address this gap, a scoping review was conducted to establish the need for a new instrument [14]. A conceptual framework, developed previously based on interviews with women experiencing pain following mesh surgery and their treating clinicians, provided a foundational basis for this study [9]. While this framework highlighted key aspects of PFD pain post-surgery, there was a scope for further refinement and expansion to incorporate new emerging topics on pain important for women [9]. Accordingly, this study aimed to (1) refine an existing conceptual framework to identify what matters most to women with PFDs regarding post-surgery pain, (2) draft potential items for a new PROM, and (3) develop a consensus set of items to ensure content validity, meaning the instrument reflects the experiences and priorities of patients. The new PROM is intended to fill gaps in existing instruments as a patient-centred follow-up questionnaire [8, 13, 14].

Methods

This study was part of the process of developing a new pain measure. The study utilised a mixed-methods design to generate items for the new instrument while defining content validity, through three phases based on the aims.

Phase 1: refining a Pre-Developed conceptual framework

Design and setting

Qualitative phenomenological methods via semi-structured telephone/Zoom interviews were used to understand women’s perceptions of their pain about PFDs and PFP [15].

Study participants and recruitment

Study participants included adult women (≥ 18 years of age) residing in Australia and New Zealand with a PFD, who had pain following a procedure for SUI or POP. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling through advertisements on social media, email invitations and the Australasian Pelvic Floor Procedure Registry website. Interested participants were sent a study explanatory statement and completed a short screening questionnaire (Table 1) to confirm their eligibility. After the study, participants were offered a $20 gift voucher for their time and contribution.

Data collection and analysis

Data was collected through interviews based on a topic guide covering topics on pain, including sensations, location, constancy, triggers, comorbidities and complications, pain relief and management (deriving from the previous interview themes and the framework) and other pain-related questions (Supplementary Material 1). Interviews were conducted between November and December 2022 until data saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new themes emerged; saturation was reached after the sixteenth interview [16]. The interviews ranged between 14 and 43 min, with an average of 25 min. Interview data was transcribed and thematically analysed using NVivo into domains and sub-domains according to the original conceptual framework [9]. Codes were cross-checked, and any differences were resolved by the research team. Following the analysis, the existing conceptual framework was amended to include the new information from patients [9].

Phase 2: drafting items under conceptual framework domains

Draft items for a new pain-specific PROM were produced under amended conceptual framework themes. These items were informed by a previously conducted scoping review of the literature and findings from semi-structured interviews with women [9, 14].

Phase 3: using the Delphi process to reach consensus on items

Design and setting

The Delphi method by RAND Corporation was utilised in the form of two-round online consensus surveys (conducted online from May to September 2023, using Qualtrics [17] to obtain expert feedback and determine items suitable for inclusion in the new pain instrument [17, 18]. Two rounds of Delphi were conducted until the (standard) target to reach consensus of at least 70% was reached, and there was stability in the responses (participant responses were consistent and did not change significantly) [18].

Selection and recruitment of the Delphi panel

The Delphi panel comprised Australian and international participants purposively selected for their clinical, consumer, or lived experience with PFDs and PFP. An international panel was convened to ensure diversity of perspectives, enhance cross-cultural validity, and increase the generalisability of the consensus. This included: (1) women from Phase 1 interviews, who contributed to lived experience perspectives; (2) consumer advocates involved in patient-centred care; and (3) clinicians such as urologists, urogynecologists, and pain specialists. Clinicians were included based on a minimum of five years of relevant practice, academic contributions, or leadership in the field. Panel members were identified via email using purposive sampling, with input from the clinical co-author (HEO). Sample size of the Delphi panel was determined based on the RAND methodology, which describes that a panel should consist of seven to 15 multidisciplinary experts with experience and expertise relevant to the research field [18].

Participants were emailed study explanatory statement for further information. A unique ID code and link to the survey in each round were generated for participants. Completing the survey online indicated implied consent. Responders were sent two reminders to complete the surveys. Panel members were asked to rank the importance of each item on a 9-point Likert scale (1 = not important, 9 = very important) and provide any comments or feedback for the items or propose new items.

Data analysis

Survey results were analysed using Microsoft Excel, where the median importance (MI) (range: 1–9) and disagreement index (DI) were calculated [18]. For MI, a median score of 1–3 indicated the item was not important, a median score of 4–6 indicated the item was uncertain, and a median score of 7–9 indicated the item was important [18, 19]. According to the RAND methodology, the DI is used to assess the level of consensus among the respondents on the topics or questions (i.e. it helps to quantify how much respondents’ opinions diverge from each other) [18]. The DI was calculated based on the variability of the responses, in which a DI of 0 represented complete agreement, whilst a DI ≥ 1 indicated significant disagreement between panel members. Cases with DI > 1 showed extreme variation in ratings [18]. Items with a MI of ≥ 7 and a DI of < 1 progressed to the final list of candidate items in the pain instrument [18, 20]. However, items rated with a DI ≥ 1 and MI ≤ 7 were removed [18, 20]. Finally, items identified as very important (MI ≥ 7 and DI < 1) were reviewed by the research team for any duplicates or to merge items [18].

Results

Phase 1: refining a pre-developed conceptual framework

Demographic characteristics

A total of 16 women (58.1 ± 12.8 years) participated in the interviews, most of whom were from Australia (n = 13, 81.3%) (Table 2). Among the interviewees, seven (43.8%) had surgery for POP, three (18.8%) for SUI, and six (37.5%) underwent surgery for both POP and SUI. Fourteen (87.5%) women reported having pelvic mesh implanted. One participant had two meshes inserted (into bladder and bowel). All women reported experiencing pain following pelvic floor surgery.

Interview findings

The interviews provided a deeper understanding of the pain experienced after PFP with mesh by women themselves. Women reported experiencing unbearable pain post-surgery, until they “wished [they] were dead” (P5). They described several different pain sensations, using analogies and metaphors, for example, “it was like being stabbed with shards of glass” (P7). Pain occurred in multiple body regions, most commonly the groin, leg, thigh, toe, back, and spine. Most participants reported intense and frequent pain, significantly worse than before mesh surgery. The interviews revealed a strong link between pain interference and reduced quality of life. More detailed accounts and quotes from women can be found in Supplementary Material 2.

Amended conceptual framework

Based on the findings from the interviews, we refined the previous conceptual framework to include eight domains and 44 sub-domains (Fig. 1). An amended conceptual framework included the following domains: (1) sensation of pain, (2) region pain, (3) continuity of pain, (4) pain triggers, (5) pain intensity, (6) pain interference, (7) pain relief and management, and (8) comorbidities and complications. “Pain intensity” and “pain interferences” were new additions. Additional new sub-domains were included. In the “pain triggers” domain, there are four sub-domains. The domains of “pain relief and management” and “comorbidities and complications” each have one new sub-domain.

Phase 2: drafting items under conceptual framework domains

Following thematic analysis and coding of the qualitative interviews, a list of potential items was produced. One hundred and fourteen items were developed to form the first draft of the new pain-specific PROM (Supplementary Material 3).

Phase 3: using the Delphi process to reach consensus on items

Panel characteristics

A total of 17 experts participated in the survey in 2023 (Table 3). Eleven out of 17 participants completed both rounds of the survey. Three participants completed Round 1 but not Round 2. Of these, one withdrew after Round 1, while the other two agreed to participate in Round 2 and did not complete the survey. Moreover, one participant started the survey in Round 1 but did not finish it, hence did not participate in Round 2. Two new participants joined the study and completed Round 2, reflecting their interest in the study and to account for the two participants from Round 1 who did not continue. The majority of the experts were female (n = 13, 76.5%), from Australia (n = 10, 58.8%), and were urologists (n = 8, 47.1%). Two (11.8%) patients and two consumer patient advocates were also invited. The patients participating in the survey also took part in the qualitative interviews in Phase 1.

Delphi rounds

The list of 114 items (from Phase 2) was presented to the Delphi panel. In Round 1, of the 114 items, 41 (36.0%) were rated very important (MI ≥ 7, DI < 1), 46 (40.4%) were rated as not important (DI ≥ 1 and MI ≤ 7), therefore excluded, while the remaining 27 (23.7%) with uncertain consensus were carried forward to Round 2 (Fig. 2; Table 4). In Round 2, 16 out of these 27 items (59.3%) were deemed very important (MI ≥ 7, DI < 1), five (18.5%) were excluded for being rated as not important, and six (22.2%) remained uncertain (DI ≥ 1) (Fig. 2; Table 4).

The six uncertain items had MI values ≥ 7, demonstrating perceived importance, but DI values ≥ 1 reflected substantial disagreement overall among panel members (Supplementary Material 4). Instead of progressing to a third Delphi round, the research team reviewed those items with the full item pool. As similar items with high importance (MI ≥ 7) and acceptable consensus (DI < 1) were already proposed for inclusion, the unresolved items were considered redundant and removed. This approach balanced methodological rigour with panel burden and ensured conceptual coverage [18].

After two rounds of the survey, 57 items (Round 1: 41, Round 2: 16) were deemed very important and proposed for inclusion in the new pain-specific PROM (Fig. 2, Supplementary Material 5). These research team revised the 57 items for clarity, resulting in the deletion of 11 items and merging of another 11, yielding a final set of 35 items proposed for the new measure.

Items

A list of the final 35 potential items is shown in Table 4. The domain of “sensation of pain” consisted of three items identifying the type of pain sensations felt, the severity of those sensations, and how bothersome the pain was. For this domain, the Delphi panel members recommended separating the different sensations and having a binary yes/no option for each pain descriptor in one question. The second domain (“region of pain”) contained items focusing on location(s) in the body where pain is present and whether the pain moved around the body. The panel suggested including a body map to pinpoint the region of pain and listing precise locations of pain, for example, having ‘lower abdomen’ instead of saying ‘abdomen’; however, this was not included in the item list.

The domain of “continuity of pain” included items asking about the occurrence of pain and the frequency of pain episodes. Panellists recommended that the items ask about the times during the day when the pain is experienced. The domain of “pain triggers” consisted of four items on the frequency of pain pre- and post-pelvic floor surgery, specific pain triggers, and whether the triggers are avoided.

The domain of pain intensity included three items that assessed how intense the pain felt over the past four weeks and whether there were any pain-free days (Table 4). The term ‘intense’ pain comes from patient interviews, where participants used it to describe pain that was highly disruptive or distressing in response to specific interview questions. This provided a clearer context to understand the varying pain levels experienced and to capture fluctuations in pain severity. Among the three items, item 5.1 was a newly added item based on the panel feedback (Table 4). For this domain, panellists wanted clarity on whether the items reflected pain overall or separate pain episodes. They suggested questions on the worst and least painful experiences in the last four weeks.

The sixth domain of “pain interference” comprised five items asking to indicate which of the list activities or tasks were difficulty to do because of pain, and how often did pain affect mood, sleep, ability to work, and relationships. There were seven items under the domain of “pain relief and management”, covering the methods implemented to relieve and manage pain, specifying any medications taken or creams used, and the effectiveness of natural methods, psychological therapy and physiotherapy in managing pain.

The last domain of “comorbidities and complications” contained nine items. Two of the nine items regarding mesh erosion (item 8.4) and the relationship between pain and mental well-being in the past four weeks (item 8.9) were rated highly by the panellists (Table 4 and Supplementary Material 5). The remaining items ask whether any complications, infection or reaction, other medical problems, or pain syndrome have developed post pelvic floor surgery. The panel for this domain recommended writing items in lay language to ensure clarity.

Discussion

This was the first study to develop a set of items for a new pain-specific PROM for women with PFDs following pelvic floor surgery. This study documents the phases involved in the development of the items under the conceptual framework designed based on the feedback obtained from patient interviews and surveys with consumer representatives and clinicians, as well as the existing literature. The qualitative interviews helped to gain deeper insight into the pain experienced by women post-pelvic mesh surgery and refine a pre-developed conceptual framework. The amended framework has now adequately captured all important attributes of pain experienced by women post-PFP. The framework also reveals how the varied experiences of pain for women following PFP. The interviews revealed profound impacts of post-PFP pain on women’s quality of life. Pain often worsened after mesh surgery, with decline in physical and psychosocial wellbeing. Women described disruption to daily tasks, work, travel, physical activity, and socialising, highlighting the underexplored domain of pain interference [9].

From the initial 114 items, Delphi consensus narrowed the list to 57, which were refined to 35 for inclusion in the pain instrument. Input from experts (including women) was crucial in designing the PROM that meaningfully captures relevant pain experiences and data after PFP [19–21]. An additional consideration is the emerging field of clinimetrics, which emphasises the clinical relevance and utility of PROMs beyond traditional psychometric testing [22]. By focusing on whether instruments capture meaningful changes, guide decision-making, and improve patient outcomes, clinimetrics offers an important framework for PROM development. Incorporating these principles in future validation may strengthen the interpretability and clinical applicability of the new measure [23].

In this study, the Delphi panel recommended adding a body map for respondents to indicate pain locations. Full-body diagrams are useful but lack the detailed genitourinary anatomy needed for treating pelvic pain, leading Aibel and Moldwin [24] to develop the Pelvic Pain Map. Although user-friendly, such maps can challenge electronic completion, numerical scoring, and anatomical accuracy [24].

Further recommendations from the panel included using lay language to accommodate varying literacy levels and improve response rates. They suggested structuring questions around frequency or bother, with a specific timeframe (e.g., the past four weeks). Experts raised the question of whether the PROM will include pre-operative assessments for patients with existing pain syndromes, as these individuals may be at higher risk for post-operative pain. While the new instrument will focus on pain post-surgery, further consolidation with consumers and clinicians during the pre-testing and field-testing phase will be conducted.

The study was strengthened by its multiphase mixed methods design, enabling detailed exploration of pelvic floor and surgery-related pain [25]. Furthermore, involving and combining the knowledge of patients, consumer advocates, and clinicians minimised individual bias, thus contributing to more stable and reliable results [26]. Inclusion of participants from Australia and New Zealand enhanced representativeness within these countries; however, the lack of broader geographic and cultural diversity may limit generalisability of the findings to other regions.

Our recruitment method, restricted to online advertisements and invitations, may have led to disparity and bias in patient selection. Given the specific nature of the study cohort, online platforms were effective in reaching individuals familiar with digital communication. Although this approach may have excluded less online-engaged women, the sample still reflected the target population. There was also a risk of recall bias from participant interviews [27, 28].

Scams and false participants seeking study participation for the gift vouchers were encountered during Phase 1 recruitment. Some individuals provided inconsistent demographic information (e.g., different dates of birth), used male names and email addresses despite the study targeting women, or signed consent forms under a different name. This unexpected interference prolonged study duration, as the research team had to manually ensure data integrity and verify participant authenticity by reviewing eligibility criteria and contact details [29]. Individuals were excluded when there was clear evidence, they were not genuine or ineligible, as recorded in a tracking log. The study advertisement required interested women to provide a valid Australian or New Zealand phone number.

Mesh-augmented incontinence and prolapse surgeries differ, and pain from these procedures may vary by mesh type, placement and surgical technique. The interviews collected information on type of PFP, whether mesh was used and the type. While grouping these procedures may oversimplify the findings, this decision was made given that procedural details were obtained through patient self-report as part of demographic data. Differentiating the procedures would be valuable in future research.

Predominance of Australian patients and urologists on the Delphi panel may have influenced the results by reflecting Australian clinical practices and patient perspectives, which could limit the generalisability of the findings to women with PFDs in other countries. It was difficult to include a more diverse group of pain specialists and allied health professionals, such as pelvic floor physiotherapists and psychologists. Although these professionals were invited and initially expressed interest, they were ultimately unable to participate due to limited availability despite the follow-up. As a result, perspectives from these professional groups were underrepresented, which may have further shaped the consensus outcomes.

Furthermore, the panel consisted of a higher clinician-to-consumer ratio, prioritising clinical input, considering a large proportion of women were involved in the qualitative interviews. There were also limitations associated with the RAND Delphi method, particularly with rigid thresholds and exclusion of nuanced perspectives [30]. The use of rigid thresholds in traditional Delphi methods may restrict the inclusion of minority opinions that do not meet a pre-defined criterion [30]. To overcome this, an inclusive threshold of 70% was incorporated, which allowed for a broader range of expert opinions to be considered even if they do not align with a pre-defined criterion [18]. This helped to ensure that diverse perspectives were included. However, despite this inclusive approach, the method may have still overlooked competing methods or external factors that could have influenced expert opinions [18, 30].

Conclusion

This study underscores the importance of developing a new, pain-specific PROM for women following PFP. Qualitative findings revealed the complex, multifaceted nature of pain across eight domains. Delphi panel recommendations yielded a list of items for the new instrument, reflective of both qualitative insights and expert consensus. While these candidate items provide strong evidence of content validity, the PROM itself has yet to be fully developed and tested. This will inform the next steps in in the developmental process, which will involve refining, testing, and validating the pain PROM with women in pelvic mesh support groups, focusing on psychometric evaluation, including test-retest reliability, validity, and known-group differences. Overall, it is anticipated that the new pain-specific PROM will be used in clinical care settings to provide insight into the nature, severity and effects of pain, identify the unmet needs of women, and monitor short and long-term health outcomes of patients following PFPs.
