Performance of Enhanced Large Language Models on Prosthodontic Multiple-Choice Questions
Authors: Shenghan Gao, Zi-Ang Wang, Zihan Gao, Jiaxuan Liu, Huishuai Zhang, Shaoxia Pan, Yongsheng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo evaluate the performance of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), in-context learning (ICL) and majority voting–enhanced large language models (LLMs) on prosthodontic questions.MethodsTwo LLMs, OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1, were used as base models in this study. The base models were enhanced using RAG, ICL and majority voting. The resulting enhanced LLMs—enhanced DeepSeek-R1 and enhanced OpenAI o1—were evaluated alongside their original counterparts. Standardised Chinese-language and English-language prosthodontics questions were used to evaluate the performance of the base and enhanced LLMs. The correctness of each answer and the error types were recorded. χ2 tests were used to compare differences among the 4 LLMs (α = 0.05).ResultsEnhanced versions of both DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o1 achieved significant accuracy improvement compared with their base versions in Chinese-language multiple-choice questions (C-MCQs) (P < .001). In English-language multiple-choice questions (E-MCQs), both enhanced versions also exhibited higher accuracy, but the difference was not statistically significant (P = .145). In error analysis, enhanced LLMs significantly reduced knowledge-based errors in C-MCQs (P < .008) but showed only a non-significant reduction in E-MCQs (P = .604). The results reveal that the significant improvement gains from model enhancement were confined to C-MCQs and were not observed in E-MCQs.ConclusionsThe LLMs enhanced using inference-time strategies including RAG, ICL and majority voting show significantly improved accuracy in answering C-MCQs. Although this approach effectively mitigates errors, our analysis reveals a lack of consistent statistically significant improvements in E-MCQs.Clinical SignificanceThis study illustrates the performance of enhanced LLMs in analysing prosthodontics questions with good accuracy and potential in dentistry education. This implies that enhanced LLMs may be more suitable than non-enhanced ones for processing dental-related tasks.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are artificial intelligence (AI) systems trained on extensive text datasets and hold significant potential for medical education,1,2 including prosthodontics, where they could assist in understanding textual information, personalised instruction, clinical scenario simulation, literature-based learning support and cross-linguistic learning assistance.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 However, the core challenge lies in their transition from general-purpose AI to reliable prosthodontics-specific education tools. General LLMs often lack professional knowledge of prosthodontics,8,9 limiting their reliability and practical application value in education.

Evaluations of general LLMs on medical exams suggest that dental students need to be cautious when using LLMs as educational tools. For instance, GPT-4 (2023 version) achieved only 58.6% accuracy on the prosthodontics part, despite its 72.1% overall accuracy in dentistry.10 Similarly, GPT-4o (2024 version) attained only 70.85% on a comprehensive dentistry exam,11 and GPT-4 (2023 version) scored just 57.3% accuracy on endodontic questions.12

To improve the performance of LLMs in specific domains, recent efforts have focused on promoting accuracy through enhancement techniques. Several training-free techniques have been demonstrated to enhance the capabilities of LLMs in domain-specific applications, including retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) for integrating external knowledge sources into the response generation process, in-context learning (ICL) for performing tasks by conditioning on examples provided within the input prompt, and majority voting for improving consistency through self-consistency.13, 14, 15, 16 For example, by integrating RAG technology into GPT-4o (2024 version), the enhanced LLM exhibited higher accuracy in answering periodontal questions.17 Furthermore, the newly reasoning models Open AI o118 and DeepSeek-R119 have emerged. These models use reinforcement learning and demonstrate significant advancements in solving complex tasks, providing a more capable platform for evaluating AI performance when answering questions in prosthodontics education.

Despite these advances, a critical gap remains in understanding how enhancement techniques (such as RAG, ICL, and majority voting) perform when applied to state-of-the-art reasoning models within prosthodontics education. Furthermore, the cross-lingual performance of the enhanced LLMs is unclear. To address this, this study aims to evaluate the accuracy and error rate of the base LLMs (OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1) and the enhanced LLMs (OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1 enhanced by RAG, ICL, and majority voting) on Chinese-language (mandarin) and English-language multiple-choice questions (MCQs) in prosthodontics. The null hypothesis was that no significant performance difference would be found between the base and enhanced LLMs.

Materials and methods

This study evaluated the performance of LLMs on prosthodontics questions. The study design, methodology description, and reporting of results were structured to adhere to accepted reporting standards for AI research.20

Two LLMs, OpenAI o1 (o1-preview-2024-09-12 version) and DeepSeek-R1, were used as base models in this study. Standardised Chinese-language and English-language questions from publicly available books were used to evaluate the performance of both the base and enhanced LLMs in answering prosthodontics questions. The outputs of the LLMs underwent accuracy rate analysis and error type analysis to systematically assess inter-model discrepancies and the efficacy of enhancement strategies. This study did not involve patient data or private information; therefore, approval from an institutional review board was not required.

Question selection

Chinese-language multiple-choice questions (C-MCQs) and English-language multiple-choice questions (E-MCQs) in prosthodontics, each with a single correct answer, were included in this study. To ensure the accurate identification of MCQs, questions containing figures or tables were excluded, because LLMs are prone to hallucination when dealing with non-textual information in questions, which would interfere with the results.21 The remaining questions were reviewed by an expert prosthodontist. Any question that was flagged by the expert as ambiguous, having disputed answers, or containing unclear phrasing was removed.

The Chinese-language questions were obtained from examination preparation books for the Chinese attending doctor examination in dentistry22 and from the study guide and exercise book series for a prosthodontics textbook.23 A total of 403 questions were obtained from the former source,22 and 269 questions were selected after applying the exclusion criteria. Similarly, 350 questions were obtained from the latter source,23 and 241 questions were selected after applying the exclusion criteria.

The English-language questions were obtained from examination preparation books for the Integrated National Board Dental Examination (INBDE), published by Mosby in 200724 and StatPearls in 2022,25 as well as from examination preparation books for the Overseas Registration Examination (ORE), published by PasTest in 2014.26,27 All these books were publicly available, and similar English-language books were recently used in another study to test LLM performance.10 A total of 77 questions were obtained from the Mosby book,24 31 questions from the StatPearls book25 and 3 questions from the PasTest book.26 All questions were selected after applying the exclusion criteria. An additional 41 questions were obtained from another PasTest book,27 of which 17 questions were selected after applying the exclusion criteria.

Model development

This study employed 2 advanced LLMs: DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o1 (o1-preview-2024-09-12 version). To improve the performance of general-purpose models in prosthodontics, both models were augmented using 3 techniques, RAG, ICL, and majority voting, to provide expert knowledge, guide reasoning patterns and improve answer consistency. The enhanced LLMs—enhanced DeepSeek-R1 and enhanced OpenAI o1—were evaluated alongside their original counterparts. The functional application of each technique is described in the following subsections.

Retrieval-augmented generation

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) was implemented to supply the LLMs with a knowledge corpus during question answering, aiming to improve factual accuracy. The knowledge corpus of prosthodontics was built by extracting the text of publicly available authoritative prosthodontics books and splitting it into segments.28, 29, 30, 31 The text was preprocessed by removing charts and applying a uniform format. The initial corpus was checked by an expert prosthodontist, to ensure consistency and reliability of the knowledge corpus. The textbooks were converted into machine-readable text and segmented into overlapping chunks using a chunk size of 1,024 tokens with a chunk overlap of 200 tokens to preserve contextual continuity. For each question, the RAG system automatically retrieved the top 6 most relevant textbook segments. This process was based on semantic similarity and implemented using the EasyRAG framework.32 The retrieval segments were validated by the same expert, who confirmed the retrieved segments were topically appropriate for the test MCQs. These segments were then provided as domain-specific information to the LLM, enabling it to give responses using prosthodontics-specific content rather than relying solely on its internal training data.

In-context learning

In-context learning (ICL) was used to guide the enhanced LLM to generate answers that align with professional answer patterns in prosthodontics. For each test question, 1 example of a prosthodontics question with its correct answer and reasoning analysis from examination preparation books was added to the enhanced LLM in the prompt. The enhanced LLM was then asked to imitate the professional reasoning patterns of the example when answering the test MCQs. The examples of prosthodontics questions were randomly selected from the C-MCQs and E-MCQs sets, excluding the one the LLMs were currently answering. For a given test question, an example question with the same language was selected for ICL. The conversation history was cleared before presenting each new query. This ensured that no information from previous ICL prompts influenced subsequent responses.

Majority voting

To reduce the variability of LLM responses, majority voting was used to improve the robustness of output. To achieve majority voting, multiple reasoning paths of the LLM were obtained. The most frequent answer was taken as the output result of majority voting. Prior work demonstrates that even with moderate sample sizes (eg, 8 responses), self-consistency approaches can achieve stable and reliable performance, balancing computational cost and output stability.16 Thus, 8 responses were generated for majority voting in this study.

Analysis of accuracy

To comprehensively evaluate the systems’ performance, the questions were entered into the 4 LLMs. The models were then prompted to generate their answers as well as their reasoning process. The official answers provided in the textbooks served as the gold standard for comparison. The correctness of each answer (correct/incorrect) was recorded. The Chinese-language and English-language question accuracy of each model was separately calculated as the percentage of correct answers relative to the total number of questions.

Analysis of error type

To analyse error patterns systematically, all incorrectly answered questions and related reasoning processes were exported. The errors were divided into 3 types, including knowledge-based errors, contextual errors, and logical errors.

Knowledge-based errors were defined as the LLM providing incorrect answers resulting from a lack of specific domain knowledge or incorrect content in its reasoning process. For example, when asked about the physiological significance of the normal axial surface convexity of tooth crowns, the LLM demonstrated a knowledge deficit by incorrectly excluding the option about ensuring proper food spillway.

Contextual errors were defined as the LLM failing to correctly extract and use key information from the question in its reasoning process and consequently providing incorrect answers. For example, a question regarding the selection of the optimal treatment for aesthetic improvement of maxillary incisors with labial inclination was answered incorrectly because the model failed to use the labial inclination information in the decision-making process.

Logical errors were defined as cases where the LLM generated a flawed chain of clinical reasoning that ultimately resulted in an incorrect answer. For example, when asked for the most likely diagnosis of a tooth with periapical radiolucency, the LLM incorrectly selected the possible aetiology, excessive load, as the diagnosis. It followed a flawed process and confused the logical steps of determining a diagnosis and identifying the aetiology.

To ensure the objectivity and consistency of the error classification, the error type classification was made by 2 independent expert prosthodontists. The classification result was then evaluated using inter-rater reliability statistics. Afterwards, all discrepancies were discussed until a consensus was reached. Each error type counts for 4 LLMs were then recorded. The Chinese-language and English-language error rate of each model was separately calculated as the percentage of errors relative to the total number of questions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with a statistical software program (IBM SPSS Statistics, v. 26). Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ) statistic. χ2 tests were used to compare the overall distribution differences in the frequencies of correct/incorrect answers and knowledge-based error rates among the 4 LLMs. A P < .05 was considered statistically significant. χ2 tests were further conducted for a post hoc comparison of the accuracy and error rate between individual model pairs. Given the 6 pairwise comparisons among the 4 models, the significance level was adjusted and a corrected P value threshold of <.008 (0.05/6) was considered statistically significant. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported.

Discussion

Rejecting the null hypothesis, the results of this study show that domain-enhanced LLMs achieve high accuracy in prosthodontics MCQs, surpassing the performance of general models reported in prior literature.10 Specifically, the enhanced DeepSeek-R1 achieved 91.57% accuracy in C-MCQs and 79.68% in E-MCQs, whereas the respective results for the enhanced OpenAI o1 were 84.12% and 86.72%. The enhancement strategy led to a statistically significant improvement in the accuracy of both enhanced LLMs in C-MCQs (P < .001) and significantly reduced the knowledge-based error rate for Chinese-language questions in DeepSeek-R1 (from 11.76% to 6.86%, P < .008) and OpenAI o1 (from 22.35% to 13.14%, P < .001). The enhancement strategy also reduced the knowledge-based error rate for English-language questions in DeepSeek-R1 (from 17.97% to 14.84%) and OpenAI o1 (from 13.28% to 11.72%) (P = .604). Our finding that the base DeepSeek-R1 model achieved high accuracy aligns with prior evaluations of LLMs in specialised dental domains. A previous study reported that DeepSeek-R1 achieved an overall score of 86.4% for traumatic dental injury knowledge.34 This consistency illustrates that general LLMs possess robust foundational knowledge of dentistry. Beyond this base LLMs performance, our key finding is that the enhancement strategy further elevates accuracy. This shows that general LLMs already possess a strong dental knowledge foundation, but targeted enhancement can achieve significantly higher performance and reliability, highlighting its critical role in adapting AI for educational tasks.

The improvement in performance results from the enhancement strategy combining RAG, ICL, and majority voting. This aligns with previous studies in which providing targeted domain information has shown to enhance LLM accuracy in specialised fields.35 Except for this phenomenon, our study showed the contribution of a composite enhancement approach to reducing errors caused by insufficient knowledge.

However, the enhancement strategy also failed to address some reasoning flaws. In the question regarding minor connectors of removable partial dentures, the base DeepSeek-R1 model correctly identified 2 clinical key points from its base training data—‘should be thin (A)’ and ‘should conform to the interdental embrasure (C)’—thereby selecting the correct option E. However, after RAG enhancement, the model retrieved numerous professional literatures emphasising that ‘minor connectors must maintain sufficient strength’, which created a potential conflict with the description ‘should be thin’ in option A, leading the model to overcorrect its original judgement. This may reflect the drawback of RAG technology: the repeated appearance of strength requirements in the retrieved content caused the model to overestimate the decision weight of this feature in this particular question.

Our findings reveal a fundamental difference in problem-solving between LLMs and clinicians. When presented with this occlusion evaluation question, the LLMs demonstrate a characteristic analytical thinking pattern: they evaluate options A, B, and C independently, recognise that none of them fully encompasses all aspects of occlusal assessment and consequently incorrectly reject the comprehensive option D (which actually contains all correct elements), ultimately selecting E (‘none of the above’). By contrast, clinicians employ synthetic thinking: while recognising that A, B and C are individually incomplete, they understand these 3 methods are complementary in clinical practice, thus correctly choosing D. This cognitive discrepancy highlights a critical limitation in current LLMs’ clinical reasoning capabilities: while excelling at analysing discrete elements, they struggle to perform the holistic, context-integrated comprehensive judgement characteristic of human experts. This error pattern underscores the limitation of LLMs in context-integrated reasoning, which may affect their effectiveness in educational applications.

Together, these findings illustrate the significance and potential risks of integrating enhanced LLMs into prosthodontic education. While they can serve as powerful tools for searching knowledge, our observation of reasoning errors illustrates AI should act as a supportive tool rather than replacing student learning.35 Therefore, educators must guide students to critically evaluate LLM outputs rather than rely on them blindly.

There were some limitations in this study. DeepSeek-R1 and its enhanced version significantly outperformed OpenAI o1 and its enhanced version in C-MCQs questions (P < .001). Conversely, in E-MCQs, OpenAI o1 showed a tendency for higher accuracy compared with DeepSeek-R1, without a significant difference. The cross-lingual performance difference in the enhancement effect between Chinese-language and English-language questions may be attributed to the linguistic composition of the pre-training corpus and the small number of E-MCQs. The models likely differ in the linguistic composition of their pre-training corpora, which leads to the performance variance between Chinese-language and English-language questions. The corpus of DeepSeek-R1 may have a higher proportion of Chinese text and could lead to stronger understanding and reasoning capabilities in Chinese.36 By contrast, OpenAI o1 may have been exposed to a higher proportion of English text in the corpus. Consequently, the applicability of this finding to questions in different languages may be limited. Besides, the current evaluation may be limited by the relatively smaller number of English questions in the set (n = 128), which could influence the statistical power to detect significant differences in performance.

Another limitation is that the questions were drawn from a set of selected textbooks in this study. Potential variations in difficulty across the questions may influence accuracy rates, comparability, and reproducibility of the results.

Finally, this evaluation of the enhanced LLMs was confined to MCQs, which cannot simulate clinical decision-making and problem-based learning in real-world education, and it did not evaluate the learning outcomes for students using these enhanced LLMs. Consequently, the performance of the enhanced LLMs may not be fully generalised to these broader and more integrative tasks.

Given that the integration of AI into dental education has been regarded as a key direction,37 future work could explore with larger and difficulty-balanced question sets in both languages and investigate the effects of improving academic performance with the enhanced LLMs to address these issues and ensure reliability of the enhanced LLMs in prosthodontics education.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that enhancing LLMs using inference-time strategies, including RAG, ICL, and majority voting, significantly improves their accuracy when answering C-MCQs. Although this approach effectively mitigates errors, our analysis reveals the lack of consistent statistically significant improvements in E-MCQs.

Looking forward, these enhanced LLMs show potential as tools for strengthening prosthodontics education. They could be deployed in intelligent teaching systems for foundational knowledge review and for generating personalised quiz banks. Future work should focus on developing frameworks that better integrate such tools into education, ensuring they complement the essential knowledge learning process of students.
