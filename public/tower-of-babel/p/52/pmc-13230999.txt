Gendered Language in Letters of Recommendation for Interventional Pulmonology Fellowship Candidates
Authors: Matthew Glick, Erin BeDiasi, Christine Argento, Jason Akulian, Laura Frye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Background: Sex-based disparities persist in procedural subspecialties, including interventional pulmonology (IP). Letters of recommendation (LORs) are critical in fellowship applications, and prior research suggests sex-based bias in LORs for female candidates. Objective: To evaluate gendered language and structural differences in LORs for IP fellowship candidates by applicant and letter writer sex. Methods: This study analyzed 461 LORs from IP fellowship applications during the 2022 and 2023 cycles. LORs were deidentified and analyzed using the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count program, supplemented by a custom dictionary capturing agentic, communal, and family terms. Statistical analyses assessed differences by applicant and letter writer sex, practice type, and academic rank. Results: A total of 461 LORs were reviewed. No significant differences were found in LOR length by applicant or writer sex. The frequency of agentic and communal terms was similar across groups. However, female letter writers used significantly more family-related terms than male letter writers did (0.16 vs. 0.09; P = 0.002). Secondary analyses revealed no consistent meaningful differences by letter writer age, practice type, or rank. Conclusion: This study found limited evidence of sex-based bias in LORs for IP fellowship candidates. The results underscore the need for continued awareness and training to promote equity in the application process.

Interventional pulmonology (IP) remains a male-dominated subspecialty within pulmonary medicine, with a persistent underrepresentation of women in leadership positions despite the increasing number of female physicians entering medical school and pursuing careers in pulmonology (1). This gap can be attributed to several factors, including implicit biases, limited access to mentorship, and structural challenges related to the procedural nature of the field (2). In an anonymous survey of 647 current or past physician members of the American Association of Bronchology and Interventional Pulmonology conducted in 2022, 17.8% of respondents were female. Although this may seem alarmingly low, it is an improvement over the 15% of respondents in the 2019 survey and only 11% in the 2016 survey data (3). Active membership data from the American Association of Bronchology and Interventional Pulmonology in 2024 would suggest an improvement, with 256 (28.6%) of the 894 members who identified their sex as female, although notably this statistic includes nonphysician providers, such as nurse practitioners, nurses, respiratory therapists, and physician assistants, which may distort the data.

Letters of recommendation (LORs) serve as a valuable tool in the assessment of candidates for residency and fellowship and play an important role in the decision of whom to interview (4). They are also heavily weighed by the fellowship selection committee in the creation of the rank list (5). Prior studies have shown that LORs for female candidates are typically shorter, contain more communal adjectives, and are less likely to emphasize research accomplishments and ability (6). In 2022, Viglianti and colleagues published their review of all letters submitted by candidates in the 2020 application cycle for pulmonary and critical care medicine (7). Of the 529 complete applications, 2,024 LORs were reviewed, with over two-thirds of the candidates and the letter writers being male. In this study, female applicants received longer and more supportive letters, suggesting the beginning of a change in the composition of LORs for female applicants. There are still concerns, however, that biases may persist in procedurally focused fields such as IP. A study of another procedure-heavy field, cardiothoracic surgery, demonstrated that male bias continues to exist in the applicant selection process (8). Another study by Yong and colleagues evaluated 739 recommendation letters extracted from 196 individual applications for cardiothoracic surgery fellowships (9). Ninety percent of the authors in this study were male. The female applicants were significantly more likely to have recommendation letters focused on their social ties, leisure activities, and the leadership status of the letter writer. Male letter writers were more likely to focus on their status and the occupation of the applicant’s father or husband. Given the procedural nature of the field, where LORs for IP reside in the spectrum has not been answered. The goal of this study was to evaluate sex-based bias in LORs for IP candidates and to assess differences in letters on the basis of the sex of the letter writer.

Methods

All LORs submitted for every applicant to the IP fellowships in the 2022 and 2023 application cycles were included in this study. The number of letters per applicant was determined on the basis of letters submitted. Exclusion criteria included letters written by multiple writers and LORs for programs other than IP. Applicant sex was extracted from the applications. The sex and age of letter writers was determined using publicly available resources (e.g., institutional websites, professional profiles, and websites such as Sharecare [10] and Healthgrades [11]). This study was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Utah.

The text of letters was deidentified to remove identifiable details (e.g., names, institutional letterheads, salutations, and signatures). Letters were then analyzed using the LIWC-22 (Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count) program, supplemented with a custom-built data dictionary tailored to capture specific language traits relevant to recommendation letters in the categories of communal, agentic, and family terms. The word dictionary was created on the basis of prior literature to capture the different adjectives used in LORs. LORs commonly use words that can be categorized as communal, family, agentic, grindstone, and ability. Because grindstone and ability terms overlap with agentic terms, they were identified in that category for analysis. Iterative testing and refinement of the dictionary ensured comprehensive word coverage. The LIWC-22 program is a validated text analysis tool widely used in research to analyze linguistic patterns, including sex-based bias in LORs for medical fellowship candidates (12–14).

The primary outcome was length and content of letters measured by total word count and presence of words categorized as communal, agentic, or family (Table 1). Secondary analysis evaluated word count and content further stratified by characteristics of letter writers, including type of practice (academic, Veterans Administration, private, or nonacademic), academic rank (instructor, assistant, associate, full professor), age (<45, 45–55, >55 yr old), and whether they are board certified in IP. Both age and academic rank were collected, given the significant variability in time to promotion at academic institutions. Age cutoffs were chosen because they divided the cohort of letter writers approximately by thirds, and this breakdown approximately delineated early-career faculty, midcareer faculty, and late-career faculty.

Statistical analyses, including t tests and one-way analysis of variance, were performed using GraphPad QuickCalcs (GraphPad Software) to assess associations between applicant and letter writer sex and outcomes of interest. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Applicant and Letter Writer Characteristics

A total of 487 LORs were obtained for the 2022 and 2023 application cycles. A total of 461 letters were included in the study after excluding duplicates, letters with multiple authors, and letters of application for non-IP programs. Applicants were predominantly male (n = 351; 76.2%), with only 110 female applicants (23.8%) (Table 2). The majority of letter writers were also male (n = 372; 80.5%), with only 19.3% female letter writers (n = 89) (Table 2).

Full letter writer demographics are displayed in Table 2. The majority of letter writers (87.5%; n = 407) were affiliated with academic institutions. Letter writer ranks varied, with associate professors (31.1%; n = 145) and assistant professors (28.3%; n = 132) collectively representing the largest group. Full professors accounted for 23.2% (n = 108) of letter writers, whereas instructors represented 1.3% (n = 6). Forty-one percent of letter writers were interventional pulmonologists (n = 191). The average age of letter writers was 51 years (±10), although 42 were of unknown age.

Differences in Letter Content

Length

There was no statistical difference in the length of LORs for either sex of applicant (male, 453 ± 196 words; vs. female, 423 ± 149 words; P = 0.15) or sex of the letter writer (male, 445 ± 185 words; vs. female, 446 ± 192 words; P = 0.97) (Table 3).

Language usage

Letters were evaluated for the presence of agentic, communal, and family-related descriptors. Overall, there were no significant differences in the use of agentic or communal terms based on the sex of either the applicant or the letter writer. However, a significant difference was observed in the use of family-related terms by letter writer sex, with female writers using these terms more frequently than male writers (0.160 vs. 0.090; P = 0.002). There was no significant difference in the use of family-related terms based on applicant sex.

Secondary Analysis

Differences by letter writer rank

Secondary analysis by letter writer academic rank did not reveal any consistent meaningful differences in length of letter or word content. Assistant professor female letter writers did write shorter LORs than their male colleagues. Associate professor female letter writers used fewer communal and agentic terms than their male colleagues, but full professor female letter writers used more agentic terms (see Table E4 in the data supplement).

Differences by letter writer age

There were no meaningful differences when letters were analyzed by age (stratified into those less than 45 yr old, 45 to 55 yr old, and older than 65 yr old). Younger female letter writers (<45 yr of age) wrote shorter letters than their male counterparts (431 ± 132 words vs. 507 ± 222 words; P = 0.04). There were no differences between the rates of communal, agentic, or family term use between male and female letter writers in any of the other age categories (Table E6).

There were no meaningful sex-based differences when letters were analyzed by letter writer practice location or whether the letter writer was an interventional pulmonologist (Tables E3 and E5).

Discussion

This study identified no significant theme of differences in word count or language content between male and female applicants to IP fellowship programs in the application cycles for 2022 and 2023. Although this lack of observed disparity may indicate progress toward equitable practices, the low overall percentage of female applicants (23.8%) and letter writers (19.3%) still suggests there is room to grow in creating an equitable workforce in IP. These findings may differ from prior research, likely because of increasing awareness of sex-based equity in academic and medical settings, which may have influenced recent letter-writing practices. Our findings contrast with a recent publication by Viglianti and colleagues showing that female applicants to general pulmonary and critical care medicine (PCCM) programs received longer and more supportive LORs (4). Recently, Byrd and colleagues demonstrated that there is regional variability in this finding for general PCCM applicants, but female applicants in a majority of regions (Midwest, South, Northeast) still have longer and more supportive LORs (15). We did not evaluate regionality in this study, and future studies may want to look into any regional disparities in LORs for IP applicants.

Although we did not analyze applicant outcomes in relation to LOR composition, these findings stand as a reminder of the importance of equitable practices in writing and evaluating LORs. Specific strategies to promote equity include providing standardized training to letter writers on avoiding gendered language, using structured templates to focus on objective achievements, and fostering awareness of implicit biases through workshops or institutional initiatives. In addition, institutions could implement review systems to assess LORs for potential biases before they are submitted, ensuring that all candidates are evaluated on an equal footing. Training programs should provide explicit guidance on avoiding language that reinforces sex-based stereotypes (16).

Limitations

It is crucial to note the limitations of this study, including that only 2 years of application cycles were examined. The biggest limitation may be that the overall prevalence of communal, agentic, and family terms was low, hindering determination of significance. In addition, linguistic analysis tools only provide rates of word usage and may not capture the full context of language usage. The data do not account for institutional or geographic variations in letter-writing practices, which have recently been demonstrated to exist in general PCCM applicants. Moreover, implicit biases may manifest in forms not captured by the methodologies applied here. Future research should expand the scope of analysis to include larger datasets and consider additional qualitative assessments of recommendation letters as well as look at applicant outcomes in relation to gendered language used in LORs.

Conclusion

This study represents a step in exploring potential sex-based bias in LORs for IP fellowship applicants. Through the analysis of 461 letters across the 2022 and 2023 application cycles, no significant differences were found in the length or content of letters based on applicant sex. In addition, there were no clear differences in the use of agentic, communal, or family-related language across applicant sex. Our findings underscore the importance of fostering awareness and providing training to mitigate implicit bias in the letter-writing process. Such efforts are essential to ensure fairness and equity in the evaluation of fellowship candidates, particularly in procedural and male-dominated subspecialties such as IP.
