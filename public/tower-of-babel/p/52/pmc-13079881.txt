Gambling disorder as a risk factor for suicidal ideation in medical students
Authors: Amandine Luquiens, Céline Bourgier, Pascal Fabbro-Peray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough suicide rates among physicians have decreased, medical students remain at high risk for suicidal ideation. While poor mental health, including depression, burnout, and stress, are well-documented contributing factors, the role of behavioral addictions, particularly gambling disorder, remains underexplored. This study aims to identify risk and protective factors associated with recent suicidal ideation among second- to fourth-year medical students, including gambling disorder. An observational study was conducted among medical students at a French University, recruited in December 2021, 2022, and 2023 to compare empathy with or without a training program. Participants completed a battery of questionnaires, including the patient health questionnaire (PHQ-9) to assess depression and the Lie-Bet questionnaire for gambling. Suicidal ideation was screened with the 9th item of the PHQ-9. Data were collected on sociodemographic characteristics, mental health variables, and chronic illness. Participants were surveyed again at 6 months and 1, 2, and 3 years, with follow-up on-going. Here, we present baseline data on factors explaining recent suicidal ideation and in particular gambling disorder. Of 1,762 students invited, 966 consented to participate, and data from 775 students were analyzed. The prevalence of suicidal ideation was 15.9%. Students with probable gambling disorder had a significantly higher prevalence of suicidal ideation than those without gambling problems (34.8% vs. 15.3%, p = 0.0120). Multivariate analysis identified probable gambling disorder, anxiety, lower empathy and emotional dysregulation as independently associated with suicidal ideation. Probable gambling disorder, anxiety, and emotional dysregulation were key factors linked to suicidal ideation in medical students. Screening for gambling disorder and promoting emotional regulation strategies should be integrated into medical curricula to address these risks.Subject terms: Epidemiology, Epidemiology

Introduction

While the rate of suicide in physicians has decreased over time, especially in Europe, physicians remain an at-risk profession1. A history of mental health problems and suicidal ideation constitute risk factors for suicide attempts2. The prevalence of suicidal ideation in large national cohorts is high among medical students3. A meta-analysis conducted in 2016 estimated the prevalence of suicidal ideation among medical students at 11.1%4. This prevalence significantly increased post-COVID-19, reaching levels between 15% and 20%, and appears to be consistent across different cultural contexts5,6.

A meta-analysis, pooling findings from 25 studies, revealed that medical students experiencing poor mental health outcomes, particularly depression, burnout, comorbid mental disorders, and stress, faced the highest risk of suicidal ideation7. While alcohol and substance use have been extensively studied in this population, the role of alcohol consumption on suicidal ideation and attempts is controversial5,7. Moreover, the role of behavioral addictions, in particular gambling disorder, remains largely unexplored. Evidence suggests an inverse correlation between socioeconomic status and the likelihood of engaging in online gambling8. Medical students tend to come from relatively affluent backgrounds, and people with low economic status are underrepresented9. However, considering the high prevalence of gambling disorder within the age group typical of medical students in clinical training, i.e. 10.1% of people 18–25 years old having gambled at least once in the last year10,11, it seems crucial to include gambling disorder as a factor when exploring contributors to suicidal ideation. This is further heightened by the strong association between gambling disorder and suicidal behavior in the general population12, with at least 31% of people with gambling problem presenting lifetime suicidal ideation13,

Only a few studies have prospectively examined the association between gambling disorder and suicidality in adolescent or young adult student populations. For instance, Turner et al. found that gambling disorder in secondary students was significantly associated with male gender, hazardous drinking, and suicidality14. Another study investigating the relationship between suicidality and engagement in “blind boxes” (a gambling-like behavior) among university students reported that, even after controlling for variables such as age, socioeconomic status, disposable income, alcohol consumption, and smoking, blind box engagement remained significantly associated with suicide risk15. Studies across various cultural contexts have also reported associations between gambling disorder and suicidality among adolescents or students aged 17–25. However, these studies have primarily focused on identifying factors associated with gambling disorder rather than elucidating the factors underlying suicidal ideation16–21. Consequently, it remains unclear whether gambling disorder constitutes an explanatory factor for suicidal ideation when adjusted for other sociodemographic, extracurricular, and mental health variables, as well as specific factors relevant to the medical curriculum, such as clinical empathy and the ability to adopt a patient-centered approach. In addition and in a pragmatic approach to provide guidance on future prevention measures, we identified several possible protective factors that could influence mental health and here particularly the suicidal risk and counter-balance individual vulnerability: engagement in artistic activities (music, dance, visual arts)22, regular sports practice23,24, and connectedness (feeling of belonging to the school community)25.

This study aims to identify risk and protective factors associated with suicidal ideation over the past 2 weeks among second- to fourth-year medical students, with a particular interest on gambling disorder. Other potential risk and protective factors were selected from the state of the art regarding suicidal risk in students and in particular in medical students. We examined sociodemographic characteristics, extracurricular activities, psychological functioning, anxiety, depression, and addictive behaviors, including gambling disorder. Our main hypothesis was that probable gambling disorder would increase the risk of recent suicidal ideation, alongside with and independently from other already documented risk factors regarding other addictions and psychological functioning, and that this risk would persist even when taking into account possible protective factors.

Methods

Design

The “debuter” study is an observational study. It was introduced to potential participants during several pedagogical sessions that presented a new coursework on psychosocial skills and the development of the patient-care provider relationship. The importance of obtaining feedback on students’ mental health and perceptions of care to adapt the coursework was emphasized. The study was further promoted by elected student representatives and the local student association. The study details were communicated orally during these sessions, in the electronic document outlining the coursework that was provided to all students, and on the first page of the survey. Students were informed that teachers and investigators would not have access to information identifying who completed the survey or to individual responses, and that participation in the survey was entirely voluntary.

Each participant received a personalized, unique survey link to ensure secure and accurate data collection. Automatic reminder notifications were sent at 3 days and 1 week after the initial invitation. The first question of the survey requested participants’ consent to join the study. Those who declined consent were not recontacted.

Data were collected through a REDCap survey distributed via MyCap at baseline, 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, and 3 years. This manuscript focuses exclusively on the baseline data to explore cross-sectionally factors explaining recent suicidal ideation; remaining outcomes and time points will be published separately.

Study population

Inclusion criteria were: (1) medical students enrolled in 2nd to 4th years of medical study in the University of Montpellier School of Medicine, in December 2021 and in December 2022 and 2023 for new students; (2) consented to complete pseudonymized questionnaires; and (3) completed the 9th item of the patient health questionnaire (PHQ-9) informing on the presence of suicidal ideation in the last 2 weeks (score > = 1 yes/no). There were no exclusion criteria.

Data pseudonymization

The study adhered to strict pseudonymization protocols to ensure participant confidentiality. Data were pseudonymized during collection by using a code derived from the first three letters of the last name, the first two letters of the first name, and the participant’s date of birth. Identifiable information was not retained beyond this coding process.

Data collection

Sociodemographic measures were age, sex, scholarship recipient (Yes/no), In a relationship (yes/no). We also collected data on the entry route into medical studies, distinguishing between students who followed a high school track primarily focused on medicine and entered via a competitive entrance exam, and those who entered through equivalence pathways with a secondary focus on medicine. This variable was important because both the traditional French competitive exam and the reform being implemented at the time of the study—which aimed to broaden access through secondary medical tracks—could influence psychological functioning during the subsequent second and third years of medical school, which were the groups of interest. Chronic illness history (e.g., metabolic disorders, hypertension, diabetes, other) was collected. Potentially protective factors were collected: engagement in artistic activities (music, dance, visual arts)22, regular sports practice23,24. Participants were asked the following question: “During the past four weeks, have you engaged in a sport or artistic activity?”. Responses were provided on a five-point frequency scale: every day, several times a week, once a week, less frequently, or never. Regular practice was defined as engaging in such an activity at least once a week.” Connectedness (feeling of belonging to the school community)25 was scored on a 10-item Likert scale.

Statistical analysis

Characteristics of participants are presented by their frequency and associated percentage for categorical variables and by the mean ± standard deviation or median with inter-quartile range for quantitative variables. Question 9 of the PHQ-9 was treated as a stand-alone item to identify the presence of suicidal ideation in the last 2 weeks44,45. Feeling of belonging to the student community was assessed on a 10-point Likert scale and dichotomized at ≥ 8 (median value), in the absence of an established norm, to distinguish students with higher versus lower perceived belonging. Low feeling of belonging to the student community, the presence of a chronic disease or disability, the engagement in regular artistic activities and in sport practice were treated as binary variables, as well as having a probable gambling disorder (with Lie-Bet ≥ 1).

Comparisons between groups for quantitative variables were made using Mann–Whitney U-test. For categorical variable, comparisons were made using either Khi2 or Fisher exact test. To explore factors associated with the presence of suicidal ideation in the last 2 weeks, a multivariate logistic regression was performed, retaining variables with initial univariate p-value of the between-group comparison of 0.15 or lower. To avoid multi-collinearity issue, PHQ-9 could not be integrated in the model, as the variable to explain was extracted from it. Missing items within a scale were imputed according to the scales with specific instructions. Analyses were performed with SAS Software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Nîmes Hospital under the code 21.11.01. Participants provided informed consent electronically by clicking on an explicit consent button prior to accessing the survey. This consent process ensured that participants fully understood the voluntary nature of their participation, the pseudonymized handling of their data, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences.

Results

Among the 1 762 students invited, 966 (54.8%) students consented to participate. We analyzed data from the 775 (44.0%) students who completed the 9th item of the PHQ-9 (past 2 weeks suicidal ideation). The prevalence of suicidal ideation in the last 2 weeks was 15.8%, distributed as follows: 13.7% in 2nd year, 19.4% in 3rd year, and 20.6% in 4th year. We found severe anxiety in 10.8% of participants, and severe depression in 4.9%. We found 37.1% with a high risk of substance use disorder including alcohol, with 2.0% of participants with probable cannabis use disorder. Discussion

In this cross-sectional study conducted among second- to fourth-year medical students, 15.8% reported suicidal ideation during the past two weeks, with higher rates observed in third- and fourth-year students. Severe anxiety and depression were found in 10.8% and 4.9% of participants, respectively. More than one-third of the sample presented a high risk of substance use disorder, and 2.0% met criteria for probable cannabis use disorder. Probable gambling disorder, as assessed by the Lie-Bet questionnaire, was identified in 3.0% of students. In the multivariate analysis, probable gambling disorder, moderate-to-severe anxiety, lower empathy and higher emotion dysregulation were the only factors independently associated with suicidal ideation. Burnout dimensions, sense of belonging to the student community, chronic condition and regular sports or artistic activities were not independently associated to suicidal ideation. This period of the curriculum is notable as students begin their hospital internships, which may contribute to heightened stress levels46.

Young people account for 12.3% of individuals with gambling disorder47. In France, 17% of young adults aged 18–24 participated in online gambling in the past year48. Among active gamblers in this population, the prevalence of gambling disorder reaches 10%, corresponding approximately 1.7% of all 18–24-year-olds11. In our study, the prevalence of gambling disorder was 3%, which is consistent with the general population, but twice the expected prevalence for this age group, highlighting a particularly concerning trend; students with probable gambling disorder had approximately five times higher odds of reporting suicidal ideation compared with their peers without gambling problems. Previous research has demonstrated the increased risk of suicidal ideation associated with gambling disorder, with an odds ratio of 4 in a study of American college students in 2015 19. A large prospective study in young people in Uk reported that the problem gambling severity index score predicted suicidal attempts over the next 4 years49. Our study completes these findings in the population of young medical students, without impact of socioeconomic status and adjusting with other psychological variables. The independent nature of the found association suggests a direct link related to the inner risk of harmful gambling50, with no necessary psychiatric comorbidity involved. Underlying mechanisms could be related to financial and familial problems51, but we assessed no data to explore these hypotheses and future studies are warranted. These findings should encourage screening for problem gambling in this population that appears surprisingly at high risk. A recent systematic review identified studies on the effectiveness of prevention programs aiming at reducing problem gambling in young adults52. Although a Personalized Normative Feedback approach has proved feasible, with promising results in reducing at-risk or problem gambling in several studies, prevention programs remain scarce and poorly disseminated. More concerted efforts are needed to expand and strengthen these preventive measures among students. High-stress curricula, such as medical studies, should be particularly targeted.

Surprisingly, the role of alcohol and other substance use disorders in suicidal ideation was not confirmed, unlike in previous studies7. These results pertain only to suicidal ideation, and it is important to note that the risk of alcohol and other substance use disorders was very high in our sample, exceeding one-third of the students. This supports previous findings in medical students from other countries53 and is associated with other substance-related harms that were not explored in this study and are not to be minimized, e.g. sexual assaults54. The inconsistency of previous findings regarding the association between alcohol/substance-related addictive behaviors and suicidality could be explained by an unrecognized gambling disorder, which might act as a latent variable, given the common co-occurrence of gambling disorder and alcohol use disorder55. This highlights the importance of controlling for gambling disorder in studies on students and suicidality.

In parallel, we confirmed the role of emotion dysregulation in suicidality among medical students. These findings underscore the importance of including training programs in the medical curriculum to improve psychosocial and emotional regulation skills. The efficacy of this kind of program has been demonstrated on empathy46,56, but should now be explored on suicidality. Such initiatives are likely to have a lasting positive impact on students’ mental health and their professional development. Furthermore, these efforts would enhance the quality of care they provide, as poor mental health has broader implications, including detrimental effects on ethical conduct and empathy3.

Depression was not included in the multivariate analyses for methodological reasons related to multi-collinearity, but was found to be associated with suicidal ideation in the univariate analysis, as consistently reported. However, burnout was included in the multivariate model and was not identified as an independent factor associated with suicidal ideation when controlled for other mental health variables. Knowing the robustly reported overlap between depression and burnout, suggesting that burnout would be a more socially accepted depression diagnostic category57, this absence of relation when adjusting with other psychological functioning variables should warn about prevention initiatives that would too narrowly focus on depression at the risk of missing the relevant group.

Similarly, empathy and patient-oriented therapeutic approach were respectively very weakly negatively and not independently associated to suicidality at this stage of the medical studies in our sample, while a higher empathy was associated to higher suicidality in univariate analysis. It suggests an indirect relation between the two constructs, that did not stand when adjusting to other psychological functioning variables. To our knowledge, no study has explicitly explored the relation between empathy and suicidality in medical students, with most studies focusing on the link between empathy and depression or burnout58, reporting an association in the reverse direction, the empathy protecting from depression and burnout. Our findings show that we cannot extrapolate the association between empathy and depression to empathy and suicidality and that a complex dynamic mechanism could possibly be involved, with suicidal ideation emerging in moments of crisis and not always in a depressive phase when empathy tend to decrease in link with depressive syndromic social withdrawal and painful feeling in the contact of others59.

Recent suicidal ideation associated with gambling disorder, anxiety, and emotional dysregulation may reflect a crisis response to specific events, such as significant losses due to gambling disorder60 and/or academic/hospital-related stressors. Such events may leave students with insufficient psychological resources to cope with these situations, rather than chronic suicidal ideation or ideation linked to a mood episode, as seen in depression. In this population, it appears particularly important not to limit suicide prevention efforts to individuals with depression or burnout but to also consider coping styles and emotion regulation strategies, which can be effectively targeted through specific curricula61,62 and gambling disorder prevention, as mentioned above.

We did not identify any protective factor independently associated with a reduced risk of suicidal ideation. Physical activity was not found to have a protective effect against suicidal behaviors in our sample after adjusting for mental health variables. However, we only assessed the frequency of physical activity, not its intensity. Oh et al.63 reported that engaging in more than one hour of physical activity per week reduced the prevalence of suicidal behaviors among medical students, with no effect of less than one hour. Our findings do not contradict these previous results, and it is possible that measuring the intensity of physical activity could have yielded different outcomes. In addition, connectedness to the school community was associated with a lower risk of suicidal ideation in univariate analyses, but was not retained in the multivariate model. This suggests that while school connectedness may be protective for mental health, as shown in previous studies, this effect may not be strong enough to protect against suicidal ideation when mental health is already compromised. Moreover, the measure used to assess connectedness could not discriminate the different dimensions of school connectedness social affiliation, school belonging, attitude about importance of school, or supportive learning environment64. In addition, it has been demonstrated that school connectedness could be a second line factor as compared to family connectedness in students65, which was not assessed in our study.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. While our sample is not fully representative of all medical students, nearly half of the eligible population participated, which is a notably high response rate for this type of survey. Typically, participation rates for similar studies are below 10%66,67. Nevertheless, there may be an overrepresentation of students facing difficulties in our sample or of students with relatives facing difficulties, who could have had personal motivation to participate and more sensitive to the theme of the survey. Moreover, this study was conducted at a single university, which may reflect a local situation. However, the rates of suicidal ideation, substance use disorder and depression are similar to the national and international rates recently reported, which supports our findings6,53. This study presents data from an online survey without confirmed clinical diagnoses. The use of the ninth item of the PHQ-9 to assess suicidal ideation has certain limitations, as it does not capture the severity or intentionality of suicidal thoughts, unlike dedicated instruments such as the Columbia–Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS)68. Nevertheless, In comparative analyses, the PHQ-9 suicide item showed substantial concordance with C-SSRS assessments, with areas under the ROC curve of 0.85 for suicidal ideation, and sensitivity and specificity around 83%69. Previous studies have demonstrated that this item has good predictive validity for suicidal attempts 35. These results support its clinical relevance as a brief, valid screening tool for suicidal ideation in large-scale surveys.”. As such, we cannot assert that individuals who screened positive for gambling disorder definitively met the criteria for a gambling disorder. However, a recent study confirmed the optimal cut-off score of 2 with very good sensitivity (82%) and specificity (93%) against a clinical interview with trained therapists70. The used cut-off of one could have led to false positives. We have no descriptive data on the gambling practice (online vs offline, type of games, gambling frequency or financial consequences) and further more detailed studies are warranted, using more detailed instruments.

Additionally, gender-based and sexual violence, which are also associated with suicidality, were not measured6. Another limitation of this study is that we used the DERS total score only, without exploring subscale differences, which may have limited the identification of more specific patterns of emotion regulation difficulties. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the reported data does not allow for causal inferences, highlighting the need for prospective studies.

Conclusion and clinical implications

Our study highlights the critical, yet underrecognized, association between probable gambling disorder, anxiety, and to a lesser extent emotional dysregulation and lower empathy, and suicidal ideation among second- to fourth-year medical students. Beyond traditional risk factors such as depression or burnout, behavioral addictions and emotional regulation difficulties appear to play a significant role in the emergence of suicidal thoughts in this vulnerable population.

These findings underline the urgent need to expand suicide prevention strategies beyond the conventional focus on depressive symptoms to include systematic screening for probable gambling disorder accompanied by brief interventions, increased awareness among pedagogical teams about this emerging addictive behavior, and the development of emotion regulation skills. Integrating targeted prevention programs within medical curricula could provide students with essential coping mechanisms and contribute to fostering a healthier future healthcare workforce.

Given the limitations of our study, including its cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported measures, further longitudinal research is needed to confirm these associations and to explore effective intervention strategies. Nonetheless, the elevated prevalence of gambling disorder and its strong association with suicidal ideation call for immediate attention from medical educators, health policymakers, and public health professionals.

Addressing behavioral addictions and strengthening psychosocial resilience among medical students represent critical steps toward reducing the risk of suicidality and promoting mental health within this key segment of the population.
