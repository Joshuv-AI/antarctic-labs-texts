Nomogram model for the diagnosis of solitary nodular pulmonary mucinous adenocarcinoma
Authors: Feiyang Zhong, Lijun Wu, Zhenxing Liu, Wenping Li, Shaohong Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study was to develop a nomogram model based on the natural progression of tumor and other radiological features to discriminate between solitary nodular pulmonary mucinous adenocarcinoma and non-mucinous adenocarcinomas. A retrospective analysis was conducted on 15,655 cases of lung adenocarcinoma diagnosed at our institution between January 2010 and June 2023. Primary nodular invasive mucinous adenocarcinomas and non-mucinous adenocarcinomas with at least two preoperative CT scans were included. These patients were randomly assigned to training and validation sets. Univariate and multivariate analyses were employed to compare tumor growth rates and clinical radiological characteristics between the two groups in the training set. A nomogram model was constructed based on the results of multivariate analysis. The diagnostic value of the model was evaluated in both the training and validation sets using calibration curves and receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC). The study included 174 patients, with 58 cases of mucinous adenocarcinoma and 116 cases of non-mucinous adenocarcinoma. The nomogram model incorporated the maximum tumor diameter, the consolidation/tumor ratio (CTR), and the specific growth rate (SGR) to generate individual scores for each patient, which were then accumulated to obtain a total score indicative of the likelihood of developing mucinous or non-mucinous adenocarcinoma. The model demonstrated excellent discriminative ability with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.784 for the training set and 0.833 for the testing set. The nomogram model developed in this study, integrating SGR with other radiological and clinical parameters, provides a valuable and accurate tool for differentiating between solitary nodular pulmonary mucinous adenocarcinoma and non-mucinous adenocarcinomas. This prognostic model offers a robust and objective basis for personalized management of patients with pulmonary adenocarcinomas.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most aggressive malignancies and currently remains the primary cause of cancer-related deaths among both males and females1. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for more than 80% of all lung cancer cases, with adenocarcinoma being the most prevalent subtype within NSCLC2. Mucinous adenocarcinoma is a rare histological subtype of lung adenocarcinoma, accounting for approximately 3–10% of all cases3,4. Compared to non-mucinous adenocarcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma exhibits significant differences in clinical, pathological, and genetic aspects with the presence of goblet and/or columnar tumor cells containing abundant intracytoplasmic mucin vacuoles4. It is associated with a higher likelihood of KARS mutations and demonstrates a higher frequency of airway dissemination and tumor recurrence, consequently leading to a different prognosis3,5–7. Therefore, early discrimination between mucinous adenocarcinoma and non-mucinous adenocarcinoma is crucial for guiding the selection of disease management strategies.

Mucinous adenocarcinoma manifests radiographically as either a pneumonic or a solitary pattern8. Prior research has indicated that the solitary form of mucinous adenocarcinoma is more prevalent than the pneumonic type, and its radiographic presentations overlap with those of non-mucinous adenocarcinoma, posing diagnostic challenges8,9. Surgical resection or biopsy remains the method for establishing a definitive diagnosis. However, due to the rarity of mucinous adenocarcinoma cases, traditional preoperative noninvasive diagnostic modalities, such as subjective radiological interpretation, often face difficulties in accurately differentiating solitary nodular mucinous adenocarcinoma from non-mucinous adenocarcinoma. Consequently, there is an urgent need for noninvasive imaging techniques that can aid in the differentiation between solitary nodular mucinous adenocarcinoma and non-mucinous adenocarcinoma.

The growth rate of tumors has emerged as a promising imaging biomarker for assessing tumor aggressiveness and growth kinetics in malignancies10–12. Tumor volume doubling time (VDT), defined as the time it takes for a tumor to double in volume, reflects the underlying biological behavior of the tumor and has been shown to correlate with histopathological features and clinical outcomes. While VDT has been extensively studied in cancer, its utility in discriminating between mucinous and nonmucinous adenocarcinomas remains relatively unexplored. Meanwhile, specific growth rate (SGR) has been demonstrated to more accurately and intuitively represent tumor growth dynamics13. In clinical observations, we have noted that mucinous adenocarcinoma often exhibits characteristics of indolent growth. We hypothesize that mucinous adenocarcinomas, characterized by their inert growth, would exhibit a slower growth rate compared to nonmucinous adenocarcinomas. This manuscript aims to investigate the potential of growth rate as a non-invasive imaging biomarker for discriminating between pulmonary mucinous and non-mucinous adenocarcinomas.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (approval number: S2022-177-01, March 31, 2022). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital owing to the retrospective nature of this study. This study retrospectively analyzed a total of 15,655 cases of pulmonary adenocarcinoma diagnosed at our hospital between January 2010 and June 2023. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histopathologically confirmed primary pulmonary invasive mucinous and non-mucinous adenocarcinoma, (2) nodules presenting as solitary nodules less than 3cm, (3) availability of at least two preoperative CT examinations with a slice thickness of 1 or 1.25 mm, and a minimum time interval of 20 days between the two examinations, and (4) complete clinical and pathological data. Exclusion criteria included (1) CT images of insufficient quality for diagnosis and (2) prior antitumor treatment before CT examination. Ultimately, 58 cases of mucinous adenocarcinoma and 2323 cases of non-mucinous adenocarcinoma were included in the study. To ensure data balance, a random number was assigned to each case in the non-mucinous adenocarcinoma group, and the cases were ranked according to these random numbers. Subsequently, 116 cases were randomly selected from the non-mucinous adenocarcinoma group at a ratio of 1:2 to match the number of mucinous adenocarcinoma cases. These patients were randomly allocated to the training group (105 cases; 41 mucinous adenocarcinoma, 64 non-mucinous adenocarcinoma) and the testing group (69 cases; 17 mucinous adenocarcinoma, 52 non-mucinous adenocarcinoma) in a ratio of 6:4.

CT scanning

The CT scans were performed using either the Brilliance 256i CT (Philips, Netherlands) or the Optima CT660 (GE, USA). All patients underwent chest CT scans during an inspiratory breath-hold in the supine position. The scanning range was from lung apices to costo-phrenic angles. CT scanning parameters were as follows: voltage 120 kV; automatic tube current; pitch, 0.993; image matrix, 512 × 512; the reconstructed slice thickness, 1.00 mm or 1.25 mm. All images were reviewed in lung window setting (width, 1500 HU; level, −600 HU).

Clinical data collection and radiological characteristics evaluation

The clinical data including gender, age, smoking history, history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), previous history of malignancy, family history of tumors and postoperative histopathological results were extracted from electronic medical records. Two study investigators performed a comprehensive and independent review of the data collection forms to ensure the utmost accuracy and reliability of the gathered information.

CT images for the last time before operation were selected. Two chest radiologists, each with 6 and 8 years of experience in thoracic imaging, independently evaluated the CT imaging features on a Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) without knowledge of the corresponding pathological results. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The CT imaging features included the maximum tumor diameter, the maximum consolidation diameter, tumor shape (spherical, irregular), tumor lung interface (well-defined, indistinct), cavitation, spiculation, lobulation, air bronchogram, vascular convergence sign, pleural retraction, lobar location, and enlarged mediastinal lymph nodes. The consolidation/tumor ratio (CTR) was calculated as the ratio of the maximum consolidation diameter to the maximum tumor diameter.

Measurement of natural process of tumor

The DICOM images were imported into 3D-Slicer and reviewed in a lung window setting. A thoracic radiologist with 6 years of experience manually segmented the regions of interest (ROI) corresponding to the tumors, another senior radiologist with 30 years of experience reviewed all tumor segmentation results and obtained the tumor volume. The VDT were calculated using the modified Schwartz formula based on an exponential growth model, as follows: VDT = (ln2 × T)/(ln [Vt/V0]), where T represents the time interval between the initial and follow-up CT scans, V0 is the initial tumor volume, and Vt is the follow-up tumor volume14,15. During univariate and multivariable analyses, the SGR was used as a surrogate measure of lung adenocarcinoma growth instead of VDT, following the formula: SGR = ln2/VDT16. SGR has been shown to more accurately and intuitively represent tumor growth, even in cases where VDT is negative.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians (interquartile range) and compared using either the t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on whether the continuous variables followed a normal distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. Univariate analysis was conducted to identify factors that exhibited significant differences, which were then incorporated into a multivariable logistic regression analysis. The final nomogram model, constructed from variables that were statistically significant in the multivariable analysis, was designed to provide a clinically interpretable tool for predicting outcomes. Internal validation of this model was achieved through bootstrapping17, a resampling technique that enhances the reliability and generalizability of the model by reducing the potential for overfitting. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the predictive model in discriminating between mucinous adenocarcinoma and non-mucinous adenocarcinoma. In addition, decision curve analysis (DCA)18 was employed to evaluate the clinical utility of the model. This approach assesses the net benefit of a predictive model at different threshold probabilities, providing a pragmatic perspective on how the model might be applied in clinical practice. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.6.3. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Human ethical and consent to participate declarations

This retrospective study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (approval number: S2022-177-01). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital owing to the retrospective nature of this study.

Results

Patient information

Among the 174 patients included in this study, there were 96 females (55.1%) and 78 males (44.8%) with the median age of 59.50 years (range: 30–79 years) and median tumor size of 15.90 mm (range: 5.00–39.50 mm). The scan interval for the 58 patients with mucinous adenocarcinoma ranged from 22 to 832 days, while for the 116 patients with non-mucinous adenocarcinoma from 24 to 1994 days. Mucinous adenocarcinoma manifested a greater abundance of characteristics associated with an indolent growth pattern, with a higher probability of negative values for the VDT compared to non-mucinous adenocarcinoma (39.6% vs. 29.3%). In both training and validation datasets, the distribution of SGR between mucinous and non-mucinous adenocarcinomas is depicted in Fig. 1, showing significantly lower SGR values for mucinous adenocarcinomas. Additionally, mucinous adenocarcinoma patients had a higher prevalence of smoking history (P = 0.045), larger tumor diameter (P = 0.012), solid component diameter (P < 0.001), and CTR (P < 0.001). They were also more likely to exhibit indistinct tumor-lung interface (P = 0.004), vascular convergence sign (P = 0.041), and less likely to show spiculation (P = 0.041). Nomogram model construction and validation

The variables selected from the multivariable regression analysis were incorporated into the final nomogram prediction model (Fig. 2a). For each patient, the maximum tumor diameter, CTR, and SGR contributed to generating individual scores, which were then accumulated to obtain a total score corresponding to the likelihood of developing mucinous adenocarcinoma or non-mucinous adenocarcinoma. To assess the stability of the nomogram, 1000 bootstrapped resamples were performed on the original dataset, generating a calibration curve, and an excellent concordance between the predicted values of the model and the observed outcomes was demonstrated (Fig. 2b and c). The ROC curve analysis revealed that the prediction model exhibited good discriminative ability in the training set (AUC: 0.784; 95% CI: 0.70–0.87) (Fig. 3a), and this performance was validated in the testing set (AUC: 0.833; 95% CI: 0.72–0.95) (Fig. 3b). Discussion

Due to the rarity of pulmonary mucinous adenocarcinoma, prognostic outcomes for affected individuals remain somewhat obscure19. Woo et al. have observed that individuals afflicted with invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma demonstrate recurrence-free survival (RFS) that is analogous to patients with lung adenocarcinoma classified as grade 2 by the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC). Furthermore, their overall survival (OS) is noted to be intermediate between the survival rates of IASLC grades 2 and 3 adenocarcinomas5. In a separate report, Matsui et al. have documented a heightened incidence of intra-pulmonary recurrence among mucinous adenocarcinoma cases when compared with invasive non-mucinous adenocarcinoma6. Lee and associates have posited that the overall survival of mucinous adenocarcinoma is congruent with that observed in acinar/papillary predominant adenocarcinoma9. Conversely, the research conducted by Xu et al. illustrates that pulmonary mucinous adenocarcinoma patients enjoy a more favorable overall survival profile in comparison to their counterparts with matched non-mucinous adenocarcinomas20. Nevertheless, surgical intervention remains the primary therapeutic choice for early-stage pulmonary mucinous adenocarcinoma patients21. Hence, early diagnosis of solitary pulmonary mucinous adenocarcinoma holds paramount importance for the selection of surgical methods, in particular, radiofrequency ablation is considered unsuitable for mucinous adenocarcinoma. Our findings indicate significant disparities in SGR between these two histological subtypes of lung adenocarcinoma, with mucinous adenocarcinomas demonstrating a notably slower growth rate compared to the non-mucinous counterparts, an observation consistent with our clinical experience. Integration of SGR into predictive nomogram models further enhances the model's ability to differentiate between mucinous and non-mucinous adenocarcinoma subtypes. By incorporating SGR with other clinical radiological parameters, including tumor maximum diameter and CTR, our nomogram provides a comprehensive tool for risk stratification and individualized patient management. The clinical implications of implementing our nomogram model in real-world practice are profound. By providing a quantitative assessment of the likelihood of developing mucinous versus non-mucinous adenocarcinoma, clinicians can make more informed decisions regarding patient management.

The disparity in SGR between pulmonary mucinous and non-mucinous adenocarcinomas reflected their inherent biological distinctions. Mucinous adenocarcinomas are characterized by increased intracellular and extracellular secretion of mucin, juxtaposed with a relatively lower density of tumor cells within the lesion compared to non-mucinous adenocarcinomas22. This pathophysiological distinction potentially accounts for the observed slower proliferative pace of mucinous adenocarcinomas relative to non-mucinous counterparts of similar dimensions. Notably, our study revealed a higher frequency of negative values for tumor VDT in mucinous adenocarcinomas, potentially attributed to diminished mucinous content within the tumor lesion.

In our study, the mucinous adenocarcinomas exhibited larger diameters and higher CTR on radiological imaging, aligning with previous research23. Pathological investigations indicate that mucinous adenocarcinomas arise from the alveolar wall's goblet and columnar epithelial cells, which are prolific in mucin production24. These malignant cells, accompanied by copious mucin, breach the alveolar barriers and propagate along the airway to distant pulmonary locales25. This process may account for the larger size and elevated CTR often observed in mucinous adenocarcinomas. Mucinous adenocarcinomas typically undergo mucinous differentiation in the early stages of carcinogenesis26, thereby rendering the tumor-lung interface more prone to blurring. However, in our investigation, this characteristic did not manifest statistically significant differences when compared to non-mucinous adenocarcinomas, potentially attributable to the small sample size. The mucin production may be modulated by the influence of TTF-1 and ALK genes. Yutaka and colleagues' animal model studies have illustrated that tall columnar neoplastic cells exhibiting TTF-1 negativity or weak positivity are associated with enhanced mucin biosynthesis27. In a complementary vein, Han et al. have delineated that the mutational landscape of the ALK gene is heterogeneous among adenocarcinoma subtypes, with mucinous adenocarcinomas being characterized by a higher prevalence of ALK mutations28.

Our study is subject to certain limitations. Firstly, inconsistencies in follow-up duration, CT scanning techniques, and reconstruction algorithms may affect the calculation of tumor growth rate. Due to limitations in sample size, we were unable to control for these factors, and further multicenter studies with larger sample sizes may help address this issue. This initiative also serves to mitigate selection bias and validate the generalizability of our model. Secondly, the determination of imaging features relied on the assessment of radiologists with different expertise, introducing inherent subjective factors. Future endeavors may explore the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) to identify internal tumor characteristics and combine them with growth rate data for pathological subtype prediction. AI has the potential to significantly reduce the heterogeneity in diagnostic assessments that may stem from the differing skill levels and states of fatigue among radiologists. However, the broader implementation of AI in medical imaging also confronts challenges associated with the variability in imaging equipment and scanning protocols. Moreover, our study focused solely on the comparison between solitary pulmonary mucinous and non-mucinous adenocarcinomas. Future investigations incorporating a broader spectrum of pulmonary lesions encountered in real-world scenarios, such as benign granulomas, small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC), and squamous cell carcinoma, could better inform clinical practice.

In conclusion, tumor growth rate stands as a reliable discriminator between pulmonary mucinous and non-mucinous adenocarcinomas. The integration of features such as tumor size, CTR and SGR into a comprehensive diagnostic model facilitates the quantitative assessment of tumor pathological subtypes. The predictive capabilities of this model could have significant implications for clinical decision-making. By providing a more nuanced understanding of a patient's disease profile, clinicians can tailor treatment strategies more effectively. Certainly, the adoption of any new diagnostic tool in clinical practice requires careful consideration of its cost-effectiveness and integration with existing healthcare systems. Future studies should assess the economic implications of implementing our model and explore strategies to facilitate its integration into routine clinical workflows.
