Evaluation of model performance in predicting sepsis after intestinal obstruction surgery: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Chaojin Chen, Jirong Yang, Lei Zhang, Pinjie Huang, Danyang Li, Jinyan Xu, Dong Yang, Haihua Shu, Shaoli Zhou, Ziqing Hei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIntestinal obstruction surgery is a high-risk procedure associated with postoperative sepsis. In this multicenter retrospective study, we aimed to employ machine-learning methods to predict sepsis after intestinal obstruction surgery and visualize its driving mechanism to assist clinical decision-making.MethodsThe study included patients who underwent surgery for intestinal obstruction at three medical centers between 2013 and 2021, as well as patients with intestinal obstruction from the publicly available perioperative dataset INSPIRE. Various algorithms, including logistic regression, support vector classification (SVC), the Gaussian Bayesian classifier, gradient boosting machine, random forest, adaptive boosting, and multilayer perceptron, were tested. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and the SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) values were utilized for model interpretation.ResultsPostoperative sepsis was observed in 60 patients (16.17%), 10 patients (20%), and 10 patients (15.15%) in three medical centers, as well as 7 patients (12.73%) in INSPIRE dataset. In the internal validation set, the SVC algorithm demonstrated favorable performance, with an AUC value of 0.876 (95%CI 0.799–0.939). The model was well-calibrated, with a Brier score of 0.108 and a non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p = 0.183). The SHAP analysis identified six influential features in the model: age, POSSUM physiology score, ASA classification, shock index at the end of surgery, intraoperative total volume of infusion, and intraoperative dose of furosemide. External validation sets showed AUC values of 0.873, 0.896, 0.926, and 0.879, respectively.ConclusionWe have developed a highly interoperable sepsis risk calculator based on the SVC model to assist postoperative clinical decision-making in patients with intestinal obstruction.Keywords: Machine-learning, intestinal obstruction surgery, sepsis, SHapley Additive exPlanations, risk factors

Background

Intestinal obstruction (IO) is a common surgical emergency worldwide, associated with high morbidity rates of 20–30% and mortality rates approaching 10% [1,2]. Recent studies show that from 1990 and 2019, the global age-standardized prevalence and incidence of bowel obstruction increased by 56.91 and 86.67%, respectively. While mortality rates continued to decline, the number of deaths rose significantly, largely due to population aging and other factors [3]. Despite ongoing research into postoperative outcomes and preoperative risk factors in abdominal emergencies, the incidence of complications following IO surgery remains high, ranging from 23 to 28% [2,4]. Among these, postoperative sepsis is one of the most serious complications and represents a major public health challenge due to its high morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [5]. Patients with IO are prone to a series of pathophysiological changes resulting from complex perioperative factors, such as ischemia-reperfusion injury [6], impaired intestinal barrier function, bacterial translocation [7,8], and altered bacterial diversity in the obstructed segment [9], particularly following surgical trauma and anesthesia. These factors contribute to making IO surgery a high-risk procedure for postoperative sepsis [1]. Studies report that sepsis occurs in up to 20.19% of patients after IO surgery, and sepsis-related deaths account for ∼93.8% of all postoperative deaths [10]. Moreover, each hour of delay in initiating effective antimicrobial therapy is associated with a 7.6% increase in mortality [11]. Hence, early prediction of postoperative sepsis is crucial for these patients.

Several scoring systems have been developed for the early prediction and diagnosis of sepsis, including Quick Sepsis-related Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS), consensus definitions (Sepsis-3), and National Early Warning Score (NEWS). However, none of these techniques satisfactorily balance sensitivity and specificity [12]. Although certain biomarkers have been found to be significantly correlated with postoperative sepsis in IO patients, their predictive performance remains inadequate [13]. Moreover, there is a notable lack of multicenter data for both developing and externally validating sepsis prediction models. Even widely used proprietary models have recently demonstrated poor performance in external validation [14]. In addition, risk factors for sepsis differ considerably between surgical and non-surgical patients, as well as across surgical types. Consequently, an accurate predictive model specifically for sepsis following IO surgery is still needed.

Therefore, this study aims to develop and externally validate a reliable predictive model for sepsis following IO surgery, and to present it as an online risk calculator. Using advanced machine learning (ML) algorithms, we retrospectively analyzed perioperative data from three medical centers and a publicly available dataset, INSPIRE.

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (NO. [2022] 02-004-02), with a waiver of informed consent and clinical trial registration requirements. This study adhered to the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis) guidelines.

Data extraction and study population

This multicenter retrospective study extracted data from the Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems of three medical centers: the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (371 patients treated between April 2013 and April 2021), Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (50 patients from 2021 to 2022), and Foshan First People’s Hospital (66 patients from 2020 to 2022). Additionally, data from the publicly available perioperative dataset INSPIRE were included as another external validation set (55 patients from 2011 to 2020). INSPIRE dataset is a publicly available resource in perioperative medicine, containing ∼130,000 cases (representing 50% of all surgical procedures) that underwent anesthesia at an academic institution in South Korea between 2011 and 2020 [15,16]. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of IO, including colorectal or small IO; and (2) undergoing surgery to relieve the obstruction. The exclusion criteria included: (1) age under 18 years; (2) previous history of IO surgery; (3) pregnancy; (4) inadequate or insufficiently related medical records; and (5) preoperative sepsis.

Eligible patients from the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University were randomly divided into a 70% training set for model development and a 30% internal validation set. Patients from Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, Foshan First People’s Hospital, and the INSPIRE dataset served as external validation set 1, set 2, and set 3, respectively. Finally, all eligible patients from the three centers and the INSPIRE dataset were combined to form the validation set 4.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of our study was to develop a machine learning model capable of predicting sepsis within 7 days after IO surgery. Postoperative sepsis was diagnosed according to the sepsis-3.0 criteria, as established in the 2016 Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) guidelines [17]. We collected daily SOFA score for each patient, which is a validated tool for assessing organ dysfunction in critically ill patients. The daily SOFA scores were manually reviewed using the electronic medical record (EMR) system. The baseline SOFA score was defined as the score on the day of surgery. An acute increase of ≥2 points in the total SOFA score attributed to infection was considered indicative of organ dysfunction [18].

Variable selection

Based on a review of previous literature and the specific context of our hospital, 127 variables were initially selected for univariate analysis. These encompassed demographics, preoperative comorbidities, laboratory values, causes and complications of IO, intraoperative events, shock index (SI), medication, and fluid administration (Table S1). Certain categorical variables were defined according to specific clinical criteria.

Univariate analysis was first performed on all 127 variables to assess their association with postoperative sepsis. Variables with a significance level of p < 0.05 were retained for subsequent feature selection. To refine the variable set and mitigate overfitting, we employed two approaches: the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and a wrapper method based on SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations). LASSO regression identified the most influential predictors, while the SHAP-based method evaluated each variable’s contribution to model performance. The final set of predictors selected through these techniques was used to construct the machine learning models for predicting sepsis following IO surgery.

Development of the ML model

Seven machine learning algorithms were compared, including logistic regression (LR), the Gaussian Bayesian classifier (GNB), support vector classification (SVC), gradient boosting machine (GBM) based on decision tree, adaptive boosting (ADA), random forest (RF), and multilayer perceptron (MLP). The Scikit-learn package was used to establish all the above models. The discrimination performance of each model was evaluated on the internal validation set using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score, with 95% confidence intervals (CI) estimated via 1000 bootstrap samples. Calibration was assessed using calibration curves, Brier scores, and Hosmer-Lemeshow tests. The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method was applied to interpret the optimal model by quantifying feature importance and explaining individual predictions.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as number (frequency). For univariate analyses, independent sample t-tests were used for normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normal distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when cell counts were below five. Missing categorical data were imputed using the mode, and continuous variables were imputed using the K-nearest neighbors algorithm. Python (version 3.9.12) was used as the base platform and the dependent packages included Scipy package (version 1.7.1), Sklearn software package (version 1.1.2), and Pandas (version 1.3.5).

Results

Study cohorts

A total of 396 patients who underwent IO surgery at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between October 2013 and April 2021 were included. After excluding 25 patients based on the exclusion criteria (Figure 1), 371 patients were included in the development cohort. Among them, 60 (16.17%) developed postoperative sepsis. External validation set 1 consisted of 50 patients from Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, while external validation set 2 included 66 patients from Foshan First People’s Hospital. From the INSPIRE dataset, which contains nearly 130,000 cases, 853 patients were identified with IO. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 55 patients with complete variables required for the model were included as external validation set 3. The combined cohort of 542 eligible patients from all four datasets formed validation set 4.

Differences in characteristics and prognosis between sepsis and non-sepsis groups

The demographic and perioperative variables of the development cohort are listed in Table 1. Among the 371 patients, 60 (16.17%) developed postoperative sepsis (Group 1: sepsis group), and 16 (4.31%) died after surgery. Compared to the non-sepsis group, patients with sepsis had significantly higher POSSUM physiological score (median 25.5 vs. 18, p < 0.001) and POSSUM surgical score (median 19 vs. 16, p < 0.001). The sepsis group also exhibited a shorter time from admission to operation (median 1 vs. 5 days, p = 0.003) and a shorter total morbidity (median 7 vs. 12 days, p = 0.007). However, no significant difference was observed in the time from onset to admission (median 4 vs. 5 days, p = 0.058). Preoperative laboratory values, including white blood cell count (WBC) (median 10.17 vs. 8.04 *10^12, p = 0.008), neutrophil percentage (NEUT%) (median 0.8 vs. 0.73, p < 0.001), and C-reactive protein (CRP) (median 68.3 vs. 11.1, p = 0.002) were significantly higher in the sepsis group. There were no significant differences in the degree (p = 0.104) or site (p = 0.076) of obstruction between groups. However, the cause (p < 0.001), mechanism (p < 0.001), and the intestinal status (p < 0.001) of obstruction were significantly associated with sepsis.

Characteristics of the study patients.

Classification variables are expressed as the frequency (scale). Continuous variables are expressed as the median (interquartile range). A total of 127 items were included in the single factor analysis. The comparison between the two groups was statistically significant (p < 0.05). There were 78 data items, including 48 preoperative variables and 30 intraoperative variables.

Intraoperatively, the sepsis group required greater volumes of crystalloid, colloids, sodium bicarbonate, red blood cells (RBC), plasma, and cryoprecipitate (all p < 0.05). Vasopressor use was also more frequent in the sepsis group (66.67 vs. 39.22%, p < 0.001), specifically adrenaline (20.00 vs. 4.82%, p < 0.001) and norepinephrine (58.33 vs. 30.23%, p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in dopamine use between groups (20 vs. 12.22%, p = 0.159). The sepsis group exhibited a higher intraoperative shock index (p < 0.05) and a higher incidence of prolonged hypotension (55 vs. 27.33%, p < 0.001), reflecting greater hemodynamic instability.

Postoperative outcomes were significantly worse in the sepsis group, including longer time to first defecation, extended drainage tube retention, higher total hospitalization costs, longer total and postoperative hospital stays, and increased ICU length of stay (all p < 0.001, Table 1).

Variable selection

A total of 127 variables were initially included in the univariate analysis, including 83 preoperative and 44 intraoperative characteristics. Among these, 78 demonstrated significant associations (p < 0.05) and were retained for further analysis. Following LASSO regression, 15 variables remained. These subsequently underwent a wrapped feature selection method. Ultimately, the top 6 features with the highest SHAP values and greatest influence on model performance were selected (Figure S1). These key features include age, POSSUM Physiology Score, ASA classification, shock index at the end of the surgery, intraoperative total volume of infusion, and intraoperative dose of furosemide.

Model construction and internal validation

As the positive and negative sample sizes were seriously unbalanced (1:5), the EasyEnsemble method was employed during training to conduct balanced sampling. Among the seven ML models evaluated, the GBM achieved the highest AUC value of 0.883 (95%CI 0.827–0.942). However, its sensitivity (0.724, 95%CI 0.5–0.929), specificity (0.843, 95%CI 0.755–0.915), accuracy (0.823, 95%CI 0.736–0.902), and F1 value (0.564, 95%CI 0.395–0.741) were inferior to those of the SVC. The SVC demonstrated more balanced performance across all metrics, with an AUC of 0.876 (95%CI 0.799–0.939), well-balanced specificity (0.873, 95%CI 0.795–0.937) and sensitivity (0.734, 95%CI 0.516–0.916), as well as the highest accuracy (0.851, 95%CI 0.79–0.906) and F1 value (0.597, 95%CI 0.423–0.762). Therefore, SVC was selected as the final model for subsequent analysis and application (Figures 2A,D). Calibration performance of the SVC model was satisfactory, with a Brier score of 0.108, an Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 0.035, and a non-significant Hosmer–Lemeshow test result (p = 0.183), indicating good model calibration (Figure 2C).

External validation performance

Table 2 presents the performance of the SVC model across the four external validation cohorts. The incidence of postoperative sepsis in the external validation sets 1, 2, 3, and 4 was 20, 15.15, 12.73, and 16.05%, respectively. The AUC of SVC model in the external validation sets 1 and 4 (0.873, 95%CI 0.670–0.995; 0.863, 95%CI 0.832–0.892) were slightly lower but comparable to that of the training set (0.876, 95%CI 0.799–0.939). In contrast, the AUC values for validation sets 2 and 3 were higher (0.896, 95%CI 0.792–0.974; 0.926, 95%CI 0.793–0.995) than that of the internal validation (Figure 2B). In addition, all four external validation sets exhibited comparable accuracy (0.880, 0.879, 0.836, 0.791 vs. 0.861), specificity (0.902, 0.895, 0.837, 0.780 vs. 0.873), F1 value (0.727, 0.667, 0.571, 0.543 vs. 0.597) and higher sensitivity (0.800, 0.818, 0.857, 0.854 vs. 0.734) compared to the internal validation set. These results indicate that the SVC model generalizes well to external datasets (Figure 2D).

Feature importance evaluated by SHAP values

The baseline Shapley value was set as the average predicted probability of sepsis in the training set, which was 28.29%. The SHAP summary plot identified the following six most important features: age, POSSUM Physiology Score, ASA classification, shock index at the end of the surgery, intraoperative total volume of infusion, and intraoperative dose of furosemide (Figure 3A). Both the SHAP summary plot and SHAP dependence plot indicated a positive correlation between these features and higher SHAP values in the SVC model. That is, increased age, elevated POSSUM Physiology Score, higher ASA grade, a higher Shock index at the end of the surgery, larger volume of intraoperative infusion, and a higher dose of intraoperative furosemide were all associated with an increased probability of sepsis following IO surgery (Figures 3A–C, Figure S2).

(E) SHAP decision plot of the incorrectly classified cases. (F) SHAP force plot of the correctly classified cases. (G) SHAP force plot of the incorrectly classified cases.

The SHAP decision plot and force plot improved the interpretability and transparency of the SVC model’s predictions. Figures 3D–G demonstrated examples of correctly and incorrectly classified cases. In the SHAP decision plots, features were presented sequentially based on their availability in EMRs, effectively simulating the clinical decision process. The force plot visually illustrates the key contributors to the final model output for an individual, allowing for an intuitive understanding of the prediction.

We visualized the model and created a web-based online risk calculator (https://analysis.aidcloud.cn/cn/sunyatsen/). By entering the values of the predictive variables, users can estimate an individual patient’s risk of sepsis following IO surgery. Discussion

Sepsis is a significant healthcare concern, particularly among surgical patients, as it contributes to high morbidity and mortality rates [5,19,20]. Patients with IO are especially susceptible to sepsis due to accompanying factors, such as fever, internal environmental disorders, infection, poisoning, shock, and other conditions [8,21]. Early detection, diagnosis, and intervention are crucial for improving the prognosis of sepsis patients [22–24]. However, effective tools for early diagnosis and prediction of sepsis following IO surgery remain scarce. In this study, we evaluated the predictive capabilities of seven ML models, including LR, SVC, GNB, RF, GBM, ADA, and MLP, to predict sepsis after IO surgery, utilizing data from three medical centers and a publicly available perioperative dataset. Although the GBM model achieved a slightly higher AUC (0.883 vs. 0.876), we ultimately selected the SVC model. First, while GBM had a higher AUC, its sensitivity (0.724) was lower than that of SVC (0.734), and its F1-score (0.564) was also lower than that of SVC (0.597). In clinical practice, especially for sepsis prediction, high sensitivity is critical to avoid missing true positive cases. The SVC model provided a more balanced and clinically reliable performance. Importantly, SVC’s maximum-margin principle inherently guards against overfitting. SVC requires fewer hyperparameter tuning steps compared to GBM, making it more suitable for clinical deployment. Furthermore, the model demonstrates potential reproducibility and generalizability to new and diverse patients, as evidenced by its consistent performance across four external validation sets.

The rapid progress in machine learning has created new opportunities for improved clinical decision support [25]. By enabling prediction of clinically relevant events, enhance clinician awareness, aiding diagnosis, and offer guidance, ML models are increasingly valuable in clinical practice [26]. While the underlying models may not be entirely novel, advancements in processor performance and widespread digitalization of healthcare data have significantly accelerated progress in this field [25]. ML offers flexibility and scalability, making it applicable to various medical practices, including risk stratification, disease diagnosis, and outcome prediction [27]. A major advantage of ML algorithms is their ability to analyze diverse data types and incorporate multiple variables into predictions related to disease risk, diagnosis, prognosis, and treatments [28]. Altogether, ML provides a powerful tool for assimilating and evaluating large amounts of complex healthcare data [29]. In our study, we utilized these advantages to develop an SVC model for predicting sepsis after IO surgery. The model demonstrated potential reliability and generalizability across multiple medical centers.

In our study, six factors were identified as significantly associated with sepsis following IO surgery: age, POSSUM Physiology Score, ASA classification, SI at the end of the surgery, intraoperative total volume of infusion, and intraoperative dose of furosemide. Advanced age impairs immune response and tissue repair [30] and is a well-established risk factor for postoperative infections [31,32]. The POSSUM physiological score reflects a patient’s overall condition, encompassing age, vital signs, blood chemistry, mental status, and cardiac disease. Previous studies have validated it as a valid prognostic factor for patients undergoing emergency abdominal surgery [33,34]. ASA classification reflects pre-existing comorbidities that compromise host defenses and is a significant predictor of postoperative outcomes [35,36]. Evidence shows a correlation between ASA class and postoperative infections rates [37], and between ASA class ≥ IV and mortality in patients with abdominal sepsis [38]. Age [39,40], ASA classification [2,41], and POSSUM physiological score [33,34] have all been recognized as independent risk factors in IO surgery. By incorporating these established risk factors along with the novel indicators of SI, intraoperative fluid infusion, and furosemide dose, our model offers a comprehensive and robust approach to predicting sepsis because they reflect the patient’s intraoperative circulatory status. Growing evidence suggests that SI holds advantages over other vital signs in evaluating global perfusion, determining the timing for vasoactive drug administration, and predicting prognosis [42–45]. Several studies further indicate that SI possesses potential value for predicting sepsis onset [46,47]. An elevated SI at the end of surgery often indicates circulatory dysfunction and heightened sepsis risk. Composite scores like the POSSUM physiology score and calculated indices like the SI are not yet part of the routine documentation in many EHR systems, especially in non-academic or resource-limited settings. However, the SI and POSSUM physiological score are derived from clinically routine indicators, including pulse rate, systolic blood pressure, age, routine laboratory tests, routine imaging examination, etc. Although they may not be directly accessible, their use is usually not restricted. Our model has also undergone multicenter validation, proving that it is equally applicable to other centers. Furthermore, intraoperative fluid volume is a critical indicator of sepsis risk. High fluid requirements may signal intravascular depletion, marking patients as high-risk. Conversely, excessive fluid resuscitation can dilute regulatory factors, promote edema, and disturb microcirculation [48,49], while insufficient fluid resuscitation can worsen ischemia. Both scenarios elevate sepsis risk in intestinal surgery. Finally, intraoperative administration of furosemide may indicate fluid overload or tissue edema requiring intervention, and the resulting furosemide-induced volume depletion can paradoxically exacerbate endotoxin-mediated injury [50].

This study has several notable strengths. Firstly, recognizing that the requirement for sepsis prediction tools depends on the target population, we developed a model specifically for patients undergoing IO surgery. This focus enabled the creation of a reliable and specialized tool for the early prediction, identification, and diagnosis of postoperative sepsis in this group, improving both accuracy and clinical applicability. Secondly, the study prioritizes interpretability, which is crucial for establishing trust in clinical decision support systems [51]. Using Shapley value, we assessed feature importance and provided individualized explanations of the model’s decision-making process, helping clinicians understand the reasoning behind its outputs and supporting clinical decision-making [52]. In addition, the model was converted into an online tool that can be directly used by clinical doctors, which is a key step in the clinical application and prospective validation of the predictive model. Finally, the external validation of the SVC model using patients from two additional independent medical centers and a publicly available perioperative dataset, as well as a merged cohort, enhances the generalizability and reliability of the findings. The fact that the model performed well across multiple datasets from different medical centers highlights its high external validity.

We also acknowledge some limitations in our study. Firstly, this is a retrospective study, which carries an inherent risk of selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Although we employed advanced imputation techniques for missing data, data completeness was reliant on electronic health records not originally designed for research. Furthermore, factors not routinely captured in our databases could influence the risk of sepsis and represent sources of unmeasured confounding. Secondly, despite encouraging performance, this model requires validation in a large, prospective, multicenter study to definitively assess its clinical effectiveness, impact on clinician behavior, and ultimately, its ability to improve patient outcomes before it can be recommended for routine clinical use. In addition, sepsis was only evaluated within 7 days after surgery in this study, so the model cannot predict delayed sepsis.

Conclusion

We have successfully developed and externally validated a risk prediction model for postoperative sepsis following IO, based on the SVC algorithm. This model has been translated into a publicly accessible, web-based risk calculator. Clinicians can input six readily available pre- and intra-operative variables into the calculator. Based on the prediction results and associated risk factors, the tool provides timely guidance for personalized therapeutic interventions. However, larger prospective validation studies are still needed in the future to determine the clinical effectiveness and predictive accuracy of this model.
