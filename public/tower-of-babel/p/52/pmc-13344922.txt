Monte Carlo-based Design and Comparative Evaluation of Intraoperative Radiotherapy Disks
Authors: Mahnaz Bagheri, Hussein Hameed Al Shareefi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Intraoperative radiotherapy (IORT) is a technique in which a single high radiation dose (10–20 Gy) is delivered directly to the tumor site during surgery. As this procedure is performed in the operating room, effective protection of the surrounding healthy tissues is of paramount importance. This protection is commonly achieved using shielding disks placed immediately adjacent to the tumor bed, where the material composition of these disks plays a crucial role in determining their shielding effectiveness.Aims and Objectives:The present study aimed to identify the optimal material combination and thickness for double-layer protective disks in order to maximize healthy tissue protection.Materials and Methods:Initially, the LIAC accelerator head, along with its applicator and a water phantom, was modeled using the MCNPX Monte Carlo code. The accuracy of the simulation was validated by comparing the percentage depth dose (PDD) obtained from Monte Carlo simulations with experimental dosimetry data. Optimization was performed by evaluating the transmission factor (TF), backscatter factor (BSF), and absorbed dose. Several new disk configurations—comprising PMMA + lead, PTFE + bismuth, steel + titanium, steel + copper, aluminum + copper, and aluminum + titanium—each with thicknesses of 6 mm and 8 mm, were simulated and compared with a reference disk.Results and Conclusion:Among all evaluated configurations, the PMMA + lead disk demonstrated the highest attenuation (65.8% at 8 mm thickness), along with the lowest BSF (7.1%) and TF (62.8%), making it the most effective option for protecting healthy tissues.

INTRODUCTION

Intraoperative radiotherapy (IORT) is a specialized radiotherapy technique in which a single high radiation dose – typically in the range of 10–20 Gy – is delivered directly to the tumor bed during surgery.[12] This approach enables precise targeting of the tumor site while minimizing radiation exposure to surrounding healthy tissues, as treatment is performed under direct surgical visualization in the operating room.[3] IORT can be delivered using either low-energy X-ray systems (low-kV IORT) or high-dose-rate electron sources (HDR-IORT), each offering specific advantages depending on tumor location, depth, and clinical requirements.[4] In the present study, the LIAC mobile electron linear accelerator (Sordina, Italy) was employed. This system is specifically designed for intraoperative electron radiotherapy and is equipped with polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) tubular applicators. These applicators have a wall thickness of 5 mm, a length of 60 cm, and inner diameters ranging from 3 to 10 cm.[5] The distal ends of the applicators are available in both flat and beveled configurations, with bevel angles of 0°, 15°, 30°, and 45°. Regardless of the tip design, all applicators maintain a consistent length of 60 cm and a wall thickness of 0.5 cm. PMMA is selected for its mechanical stability, electron transparency, and ease of sterilization for surgical applications.[6] In conventional electron therapy, an applicator with a diameter of 10 cm is commonly considered the reference standard for dose calibration and quality assurance procedures.[7] In contrast, applicator selection in IORT is determined by the size and geometry of the surgical field, as well as the proximity of critical anatomical structures. Radiation-sensitive organs and healthy tissues adjacent to the tumor resection cavity must therefore be carefully excluded from the direct beam path or adequately shielded using dedicated beam-blocking devices.

The standard self-protective disk provided with the LIAC system consists of a two-layer structure, comprising a 3 mm steel plate for electron attenuation and a 3 mm PMMA plate to provide mechanical support and biocompatibility. Although this configuration is effective, it may not offer optimal attenuation under all clinical conditions, particularly at higher electron energies. Consequently, the present study investigates an alternative shielding disk design, emphasizing improved material selection and increased thickness to enhance healthy tissue protection without disrupting surgical workflow. Commonly used IORT systems include MOBETRON, NOVAC, and LIAC accelerators,[8] all of which employ similar beam delivery and shielding concepts, albeit with differences in mechanical design and beam characteristics.

While previous studies have largely focused on conventional single-layer protective disks or limited material combinations, the present work introduces a systematic evaluation of novel double-layer shielding disks with various material pairings and thicknesses. Furthermore, multiple dosimetric parameters – including transmission factor (TF), backscatter factor (BSF), and absorbed dose – are simultaneously assessed to achieve comprehensive optimization. The LIAC model was validated against experimental percentage depth dose (PDD) data, ensuring the reliability of the simulation results. By identifying the PMMA + lead disk with an 8 mm thickness as the most effective configuration, this study not only provides a comparative dosimetric analysis but also proposes a clinically feasible solution that can be directly implemented in IORT practice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A Monte Carlo simulation was performed using the MCNPX code to model dose distribution and shielding characteristics in an IORT setting. MCNPX (version 2.7.0, developed and distributed by Los Alamos National Laboratory (Los Alamos, New Mexico, USA)) is a general-purpose Monte Carlo radiation transport code capable of simulating coupled electron, photon, and neutron transport over a wide energy range. In this study, electron and photon transport physics were enabled using the standard condensed-history electron transport algorithm (CH-I), which is suitable for medical electron beam simulations.[9] The code has been extensively validated and widely used in radiotherapy dose calculation studies. The simulation was based on the LIAC® mobile linear accelerator, which is capable of producing electron beams with nominal energies of 6, 8, 10, and 12 MeV. In the present study, the 12 MeV beam was selected, as higher-energy electrons are frequently required in IORT procedures to achieve adequate target coverage in deeper tissues. Due to the increased penetration depth and dose associated with higher-energy electrons, effective shielding is essential to protect the surrounding healthy tissues. For this purpose, the protective disk commonly used in breast IORT was modeled. This disk typically consists of two distinct layers:

Low-Z layer: composed of a low atomic number material, designed to decelerate incident electrons and reduce bremsstrahlung productionHigh-Z layer: composed of a high atomic number material, designed to completely stop the residual electron beam and attenuate any secondary photons generated.

All geometric parameters, material compositions, and beam characteristics were implemented in the MCNPX model in accordance with manufacturer specifications and data reported in the literature. The simulation output included depth–dose profiles, dose distribution maps, and bremsstrahlung photon spectra, which were used to evaluate the shielding performance.

First, the accelerator applicators and the water phantom were simulated, followed by the simulation of shielding disks made of PMMA + Lead, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) + Bismuth, Aluminum + Titanium, Aluminum + Copper, Steel + Titanium, and Steel + Copper, each in total thicknesses of 6 mm and 8 mm.

Standard coupled electron–photon transport physics available in MCNPX were used, with user-defined transport cutoff energies. The electron source was defined as a circular foil with a diameter of 4 mm and a semi-aperture angle of 3°, positioned 5 mm above the titanium exit window. The energy distribution of the source was modeled as a Gaussian with a full width at half maximum of 0.1 MeV. A detailed evaluation of the effect of the air gap on patient dose distribution was conducted, following the approach described by García Marcos et al.[11] Results indicated that the air gap could affect dose distributions by approximately 3%–4% of the maximum dose, particularly at the edges of the shallowest transverse dose profiles. Based on these findings, a 5 mm air gap was incorporated into the model, as it produced the best agreement with measured dose profiles.

The mass densities used in the Monte Carlo simulations were explicitly defined as follows: PMMA (1.19 g/cm3), PTFE (2.20 g/cm3), aluminum (2.70 g/cm3), steel (7.85 g/cm3), copper (8.96 g/cm3), titanium (4.51 g/cm3), bismuth (9.78 g/cm3), and lead (11.34 g/cm3).

The corresponding continuous slowing down approximation (CSDA) ranges of 12 MeV electrons in these materials were obtained from the NIST ESTAR database and were approximately: PMMA (5.8 cm), PTFE (3.9 cm), aluminum (2.1 cm), titanium (1.4 cm), steel (0.95 cm), copper (0.78 cm), bismuth (0.42 cm), and lead (0.39 cm). These values confirm that the selected shielding thicknesses (6–8 mm) are clinically relevant and sufficient to achieve significant electron attenuation under IORT conditions.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

To determine the optimal thickness of the first layer for each shielding configuration, a stepwise evaluation was conducted. The thickness of the first-layer material was varied from 0.5 mm to 8 mm in increments of 0.5 mm. At each step, the energy deposition was calculated using the *F8 tally in MCNPX, which reports the energy absorbed within the scoring volume. The raw simulation outputs, initially expressed in units of MeV, were subsequently converted to absorbed dose values in Gray using appropriate conversion factors.

The criterion for selecting the optimal thickness was based on minimizing the transmitted dose through the first layer. In other words, the thickness corresponding to the lowest *F8 tally value was considered optimal, as it provided the maximum attenuation of the primary electron beam while minimizing bremsstrahlung production. The comparative results for all investigated materials are summarized in the Figure 2 and 3. These results clearly demonstrate the differing attenuation capabilities of low-Z materials in the first layer and their impact on overall shielding performance.

According to Figure 4, the optimal first-layer thicknesses were determined as follows: 1 mm for PMMA, 0.5 mm for PTFE, 4 mm for steel, and 0.5 mm for aluminum. The remaining materials – due to their higher atomic numbers and the results obtained from the attenuation graphs – were selected for use in the second layer.

The results of this study are consistent with previously published Monte Carlo investigations on radioprotective disks used in IORT. For example, Baghani et al.[12] reported that double-layer shielding disks combining low-Z and high-Z materials provide improved attenuation and reduced backscatter compared with conventional single-layer designs. Similarly, Iaccarino et al.[10] demonstrated the importance of accurate electron beam modeling for LIAC-based IORT systems, highlighting the sensitivity of dose distributions to shielding geometry and material selection. In comparison with these studies, the present work systematically evaluates multiple material combinations at clinically relevant thicknesses and shows that the PMMA + lead configuration achieves superior attenuation while maintaining acceptable backscatter levels. This indicates that optimized double-layer designs can offer incremental improvements over previously proposed shielding solutions.

CONCLUSION

According to the obtained results, all shielding disk combinations – except for the aluminum–titanium configuration – demonstrated more favorable performance than the device’s reference disk. This finding indicates that appropriate selection of material combinations and thicknesses can significantly enhance attenuation efficiency in IORT applications. However, the use of a lead disk alone as the second layer is not recommended due to its inherent toxicity, the potential for radiation-induced secondary contamination, and additional handling challenges in the surgical environment.

To address this limitation, a practical and effective solution is to apply a thin PMMA protective coating, with a thickness of 1 mm or less, over the lead disk. This approach reduces the risks associated with direct lead exposure while providing a smoother contact surface, thereby improving clinical compatibility. The clinical benefit of using optimized double-layer shielding disks to reduce healthy-tissue exposure in IORT. The same protective strategy may also be applied to other disk configurations, if necessary, ensuring both safety and optimal shielding performance without compromising the mechanical or radiological properties of the design.
