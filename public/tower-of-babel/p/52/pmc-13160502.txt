Technology for Finding Misplaced Objects in a Home: Protocol for a Scoping Review
Authors: Bing Ye, James McDonough, Cynthia Chui, Alex Mihailidis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs people age, it becomes more common for individuals to misplace household items, such as keys or phones. This could inevitably lead to disruption of daily routine and cause frustration. In recent years, many technologies have been rapidly developed to support older adults in maintaining their independent living and enhancing their quality of life. Among these, many have been designed as memory aids to help locate misplaced objects, reducing both the time spent searching and the stress it causes. However, to the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive review has mapped out the existing technologies for misplaced object retrieval. Therefore, this scoping review will serve to help understand the landscape of the current technologies, features, and challenges, including their ethical and usability concerns, in retrieving misplaced household items.SummaryAn initial data search has been conducted by an information specialist targeting a set of databases to find studies that outline a technology aiding misplaced object retrieval in a home setting. Databases included MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, Compendex, Inspec, IEEE Xplore, and ACM Digital Library. Studies will be included if written in English, peer-reviewed, and depict a tested technology that aids in the specific retrieval of misplaced household objects. Screening will be done independently by two reviewers to ensure consistency and reduce bias. Reviewers will review decisions at the end of screening, and discrepancies will be solved through discussion between the two reviewers first or a third reviewer, if necessary. Both screening and extraction will be conducted on Covidence. Analysis of extracted data will be tabulated, and thematic analysis will be conducted through NVivo. The initial search has generated 6,556 records. The scoping review is expected to be completed by December 2025.Key MessagesThis review will provide developers and researchers with a guide to the current landscape of the relevant technologies, along with their features, issues, and gaps. The review is intended to inspire innovation in future similar technologies. In addition, the review will explore the gap in technology translation and underscore the importance of an interdisciplinary approach that engages all relevant stakeholders to work in collaboration for better outcomes and successful technology design and adoption.

Introduction

Background

Memory plays an important role in tracking household items and their location, which is an essential part of an individual’s everyday functioning [1–3]. Memory typically peaks in a person’s 20s, after which it begins to slowly decline. The decline often becomes noticeable in a person’s 40s and 50s [4]. An individual may find themselves repeating questions like, “Where did I leave my phone? Did you see my keys?”. Misplacing everyday household items is a common experience encountered in daily life [1, 5–7].

Looking for frequently used items often leads to frustration. At the same time, an individual may also experience memory lapses when they are trying to recall the storage location of objects that are not frequently used, such as seasonal clothing or toys. Searching for these items is usually time-consuming and even causes emotional stress if they cannot be found [1, 6, 8, 9].

This stressful experience is further amplified in individuals with pathological memory loss, such as people with mild cognitive impairment or dementia [8, 9]. In such cases, the frustration and anxiety resulting from forgetfulness can also extend to their family members, often their aging spouses, who may be experiencing similar cognitive difficulties [7, 9]. Family members may struggle to recall the item’s location or may not be aware of it, which can further exacerbate the stress for everyone involved [7]. Therefore, the inability to timely find the misplaced items could have a significant impact on daily life and overall well-being for both individuals with memory issues and their family members.

Technologies that can support a person’s memory and assist in finding misplaced objects are, therefore, important and valuable. These technologies could offer an effective way to prevent item loss and can quickly help people locate these items. They have the potential to enhance independent living and alleviate emotional strain, thereby improving both well-being and quality of life [8–12]. Therefore, a scoping review that maps out the current landscape of existing tools, their retrieval features, target population, and identifies current gaps in technologies, such as usability and ethical limitations, and the state of the research for future innovation is necessary. The research outcomes related to identifying technology gaps will be guided by the person-environment (P-E) fit framework. This framework indicates that a person achieves their best functioning when their needs and abilities fit with the resources and supplies of their environment [13]. In this case, technology is part of the environmental resource. Recognizing users’ usability and ethical concerns through the lens of P-E fit helps understand where misfit occurs between the user and the technology. For example, for people with vision impairment, lack of customization of the font size or absence of voice interaction represents a misfit between user’s needs and technology demands [14]. Thus, identifying and synthesizing these gaps using a P-E fit framework provides a meaningful and deeper understanding of how-to better design technology to support user’s functions and their technology expectations.

Objective

This scoping review will aim to examine the current technologies that aid in the retrieval of misplaced objects in a home setting. To support the main objective, the following questions are also explored:What are the common types of objects that are being retrieved (e.g., frequently misplaced daily items vs. long-term unused items)?What are the features of the technologies that specifically aid in object retrieval (e.g., voice and/or maps)?What gaps or challenges remain unaddressed by current technologies, including ethical and usability concerns, as well as technology translation issues? For example, is the technology in an algorithm design, a prototype, or has it been commercialized? Has technology been tested with a large number of participants and in what groups? Has technology been evaluated in a real-world setting or restricted in a laboratory? Has a usability study been conducted to test the technology? Have authors addressed any ethical issues regarding the use of technology?

This review is unique in several ways. Reviews on memory support usually focus on digital organizers, reminder systems, such as daily appointments and medication schedule, and prompting systems to complete daily tasks, such as hand washing and cooking [11, 15–19]. There has been no review of technology that specifically supports the retrieval of misplaced items. Such technology includes for example a personal robot that can scan the room and inform the user of the location of the misplaced items, like keys, through voice feedback “The keys are on the kitchen counter.” Since experiencing misplaced items is common in daily life, the review presents an important but overlooked area. Additionally, most memory support reviews are often limited to a specific participant group, such as people with dementia or mild cognitive impairment [11, 15–19]. The review will examine the various populations that retrieval technology intends to serve and will present the use of the technology not only limited to the clinical population but also to a wide range of users, emphasizing its greater relevance in everyday life. Lastly, the review will underscore the importance of applying user-centered design in research for ongoing technology development and refinement, moving innovations beyond the laboratory to communities, increasing public awareness of the new invention, and improving its accessibility. Thereby, technology would have a real impact on people’s daily lives. The current assistive technology policy in Canada remains outdated, underfunded and does not meet users’ increasing needs for technology. Therefore, people cannot fully benefit from the newly developed technology [20]. The review advocates the urgent need to involve policymakers in research to help them recognize the value of these technologies and gain a better understanding of how they should align with people’s needs. Thus, to prioritize these needs in policymaking, develop up-to-date policies, and increase funding resources to remove financial barriers for universal technology access [20, 21].

Methods

This scoping review will follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) reporting guideline [22] (see online suppl. document C; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551522). This protocol has been pre-registered on the Open Science Framework at https://osf.io/28kve.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

The search strategy has been developed in consultation with and conducted by an information specialist at the University Health Network. This is to ensure the database search is comprehensive and the search results align with the scoping review goals. A total of seven databases have been searched from inception to July 11, 2025: MEDLINE (R) ALL, Embase, Web of Science Core Collection, Compendex, Inspec, IEEE Xplore, and ACM Digital Library. The search terms comprised misplaced items, finding items, retrieval, and home. Please see the online supplementary document A for the full search strategies. A manual search was also conducted through the reference lists of relevant reviews and studies to identify additional records.

Search Strategy Eligibility Criteria

The search was limited to human participants with studies written in the English language only. Due to the rapid pace of technology development, the publications were limited to the past 10 years, between 2015 and 2025, to capture the most recent advancements.

Study Selection and Screening

The initial searched references will be imported to EndNote for duplicate removal. This is done by one reviewer. After duplicate removal, the remaining references will then be exported to Covidence for screening. The screening procedure will involve two stages: title and abstract screening, and full-text screening, which will be completed by two reviewers independently based on the above inclusion and exclusion criteria. In order to ensure consistency of screening between the two reviewers, the screening process will begin with a pilot phase. The first 100 references will be screened based on title and abstract by both reviewers independently. The screening results will then be compared and checked. Any conflicts will be resolved through discussion. Any uncertainties about the inclusion and exclusion criteria will be clarified and refined. Both reviewers will then continue to finish the rest of the title and abstract screening. Any discrepancies during the title and abstract screening will first be discussed to reach a consensus. A third reviewer may be involved if the disagreements remain unresolved. The rest of the references will then undergo full-text screening, following the same procedure as the title and abstract stage. The PRISMA study flow diagram will be used to illustrate the study selection process.

Data Charting

Data extraction will be performed by one reviewer through Covidence. The extraction form will be adapted from the Covidence template and revised according to the research questions. The form will first be pilot tested with three studies from the final included studies to ensure its clarity and comprehensibility. One reviewer will select these three studies. Two reviewers will abstract these studies independently. Discrepancies will be discussed between the two reviewers to reach a consensus, and any unresolved conflicts will be solved by a third reviewer. The form will then be refined and revised between the two reviewers.

The remaining studies to be extracted will be conducted by one reviewer and verified independently by a second reviewer. Any conflict found in the data will be discussed first. Unresolved discrepancies will be reviewed and determined by a third reviewer. Synthesis and Presentation of Results

The extracted data will be summarized in tables to provide a clear view of the results. In addition, the thematic analysis will be conducted for unaddressed challenges or gaps in existing technologies for misplaced household items retrieval. The qualitative data will include uncovering patterns around usability and ethical concerns. The qualitative analysis will be performed using NVivo 15 software. One reviewer will complete the qualitative data analysis. The reviewer will first read all selected studies to become familiar with the data. The data will be manually coded into different meaningful nodes in NVivo. Each node will be labeled with a description to capture its main idea. Similar nodes will be grouped into broader categories, which will then be synthesized into themes [24]. The coauthors will check the identified themes to ensure their accuracy and comprehensibility.

Discussion

In recent years, there has been increasing research focusing on technology designed for memory support and cognition [11, 12, 15, 25]. Among these studies, it covers research on supporting memory by helping individuals locate misplaced items. To the best of our knowledge, this scoping review is the first comprehensive review that maps existing technology developed to support misplaced household items retrieval. It provides a complete overview for industrial developers and researchers about the types of technology, retrieval features, and types of retrieval objects.

Current reviews indicate that many emerging technologies for people with memory issues are still at the conceptual or prototype stage, and only a few have progressed to the commercialization stage [16, 26, 27]. For example, the scoping review by Palmdorf et al. [27] presented that most home-care technologies for people with dementia remain in the early stage of development, with most studies focused on technical evaluation and exploration. Similarly, another systematic review in dementia care apps found that only a few (14%) newly developed apps had been commercially published [26]. This review will explore the current state of technology development, whether they are limited in algorithm design or have evolved beyond to commercialization. Thus, identifying the gap between laboratory research and market implementation and underscoring the need to advance technology translation for real-world applications.

Ethical concerns have been identified as important factors in technology adoption [26, 28–32]. A survey study found that most participants did not trust that their privacy was securely protected when using smart products, which negatively affected their intention to use these technologies. Similarly, an interview study reported that older adults were afraid their personal information would be misused through online platforms. These studies indicate that the concerns of technology in relation to privacy and security could hinder people’s willingness to accept and use the technology [28, 32]. However, a recent systematic review pointed out that researchers and developers had not put ethical concerns as their priority in technology development [26]. Therefore, it is essential to understand any unaddressed ethical challenges in technology that is designed for misplaced items retrieval. In order to enhance technology design and its adoption, the review will highlight the need for an interdisciplinary approach through a user-centered design to promote active collaboration and bring expertise from various fields, such as policymakers, end users, and engineers/researchers [16, 17]. More importantly, continuous involvement of these relevant stakeholders throughout the design and development of technology is crucial [16, 17, 33, 34]. Furthermore, technology should be tested in a real-world environment for its actual effectiveness [34]. The scoping review will examine these aspects to identify existing research gaps (e.g., whether the studies have been tested in a real-world environment or are still restricted in the laboratory environment; if the study has employed a usability study; if any privacy protection feature has been employed in the technology) and guide future research in this field.

The review also summarizes the retrieval strategies, information on the key features, how retrieval strategies are being used (e.g., whether used independently or in combinations), and what retrieval strategies might be the most beneficial for users. For example, a voice-based retrieval approach could support people with memory issues but could also be used by people with visual impairments to find misplaced objects [35, 36]. Additionally, by investigating the target population, the review could underscore gaps in user groups that remain underexplored in the use of retrieval technology. Thus, it will offer future research opportunities and underline the importance of personalization of technology to adapt to different user groups. Lastly, this review will also examine the types of objects being retrieved – whether technology has been developed for frequently used daily items or for less frequently used items, such as those kept in storage. By identifying the differences, the review will present research gaps, highlighting areas with greater focus and those with less attention, providing opportunities for further research.

In summary, this scoping review is novel and valuable. It provides meaningful insights not only for academic researchers but also for industry developers, policymakers, end users, and caregivers. By investigating the findings from usability studies, researchers, developers and policymakers could gain a deeper understanding of users’ needs, such as cost-effectiveness, enhancement of privacy and security, and ease-of-use technology. Designing technology that meets users’ needs is indeed critical, as it is one of the visions for equitable technology access in Canada. The review will provide further evidence to encourage policymakers to design policies that are more aligned with users’ needs and benefits, increase funding allocation for ongoing technology refinement and commercialization, and remove financial barriers to technology access, hence improving the equitable accessibility for technology use [20, 37]. The review is also beneficial to actual users and their caregivers by informing them of the existing technologies and raising awareness of the values of their involvement in technology design to create products that meet their needs.
