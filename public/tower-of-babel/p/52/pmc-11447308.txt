An empirical application of the consumer-based authenticity model in heritage tourism of the George Town historic district, Penang, Malaysia
Authors: Shuaizheng Zhao, Azizan Marzuki, Weihan Rong, Xu Ran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe primary objective of this paper is to investigate the specific application of the Consumer-based Model of Authenticity (C-BA) in historic districts. The paper building upon the C-BA model and examines the direct effects between the variables, as well as the mediating role of tourists' involvement, and place attachment in the connection between, authenticity (encompassing object-based authenticity and existential authenticity) and revisit intention. This study applied the C-BA model to the context of heritage tourism within a historic district. The analysis utilized structural equation modelling and involved 394 samples. The findings indicate that authenticity, influences tourists' intentions to return from a dual perspective. The results reveal that object-based authenticity mediates the relationship between tourists’ involvement and satisfaction, while existential authenticity mediates the connection between place attachment and satisfaction. The study suggests that stakeholders ought to prioritize the preservation of the objective authenticity of the historic district and the provision of high-quality activity experiences.Keywords: Authenticity, Place attachment, Heritage tourism, Historic district, George town

Introduction

The most recent report from the World Tourism Organization (WTO) indicates that international tourism has recovered to approximately 88 % of its pre-pandemic level. The organization predicts that the number of international tourists will reach an estimated 1.3 billion by 2024 [1]. The popularity of heritage tourism has increased, although the relationship between tourism and heritage is often contradictory. This conflict has become more pronounced [2]. Current research focuses on two main aspects: the recognition of tourism as a primary impetus for heritage conservation, transforming tourism products and activities into commodities for the benefit [3]. However, tourism poses a threat to both tangible and intangible cultural heritage. The rise of tourism gentrification has led to residents leaving their homes and relocating to more affordable communities [4]. Moreover, many cities have repurposed their built heritage into modern facilities, such as theme hotels and cafes, which has resulted in the loss of their unique character.

Tourism plays a significant role globally as an avenue for socio-economic development and job creation [5]. For developing countries, ‘heritage tourism’ is not only the largest, most widely accessible, and fastest-growing cultural industry but also an effective tool for community economic development and poverty reduction [6]. Pintossi et al. [7] suggest that countries should prioritize conserving and reusing urban heritage to achieve lasting, inclusive, and sustainable economic growth. They argue that this approach has ‘enhance[ing] long term tourism benefits’ Balancing the relationship between heritage conservation and reuse is challenging [3]. Historic districts must remain functional in contemporary community life while being well-conserved for future generations [8]. Although heritage conservation and tourism have different concerns regarding heritage authenticity, their goals of conserving objective authenticity while providing an authentic experience are essentially the same [9]. Relevant studies have also confirmed this, emphasizing that stakeholder consensus and cooperation are essential for the conservation and sustainable development of local tourism heritage and resources [10].

The conservation of heritage authenticity is widely recognized as a crucial factor in fostering a sense of place and creating strong attachment to it [11]. Pervious reference demonstrated significant relationships between perceived authenticity and tourist satisfaction as well as revisit intentions [[12], [13], [14]]. The consumer-based model of authenticity distinguishes different dimensions of authenticity and emphasizes the importance of object-based authenticity. This model has been extensively used in future studies, primarily focusing on heritage sites [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. However, studies on historic districts, particularly urban districts limited. The consumer-based model has never been applied to historic districts. As major destinations for heritage tourism, historic districts face greater threats to their authenticity than other heritage sites [21]. Economic and political factors often prioritize economic development over the conservation of authenticity in historic districts, particularly in developing countries [21]. Therefore, to ensure the sustainable tourism of historic districts, a deeper understanding of tourists’ perspectives and the mechanisms through which authenticity operates is necessary.

The research study was conducted in Penang, Malaysia, a World Heritage Site (WHS). The study aimed to promote sustainable development within WHS and provide insights for non-WHS cities to better conserve historic districts and enhance tourist experiences. This study utilized the consumer-based model of authenticity (C-BA) to evaluate tourists’ experiences and revisit intentions (RI) in historic districts by examining the correlation between various dimensions of authenticity and RI. The following questions guided this paper:

Literature review

Historic districts and heritage tourism

Historic districts are popular destinations for heritage tourism [21]. These areas contribute to the conservation of urban context and sustainable development of urban regions [9,22,23]. As an important part of tangible cultural heritage, historic areas effectively preserve and promote local history and culture [24].

Research has demonstrated that tourists visit historic areas for various reasons, such as recreational experiences, heritage motivation, and intellectual exploration [25]. Su et al. [26] further categorized these motivations as biological needs, intellectual exploration, social motivations, authenticity experiences, and cultural motivations. The search for authenticity is a significant focus in heritage research [27,28]. Moreover, the study confirmed that the perceived value of built heritage and the unique experience of multiculturalism are attractive to tourists [29]. The C-BA model is an effective way to evaluate tourists’ satisfaction and RI with tourism in urban heritage [15].

Consumer-based model of authenticity (C-BA)

The consumer-based model of authenticity (C-BA) was developed and validated by Kolar and Zabkar [15]. This model examines the impact of object-based authenticity (OBA) and existential authenticity on consumer loyalty, with cultural motivation serving as the central premise. Initial evidence suggests that authenticity plays a crucial role in mediating the relationship between cultural motivation and consumer loyalty. Bryce et al. [17] subsequently expanded the C-BA model by incorporating leisure, self-relevant structures, and engagement. Scholars have recognized the subtle yet critical role of the C-BA model in shaping consumer involvement with physical, social, and cultural environments [17,20,30].

In the C-BA model, cultural motivation refers to a set of motives that indicate a general desire for cultural activities rather than specific cultural goals [15]. This concept helps researchers understand tourist behaviour [17,25]. McKercher [31] defines tourists involved in cultural activities as those who visit or plan to visit cultural attractions, art galleries, museums, or historical sites, attend performances or festivals, and those who do not engage in cultural activities. Cultural tourism can be defined as visiting a cultural attraction outside of one's residence to gain new information and experiences that fulfil cultural needs [17]. Research has shown that cultural motivation significantly influences tourists' perception of objective authenticity [15,17].

Authenticity is a multifaceted concept in the field of heritage studies and it is a fundamental element of sustainable heritage tourism [32]. The C-BA model distinguishes between two different types of authenticity: OBA and EA [15,17]. OBA refers to an objective aspect of heritage that can be evaluated using straightforward criteria, such as whether the object is original or was made by local artisans using traditional methods [27,33,34]. EA, on the other hand, is a subjective state that can be stimulated by tourist activity, even if it has no connection to the tourist object [34,35]. This type of authenticity encompasses aspects such as one's mindset, perception, self-discovery, and emotional response to things. According to Kolar and Zabkar [15], there is a significant direct impact of OBA on EA in heritage tourism. Loyalty, which is a critical factor in consumer decision-making in the service sector, reflects the effectiveness of authenticity in the C-BA model. The results of Kolar and Zabkar's study confirm the positive influence of heritage authenticity on tourist loyalty [15]. The C-BA model has been widely utilized in heritage tourism research and has produced significant outcomes on a global scale [17,20].

In academic research on tourism, scholars differentiate between tourist groups with diverse motivations. These motivations dictate the choices made by tourists in selecting destinations and engaging in tourist activities [36]. Earlier studies have found that tourists are primarily driven by recreational experiences, heritage visits, and knowledge-seeking [25]. This aligns with Su et al.‘s [26] categorization of heritage tourist motivations into biological needs, knowledge exploration, social motivations, authenticity experiences, and cultural motivations. This study considers authenticity experience, cultural experience, and knowledge exploration as primary motivation for heritage tourism. Previous research has shown that tourists' perceptions of authenticity are influenced by their heritage motivations [15,17,20]. Based on the previous findings, the following hypotheses are proposed:

Authenticity is a vital aspect of heritage tourism [27]. The concept originated in the realm of art to verify the authenticity of an artwork, helping people evaluate its worth [37]. However, the development of existentialism and phenomenology has expanded the scope of authenticity theories beyond their traditional boundaries [34]. EA is now widely employed in various types of tourism research. In contemporary society, individuals confront numerous challenges, including division, chaos, emptiness, alienation, and moral and identity crises. Consequently, people tend to seek identity, meaning, and values and develop a greater interest in nostalgia and history [15]. The pursuit of authenticity is particularly prevalent in postmodern societies [38]. Therefore, the hypothesis is as follows:

Object-based authenticity positively influences existential authenticity.

Involvement serves as a critical psychological construct within the academic study of consumer behaviour [39]. This notion pertains to the extent of consumer input in different stages of the consumption process, as defined by Broderick and Mueller [40]. Additionally, involvement has been widely utilized in leisure, recreation, and tourism research since the 1990s [41]. In the context of tourism, involvement is defined as a tourist's interest in an activity and the resulting emotional experience [42]. Bryce et al. [17] emphasizes that “consumer engagement differs from consumer involvement” and argues that engagement represents a more profound manifestation of involvement. This study suggests that involvement is influenced by knowledge, motivation, and the number of purchases [43,44].

In this study, involvement is characterized as the degree to which a tourist participates in an activity, event, or artifact. It is considered a vital component of the tourism experience and is typically assessed using items from Zaichkowsky's [45] Personal Involvement Inventory with suitable modifications. The definition of involvement used in this study is one-dimensional. It is important to note that at present, there exists no standardized instrument for measuring involvement [46]. Prior research has demonstrated a strong correlation between involvement and two dimensions of authenticity [17,20]. Consequently, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

Object-based authenticity positively influences involvement.

Existential authenticity positively influences involvement.

The concept of place attachment (PA), originating from environmental psychology, refers to the emotional bond individuals form with a specific place. In tourism research, PA examines tourists’ emotional, functional, affective, and social attachments to particular destinations [47]. Research has demonstrated that this connection with a specific place affects the intentions or behaviours of tourists [48].

PA is a multidimensional concept that comprises four aspects: place identity, place dependence, affective attachment, and social attachment. Place identity refers to tourists' identification with a location or its symbolic values [48]. Place dependence reflects the functional attachment to a place generated by the ‘perceived behavioural advantage’ of the setting [48]. Affective attachment describes the sense of connection tourists develop to a destination [48]. Social attachment is related to people living in specific places, rather than to sites themselves [11,48].

The relationship between authenticity and PA has been extensively studied in the context of heritage tourism [11,49,50]. Wu et al. [50] and Zhao and Li [51] found that OBA and EA have a prominent effect on tourists' PA. Although few studies directly link the two types of authenticity and place identity, the literature suggests a potential connection [49,52]. This study uses one-dimensional structure to investigate the impact of authenticity on tourists’ PA, mainly through place identity. Therefore, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

Object-based authenticity positively influences place attachment.

Existential authenticity positively influences place attachment.

In the study of Viking heritage tourism, Olsen [53] highlighted the potential negative impacts of tourist identity on tourism activities. He suggested that heritage sites use strategies such as rituals and performances to help tourists temporarily forget identities. This finding implies the importance of involvement in shaping tourists’ attachment to heritage sites.

Satisfaction is key performance indicator for products and services in tourist destinations [26]. According to Oliver [54], satisfaction is defined ‘the consumer's response to the evaluation of the perceived discrepancy between prior expectations (or some other norm of performance) and the actual performance of the product as perceived after its consumption.’ In the field of tourism study, satisfaction is an evaluative judgment that combines cognitive and affective assessments, reflecting tourists' overall feelings about their tourism experience [12]. Previous research has shown that enhancing tourist satisfaction is crucial for the success and sustainable development of any tourist destination, especially cultural heritage sites like historic districts [21,54]. To evaluate tourists' satisfaction with their travel experience, an in-depth assessment of their experience is required [12].

Studies have found that tourists with higher involvement tend to show higher levels of satisfaction. Lu et al. [21] confirmed a positive relationship between tourists’ involvement and satisfaction in heritage tourism. Based on this, the following hypotheses have been proposed:

Involvement positively influences place attachment.

Involvement positively influences Satisfaction.

The correlation between place attachment (PA) and satisfaction is widely accepted in academic circles. However, there is still debate over the specific mechanisms of their interaction. Some scholars argue that satisfaction is a prerequisite for PA formation [55], while others believe that PA further enhances tourist satisfaction. Empirical evidence supports the latter view in various tourism contexts, including nature parks, vacation trips, and heritage tourism [47,50,56]. This study aims to investigate whether there is a positive correlation between tourists’ place attachment and satisfaction in historic districts. The findings of this study can provide theoretical support for improving tourism quality and tourist satisfaction in historic districts. Therefore, the following hypotheses have been proposed:

Place attachment positively influences satisfaction.

Studies have indicated that repeat purchases and repeat visitation to tourism destinations can be beneficial for both consumers and businesses [57,58]. In general, repeat visitation can lead to more stable and predictable economic revenues for tourism destinations [59]. A number of studies have also found a significant association between high levels of tourist satisfaction and their subsequent likelihood of engaging in positive behaviours, such as recommending the destination to friends and expressing a willingness to revisit [60,61]. While some novelty-seeking tourists may not be inclined to visit the same destination repeatedly during their travel experience, it can still influence their choice of similar destinations [62].

This study focuses on specifically tourists’ repeat visitation intentions in historic districts. The findings of this research will contribute to a better understanding of tourist repeat visitation and satisfaction and will also provide strategic guidance for destination management. Therefore, the following hypotheses have been proposed:

Satisfaction positively influences revisit intention.

Scholars have emphasized the importance of repeat visitation as an indicator of tourism destination development maturity. In tourism research, Ding et al. [63] found that place attachment and satisfaction have a positive impact on repeat visitation intention. Zhou et al. [14] also proposed that the strength of place attachment impacts tourists’ repeat visitation intention, although the effects of place identity and place dependence on repeat visitation intention are different. The impact of involvement on repeat visitation intention has also been mentioned in existing literature [46]. Therefore, the following hypotheses have been proposed:

Tourist involvement will have a positive influence on their repeat visitation intention.

Place attachment will have a positive influence on tourists' repeat visitation intention.

Methodology

Survey design

This study developed a comprehensive questionnaire for tourists visiting George Town based on a thorough examination of relevant research. The questionnaire aims to provide a detailed understanding of tourists’ attitudes, and behaviours towards historic district tourism.

The questionnaire comprises two parts. The first part collects information on the socio-demographic characteristics of the participants. The second part consists of 30 well-designed questions that measure various structural dimensions of the conceptual model (as shown in Table 1). To ensure the scientific validity and rigor of the questionnaire, some of the measurement items were revised based on the recommendations of expert scholars. Before administering the questionnaire, four pretest participants who met the study's criteria were invited to review the questionnaire to assess its readability and to ensure that there were no possible misunderstandings. This effort aimed to confirm that respondents could express their views more accurately.

Constructs and measurement items.

The questionnaire items are adapted from well-established scales used in previous studies. A seven-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7) was used to analyse the respondents’ views.

Data collection

The research chosen for this study focused on George Town as its site of investigation (as depicted in Fig. 2). George Town's recognition as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2008 highlights its importance as a well-regarded tourist destination in Southeast Asia.

The study scope of George Town (Sources: Mapbox and author's illustration).

Penang, situated in the northern part of Peninsular Malaysia, embodies the amalgamation of Eastern and Southeastern Asia cultures. The architecture, cultural legacy, and urban landscape of George Town are significantly shaped by the European colonial era and the multicultural milieu of Asia (as depicted in Fig. 3). This fusion of cultures has earned Penang a worldwide reputation as a popular tourist destination [71].

Record of buildings, streets and environment of George Town, Penang (Source: authors).

George Town was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 2008, resulting in a significant increase in tourism and economic growth. However, this growth has also led to commercialization and tourism gentrification, which have had far-reaching effects on the heritage site and traditional culture. These changes have sparked heated debates on development and conservation [72]. The impacts of commercialization and gentrification have garnered widespread attention, making George Town an ideal location for this study.

This study utilized a convenient sampling approach, involving only tourists who had completed their trips. Data collection took place during the peak tourist season of Spring 2024. A questionnaire survey was employed to gather data and a pilot study was conducted by the researcher with 84 tourists to assess the validity and feasibility of the questionnaire. Based on feedback, some items were removed, and others were rewritten to improve readability. During the official data collection stage, 527 responses were collected using an online questionnaire platform. After eliminating 133 invalid responses, a total of 394 valid responses from recent visitors to George Town were retained. This sample size meets Gorsuch's requirement of 5–10 times the number of question items, and provides a robust foundation for further data analysis [73]. Comrey and Lee [74] also suggest that a sample size greater than 300 is ideal.

Data modelling

Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) is a widely utilized in behavioural studies that examines intricate and dynamic situations. There are two primary types of SEM: covariance-based (CB-SEM) and partial least squares (PLS-SEM), with the latter being recommended when researchers aim to extend an established theory [75]. In this study, the SmartPLS 4 software (version 4.1.0.0) was employed. To determine the coefficients of the path relationships between the variables in the model, the bootstrap self-sampling method was utilized, with 5000 samples taken for the purpose.

Results and findings

Reliability test

The evaluation of the measurement model followed rigorous criteria for construct validity and reliability. The research makes use of Cronbach's alpha coefficients to determine the dependability of the central variables in the measurement scheme. As clearly illustrated in Table 3, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient values for all variables ranged from 0.805 to 0.887. These values significantly surpass the recommended threshold of 0.7 [76]. Additionally, the model's composite reliability (CR) values ranged from 0.865 to 0.987 all of which exceed the benchmark criterion of 0.7 [77]. Furthermore, the factor loadings for each item surpassed the recommended value of 0.7, indicating strong convergent validity. The average variance extracted (AVE) values for each construct ranged from 0.562 to 0.797, which is above the recommended threshold of 0.50 [75]. Consequently, all the constructs meet the criteria for convergent validity and demonstrate robust reliability and validity.

Discussions

The study's results corroborated that heritage motivation (HM) exerts a substantial impact on tourists' perceptions of object-based authenticity (OBA) and existential authenticity (EA). Furthermore, the study established that OBA wields a significant and direct influence on EA, validating hypotheses H1, H2, and H3. Notably, HM had the most pronounced effect on OBA. This study suggests that tourists with stronger motivation pay more heed to object-based authenticity, which culminates in a higher level of existential authenticity and enhances their travel experience. This finding aligns with previous research [15,17,20]. In addition, the results indicate that the OBA of the historic district significantly influences the level of tourist involvement (supported by H4). It suggests that tourists' involvement augments with their perception of authenticity. Tourists' EA also significantly impacts their involvement (supported by H5), and the results showed that OBA indirectly influences tourists' involvement through EA. The findings adhere to previous research [17]. The results indicate that the C-BA model is effective in the historic district, a unique heritage site.

The significant impact of EA on tourist involvement and PA, is supported by H5 and H7, as stated in the results of this study. Although H6 did not show a significant direct effect of OBA on PA, OBA still affects the formation of tourists' PA through the mediating effect of EA and involvement. Few previous studies in the field of heritage tourism have validated this hypothesis. The study's results align with Jiang's [84] findings, which indicate that EA has a direct and significant effect on PA formation. The study also supports the findings of other tourism studies on perceived authenticity and PA [42,67]. The study also confirms the impact of involvement on PA, as indicated by H9. This result consistent with Olsen's findings [53]. Furthermore, the study confirms the significant effect of involvement and PA on tourist satisfaction in historic district tourism, as indicated by H8 and H10. Specifically, a higher level of involvement and PA significantly increases tourist satisfaction. This finding confirms the positive impact of involvement tourist satisfaction [21], and suggests that tourists are more satisfied when they are involved in heritage sites and sense of authenticity in a historic district. Although the study does not support a direct link between involvement and RI (H12), the results suggest that involvement indirectly affects RI through PA and satisfaction, as indicated by previous findings [67]. The result implies only memorable experience could stimulate tourists' revisit intention, which confirms Taheri et al. [19] and Kesgin et al.‘s [20] study. The experiences which is dissatisfaction and do not achieve the expectation will not stimulate tourists' revisit intention. The study's important finding is the direct effect of PA on RI (H13). PA refers to the deep connection between a person and a place, and when established, tourists are more willing to revisit the historic district. Previous studies have also shown that tourists with higher PA have higher RI for destinations [14].

Implications and conclusion

Theoretical and policy implications

This research enhances and deepens the existing C-BA model. Previous studies have primarily focused on the impact of authenticity on loyalty [15,17] and have gradually uncovered the intrinsic connection between authenticity, visitor involvement, and RI [85]. Recent studies have also explored the influence of affective factors (PA) on RI [14]. However, the mediating role of involvement and PA in heritage authenticity studies has received less attention. This research introduces involvement and PA as two constructs of the C-BA model. The study demonstrates that the authenticity of historic districts affects tourists’ involvement and PA, which in turn enhances their satisfaction and intention to revisit.

This research presents a comprehensive model of the relationship between authenticity and RI based on extensive research and empirical analysis. The model expands the internal hierarchy and explanatory power of the C-BA model. Previous studies have suggested that PA mediates the relationship between authenticity and RI [14]. This research finds that involvement and PA have a mediating role between authenticity and satisfaction. Additionally, involvement and PA played multiple mediating roles between authenticity and RI and distinguished different mediating effects of OBA and EA. This mechanism elucidates the psychological processes of tourists regarding authenticity and RI.

Lastly, this research provides new evidence for the relationship between OBA and satisfaction in heritage tourism. The impact of OBA, EA, PA, and involvement on satisfaction was thoroughly examined, further validating the findings of Domínguez-Quintero et al. [12]. The study aims to offer insights and implications for theoretical development and practical applications in heritage tourism.

Practical implications

The study examines the behavioural responses of tourists to authenticity in historic districts, with the aim of providing practical recommendations for enhancing these areas. To achieve this objective, local authorities in historic districts should work to preserve the authenticity of both tangible and intangible cultural elements, such as built heritage and traditional crafts. Additionally, they should develop activities that promote tourists’ perceptions of authenticity encourage involvement and interaction with the local community, such as visiting historic buildings and participating in traditional crafts.

The study found that both perceived authenticity and satisfaction have a significant impact on RI repeat intentions. Building positive interactions between tourists and the destination can increase tourist PA, motivating them to return to historical districts. On the other hand, repeat intentions are influenced by satisfaction, and heritage tourism managers should provide opportunities for tourists to participate in activities during their travel experience, which fosters PA and leads to high levels of satisfaction. Consequently, tourists are more likely to return, contributing to the sustainable development of heritage tourism destinations. The study confirms that the conservation of the authenticity of historic districts is the crucial for local tourism development. It shows that conservation can positively affect tourists’ experience and involvement, resulting in higher satisfaction and repeat intentions. The study demonstrates that maintaining the objective authenticity of historic districts contributes to sustainable development and long-term conservation of these areas.

Conclusions

This research investigates the influence of authenticity, as defined by the C-BA model, on tourist satisfaction and repeat intentions (RI) in George Town, a World Heritage Site in Malaysia. The study explores the role of involvement and place attachment (PA) in this process, and examines the effects of object-based authenticity (OBA) and existential authenticity (EA) on tourist involvement and PA. The findings indicate that OBA has a positive influence on tourist involvement, while EA has a positive influence on tourist PA. Moreover, the study confirms that the authenticity of the historical district significantly affects tourists’ RI.

Subsequent research has confirmed that tourist involvement and PA serve as mediating variables in the historic district, directly impacting tourist satisfaction and acting as a link between OBA, EA, satisfaction, and RI. Tourists’ pursuit of OBA reflects their quest for a connection between self and the world. Studies have shown that tourists are interested in experiencing built heritage, traditional skills, and rich historical information. Furthermore, the research indicates EA influences RI through involvement and PA. This suggests that visitors who actively participate in various activities in George Town experience higher satisfaction and RI. This finding provides practical guidance for managers of heritage tourism destinations. By emphasizing and enhancing the authenticity of the destination and encouraging active tourist participation, they can promote increased satisfaction and return intention.

Limitations

The research in this manuscript concentrates on applying the consumer-based model of authenticity within historic districts, and it introduces place attachment into the model. Due to the complexity of constructing authenticity and place attachment, researchers employ different scales to reflect these constructs. For instance, place attachment possesses a first-order two-dimensional construct, a first-order three-dimensional construct, a first-order four-dimensional construct, and others. Future research could examine how authenticity impacts place attachment across various dimensions. Additionally, the scale may restrict the relative study; future work could explore better scales for authenticity, place attachment, involvement, and satisfaction. Moreover, the current study has not differentiated between the different categories of international and domestic tourists, which is crucial for a more comprehensive understanding of the effect of heritage tourism authenticity on revisit intention. Future research should adequately address this distinction. Lastly, the study data was collected from a cross-sectional survey conducted over four months. Future studies should conduct multiple surveys at different period intervals to capture dynamic changes and potential influences.
