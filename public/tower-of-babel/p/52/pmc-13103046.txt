Geomorphological changes of natural and engineered vegetated foredunes since completion of Kustwerk Katwijk
Authors: Amber Spears, Caroline Apraku, Robert W. Whalin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For centuries, vegetation has been utilized to enhance dune stability and provide coastal protection. Marram grass (Calamagrostis arenaria) is the most commonly transplanted species on engineered coastal dunes in the Netherlands. However, the geomorphic behavior of engineered foredunes relative to neighboring natural foredunes remains unknown. This study utilizes historical airborne laser surveys, multispectral satellite data, and 2023 field surveys to compare the spatial and temporal patterns of sand accretion and vegetation health of the dike-in-dune in Katwijk, NL, with those of the southern neighboring Berkheide from 2015 to 2023. Results show that average annual sand accretion at the Berkheide foredune was about 1.5 times as much as at the dike-in-dune. Average annual increases in vegetation health, as represented by normalized difference vegetation indices, are roughly equal and less than 0.1 at both the dike-in-dune and Berkheide foredunes. This study observed a generally positive correlation between average annual accretion and vegetation health for each foredune. However, in areas of permanent and temporary beach structures, the Berkheide dune appeared to be the most adversely impacted. This information suggests that the foredunes are growing similarly—although the dike-in-dune is engineered—which is promising for urban coastal communities with coastlines visible leeward.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42772-w.

Introduction

In Katwijk aan Zee, the relatively weak state of protection along the town’s coastline compared to neighboring areas prompted an evaluation of mitigation methods by Rijkswaterstaat, the Dutch Ministry of Infrastructure and Water Management1. With several innovative projects completed in the Netherlands to enhance flood protection in low-lying inland areas—including beach nourishment, sediment relocation to support the growth of salt marshes, and movable storm surge barriers—a place-based approach was essential to optimize the needs of all stakeholders2. Located in the province of South Holland in the mid-western Netherlands, Katwijk is a small town that attracts thousands of tourists annually3. The average annual temperature and rainfall are 9.5 °C (49.1 °F) and 779 mm (30.7 in), respectively. Average windspeed varies from 23.2 (14.4 mph) in July to 35.2 kph (21.9 mph) in December4. The Oude Rijn runs through the city, making fishing and family water recreational activities a rich part of the city’s heritage. Before the Kustwerk Katwijk project, also known as the Coastal Work Katwijk project, the coastline consisted of naturally occurring dunes, with flood protection infrastructure consisting of a barrier running through the village rather than along the coastline5.

Dutch safety regulations required that the newly implemented solution would be resistant to a one-in-10,000-year storm (1/10,000 yr storm), which considers several hazards, including wind, rain, waves, and extreme high storm tides. This undertaking involved a variety of stakeholders, including Rijkswaterstaat, Waterboard Rijnland, the Municipality of Katwijk, the Province of Zuid-Holland, and the general public. The comprehensive decision-making process resulted in the construction of a dike-in-dune, a restored coastline, and an underground parking garage. Supplementary Fig. S1 illustrates the coastal system before and after Kustwerk Katwijk6,7. The shoreline was extended by 80–100 m (263–328 ft) seaward. The beach nourishment of the coastline at Katwijk aan Zee was calculated to be fully dispersed after ten years8. As the solution to the last weak link in the coastal protection system of Province Zuid-Holland, the dike-in-dune—like the Berkheide dune to the south—is a coastal protection system with urban development seaward of the foredune. Both beaches become highly urbanized during the warm seasons, with regular maintenance of the beachfront in the area around the permanent structures, including the use of bulldozers to move sand. The purpose of this research is to assess geomorphological changes of the engineered vegetated foredune of the dike-in-dune in comparison to the vegetated foredune of the older naturally existing Berkheide dune. While quantifying the impact of urban development is beyond the scope of this study, determining if differences in the growth of engineered and natural foredunes can be surmised from topographical and multispectral surveys is an objective.

All dunes along the Netherlands’ coastline are part of the European Union’s Natura 2000 program areas that are protected under the Nature Conservation Act9. Dune systems are essential for the Dutch not only in flood protection but also in their heritage10. However, grey infrastructure is traditionally more frequent in coastal flood management11. The dike-in-dune constructed in Katwijk can be attributed to the long history of the Dutch utilizing dikes managed by the Waterboards and Rijkswaterstaat as part of the Ministry of Infrastructure and Water Management of the Netherlands. Dikes typically consist of well-compacted materials. The dike underneath the dune utilized heavy construction techniques, combining natural and artificial materials such as basalt rock, a sand core, geotextile, and a filter layer to regulate seepage. These standard materials involved in hard engineering alter the natural subgrade. Located behind the dike on the landside, an underground parking garage is another feature of hard engineering used in the project. The parking garage reduced the amount of sand transported to the site through a large sand supplementation operation by 50,000 m3 (65,000 yd3). No evidence was found to indicate that the reduced volume would negatively impact dune vegetation. The total sand supply was 3,000,000 m3 (4,000,000 yd3), excluding the sand used to restore the original beach width8.

As with most urban natural landscapes, the natural processes coexist with human activity. Hence, marram grass was incorporated into the design to stabilize the constructed row of dunes and promote future dune development and plant succession. The dunes are not to be walked on except on paved, designated walking paths. While the researchers do not aim to isolate the impact of vegetation on geomorphology amid anthropogenic activity, the contribution of vegetation withstanding these impacts may be ascertained using remote sensing techniques. Is the dike-in-dune foredune growing similarly to the natural Berkheide foredune south of the dike-in-dune? Are there correlations between changes in sand accretion and vegetation health that may indicate the impact of marram grass on the development of each foredune system?

Approximately one-half of the Netherlands’ shorelines are protected by vegetated coastal dunes, compared to one-third of the world’s coastlines10,12. The Dutch are experienced in implementing the engineered dune solution, as a dike-in-dune was constructed in another village in South Netherlands years before its implementation in Katwijk. Noordwijk also faced the challenge of protecting the coastline within a limited space due to the proximity of the urban community to the shore.

The dike-in-dune is a coastal green infrastructure (CGI) solution integrated into the existing dune system of the Natura 2000 program. Natural dunes are formed through aeolian processes, where sand sedimentation transport occurs via creep, saltation, and suspension13. Natural dunes are also typically higher than engineered dunes when not impacted by urbanization. The pre-existing dune in Katwijk was at a height of NAP + 7.5 m (24.6 ft) with a narrow width of about 30 m (98 ft) before construction of the dike-in-dune. The engineered dune widened the dune system by 90 m (295 ft) and raised the height to NAP + 8 m (26.2 ft) at the center8. The dune was constructed with dredged sand and covered with 10 cm (4 in) of sand from the pre-existing dune, thereby including seeds from former vegetation. Traditional construction of a dike does not allow for aesthetically pleasing views and access to the shoreline14. European marram grass (Calamagrostis arenaria, formerly Ammophila arenaria) was transplanted from nearby dunes.

During a study by van Bergen et al. (2021), several design principles for urban dune formation were studied at the dike-in-dune in Noordwijk, including eco-trapping, whereby plants trap sediment13. Marram grass was found to be effective in creating foredunes on bare sands, as it can grow in conjunction with the deposition process and develop a dense root system. By reinforcing the dunes, marram grass provides erosion resistance during coastal storms, allowing the dunes to act as a buffer to wave action for the underlying dike. In one study comparing the morphological performance of various dunes under storm conditions, a vegetated dune with a rocky core had less erosion than a vegetated dune with no underlying structure15. Therefore, there is potential for a positive impact of engineered vegetated dunes with marram grass; however, further research is needed to establish and solidify this potential.

In engineered environments, marram grass is traditionally transplanted by hand as individual culms, tillers, or tufts, rather than seeds14. Taken from neighboring foredunes, they are transplanted in a grid pattern, with a spacing of 30 to 60 cm (12 to 24 in) and a planting depth of 15 to 20 cm (6 to 8 in). This corroborates the account from Waterboard Rijnland that the grass was transplanted in 9 to 11 tillers per 1 m2 (11 ft2) for a total of 2,000,000 bundles over the entire dune area during Kustwerk Katwijk16. In Rijnland, planting marram grass is a centuries-old practice that relies on traditional knowledge rather than several technical specifications. Historical archives indicate that disputes over whether to plant grass or not were so common by the year 1500 that an imperial mandate on marram grass planting was issued in 1510. Rijnland frequently shares its marram grass with other coastal communities to reinforce their dunes and mitigate storm impacts. The engineered vegetated dune in Katwijk has not been studied widely in the literature.

Spatial and temporal changes in the elevation of the foredune and the normalized difference vegetation indices (NDVI)—a measure of vegetation health that incorporates density, greenness, and other factors—are possible indicators of the benefits of marram grass17,18. In this study on Kustwerk Katwijk, spatial changes in elevation and NDVI were determined from several sources, representing information spanning nearly a decade, and compared to those of the southern neighboring Berkheide dune.

Methods

An initial site reconnaissance and data collection, an on-site field investigation, an acquisition of historical data, and data processing were performed in this study19. The toe and crest areas of the foredune were primarily observed during reconnaissance rather than the entire foredune. Permission was granted to walk in these areas, as walking on the dunes is otherwise off limits. After walking along the dike-in-dune system, as well as portions of the Natura 2000 dunes to the north and south, locations for the field and historical data collection were determined as illustrated in Fig. 1a20,21. The initial site reconnaissance consisted of an assessment of vegetation, urban conditions, and any potential compromises to the dune system.

Photos of vegetation were captured and uploaded to an app that provides information about each plant using artificial intelligence. The plant species were later confirmed during a meeting with a representative from Waterboard Rijnland16. Many other plant species now exist on the foredune of the dike-in-dune and have likely existed on the south dune before construction of the dike-in-dune due to its much older age. Field wormwood, sea-buckthorn, common wormwood, and rough horsetail were in the foredune section of the dike-in-dune. Perennial sow thistle, coltsfoot, and rugosa rose were observed at the Berkheide dune in addition to those observed at the dike-in-dune. The densities of other species, compared to marram grass, were visually observed to be lower, as marram grass dominated the slopes in most cases, and the different species increased at the crest and landward. Due to the summer season, there was a lot of pedestrian and vehicular traffic at the beach.

Located from north to south along the seaside of the dike-in-dune field investigation section are eight beach homes, a restaurant, 64 beach stalls in rows and columns of eight, another restaurant, and four more beach homes. Roughly 40 m (131.2 ft) is free of any obstruction between the sea and the foredune; this area is roughly in the middle of the dike-in-dune section. Sixteen beach homes are along the seaside of the Berkheide dune section in the north. The south has approximately 135 m (443 ft) of unobstructed space, as boats were stored just beyond the walking path. All restaurants and beach homes are permanent structures. Some areas of the foredunes appeared to be compromised within the vicinity of urban activity.

Berkheide’s foredune section was selected based on its proximity to urban development seaward of the dunes—similar to the dike-in-dune. The foredune vegetation appeared to be sparse and new in some areas, likely due to a blowout caused by wind effects between beach houses (Fig. 2d and e). The scant inflorescences were later determined to be located behind the southernmost seven newly constructed beach houses, built between 2014 and 2021, based on historical aerial images. The dune crest was maintained with mature marram grass, while portions of the foredune downstream slope showed sparsity of grass with proximity to the toe. Figure 2f shows embryonic dunes in front of the foredune areas that were unobstructed by urban development. The foredune of Berkheide was delineated by locating the initial crest of the dune. This location was usually marked by a walking path of bare ground amongst vegetation. The field section extends beyond this path by approximately 20 m (66 ft) in some areas. During the field visit, a bulldozer was observed moving sand from the paved path in front of the beach houses.

The sections at the dike-in-dune and Berkheide dune were delineated to compute the spatial average of the change in ground surface elevation for the period after the completion of Kustwerk Katwijk, which was constructed from October 2013 to April 2015. Aerial photos show that the dike-in-dune foredune areas in the study were completed by approximately August 2014. However, the 2014 UAV LiDAR survey was completed before then, so historical topographical data begins in 2015.

The LiDAR survey was completed using a ground-based LiDAR scanner, achieving an accuracy of 5 mm (0.2 in) within the point cloud and an average absolute error of 2–3 cm (0.8–1.2 in). Each scan was completed at features deemed significant to the researchers, such as changes in terrain or vegetation of the foredune. Approximately 6–8 LiDAR scans were completed for each section. Additional ground elevation points were collected in June 2023 using a Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) RTK rover to refine the point cloud further. The RTK was connected to the GNSS Local Data Center Delft (GNSS-LDC-Delft). All points were collected in the RD New, EPSG 28,992 project coordinate system, the Amersfoort (EPSG 4289) geographic coordinate system, and the Normaal Amsterdams Peil (NAP) (EPSG 5709) vertical coordinate system to coincide with the JarKus surveys. The point clouds were registered. Digital terrain models (DTMs) of topography, and digital surface models (DSMs) including all above-ground features were created. Later, each elevation model was exported as a geographic tagged image file format (geoTIFF) and imported as a raster, with pyramids built, and feature polygons created from the surveyed sections.

The multispectral survey was completed using a drone and camera equipped with GNSS, featuring a resolution of 2 cm (0.8 in) and a horizontal absolute accuracy of 3 cm (1.2 in) via Post-Processing Kinematics (PPK). The multispectral camera included a panchromatic sensor and measured five multispectral bands: blue, green, red, red edge, and near infrared. The calibration panel was captured before and after the survey. Before the multispectral survey, photogrammetry was taken to obtain an aerial image of the site. Both photogrammetry and multispectral imagery were processed to produce a map of NDVI with a resolution of 4 cm (1.6 in), as set by the software. The orthomosaic was converted to EPSG 28,992 and EPSG 4289 coordinate systems, introducing up to 20 cm of error. The feature polygons created from the LiDAR survey were used to clip the orthomosaic and create rasters of the sizes of the feature polygons that outlined each foredune section.

Historical LiDAR and multispectral surveys were acquired for comparison with the field data. Access to the JarKus data provided DTMs with 2 m (6.6 ft) resolution from Rijkswaterstaat. The surveys were completed at a flying height of approximately 1,500 m (4,921 ft). The maximum vertical error is 20 cm (7.8 in), with over 68% of the data having a vertical error less than 10 cm (3.9 in) and over 99% having a vertical error less than 20 cm (7.8 in)24. DSMs were omitted since surveys were typically collected outside of the growing season to reduce ground cover. Every point was classified as ground, water, or not classified. A manual classification followed an automated classification. Therefore, the JarKus airborne LiDAR dataset has been classified to distinguish between ground points, water, and unclassified points. GeoTIFFs were imported, and pyramids were built to create rasters. The multispectral data was obtained from the Space Office, provided by the Dutch government via the Satellite Data Portal, and managed by Helpdesk NSO Satellietdataportaal Geoserve BV. Only multispectral data with little to no cloud cover is provided. The multispectral images were pansharpened using a panchromatic image and geometrically corrected to maintain full spectral depth, with a metric projection of EPSG 28,992. The satellite data closest to the historical LiDAR survey dates were selected, with some dates being within days of the LiDAR surveys and others within months of the surveys. Closer dates allow for a more accurate comparison of elevation and NDVI, as daily changes can impact the validity of any correlations. The resolution varied for each orthorectified panchromatic mosaic but was later converted to 2 m (6.6 ft) from a higher-resolution image. Each geoTIFF was imported, and pyramids were built to create rasters. The NDVI is widely used in agriculture, engineering, and geosciences to determine the relative health of the vegetation over vast areas where visual observations would be limited in frequency and extent24–29. It is directly related to the photosynthetic activity of vegetation, total green biomass, leaf area index, percentage of ground cover, and plant elevation. NDVI values can range from -1 to 1, with negative values indicating clouds and water, positive values near zero indicating bare soil, and higher positive values indicating sparse to dense vegetation28. The change in NDVI is helpful to assess the behavior of marram grass. No NDVI of healthy marram grass, using the same equation for the index, was available in the literature.

One exception is that the historical and field data comparison for 2023 used the timeframe between the dates collected, represented in weeks (17 weeks for LiDAR and 10 weeks for multispectral). Rasters were also used to determine differences between each survey for each subsequent year. The optimized hotspot analysis is a geostatistical tool that utilizes a minimum of 30 input features (such as the average annual difference between elevations or NDVI) to create local clusters spatially by joining each input feature with its neighbors. The sum of each feature is proportionally compared to the total sum of all features and neighboring features, using spatial weights. For clusters of features with sums very different from what is expected based on nearby features, where the likelihood of random influence is minimal, the cluster is considered statistically significant. The statistic (Getis-Ord Gi* statistic) for each feature is calculated from the sample mean (X̄), standard deviation (S), and spatial weight (wi, j)30. The optimized hotspot analysis identifies locations that are statistically significant from the spatial average of a given foredune (or foredune and beachfront) section. In the case of the average annual change in elevation, hotspots (in red) indicate areas where the differences were higher than the sample mean (also known as the spatial average) when considering the likelihood (p-value) of random effects. A hotspot with a Gi* of + 3 is statistically significant with a 99% confidence, corresponding to a p-value of 0.01 (p = 0.01), which means that the greater change in elevation value, indicating accretion, is very likely not due to random chance. Similarly, a coldspot (in deep blue) with a Gi* of − 3 is statistically significant at a 99% confidence level (p = 0.01), corresponding to an area with a lower change in elevation than the spatial average, indicating erosion, which is unlikely due to random chance. The same approach was applied to the hot and cold spots for the average annual change in NDVI. These locations indicate possible geomorphological changes between surveys. Any areas in which 1 < Gi* < + 1, the confidence level was less than 90%, were classified as not significant (p > 0.1). If most of the cells represent hot or coldspots with a 99% confidence, it indicates that each cell’s average is significantly (p = 0.01) higher or lower compared to its neighboring cells, respectively. This could be a result of the distance band being too large when comparing each cell to its neighbors, or the large sample size (number of cells) reducing the confidence interval, thereby leading to slight variations between neighboring cells being represented as statistically significant (p = 0.01). The default distance band settings were used in this study, as a sensitivity analysis on the distance band was beyond the scope of this study. Nevertheless, this is one method to compare large datasets over temporal scales.

Once the optimized hotspot analysis was completed, the geostatistics table was created to report the spatial mean, standard deviation, ranges, and total count of cells. Average annual changes in elevation, standard deviations, and ranges were later converted to changes in volume per longshore distance by multiplying by the area of the section and dividing it by the longshore length of each section. Additionally, a hotspot analysis comparison was completed to determine any correlation between the average annual changes in elevation and NDVI.

Discussion

The results overall indicate a relationship between the dike-in-dune and Berkheide foredunes, as well as within these dune systems, in terms of the average changes in elevation and NDVI. These relationships refer to the geostatistical surfaces when limiting the beachfront sand to the upper beach or considering only the foredunes. The foredunes overall showed several areas where the annual or weekly average changes in elevation or NDVI exceeded the spatial average changes, indicating statistically significant (p = 0.01) areas of accretion and healthy vegetation. There were fewer of these accretional areas located on the beachfront, except those where embryonic dunes were present at Berkheide. Areas around built structures were statistically significant (p = 0.01) overall, indicating that accretion and vegetation health changes were less than the spatial average. In some instances, these areas transitioned to accretional hotspots seaward of the structure. The foredune accretional hotspots also correlated overall with the vegetation health hotspots. The comparison between the foredune and upper beachfront must consider factors that influence the beachfront.

Coastal changes, including anthropogenic activity, beach nourishment, hydrodynamics, and storms, can impact the beachfront. A sand suppletion occurred on the coast seaward of both dune sections between May 2019 and June 2019, resulting in 400,000 m3 (523,180 yd3) over 1750 m (5741 ft), beginning at the dike-in-dune section at transect T8650 from the JarKus dataset and ending at the embryonic dunes just south of the beach houses in the Berkheide section at transect T8825. Another sand suppletion occurred between October 2022 and March 2023 at the dike-in-dune section, resulting in a 600,000 m3 (523,180 yd3) over 2500 m (8202 ft), beginning at the Oude Rijn section at transect T8600 from the JarKus dataset and south of the embryonic dunes in front of the sailboats in the Berkheide section at transect T8850. Sand nourishment is conducted in the Netherlands to maintain the 1990 coastline; however, only a portion of the underwater nourishment reaches the dunes, as indicated by a decadal study31. The prevailing wind direction in the Netherlands is southwest, mainly because the country lies near the North Sea, where the wind typically blows from southwest to northeast. The average changes in volume are 3–12 times greater on the Berkheide foredune when including the beachfront, which can be attributed to several factors: beach erosion, marine accretion, shoreward transport, longshore transport, aeolian transport, urban impact, distance from shore, and foredune geometry, including height and width32. Aeolian longshore and shoreward transport appears to be more prevalent at the Berkheide foredune, where permanently built structures account for a smaller percentage of the foredune length in the study. Areas in the vicinity of built structures and bulldozing activity also had statistically significantly (p = 0.01) lower average annual changes in accretion that were more distinct at the Berkheide foredune, where the higher dune slopes did not necessarily translate to more accretion in these areas33–35. The comparison of the foredunes only, on the annual scale, is therefore the most relevant comparison to the discussion, where average changes in volume are 1.5 times greater on the Berkheide foredune.

There appears to be no significant advantage of the dike-in-dune or the Berkheide foredune in terms of accretion or vegetation health on the annual or weekly scale, with the exception that the Berkheide foredune is experiencing more accretion since the completion of Kustwerk Katwijk. Therefore, the dike-in-dune foredune is growing similarly to the Berkheide foredune. In other studies, correlations have been found between changes in accretion and vegetation health, which are associated with dune geomorphology.

In a study conducted in Greater Manchester, England, urban areas with high vegetation density were used to determine changes in NDVI and corresponding changes in densities of total greenspace, tree canopy, forbs, shrubs, and grass over one year, for a better assessment of the impacts of greenspace on health36. NDVI maps generated using Sentinel-2 10 m (33 ft) resolution NDVI data determined greenness exposure within distances of 100 m (328 ft), 300 m (984 ft), and 500 m (1640 ft). They showed that the average NDVI is higher for 100 m zones than for greater distances, and that the density of forbs and shrubs is less skewed for 500 m zones than for closer distances. The researchers also completed univariate linear and nonlinear generalized additive models (GAMs) that determined nonlinear relationships between average NDVI and the four greenness metrics (i.e. densities of total greenspace, tree canopy, forbs and shrubs, and grass), which suggested that an increment in average NDVI does not indicate the same unit increment in density but a generally positive correlation between average NDVI and density was observed for increasing the average NDVI up to 0.5. At the 100 m-scale, a unit increment (0.1) of the low average NDVI values (e.g., average NDVI increases from 0.2 to 0.3) was associated with a 17.8% increase in total greenspace, a 10% increase in forbs and shrubs, and a 5% increase in tree canopy and grass coverage. These associations highlight the importance of quantifying vegetation health for the benefit of critical infrastructure and social well-being.

The dike-in-dune integrates coastal resilience and social welfare; marram grass is a contributor to both aspects. Transplanted marram grass does not require strenuous upkeep. The current maintenance practice at the dike-in-dune is to refrain from re-planting marram grass in areas of erosion and allow the transport of eroded sand, seeds, and plants, which encourages plant succession and biodiversity. Thus far, drifting has not disturbed any walking paths or other pedestrian-friendly aspects of the dune16. A study determined that dune toe elevations on the North Sea coast in the Netherlands are increasing at a rate of 7–8 times the rate of sea level rise, with seaward movement37. This suggests that foredunes will require more beachfront space to expand beyond their existing slopes and crests, as observed in the embryonic dunes south of the beach houses at the Berkheide dunes. With the dunes limited by permanent structures at the dike-in-dune and Berkheide, obtaining optimal burial rates for marram grass growth may be a challenge, which could lead to plant succession17,38. Since the rising sea level is expected to erode 60 cm (24 in) of sand from the active foredune at the dike-in-dune within 50 years, and increasing storm surge and wave heights will also increase the risk of wave overtopping, ensuring the vegetation health of all plant species contributing to dune growth will serve as an additional safety measure7. Another study linking marram grass growth to increases in soil moisture found that soil moisture relies more on precipitation than on soil depth or topography, indicating the susceptibility of marram grass to changing climatic conditions39. NDVI has also been used to estimate soil moisture. Future studies capturing pairs of laser and multispectral survey data on a more consistent basis would be advantageous for including considerations of beach width, burial depth, canopy, density, etc.

Conclusion

A field investigation was conducted in June 2023 to compare accretion and vegetation health of the dike-in-dune foredune, as an engineered dune system, and the Berkheide foredune, a naturally existing dune south of the dike-in-dune in Katwijk, NL. Historical LiDAR and multispectral survey data, dating back to the completion of Kustwerk Katwijk, were analyzed to determine the impact of transplanting marram grass on the dike-in-dune. The data indicated average changes in elevation and NDVI for the dike-in-dune and Berkheide foredunes. The Berkheide foredune experiences an average annual volume increase of approximately 1.5 times that of the dike-in-dune foredune, and a yearly change of NDVI that is approximately equal. The annual assessment of the North Sea coastline and dune system does not currently consider vegetation health beyond inspection after storms. However, this study found that there are areas in the foredunes of both the dike-in-dune and Berkheide dune that have statistically significantly (p = 0.01) higher than average annual changes in volume and NDVI, suggesting a generally positive correlation between average accretion rates and average vegetation health. Future studies quantifying the benefits of marram grass on coastal dunes and the community, particularly during growing and storm seasons, can provide more evidence for a centuries-old practice in the Netherlands and aid in mitigating potential cases of coastal squeeze or adverse impacts of extreme weather40.
