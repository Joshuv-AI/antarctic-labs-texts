Improving nutrition in pediatric heart failure
Authors: Shannon Oliver, Stephanie Lavoie, Chentel Cunningham, Jennifer Conway
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeart failure occurs in 0.9 to 3 per 100,000 children, and can be the result of either structural heart disease, genetic, or acquired cardiomyopathies. Malnutrition remains a major concern in this population, and results from the complex interplay between decreased dietary intake, decreased absorption, and an altered metabolic state. Assessing nutritional status remains a challenge, with conventional anthropometric measures often being unsuitable. To this end, the Subjective Global Nutrition Assessment has been developed and this, in combination with indirect calorimetry, can be used to give a better estimate of a child’s nutritional status and caloric needs. Determining the best way to meet these needs requires a multidisciplinary team approach, determining both the most appropriate feeding route and most appropriate type of feed. This is particularly important with the trend toward blended feeds, as these feeds must not only meet protein-energy requirements but must also not exceed daily sodium requirements or fluid restrictions. To further optimize heart failure through nutrition, the use of micronutrient supplementation has evolved. In particular, optimizing vitamin D, selenium, and iron has been shown to be beneficial from a heart failure management perspective. As nutrition plays such a vital role in the medical optimization of pediatric patients with heart failure, it is important to acknowledge the impact this can have on the child and the family unit.KEYWORDS: heart failure, nutrition, pediatrics, blended diet, rapid tube wean, anemia

Background

Heart failure (HF) in pediatrics can be secondary to a variety of causes, including structural abnormalities from congenital heart disease (CHD), myocardial abnormalities such as genetic cardiomyopathies, and acquired causes such as anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathies.1 The annual incidence of HF is reported to be between 0.9 and 3 per 100,000 children in Europe2 and results from a combination of increased preload, increased afterload, decreased myocardial contractility, and/or arrhythmias leading to a mismatch between cardiac output and the body’s metabolic demands.3 HF is common in patients diagnosed with dilated cardiomyopathies, with data from an Australian registry study demonstrating that 89.7% of cases will present in HF.4 Meanwhile, for children with CHD, HF symptomatology has been reported to occur between 6.2% and 23.7% of patients.5, 6

The importance of nutrition in critical illness, such as HF, in the pediatric population has been well described, with studies suggesting that malnutrition is associated with increased morbidity and mortality.7 The cause of malnutrition in pediatric patients with HF is the result of increased energy expenditure and decreased energy supply, secondary to a complex interplay of factors such as decreased intake, malabsorption, and existing in a catabolic state.8, 9 The problems associated with malnutrition are further compounded by deficiencies of certain micronutrients that can exacerbate HF symptomatology.8 The details of the pathophysiology of malnutrition and HF have been well described in Lewis et al’s 2018 paper.9 The aim of this review article is to describe approaches to assessing the nutritional status in pediatric patients with HF and how nutrition can be used to optimize the clinical state of children with HF.

How to assess nutritional status and requirements

Malnutrition remains a major concern in children with HF and is associated with poor clinical outcomes, including delayed postoperative recovery, higher infection rates, and increased morbidity and mortality.10, 11 Causes of malnutrition arise from the unique metabolic challenges faced by these patients, including metabolic changes, reduced dietary intake, and disruptions in nutrient absorption.12, 13 Chronic conditions such as CHD and cardiomyopathy, both of which can lead to HF, frequently lead to growth delays and protein-energy malnutrition, with nearly half of these patients demonstrating anthropometric indicators of malnutrition during their treatment journey.14, 15

Accurately assessing nutritional status in children with HF is challenging because standard markers, such as weight, weight for age, and BMI (Body Mass Index) z-scores, are often impaired by confounding factors such as fluid retention.16 These issues reduce the reliability of traditional methods, leaving practitioners to explore alternative strategies for assessing nutritional status in this population. Recently, there has been a move toward using validated assessment tools such as the Subjective Global Nutritional Assessment (SGNA). The SGNA has been validated in the pediatric population and it has a significant role in identifying malnutrition in children with CHD due to its comprehensive nature that evaluates multiple dimensions of nutritional health. It includes a detailed assessment of medical history, feeding practices, gastrointestinal functionality, and physical measurements such as weight, height, mid-arm circumference, and skin folds along with the respective z-scores.17, 18, 19 It emphasizes trends rather than single measurements, which allows the SGNA to identify malnutrition in children who may be missed when anthropometrics alone are not accurate when edema is present.

In adult literature, body composition (specifically decreased muscle mass) is reported as being associated with increased morbidity and mortality in patients with HF, with both dual-energy X-ray absorption and BOD-POD scans are gaining traction as noninvasive ways of objective quantification.20, 21 While there is currently no literature reporting the use of either of these technologies in the pediatric HF population, the possibility is intriguing. However, there are some limitations that come with the use of dual-energy X-ray absorption scans for nutritional assessments in children, given the radiation exposure and limited availability in clinical settings. For this to be justified, there would need to be further validation of BOD-POD in a research setting, as currently, the limited information available suggests significant technical challenges.22

Children with HF have variable energy requirements, with some having increased demands (hypermetabolism) due to chronic tachycardia, increased myocardial workload, systemic inflammation, and neurohormonal activation,23 while others may have lower energy needs (hypometabolism) due to reduced physical activity and worsening organ function.18 This variation makes it difficult to use predictive equations alone to determine energy requirements and for prevention or treatment of malnutrition. Therefore, other tools such as indirect calorimetry are important when traditional equations are less reliable. Indirect calorimetry is considered the gold standard for determining basal metabolic rate (BMR) and is patient-specific, with direct measures of resting energy expenditure through measurements of oxygen consumption (VO2) and carbon dioxide production (VCO2). This tool can be advantageous in determining the caloric needs of pediatric patients with HF, as the metabolic state varies significantly based on disease severity. BMR represents the energy your body requires to perform essential functions at rest, such as maintaining your heartbeat, respiratory processes, brain activity, and cellular repair. It accounts for the largest part of total energy expenditure, which makes it a critical factor when estimating overall energy requirements.23 Various studies have shown that the use of predictive equations inaccurately estimated the energy expenditure and, therefore, the energy needs of these patients.24, 25, 26 Importantly, Lewis et al27 highlighted that energy requirements can be as much as 2 times higher in children with HF than what predictive equations suggest, underscoring the hypermetabolic state observed in many of these patients. However, these energy needs are dynamic and can significantly shift postoperatively or during other stages of treatment.24 For example, after surgeries such as the Fontan procedure, indirect calorimetry has revealed the variability in resting energy expenditure ranging from hypermetabolic to hypometabolic states depending on factors such as age, recovery progress, and inflammation. Due to this variability, indirect calorimetry will not only ensure accurate initial assessments but should be conducted serially to adjust energy delivery as the child's condition changes.

Access to indirect calorimetry for determining energy requirements in pediatric patients with HF can be challenging; hence, clinicians may rely on the validated predictive equations and tailored clinical judgment. We know that formulas such as the Schofield or WHO (World Health Organization) equations, which estimate BMR based on age, weight, height, and sex, are widely used but can yield variability, potentially underestimating or overestimating energy needs.28 Close and serial monitoring of patient-specific growth patterns, weight trends, and clinical indicators remains essential to accurately address energy needs, promote growth and recovery, and prevent risks associated with underfeeding or overfeeding. A study conducted by Marino et al29 has shown that nutritional care must be a central component in the management of patients with CHD. They used a protocol that involved early identification of growth failure by regular and serial anthropometric checks, individualizing energy needs, optimizing feeding methods, pre and post op nutrition support, and ongoing monitoring and adjustments. This data support that using an assessment tool, such as the SGNA, serially can be helpful to monitor a patient's progress of malnutrition and growth.

How do we deliver adequate nutrition?

The choice of nutrition and route of providing nutrition in children with HF can play an important role in ensuring they are meeting their metabolic demands and nutritional requirements. Children with HF can struggle to absorb nutrients due to a combination of increased gut wall edema and decreased gut perfusion, with adult literature suggesting that patients with HF have a 30% to 45% decrease in blood flow through the superior and inferior mesenteric arteries and celiac artery compared to healthy patients.30 With this in mind, it is important to select feeds that can be easily digested and absorbed.30 The use of elemental feeds, which consist of amino acids and predigested proteins, can help mitigate the absorption challenges and feeding intolerance which is often encountered.31 An additional challenge faced with providing enteral nutrition is that patients are often fluid-restricted as part of their HF management.9 In context of this, feeds may need to be fortified to meet protein and energy requirements.32

Recently, blended food diets have started to replace formula-based diets, due to the evidence that they can help with some of the gastrointestinal symptoms such as vomiting and diarrhea which can be seen with commercial formulas, as well caregiver preference view this diet as more “natural,” and blended diets improve the sense of normalcy with feeds.33, 34 Current recommendations from the British Dietetic Association with respect to blended diets include:

In patients with HF symptomatology, it is particularly important to ensure that these feeds do not exceed the daily recommended sodium intake, as high sodium diets can result in fluid retention and clinical symptoms of fluid overload.9 Specific contraindications to a blended diet include children unable to tolerate gastric feeds (requiring transpyloric feeding), children at increased risk of foodborne infections, children who require a nasogastric tube (NGT) rather than a gastrostomy tube, and infants under 6 months of age.34

A further factor to consider is the route by which the nutrition is provided. The factors to consider are whether a child can take adequate nutrition on their own orally or whether a child requires a feeding tube. The choice of feeding tube is predominantly driven by feeding tolerance and risk of a procedure. For children who can be fed into their stomach, an NGT can be utilized as can a gastrostomy (G-tube). Both G-tube and NGT feeding have been demonstrated as an effective way to provide nutrition for children who are not able to meet their goals orally.35, 36 G-tubes are typically thought to be more secure than NGTs; however, the insertion is more invasive, with a risk associated with the need for general anesthesia in a patient with HF and the ongoing risk of stoma site infections.37, 38 Despite the perceived benefit of G-tubes having more stability than NGTs, a 2017 study by Khalil et al comparing infants on home NGT feeds with those on home G-tube feeds found that infants with G-tubes had more emergency department presentations for device-related issues in the first 6 months postdischarge than those with NGTs, and that 39.1% of those presentations were due to G-tube dislodgement.39 This would suggest that for patients for whom a G-tube would pose an unnecessary anesthetic risk that the use of an NGT is a reasonable alternative.

A final option for enteral feeding is the use of transpyloric feeds. Transpyloric feeds can be beneficial in that the nutrition is delivered directly to the site of absorption and can help bypass difficulties with gastric emptying, vomiting, and aspiration.40, 41 However, the downside to transpyloric feeds is that these feeds need to be given continuously rather than as bolus feeds. This not only provides social limitations in that children will be attached to feeds for most of the day, but also limits feed options such as no longer being able to have blended diets.

There is a subgroup of pediatric patients with HF who are either unable to absorb or unable to tolerate adequate amounts of enteral feeds, who will require parenteral nutrition. While there are no specific guidelines for pediatric patients with HF, the American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition guidelines exist for the use of parenteral nutrition in critically unwell patients.7 Importantly, the inability to tolerate enteral nutrition is suggestive of advanced HF, and treating teams should be considering referring to an advanced HF service for consideration of ventricular assist device (VAD) support.42, 43

How can we optimize heart function through nutrition?

The importance of adequate macronutrients has been well described in patients with HF,9 with a recent focus on supplementation of micronutrients8 as part of the goal of optimizing nutrition in patients with HF. Below, we discuss key macro and micronutrients that should be considered when trying to optimize.

Macronutrients

Protein

Decreased protein intake in both adults and children with HF has been associated with loss of skeletal muscle loss and increased mortality.9, 44 Children with chronic diseases such as HF can develop protein-energy wasting, otherwise known as cachexia, secondary to increased BMR, lean muscle wasting, and inefficient use of fat for energy.45 In context of this, there have been suggestions that infants should have a caloric intake of 150 kcal/kg/d and children and adolescents 25 to 30 kcal/kg/day with 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg/day of this being protein,46 or an increase in protein by 50% to 100% of the dietary reference intake.12 Adequate protein intake is essential to ensure there is a consistent supply of amino acids9 which is essential for protein synthesis, tissue repair, and energy.9, 47 Furthermore, certain amino acids such as carnitine or glutathione (made from glycine, cysteine, and glutamic acid) are thought to be associated with cellular function of the heart.47

Carbohydrates

Adequate carbohydrate intake is important as it is the primary source of energy for the brain.12 The current recommendation in pediatric HF is to keep daily carbohydrate intake to 6 g/kg/day.44 In a normal heart, 60% to 90% of cardiac adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is provided by fatty acid oxidization; however, in patients with HF, there is thought to be a switch to glucose oxidation, as carbohydrates are more oxygen-efficient than fatty acids.48 This would suggest ensuring adequate carbohydrate intake is an important part of optimizing nutrition for patients in HF. A recent pilot study in the adult diabetic cardiomyopathy population suggested that while a low-carbohydrate diet improved weight loss and quality of life, there was no improvement in their Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire summary score, and acknowledged further studies are needed to assess the effects of a low-carbohydrate diet on cardiac function.49 A potential explanation for the benefit of low low-carbohydrate diet is that it mimics the effects of sodium glucose cotranspoort-2 inhibitors, which have been demonstrated to be effective in HF management.50 However, this study has only been performed in a specific subtype of cardiomyopathy and may not be generalizable to pediatric patients with HF. At this stage, there is no convincing evidence to recommend low-carbohydrate diets in the pediatric population.

Fatty acids

Omega-3 fatty acids supplementation in the adult HF population has been demonstrated to be associated with improved cardiac function, decreased risk of arrhythmias, and reduced hospitalizations.51 This is likely in part due to the fact that omega-3 is associated with lowering inflammation and helping with ventricular remodeling.51 Omega-3 has been demonstrated to have additional benefits in the pediatric population, including supporting vision and cognitive function.9, 52 In light of these benefits, we would suggest supplementation if children are not reaching the adequate intakes outlined by the National Institutes of Health.53

Micronutrients

Iron

Iron deficiency is common in patients with HF symptoms, secondary to abnormalities in iron storage, reduced intake, and decreased availability of iron through the reticuloendothelial system.54, 55 As cardiomyocytes have high metabolic demand, the presence of iron deficiency and the resulting impairment of mitochondrial ATP generation worsen cardiac dysfunction and HF symptomatology.55, 56 Studies into iron deficiency in the pediatric HF population have demonstrated that iron deficiency is associated with worse ventricular function.56, 57 While both these studies demonstrated a decrease in function associated with iron deficiency, they had conflicting results with regards to longer-term sequelae such as need for a VAD, death, or transplant,56, 57 which needs to be taken in context of the fact that iron deficiency was not routinely monitored. Use of iron supplementation has been well studied in the adult population, which suggested that oral iron supplementation has limited impacts on a patients’ clinical status, while intravenous supplementation is associated with reduced hospitalizations, improved functional status, and quality of life.56 In context of this current adult guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology and the American Heart Association recommend using ferric carboxymaltose, an intravenous formulation to treat iron deficiency, with 1 study suggesting that there can be benefit even in the absence of anemia.55, 58, 59 A recent study in the pediatric population examining the use of oral iron supplementations demonstrated similar results to the adult IRONOUT-HF study, which was that oral supplementation was ineffective at increasing serum iron levels.60 The explanation for this is likely a combination of reduced absorption due to gut wall edema, and the fact that as HF is a chronic inflammatory state, there may be an increase in hepcidin inhibiting iron absorption.60 In context of the fact that iron deficiency has been demonstrated to be associated with worse function, and may potentially increase the risk of adverse sequelae, we would recommend routine monitoring and supplementation, While standard iron deficiency guidelines suggest oral supplementation initially before escalating to intravenous,60 given oral iron has been demonstrated at being ineffective at raising serum iron levels in patients with HF, consideration should be given to intravenous iron as first line therapy in all patients, irrespective of whether they are hospitalized or at home.

Selenium

Selenium is a micronutrient that is incorporated into selenoproteins, which mediate the cardiovascular system via inflammatory and antioxidative effects.61 Deficiencies in selenium have been associated with a specific form of cardiomyopathy (Keshan disease).61, 62 However, even in the absence of Keshan disease, studies have demonstrated that patients with dilated cardiomyopathy tend to have lower serum levels of selenium compared to those without it.61, 63 While there have been no studies determining the effectiveness of routine selenium supplementation in pediatric patients with HF, an analysis of patients from the BIOSTAT-CHF study, suggested in older adults that selenium supplementation may improve patient outcomes, however this was an observational study, and there were multiple confounding factors including the coadministration of coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10), and as such they recommended further prospective clinical trials to determine the benefits of selenium supplementation.64

Zinc

Zinc plays an important role in cell membrane stabilization by acting as a cofactor for enzymes involved in the antioxidant systems and by inhibiting pro-oxidant enzymes.61 Deficiencies in zinc have been demonstrated to be associated with dilated cardiomyopathies, coronary artery injury, and myocardial infarction and reduced function.61 Dietary deficiencies may be compounded in patients with HF due to activation of atrial natriuretic peptide and subsequent increased urinary excretion of zinc.61 There is some early evidence to suggest that zinc supplementation may be beneficial if deficient,65 but this is controversial as current American Heart Association guidelines suggest that there is no direct benefit from routine supplementation.58

Vitamin D

Patients with HF are at risk of vitamin D deficiency due to a combination of renal insufficiency, medications, and poor nutrition.9 Vitamin D plays an essential role not only in bone health, but also with regards to supporting myocardial contractility and cardiac remodeling.8, 9 There is observational evidence that vitamin D deficiency is associated with worse outcomes for patients with HF,66 and small studies in the adult population demonstrating an improvement in ejection fraction with vitamin D supplementation.67, 68 While 25(OH[Hydroxy]) vitamin D levels of ≥20 ng/ml (≥50 nmol/liter) are typically considered sufficient, there has been suggestions that in patients with HF, having a 25(OH) vitamin D level <30 ng/ml (<75 nmol/liter) may actually be insufficient.69 While there currently is no specific recommendations for what the target 25(OH) vitamin D level should be in pediatric patients with HF, given the fact that they may require higher than standard targets to be considered sufficient, it would be reasonable to continue to follow the suggestion of Lewis et al, and aim to keep serum 25(OH) vitamin D levels 32 ng/ml (≥80 nmol/liter),9 to ensure an adequate buffer. There are currently no guidelines as to how frequently therapy should be monitored in patients with HF. We would suggest using a similar approach to that which is recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics for monitoring vitamin D levels in rickets patients, whereby this is assessed at diagnosis, then if treatment is commenced, 3 months after this to ensure an adequate response, and then annually.70

Coenzyme Q10

CoQ10 is involved in the synthesis of ATP, cell membrane stabilization, and has antioxidant properties which may help prevent cardiac myocyte injury.8 Dietary deficiency in CoQ10 is reported to be associated with lower ejection fraction, increased NT-proBNP levels, and worse New York Heart Association functional class.8 A recent Cochrane review suggests that CoQ10 may reduce all-cause cardiovascular mortality, but that the current evidence is not robust enough to draw conclusive recommendations with regards to the benefit of CoQ10 supplementation and HF optimization.71

What is the family perspective on nutrition?

Qualitative analyses have shown that a diagnosis of HF is a source of significant stress for parents and caregivers.72, 73 One of the identified stressors that has been reported is feeding and nutrition.72 The reason that feeding and nutrition is a stressor is multifactorial, resulting from a combination of feeding and weight gain being considered a surrogate marker for disease progression, and that nutrition is typically a basic need provided by caregivers.72, 74 In children with complex health needs, who are highly medicalized with very little normalcy, striving to orally feed can result from a parent hoping that their child can participate in something that is considered “normal.”74

A desire for children to feed orally has led to the rise of rapid tube weaning programs, which aim to use a multidisciplinary approach to rapidly transition a child from NGT or G-tube to full oral feeding over a period of weeks.75, 76 While these programs have been demonstrated to be successful and show that at 1 year post wean children are gaining weight appropriately,77 they are associated with a decrease in both protein and energy intake at the time of wean, with associated weight loss.77, 78 There have been no studies specifically examining rapid tube weans in the HF population. Considering the importance of adequate caloric and protein intake in our cohort, we would recommend proceeding with caution in this group, as they may not tolerate the period of decreased energy intake and weight loss as well as children without underlying HF.

Conclusion

Managing nutrition in children with HF is a vital part of their medical care. These children often have higher than expected metabolic demands, and assessing these can be challenging. Ensuring children are taking the right types of nutrition, via the most appropriate route is paramount to ensuring they are meeting their nutritional goals, acknowledging that if they are not able to reach this with enteral nutrition alone, this would be an indication to consider advanced HF therapies such as VAD support.

Adequate protein intake is essential for children with HF, and feeding regimes and diets should be designed to target a daily goal of 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg/day. While there has been some considerations of low-carbohydrate diets in specific adult HF populations, at this stage, we would recommend continuing with standard carbohydrate intake. From a supplementation perspective, there is currently evidence to suggest supplementation with omega-3, vitamin D, and intravenous iron to improve nutritional outcomes. While there is currently no conclusive evidence to support routine supplementation of micronutrients such as zinc, selenium, or CoQ10, this is an area of evolving interest.

As feeding and nutrition play such an integral role in the management of children with HF, it is important to consider the impact this can have on families, and to work as a team with the families to ensure that the child’s clinical state and the mental health of the family are both being supported during this journey.
