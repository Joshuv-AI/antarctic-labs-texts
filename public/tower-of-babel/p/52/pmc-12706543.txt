A Cross‐Sectional Analysis of Social Determinants of Health in Bipolar Disorder: Exploring Gender‐Related Differences
Authors: Manuel Gardea‐Resendez, Manuel Fuentes‐Salgado, Francisco Romo‐Nava, Miguel L Prieto, David J Bond, Aysegul Ozerdem, Hannah K Betcher, Katherine M Moore, Melissa Solares‐Bravo, Mete Ercis, Alessandro Miola, Jorge A Sanchez‐Ruiz, Marin Veldic, Balwinder Singh, Alfredo B Cuellar‐Barboza, Brandon J Coombes, Joanna M Biernacka, Susan L McElroy, Monica J Taylor‐Desir, Mark A Frye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo explore the clinical impact of social determinants of health (SDoH) stress in bipolar disorder (BD) course of illness and to explore potential gender differences in SDoH stress endorsement.Methods2280 individuals living with BD type I or II (62% women; 67.5% BD‐I) participating in the Mayo Clinic Bipolar Biobank who completed a questionnaire assessing positive and negative‐valent stressors in the 12 months prior to study enrollment were included for this analysis. Six negative stressors were used as proxies of SDoH. Generalized linear models were used to test the association between SDoH stressors and markers of illness severity as well as potential differences by sex by including an interaction term.ResultsAfter adjusting for age, sex, and recruitment site, SDoH‐related stressors were significantly associated with a more severe course of illness in both men and women. Except for unemployment rates, women reported more frequently SDoH‐related stress (i.e., lack of family support, financial problems, health coverage and access problems).ConclusionsThe study found significant gender differences in both the endorsement of specific SDoH stressors and in the prevalence of specific illness severity markers; however, it did not directly test the predictive relationship between gender differences in SDoH and illness severity markers. Future studies aiming to achieve health equity in mental health care should continue assessing social determinants of health stressors, incorporating a gender perspective to better understand their influence on bipolar disorder.Keywords: bipolar disorder, sex differences, social determinants of health, stress

Introduction

Individuals living with bipolar disorder (BD) struggle to achieve a satisfactory social and occupational level of functioning, impeding attainment of their personal goals [1, 2]. Psychosocial difficulties, illness‐related functional impairment, and healthcare‐related (i.e., economic costs, access to treatment) factors are among the most frequent drivers of stress in BD [3, 4, 5]. These difficulties translate into a disproportionately high occurrence of a spectrum of socioenvironmentally disadvantaged circumstances that negatively impact mood outcomes and quality of life [6, 7]. As conceptualized by the World Health Organization (WHO), adverse social determinants of health (SDoH), namely, non‐medical social and environmental circumstances in which people live that adversely influence health outcomes (Figure 1a), are increasingly recognized as fundamental drivers of mental health disparities, with those experiencing social marginalization and injustice bearing a disproportionately higher burden of psychiatric morbidity, ultimately impacting lifespan and quality of life [8]. In BD, adverse SDoH have been associated with increased odds of nonadherence to atypical antipsychotics, higher likelihood of hospitalizations, and higher health‐related BD costs [9, 10]. Given the heightened vulnerability of individuals with BD to disruptive life events and adverse SDoH, the combination of these factors with illness‐related stress and disability may create a synergistic negative impact on well‐being [11, 12].

Evidence suggests that individuals with BD, when compared to controls, have an increased perception of stress, particularly prior to illness onset and at admission for inpatient care [13]. We have previously reported significant differences in stress experiences with higher endorsement of stress burden in those with type II BD (BD‐II) when compared to those with type I BD (BD‐I) [14]. In this study, the BD‐I cohort was composed of significantly more males than the BD‐II cohort, raising the question about potential gender differences in stress burden and perception. While few studies have focused on this topic, there is evidence suggesting that women with BD, when compared to men, are exposed to more social and health stressors, including lower scholarly achievement, more unemployment, and a longer duration of untreated illness, which may point towards gender‐based inequalities and barriers to accessing mental health services [15].

Despite negative social stressors being consistently identified in BD, there is a significant gap in the study and understanding of the impact of adverse SDoH factors in BD and other mental disorders. This is due, in part, to the significant challenges of assessing SDoH in research settings due to their inherent complexity and lack of standardized measurement tools, which makes it difficult to isolate the specific impact of one SDoH on mental health [16].

By utilizing pre‐defined, clinically relevant socioenvironmental factors as proxies, in this secondary analysis, we sought (1) to evaluate the association between SDoH‐related stress and BD severity and (2) to compare gender differences in the influence of these stressors in participants with BD [17].

Methods

Participants

Participants from the Mayo Clinic Bipolar Disorder Biobank who completed the Stanley Foundation Bipolar Network (SFBN) Life Stress Questionnaire were included in this study [18]. Established in 2009, this Biobank consists of 2286 individuals aged 18 through 80 years of age with a known diagnosis of types I or II bipolar disorder or schizoaffective disorder, bipolar type, further confirmed with the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM‐IV (SCID‐IV) [19], and with no active suicidal ideation or psychosis at the time of enrollment [17]. Participants completed sociodemographic questionnaires and a detailed clinical phenotyping and provided a blood sample to investigate biomarkers for disease risk and pharmacogenomic treatment response. Details on the study design and specific phenotyping measures have been reviewed in earlier work [17].

Recruitment sites included Mayo Clinic (Rochester, MN), the Lindner Center of HOPE/University of Cincinnati (Mason, OH), the University of Minnesota (Minneapolis, MN), the Universidad Autónoma de Nuevo León (Monterrey, Mexico) and the Universidad de los Andes (Santiago, Chile). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board from each participating institution, and every participant provided written informed consent.

Clinical Phenotype and Stressor Measurements

In addition to the SCID‐based diagnostic confirmation, research clinicians administered the Bipolar Biobank Clinical Questionnaire (BiB‐CQ) to further phenotype BD illness trajectory, including psychiatric and medical comorbidities, medication use, and measures of illness severity, among others [17]. For the current analysis, we focused specifically on the lifetime presence of clinical features indicative of illness severity: history of psychosis, attempted suicide, alcohol dependence, substance abuse, rapid cycling, and anxiety disorders. Based on prior studies on the association between binge eating disorder and greater illness burden in BD, and its increased prevalence in patients with BD, we included this comorbidity as an indicative of illness severity [20, 21].

Participants completed a modified version of the Stanley Foundation Bipolar Network (SFBN) Life Stress Questionnaire [4, 18], which assesses 11 positive (e.g., engagement/marriage, adoption/birth of a child, etc.) and 15 negative stressors common in adulthood. Specific items of the SFBN can be reviewed in Table S1. The modification sought to capture the occurrence of specific life events during the 12 months prior to the biobank enrollment [14]. Participants rated the impact of the stressor as 0 = stressor not present, 1 = present but no impact, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate, and 4 = severe. In the absence of a unique standardized tool to routinely assess SDoH, six negative stressors from the SFBN that reflected the WHO five core domains were chosen (Figure 1) [22]. As shown in Figure 1C, these included stressors related to economic stability (unemployment, financial problems), educational access (educational problems), healthcare access (inadequate coverage and access), neighborhood environment (housing problems), and social support (lack of family support). This approach allowed for the operationalization of complex social constructs into quantifiable variables, facilitating the examination of associations with BD course of illness.

Statistical Analysis

In descriptive statistics, we reported frequencies with percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations to report age. Logistic regression models were used to examine associations between gender and SDoH‐related stress, adjusting for age, sex, and recruitment site. Generalized linear models were used to test the association between SDoH‐related stressors and selected clinical outcomes. All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (v4.4.1; R Core Team 2024).

Results

A total of 2280 participants with BD were analyzed, including 1540 with BD‐I (67.5%) and 740 with BD‐II (32.5%). Demographic data and clinical characteristics related to illness severity of participants are presented in Table 1. 62% of the sample (n = 1412) was female and mean (SD) age was 41.7 (14.9) years; there were no differences in age nor age at diagnosis between male and female participants. Regarding clinical markers of illness severity, history of suicide attempts (40% vs. 25%; p < 0.001), rapid cycling (60% vs. 51%; p < 0.001) and binge eating (16% vs. 6%; p < 0.001) were more common in women, while history of alcohol dependence (34% vs. 44%; p < 0.001) and substance abuse (17% vs. 22%; p = 0.003) were significantly more common in men.

Gender differences in SDoH stressors were observed (Figure 1C). While unemployment problems were more common in men (36% vs. 29%; p = 0.001), women more frequently endorsed lack of family support (25% vs. 42%; p = 0.001), financial problems (49% vs. 53%; p = 0.05), health coverage (16% vs. 21%; p = 0.006) and access to health care (14% vs. 18%; p = 0.03). No significant differences were observed in educational or housing problems.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first cross‐sectional study to specifically explore the association between social determinants of health and markers of BD illness severity. Aligned with gender differences clearly established in the literature, in our cohort, men had higher rates of alcohol and substance use disorders, while women had higher rates of rapid cycling, suicide attempts, and binge eating. Our findings further suggest differential gender differences in SDoH associations with illness severity. Women from our sample, compared to men, were also more frequently affected by specific SDoH stress experiences, including lack of family support, financial problems, and health coverage and access problems. While the study demonstrates these distinct gender‐based patterns in both SDoH stress and illness severity, it did not directly test whether gender differences in SDoH are predictive of gender differences in specific illness severity markers.

In the absence of studies focused on BD and SDoH, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the high rates of unemployment and socioeconomic burden in BD might be indicative of broader environmental struggles [5, 6, 10]. For instance, socioeconomic and neighborhood deprivation in BD has been associated with all‐cause mortality [23, 24]. To our knowledge, the exact pathways that drive the association between SDoH stressors and illness severity in BD have not been studied. However, restricted access to high‐quality education and health care, combined with higher availability of unhealthy foods, reduced possibilities to exercise, and fewer community and neighborhood resources, as a consequence of socioeconomic challenges and as a source of stress, could potentially contribute to the mechanisms behind the association between SDoH stressors and BD illness severity [23]. Another possible contributing pathway could involve an increased susceptibility to negative stress perception and dysfunctional coping styles; as previously mentioned, this has been identified more prominently in patients with BD‐II compared to BD‐I [14, 25]. Factors such as adverse childhood experiences, cumulative and repetitive stress coupled with adverse socioeconomic conditions impact problem‐approaching coping strategies and the perception of solvability and controllability of stressors, including the capacity to overcome them [26]. Consequently, the bidirectionality between adverse socioeconomic conditions and mental health outcomes is further worsened when other negative stressors, including other biological, psychological, and social factors cooccur [27, 28].

While a significant proportion of the participants in our study endorsed SDoH related stress during the 12 months prior to their study enrollment, women reported higher rates of SDoH stressors in five of seven domains questioned. Interestingly, women were more often employed than men but, despite this, they still reported higher rates of financial problems and more barriers to access healthcare and health coverage. These findings highlight previously recognized gender disparities in mental health care that might hinder help‐seeking behaviors in women living with BD [15, 29]. Further studies looking into illness‐related and external factors contributing to these increased SDoH stress burdens are needed to facilitate pathways to care and psychosocial interventions for women with BD.

This study has several strengths and limitations that need to be taken into consideration. First, this study included participants with a diagnosis of BD confirmed through a semi‐structured interview that underwent detailed clinical phenotyping, including assessment of markers of illness severity and of frequent psychiatric comorbidities. Second, our cohort was large in size and composed of individuals from different sociocultural backgrounds (i.e., recruitment sites, race) which allowed us to capture life and stress experiences unique to each site. While the selected stressors questionnaire is limited by the absence of clinical validation, it was modified to examine stress experiences in the 12 months prior to the study and assessed the presence/absence of preestablished scenarios, including common real‐world live events [14]. In this line, this study addresses two understudied areas within research on BD: health inequities and disparities, and women's mental health. Limitations of this study need to be considered for interpreting our findings. First, the cross‐sectional design of this study and the retrospective assessment of stress can be considered by itself a limitation, particularly because of the risk of recall bias and the fact that a confirmatory answer to an item does not necessarily mean that the given stressor was present at the time of enrollment. This design limited the possibility to examine whether gender differences in SDoH predicted corresponding differences in illness severity markers. Furthermore, we did not assess for sex‐ and gender‐specific physical and psychological stressors (e.g., intimate partner violence, sexism, gender stereotyping and challenges to reproductive liberty) that reflect the unique SDoH challenges that, oftentimes, women are exposed to [8, 30].

Despite the recognized deleterious effect of stress in BD, there is an incomplete understanding of the processes linking stressors and bipolar illness trajectory. Approaching the unique socioenvironmental background of an individual with BD as a non‐biological factor that increases the likelihood of chronic stress, recurrent stressor exposure, and impaired controllability perception may result in an earlier identification of timepoints of increased risk for mood relapse [4, 26]. In this line, we propose that adverse SDoH and gender‐specific stressors should be viewed as contributors/participants in amplifying the allostatic load where, together with chronic stress, medical and psychiatric comorbidities, and functional impairment affect and are affected by BD [31]. Adverse SDoH, the resolution of which is highly dependent on external factors, can act as chronic stressors, leading to heightened HPA axis activity and increased neuroinflammation, among other pathways implicated in BD pathophysiology [32, 33]. This chronic stress, in turn, can exacerbate mood instability, leading to increased comorbidities and functional impairment, further compromising social circumstances and perpetuating the cycle (e.g., unemployment leading to financial hardship, which in turn worsens BD symptoms, making it harder to find and maintain employment) [16]. This cycle illustrates how SDoH‐related stressors, interacting with illness‐related factors, can create a self‐reinforcing loop that worsens BD outcomes.

In conclusion, findings from this analysis, focused on exploring associations between specific SDoH stressors and specific severity markers within each gender, align with existing literature on gender disparities in mental health. Hence, conceptualizing adverse SDoH as both contributors to and consequences of bipolar illness severity can reduce the underappreciation of their impact in BD and lead to a routine monitoring of social circumstances in clinical practice. Considering our findings and to achieve health equity, future studies should prospectively assess the impact on bipolar disorder of unique stress experiences, including social determinants of health stressors, which can worsen illness trajectory, and focus on strengthening causal inference in research on social determinants of health and primary prevention in bipolar disorder.
