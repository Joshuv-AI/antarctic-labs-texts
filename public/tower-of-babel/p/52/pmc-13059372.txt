Multiple transcription factors and genes jointly regulate the light-dependent flavonoid biosynthesis mechanism of stem color in Hydrangea macrophylla
Authors: Jiqing Peng, Shiqin Gu, Xujie Dong, Chaozhen Zeng, Ruoyun Zhou, Shuzhan Li, Sumeiqi Zhao, Song Sheng, Zhongquan Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe coloration of hydrangea stems significantly enhances their ornamental value and resilience, yet its molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. The formation of colored stems is regulated by biological macromolecular such as transcription factors and structural genes. This study investigated the biochemical and molecular mechanisms underlying dark purplish-red stem formation in the self-crossbred hydrangea variety "Taube," with a particular focus on light-dependent regulation. Biochemical assays revealed light-regulated flavonoid biosynthesis, pivotal for aesthetic and functional traits. Network analysis identified key genes and transcription factors linked to pigmentation. Integration of metabolomic data with transcription factor analysis identified four key regulatory elements—MYC1, MYC2-like, MYB12, and MYB1R1—as significantly associated with stem pigmentation. These findings elucidate the complex interplay of physiological and biochemical processes governing stem color formation in hydrangeas, involving the coordinated regulation of flavonoid biosynthesis, accumulation, and distribution by multiple transcription factors and genes. Introduction

Ornamental plants captivate our senses with a kaleidoscope of vibrant hues, from the delicate petals of flowers to the lush foliage and stems. The aesthetic value of these plants largely depends on their colorful displays, which are crucial for attracting pollinators and protecting against various environmental stresses [42, 51]. The presence of pigmented stems adds an additional layer of visual interest, thereby increasing the overall ornamental and market value of plants. More importantly, pigmented stems enriched in anthocyanins and other flavonoids contribute substantially to plant defense by effectively mitigating damage caused by harmful ultraviolet radiation [10]. In addition, stem pigmentation can act as a deterrent to herbivorous insects, reducing the likelihood of stem damage due to feeding [13, 16]. This dual role of stem pigmentation, integrating both ornamental and stress-protective functions, highlights its ecological and horticultural significance in both natural ecosystems and cultivated environments [5]. In hydrangea, deeply pigmented stems are relatively rare and therefore markedly enhance cultivar distinctiveness and commercial value, making stem coloration an increasingly desirable trait in ornamental breeding.

Flavonoids, key plant pigments, contribute to both aesthetic traits and stress resistance. These compounds are responsible not only for the red, blue, and purple hues typical of anthocyanins but also for the yellow and pale colors observed in many plant parts [1, 46]. The formation of flavonoids in plant tissues is affected by environmental factors including pH, light, temperature, and metal ions [17]. These factors contribute to the diversity in pigmentation patterns seen across various plant organs, such as stems and flowers [20, 22]. Flavonoids serve multiple roles in plant physiology, including UV protection, pathogen defense, and signaling. These functions are particularly important in stems, where pigmentation can protect against abiotic stress and herbivory, thereby contributing to the overall fitness of the plant [37, 40]. As a major subclass of flavonoids, anthocyanins have also been shown in previous studies to accumulate extensively in young stems and hypocotyls in response to light, where they alleviate photooxidative damage and enhance tolerance to ultraviolet radiation and other abiotic stresses [7, 14]. This dual functionality of flavonoid compounds further underscores their significance in plant physiology and ecological adaptation [1].

The structural differences in anthocyanins, including glycosylation and acylation, affect their stability and color expression differently across various plant organs. For example, specific anthocyanin glycosides can result in different hues in leaves compared to flowers [19]. The microenvironment within the cell, such as vacuolar pH, plays a crucial role in determining the final color of anthocyanins, especially in flowers and fruits where pH-induced color changes are more pronounced [44]. Furthermore, environmental factors such as light, temperature, and nutrient availability can differentially influence anthocyanin accumulation in various plant organs [27]. Light, in particular, directly regulates the expression of genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis, leading to varied coloration in different tissues. Flavonoids beyond anthocyanins also significantly influence the coloration of plant organs, including stems. The biosynthesis of flavonoids in stems, akin to other plant parts, is regulated by a complex network of genes, including those encoding MYB transcription factors [4]. These genes are essential for the spatial and temporal regulation of flavonoid production, ensuring appropriate pigment distribution and intensity [18]. While anthocyanins are ubiquitous across various plant organs, recent research has highlighted significant differences in the mechanisms of their coloration among tissues [49]. The expression of anthocyanins is often regulated by specific genetic factors, with MYB transcription factors playing a predominant role in anthocyanin biosynthesis [6, 9]. However, their expression can vary significantly among flowers, fruits, and leaves [48]. Such differences in gene regulation can lead to diverse pigmentation patterns within the same plant species. For instance, the MYB12 and MYB19S genes regulate pigmentation in distinct flower parts of lilies, resulting in unique color patterns [47]. Collectively, anthocyanins participate in the regulation of diverse pigmentation patterns across plant organs through complex interactions among genetic, environmental, and structural factors. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms provides critical insights into the adaptive significance and functional roles of anthocyanins in different plant tissues.

Hydrangea macrophylla, commonly known as bigleaf hydrangea, is a popular ornamental plant widely cultivated for its large and colorful inflorescences. The coloration of flowers is primarily influenced by anthocyanins, which are crucial for the vibrant hues that enhance the plant's attractiveness. Previous studies have demonstrated that hydrangea flower color is jointly determined by the composition, abundance, and intracellular status of anthocyanins, among which delphinidin-based anthocyanins predominate in blue-flowered cultivars [20, 22]. In recent years, substantial progress has been made in elucidating the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying anthocyanin biosynthesis, modification, and stabilization, particularly with respect to the synergistic interactions between anthocyanins, metal ions, and co-pigments [50]. In H. macrophylla, the blue coloration of sepals is mainly attributed to the specific interaction of aluminum ions (Al3⁺) with delphinidin-3-O-glucoside and co-pigments such as 5-O-caffeoylquinic acid within an acidic vacuolar environment, leading to the formation of a stable blue anthocyanin–metal–co-pigment complex [20, 50]. At the molecular level, integrative transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses have revealed the central roles of key structural genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis (such as F3′5′H, DFR, and ANS) as well as regulatory transcription factors in hydrangea flower color formation [8, 32, 33]. Notably, several MYB transcription factors, including MYB2-related regulators, have been identified as critical components controlling anthocyanin accumulation and spatial color patterning in hydrangea floral tissues [33]. These studies provide a solid theoretical foundation for a systematic understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing flower color formation in H. macrophylla.

Despite the substantial progress made, there remains a relative paucity of genetic resources dedicated specifically to stem color research, not only in H. macrophylla but across various ornamental species. To explore the mechanisms by which light influences the formation of deep purplish red stems in hydrangeas, this study utilized a purple-stemmed hydrangea cultivar developed by self-cross of cultivar “Taube”. Investigations were conducted at phenotypic, physiological, metabolic, and transcriptional levels on the epidermis of green stems at the early developmental stage (S1, green), the epidermis of normally developing purple stems (S2, deep purplish red), and the epidermis of stems treated with aluminum foil to prevent coloration (shading treatment(ST), colourless). This study investigates the molecular basis of stem coloration in H. macrophylla, focusing on light-dependent flavonoid regulation.

Materials and method

Plant materials and treatments

Hydrangea varieties were cultivated in the nursery of Central South University of Forestry and Technology (Changsha China), with a total of 103 varieties preserved. The experimental soil had a pH value of 6.8. Among the self-pollinated progeny of the hydrangea variety “Taube”, a plant with deep purplish red stems was identified, which exhibited emerald green stems at the young stem stage (S1) and dark purplish red stems at the mature stem stage (S2). Clones propagated from this plant served as the study subjects. To investigate stem color changes, stems at the S1 stage were covered with aluminum foil for 20 d, and their color changes were observed, with normally developing stem color serving as the control. The epidermis of the stems was collected as experimental material, with three samples collected for each treatment as biological replicates. The samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for later analysis.

Analysis of hydrangea stem color using colorimeter

Photographs of the stems of various hydrangea varieties in the nursery were captured, and a colorimeter was employed to detect the. stem color of varieties exhibiting significant color differences. Particular attention was directed towards the stem color changes in the progeny of the dark red-stemmed hydrangea variety “Taube” The mean of five measurements was considered one effective value, with 30 effective values measured per variety. The Royal Horticultural Society's color chart was utilized to document the color values of different treatments, and a stereomicroscope was employed to observe differences in the stem epidermis. This methodological approach ensured precise and consistent measurement of color variations, contributing to a comprehensive understanding of phenotypic changes in hydrangea stems.

Measurement of anthocyanins and flavonoids in hydrangea stem epidermis

Anthocyanin level measurement

The stem epidermis of hydrangeas, stored at −80 °C, was pulverized into powder utilizing liquid nitrogen. A 0.2 g aliquot was promptly weighed and subjected to extraction in 1% acidic methanol solution at 4 °C, in darkness for a duration of 24 h. This was followed by an ultrasonic suspension phase lasting 1 h. The resultant mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was subsequently filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Anthocyanin levels were measured by extracting stem epidermis tissue and analyzing absorbance at 530 nm using an ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometer (UV-3802, Unico, Suite E Dayton, OH, USA). For accuracy and reproducibility, three biological replicates were prepared for each time point.

Flavonoid level measurement

The stem epidermis of hydrangeas stored at −80 °C was ground into powder using liquid nitrogen. A sample of 0.2 g of the powder was weighed and added to 10 mL of methanol solution, followed by extraction at 4 °C in the dark for 24 h. Subsequently, the mixture underwent ultrasonic suspension for 1 h. After centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. An aliquot of 2 mL of this supernatant was mixed with 2 mL of 1.5% AlCl3 solution and 3 mL of 1 M sodium acetate (pH 5.0), and the final volume was adjusted to 10 mL. The mixture was then incubated for 10 min, and the absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Three biological replicates were prepared for each experimental condition to ensure accuracy and reproducibility of the results.

RNA extraction, detection and reverse transcription

Total RNA was extracted from the hydrangea stem epidermis using Total RNA Extraction Kit developed by TianGen Biochemical Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd. The extraction method followed the kit's instructions, with the centrifugation time extended from 30 s to 1 min. The purity and concentration of total RNA were detected using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop spectrophotometer 2000. The reverse transcription of total RNA was performed using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) kit provided by Nanjing Novozan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Three samples from each treatment group were subjected to transcriptome sequencing at Beijing Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. using the Illumina HiSeqTM sequencer. Clean reads were assembled via Trinity [15], and comprehensive gene function information was annotated using seven databases: Nr, Nt, Pfam, KOG/COG, Swiss-Prot, KEGG, and GO. Plant transcription factors were predicted with iTAK software. RSEM software [23] employing default bowtie2 parameters was utilized, with the Trinity-assembled transcriptome serving as the reference sequence. Each sample's clean reads were mapped to this reference sequence. Statistical analysis of the bowtie mapping results provided the number of reads mapped to each gene per sample, subsequently converted to Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) for gene expression level analysis. Differential gene expression was identified using DESeq with a threshold of padj < 0.05 [3]. Venn diagrams illustrating differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at various flowering stages of hydrangea were plotted. Further, GO enrichment analysis, KOG analysis, KEGG classification analysis, and KEGG enrichment analysis were conducted on the DEGs to elucidate their functional implications.

WGCNA methodology and clustering analysis

In this research, Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was utilized, employing the R package to construct co-expression networks and identify gene modules. The analysis was executed with specific parameters: a soft threshold power of 10, a maximum block size of 20,000, a minimum module size of 30 genes, and a merge cut height of 0.25. The relationship between the modules and phenotypes was depicted using the “ggplot2” package in R to create a heatmap. Further, to elucidate the function of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in various tissues of H. macrophylla, clustering analysis was conducted. This analysis incorporated a hierarchical approach based on Euclidean distances and Ward’s clustering method.

Flavonoid targeted metabolomics analysis

Hydrangea stem epidermis samples from various treatments were analyzed by Wuhan MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. to determine flavonoid species and their contents. Three biological replicates were prepared for each experimental condition to ensure accuracy and reproducibility of the results. Differential metabolites were identified based on fold change and VIP values derived from the OPLS-DA (Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis) model, using criteria of fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5, and VIP ≥ 1. Statistical analyses of these differential metabolites in infertile flowers at the full-bloom stage of hydrangea were performed using Excel and SPSS software.

Screening of key transcription factors

The transcription factors associated with flavonoid metabolites were screened based on a Pearson correlation coefficient of less than −0.6 or greater than 0.6, and the results were visualized using cytoscape 3.6.1. A phylogenetic tree of transcription factors from different species was constructed using the maximum likelihood method with 1000 bootstrap replicates with MEGA version 6.0. pearson correlation analysis was performed using SPSS 17.0 according to the Fpkm values of DEGs and transcription factors.

Validation of transcriptome data

The accuracy of gene expression levels in the transcriptome data was verified by selecting ten genes for quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis, using Actin as the internal reference. Specific primers were designed utilizing Primer software version 5 (Table S1). The qPCR reaction system was prepared according to Sigma's 2 × SYBR Green Master Mix Enzyme kit protocol. The amplification process included predenaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s, and annealing at 60 °C for 40 s. Dissolution curves were recorded from 60 °C to 95 °C with a 0.5 °C increment every 5 s. Each sample was analyzed in three biological replicates. The relative expression levels of the target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCq method.

Results

Variation in stem color of hydrangeas across developmental stages and shading conditions

To understand the differences in stem color variation of hydrangeas and provide guidance for the cultivation of hydrangea varieties with colored stems, we investigated 103 hydrangea varieties, including oakleaf hydrangea, panicle hydrangea, and bigleaf hydrangea. The findings revealed that over 95% of hydrangea stems were green, with only a few varieties, such as hydrangea “Taube”, exhibiting light red stems. Interestingly, a new hydrangea variety was developed through self-crossing hydrangea “Taube”, where juvenile stems (S1) were green, and mature stems (S2) displayed a dark purplish red color (RHS 59 A). Shading treatments showed that the juvenile green stem (S1) maintained its green color under shading treatment (ST), suggesting that light exposure promotes the formation of deep purplish red stems. To further investigate the stem color differences at the juvenile stage (S1), mature stage (S2), and mature stage under shading treatment (ST), cross-sections of stems were observed, revealing that the epidermal color at the mature stage (S2) was deep purplish red. Using a colorimeter, significant differences were found in the five indicators: a*, b*, L*, c*, and h*, with the a* value indicating the change from green to red, b* from blue to yellow, and L* from dark to light. The trend of stem color change was consistent with the colorimeter analysis data. Light exposure has been found to play a differential role in the regulation of flavonoid and anthocyanin biosynthesis. In the present study, total anthocyanin levels remained relatively stable across different developmental stages and light conditions. In contrast, flavonoid accumulation exhibited significant changes under shading treatments, with flavonoid content being markedly reduced in the ST and S1 stages compared to the fully exposed S2 stage (Fig. 1). This suggests that the biosynthesis and accumulation of flavonoids may be more sensitive to light exposure than anthocyanins.

Differential gene expression associated with stem pigmentation

Shading reduced the accumulation of flavonoids in the stem, leading to less intense coloration. In order to explore the causes of the color formation of ST, S1 and S2 stem epidermis from the transcript level, and elucidated the mechanism of inhibiting the formation of deep purplish red stems by shading treatment, stem epidermis of ST, S1 and S2 samples were conducted with high-throughput sequencing (Table S2). This sequencing yielded a total of 61.3G raw bases. Following the removal of adapter sequences, undetermined sequences, and low-quality sequences, 59.5G clean bases were obtained. The sequencing results were of high quality, as evidenced by error rates below 0.03%, with Q20 exceeding 97.49% and Q30 surpassing 94.23%. Pairwise differential expression analyses were conducted among all three groups (S1 vs. S2, S1 vs. ST, and S2 vs. ST) to provide a comprehensive overview of transcriptomic variation associated with both developmental progression and shading treatment. A total of 6,039, 9,373, and 7,052 DEGs were identified in S1 vs. S2, S1 vs. ST, and S2 vs. ST, respectively (Table S3), indicating substantial transcriptomic reprogramming among the three physiological states. To distinguish genes associated with developmental progression from those related to shading treatment, Venn diagram analysis was performed to identify shared and specific DEGs across the three comparisons (Fig. 2A–D). In particular, the S2 vs. ST comparison primarily reflects shading-responsive transcriptional changes, whereas S1 vs. S2 represents developmental stage–associated differences during stem pigmentation. For downstream functional analysis, we focused on DEGs identified in S1 vs. S2, especially those uniquely upregulated or downregulated in this comparison. A total of 1,755 upregulated and 1,308 downregulated DEGs were selected (Fig. 2C, D). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these DEGs revealed significant enrichment in biological processes including “regulation of DNA-templated transcription”, “protein phosphorylation”, and “signal transduction”. In addition, enrichment was observed in the category “defense response”, which included genes associated with secondary metabolism and stress-related functions (Fig. 2E, F). The expression patterns of DEGs assigned to the “defense response” category were further visualized using heatmap analysis (Figure S1). Given the clear differences in stem coloration among S1 (green), S2 (dark purplish red), and ST (light green), together with the quantitative pigment accumulation trend (ST < S1 < S2; Fig. 1B, F), DEGs that were consistently upregulated in S2 relative to both S1 and ST were prioritized for subsequent co-expression network analysis.

Identification of key genes in pigment synthesis through WGCNA

To further explore the relationships between gene expression patterns and pigmentation-related traits, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using transcriptomic data from ST, S1, and S2. Based on expression similarity, DEGs were clustered into 12 distinct co-expression modules (Fig. 3). Among these modules, the black module exhibited the strongest positive correlations with total anthocyanin and flavonoid contents, with correlation coefficients of 0.95 and 0.90, respectively (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3A, B). The expression profiles of genes within the black module showed an increasing trend from ST to S1 and S2, consistent with the pigment accumulation patterns observed among the three stem types. Within the black module, nine hub genes were identified based on intramodular connectivity (Fig. 3C). These genes were upregulated in deep purplish red stems (S2) and downregulated in green or shaded stems (S1 and ST). Functional annotation indicated that these hub genes encode proteins including short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase, tyrosine phosphatase, and other enzymes potentially associated with secondary metabolism and regulatory processes.

Complex regulation of flavones and flavonols biosynthesis by differentially expressed genes

To elucidate the impact of key genes in stem color formation of ST, S1 and S2, a comprehensive analysis of the DEGs was conducted (Fig. 4). In total, 25 DEGs were identified in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway. Specifically, four DEGs related to anthocyanin synthesis were identified, encoding the enzymes CYP73A, CHS, and DFR. Notably, the DEGs associated with anthocyanin synthesis exhibited high expression in ST, irrespective of whether they were early or late genes, with expression levels significantly exceeding those in S1 and S2. Among these, early genes such as CYP73A (Cluster-26380.21025 and Cluster-26380.38315) and CHS (Cluster-26380.46197) showed a decreasing expression trend from S1 to S2 as stem color formed. In contrast, the late-stage key gene DFR (Cluster-26380.41187) displayed an increasing expression trend as stem color developed. In the biosynthesis pathway of flavones and flavonols, seven DEGs encoding FLS and FG3 were identified. FLS, as the initial enzyme facilitating the entry into the flavone and flavonol biosynthesis pathway from the broader flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, plays a critical role in the synthesis of flavone and flavonol substances. However, in ST, S1, and S2, the DEGs encoding FLS did not exhibit a consistent pattern, potentially due to the complex accumulation of flavonoid substances in different samples. Additionally, in the branch pathway where p-coumaroyl CoA forms caffeoyl CoA via HCT (shikimate O-hydroxycinnamoyltransferase), then feruloyl CoA via E2.1.1.104 (caffeoyl CoA O-methyltransferase), and ultimately homoeriodictyol via CHS, multiple DEGs encoding HTC and E2.2.2.204 were observed to have high expression levels. These findings suggest a complex regulation and interaction among various DEGs within the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, impacting the synthesis of anthocyanins, isoflavones, flavones, and flavonols in a context-dependent manner.To elucidate transcriptional control of stem pigmentation, we examined DEGs mapped to the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway.

Distinct anthocyanin and flavonoid metabolites drive color variation in hydrangea stems across developmental stages

While the total content of anthocyanins remained stable across different stages and treatments, the variation in stem color is attributable to specific anthocyanin metabolites, rather than overall quantity. Petunidin 3-O-glucoside and malvidin 3-galactoside were key contributors to purplish-red coloration. These findings highlight the importance of analyzing specific metabolites, as total anthocyanin content alone does not account for the observed color variations (Figs. 5 and 6). Given the unknown specific flavonoid/anthocyanin components, targeted metabolomic analysis was conducted (Fig. 5). A total of 174 types of anthocyanins and flavonoids, including 16 anthocyanins, were identified, with 70 exhibiting differential accumulation. Statistical analysis revealed the types of differential metabolites to be anthocyanins (6), chalcones and dihydrochalcones (3), flavanones (14), flavones and flavonols (2), flavonoids and flavonols (34), and isoflavonoids (9) (Fig. 5A). Among the 16 anthocyanin metabolites, petunidin 3-O-glucoside, malvidin 3-galactoside chloride, oenin chloride, malvidin 3-O-galactoside, and pelargonin chloride showed significant differences (Fig. 5B). These metabolites, which are methylated derivatives of delphinidin, exhibit a deeper color than delphinidin, resulting in a deep blue color in plants. Other anthocyanins, such as cyanidin-3-O-glucoside and pelargonin chloride, cause orange-red and red coloration in plants. The differential changes in the six anthocyanin glycosides in stages ST, S1, and S2 are likely the primary reason for the variations in stem color. Procyanidin A2, Procyanidin B2, and Procyanidin B3 are proanthocyanidins and direct precursors for the synthesis of anthocyanins, although they do not directly cause color changes, they play a role in the antioxidant and protective mechanisms of plants, contributing to the long-term retention of color. Rhein and Rhein 8-Glucoside, compounds related to yellow pigments, show significant differences in stages ST, S1, and S2.

Flavonoid substances provide yellow, orange, or red influences on plant color. A systematic analysis of the differential flavonoid metabolite contents, excluding anthocyanins, revealed significant differences among stages ST, S1, and S2 (Fig. 6). Many metabolites exhibited higher accumulation levels in S1 or S2, particularly in S2, where the number of highly expressed flavonoid differential metabolites was significantly higher than in S1. In contrast, in ST, almost all differential flavonoid metabolites were between the levels of S1 and S2, maintaining an intermediate level. The differences in flavonoid differential metabolite contents among ST, S1, and S2 play an important regulatory role in the formation of stem color differences in hydrangeas. Flavonoids, proanthocyanidins, and similar compounds to Rhein affect plant color differently from anthocyanins. While anthocyanins are directly responsible for vivid color expression, flavonoids, proanthocyanidins, and similar compounds can regulate the formation of stem color and influence anthocyanin color by affecting the intracellular microenvironment, ultimately impacting color presentation in plants.

Regulatory roles of transcription factors in flavonoid biosynthesis and plant color formation

To identify transcription factors related to the formation of deep purplish red stem color in hydrangeas, a combined transcriptome and metabolome analysis was conducted (Fig. 7A, B). From 542 transcription factors in the bHLH, ERF, AP2, MYB, WRKY, WD40, HY5, and NAC families, 41 transcription factors were identified as associated with 14 flavonoid differential metabolites. Specific focus was given to Petunidin 3-O-glucoside, Malvidin 3-O-galactoside, and Dracorhodin due to their roles in imparting blue and deep purplish red colors in plants. Consequently, 26 transcription factors(including 28 DEGs) associated with these metabolites were identified (Fig. 7B; Table S4), showing significant differences in gene expression levels in the ST, S1, and S2 stages. To integrate transcriptomic and metabolomic evidence, we correlated flavonoid/anthocyanin differential metabolite accumulation with transcription factor expression and prioritized robust associations for network and phylogenetic analyses.

A regulatory model for light-dependent stem pigmentation in H. macrophylla

Based on integrated phenotypic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses, a regulatory model was constructed to elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying light-dependent stem pigmentation in H. macrophylla (Fig. 9). In this model, light acts as a key upstream environmental signal regulating pigment biosynthesis in stem epidermal cells. Under light conditions, multiple transcription factors closely associated with flavonoid and anthocyanin metabolite variation, including MYC1, MYC2-like, MYB12, and MYB1R1, function cooperatively through key structural genes involved in color formation, such as FLS, CYP73A, and DFR, thereby modulating flavonoid metabolic flux. This coordinated transcriptional regulation enhances the expression of flavonoid biosynthetic genes, leading to differential accumulation of specific anthocyanin derivatives (e.g., petunidin- and malvidin-based glycosides) and flavonoid compounds. Compared with changes in total anthocyanin content, alterations in pigment composition play a dominant role in the formation of deep purplish red stem coloration under light conditions. In contrast, shading suppresses light-dependent transcriptional activation, resulting in reduced expression of flavonoid biosynthetic genes, altered pigment composition, and the development of light green or nearly colorless stems. This model provides a framework for understanding how light promotes deep purplish red stem coloration in H. macrophylla through coordinated regulation of flavonoid biosynthesis–related transcription factors and structural genes.

Regulatory model of sunlight influence on deep purplish red formation of Hydrangea marophylla stem

Discussion

Stem coloration is an important ornamental trait in H. macrophylla, contributing not only to visual appeal but also to ecological adaptability and stress resilience. Pigmented plant organs are known to enhance interactions with pollinators and seed dispersers, improve tolerance to environmental stresses, and increase commercial value in horticultural crops [8, 25, 41]. Compared with floral coloration, stem pigmentation has received considerably less attention, despite the crucial structural and physiological roles of stems in plant growth, transport, and stress responses. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying stem color formation therefore has both theoretical and practical significance. In this study, a purple-stemmed H. macrophylla line derived from self-crossing the cultivar ‘Taube’ was used to investigate light-dependent stem coloration through integrated phenotypic, metabolomic, and transcriptomic analyses. The most striking observation was that the formation of deep purplish red stems was strictly light-dependent, as shading treatment effectively suppressed pigmentation. Importantly, total anthocyanin content did not differ significantly among green (S1), dark purplish red (S2), and shaded (ST) stems, whereas flavonoid content showed a clear light-responsive increase. This finding challenges the common assumption that stem color intensity is directly determined by total anthocyanin abundance and instead highlights pigment composition and metabolic balance as key determinants of coloration.

Targeted metabolomic analysis further revealed that specific anthocyanin derivatives, rather than total anthocyanin content, were the key determinants of stem color differences. Anthocyanin derivatives such as delphinidin- and malvidin-based glycosides exhibited significant variation among different treatments and are known to confer bluish to purplish hues in plants [2, 11]. Although their absolute levels decreased under stress or shading conditions, changes in the relative proportions of residual anthocyanins and co-pigments were sufficient to produce a purplish-red phenotype. In addition to anthocyanins, other subclasses of flavonoids also played important roles in stem coloration. Flavonoids can act synergistically with anthocyanins to enhance pigment stability and color expression [14, 35]. Flavonols and related flavonoid compounds displayed stage-specific accumulation patterns, suggesting multifunctional roles in stress adaptation, signal regulation, and copigmentation [12, 28]. Flavonols not only function as UV protectants and antioxidants but also modulate anthocyanin color expression through copigmentation effects [20, 46]. Consequently, the pronounced accumulation of flavonoids in S2 stems likely enhanced color stability and visual intensity despite minimal changes in total anthocyanin content. These results support the conclusion that stem coloration is jointly determined by anthocyanins and other flavonoid compounds.

Transcriptomic analysis further demonstrated that light-induced stem pigmentation is accompanied by extensive transcriptional reprogramming. Genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis and stress-related processes were preferentially upregulated in S2, consistent with pigment accumulation trends. GO enrichment analysis highlighted processes such as regulation of transcription, protein phosphorylation, and signal transduction, indicating that stem coloration is embedded within broader regulatory and stress-response networks. Notably, genes associated with defense responses were significantly enriched, supporting the idea that pigmentation and stress adaptation are tightly linked, particularly under light exposure. Among the differentially expressed genes, several transcription factors emerged as key regulators of stem pigmentation. Integrative correlation analysis between transcriptomic and metabolomic data identified four transcription factors—MYC1, MYC2-like, MYB12, and MYB1R1-like—as being closely associated with differential flavonoid and anthocyanin metabolites. MYC1 and MYC2-like belong to the bHLH family, whereas MYB12 and MYB1R1-like are members of the MYB family. These transcription factors are well known for their roles in regulating flavonoid biosynthesis in diverse plant species [29, 34, 36, 48]. MYB12 has been characterized as a flavonol-specific activator in Arabidopsis thaliana, directly regulating CHS and FLS expression [26]. Studies have shown that MYB12 not only enhances flavonol accumulation in cruciferous plants but also increases anthocyanin accumulation in Asiatic hybrid lilies [38]. The significant correlation between MYB12 and FLS observed in this study suggests a conserved function in hydrangea, promoting flavonol accumulation under light conditions. In contrast, MYC1 and MYC2-like were correlated with key structural genes such as CYP73A and DFR, indicating their involvement in modulating metabolic flux between flavonol and anthocyanin branches [30, 39]. MYC2 is known to integrate jasmonate signaling, stress responses, and secondary metabolism, and can act as either an activator or repressor of anthocyanin biosynthesis depending on its interaction with MYB–bHLH–WD40 (MBW) complexes [21, 31]. The identification of MYC2-like as a hub regulator in stem pigmentation underscores the complexity of transcriptional control in light-responsive flavonoid pathways. In addition to transcription factors, functional genes such as serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT) and major latex protein domain-containing proteins were also highly expressed in pigmented stems. SHMT participates in one-carbon metabolism, which influences nucleotide synthesis, methylation reactions, and cellular differentiation, processes that are indirectly linked to pigment biosynthesis and accumulation [43, 45]. Major latex protein family members are associated with stress and defense responses and may modulate metabolic homeostasis under light-induced stress, thereby indirectly affecting pigment composition.

Based on these results, a regulatory model for light-dependent stem pigmentation in H. macrophylla was proposed. In this model, light functions as an upstream environmental signal that activates MYB- and bHLH-type transcription factors, including MYC1, MYC2-like, MYB12, and MYB1R1-like,which are known regulators of flavonoid biosynthesis [34, 48].. These transcription factors coordinately regulate key structural genes such as FLS, CYP73A, and DFR, thereby reshaping flavonoid metabolic flux [24, 26]. The resulting changes in pigment composition, particularly shifts in the balance between specific anthocyanin derivatives and flavonoid compounds, ultimately lead to the formation of deep purplish red stems. Under shading conditions, this transcriptional activation is suppressed, flavonoid biosynthesis is reduced, pigment composition is altered, and stems remain green or nearly colorless. Stem coloration in H. macrophylla is therefore primarily driven by light-induced changes in flavonoid and anthocyanin composition rather than by a simple increase in total anthocyanin content. MYB and bHLH transcription factors play central roles in this process by integrating environmental signals with metabolic regulation. These findings advance our understanding of plant pigment biology and provide a molecular basis for breeding hydrangea cultivars with enhanced ornamental value and improved stress tolerance.

Conclusion

The results of this study provide novel insights into the regulation of stem pigmentation in H. macrophylla, particularly the roles of flavonoids and anthocyanins in shaping color. Our findings demonstrate that light plays a critical role in regulating flavonoid biosynthesis, significantly altering flavonoid accumulation in response to shading treatments. This light-dependent regulation was not observed for total anthocyanins. The ability of light to modulate flavonoid levels in stems offers breeders new avenues to manipulate pigmentation, enhancing the aesthetic value of hydrangea cultivars. Furthermore, our transcriptomic analysis identified key transcription factors, such as MYC2-like and MYB12, that regulate flavonoid biosynthesis under different light conditions (Fig. 9). These transcription factors provide molecular targets for future breeding programs aimed at optimizing stem pigmentation in ornamental plants. By leveraging these regulatory elements, breeders could develop hydrangea varieties with enhanced color stability and aesthetic appeal, which holds significant commercial potential. Future studies should investigate how these environmental factors interact with identified transcription factors to modulate pigmentation in other ornamental plants, aiming to enhance ornamental plant traits and crop resilience through advanced breeding strategies.
