Efficacy of Step-down Infusion Method of a Novel Barbiturate Therapy for Severe Subarachnoid Hemorrhage with Intracerebral Hematoma
Authors: Jin KIKUCHI, Yu HASEGAWA, Sosho KAJIWARA, Yusuke OTSU, Tetsuya NEGOTO, Kimihiko ORITO, Osamu TAKASU, Masaru HIROHATA, Motohiro MORIOKA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Currently, there is no effective treatment for elevated intracranial pressure in the acute phase of subarachnoid hemorrhage. Recently, we developed “step-down infusion of barbiturate,” a therapeutic strategy for severe traumatic brain injury, which decreased intracranial pressure and significantly reduced mortality without serious side effects. This study aimed to examine the efficacy of step-down infusion of barbiturate in patients with severe subarachnoid hemorrhage. Of the 342 patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage treated surgically at our hospital from January 2010 to May 2022, 17 with ruptured middle cerebral artery aneurysms and intracerebral hematoma graded as World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies Grade IV or V underwent hematoma removal, aneurysmal clipping or coiling, and external decompression. These patients were divided into 2 groups: the step-down infusion of barbiturate group and the control group, which was treated without step-down infusion of barbiturate. Thiamylal sodium was initiated immediately after surgery at 4 mg/kg/h and tapered to 3, 2, 1.5, and 1 mg/kg/h every 24 hrs after the start of normothermia administration. We evaluated the modified Rankin scale and Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale scores 1 year after admission. The step-down infusion of barbiturate group showed significantly better outcomes and well-controlled intracranial pressure (<20 mm Hg); moreover, cerebral perfusion pressure was maintained above 50 mm Hg, and there were no deaths or serious side effects associated with barbiturate use. Our novel method of prophylactic step-down infusion of barbiturate with normothermia for severe subarachnoid hemorrhage can safely lower intracranial pressure while maintaining cerebral perfusion pressure without serious side effects. This method may improve the outcomes in severe subarachnoid hemorrhage with intracerebral hematoma, although further studies are needed to confirm its efficacy and safety.

Introduction

The outcome of severe subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) remains poor, despite recent improvements in treatment techniques.1) Early brain injury and delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) are considered major contributing factors to the outcome of SAH,2) especially in patients with severe SAH, in whom early brain damage is particularly pronounced, followed by secondary brain injury through various mechanisms that exacerbate the outcomes.3) Although 1 of the important pathophysiologies processes originates from elevated intracranial pressure (ICP), effective treatments are limited, and there are no established guidelines for interventions targeting elevated ICP in SAH.4) Elevated ICP leads to decreased cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) and subsequent ischemic changes, resulting in brain injury and poor outcomes.5) In particular, SAH with intracerebral hematoma (ICH) causes intracranial hypertension due to both the hematoma itself and the surrounding edema, even when hematoma removal is performed. This results in additional increase in ICP compared with SAH without ICH.6) Therefore, controlling ICP is important in patients with SAH and ICH.

Barbiturates exert sedative and anesthetic effects by potentiating the action of γ-aminobutyric acid on γ-aminobutyric acid type A receptors.7) They suppress cerebral metabolism and decrease cerebral blood volume, thereby lowering elevated ICP,8) and may be a potential treatment for ICP control in patients with SAH. However, barbiturates have serious side effects, such as cardiac depression, electrolyte imbalance, and increased susceptibility to infection, which can sometimes be fatal.9) Hence, they are considered a last-resort therapeutic intervention for intracranial hypertension, primarily in cases of severe traumatic head injury.10) Recently, we developed a novel safe barbiturate therapy, named “step-down infusion of barbiturate (sdB),” for severe traumatic brain injury.11) This method effectively reduced ICP without serious side effects by using thiamylal, a barbiturate. The sdB technique prevents overshoot of the serum concentration of thiamylal and maintains an adequate serum concentration for several days.11,12)

In this study, we aimed to examine the efficacy of sdB in patients with severe SAH due to ruptured middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysms accompanied by ICH, graded as World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) grade IV or V. The reasons for selecting these patients are as follows: 1) early brain injury is transient in some cases, so the severity of SAH can vary, even among WFNS Grade V patients; and 2) patients with severe SAH and ICH caused by ruptured MCA aneurysms tend to exhibit similar severity profiles, and ICP management is often more critical in these cases than in other types of SAH.

Materials and Methods

Patient selection and characteristics

This study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The current study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Kurume University Hospital (approval numbers 12268, 21056, and 22133). Informed consent for sdB therapy and participation in this prospective study was obtained from the patients or their families. The patients or their families provided written informed consent for the publication of data and photographs.

We retrospectively collected data from all patients at the Kurume University Hospital Advanced Critical Care Center between January 2010 and May 2022. We selected patients with high-grade SAH (WFNS Grade IV or V) and ICH due to a ruptured MCA aneurysm, applying the following inclusion criteria: prophylaxis against rerupture (clipping or coiling), hematoma removal, and decompressive craniectomy. Patients with bilateral mydriasis were excluded. These patients were divided into 2 groups: an sdB group treated with sdB (group A) and a control group treated without sdB (group B). Group A (with sdB) included patients who had high-grade SAH with ICH due to a ruptured MCA aneurysm and who underwent prophylaxis against rerupture (clipping or coiling), hematoma removal, and decompressive craniectomy. The sdB treatment was initiated in August 2014 after approval from our hospital ethics committee. Thereafter, all patients with high-grade SAH who required decompressive craniectomy underwent sdB, except for 2 patients who showed cardiac dysfunction. The decision to perform the sdB method was made in accordance with the study protocol and was finalized by the neurosurgical director after considering cardiac function. Similarly, patients with SAH plus ICH who were admitted to our hospital before August 2014 and underwent hematoma removal with subsequent craniectomy were assigned to group B, along with the 2 patients mentioned above. We assessed their clinical data, including age, sex, past medical history, use of antithrombotic medications, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score on admission, WFNS grade, aneurysm side, ICH volume (mL), number of decompressive craniectomies, type of operative procedure, and postoperative complications, including radiological vasospasm and DCI. Radiological vasospasm was defined as cerebral artery narrowing of ≥30% compared with the original cerebral arterial size.13) DCI was defined as cerebral infarction not attributable to surgery, diagnosed using computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging between days 4 and 28. Outcome measurements were collected from follow-up records. We evaluated favorable outcomes (modified Rankin scale score ≤2, Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOSE14)) score 4-8) 1 year after admission, as well as the mortality rate at the same time points.

Surgical indication

Indications for surgery included the absence of bilateral pupillary dilatation and fixation, preservation of brainstem function, and the family's desire for active treatment. Regarding the surgical indications, clipping was initially considered for the aneurysm, whereas coiling followed by hematoma removal was selected as an alternative if clipping was deemed unfeasible. Decompressive craniectomy was performed for intraoperative brain swelling based on the judgment of the operating surgeon.

Step-down infusion of barbiturate therapy with normothermia (Fig. 1)

This schema presents the protocol of step-down infusion of barbiturate therapy with normothermia.

Prophylactic sdB with thiamylal was initiated immediately after surgical treatment. All patients were intubated and mechanically ventilated with administration of drugs such as fentanyl, rocuronium bromide, and propofol.11) The sdB infusion protocol was as follows: 4.0 mg/kg/h at 0-24 hrs, 3.0 mg/kg/h at 24-48 hrs, 2.0 mg/kg/h at 48-72 hrs, 1.5 mg/kg/h at 72-96 hrs, and 1.0 mg/kg/h at 96-120 hrs.11) Sedation was administered with propofol (5 mg/kg/h).11) If blood pressure decreased, propofol was reduced or a vasopressor agent was administered. The sdB was performed under normothermia (the patient's body temperature was maintained at 36-37℃ for 120 hrs) using a cool line (Asahi Kasei ZOLL Medical Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) with an intravascular cooling device.11) This method is not used in patients with impaired cardiac function due to circulatory dynamics. The protocol was completed 120 hrs after initiation; however, a limited number of patients underwent repeated sdB if ICP values remained elevated or significant cerebral edema was observed on imaging.

ICP and CPP monitoring

Continuous ICP monitoring was performed using an ICP sensor (Johnson and Johnson, Raynham, MA, USA) in the sdB group. Refractory increased ICP was defined as ICP > 20 mm Hg lasting for more than 1 hr.11) When refractory increased ICP was observed, osmotic diuretics were administered to maintain the value within 20 mm Hg.11) In the sdB group, changes in the maximum ICP values were recorded every 6 hrs for the first 24 hrs and then every 24 hrs for 120 hrs during treatment.11) ICP values continued to be measured until the ICP sensor was removed. CPP was calculated by subtracting ICP from mean blood pressure in the same manner.11)

Postoperative management

To prevent DCI, patients in both groups were treated with fasudil hydrochloride, ozagrel sodium, and cilostazol. Cranioplasty after decompressive craniectomy was performed at an appropriate time when brain swelling had reduced and there were no signs of infection. Ventriculoperitoneal shunting was performed if secondary hydrocephalus was observed.

Complications associated with barbiturate therapy

This study was conducted with regard to complications reported to be common with barbiturate therapy.9) The definition of each side effect was as follows: Hypotension was defined as the need for administration of vasopressor.11) Hypokalemia was defined as a serum concentration <3.5 mEq/L.11) Pneumonia was diagnosed based on the presence of an infiltration shadow on chest X-ray.11) Arrhythmia was defined as treatment with antiarrhythmic drugs.11) These are the same definitional methods used in our previous study that investigated barbiturate-related complications in severe traumatic brain injury.11)

Outcomes

Patients' outcomes were assessed using the mRS and GOSE14) 1 year after admission. The outcome was classified as favorable (mRS score ≤ 2, GOSE14) score 4-8) or unfavorable (mRS ≥ 3, GOSE14) score 1-3).

Statistical analyses

We evaluated the differences between patients in the sdB and control groups. Age is expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The GCS score on admission is expressed as the median value. Age, GCS score on admission, and ICH volume were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test between the 2 groups, and other evaluations were performed using Fisher's exact test with the JMP version 17 software package (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Of the 342 patients with SAH treated surgically at our hospital during this period, 17 with ruptured MCA aneurysms and ICH graded as WFNS Grade IV or V who underwent hematoma removal, aneurysmal clipping or coiling, and external decompression met the inclusion criteria. There were 7 patients in the sdB group (group A) and 10 patients in the control group (group B). Table 1 shows the characteristics of the patients eligible for this study. No significant differences were observed in age, sex, history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, use of antithrombotic medications before onset, GCS score on admission, side of the MCA aneurysm, ICH volume (mL), or surgical procedure. Decompressive craniectomy was performed only once in each case. There was no statistically significant difference in the frequency of radiological cerebral vasospasm or DCI between the 2 groups. When patients were evaluated using the mRS and GOSE14) at 1 year, group A (with sdB) had a statistically better outcome (Fig. 2). Barbiturate-related complications, such as hypokalemia, pneumonia, and central nervous system effects, were observed but were managed with standard medical therapy (Table 2). There were no deaths in the sdB group (group A) and no serious side effects associated with barbiturates. There were 4 deaths in the control group (without sdB), all occurring during the acute phase. The causes of death were uncontrolled cerebral swelling, brain herniation, and subsequent global cerebral ischemia, as confirmed by imaging. Postoperatively, imaging findings were consistent with high ICP. In the sdB group (group A), ICP was well-controlled; most of the measured ICP values were <20 mm Hg (Fig. 3A). The CPP in the sdB group (group A) was maintained above 50 mm Hg (Fig. 3B). There were no cases of re-elevation of ICP values after termination of sdB therapy.

Discussion

In SAH with ICH, even after hematoma removal or decompressive craniectomy, ICP increases postoperatively owing to brain damage caused by the hematoma and perihematomal edema, and higher ICP has been reported in more severe SAH.15) SAH with ICH presents with vasogenic edema due to hematoma or surgical operation and induces significant brain swelling after 1-7 days (average, 4 days).16) High ICP values are associated with poor outcomes.6) Therefore, it is necessary to address early brain injury originating from elevated ICP.17) Although candidate treatments, including anti-edema medications, ventricular drainage, hematoma removal, and decompressive craniectomy, are commonly considered for managing elevated ICP, the effectiveness of other methods is limited.4,6) Barbiturate therapy is a potent medical treatment for lowering ICP. Barbiturates inhibit cerebral metabolism, reduce oxygen consumption, decrease cerebral blood volume, and lower ICP in refractory ICP hyperactivity.17) In this study, sdB improved the prognosis of severe SAH with ICH due to rupture of an MCA aneurysm. Outcomes in the sdB group were superior compared to those in a similar patient population, i.e., patients with high-grade SAH (WFNS Grade IV or V) and ICH due to ruptured MCA aneurysm, including prevention of rerupture, hematoma removal, and decompressive craniectomy.18)

In the sdB group, ICP was controlled at <20 mm Hg, and CPP was not reduced, suggesting that good ICP control and inhibition of CPP reduction led to improved outcomes. In the present study, there was no difference in the incidence of DCI or radiological vasospasm between the 2 groups, suggesting that the suppression of early brain injury associated with elevated ICP in the sdB group contributed to an increased proportion of patients with good outcomes. We previously reported that barbiturate therapy suppressed cerebral edema in experimental re-bleeding SAH rats, which was associated with a reduction of microglial activation.19) We speculate that the reduction in edema may be one of the reasons for the improved outcomes.

Our novel sdB, which defines the clear use of barbiturates in addition to lowering ICP, can maintain CPP and reduce the risk of metabolic disturbances and inflammatory reactions. According to our preliminary analysis, when ICP became uncontrollable, our novel sdB was unable to manage it effectively. This highlights the importance of managing ICP before it elevates. Hence, starting sdB immediately after surgery can minimize early brain injury by decreasing ICP before it elevates; therefore, prophylactic administration may be recommended in such cases.

Previous studies have reported a high frequency of side effects of classical barbiturate therapy; therefore, it has not been widely recommended.20) The primary cause of these side effects is elevated blood levels of barbiturates resulting from continuous administration of a fixed dose over several days or in combination with hypothermia.12,21,22) Hence, we developed a novel “step-down” infusion method to maintain serum barbiturates concentrations within the safety margin, and we previously reported its safety and efficacy in severe head trauma.11) This study showed that the actual incidence of barbiturate therapy-related side effects was not significantly different from that in the control group. Though some side effects were observed, they could be managed with standard medical interventions.

A unique feature of the proposed sdB method is the concomitant use of normothermia. Hyperthermia is a frequent complication of SAH, with or without infection, and is associated with poor outcomes and increased mortality.23) Hyperthermia contributes to secondary brain injury through various mechanisms, including increased cerebral metabolism.24) Hypothermia is used to suppress brain metabolism; however, animal studies have shown that barbiturates blood levels increase under hypothermia, resulting in a higher frequency of adverse effects.12) Hyperthermia is also associated with increased frequency of symptomatic cerebral vasospasm and poor outcomes in SAH.25) Therefore, maintaining normothermia during barbiturate therapy is an important factor. Finally, prophylactic sdB with normothermia may improve outcomes in patients with severe SAH.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective, single-center study with a small number of patients. Therefore, the possibility of confounding factors cannot be excluded. Second, the current study evaluated the outcomes using a limited number of patients in the decompressive craniectomy group for severe SAH caused by a ruptured MCA aneurysm with hematoma. Further studies are required to determine the effectiveness of this approach in patients with other types of SAH. Additionally, randomized controlled trials are necessary to evaluate its efficacy. Third, thiamylal was used among the barbiturates, and other barbiturates were not studied. Fourth, in group A (with sdB), the sdB protocol required implantation of an ICP sensor and recording of ICP values. However, in group B (without sdB), ICP and CPP values could not be compared with those of group A because implantation of an ICP sensor was not mandatory. As a result, only a few patients had their ICP and CPP values measured, and the available records were not detailed.

Conclusions

In severe SAH with ICH due to a ruptured MCA aneurysm, the sdB method can safely lower ICP while maintaining CPP. This method may improve outcomes in severe SAH with ICH; however, further studies are needed to clarify its effectiveness and underlying mechanisms.
