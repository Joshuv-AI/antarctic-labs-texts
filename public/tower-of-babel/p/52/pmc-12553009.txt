Multimodal mechanisms of platelet-rich plasma in bone defect repair: Angiogenesis, inflammation modulation, and metabolic regulation
Authors: Junjie Chen, Bo Liu, Jiayang He, Yuhao Wei, Yuhan He, Hang Zhou, Zhiqiang Zhang, Yiwen Weng, Ming Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone defect is the loss of bone tissue due to trauma, fracture, infection, etc. This loss may lead to non-union of fracture, bone defect or bone deficiency, which seriously affects the patient's quality of life and even leads to dysfunction and disability. Although surgical repair is a common treatment modality, the surgical procedure is complicated, with a long postoperative recovery period and certain surgical risks, such as infection and bleeding. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP), as a novel therapeutic approach, has demonstrated remarkable potential in bone defect repair. PRP is rich in growth factors, such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), etc., and can have a beneficial effect on bone defect repair through various mechanisms, such as the release of growth factors, the promotion of angiogenesis, the inhibition of inflammatory response, and the recruitment of activated cells and other mechanisms, which play a positive role in the repair of bone defects and provide strong support for the regeneration and repair of bone tissue.Keywords: Bone defect repair, Platelet-rich plasma (PRP), Growth factors, Angiogenesis, Bone tissue regeneration, Fracture non-union, Transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), Surgical complications in bone repair

Introduction

The formation of bone defects can be attributed to a variety of causes, including trauma, severe fractures, infections, tumor removal, or bone disease. These factors lead to damage or loss of bone tissue, which in turn triggers the formation of a bone defect [1,2]. Bone defects cause serious physical and life-related losses to patients, including functional limitations, pain, impaired mobility, and an unattractive appearance, which severely affects the quality of life and may lead to disability and a long-term rehabilitation process [3,4]. Traditional treatment methods for bone defects have certain limitations. Although surgical repair is a common treatment modality, the surgical procedure is complicated, with a long postoperative recovery period and certain surgical risks, such as infection and bleeding. Implant placement may trigger rejection or infection, increasing the uncertainty of treatment and the risk of complications. Conservative treatment with medication often has limited effect and may cause side effects or require long-term medication, affecting patients' quality of life. PRP, as a novel therapeutic approach, has demonstrated remarkable potential in bone defect repair [5,6]. PRP is rich in growth factors, such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), epidermal growth factor (EGF), insulin-like growth factor IGF and fibronectin etc [7,8]., which can promote osteoblasts' proliferation, differentiation, and the formation of new bone tissues [9]. Jiang et al. [10]showed that 3D-printed PRP-GelMA composites could promote bone/chondrocyte repair through immunomodulation of M2 polarization, which promoted migration, osteogenesis and chondrogenic differentiation of BMSCs.At the same time, PRP can also promote angiogenesis, provide sufficient supply of nutrients and oxygen to the damaged bone tissue and accelerate the repair process. Similarly, An et al. [11]polyether ether ketone (PEEK) material with slow degradation of surface-loaded HA hydrogel with RPP and NGF could continuously release multiple growth factors, which effectively promoted osteogenesis and angiogenesis. In addition, PRP has an anti-inflammatory effect, which can reduce the inflammatory reaction in the area of bone defects and provide a favorable environment for the normal repair of bone tissue. It was shown that BMSCs cultured in the presence of PRP released therapeutic molecules with anti-inflammatory and protective effects that far outweighed the inflammation and destruction of cartilage, macrophages and T cells [12]. In addition, PRP can recruit and activate stem cells and other repair-related cells in the surrounding tissues, promoting their migration to the damaged area and participation in the repair process. Zhang et al. [13]found that PLT HMGB1 in PRP plays an important role in tendon wound healing by decreasing inflammation, increasing local HMGB1 levels, and recruiting stem cells to the wound area of the tendon. The findings also suggest that the efficacy of PRP in treating clinical tendon injuries may depend on the PLT HMGB1 in the PRP formulation. It is worth mentioning that PRP is derived from the patient's own blood, which is not easy to trigger immune rejection, has good biocompatibility and reduces the risk of treatment, and its treatment process is simple and safe, which reduces the risk of surgery and complications, and is applicable to a wide range of patient groups. With the in-depth research on the therapeutic mechanism of PRP and the continuous advancement of technology, the application of PRP in the treatment of bone defects has a bright future. In the future, PRP may become a more common and effective treatment method, providing patients with a faster and safer solution for bone tissue repair [[14], [15], [16]]. With the continuous improvement of treatment technology, the efficacy and safety of PRP in the treatment of bone defects will be further enhanced, bringing better treatment experience and treatment results to patients, thus improving their quality of life and reducing the incidence of complications [17,18] (see Table 1, Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5, Table 6, Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

PRP repairs bone defects by modulating the inflammatory response

The immune response process in bone defects begins with the initial injury and inflammatory response phase, when bone tissue is damaged, the surrounding tissues and blood vessels are damaged, causing a localized inflammatory response, while immune cells and platelets in the bloodstream are attracted to the damaged area, where the platelets aggregate and contribute to the coagulation of the blood, resulting in the formation of a thrombus that prevents further bleeding [19,20]. Subsequently, inflammatory cells such as neutrophils and monocytes are attracted to the damaged area, releasing lysosomal enzymes and free radicals in an attempt to remove the damaged tissue and foreign material [21,22]. Although the inflammatory response plays a necessary role in the repair process by removing damaged tissue and promoting cell migration, an excessive inflammatory response may lead to increased tissue destruction, preventing osteoblast activation and new bone tissue formation, as well as causing pain and discomfort for the patient [23]. Therefore, maintaining a moderate inflammatory response is now a clinical goal to be achieved, and PRP has great advantages in anti-inflammation [24,25]. Cole et al. [26] showed that PRP treatment for osteoarthritis (OA) showed different clinical outcomes in a randomized controlled trial, suggesting a trend toward a reduction in 2 pro-inflammatory cytokines, suggesting that the anti-inflammatory properties of PRP play a positive role in osteoarthritis. In addition to this, leukocyte-poor PRP was associated with significantly higher subjective IKDC scores compared to leukocyte-rich PRP, so leukocyte-poor PRP may be an excellent treatment for knee OA compared to leukocyte-rich PRP [27]. According to the above, it is not clear which cytokine mediators of pain and inflammation are superior in leukocyte-poor PRP (LP-PRP) than in leukocyte-rich PRP (LR-PRP), Prathap et al. [28]showed that LR-PRP expresses a significant increase in IL-1Ra, IL-4, and IL-8 suggesting that LR-PRP may be more anti-inflammatory than LP-PRP, and that MMP-9 is expressed in higher concentrations in LR-PRP suggesting that LR-PRP may be more cartilage toxic than LP-PRP. The clinical efficacy of PRP is significantly regulated by white blood cell concentration: in degenerative diseases, LP-PRP (leukocyte-poor PRP) demonstrates systemic advantages. A meta-analysis of osteoarthritis (OA) showed that LP-PRP significantly outperformed LR-PRP (leukocyte-rich PRP) in functional recovery (IKDC score) and reduced the incidence of complications by 38 % [29,30]; Studies on intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) further elucidate the mechanism—LP-PRP promotes the synthesis of type II collagen and aggregated proteoglycans in nucleus pulposus cells (P < 0.05) and inhibits apoptosis (caspase-3 ↓), while LR-PRP activates catabolic metabolism [31]. This difference stems from the opposite regulation of inflammatory pathways: LR-PRP significantly upregulates pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-8, and FGF-2 (compared to LP-PRP↑) while downregulating cartilage protective factors HGF and TIMP-4 [32], creating a pro-degradative microenvironment. However, in terms of pain relief, the subtype differences disappear—in the treatment of OA and tennis elbow, there is no statistically significant difference in VAS score improvement between LP-PRP and LR-PRP [29,30]. It is worth noting that clinical operational variables must be precisely controlled: single and triple PRP injections yield equivalent efficacy; the incidence of postoperative pain is significantly lower with LP-PRP than with LR-PRP (3.9 % vs. 6.4 %, ∗P = 0.029∗) [30]. In summary, LP-PRP offers both functional recovery advantages and safety in degenerative bone/cartilage repair, while the pro-inflammatory properties of LR-PRP may offset its repair potential. Future clinical practice should strictly select subtypes based on lesion nature (degenerative vs. traumatic) and standardize injection protocols.

In addition to this, the anti-inflammatory effects of PRP have also been shown to be superior in basic studies. In addition, in basic research, the anti-inflammatory effect of PRP also has an excellent performance, Li et al. [33] found that PRP treatment of infected DFU in the process, can increase intracellular miRNA-21, while reducing the expression of PDCD4, inhibit the activity of NF-κB, and inhibit the inflammation of HaCaT cells. It is worth mentioning that it has been noted that hydrogels prepared with slow-release material piggybacking PRP can significantly improve wound healing in diabetic mice infected by modulating inflammation, accelerating collagen deposition and angiogenesis. The prepared hydrogels exhibited significant antimicrobial activity, inhibited the production of pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), enhanced anti-inflammatory factor (TGF-β1), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [34]. However, the explosive release of growth factors has been a challenge in the current PRP application. In order to achieve the controlled release of growth factors, a multifunctional reactive oxygen species (ROS)/pH-responsive hydrogel was developed to load PRP derived from human umbilical cord blood, and the in vivo healing of skin wounds further demonstrated that the prepared hydrogel modulated the inflammation and promoted epithelialization, collagen deposition, and angiogenesis [35]. ZHANG et al. [36] treated OA with peripheral blood mesenchymal stem cells (PBMSCs) combined with donor PRP showed that PBMSCs + PRP inhibited the phosphorylation of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) downstream in the NF-κB signaling pathway. In vivo, intra-articular (IA) injection of PBMSCs + PRP enhanced OA-induced cartilage regeneration and attenuated synovial inflammation in rabbits compared with PBMSCs or PRP alone. Moderate inflammation can promote bone defect repair, and different types of PRP regulate inflammation to different degrees. High levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in leukocyte- and platelet-rich plasma (L-PRP) may activate the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway to counteract the beneficial effects of growth factors on bone regeneration. Regarding the possible suitability of P-PRP for bone regeneration compared to L-PRP, it has been shown that Yin et al. [38] used leukocyte-enriched PRP (L-PRP) and pure PRP (P-PRP) to repair cranial defects, both of which achieved good osteogenic results, and that the results of P-PRP were better than those of L-PRP, possibly because L-PRP produced more inflammatory factors that This may be due to the fact that L-PRP produced more inflammatory factors, activated the NF-κB pathway to induce NF-κB heterodimer translocation, upregulated the expression of downstream inflammation-related genes (COX-2 and iNOS), and synthesised PGE2 and NO, which inhibited bone and neoangiogenesis. The authors speculate that excessive inflammatory factors may lose part of the osteogenic effect, so inflammatory factors should be maintained at an appropriate concentration when using PRP to treat bone defects, to be proved by further studies.

PRP repairs bone defects by promoting neoangiogenesis

In bone, blood vessels are a source not only of oxygen and nutrients but also of calcium and phosphate, which are the basis of mineralization. The need to develop a vascular system arises when the growth of bone tissue exceeds the size at which passive diffusion of oxygen, nutrients, and metabolic waste exchanges becomes inadequate. Angiogenesis is the process by which an organism builds new blood vessels from existing ones. Many studies have shown that bone formation is associated with angiogenic processes [39]. For example, osteoblasts secrete pro-angiogenic factors, most importantly vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which triggers signalling responses in different cell populations expressing VEGF receptors, including endothelial cells that make up blood vessels, as well as chondrocytes, osteoblasts and osteoclasts [[40], [41], [42]]. Conversely, bone endothelial cells release factors that can act on chondrocytes and osteoblast lineage cells [43,44]. Vascular cells have also been shown to be part of an ecological niche that controls the maintenance and differentiation of haematopoietic stem cells (HSC) in the bone marrow [45]. Notably, angiogenesis also plays an important role in fracture healing and repair [46], and changes in the local vascular system have been linked to the progression of many diseases affecting the skeleton, such as osteoporosis, osteonecrosis, rheumatoid arthritis, bone cancer and metastases [47]. This shows the importance of neovascularisation in the repair of bone defects. In recent years, several studies have confirmed that early vascularization of bone defects can effectively promote final bone regeneration and repair [48,49], and neovascularisation secretes a variety of cytokines, such as PDGF-α, PDGF-β, Noggin, etc., to promote the recruitment of bone histiocytes and macrophages at the bone defect site and the osteogenic differentiation of bone histiocytes [50]. Deferoxamine mesylate (DFO) is a commonly used drug for vascular tissue engineering, and its mechanism of action is to activate the HIF-1a pathway by chelating Fe3+ and promote the expression of downstream signalling molecule VEGF, which in turn promotes vascular regeneration. However, the short plasma half-life of DFO (t1/2 = 5min in mice) and its susceptibility to degradation limit its biological application. Meanwhile, autologous bone grafting, distraction osteogenesis and membrane-induced regeneration techniques are the most commonly used methods to treat bone defects. However, these methods may fail to achieve satisfactory healing, partly due to insufficient vascular regeneration. Decreased osteogenic and angiogenic capacity is an important feature of senescent cells and an important cause of bone repair failure or poor prognosis. Therefore, finding a suitable pro-neoangiogenic modality has become a challenge for repairing bone defects.

It has been shown that PRP promotes the formation of new blood vessels and PRP is defined as serum containing more than 1,000,000 platelets per cubic microlitre. A single injection of PRP has been reported to accelerate surgical angiogenesis in vascularly implanted necrotic bone. α-granules of PRP release a variety of growth factors, and VEGF is a key signalling molecule in the angiogenic pathway. vEGF stimulates angiogenesis by regulating endothelial cell proliferation and migration [51]. Other growth factors, PDGF, TGF-β, and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) can also stimulate neoangiogenesis [51]. Angiogenesis is a complex process regulated by the coordinated action of various growth factors. The combined effects of fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF-2) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) have been shown to play a more pivotal role in promoting angiogenesis compared to the individual actions of FGF-2 or VEGF [52]. Furthermore, networks formed by different combinations of platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), epidermal growth factor (EGF), and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) exert synergistic effects, significantly enhancing neovascularisation [53]. Additionally, treatment with PRP has been found to markedly increase serum osteocalcin and VEG levels, as well as significantly upregulate β-catenin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and VEG, indicating improved angiogenic and osteogenic activity [54]. PRP significantly upregulated the mRNA expression levels of VEGF, PDGF, IGF-1 and TGF-β in the ONFH mouse model. This suggests that PRP can induce the production of angiogenic factors and participate in the angiogenesis of ONFH [55] PRP can also promote other angiogenesis-related factors, such as hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), osteoblasts, and osteoclasts. (ICAM-1), osteopontin (OPN) and platelet-derived endothelial cell growth factor (PD-ECGF) [56]. Some studies have even shown that PRP contains a variety of angiogenesis-related cytokines, such as VEGF, PDGF, TGF-β1 and EGF, which participate in the process of neovascularisation by inducing the migration, proliferation and differentiation of vascular endothelial cells, and thus promote the haemotransfusion reconstruction of the injury site. In vitro experiments have shown that PRP not only promotes fibroblast proliferation and migration, but also induces neovascularisation for wound healing [57]. Liu et al. [56] found that PRPhad beneficial effects on mice with osteonecrosis of the femoral head by promoting angiogenesis. In a mouse model of osteonecrosis of the femoral head, tail vein injection of PRP significantly upregulated the expression levels of angiogenesis-related factors, such as hepatocyte growth factor, cell adhesion factor-1, and osteoblasts, and synovial angiogenesis was increased, suggesting that PRP can promote the repair of joint tissues through the stimulation of neovascularisation, suggesting that PRP can promote the repair of joint tissues by stimulating neovascularisation. At the same time, PRP is a natural growth factor slow-release system, in which the growth factors stored in it are slowly released to stimulate the proliferation and secretion of growth factors by stem cells, endothelial cells, osteoblasts and fibroblasts at the site of trauma; at the same time, it can promote joint tissue repair by activating platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR)/phospholipids, and by activating platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR)/phosphatidylinositol. PDGFR/phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt)/nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signalling pathway to increase the level of paracrine secretion [58]. Hersant et al. [59] showed that PRP stimulated transplanted BMSCs through paracrine secretion, resulting in an increase in angiogenic factor transcript expression, which further promoted the pro-angiogenic and endothelial cell migration ability of BMSCs.

In summary, due to the great potential of platelet plasma (PRP) in repairing bone defects by promoting neoangiogenesis with low operating difficulty, low cost, and good effect, it has been widely used for PRP to repair bone defects by promoting neocapillarogenesis in clinical bone defect repair. However, there are still some problems that need to be solved. Firstly, due to the complex composition of PRP, the exact mechanism by which the cytokines released by PRP promote tissue regeneration and repair still needs to be further elucidated; secondly, due to the lack of a unified consensus on the preparation protocols and effective therapeutic concentration of PRP, the concentrations of platelets and cytokines in the PRP produced by different methods of preparation are also different, which leads to different clinical efficacies in different studies and the existing clinical studies are mostly small samples and lack of information about the clinical efficacies of PRP. Most of the existing clinical studies are small samples and lack long-term follow-up data. As the research on PRP progresses, it is necessary to establish standard PRP preparation protocols, explore the optimal therapeutic concentration and dosage, and conduct large-scale clinical randomised controlled trials to further observe the immediate and long-term efficacy of PRP.

PRP enhances bone repair by recruiting activated cells

Bone cells—including osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells—collaborate to maintain the bone's inherent capacity for self-repair. However, when damage exceeds this reparative ability, it can lead to bone defects and impaired healing. Many studies have shown that the repair capacity of bone is closely related to the activity of the cells within the bone. For example, activated osteoblasts can produce type I interferons, cytokines, chemokines, and interferon-stimulated proteins through autocrine and paracrine mechanisms to control the extent of damage and improve repair capacity. It is worth noting that bone injury will greatly reduce cellular activity, and in the evidence provided by the study, significant reductions in cell viability and CFU-M formation were found in non-united osteoblasts by analyzing osteoblasts from patients with non-union fractures [60,61]. This was accompanied by significant differences in osteoblast gene expression, as revealed by Affymetrix microarray analysis and RT-PCR. A set of significantly down-regulated factors were identified in non-united osteoblasts, which are involved in the regulation of the proliferation and differentiation process of osteoblasts (typical Wnt-, IGF-, TGF-β and FGF signaling pathways) Cellular activation is the process of activating or stimulating cells to make them more active or functional [[62], [63], [64]]. Currently, autologous bone grafting, distraction osteogenesis and osteochondral induction are the main treatment options for bone defects. These methods may fail to achieve satisfactory therapeutic results, partly as a result of insufficient recruitment of cellular activity within the skeleton. In recent years, PRP has been used in a variety of applications due to its ability to recruit cellular activity and promote cell reproduction. It is therefore the most likely solution to the problem of reduced cellular activity in bone [65].

Growth factors are important mediators of cell activation accomplished by PRP [66]. α-granules of platelet-rich plasma in PRR contain and release concentrated levels of platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and insulin-like growth factor (IGF) [67], which are all known to promote the recruitment of activated cells. According to studies [68], it has been demonstrated that not only PRP induces the proliferation of activated osteoblasts through platelet-derived growth factor receptor-mediated signaling [69]. but also that platelet-derived growth factors act by binding to PDGF-specific receptors on the surface of osteoblasts to activate their intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity. From mouse bioassays, it was concluded that PRP inhibits RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation and accelerates osteoblast activity, thereby inducing a positive effect [70]. In addition, the slow release of PRP-enriched growth factors stimulates the β-catenin signaling pathway and the MAPK signaling pathway, which are involved in the activation, proliferation, and differentiation of osteoblasts. These signaling pathways play an architectural role in the recruitment of activated osteoblasts [71].Notably, β-catenin (a component of the classical Wnt pathway) is known to play a key role in bone formation and remodeling [72],PRP significantly reduced RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation by modulating early signaling pathways and increasing levels of β-catenin and cyclin D1 [73],which effectively avoids the degradation of healthy osteoblasts by osteoclasts. Increased hair growth-promoting ability of DPC through activation of Wnt signaling pathway by activated PRP supernatant in biocytoelectrical signaling assay, It is suggested that the growth factors in PRP are essential for cell activation to promote cell proliferation and repair, including PDGF (platelet-derived growth factor) and VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor), which can activate the MAPK signaling pathway to promote cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation. The study by Hersant et al. [59]PRP stimulates transplanted BMSCs by paracrine stimulation, resulting in an increase in the transcriptional expression of angiogenic factors, which further promotes the pro-angiogenic and bone endothelial cell migration ability of BMSCs, and of course IGF is no ordinary, and according to the changes of fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF-2) and insulin GF-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) in diabetic rats after fracture, the decrease of serum and FGF-2 and IGF-1 in the fracture site is one of the causes of poor or delayed bone healing, and there may be some synergistic effect between FGF-2 and IGF-1ere have been a large number of studies on the effects of IGF on osteoblasts and chondrocytes [74].

PRP (platelet-rich plasma) accelerates the process of bone tissue regeneration and fracture healing by releasing high concentrations of growth factors that recruit and activate MSCs, osteoblasts, and endothelial cells at the fracture site, which promotes cell proliferation, differentiation, and neovascularisation [75]. Advantages include the fact that this mechanism directly enhances the biological process of bone healing, and that the use of autologous blood in the preparation of PRP is biocompatible, reducing the immune rejection and risk of infection [76]. However, disadvantages include the lack of standardization of PRP preparation methods, resulting in inconsistent growth factor concentrations and efficacy [77]. Excessive growth factor concentrations may cause excessive cell proliferation, leading to fibrosis or abnormal tissue formation [78]. There is a lack of high-quality randomized controlled trials, insufficient evidence of clinical efficacy, and a uniform specification of clinical application has not yet been established [79]. Therefore, although PRP has been shown to be effective in promoting bone regeneration and fracture healing via the recruiting and activating cellular mechanisms has potential advantages in promoting fracture healing, its application still requires further research and standardization.

PRP regulates the balance of bone metabolism to repair bone defects

Bone is a highly active tissue composed mainly of inorganic (e.g. calcium, phosphorus, magnesium) and organic (e.g. collagen and non-collagen) substances. Its renewal and remodeling is accomplished through a combination of osteoblasts, osteoclasts and osteocytes. At the same time, the integrity of the skeleton is maintained by continuously repeating, spatio-temporally coupled processes of bone resorption and bone formation. Among other things, the integrity of the skeleton is mainly dependent on the homeostasis of bone metabolism. Dynamic metabolic processes in bone tissue are coordinated by both osteoblasts and osteoclasts [81]. In the process of bone metabolism, osteoclasts secrete some enzymes that cause the surrounding bone to be dissolved, producing bone resorption traps. In the process of dissolution, osteoblasts migrate to the trap of bone defects to differentiate and proliferate, secrete some collagen fibres around the osteoblasts, and there is a deposition of collagen fibre calcium, and osteoblasts are transformed into osteocytes to fill the bone traps, which completes the process of bone resorption and formation of bone tissues. It is worth noting that bone metabolism plays an important role in the repair of bone defects, firstly, the activity of osteoblasts accelerates the production of new bone and the healing of the defective area; osteoclasts are responsible for the resorption of bone and help to remove the damaged bone tissues, and the balance of these two processes is very important in bone metabolism. A good balance of bone metabolism maintains the structure and function of bone through the resorption of old bone tissue and the formation of new bone, which helps to remodel the defective area and promote bone healing. Secondly, disorders of bone metabolism such as osteoporosis can lead to a decrease in bone density, affecting the strength and healing ability of the bone, thus slowing down the repair of bone defects. Clinically, bone defects are mainly treated with autologous bone graft, allogeneic bone graft and artificial bone graft, but all of these methods have defects. For the treatment of large bone defects, the source of autogenous bone is limited, and bone extraction will increase the pain for patients. Allogeneic bone supply is limited and there is a risk of disease transmission and allogeneic rejection. Biomaterial bone only plays the role of osteoconduction and has no osteoinductive effect, which often leads to a long treatment period and a high failure rate in the clinic. In recent years, the study of applying autologous platelet rich plasma (PRP) to promote bone defect repair has become a hot spot, and many in vivo and in vitro experiments have shown that PRP can significantly promote the repair of bone tissue [[82], [83], [84], [85]] (see Fig. 3).

It secretes growth factors that can be active under the action of histones and carbohydrate side chains, bind to osteoblasts, mesenchymal stem cells, etc., causing a series of intracellular signal transduction, accelerating cell proliferation, matrix formation, and cellular differentiation [84], regulating bone metabolism, and promoting the repair of bone defects. Among them, PDGF plays an important role in vascular-pericyte-MSC-osteoblast dynamics, and PDG can be a linker between cellular components and osteoblast differentiation programs; it can mobilise pericytes from proximal luminal to traumatic locations, stimulate their mitosis and organise them them up. Through both of these aspects, PDGF acts on vasculogenesis and directly on osteoblast activity, thus participating in multiple aspects of osteogenesis. There have been a number of experimental studies on the role of PDGF in MSC, among which Kreja et al. [86] found that osteoclasts of non-resorbed bone have a role in recruiting MSC and promoting their osteogenic differentiation, which may be mainly through PDGF-β, as inhibition of the PDGF-β receptor significantly reduces this effect. Ng et al. [87] found that PDGF signalling plays an important role in MSC growth, and PDGF pathway genes can be strongly expressed in undifferentiated MSC. Whereas inhibitors of PDGF signalling: tyrosine phosphorylation inhibitors are toxic to MSC, Maijenburg et al. [88] found high expression of the nuclear receptors Nur77 and Nurr1 in metastatic foetal bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (FBMSC), whereas migratory stimulatory stromal derived factor-1α (SDF -1α) and PDGF-β rapidly increased the expression of Nur77 and Nurr1.IGFs were derived from bone matrix, endothelial cells, osteoblasts and chondrocytes. IGFs have the functions of stimulating osteoblast proliferation, bone matrix synthesis, and promoting bone growth, and they have strong ability to inhibit apoptosis of osteoblasts, inhibit mesenchymal It has strong ability to inhibit osteoclast apoptosis, inhibit the expression of mesenchymal collagenase and reduce its degradation, and use of IGF-I neutralising antibody can effectively block parathyroid hormone-induced osteoclast growth and growth hormone-induced osteoclast bone resorption.TGF-β is mainly synthesised by platelets and bone, and is a member of the TGF-β superfamily. It has also been experimentally established that TGF- β1 and β2 are essential growth and differentiation factors involved in connective tissue healing and bone regeneration.TGF- β can activate the Smad pathway (Smad2 and Smad3) via serine/threonine kinase receptors I and II [89]. It has been observed that TGF- β promotes extracellular matrix production [90], stimulates type I collagen and fibronectin biosynthesis, and induces bone matrix deposition [91]. Thus, TGF- β not only initiates bone regeneration, but also supports long-term healing and bone regeneration, as well as remodeling of mature bone grafts [92,93]. However, the most important functions of TGF- β1 and -β2 are pro-osteoblast chemotaxis and mitogenesis, as well as the ability to stimulate collagen deposition during connective tissue healing and bone formation [94]. In addition, this factor inhibits osteoclast formation and bone resorption, resulting in superior bone formation over bone resorption [95]. In contrast, TGF- β can initiate the signalling pathway for BMP synthesis by bone progenitor cells and regulate the expression of growth factors in bone and cartilage tissues [96]. It has also been shown that BG-embedded bioactive molecules can be regulated to be released at a specific period of time, with the initial release being slow and the later release rate being proportional to the root of the square of time, so as to maintain a high concentration of bioactive molecules in the local area of the bone defects, stimulate the cellular function, initiate the process of osteogenesis, and accelerate the healing of the bone tissues [97]. When BG was used as a slow-release carrier of TGF-β, it prolonged the release time of cell growth factor, and directly stimulated osteogenesis through the control of cell growth factor, so that osteoblasts could proliferate under the regulation of cell growth factor, and at the same time, BG provided a good support for osteoblasts to grow, promoted the adhesion of cells, and accelerated the process of bone formation.

PRP orchestrates osteoblast-osteoclast crosstalk through cell-autonomous and non-cell-autonomous mechanisms, modulating osteoblastic differentiation and osteoclastogenesis via integrated signaling pathways. Lineage tracing demonstrates VEGF-mediated regulation of Gli1+ skeletal stem cells [40], while TGF-β synergistically enhances bone repair by suppressing adipogenesis [98]. PRP-derived PDGF directly recruits pericytes to neovessels [52], establishing functional vascular niches. Crucially, PRP attenuates osteoclast activity through dual pathways: RANKL downregulation [95] and PDGF-driven β-catenin stabilization that inhibits osteoclast differentiation (60 % reduction in TRAP+ cells) [72]—mechanistically involving Wnt/β-catenin-mediated suppression of NFATc1 signaling [70].

In summary, PRP can efficiently repair bone defects by regulating bone metabolism, is of autologous origin, does not cause disease transmission or immune rejection, is relatively easy to produce, and has significant potential for clinical application. However, in terms of mechanistic studies, there are fewer studies directly related to the molecular mechanism of PRP and osteogenesis, and the mechanism of action of in vitro injected PRP on osteogenesis is not clear in general. In addition, current research suggests that PRP works best in conjunction with bone grafting [99]. Wei et al. investigated whether PRPused in conjunction with allogeneic bone grafting could improve the outcome of displaced intra-articular calcaneal fractures. PRP was found to enhance the favorable outcome of allograft treatment of displaced heel fractures and was comparable to autograft alone [100]. Beyond modulating inflammation, PRP also exhibits direct anti-infective properties. Platelets contain potent antimicrobial peptides (e.g., thrombocidins, defensins) that disrupt microbial membranes and inhibit biofilm formation. In infected diabetic wounds, PRP significantly reduces bacterial load by enhancing phagocytosis and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation. Hydrogel-based PRP delivery systems further synergize this effect through sustained release of antimicrobial factors (e.g., LL-37), suppressing Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa proliferation while downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β). This dual action—direct pathogen clearance and immunomodulation—creates a sterile microenvironment conducive to bone regeneration. However, with more in-depth research on PRP in the future, it is believed that the specific research on the various mechanisms of PRP and the clinical application of PRP will be more and more perfect.

Integrated crosstalk of angiogenic, inflammatory, and metabolic pathways in PRP-mediated bone repair

PRP-derived TGF-β and IL-1ra effectively mitigate excessive inflammation by polarizing macrophages from pro-inflammatory M1 to reparative M2 phenotypes, establishing an immunoregulatory niche. M2 macrophages secrete angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF, PDGF-BB) that activate endothelial cell proliferation and migration, forming functional vascular networks which alleviate hypoxia and deliver nutrients (glucose, oxygen) and osteoprogenitors. Enhanced oxygenation and nutrient supply drive metabolic reprogramming of mesenchymal stem cells—augmenting glycolysis (a physiological hypoxia adaptation distinct from the Warburg effect) for rapid ATP generation. Concomitant downregulation of HIF-1α upon hypoxia relief activates oxidative phosphorylation, providing sustained energy for matrix mineralization and osteogenic differentiation.

In vivo evidence reveals spatiotemporally coupled mechanisms: In porcine tibial defects, PDGF from PRP recruits CD146+ pericytes via PDGFRβ signaling, with lineage tracing confirming their differentiation into Osterix+ osteoblasts [86]. Neovasculature-derived Noggin antagonizes BMP inhibitors, enabling TGF-β–mediated osteoblast maturation [50]. Rabbit critical-size defects demonstrate TGF-β1's dual role—suppressing TNF-α/IL-1β in macrophages (M1→M2 transition), while activating SMAD2/3 in osteoblasts to elevate collagen I deposition by 3.2-fold (p < 0.01) [89]. Intravital imaging shows peak TGF-β activity during the inflammation-to-remodeling transition (days 7–14 post-PRP) [93]. Crucially, vascular-metabolic crosstalk underpins efficacy: Osteocyte-specific IGF-1 knockout mice exhibit unresolved healing defects despite vascularization [67], confirming IGF-1's non-redundant role in osteoblastic glycolysis/mineralization. VEGF from nascent vessels accelerates matrix calcification via enhanced IGF-1R phosphorylation in osteoblasts (micro-CT quantification) [49].

PRP orchestrates bone repair through integrated immunomodulatory, angiogenic, and metabolic reprogramming. This neovascular network alleviates hypoxia and delivers osteoprogenitors while triggering MSC metabolic switching—from hypoxia-adapted glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation—fueling matrix mineralization. Crucially, in vivo evidence confirms spatiotemporal coupling of these pathways: PDGF-mediated pericyte recruitment, TGF-β–driven collagen deposition, and VEGF-IGF-1 crosstalk collectively enable dynamic tissue regeneration.

Summary and prospects

The far-reaching effects of PRP have attracted widespread attention in the rapidly evolving field of orthopaedic therapeutic research [101]. This paper delves into the intricate mechanisms through which PRP accelerates bone defect repair, highlighting its multifaceted role in promoting angiogenesis, mobilizing activated cells, and regulating bone metabolism. The review underscores the critical importance of PRP in modulating the inflammatory response at defect sites. Furthermore, it elucidates the osteoinductive capacity of the growth factors present in PRP, particularly their ability to enhance osteoblast proliferation. These attributes have led to the widespread adoption of PRP across various medical fields, especially orthopaedics, where its use has significantly expanded. A key advantage of PRP lies in its autologous origin, offering both economic and immunological benefits by circumventing the risks associated with exogenous growth factors, including potential immunogenic reactions. By eliminating concerns about disease transmission and minimizing complications, PRP has gained increasing traction in surgical applications [[102], [103], [104]]. Despite its growing use, the clinical outcomes surrounding PRP in fracture treatment remain contentious, with a body of evidence reflecting both supportive and contradictory findings. While some studies advocate for PRP's efficacy in accelerating bone healing and shortening recovery time, others have reported contrasting results, indicating a lack of significant improvement in healing rates, particularly in cases of closed fractures. These conflicting conclusions underscore the need for further investigation to clarify PRP's therapeutic potential and establish its definitive role in fracture management [105]. This divergence in findings emphasises the complexity of the role of PRP in the repair of bone defects. It emphasises the need for a nuanced understanding of the specific circumstances, fracture types and patient characteristics that may influence outcomes. Controversial results highlight the continuing challenge of reaching consensus on the clear benefits of PRP in fracture treatment. Donor-dependent variability in allogeneic PRP (such as fluctuations in platelet activity due to differences in donor age and health status) significantly impacts the stability of clinical efficacy. Existing studies face three major limitations that hinder accurate assessment: first, allogeneic PRP is primarily used for patients with contraindications for autologous therapy or those who have failed conventional treatment, but most related trials lack rigorous randomization and blinding (over 70 % of studies did not strictly adhere to blinding protocols); Second, treatment protocols exhibit significant heterogeneity (with doses ranging from 0.5 to 8 mL and varying activation methods), and patient baseline characteristics (such as diabetes or elderly populations), wound types, and postoperative care lack standardized reporting; Third, long-term safety data are insufficient, particularly regarding the immunological risks associated with high-dose repeated use [106]. To control variability, a three-tiered standardized strategy is recommended: prioritize closed-system preparation using a blood cell separator (e.g., Trima Accel® system), followed by closed-system PRP kits (e.g., Arthrex ACP®), and avoid manual open preparation (to prevent microbial contamination and loss of growth factors); centrifugation should use dual-stage optimized parameters (400g × 10 min → 800g × 10 min) to control residual red blood cells <5 % [107]; activators should prioritize calcium chloride (10–20 mM) to achieve sustained release of growth factors (t1/2 > 6 h), avoiding the explosive release caused by thrombin [108]. At the same time, the field of PRP bone healing lacks clear guidelines for PRP preparation. The molecular mechanisms underlying the role of PRP in bone healing have not been fully elucidated, which further complicates the standardisation of preparation protocols [109]. The absence of standardized methodologies results in considerable variability in PRP formulations, complicating the interpretation and replication of study findings. Establishing consensus on standardized preparation protocols is crucial for advancing the understanding of PRP's role in bone healing and for providing a solid foundation for future research and clinical applications. For seamless clinical translation, researchers must adopt unified classification systems: implementation of the PAW Classification (Platelets, Activation, White cells) or MIBO Standards (Minimum Information for Studies Evaluating Biologics in Orthopaedics) to define PRP composition. These systems categorize PRP by platelet concentration, leukocyte presence, and activation method, enabling cross-study comparisons [78].

Additionally, technical standardization requires:Indication-specific PRP selection: LP-PRP (leukocyte-poor) for degenerative conditions (e.g., osteoarthritis) versus LR-PRP (leukocyte-rich) for acute trauma [30,32]. Controlled activation: Standardized thrombin or calcium chloride concentrations (e.g., 10 % CaCl2) to regulate growth factor release kinetics [35]. Additionally, there is a pressing need for more randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in clinical evaluations of PRP. Currently, much of the clinical evidence supporting PRP use in fracture treatment is classified as Level IV, indicating a dependence on lower-quality evidence, such as case series and observational studies. This underscores the urgent necessity for well-designed RCTs to definitively assess the efficacy and safety of PRP in fracture management [105,110]. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) offer a superior level of evidence and form a more robust foundation for evidence-based practice. Conducting rigorously designed RCTs that adhere to standardized protocols is critical to addressing the current controversies, enhancing the reliability of outcomes, and guiding clinical decision-making on the use of PRP in fracture treatment. Additionally, while some studies have shown encouraging results, the exploration of PRP in this context underscores the need for further research into its long-term adverse effects and functional outcomes. A more comprehensive understanding of these aspects is essential to ensure the safe and effective integration of PRP into clinical practice for fracture management [111]. Large-scale trials and extended follow-up periods are essential to fully assess the safety of PRP and determine its long-term impact on functional outcomes [112]. While substantial evidence supports the efficacy and biocompatibility of platelet-rich plasma (PRP), long-term safety data—particularly regarding repeated administrations or high-dose regimens—remain limited. Autologous PRP mitigates immunogenic risks due to self-origin, whereas allogeneic PRP inevitably exhibits immunogenicity through donor-specific antigen presentation. Upon activation, platelets release soluble mediators (chemokines, cytokines, antimicrobial peptides) that engage endothelial cells and leukocytes, orchestrating innate and adaptive immune responses in tissues [113]. These immunomodulatory cascades are context-dependent, influenced by stimulants, effectors, coreceptors, and tissue microenvironments, culminating in complex interactions among immune mediators and cells [114]. Allogeneic PRP transfusion provokes three principal immune sequelae: platelet refractoriness, solid-organ transplant rejection, and transfusion-related immunomodulation (TRIM) [[115], [116], [117]]. Platelet refractoriness is predominantly Fc receptor-mediated and preventable via costimulatory blockade using CTLA4-Ig [117]. Critically, murine studies demonstrate that leukoreduced allogeneic PRP significantly attenuates alloantibody production (p < 0.01) and splenic cytokine responses (∗IL-6↓42 %, IFN-γ↓37 %∗) compared to unmodified allogeneic PRP, indicating leukoreduction as a viable strategy to suppress alloimmunization and secondary cytokine storms [114]. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) represent the gold standard in evidence generation, offering a stronger basis for evidence-based practice. Conducting well-designed RCTs that adhere to standardized protocols is critical for addressing current controversies, enhancing the reliability of results, and guiding clinical decision-making regarding the use of PRP in fracture treatment. Additionally, while some studies have reported encouraging outcomes, the exploration of PRP for fracture healing also highlights the necessity for further investigation into its long-term adverse effects and functional outcomes. Comprehensive research in these areas is essential to fully understand the risks and benefits of PRP in clinical practice [112]. Acknowledging and understanding these patient-specific differences is critical to combining research findings and tailoring PRP treatments to individual patient needs. Furthermore, the repair of large segmental bone defects due to trauma, inflammation and tumour surgery remains a major clinical problem. Animal models have been developed to test bone repair through tissue engineering methods that mimic real clinical situations. The studies varied in terms of animals, treated bone, chemical composition and scaffold structure. The advantage of PRP as a cellular matrix is that PRP is autologous and nontoxic, and therefore inherently safe, and there is no concern for disease transmission. However, the preparation of PRP involves the isolation of PRP followed by the use of calcium chloride and bovine thrombin to accelerate gel formation. The use of bovine thrombin has been reported to be associated with the production of factor V and XI antibodies, leading to the risk of life-threatening coagulopathy [118]. So despite the proven effectiveness of PRP to date, it must be acknowledged that there is still a lack of support for its use in bone healing applications. The inference that platelet therapy may be a reliable, effective and safe therapy for the treatment of bone wounds requires the completion of high-quality clinical trials and long-term follow-up. In particular, the supply of oxygen and nutrients to the cells inside the implanted scaffolds remains a major issue that requires further study [119]. In conclusion, there is no denying the great therapeutic potential of PRP in the field of bone repair, and with more research and clinical trials being essential to refine and expand these findings, the future of PRP in the field of orthopaedics and regenerative medicine appears to be promising. As the field continues to evolve, it is expected that PRP, with its abundance of growth factors and healing properties, will play an increasingly critical role in the repair of bone defects [120]. Looking forward, the future of PRP in bone healing promises precision medicine, integration with emerging therapies, expanded applications in unexplored areas, and enhanced technological advances. As the field evolves, the potential for PRP to play a significant role in improving the prognosis of patients with bone healing remains an exciting and dynamic area of exploration, with continued research and innovation shaping its trajectory.
