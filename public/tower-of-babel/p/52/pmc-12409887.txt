Navigating ethical challenges in digital transformation: insights on climate adaptation, microbiology, healthcare, robotics, and AI under the EU AI act: an experts panel discussion
Authors: Saeed M. Alghamdi, Okpala Charles Chikwendu, Okafor Ugochukwu Chukwuma, Daniel Otieno Okech, Modestus O. Okwu, Samina Khalid, Aggeliki Vlachostergiou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe ethical complexities of technological advancement are growing as fields such as climate adaptation, microbiology, healthcare, robotics, and artificial intelligence (AI) evolve rapidly. While these technologies offer innovative solutions to global challenges, they raise significant ethical concerns. In climate adaptation, AI-driven models and remote sensing technologies prompt questions about data privacy, environmental justice, and equitable access, especially for vulnerable populations. Similarly, advancements in microbiology and healthcare, such as genetic research and digital health tools, present ethical dilemmas related to informed consent, data security, and the exploitation of marginalized communities. In robotics and AI, ethical concerns are heightened due to their potential to automate decision-making, affect employment, and infringe on personal freedoms. The influence of AI in healthcare, law enforcement, and public services highlights the urgent need for ethical oversight to prevent bias and protect human rights. The EU AI Act addresses these challenges by categorizing AI systems by risk and setting stringent guidelines for high-risk applications, especially in sensitive sectors like healthcare. This article emphasizes the importance of balancing innovation with ethical responsibility, advocating for comprehensive regulatory frameworks, interdisciplinary collaboration, and global cooperation to ensure that technological advancements align with ethical standards and societal values.

Introduction

As digital technology rapidly advances, its integration across various domains presents both opportunities and ethical challenges (Dahlke et al., 2024). The incorporation of digital tools in sectors like climate change adaptation, microbiology, healthcare digitization, robotics, and artificial intelligence (AI) has led to transformative changes. These advancements provide new strategies to address pressing global issues but simultaneously require a deep reflection on their ethical implications.

Climate adaptation

In the context of climate change adaptation, digital technologies have played a crucial role in enhancing resilience, especially within marginalized communities (Ghosh & Orchiston, 2022). Tools for precise data collection, analysis, and dissemination have proven invaluable. Despite these benefits, ethical dilemmas arise, notably concerning data privacy, as personal and environmental data collection can infringe on individual rights. Moreover, access to these technologies is often limited to wealthier organizations, leaving less resourced communities disadvantaged and widening technological disparities in climate resilience.

Microbiology

Ethics in microbiology research is also a fundamental aspect of the field, requiring adherence to ethical standards, such as protocol reviews, informed consent, and proper documentation of any adverse events or deviations (Rhodes, 2016). Ethical practices in research emphasize principles such as voluntary participation, confidentiality, and communication of results (Gelling, 2016). Researchers are expected to follow strict codes of conduct when handling sensitive data (Shamoo & Resnik, 2009).

Current challenges in the microbiology field are twofold: handling ethical issues during laboratory work and ad dressing ethical concerns in the data and reports generated for patients or research (Steneck, 2006). There is pressure to perform, exerted by both the community and peers (Fanelli, 2009). Recognizing that unethical actions might be a frequent reality, microbiologists must be aware of and equipped to meet these issues in a prepared and measured way (Emanuel et al., 2004).

Healthcare

Just as microbiology grapples with the ethical implications of data handling and laboratory practices, the broader healthcare sector faces similar ethical challenges with the advent of digital technologies and AI-driven solutions. These innovations, driven by advancements in AI and big data, promise to revolutionize patient care by enabling early disease detection and personalized treatment plans (Jobin et al., 2019; Khalid et al., 2018). Yet, this transformation also brings significant ethical challenges, particularly in safe guarding patient privacy, ensuring data security (Vayena et al., 2018), and maintaining trust in digital health systems. These ethical concerns range from the need to follow current evidence-based standards, to privacy concerns, oversight, public trust, and accountability, as well as national and international data governance and management.

The healthcare industry has seen a considerable rise in data breach costs since 2020, with the average cost of a data breach in 2023 reported at USD 10.93 million. Therefore, healthcare information security is becoming a substantial responsibility for all health organizations. Healthcare applications and innovative equipment are crucial for patient care, but these tools are targets for hackers, who are silently working against healthcare data (Javaid et al., 2023). The main concern in healthcare digitization is the security of information and patients’ data, as the healthcare industry is particularly broad and new. Numerous ethical concerns seem crucial to follow to ensure improvement and fair benefit sharing in healthcare digitization.

Accessing patient data via technology, particularly health status information, has always been a critical aspect of healthcare delivery. Healthcare workers face numerous challenges in this area, further highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic. An innovative solution could be authorized limited access to patients’ data to avoid data fragmentation, as patient data is often scattered across various systems and institutions. For example, a personal digital file via a mobile app is one solution that allows patients to control access to their data and share it without any ethical violations (Alghamdi & Alghamdi, 2022).

Robotics

The robotics field faces its own set of ethical dilemmas, particularly as robots increasingly take over roles traditionally held by humans. While this transition could enhance efficiency and safety, it raises ethical concerns regarding privacy, security, liability, and the ethical treatment of autonomous systems. Other critical issues include the impact on employment and the responsibilities of developers and operators (Fosch-Villaronga et al., 2020). As AI continues to drive innovation across various sectors, careful consideration must be given to ensure these systems are trustworthy, transparent, and resistant to bias. Ethical concerns in AI often center on preventing the reinforcement of existing social inequalities.

Artificial intelligence

The EU AI Act serves as a pivotal regulatory framework designed to ensure the ethical development and use of AI systems. It plays an essential role in addressing the challenges posed by AI technologies across sectors like health care, robotics, and climate adaptation.

This article aims to critically examine the ethical considerations associated with these technologies, with a particular focus on the role of the EU AI Act in guiding ethical practices. By exploring these issues, the article seeks to illuminate how technological integration blurs traditional disciplinary boundaries.

As digital infrastructures increasingly underpin core functions in healthcare, environmental monitoring, robotics, and biosciences, these once-distinct fields now form a tightly woven ecosystem. This convergence brings about not only technological innovation but also new ethical challenges that transcend sectoral boundaries – challenges that require equally integrated ethical responses. The article will explore the ways these fields complement one another, ultimately advancing a comprehensive approach to innovation that aligns with the broader goals of social equity, justice, and sustainability.

Materials and methods

This article employs a qualitative, interdisciplinary reflective inquiry approach grounded in expert panel discussions conducted during the Future Digital Healthcare Innovation Short-Term Fellowship Program hosted by Brunel University, which took place from May to September 2024. The methodology involved structured and unstructured exchanges among a group of international researchers from diverse academic and professional backgrounds, each contributing domain-specific insights into the ethical dimensions of digital transformation. The panel included experts in clinical technology, industrial engineering, microbiology, educational policy, mechanical engineering, computer science, and artificial intelligence, representing institutions from Saudi Arabia, Nigeria, Kenya, Pakistan, and Greece. Each author offered critical reflections and thematic contributions based on professional experience and disciplinary expertise, which were collaboratively synthesized into a cohesive narrative. The discussions were iterative and dialogic in nature, enabling the identification of converging and diverging ethical concerns across sectors such as climate adaptation, microbiology, digital health, robotics, and AI regulation. Contributions were analyzed through thematic synthesis to explore how ethical challenges manifest in each domain and how regulatory frameworks like the EU AI Act can guide responsible technological innovation. Supplmentery file provided more details about the methodology used.

Results

Critical ethical reflections on digital technology in climate change adaptation

Digital tools, such as remote sensing, geographic information systems (GIS), and climate modeling software, have become essential components of climate adaptation research (Dube et al., 2022). These technologies allow researchers to collect accurate data on climate effects, model future outcomes, and refine adaptation strategies with higher precision (Goodchild, 2010). The availability of high-resolution satellite imagery has also enhanced real time monitoring of environmental changes. Despite their advantages, the adoption of these tools introduces ethical challenges that require careful consideration. A major concern revolves around the handling and safeguarding of personal data, particularly location-based information. As big data and machine learning expand their capacity to analyze climate data, they present new opportunities for identifying patterns and forecasting trends (Reichstein et al., 2019). However, these advancements also pose risks related to digital privacy and data protection if stringent safeguards are not implemented.

The increasing reliance on digital tools in climate adaptation research affects both the methodologies used and the development of policies. While these technologies facilitate more detailed studies, enhancing our understanding of climate impacts and adaptation requirements, they can also lead to an overdependence on technological solutions, potentially disregarding the value of local knowledge and low- tech alternatives, which are often more accessible and con textually appropriate (Nightingale et al., 2020). This evolution in digital technology use has contributed to disparities in research capabilities, with well-funded institutions in developed countries leading high-tech research, while resource-limited regions struggle to access these advanced tools. Such discrepancies highlight the need to address geographical and economic barriers that limit global research equity (Adeola et al., 2023).

The incorporation of digital tools in climate adaptation raises critical social and ethical issues. Researchers with access to advanced technologies often have greater influence over global climate policies, which can perpetuate the dominance of more privileged institutions while sidelining less resourced voices (Cunningham, 2015). This dynamic is particularly problematic for marginalized communities, which, despite contributing the least to global emissions, face the greatest climate risks. Over-reliance on digital solutions can shift focus away from addressing the root causes of climate vulnerability, such as social and economic inequities. A more comprehensive approach, integrating social, cultural, and economic factors alongside technical innovations, is essential for addressing the multifaceted challenges of climate adaptation (Adger et al., 2013).

Ethical considerations in microbiology research

The ethical considerations of using microorganisms, especially in biotechnology, revolve around potential risks to human health, environmental impacts, and issues of genetic modification. While some microorganisms are inherently pathogenic and pose serious dangers to humans, such as Bacillus-anthracis, which causes anthrax, others are not expected to harm humans. However, since certain species can become pathogenic under specific conditions, it is prudent to treat all microorganisms as potentially pathogenic (Rhodes, 2016). This cautious approach is crucial due to ethical concerns related to potential harm, informed consent from individuals and communities, self- experimentation, the rights of pregnant women with vertically transmissible diseases, mandatory screening tests, pandemic control, and immunization (Jonsen, 2003).

The use of bacteria in biotechnology exemplifies these ethical concerns. For instance, while bacteria can be engineered to produce beneficial substances such as insulin or antibiotics, there is a risk that these genetically modified organisms could escape into the environment, potentially transferring their engineered genes to other bacteria and creating harmful or treatment resistant strains (Macrae, 2007). The deliberate or accidental release of genetically modified bacteria into the environment can disrupt ecosystems, as engineered bacteria might consume essential nutrients needed by other organisms or contribute to the emergence of antibiotic resistant “superbugs”, posing significant risks to human health and biodiversity (Rhodes, 2016).

Working with known pathogenic microorganisms involves clear protocols, as their pathogenicity is usually well understood, allowing for the establishment of rational and effective containment measures. Various biosafety levels are implemented based on the risk level posed by different pathogens, guiding the handling of these microorganisms. Techniques such as disinfection, which removes most microorganisms from objects, and sterilization, which eliminates all forms of microbial life, including spores and viruses, are employed to manage these risks.

Moreover, ethical concerns about genetic modification also persist. Some argue that manipulating the genetic code of organisms is tantamount to “playing God,” raising fundamental questions about the sanctity of life (Jonsen, 2003). Thus, while the use of microorganisms in biotechnology holds promise for significant benefits, it is imperative to consider and address these ethical challenges. This careful consideration ensures that the application of biotechnological techniques is conducted responsibly, safeguarding both human health and the environment.

Ethical concerns in cyber security considerations in healthcare digitalization

In the era of digital healthcare, the protection of patient privacy and data security has become increasingly complex, with advanced analytics and the proliferation of big data posing significant challenges. As cyber-attacks and data breaches continue to rise, there is a critical need for robust security measures and ethical frameworks to safeguard sensitive health information and maintain trust in digital health systems.

Privacy is the main concern when we talk about big data uses in healthcare. As advanced analytics are applied to patients’ data for different purposes and more data sources become available, protecting privacy is an intricate challenge (Alghamdi et al., 2020). When this data is attacked by hacking databases for ransom and with cyber-attacks being frequently reported, data security has also become challenging. There are several data breaches reported previously by Breach Portal of the Health and Human Services (HHS) office. As of 2017, in about one hundred countries a ransom ware attack was reported with a ransom of 300 dollars in bit coins to regain access to affected machines. Information commissioner's Office UK reported that the health sector is facing most of the data incidents. Such incidents do not create a conducive environment as per the demand of digital health to easily circulate data between people, machines and institutions. Therefore, there is a need to ensure robust security measures by enforcing articulated policies for sharing data.

These concerns can be addressed by the adoption of proper technology, monitoring and evaluation of security systems, providing transparency and accountability mechanisms such as legal compensation in case of privacy harm resulting from privacy breach, and legal remedies to affected individuals. With the passage of time security and privacy concerns will continue to evolve but big data requirements demand more technical expertise, approachable policies, and regulatory supervision (Rezaei et al., 2021). There are six key ethical challenges highlighted in (Rezaei et al., 2021) referred to as justice, autonomy, privacy, security, responsibility, and procedural values. Out of these six ethical challenges, procedural values were found to be a key indicator of ethical concern in healthcare digitization. The procedural values concept can be defined by three components, inclusiveness, accountability, and transparency.

Another key indicator highlighted in this study is responsibility. This challenge comprises of trust, balance of power and the relationship between stakeholders and benefit sharing, these stakeholders include technical people, government, and provider. Considering responsibility as an essential element in the development of health systems means accepting the responsibility to build up the level of acceptance, satisfaction and trust between users and patients. Responsibility can also be seen as balance of power, authority and benefit sharing.

As a third key ethical indicator, Autonomy is relatively new in healthcare digitization and has not given enough attention previously although patient awareness and informed consent are most important which are directly related to human dignity. Most of the studies indicate that digital health care tools must protect the autonomy and independence of patients and their families during any treatment period. The best practice to utilize patients’ consent in the choice of treatment, awareness about how patients’ data will be collected and shared, as well as knowledge about the medical results. Justice is an important ethical concern defined by authorizing equal access to healthcare digital technology facilities, equal access to treatment without any discrimination, equality in data ownership and empowerment.

Therefore, healthcare digitization must compile all these ethical concerns before deployment of such systems. With the passage of time as digital healthcare system merge into practice, many more ethical concerns are raised. The biggest threat to digital healthcare in today's era is cyber-attacks which compromise all other ethical concerns. It is therefore needed to first keep in concern privacy and security of digital healthcare systems to make it safe from any cyber-attack and in case if system get compromised there must be well designed policies to rescue recovery from such attacks.

Ethical concerns in the field of robotics

As robotics technology continues to advance, it presents a complex and evolving set of ethical challenges that require careful consideration and proactive management. The increasing autonomy of robots, capable of making decisions without human intervention, raises critical questions about responsibility and accountability (Bryson, 2020). As these machines take on more sophisticated roles, it becomes imperative to establish clear guidelines that delineate who is liable when a robot's actions lead to unintended consequences. Without such frameworks, society risks navigating a grey area where accountability is ambiguous and potentially unjust.

Privacy is another significant concern, particularly as robots equipped with advanced sensors and AI become more prevalent in both public and private spaces. For example, robotic assistants in homes might inadvertently record personal conversations, while surveillance drones could monitor both public and private areas (Okwu et al., 2022). The potential misuse of this data by corporations or governments underscores the urgent need for stringent regulations. These regulations must regulate how data is collected, stored, and used, ensuring that individual privacy rights are upheld and that the benefits of robotics do not come at the cost of personal freedom and security.

Moreover, the rise of robotics poses substantial economic challenges, particularly concerning job displacement. As robots increasingly automate tasks, especially those that are repetitive or hazardous, there is a real risk of significant job losses, leading to economic disruption. To address this, policymakers and industry leaders must collaborate on strategies that include retraining and up skilling the workforce. This approach would help ensure that the economic benefits of robotics are shared equitably across society, rather than exacerbating existing inequalities in job displacement. Ethical considerations must also be woven into the very fabric of robotic design and development. Ensuring that robots are built with principles such as fairness, transparency, and inclusivity from the outset is crucial. Developers need to be mindful of the societal impacts of their creations and work to prevent biases in AI algorithms that could lead to discriminatory practices. This ethical vigilance should extend throughout the entire lifecycle of robotics, from initial conception to final deployment, to safeguard against unintended negative consequences (Okwu et al., 2020).

Ethical concerns in digital health technologies’ deployment

The deployment of Digital Health Technologies (DHTs) has revolutionized healthcare delivery by enhancing the precision, efficiency, and accessibility of medical services. Technologies such as wearable, mobile health applications, telemedicine, and Electronic Health Records (EHRs) have empowered healthcare providers with new tools to improve patient outcomes and enable personalized medicine (AlWatban et al., 2024). However, these advancements bring significant ethical challenges that must be addressed to ensure that the benefits of DHTs are realized without compromising ethical standards (Vayena et al., 2018) The key ethical considerations in the deployment of DHTs include privacy and confidentiality, informed consent, equity, and access, autonomy and control, and the broader implications of data security and regulation. Privacy and confidentiality are paramount in healthcare, where patient data is highly sensitive. The digitization of health records and the widespread use of connected devices in healthcare raise concerns about the protection of personal health information. Traditional methods of safeguarding privacy, such as anonymization and encryption, are increasingly inadequate in the face of advanced data analytics and AI-driven insights (Jumelle & Ispas, 2014). The ability to re-identify anonymized data, combined with the vast amounts of data collected by DHTs, poses significant risks to patient privacy (Brall et al., 2019). Ethical deployment of DHTs re quires stringent data protection measures, including robust encryption, access controls, and transparent data management practices that prioritize patient consent and control over their data.

The ethical concern of privacy is exacerbated by the potential for data breaches, which have become increasingly common in the healthcare sector. As highlighted by the 2023 Cost of Data Breach Report, the healthcare industry has seen a significant rise in the cost of data breaches, with an average cost of USD 10.93 million (Javaid et al., 2023). This underscores the need for healthcare organizations to prioritize data security as a critical ethical responsibility. Ensuring that patient data is protected from unauthorized access and cyber threats is essential to maintaining trust in digital health systems and safeguarding the integrity of patient care.

Informed consent is a fundamental ethical principle in healthcare, ensuring that patients are fully aware of and agree to the procedures and treatments they receive. However, the complexity of DHTs, particularly those involving AI and big data, can make it challenging for patients to understand what they are consenting to. The intricate nature of data collection, processing, and analysis in digital health can obscure the potential risks and benefits from users (Brall et al., 2019). To address this ethical concern, healthcare providers and technology developers must ensure that patients are provided with clear, comprehensive, and accessible information about how DHTs work, what data will be collected, how it will be used, and the potential risks involved. This transparency is crucial to empowering patients to make informed decisions about their healthcare. The deployment of DHTs also raises significant concerns about equity and access. While these technologies have the potential to democratize healthcare by making it more accessible to remote and underserved populations, there is a risk that they could exacerbate existing health disparities. Access to digital health solutions often depends on factors such as internet connectivity, digital literacy, and financial resources, which are unevenly distributed across different populations. This digital divide can result in unequal access to the benefits of DHTs, leaving disadvantaged groups further behind. Ensuring equitable access to these technologies is a critical ethical challenge that must be addressed through thoughtful design, policy interventions, and targeted initiatives to bridge the digital divide.

Another ethical concern in the deployment of DHTs is the potential impact on patient autonomy. As AI and machine learning algorithms are increasingly integrated into health care, there is a risk that decision-making could become overly reliant on these technologies, potentially marginalizing the role of human judgment and patient preferences. Ensuring that patients retain control over their healthcare decisions is essential to preserving their autonomy. This includes providing patients with the ability to understand and question the recommendations made by digital health tools, as well as the right to opt out of certain technologies if they prefer more traditional forms of care (Fosch-Villaronga et al., 2020).

With the rise of cyber threats and the increasing value of health data, ensuring robust data security is a critical ethical concern in the deployment of DHTs. Health data breaches can have severe consequences, including identity theft, financial loss, and the erosion of trust in healthcare systems. Organizations deploying DHTs must implement stringent security measures to protect against data breaches and cyber-attacks. This includes employing advanced encryption techniques, regular security audits, and establishing clear protocols for responding to data breaches. Additionally, there must be clear policies in place to address the potential misuse of data by third parties, including data commercialization and unauthorized access.

The regulatory landscape for DHTs is still evolving, with ongoing debates about the appropriate level of oversight needed to ensure the ethical deployment of these technologies. The EU AI Act represents a significant step toward establishing a regulatory framework that addresses the ethical concerns associated with AI in digital health. This regulatory approach emphasizes the need for transparency, accountability, and fairness in the development and deployment of AI-driven healthcare solutions. Continuous monitoring and evaluation of DHTs are essential to identify and address emerging ethical issues as technology evolves.

Best practices to meet ethical concerns, discussion, recommendations and the way forward

The integration of digital technologies across various sec tors, including climate adaptation, microbiology, health care, robotics, and AI, presents both remarkable opportunities and significant ethical challenges. To ensure that these innovations align with societal values and promote equitable outcomes, it is crucial to establish and follow best practices tailored to the ethical concerns in each domain. In climate adaptation, for example, the adoption of digital technology offers significant benefits but also presents challenges related to inclusively, access, and ethical considerations. There is a need for a balanced approach that leverages the advantages of digital tools while ensuring equitable access and integrating diverse knowledge systems. Efforts should be made to enhance access to digital technology in developing regions through capacity building initiatives, funding support, and technology transfer programs. Researchers should strive to incorporate traditional and indigenous knowledge into digital frameworks to create more holistic and culturally relevant adaptation strategies. Ethical guidelines should be developed to ensure that the use of digital technology in climate adaptation research respects local contexts and avoids reinforcing existing in equalities. International collaboration and partnerships can help bridge the gap between well-resourced and resource constrained institutions, promoting a more equitable distribution of technological benefits. These recommendations may assist the research community in harnessing the potential of digital technology to advance climate adaptation efforts while ensuring that the benefits are shared broadly and equitably.

In microbiology, best practices should revolve around maintaining the integrity of research processes, protecting human subjects, and ensuring the responsible use of data. Ethical considerations in microbiology involve obtaining informed consent, ensuring confidentiality, and safeguarding the welfare of participants involved in research (Rhodes, 2016). Researchers must adhere to protocols that involve ethical reviews by institutional boards, accurate reporting of adverse events, and complete transparency in documenting deviations from established procedures. In terms of data management, microbiologists must apply principles such as anonymity and confidentiality, especially when working with sensitive patient data or community health information. Ensuring voluntary participation and minimizing harm to individuals or populations through safe laboratory practices, including adherence to biosafety regulations, also represent core best practices in addressing ethical concerns in the field. Moreover, microbiologists must prioritize ethical data sharing, ensuring transparency, reproducibility, and respect for intellectual property.

In healthcare cyber security, the protection of patient privacy and securing sensitive medical data are paramount. Healthcare institutions must implement robust data encryption techniques, secure access controls, and continual monitoring of information systems to prevent data breaches.

Best practices also involve the adoption of the highest international standards, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), to ensure compliance with legal requirements for data protection (Vayena et al., 2018). Ethical cyber security practices also extend to the responsible handling of health records and avoiding unauthorized access or misuse of patient data. As healthcare digitization continues to expand, institutions should adopt cyber security frameworks that include regular audits, incident response plans, and staff training on privacy regulations and ethical data handling. By ensuring robust privacy protections and adopting transparency in data management, healthcare providers can foster trust and accountability in their digital health systems.

The evolving field of robotics also necessitates a proactive approach to address ethical concerns. As robotics technology continues to evolve, ethical considerations must keep pace with technological advancements. By establishing clear accountability frameworks, protecting privacy, man aging economic impacts, recognizing the moral status of robots, and promoting ethical design practices, society can harness the benefits of robotics while mitigating potential risks.

Addressing the ethical concerns in robotics involves adopting a set of best practices that ensure the responsible development and deployment of robotic technologies. Implementing best practices can help address the ethical concerns associated with robotics, ensuring that the development and deployment of these technologies are aligned with societal values and ethical standards (Draper & Sorell, 2017). Some of the best practices designed to meet ethical concerns include accountability, protecting privacy, managing economic impacts, respecting the moral status of robots, promoting ethical design, and ensuring transparency and inclusivity. The robotics industry can navigate the complex ethical landscape and contribute positively to society. The ethical considerations surrounding the deployment of DHTs are multifaceted and require a proactive, multidisciplinary approach to address. As these technologies continue to evolve and become more integrated into healthcare, it is essential to balance innovation with the protection of fundamental ethical principles such as privacy, equity, autonomy, and justice. One suggested innovation is patient prompt access to their health information via patient online portals, as it can enable them to manage their health more proactively. However, there are concerns about the anxiety that might arise from viewing abnormal results without prior counseling (Steitz et al., 2023). By addressing these ethical challenges head on, we can ensure that the deployment of DHTs leads to a more equitable, effective, and trustworthy healthcare system that benefits all members of society. The future of digital health depends on our ability to navigate these ethical concerns and create a framework that supports the responsible and sustainable development of these transformative technologies.

The rise of AI introduces another layer of complexity, especially regarding bias, transparency, and the perpetuation of social inequalities. The EU AI Act provides a regulatory framework to ensure AI systems are developed and used ethically. It emphasizes the need for AI to be trustworthy, explainable, and robust, adhering to the “do no significant harm (DNSH)” principle, which mitigates risks and promotes fairness across sectors. Upholding the core principles of transparency, technical soundness, privacy, and diversity will foster ethical innovation while tackling the challenges of integrating AI into domains such as climate adaptation, healthcare, and robotics. Strengths and limitations

This study's key strength lies in its interdisciplinary scope, integrating ethical reflections across diverse domains – climate adaptation, microbiology, healthcare, robotics, and artificial intelligence. By drawing on expert perspectives and recent regulatory developments such as the EU AI Act, the study offers a comprehensive overview of the ethical tensions arising from digital transformation. The use of reflective inquiry and thematic synthesis enhances the richness of insights, allowing the article to present practical recommendations and best practices that are relevant across sectors. Moreover, by framing the analysis within the context of regulatory ethics, the paper bridges theoretical discourse with emerging policy frameworks, making it valuable for academics, practitioners, and policymakers alike.

Nonetheless, the study also presents certain limitations. The expansive scope, while offering breadth, may compromise depth in the ethical analysis of each individual field. Additionally, the conceptual nature of the research, which relies on expert panel discussions rather than empirical data, limits the ability to generalize findings or evaluate ethical outcomes in real-world contexts. While the EU AI Act provides a solid anchor for regulatory discussion, it reflects a Eurocentric perspective that may not capture the nuances of ethical governance in other global regions. Finally, given the rapid evolution of digital technologies, some of the ethical concerns and regulatory recommendations discussed may require continual reassessment to remain timely and effective.

Conclusion

This study highlights the ethical complexities emerging from digital transformation across interconnected fields such as healthcare, climate adaptation, microbiology, robotics, and AI. By framing these issues through the lens of the EU AI Act, the paper underscores the need for transparent, inclusive, and accountable governance. The interdisciplinary insights emphasize the importance of aligning innovation with ethical responsibility. Future research should build on this foundation by incorporating real-world case studies and expanding the regulatory analysis to capture diverse global perspectives.
