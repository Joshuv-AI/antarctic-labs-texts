Best treatment option for secondary mitral regurgitation surgery: a network meta-analysis of randomized and non-randomized controlled studies
Authors: Francesco Nappi, Antonio Salsano, Arnaldo Dimagli, Francesco Santini, IvanCarmine Gambardella, Omar Ellouze
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study is to ascertain whether subvalvular papillary muscle repair in conjunction with restrictive mitral valve annuloplasty represents the most efficacious treatment for patients presenting with secondary ischemic mitral regurgitation, as compared to restrictive mitral valve annuloplasty alone and to mitral valve replacement. A network meta-analysis was conducted to investigate outcomes of randomized controlled trials, propensity-matched studies, and observational studies, comparing various treatments for secondary ischemic mitral regurgitation. The average follow-up duration for late mortality was 4.4 years. Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) without mitral valve surgery had a late mortality incidence of 3.7%. Restrictive mitral annuloplasty demonstrated a rate of 6.5%, while restrictive mitral annuloplasty + CABG resulted in a rate of 4.1%. Subvalvular papillary muscle repair plus restrictive mitral annuloplasty ± CABG and mitral valve replacement + CABG had rates of 4.4% and 5.1%. SUCRA analysis showed that CABG was the most effective treatment for reducing late mortality (70.0%). This was followed by subvalvular papillary muscle repair plus restrictive mitral annuloplasty with or without CABG (62.4%). The top strategy for decreasing early death, reoperation, and readmission to the hospital for heart failure is subvalvular papillary muscle repair plus restrictive mitral annuloplasty with or without CABG, based on SUCRA probabilities (84.6%, 85.54%, and 86.3%, respectively). Subvalvular papillary muscle repair plus restrictive mitral annuloplasty ± CABG has potential to reduce the risks associated with early mortality, reoperation, and re-hospitalization for heart failure. However, further research is required to substantiate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75173-y.

Introduction

Extensive research indicates that combining subvalvular papillary muscle (PM) repair (SPM-r) with restrictive mitral annuloplasty (RMA) may provide a survival benefit when compared to using RMA alone in patients with secondary ischemic mitral regurgitation (SIMR)1–10. These procedures may also be performed with1–4 or without CABG11–15. However, current guidelines do not endorse combining subvalvular papillary muscle repair with restrictive mitral annuloplasty and CABG16,17 due to the method’s intricacy, which requires the papillary muscles to be approximated1–3, encircled1–3,7,9,18, or relocated4,8,10. As a result, currently there is a preference for restrictive valve procedures or valve replacement procedures16,17,19–24. In addition, papillary muscles surgery’s advantages have solely been studied in small randomized controlled trials carried out at one centre1–4 and non-randomized studies5–10.

It has been proposed that the better results linked to subvalvular papillary muscle repair with restrictive mitral annuloplasty when compared with restrictive mitral valve annuloplasty alone could be due to unacounted-for variables and prejudice in treatment, or sample sizes that lack repeatability, exhibit excessive bias in patient selection, and do not accurately mirror real-world situations1–10,18. Additionally, a significant unresolved question pertains to the comparative benefits of incorporating mitral valve repair into CABG for patients with moderate secondary ischemic mitral regurgitation. Although ischemic mitral regurgitation in patients who received CABG is linked to adverse outcomes, the advantages of incorporating mitral valve repair remain inconclusive and contradictory25–29.

Network meta-analysis (NMA), which applies adjusted30,31 and unadjusted32 indirect comparison among treatments, offers a valuable approach to minimize heterogeneity and allocation biases, especially when analyzing both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies. This technique has proved useful in attaining unbiased findings30–32. To date, there has been no network meta-analysis comparing the treatment strategies of SPM-r, RMA, mitral valve replacement (MV-rpl) with and without CABG for secondary ischemic mitral regurgitation requiring surgery, even though we only have evidence reported in pairwise meta-analysis33–36.

We conducted a network meta-analysis to examine late survival (primary outcome) and other clinical outcomes based on the type of surgical option employed for SIMR.

Results

Description of the included studies and of the population

21 studies met the inclusion criteria and were analysed (see Table 1and Fig. 1). Of these, four studies were international or multicentre. Six studies were conducted in the United States, three each in Italy and Japan, two in Germany, and one in France, Iran, and the United Kingdom. A total of 4458 patients were included in the seven RCTs and 14 observational studies. Further details and demographics of the studies are presented in Table 2. The patient cohort for the studies ranged from 26 to 1250. Most of these individuals exhibited a low or low-normal ejection fraction, with a mean range of 30–35%. Furthermore, Table 2 presents the mean of left ventricular end diastolic dimension (LVEDD), which fell in the range of 60–70 millimeters. Supplementary Table 1 denotes that half of the domains assessed in most randomized control trials had a low or low risk of bias. Based on the Newcastle-Ottawa scale, observational studies achieved a median rating of 8 or 9 stars in Supplementary Table 2. The network plot is shown in Fig. 2.

All-cause late mortality

Tables outlining the network, treatments, and comparisons can be located in the Supplementary Materials. A comprehensive analysis was conducted on late mortality due to any cause following treatment, accounting for 4458 patients from 21 studies. The mean follow-up time was 4.4 years. The network meta-analysis found that the mortality rate for coronary artery bypass grafting alone was 3.7% (1.0-6.3%). When compared, the use of restrictive mitral valve annuloplasty was associated to a mortality rate of 6.5% (0–10.0%). Combining RMA with CABG led to a mortality rate of 4.1% (1.3–10.0%), while subvalvular papillary muscle repair with restrictive mitral annuloplasty ± CABG resulted in a mortality rate of 4.4% (0-10.5%). Finally, mitral valve replacement plus CABG was associated with a mortality rate at follow-up of 5.1% (2.1–11.6%). These findings are fully presented in Table 3 and Supplementary Table 3. Figure 3A presents the effectiveness and associated uncertainty of each intervention for reducing late mortality. A pairwise meta-analysis has confirmed these findings (see Supplementary Fig. 4).

Early mortality

Tables showing the network, treatments and comparisons are provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Early mortality estimates were collected from 21 studies, comprising 4,458 patients, with 301 identified events (refer to Table 3, Supplementary material). CABG surgery without mitral valve intervention exhibited an aggregate early mortality rate of less than 0.01% (0.0-3.7%). Early mortality rates were as follows: 7.6% (0.0–13.3%) for restrictive mitral annuloplasty, 5.5% (1.5–10.3%) for RMA in conjunction with CABG, 5.3% (0-11.5%) for subvalvular papillary muscle repair with restrictive mitral annuloplasty ± CABG, and 10.2% (4.0-21.7%) for MV-rpl coupled with CABG (refer to Table 3 and Supplementary Table 7). Figure 3B indicates the comparative efficacy and uncertainty of available interventions concerning early mortality. The data indicates that patients who underwent mitral valve replacement with CABG have a higher risk of early mortality compared to those who received subvalvular papillary muscle repair with restrictive mitral annuloplasty ± CABG (OR 3.13, 05%CI: 1.08–10.21) and RMA with CABG (2.17, 95%CI: 1.51–3.46, as shown in Fig. 3B).

According to SUCRA analysis, the treatment with the highest effectiveness was determined to be subvalvular papillary muscle repair with restrictive mitral annuloplasty and optional CABG (SUCRA = 84.58). This was followed by CABG (SUCRA = 61.21), restrictive mitral annuloplasty with CABG (SUCRA = 59.80), RMA (SUCRA = 36.31), and finally MV-rpl with CABG (SUCRA = 8.10), as demonstrated in Fig. 4B. The Supplementary Materials provide descriptive tables for the network, treatments, and comparisons. Specific terms, abbreviations, and symbols were used consistently once introduced. A total of 3433 patients underwent reoperation assessment across 15 studies, with an average follow-up of 4.6 years. The NMA revealed that for CABG surgery not involving the mitral valve, the total re-operation rate was 0.8% (0.0-6.3%). However, for restrictive mitral annuloplasty, it was 2.7% (0.5–7.8%). Meanwhile, the rate for RMA with CABG was 1.0% (0.0-3.70%). Additionally, for subvalvular papillary muscle repair with restrictive mitral annuloplasty with or without a CABG procedure, the re-operation rate was 1.4% (0-3.9%). Tables outlining the network, treatments, and comparisons are available in the Supplementary Materials. We assessed 3511 patients across 13 studies during a mean follow-up of 4.6 years for re-hospitalization due to heart failure. The NMA found that the re-hospitalization rate for heart failure following CABG without mitral valve surgery was 14.2% (0.5–23.5%). Restrictive mitral annuloplasty alone had a re-hospitalization rate of 5.5% (2.0-7.8%), while RMA and CABG showed a lower rate of 2.8% (1.8–21.3%). Discussion

Several techniques, including isolated restrictive mitral annuloplasty11–15,19–24 RMA combined with leaflet procedure37, and subvalvular papillary muscle surgical repair like papillary muscles sling1–3,7,9,18, Papillary muscles approximation1–3,5,6, Papillary muscles relocation4,8, and Ring plus String10, have gained popularity as alternatives to the former routine of mitral valve replacement19–24 for the surgery of secondary ischemic mitral regurgitation. In the last decade the introduction of subvalvular procedures appeared to enhance the outcomes of valve repair. However, the technical complexity involved with subvalvular repair and the lack of randomized clinical trials with robust evidence have limited its widespread use, resulting in its exclusion from the AHA/ACC and ESC guidelines16,17. A meta-analysis comparing subvalvular papillary muscle repair with restrictive mitral annuloplasty versus RMA alone found that the addition of subvalvular papillary muscle repair was linked to a notably lower frequency of MR ≥ 2 as compared to only RMA (OR 0.27, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.38, P = 0.0001)35. In addition, two recent published pairwise meta-analyses examining the treatment options for SIMR have either focused solely on mortality rates or on a range of clinical outcomes following surgical intervention33,36. Based on our meta-analysis33, mitral valve replacement is associated with elevated early mortality rates but decreased reoperation and readmission rates, relative to restrictive mitral annuloplasty for moderate-to-severe SIMR. Although isolated outcomes comparing valvular and adjunct subvalvular procedures do not show significant advantages, the inclusion of subvalvular procedures lowers the possibility of significant postoperative adverse events. Vassileva et al.36 identified a notably increased likelihood of short-term mortality (summary odds ratio 2.667 (95% confidence interval (CI) 1.859–3.817) and long-term mortality (summary hazard ratio 1.352 (95% CI 1.131–1.618) among patients administered MR-rpl, when contrasted with those treated with RMA.

The mentioned meta-analyses were limited to paired comparisons between two treatments, and therefore only partially support surgeons. Our study aims to compile evidence from various head-to-head studies into one comprehensive network meta-analysis for comparing all treatment modalities. Network meta-analysis can amalgamate results into a unified analysis comparing three or more interventions.

The results of our research indicate: (1) The optimal strategy is to perform subvalvular papillary muscle repair along with RMA and correlate it with CABG. This method is safe and effective regarding early and late mortality, readmission for heart failure, and repeat surgery. (2) Myocardial revascularization is essential to manage ischemic mitral regurgitation, with CABG representing the optimal solution in reducing delayed mortality. (3) mitral valve replacement, when combined with CABG, seems to be the least effective treatment regarding short-term and long-term mortality rates, as well as hospitalization for heart failure after surgery.

Managing secondary or functional MR presents a particular challenge since these types of MR are predominantly related to the left ventricle rather than a specific disease process of the mitral valve1–3,38,39. Therefore, the focus of therapy is primarily on easing the fundamental LV condition and tethering forces due to papillary muscles displacement2,38–40. Managing the secondary ischemic mitral regurgitation should prioritise restoring LV structure and function, rather than resorting to direct mechanical interventions to reduce MR caused by valvular disorders2,38–42. In fact, the valve leaflets and chordal structures of affected patients are bystanders and not involved in the disease process. On the other hand, displacement of the papillary muscles is seen during SIMR, specifically the posterolateral displacement of the posteromedial papillary muscle with increased apical leaflet tethering2,43.

Our analysis showed that the combination of subvalvular papillary muscle repair with restrictive mitral annuloplasty provided a survival advantage when compared to RMA alone in cases where CABG was performed, with a SUCRA value of 62.41 compared to 58.37. This finding agrees with what was stated in previous pairwise meta-analyses33 and in a post hoc analysis of the RCTs1–4, and observational studies5–10 and seems to be linked to the significant improvement of LVEDD1–10. Furthermore, our analysis emphasises that subvalvular repair targets LV dysfunction, resulting in cardiac dilatation and geometric distortion that may significantly compromise the muscle supporting normal mitral valve coaptation. In the studied patient groups, no significant hemodynamic levels of recurrent MR were found after using subvalvular PMs repair procedures. Patients who undergo both subvalvular papillary muscle repair with restrictive mitral annuloplasty have a significantly lower risk of reoperation and rehospitalization for heart failure than those who undergo RMA alone (SUCRA score = 85.54 vs. 30.49 and SUCRA score = 86.30 vs. 35.79). The risks associated with PMs surgical repair were significantly reduced.

Timely execution of subvalvular PMs repair procedures is essential to minimize the onset of ventricular damage and early remodeling. Prioritising this stage guarantees the best possible outcome. Hence, it is essential to carry out the surgical procedure on the papillary muscles prior to significant ventricular enlargement, which can impede mitral valve leaflet al.ignment. The importance of papillary muscles handling has been confirmed by the Osaka group9,44. Researchers discovered that implementing restrictive mitral annuloplasty alone failed to address leaflet tethering, indicating that the reemergence of mitral regurgitation is primarily associated with the distance between the interpapillary muscles. Reverse remodeling of the LV following a restricted mitral annuloplasty decreases the gap between interpapillary muscles from 31 ± 6 to 25 ± 5 mm. Thus, it potentially offsets the amplified angle of the posterior leaflet and improves its advantages44.

Due to the fact that most patients with ischemic mitral regurgitation have multivessel coronary artery disease that necessitates revascularization, surgeons must contemplate whether to incorporate mitral valve repair into CABG. There is an ongoing debate over the effectiveness of the combined MV repair procedure with CABG. Although various RCTs and observational studies indicate the advantages of including MV repair to CABG3–13,25,26, as it can prevent further negative changes and decrease the possibility of heart failure13,15,26, some contradict these findings12,27,28,45 and others have neutral results29,46. For patients with severe ischemic mitral regurgitation, surgical revascularization’s benefits are well-established, provided that they have coronary targets that are susceptible to high-grade proximal lesions affecting ischemic yet viable myocardium.

In this analysis, it was demonstrated that myocardial revascularisation effectively reduces long-term mortality rates, resulting in a higher SUCRA score of 70.04. Our findings further corroborate and reinforce the conclusions of previous research, which underscores the pivotal role of CABG in facilitating left ventricular remodeling and desynchrony. Improvements in global and regional wall motion, as well as reverse LV remodeling, following CABG alone are indicative of viable myocardium28,47. For example, Penicka et al.27 found that in patients with moderate IMR who underwent CABG alone, the resolution of MR after surgery was associated with a rise in viable cardiac segments and a reduction in LV desynchrony at baseline. LV dimensions and remodeling showed a significantly greater improvement in the study population where CABG was performed in the entire group than in other studies where CABG treatment was excluded in some patients, resulting in worse outcomes due to adverse left ventricular remodelling19. In the CTSNet randomized controlled trial, this condition arose when only 73% of patients received revascularisation. The inferior long-term outcomes in left ventricular remodeling could be attributed to a lack of myocardial revascularisation and previous coronary intervention19.

Our findings indicate a notable decrease in re-hospitalization rates for heart failure with patients who undergo a procedure combining restrictive mitral annuloplasty and CABG (2.8%) or subvalvular papillary muscle repair with restrictive mitral annuloplasty ± CABG (3.83%) as opposed to individuals treated solely with CABG (14.2%) or solely with RMA (5.5%). Our results are consistent with a pairwise meta-analysis including four randomized controlled trials (n = 505) and 15 observational studies (n = 3785)34. In patients with moderate-to-severe IMR, concomitant MV surgery reduced the likelihood of moderate-to-severe MR recurrence at follow-up, according to Virk et al.34. However, it did not reduce late mortality. The discrepancy in long-term survival could be attributed to the use of the network meta-analysis. Direct and indirect comparisons offer greater accuracy when estimating outcomes, in comparison to pairwise comparisons30–32.

It should be noted that patients who receive mitral valve replacement are typically older and have more accompanying diseases than those who undergo repair. As such, NMA can account for baseline disparities- a crucial element in nonrandomized studies. Moreover, an NMA enables accounting for differences when specific risk factors are not disclosed or measured. This may explain why certain studies have found no differences in short- or long-term survival rates between patients undergoing restrictive mitral repair compared to those undergoing MV replacement20, while several other reports have shown better outcomes for restrictive mitral repair due to the assumed advantages of preserving the subvalvular apparatus to maintain LV function36,48–51 Finally, two randomized controlled clinical trials11,19 were conducted involving patients with moderate or severe MR caused by ischemic disease but with relatively preserved LV function (ejection fraction ≥ 40%). The trials found that mitral valve repair using a restrictive mitral annuloplasty or chordal-sparing mitral valve replacement did not achieve pre-specified benefits on cardiac geometry, yielded no favorable long-term effects on clinical outcomes, and had a high recurrence rate of MR. Therefore, the surgical management of secondary or functional MR has created uncertainty regarding whether to restore LV structure and function with drugs and devices or to directly intervene to reduce MR. The proficient application of neurohormonal antagonists and resynchronization devices can reverse adverse remodeling. This, in turn, can lead to a significant reduction in LV volumes, thereby alleviating MR in a substantial number of patients52–54.

Study limitations

Common limitations related to meta-analyses of observational studies are also present in this NMA. Although statistical adjustment and the use of NMA may counteract these issues, a source of bias remains due to between-studies heterogeneity. Important issues to report relate to the surgical protocols employed in restrictive mitral annuloplasty such as undersizing by one or two sizes, the subvalvular repair technique used (re-approximation/sling or relocation) and mitral valve replacement (total sparing or partial sparing), as well as the underlying cause of death during follow-up. Furthermore, it is essential to highlight that despite our analysis encompassing solely adjusted and unadjusted studies, the existence of unmeasured confounders and treatment allocation biases cannot be ruled out, as mentioned in other meta-analyses55. Nevertheless, we firmly believe that the network meta-analysis methodology and the low-moderate grade heterogeneity reported in the studies would have alleviated these biases.

Conclusions

We present the initial network meta-analysis exclusively focusing on disparities in clinical outcomes based on the treatment approach employed for secondary ischemic mitral regurgitation. The analysis results indicate that subvalvular repair in conjunction with restrictive mitral annuloplasty is superior to isolated restrictive mitral annuloplasty. Furthermore, it is clear that subvalvular repair in conjunction with restrictive mitral annuloplasty offers comparable long-term and perioperative outcomes. Isolated CABG carries a reduced risk of early mortality but has negative long-term outcomes. Therefore, it is recommended only for high-risk patients unable to undergo valve procedures with aortic cross clamp and cardioplegia. Future studies will investigate whether certain anatomical features of secondary mitral regurgitation would benefit from subvalvular repair or whether this operation can be considered a “one-size-fits-all” approach.

Methods

Search Strategy and Registration

This network meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension statement (PRISMA)56. To facilitate the search, we created a PICO protocol consisting of the following elements: population (P), intervention (I), comparison (C), and outcome (O). P: Participants with ischemic secondary mitral regurgitation (ISMR); I: Intervention of subvalvular-papillary muscle surgical repair (SPM-r) and restrictive mitral annuloplasty (RMA); C: Comparison group: RMA, Mitral Valve Replacement (MV-rpl), Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting (CABG) and O: all-cause late mortality, 30-day or in-hospital mortality based on each study’s definition, reoperation, and rehospitalization for heart failure. The current network meta-analysis has been registered in Prospero (CRD42021253525).

Literature search

In the current study SIMR is defined as echocardiographic evidence of any structural (chordal or leaflet) mitral valve disease or ruptured papillary muscle. We conducted a comprehensive analysis of databases (PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane, Web of Science) from their inception, considering only English language articles. All searched materials were published through 15th June 2023. Our search syntax incorporated specific keywords: “ischemic secondary mitral regurgitation, “functional mitral regurgitation”, “papillary muscle approximation”, “papillary muscle sling”, “papillary muscle relocation”, “restrictive mitral annuloplasty”, “mitral valve replacement”, and “CABG coupled with papillary muscle approximation, papillary muscle sling, papillary muscle relocation, restrictive mitral annuloplasty, or mitral valve replacement”. Studies (randomized controlled trials, propensity-matched studies and observational studies) were selected for the meta-analysis by applying the following inclusion criteria: studies that compared treatments for SIMR, including procedures on papillary muscles, replacement or repair of the valve, as well as valvular and subvalvular procedures associated with CABG. Additionally, studies were considered if they reported data on recurrence of mitral valve regurgitation, mortality, and reintervention rates during short- and/or long-term follow-up, and complications such as rehospitalizations for each group. Studies with less than 10 participants per arm at the point of extraction, as well as those containing already reported data from other included studies, were excluded.

Data extraction

The literature search results were independently extracted and analysed by two investigators, namely A.S. and A.D. resolved by a third investigator, F. N. Relevant information included study design, participant characteristics, inclusion criteria, procedure details, outcome efficacy measures, and study quality (risk of bias).

Assessment of the risk of Bias

The risk of bias was assessed independently by two researchers (A. S. and A. D.) using a validated checklist developed by Downs and Black57. This checklist was adapted to include the specific parameters relevant to this study. There were 27 items on the checklist, each with a binary score. A score of 1 indicated sufficient information provided while a score of 0 indicated insufficient or missing information. Scores ranged from 0 to 13. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by a third investigator (F. N.).

Interventions

For the treatment of secondary mitral regurgitation, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), mitral valve replacement plus CABG, restrictive mitral annuloplasty, restrictive mitral annuloplasty plus CABG, and subvalvular papillary muscle repair with restrictive mitral annuloplasty with or without CABG were compared (Table 2).

Subvalvular papillary muscle repair group included: papillary muscle approximation1,5–7,9, papillary muscle relocation4–8, and secondary chordal-cutting arising from the papillary muscle or muscles affected by the infarcted.

myocardium37.

Outcomes

Primary endpoint was all-cause late mortality. (mortality at follow-up occurring at least 30 days after surgery). Secondary outcomes included early mortality (30-days or in-hospital, based on each study definition), reoperation and, rehospitalization for heart failure.

Statistical analysis

Network meta-analysis evaluates intervention efficacy by analyzing direct comparisons within studies and indirect comparisons across studies based on a common comparator. Unlike traditional meta-analysis that summarizes the results of studies evaluating the same treatment/placebo combination, network meta-analysis compares the results of two or more studies. The advantages of network meta-analysis are precise estimates and relative ranking of all interventions. This level of accuracy is attained by incorporating direct comparison evidence with information from two treatments derived from a common comparator in indirect comparisons30–32.

A network meta-analysis was conducted using multivariate meta-analysis to combine evidence from multiple studies and assess the effectiveness of mixed interventions. Heterogeneity among comparisons was accounted for using a random-effects model. A network diagram was created to provide both qualitative and quantitative synthesis of the studies included in this analysis. The random-effects model was used to analyze all outcome measures and obtain odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) to compare different treatments. Predictive interval plots were then generated to present the results for both direct and indirect comparisons.

As far as the network meta-analysis is concerned, the outcomes were analyzed across all arm-level studies, with direct and indirect comparisons using a mixed-treatment comparison based on a Bayesian hierarchical model. The network meta-analysis was performed using the “BUGSnet” package of the R software (version 3.6.3; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo modeling was used to rank the treatments according to the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) probabilities. We specified a burn-in period of 50,000 iterations followed by 100,000 iterations with 10,000 adaptations. Both fixed and random effects models were performed, and evaluation of model fitting was based on the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC). Consistency, which assesses statistical agreement between indirect and direct evidence required for validation of the transitivity assumption was examined through DIC and unrelated mean effects model.

The ranking is derived by the probability scores based on the meta-analytic point estimates and standard errors. Probability scores estimate the extent of certainty that the considered treatment is better than another comparative treatment, averaged over all competing treatments. Ranking is made as follows: treatment ranked 1 is considered the best as it leads to the greatest reduction in the outcome, while the last rank is the worst as it is associated with higher risk of the outcome. To show the estimated relative effect sizes for the treatment strategies, league heat plots were constructed: the estimated treatment effect is shown at the intersection of the row, which defines the index treatment, and the column, which defines the comparison treatment.

The current analysis was conducted in a random effects and consistency model. The outputs of the meta-analysis were presented as odds ratio (OR) or hazard ratio (HR) with the corresponding credible intervals (Crl).

Pairwise meta-analysis has been conducted for long-term mortality accounting for direct comparisons only. As far as the pairwise meta-analysis is concerned, variables present the results from the direct evidence where available. Analysis of the long-term mortality was carried out using the incidence rate ratio (IRR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) as the summary statistic. IRR represents the number of events observed per total number of patient-years and takes in account the different length of follow-up in each study. Inter-study heterogeneity was examined with the Cochran’s Q (χ2) test, and we further quantified inconsistency by calculating I², interpreted using the following guide: 0–40% might not be important; 30–60% may represent moderate heterogeneity; 50–90% may represent substantial heterogeneity; and 75–100% may represent considerable heterogeneity.
