Nuclear speckle dynamics are controlled by polyphosphate inhibition of CLK proteins
Authors: Blanca Lázaro, Francisco J Tadeo, Andrea Rodríguez, Lucía Ayuso-Molina, Joan Marc Martínez-Láinez, Eva Quandt, Maribel Bernard, Filipy Borghi, Adolfo Saiardi, Jonàs Juan-Mateu, Javier Jiménez, Josep Clotet, Samuel Bru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNuclear speckles (NS) are membraneless nuclear organelles that act as critical hubs for pre-messenger RNA splicing. Defects in splicing are linked to several human diseases, including cancer, Alzheimer’s disease, and dystrophies. While CLK kinases regulate the mobilization of splicing factors from NS, the molecular mechanisms underlying NS assembly and dissolution remain unclear. Using an adaptation of the Biotinylation by Antibody Recognition technique, we identified polyphosphate (polyP) as a novel and essential regulator of NS dynamics. Polyphosphate, a highly conserved polyanion composed of a chain of phosphate molecules, is involved in several functions in mammalian cells. Here, we show that polyP interacts with the NS core component SRRM2, and its depletion disrupts NS organization releasing splicing factors into the nucleoplasm. RNA-seq analysis reveals that polyP depletion increases exon exclusion, particularly in transcripts with multiple isoforms, highlighting its role in splicing regulation. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that polyP acts as a physiological inhibitor of CLK3 kinase, preventing the phosphorylation of SR proteins and thereby maintaining NS stability. Our findings not only expand our understanding of NS biology but also provide new insights into the polyP involvement in splicing-related diseases.

Introduction

Initially shown by Ramón y Cajal [1], human cells nucleoplasm is compartmentalized in several types of membraneless nuclear bodies whose main role is concentrating the biochemical machinery required for efficiently performing a function [2]. Specific proteins and polyanionic molecules such as RNAs, and DNA can gather in the nucleoplasm to form the nucleolus, Cajal bodies, PML bodies (ProMyelocytic Leukemia), speckles, and others [3]. Particularly, nuclear speckles (NS) are membraneless organelles enriched in pre-messenger RNA (mRNA) splicing factors located mainly in the interchromatin areas of the nucleoplasm [4]. NS play a crucial role in buffering the nucleoplasmic concentration of different splicing factors, thereby influencing splicing efficiency and alternative splicing of nascent RNAs [5]. In addition, NS are associated with active transcription sites to enhance the transcription of proximal genes [6–8]. Biochemical purification methods [9] and, more recently, high-throughput screens [10, 11] have produced several catalogs of NS resident proteins. Among these, SRRM2 and SON stand out as proteins of particular interest due to their essential role in maintaining NS structure [12]. However, the depletion of either SRRM2 or SON alone cannot fully disrupt NS formation. This suggests that both proteins function as central nodes within the NS network, acting together to preserve their integrity [13].

For a protein to be included into the NS, several sequence elements have been proposed, including poly-histidine stretches [13, 14] and arginine-serine (RS) repeats [15]. More recently, other sequence elements have been added to the former list such as mixed-charged domains, particularly stretches of arginine (but not lysine) and glutamic acid residues within low-complexity regions [16]. Interestingly, both SRRM2 and SON contain RS domains embedded within low-complexity regions, which are essential for the formation of higher-order oligomers triggering NS condensation [13].

The RS domains undergo dynamic cycles of phosphorylation and dephosphorylation, modifying the negative charge on these domains that, in turn, can influence the aggregation of splicing factors [17]. Specifically, increasing the negative charge (phosphorylation) facilitates the splicing factor’s mobility and the disassembly of the NS [16]. Accordingly, the expression of dual-specificity kinases, such as CLK1 [18] and DYRK3 [19], triggers a phosphorylation-dependent disassembly of NS, while the overexpression of PP1 phosphatases increases NS cohesion and mRNA enrichment [20]. These findings underscore the pivotal role of kinases and phosphatases in regulating the physiology of NS and splicing.

Alternative splicing is an essential co-transcriptional process that allows the exons of a gene to join in different combinations resulting in distinct but related mRNA transcripts. The splicing of pre-mRNAs derived from small genes, which contain short introns and a high percentage of GC content, occurs near NS [21]. In contrast, pre-mRNAs from large genes, with long introns and a low percentage of GC content, are spliced farther away from the NS, requiring the mobilization of splicing factors from NS [21, 22]. Additionally, the presence of free splicing factors in the nucleoplasm has been associated with a faster release of the mRNA from the gene [18]. This indicates that cells can regulate gene expression by modulating the availability of splicing factors.

Polyphosphate (polyP) is a polymer of a varying number of ortho-phosphates (from a few to several hundred) linked by high-energy bonds [23] and, in consequence, a highly negatively charged molecule. Polyphosphate is an intriguing cellular element present in all cells from archaea to humans and involved in a plethora of physiological functions. Indeed, human polyP functions in blood coagulation [24], inflammation [25], stress response [26], mitochondria and energetic metabolism [27], cell cycle [28], bone formation [29], and protein folding [30]. In addition, polyP levels are altered in pathological conditions such as amyloidogenic diseases [31], other neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS and FTD [32], and proliferative maladies [33–35]. A proposed mechanism for regulating the diverse array of polyP cellular functions involves the poly-phosphorylation of lysine residues embedded in regions enriched with aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and serine—referred as PASK (poly-acidic-serine-lysine) domains [36]. Another suggested mechanism is the polyP specific interaction with lysine stretches [37]. Additionally, polyP has been shown to inhibit DYRK1 kinase activity through selective binding to histidine repeats [38]. Further evidence will help in establishing whether the polyP interaction with PASK, Lys, and His regions represents the general mechanism by which polyP modulates cellular processes in mammals. In human cells, polyP levels are regulated by NUDT3, which functions as an endopolyphosphatase [39] and PRUNE1 that exhibit exopolyphosphatase activity in vitro [34]. Together, they play a key role in controlling polyP metabolism.

Metazoan polyP is a highly dynamic molecule with a high turnover rate [40] prominently localized in the nucleus [40–42]. Recently, localization methods using the Xpress tagged PPBD peptide (Ppx1 PolyP Binding Domain) which specifically binds to polyP [39, 43], show a characteristic punctuated nuclear signal suggesting that polyP might localize within some of the membraneless nuclear bodies [39, 44].

Identifying interactors under the rationale of ‘guilty by association’ [45] remains an interesting strategy for characterizing the biological functions of poorly understood molecules. Over the last decade, Proximity Labeling has enabled the dissection of complex interactomes with remarkable spatial and temporal resolution [46]. To overcome some limitations of traditional Proximity Labeling methods, the Biotinylation by Antibody Recognition (BAR) method was developed, which replaces fusion enzymes with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated antibodies [47]. The BAR method directs biotinylation to neighboring proteins in fixed cells, enabling the detection of transient interactions of high-turnover molecules within their native spatial context, interactions that are often challenging to capture with conventional protein interaction techniques.

Here, we provide a workflow to target polyP proximal proteome (PPP) using an adaptation of the BAR technique. We have taken advantage of the PPBD peptide to target polyP in fixed cells and drive the biotinylation of its proximal proteins. We found that polyP is an unexpected element of the NS that maintains its integrity and functionality by restricting the action of CLK kinases.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture conditions

All experiments were performed using HEK293T cell line. Cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (Sigma, D5671) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Labclinics, FBS-12A), 1% glutamax (Labclinics, X0551-100), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma, P0781-100). Cells were grown in humidified air at 37°C and 5% CO2 atmosphere. Mycoplasma contamination was monitored periodically. In some experiments, the cells were treated with doxycycline (Sigma, D1822), menadione (Sigma, M5625), or CLKs inhibitor (Sigma 2650)

Transfection

FuGENE (Promega, E2311) system was used to transfect DNA plasmids. The protocol was as indicated by the purchaser. Briefly, 25 µl of the transfecting mix was added to every p24 plate well. Transfecting mix includes 250 ng of the plasmid and 1 µl of FuGENE reagent in 25 µl of culture medium without FBS and penicillin–streptomycin. The mix was incubated at RT (room temperature) for 5 min and then added to the cells. For larger wells, the volumes were scaled up.

Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher, 11 668 019) system was used to transfect siRNAs. The protocol was as indicated by the purchaser. Briefly, 100 µl of the transfecting mix was added to every p24 plate well. Transfecting mix includes 5 pmol siRNA oligomer (10 nM final concentration) in 50 µl of culture medium without FBS and penicillin–streptomycin and 50 µl of lipofectamine mix (0.8 µl of lipofectamine 2000 in 50 µl of culture medium without FBS and antibiotics). Both components, previously kept at RT for 5 min, were mixed gently and incubated for 20 min at RT. Once the lipofectamine-siRNA was added to the cells, the plates were gently agitated and incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 containing atmosphere for 24–72 h. For larger wells, the volumes were scaled up. The sequence of the siRNAs used is described in Supplementary data S1 (oligonucleotides table).

Plasmids and recombinant proteins

The plasmids are in Table 1. Escherichia coli BL21-CodonPlus(DE3)-RIPL was used for expression of recombinant proteins. Proteins were induced by adding 1 mM IPTG (isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside) to a culture in exponential phase and incubated for 4 h at 30°C. Cells were lysed in lysis buffer (25 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM imidazole, 1% triton, 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 150 mM NaCl, protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher, A322955), 0.4 mg/ml lysozyme, 4 U/ml benzonase). Proteins were purified using AKTA (Cytiva, 29 022 094), quantified by Bradford and its purity was check by PAGE and Coomassie staining (Bio-Rad, 1610787).

Plasmids

Recombinant PPBD purification

The PPBD purification was performed as essentially described [44]. Briefly, the E. coli Ppx1 polyP-binding domain (residues 306–534) was PCR-amplified and cloned into the pRSET-A vector to generate an N-terminal 6 × His-Xpress-tagged construct. Expression was performed in E. coli BL21(DE3)pLysS cells grown in LB with ampicillin and chloramphenicol, induced with 1 mM IPTG for 6 h at 25°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and stored at –80°C. For purification, pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer with protease inhibitors, incubated 15 min at 4°C, sonicated (30 s at 30 kHz), and centrifuged twice. Supernatants were adjusted to 2 mg/ml and loaded onto a HisTrap HP column, followed by desalting on a HiTrap 5 ml column using 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 9. Protein fractions were combined with glycerol, concentrated using 10 kDa Amicon filters to ∼1 ml, and stored in 50% glycerol at –20°C. Purity and size (∼25 kDa) were confirmed by PAGE and Coomassie staining.

Polyphosphate extraction, purification, and quantification

The protocol was performed as described in [48]. Briefly, HEK293T cells were lysed in AE (50 mM sodium acetate pH 7, 10 mM EDTA and 10 mM NaF) buffer and extracted with phenol–chloroform. After sequential chloroform extractions, the aqueous phase containing polyP was recovered, precipitated with ethanol and stored at −20°C O/N. Next, samples were centrifuged, washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in nuclease-free water. For quantification, purified polyP was incubated with or without recombinant scPpx1, and the released inorganic phosphate was measured using the malachite green assay.

Western blots

For western blot, cells were scrapped in lysis buffer [20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1% NP-40, and 150 mM NaCl] containing protease and phosphatases (when necessary) inhibitor cocktails (Thermo Fisher, A32955 and Thermo Fisher, A32957, respectively). The lysate was centrifugated and the protein concentration was quantified using Bradford (Bio-Rad, 5 000 006). Samples were denatured at 95°C for 5 min and 30 µg of total protein was separated in 4%–15% sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) gels (Bio-Rad, 5 671 084) and transferred to PVDF (polyvinylidene fluoride) membranes (Sigma, IPVH00010). Membranes were incubated in blocking buffer [TBS-T (250 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl and 0.01% tween 20) containing 5% nonfat milk or PhosphoBLOCKERTM (Cell Biolabs, AKR-103)] for 30 min at RT and incubated with the primary antibody (Table 2) in blocking buffer O/N at 4°C. After washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. After additional washes, the membranes were developed using Luminata Forte western HRP substrate (Sigma, WBLUF0500) following manufacturer’s instructions. Images were taken using the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad) and analyzed with Imagelab software.

Antibodies

Dot blots

Dot-blot assays were performed using different membranes depending on the target: PVDF (Immobilon, Millipore, Ref: IPFL00010) for PIP₂ detection, and Nylon Biodyne B (Thermo Fisher, 77 016) for RNA and polyP. Samples (1 μl for PIP₂; 5 μl for polyP or RNA) were spotted onto membranes, dried, and blocked in blocking reagent [TBS, 0.01% Tween-20, 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA)] for 1 h at room temperature. For PIP₂ detection, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-PIP₂ (2C11) primary antibody (1:500 dilution). For polyP detection, a His-tagged PPBD probe (10 μg/ml) was used, followed by anti-6xHis primary antibody. Secondary antibodies and signal detection followed standard western blot procedures.

To test the in vitro binding between SRRM2 domains and polyP or RNA, samples were spotted on PVDF membranes and incubated overnight at 4°C with 2 µM of mCherry-tagged peptides (PASKSRRM2-mCherry, RS1SRRM2-mCherry, or mCherry). After washing, interactions were detected by fluorescence imaging (Bio-Rad ChemiDoc; excitation 587 nm, emission 610 nm).

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

For immunofluorescence, cells were grown in plate wells containing a poly-lysine treated coverslip (Sigma, P4707). In experiments where recombinant Ppx1 was used, Ppx1 treatment was performed after cell permeabilization to degrade polyP before the incubation with the PPBD probe and the anti-Xpress antibody. Cells were fixed using phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing 10% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at RT, blocked and permeabilized with blocking solution (PBS; 0.5% Tween 20 and 2.5% goat serum) for 1 h at RT. Cells were incubated O/N at 4°C with the specific antibodies (Table 2) in blocking solution. For polyP detection, cells were incubated O/N at 4°C with the Xpress-tagged PPBD probe and the anti-Xpress antibody as described in [44]. After washing three times with PBS, secondary antibodies with different Alexa fluorophores (Invitrogen) were added (1:1000 in blocking solution) for 1 h at RT. Samples were washed three times with PBS and Hoechst 33 342 (Sigma, 14 530) for 5 min at RT (1 µg/ml) was used for nucleus staining. Samples were mounted using Fluoromount-G (Southern Biotech, 0100-01). Imaging was performed using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope. Quantification was carried out in Fiji (ImageJ). Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined by applying a threshold to the Hoechst channel following Gaussian B filtering. To refine the mask, the “Fill Holes” and “Watershed” plugins were applied. The resulting ROI mask—covering the entire nucleus—was overlaid onto the channel of interest to extract the raw integrated density. Notably, comparable results were obtained when using ROIs restricted to individual NS, but for simplicity, whole-nucleus ROIs were used. Only in the analysis of NS circularity were ROIs restricted to individual NSs. The average raw integrated density per field was then calculated. Colocalization was assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient calculated with the “Coloc2” plugin in Fiji.

Biotinylation by antibody recognition

As described in [47]. The kit was Biotin XX Tyramide SuperBoost™ biotin labelling kit (Thermo Fisher, B40911) and following manufacturer’s instructions.

Briefly, 2 × 106 cells were fixed in PBS with 10% PFA, treated with 3% H2O2 for 10 min at RT (to abrogate endogenous peroxidase activity) and blocked with PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.5% Tween-20 for 1 h at RT. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4°C in blocking buffer with the following primary antibodies: Alexa 647-conjugated SC35 (to label the speckle proteome), a mix of Xpress-tagged PPBD (2 μg/ml) and anti-Xpress antibody (4 μg/ml) to detect polyP, and anti-FBL for the fibrillarin proteome. After washing, cells were incubated with the HRP-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody provided in the kit.

The biotinylation reaction was carried out for 3 min (Supplementary Fig. S2A) using the provided reaction buffer containing H₂O₂ and biotin-tyramide, and was stopped with the kit’s stop buffer Cells were then scraped into 100 μl of PBS-T (PBS with 0.1% Tween-20) and lysed by incubation at 99°C with shaking for 1 h in the presence of 30 μl 10% SDS and 20 μl 10% sodium deoxycholate.

Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at maximum speed, brought to 1 ml with PBS-T, and incubated for 2 h at RT with 50 μl of streptavidin-conjugated magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher, 88 816) under gentle agitation. Beads were sequentially washed with PBS-T, PBS-T containing 1 M NaCl, and PBS. After drying, proteins bound to the beads were analyzed by mass spectrometry. All steps were monitored by western blot.

Protein identification by LC-MS/MS

The proteomic analysis was performed in the Proteomics Unit of Complutense University of Madrid. Briefly, the dried beads containing the biotinylated proteins were digesting using IST kit (Preomics) and following the manufacturer instructions. Peptides (1 µg) were analysed by liquid nano-chromatography (Easy-nLC 1000, Thermo Scientific, Bremen, Germany) coupled to a high-resolution mass spectrometer Q-Exactive HF (Thermo Scientific). Peptides were concentrated by reverse phase chromatography using an Acclaim PepMap 100 precolum (Thermo Scientific) and separated in a reverse phase C18 Picofrit analytical column (Thermo Scientific) Peptides were detected in the range of 2–350 KDa. For the protein identification the peptides data were analysed using Proteome Discoverer 2.5 (Thermo Scientific) software using MASCOT v.28 as the search engine. The database was Uniprot restricted to Homo sapiens and a contaminants database.

For a hit to be considered a polyP potential interactor two thresholds were applied. First, a Mascot Score greater than 85. Second, the Mascot score must be (at least) 9 times higher in the experimental sample than in the control.

Gene ontology analysis of the PPP

To determine the subcellular localization of proteins within the PPP, we used the g:Profiler database (version e111_eg58_p18_f463989d). General localization was assessed by comparing enrichment scores for the nucleoplasm (GO: 0 005 654) and cytoplasm (GO: 0 005 829). To refine nuclear localization, we evaluated enrichment for specific membraneless nuclear compartments: NSs (GO: 0 016 607), nucleolus (GO: 0 005 730), nuclear matrix (GO: 0 016 363), chromatin (GO: 0 000 785), Cajal bodies (GO: 0 015 030), nuclear envelope (GO: 0 005 829), nuclear membrane (GO: 0 031 965), PML bodies (GO: 0 016 605), and paraspeckles (GO: 0 042 382).

To investigate the biological functions of PPP proteins, we used the ShinyGO 0.80 web tool, querying the KEGG database. The pathways with the most significant enrichment [lowest false discovery rate (FDR)] included: spliceosome (hsa03040), DNA replication (hsa03030), ribosome biogenesis (hsa03008), mRNA surveillance (hsa03015), HIV-1 life cycle (hsa03250), nucleocytoplasmic transport (hsa03013), nucleotide excision repair (hsa03420), cell cycle (hsa04110), mismatch repair (hsa03430), RNA polymerase (hsa03020), homologous recombination (hsa03440), base excision repair (hsa03410), Fanconi anemia pathway (hsa03460), and cytosolic DNA-sensing (hsa04623).

To identify enriched protein domains within the PPP, we used the MEME Suite tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme), version 5.5.5. PPP protein sequences were compared to domains in the InterPro database. Enriched domain families were selected based on their representation and associated FDR values.

Sample preparation for RNA seq analysis

Cells were transfected with either pWPI empty vector or scramble constructs as controls, or with pWPI-NUDT3, pWPI-PRUNE1, pWPI-CLK3, or siSRRM2. After 48 h, total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA sequencing was performed by Macrogen (https://dna.macrogen.com/) using the NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 100 million paired-end reads (150 nt) per sample. All conditions were analyzed in four biological replicates, except for siSRRM2, which was analyzed in two replicates.

Alternative splicing analyses

Alternative splicing was quantified using VAST-Tools (v2.5.1) [49]. Changes in percent-spliced-in values (ΔPSI) were quantified for all types of alternative events, comprising single and complex exon skipping events (S, C1, C2, C3, ANN), microexons (MIC), alternative 5′ and 3′ splice sites (Alt5 and Alt3, respectively) and retained introns (IR-S, IR-C). Briefly, FASTQ reads were aligned with VAST-Tools align with the VASTDB human library (hg38, vastdb.hs2.23.06.20). Next, vast-tools align outputs for each RNA-seq sample were combined into a single table containing all splicing events using vast-tools combine with default parameters. Differentially spliced events following NUDT3 or PRUNE1 overexpression, or SRRM2 silencing were identified using vast-tools compare, with |ΔPSI| > 10 between the means of the two compared groups (–min_ΔPSI 10) and a nonoverlapping PSI distribution between sample groups of at least 5 (–min_range 5). Structural analysis of alternative exons was performed using the function cmpr_exons of the bioinformatic tool MATT [50]. To identify potential functional associations between the overexpression of NUDT3 and PRUNE1 or SRRM2 knock-down, ΔPSIs values of regulated exons for each gene were compared against values obtained following analysis of siRNA-mediated individual knock-down of 305 splicing-related protein-coding genes in HeLa cells [51]. For each factor, correlation coefficients were obtained using the Pearson coefficient method and the corrplot R library (v0.92).

RNA-seq validation

Among all events with a ± 10 ΔPSI in NUDT3 or PRUNE1 overexpression, eight events that were common to both conditions were selected randomly for validation by PCR. Reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) was performed to generate complementary DNA (cDNA) from the four replicates of the control and the four replicates of each condition. Using this cDNA, four independent PCR reactions were carried out, one for each replicate, with primers obtained from VastDB that are specific for each splicing event (Supplementary data S1). PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel. Finally, the band intensities corresponding to splice-in and splice-out forms were quantified, and the PSI (percent spliced-in) was calculated for each condition.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

Peptides were expressed as mCherry fusion proteins (with mCherry alone used as a negative control) and purified by affinity chromatography using a 6xHis tag on Ni-NTA columns. Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) assays were performed by incubating various amounts of commercial polyP (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 µg) with 10 µg of each peptide-mCherry fusion in water for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were then separated on a 1% agarose gel in TBE (100 nM Tris, 100 mM boric acid and 2 mM EDTA) buffer for 1 h at 100 V. Mobility shifts were visualized using mCherry fluorescence on a ultraviolet transilluminator (ChemiDoc Imaging System, Bio-Rad).

The peptides tested included the PASK region of SRRM2 (PASKSRRM2, residues 186–246); a mutated version of this region (PASKmutSRRM2), in which the 7 consecutive lysine residues were replaced with alanine residues; the RS1 domain of SRRM2 (RS1SRRM2), comprising three repeats of a serine/arginine-rich motif (GRSRSRTPA) and the PASK region of SON (PASKSON, residues 106–141). Classification as a PASK region requires a sequence to contain ≥30% lysine, ≥10% serine, and ≥ 10% acidic residues, as defined by the rules implemented in: https://github.com/jclotet/PASK_predictions.

Splicing luciferase assay

The splicing reporter system was performed as described in [52], with some modifications. Briefly, cells were seeded in six-well plates and transfected 24 h later with empty pWPI, pWPI-NUDT3, pWPI-Ppx1 plasmids, or transfected with siRNA (Scramble or siSRRM2). Cells were then reseeded into 96-well plates and, after 24 h, transfected with the luciferase reporter plasmid CMV-LUC2CP-intron or control CMV-LUC2CP (Table 1). For Ppk1, the workflow was slightly modified: cells were first transfected with the luciferase reporters and then transfected with pWPI-Ppk1 after 24 h. D-luciferin (Sigma–Aldrich, 2591-17-5) was added 48 h after the final transfection for NUDT3, Ppx1, and siRNA conditions, and 24 h after the final transfection for Ppk1. The final concentration of D-luciferin was 1 mg/ml. Luciferase activity was measured at 37°C using a Synergy HT plate reader, and the peak luminescence value (typically 1–2 min after substrate addition) was used for analysis. The splicing inhibitor isoginkgetin (Sigma–Aldrich, 416 154) was added at a concentration of 35 µM.

In vitro phosphorylation assay

GST-CLK3 (0.12 µM), either purified from E. coli or from insect cells (SinoBiological, C60-10G-10) or GST-CLK1 (0.12 µM) purified from insects (Signalchem Biotech (Labclinics), C57-11G-10) was added with either GST-G(RS)4, mCherry-RS1SRRM2 or HIS-SRSF1 (1.6 µM each) to kinase buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, and 1 mM ATP) to a final volume of 20 µl. polyP P14 or P130 was added. The reaction mix was incubated at 30°C for 45 min. Samples were subjected to SDS–PAGE and probed for SR-phosphorylation. In the CDK16 kinase assay, GST-CDK16 (0.2 μM) and GST-CCNY (0.4 μM) were mixed in a kinase buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT) and 1 mM ATP-γS (Axxora, Farmingdale, NY, USA) were mixed ant incubated at 30°C for 45 min. Reactions were quenched by adding 50 mM EDTA and incubated with 0.5 mg/ml para-nitro-benzo-mesylate (PNBM), kindly provided by K. M. Shokat, at 25°C for 45 min. PNBM samples were subjected to SDS–PAGE and probed for thio-phosphorylation with specific antibodies.

SRRM2 and PPBD immunoprecipitation

Protein extract (2 µg) from HEK293T cells lysates incubated previously with 1 µg of recombinant yeast Ppx1 were incubated with 1 µg of the 6xHis-Xpress-PPBD probe and 2 µg of anti-Xpress antibody. For a positive control of SRRM2 detection, the same amount of extract was incubated with 2 µg of anti-SRRM2 antibody. Incubations were carried out overnight at 4°C under gentle agitation. Afterwards, 20 µl of protein A/G–conjugated magnetic beads, previously equilibrated in lysis buffer, were added and incubated for 1 h at 4°C with gentle agitation. The beads were washed repeatedly with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted using 30 µl of 1 × sample buffer by boiling the samples at 95°C for 5 min. Finally, an anti-SRRM2 western blot was performed to assess SRRM2 binding to PPBD, and an anti-histidine western blot was used to verify PPBD binding to the beads.

Statistical analysis

In all figures in the Results section, data are from a minimum of three independent repetitions of each experiment (the exact number of repetitions is indicated in the figure legends). In the Supplementary Figures section, some figures show representative experiments (the exact number of repetitions is also provided in the corresponding legends). For microscopy experiments, a minimum of five fields containing 30–100 cells each were analyzed. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and statistical significance was determined using the Mann–Whitney test. P-values: ***P <.001; **P <.01; *P <.05.

Results

Proximal biotinylation reveals the NS as a major site of polyP localization

To investigate the function of polyP in human cells, we aimed to characterize its molecular environment. To this end, we adapted the BAR technique [47], which enables the identification of proximity interactomes in fixed cells. Given that formaldehyde fixation most likely preserves polyP structure while halting cellular activity, this approach is particularly suited to detect and label proteins in the vicinity of polyP for subsequent mass spectrometry analysis (Fig 1A).

As a binding probe, we employed the Binding Domain (PPBD) derived from the E. coli exopolyphosphatase Ppx1, previously validated as a highly specific nuclear polyP sensor [32, 39, 43, 53]. To confirm its applicability for BAR, we first validated the specificity of recombinant PPBD in our system. In vitro and in vivo analyses showed that PPBD binds selectively to polyP, with no detectable interaction with major nuclear polyanions such as DNA, RNA (Supplementary Fig. S1A), or PIP₂ (Supplementary Fig. S1B), nor with the speckle protein SRRM2 (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Furthermore, treatment of fixed cells with recombinant Ppx1—an enzyme that degrades polyP—led to a marked loss of the PPBD signal (Supplementary Fig. S1D), supporting its specificity. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, PPBD accurately reported polyP levels across various genetic backgrounds (Supplementary Fig. S1E), and no signal was observed in strains lacking polyP. Similarly, in human cells, PPBD-associated fluorescence correlated with either overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S1F and G) or depletion (Supplementary Fig. S1H) of polyP-modulating enzymes. Together, these results validate PPBD as a robust and specific tool for BAR-based proximity labeling of polyP-associated proteomes.

After setting up the biotin labeling for reducing noise and false positives (Supplementary Fig S2A), the biotinylated proteins nicely colocalize with polyP (Fig. 1B). In addition, biotin detection shows that only biotinylated proteins are obtained when the polyP-binding PPBD is present, indicating the high specificity of the assay (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Interestingly, both the polyP and biotinylated proteins are found in the nucleus areas poorly stained with Hoechst (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S2C for a quantification) proposed as regions of intense transcription [54]. Mass spectrometry identified a list of 285 proteins (Supplementary data S2). Interestingly, 10 of the top 22 polyP-binding proteins are known NS residents (highlighted in red in Fig. 1D), including the two scaffold proteins SON and SRRM2, essential for NS integrity [12]. GO analysis of the PPP shows a clear nucleoplasm localization (Fig. 1E) and enrichment in the NS (Fig. 1F). Looking at the protein functions, splicing is the most prominent (Fig. 1G). This is confirmed by a domain-containing analysis showing the presence of RNA recognition domains, helicase domains, and well-recognized polyanion interacting domains (Fig. 1H). Finally, we performed a second BAR analysis using an antibody against SRRM2 (one of the two scaffold proteins of the NS), which yielded a list of proteins consistent with previous NS proteomic studies [9–11] (Supplementary Fig. S2D). Notably, 76% of the SRRM2-proximal proteins overlapped with those identified in the PPP dataset (Fig. 1I, E and F). As a control, a third BAR analysis using the nucleolar protein fibrillarin (Supplementary Fig. S2G and H) showed virtually no overlap with the PPP proteins (Fig. 1J). These results further support that the PPP is largely composed of NS proteins, reinforcing the notion that polyP is a novel component of the NS. Confirming the analysis presented above, the polyP immunolocalization mostly colocalizes with SRRM2 (Fig. 1K and Supplementary Fig. S2I), with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.8 much higher than the correlation coefficient for other nuclear organelles markers (Fig. 1L).

Polyphosphate interacts with the core NS element SRRM2

To identify which NS components interact with polyP, we focused on PASK domains—regions previously linked to polyphosphorylation events [36]. Using a custom Python script, we searched for PASK domains within our PPP. Remarkably, only three proteins exhibited a clear PASK domain, with SRRM2 and SON showing the highest score value (Fig. 2A). Notably, both proteins, within their PASK region, contain distinct lysine-rich stretches described as important regions for polyP interaction [38]. In particular, the PASKSRRM2 region coincides with a highly disordered and polar sequence (Supplementary Fig. S3A), making it an excellent candidate for interaction with a polyanionic molecule such as polyP. Consistent with our prediction, EMSA analysis demonstrates that polyP interacts in vitro with the PASKSRRM2 region (Fig. 2B). Specifically, we detected a downshift in peptide mobility, a pattern previously reported in EMSA studies involving polyP–protein interactions [55]. The interaction is independent of the polyP size except for the shortest polyP 3-mers, which show negligible binding (Fig. 2C). The presence of equivalent amounts of monomeric phosphate does not produce any shift (Fig. 2C) indicating specificity for polyP. In contrast, the electrophoretic mobility of RS-rich (Arg-Ser) regions—despite being disordered and polar—was only marginally affected and did not display polyP dose dependency, indicating a much weaker interaction (Fig. 2B). Mutating the 7-lysine stretch within PASKSRMM2 to alanine residues (PASKmutSRRM2) significantly impaired the mobility shift, suggesting a prominent role for the Lysine cluster [37] (Fig 2B). Polyphosphate also binds PASKSON region although with lower affinity (Fig. 2B) and finally, while RNA binds both the PASK and RS1 regions, as previously described [56, 57]), polyP exhibits specific and selective binding to the PASK region (Supplementary Fig. S3B).

To assess the in vivo relationship between polyP and SRRM2 suggested by the in vitro experiments, we quantified polyP and SRRM2 levels at the single-cell level. This analysis revealed a positive correlation between the abundance of SRRM2 and polyP, consistent with the idea that the presence of polyP in NSs depends on the polyP-binding protein SRRM2 (Fig. 2D). Indeed, silencing SRRM2 dramatically reduces the amount of polyP in the NS without affecting other NS components (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. S3C) but not the total cellular polyP levels (Supplementary Fig. S3F). Interestingly, SON depletion triggered an increase in SRRM2 levels, which was accompanied by a corresponding rise in polyP content within NSs (Fig. 2F and G). A similar increase in polyP was also observed upon depletion of other NS components such as SRRM1 (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Finally, NS and polyP behave similarly and change their shape coordinately to a round and sharply punctuated pattern when cells are treated with actinomycin, a transcription inhibitor that induces such a variation in the shape of the NS (Fig. 2H, Supplementary Fig. S3E and [58]). All the above results point SRRM2 as an important recruiting factor for polyP in the NS.

Considering the prominent role of NS in mRNA splicing processes, it is timely to investigate the possible role of polyP in splicing.

Polyphosphate regulates splicing

NS have been implicated in various functions, most prominently with RNA splicing [5], raising the possibility that polyP may regulate this process. Consistently, “RNA splicing” appears as the top gene ontology (GO) category in our PPP analysis (Fig. 1G). To further investigate this link, we employed a luciferase reconstitution assay to assess intron removal [52] (Fig. 3A). As expected, splicing efficiency was significantly impaired upon treatment with the pre-mRNA splicing inhibitor isoginkgetin [59], validating the assay (Fig. 3B). Notably, polyP degradation—achieved by overexpressing either Ppx1 or NUDT3—resulted in a splicing defect comparable to that observed upon SRRM2 depletion (Fig. 3B). To assess whether increased polyP levels could enhance splicing, we transiently overexpressed the bacterial polyP kinase Ppk1. Although Ppk1 expression is highly toxic and limited to 24 h before inducing cell death [60], this short window was sufficient to observe a clear increase in splicing efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S4A), supporting a positive role for polyP in regulating this process.

Prompted by these findings, we performed RNA-seq analysis (Supplementary data S3) to evaluate alternative splicing changes in cells overexpressing two human polyphosphatases: NUDT3, an endopolyphosphatase [39] and PRUNE1, a human exopolyphosphatase [34]. Both genetic models exhibited a similar distribution of alternative splicing events, with exon skipping emerging as the most predominant type (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S4B). Validation by RT-PCR of randomly selected targets confirmed these RNA-seq results (Fig. 3D and Supplementary data S4). A strong correlation (r = 0.82) was observed between PSI values obtained by RNA-seq and RT-PCR for each event across the three conditions (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Notably, approximately 12% of the total spliced genes were commonly regulated in both NUDT3- and PRUNE1-expressing cells (Supplementary Fig. S4D), supporting a shared mechanism of action. GO analysis of this overlapping gene set revealed a significant enrichment in morphogenesis-related functions (Fig. 3E), suggesting that polyP-regulated splicing may impact developmental pathways.

At the molecular level, overexpression of both: NUDT3 and PRUNE1 primarily promotes exon exclusion (Fig. 3F). In fact, a significant correlation (r = 0.46) was observed between the ΔPSI values of all exon-skipped events in the two models (Supplementary Fig. S4E), as well as for intron retention events (r = 0.6) (Supplementary Fig. S4F); although from this point onward we focus exclusively on exon skipping. About 7% of the exon-skipping events are shared between the NUDT3 and PRUNE1 datasets, defining a core set of polyP-regulated splicing changes (Supplementary Fig. S4G). Furthermore, when focusing on events with a ΔPSI of ±10 in the NUDT3 condition and comparing the same events in PRUNE1 overexpression, the correlation strengthened (Fig. 3G). Interestingly, the exon exclusion bias of polyP depleted cells, is similar to the splicing alterations in SRRM2 knock-down RNA-seq data (Supplementary Fig. S4H and I; see also [57]. Altogether, these findings reinforce the notion that polyP is critical for proper transcript processing within NSs.

Comparative sequence analysis of cassette exons regulated by NUDT3 and PRUNE1 overexpression identified a set of conserved sequence and structural features associated with NUDT3/PRUNE1-dependent splicing. This analysis revealed that the excluded exons tend to be short (Fig. 3H), GC-rich (Fig. 3I and Supplementary Fig. S4J), present in transcripts containing few exons (Supplementary Fig. S4K and L), and associated with genes expressing multiple isoforms (Fig. 3J and Supplementary Fig. S4M). Their flanking introns (both upstream and downstream) are also short (Supplementary Fig. S4N), GC-rich (Supplementary Fig. S4O and P) and exhibit reduced 3′ SF1 score, as determined by a model trained with human data in the last 150 nucleotides of 3′ intron position. (Fig. 3K and Supplementary Fig. S4Q), suggesting that polyP is involved in the regulation of splicing at weak splice sites. Our data indicates that polyP facilitates the processing of transcripts with well-defined molecular features (Fig. 3L).

Splicing involves the coordinated activity of over 300 proteins, including core spliceosomal components, regulatory factors, and splicing kinases [51]. Rogalska et al. performed a large-scale RNA-seq study coupling knock-down of more than 250 splicing regulators, enabling the identification of specific regulatory events and uncovering functional relationships in an unbiased manner [51]. To identify potential co-regulators of polyP-sensitive splicing, we performed Pearson correlation analysis comparing our ΔPSI profiles from NUDT3 and PRUNE1 overexpression and SRRM2 knock-down to the ΔPSI signatures in the Rogalska dataset. As a result, we identified SRRM2 as showing the highest positive Pearson correlation with our siSRRM2 dataset (Fig. 3M), further validating the approach. Notably, when comparing the NUDT3 and PRUNE1 datasets to the same collection, several members of the CLK family also appeared among the most anti-correlated (Fig. 3M), indicating a potential genetic interaction between polyP and CLKs.

Polyphosphate restricts CLKs activity

CLK kinases regulate the intranuclear distribution of SR proteins influencing pre-mRNA splicing [61]. Accordingly, the overexpression of CLK1 triggers the disassembly of speckles [20]. Here, we focused on CLK3, one of the less-characterized members of the family [62]. We found that recombinant CLK3 efficiently phosphorylates a synthetic peptide containing four RS repeats [(RS)₄] (Supplementary Fig. S5A). CLK3 also retains the temperature-sensitive activity described for CLK1, including the conserved regulatory residues (Supplementary Fig. S5A and B, [63]), suggesting that both kinases may be subject to similar regulatory mechanisms.

Importantly, CLK3 activity is efficiently inhibited by polyP in a dose dependent manner (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S5C). Similar inhibition was observed when CLK3 was produced in insect cells (Supplementary Fig. S5D), suggesting that no specific posttranslational modification is required for its polyP sensitivity. Furthermore, the inhibitory effect was consistent across different polyP chain lengths (Supplementary Fig. S5E), indicating that polyP size is not a critical factor. Polyphosphate also inhibits CLK3 activity when tested on more physiological substrates, such as the RS1 region of SRRM2 (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S5F). To our knowledge, this represents the first demonstration that a RS-enriched region of SRRM2 serves as an in vitro substrate for a CLK kinase, and that this phosphorylation is regulated by polyP. Finally, using SRSF1, a well-known substrate of CLK kinases, we observed that polyP inhibits the activity of both CLK3 (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S5G) and CLK1 (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. S5H), suggesting that polyP-mediated inhibition may be a conserved regulatory mechanism across the entire CLK family. Moreover, increasing ATP concentrations in the reaction do not rescue CLK3 activity from polyP inhibition, suggesting a noncompetitive mode of inhibition (Fig. 4E).

To rule out the possibility that the observed inhibitory effect of polyP on CLK kinases is due to nonspecific artifacts, we conducted several control experiments. First, increasing concentrations of polyP did not cause visible precipitation of CLK3 (Supplementary Fig. S5I), indicating that loss of activity is not due to kinase aggregation. Second, neither equivalent amounts of monomeric orthophosphate nor other negatively charged polyanions, such as heparin, were able to inhibit CLK3 (Fig. 4F) or CLK1 (Supplementary Fig. S5J), highlighting the specificity of the interaction. Third, polyP failed to inhibit CDK16 (Fig. 4G), a kinase structurally related to CLK3 and also a member of the CMGC family [64, 65] further supporting the selective effect of polyP on CLKs. Finally, the purity of all kinases and substrates used in the assays was verified by SDS–PAGE (Supplementary Fig. S5K), ruling out potential contamination as a source of activity loss. Together, these controls demonstrate that polyP inhibits CLK kinases through a specific and previously undescribed mechanism.

Moving forward to the in vivo regulation, the reduction of polyP levels results in notable in vivo phosphorylation of various SRSF proteins (Fig. 4H and Supplementary Fig. S5L), well-known targets of CLK kinases [66]. All the results presented above point to polyP as a physiological inhibitor of CLK kinases.

NS integrity is controlled by polyP/CLK axis

CLK phosphorylation on SR proteins has been shown to produce the NS disassembly [61]. Based on our findings, we predict that nuclear polyP levels modulate NS integrity through the activation of CLK kinases. Indeed, reducing cellular polyP levels by overexpressing polyphosphatases (Supplementary Fig. S6D and E) significantly decreases the signal of three independent NS markers: SRRM2 (Fig. 5A), SON (Supplementary Fig. S6A), and RBM25 (Supplementary Fig. S6B) without altering overall SRRM2 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S6F). Importantly, this effect is abolished when an enzymatically inactive version of Ppx1 (D127N) is overexpressed (Supplementary Fig. S6C). Controlled overexpression of NUDT3, which depletes polyP (Supplementary Fig. S6G), leads to a comparable, dose-dependent reduction in SRRM2 and SON localization (Fig. 5B). Importantly, this effect is dependent on CLKs kinase activities, as it is abolished in the presence of T3, a specific CLKs inhibitor [67] (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S6H). In a genetically opposed approach, increasing polyP levels—either by silencing NUDT3 (Supplementary Fig. S7C) or overexpressing the bacterial Ppk1 (Supplementary Fig. S7E–G)—leads to an enhancement of NS (Supplementary Fig. S7A, B, and D). These effects are again dependent on CLK kinase activity, as elevated nuclear polyP renders cells resistant to NS disassembly induced by CLK1 or CLK3 overexpression (Fig. 5D).

Collectively, these experiments show that polyP regulates CLK activity, thereby orchestrating the mobilization of splicing factors from NS. Supporting this model, cells overexpressing CLK3 display a distribution of splicing event types (Fig. 5E) similar to that observed in polyP-depleted cells (Fig. 3C), including a comparable predominance of exon skipping (Fig. 5F versus Fig. 3F). Moreover, changes in the splicing patterns of events overlapping with those in NUDT3-overexpressing cells show a significant overall correlation (r = 0.69) (Fig. 5G). All these results support the existence of a regulatory polyP/CLK axis that controls splicing outcomes.

Model and physiological relevance

We propose a model in which polyP associated with SRRM2 works as a sort of shield inhibiting the CLKs, thus preventing the CLK-mediated phosphorylation of SR domains and, in turn, keeping the NS assembled and visible (Fig. 6A). It is known that oxidative stress reduces polyP levels [39], making it a physiological condition to test the model. Indeed, the addition of menadione, besides polyP depletion perturbs NS integrity (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S8A). Interestingly, polyP depletion precedes SRRM2 delocalization (Fig. 6B), suggesting a stepwise regulatory mechanism. Supporting the model, NS disorganization under oxidative stress is entirely dependent on NUDT3 and subsequent CLK activity, as demonstrated by two key observations. First, NUDT3 KO cells, which are unable to degrade polyP, do not exhibit NS mobilization under stress (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S8B). Second, even when polyP is degraded, NS disorganization does not occur if CLK activity is inhibited (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. S8C).

In sum, we propose that under specific stress conditions—such as oxidative stress—polyP degradation is required to activate CLK kinases. These kinases phosphorylate NS elements, modulating their localization and facilitating proper splicing of stress-responsive mRNAs. Overall, this model identifies polyP regulation as a novel layer of stress adaptation and survival.

Discussion

The PPP: a novel approach to understanding human polyP functions

The polyP role in human cells has been difficult to define, largely due to the challenges in characterizing its metabolic machinery and in isolating it from cells and tissues. To clarify its function, we aimed to identify the proteins interacting with polyP. Three critical issues must be considered when pursuing this strategy: First, polyP is highly negatively charged, which causes it to interact nonspecifically with basic proteins, making co-immunoprecipitation-based approaches problematic [68]. Second, polyP has a high turnover rate [40] making difficult the detection in approaches requiring stable interactions. Third, polyP lacks a defined spatial structure, and despite efforts, no sufficiently specific antibodies have been produced [69]. Proximity labelling strategies, however, can overcome these challenges by rapidly tagging transient interactions and enabling analysis in intact and metabolically arrested cells. Using the high specificity of the PPBD peptide in binding to polyP [39, 43], we adapted the BAR technique to generate a reliable PPP. Several results in this study (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S2) validate the approach, demonstrating for the first time that BAR can be applied to detect the proximal proteome of metabolites if an appropriate sensor for the specific metabolite is available.

The first major finding from the PPP is that polyP localizes in several nuclear membraneless organelles. Here, we focused on the polyP role in regulating splicing within NS; however, our findings open new avenues for investigating the polyP functions in other locations, particularly in the nucleolus, where polyP has been previously reported [60, 70]. Additionally, we have found polyP in chromatin, Cajal bodies, and the nuclear membrane (Fig. 1F), suggesting that polyP may play roles yet to be explored in these structures. The widespread presence across various nuclear condensates points to polyP as a potential common structural or regulatory factor in these highly dynamic cellular compartments.

NS from a membraneless condensate to a complex structure

Initially, nuclear condensates were considered disordered accumulations of molecules gathered by phase separation [3]. However, focusing on NS, alternative models have emerged, including a complex conformation of different elements mediated by specific binding interactions, highlighting the role of electrostatic forces supported by polyanions such as RNA (including long noncoding RNAs) and DNA [3, 71]. The nucleation of NS via mechanisms like the spatial clustering of active genes reinforces the notion of the high complexity of these structures [72]. Moreover, modern high-resolution microscopy reveals intricate layers within NS [73], as well as coordinated protein subdomains [13]. Undoubtedly, future research will uncover additional components in the NS network, further expanding our understanding of this organelle.

In this context, we propose polyP as a novel component of NS. Functionally, polyP stands out as the only known factor whose depletion leads to structural remodeling. While we propose that polyP interacts in vitro with SRRM2 through the 7xLys cluster within its PASK domain, other proteins may also contribute to polyP recruitment to NS. Indeed, two more proteins in our PPP (SON and THOC2) contain robust PASK domains that could mediate polyP recruitment. Additionally, DYRK1A, a protein found in NS [38], contains a poly-His stretch that might also serve as an anchoring site for polyP in NS. Whether polyP is synthesized near NS or transported to NS remains an open question; addressing it will be crucial for a more complete understanding of NS dynamics.

Polyphosphate as a new splicing regulator

The nucleus can be divided into two transcriptional hot zones: one located within NS, typically found in the nuclear interior, and another at the nuclear periphery [74, 75]. This spatial organization is accompanied by distinct gene architectures and splicing programs. Genes residing in the NS vicinity tend to have short exons, high GC content, and short flanking introns with weak splice sites—features that predispose them to exon skipping when splicing is compromised [74, 76]. Interestingly, this same class of structurally constrained genes is also affected by SRRM2 depletion, which induces a comparable splicing shift toward exon skipping [57]—a pattern that closely mirrors what we observe upon polyP depletion. Our results suggest that polyP is not merely involved in the mobilization of splicing factors but rather facilitates efficient splicing within NS. NSs are enriched in splicing regulators such as SRRM2 and SON, and serve as functional hubs for pre-mRNA processing, particularly for transcripts encoded by structurally constrained genes [5, 76]. In this context, the loss of polyP may impair the splicing competence of NS, specifically affecting a subset of short, GC-rich transcripts that depend on intranuclear speckle environments for accurate exon recognition. These findings support a revised model in which polyP enables exon definition in a chromatin context where the inherent gene structure and speckle localization predispose transcripts to exon skipping in its absence.

A model for polyP-mediated splicing control

SR proteins contain RS repeat domains essential for maintaining the NS structure. These domains are targets for phosphorylation contributing to NS disassembly [16]. CLKs, along with SRPKs and DYRKs, are the kinases responsible for regulating the phosphorylation of SR proteins [77]. Interestingly, our study identified CLK3, the least known member of the Ser/Thr kinase CLK family [62], as a target for polyP inhibition.

It is known that polyP inhibits DYRK1A by interacting with its poly-His stretch [38]. Since CLK3 lacks clear poly-His or poly-Lys stretches, the exact mechanism by which polyP inhibits CLK3 kinase activity remains unclear and, therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility that polyP inhibitory effect could be extended to other CLK family members. Given that CLKs are more promiscuous in substrate selection compared to SRPKs [78], and considering that CLKs localization is compatible with NS (kinome_atlas: http://cellimagelibrary.org/pages/kinome_atlas), a mechanism inhibiting the CLKs gains relevance in the NS control. Therefore, we propose a model in which polyP modulates splicing by regulating CLK kinase activity (Fig. 6A). When polyP is present above a critical level, CLK kinase activity is restrained, preventing the phosphorylation and reorganization of SR proteins. However, when splicing activity needs to be adjusted a reduction in polyP levels makes SR proteins more vulnerable to CLK action, leading to their mobilization from the NS and, eventually, to NS partial disassembly.

One well-documented instance of polyP level regulation occurs during oxidative stress, where nuclear polyP is degraded by the activation of the endopolyphosphatase NUDT3 [39]. According to our model, polyP depletion leads to the displacement of SRRM2 enabling cells to adapt and respond. A similar polyP-regulated mechanism could occur during the cell cycle. It is known that NS dissolve in early mitosis through the activity of CLK1 and DIRK3, and reform later in the cell cycle. [19, 79, 80]. In both cases, we speculate that the degradation and resynthesis of polyP could be the key factor triggering the assembly/disassembly cycles of NS. The fact that polyP is cyclically regulated in lower eukaryotes [28] provides additional support for this speculation.

In summary, we propose a model in which the highly dynamic nature of NS is regulated by polyP through its inhibition of CLK activity. Our model suggests that by modulating splicing factors localization, polyP may exert control over the wide array of cellular functions in which it is involved. This new role of polyP opens new avenues for understanding how cells fine-tune gene expression in response to various physiological conditions, positioning polyP as a critical player in nuclear organization and RNA processing.

Limitations of the study

It is important to consider that BAR labelling has a limited spatial resolution, typically affecting proteins within 250 nm of the target epitope [81]. Therefore, not all identified proteins may be direct polyP interactors, and some proximal partners could be missing. Additionally, this study was conducted in HEK293T cells, a human cell line. Given that polyP levels and metabolism vary significantly between cell types [60], the role and localization of polyP may differ across tissues, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings.
