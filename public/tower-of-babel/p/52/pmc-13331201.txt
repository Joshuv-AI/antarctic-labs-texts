Occupational burns among migrant workers in South Korea: a qualitative study of challenges and coping strategies
Authors: Ju-Yeun Lee, Jinjoo Chung, Seung-Sup Kim, Sung-il Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveBurns are a global public health concern, and migrant workers face a disproportionately high risk for occupational burns. We traced their trajectory from injury occurrence through treatment to return to work, to identify key public health issues along this pathway.Design, setting and participantsThis qualitative study involved repeated semi-structured interviews with seven migrant workers in South Korea who had experienced occupational burns. Participants had diverse nationalities, occupations and legal statuses. Interpreters were employed when needed. Thematic analysis was used to analyse the data, guided by a general inductive approach to identify recurring patterns and issues systematically.ResultsSix themes were identified and organised into three phases: pre-burns, burns-phase and post-burns challenges. Pre-burns challenges included (1) insufficient safety management and the difficulty of asserting occupational safety. Burns-phase challenges encompassed (2) worsened burn severity associated with inadequate first aid and treatment delays. Post-burns challenges involved (3) psychological trauma compounded by systemic gaps in mental health care, (4) premature return to work due to insufficient compensation, (5) inability to return to pre-injury work and limited reemployment due to lasting impairments and a passive job-seeking process, and (6) concerns about legal status.ConclusionsThe interviewed migrant workers with occupational burns face interconnected challenges rooted in systemic failures. This study highlights the need for improved workplace safety, timely medical treatment, mental health support, and adequate compensation, as well as reforms of visa renewal regulations and employer-dependent employment systems. Above all, occupational and public health policies must explicitly recognise and address the distinct vulnerabilities of migrant workers in order to protect this population and promote health equity.

Introduction

Occupational burns represent a critical public health challenge. Unlike non-occupational burns, they involve a disproportionate share of high-severity injuries, including chemical and electrical burns, and generate socioeconomic costs that extend well beyond medical discharge.1 2 They also more frequently affect critical functional areas such as the hands, feet and eyes, with eye injuries occurring nearly twice as often as in non-occupational cases.1

An occupational burn initiates a multistage pathway extending far beyond clinical treatment, from the conditions that produced the injury through rehabilitation to return to work. Vulnerabilities at any one stage compound outcomes at the next. Occupational burns predominantly affect young workers during their peak productive years,2 and 1 year after injury the unemployment rate among those affected remains twice that of workers with non-occupational burns (44% vs 22%).3 The economic consequences extend well beyond acute medical care. Evidence from Switzerland4 and Germany5 indicates that only one-fifth of total burn-related costs derive from medical treatment, with the majority arising from work absence payments, pensions and compensation. Ensuring timely and adequate support at each stage of the injury pathway is therefore critical not only for clinical recovery but as a determinant of successful return to work.6

While studies have identified migrants as a high-risk group for occupational burns,79 migrant workers’ occupational burns have been less studied. Existing studies have been conducted predominantly in high-income countries with large foreign-born populations, such as the USA,2 10 11 the UK,12 Australia and New Zealand,1 Canada13 and Finland.14 However, these studies have largely focused on isolated stages of the injury pathway, such as injury incidence or return to work outcomes, without examining how structural vulnerabilities accumulate across the entire trajectory.

This gap is especially pronounced in rapidly growing migration destinations governed by employer-dependent legal frameworks, such as South Korea. The country recorded the largest increase in labour migration among OECD(Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) countries from 2022 to 2023,15 with over 1.4 million migrant workers concentrated in high-risk sectors including manufacturing, construction, agriculture and fisheries.16 The government-managed Employment Permit System (EPS) ties legal residency directly to the employing company’s consent,17 reinforcing structural vulnerabilities across every stage of the injury pathway. This arrangement constrains workers’ ability to report unsafe conditions, contest inadequate compensation, or change employers following an injury without jeopardising their legal status.18 19

Despite this legal and structural complexity, national statistics on occupational burns among migrant workers in South Korea are absent, and empirical research on this population remains sparse. Lee et al identified burns as a leading injury type among female migrant workers in the country20; however, how structural vulnerabilities shape workers’ experience across the full injury pathway remains unexplored. Given this gap, this study addresses the issue through a qualitative approach, examining workers’ experiences at each stage of the injury pathway and deliberately including those without documentation, who are systematically absent from quantitative surveillance.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study analysed seven cases of occupational burns among migrant workers in South Korea. Data for this study were collected through semi-structured interviews21 from March to August 2023. To ensure analytical rigour, a collaborative approach was adopted, where the first author led the fieldwork and coding, while the co-authors provided critical oversight throughout the process.

Data collection

Each interview session typically lasted 90 to 120 min. The participants provided written informed consent for the recording of the interviews. The recorded content was transcribed and systematically organised. To ensure linguistic accessibility and contextual relevance, community-based bilingual interpreters recommended by migrant support organisations or participants were selected for their familiarity with participants’ labour and social conditions. All participants except one (participant E) used an interpreter for at least one session. The study summary and consent form were translated into each participant’s primary language. Considering that not all migrant workers may be literate, key aspects of the study and consent process were also explained verbally before signatures were obtained.

During the interviews, participants were asked questions such as “Could you recount the day you got the burn, starting from the beginning of your work that day?” Each participant took part in at least two interviews. The interval between injury and initial interview ranged from 3 months to over 2 years. The minimum of 3 months was set to ensure participants had passed the acute medical phase and reached relative psychological stability. The interview questions developed for this study are provided separately in online supplemental A.

Study participants

The participants were limited to adult migrant workers aged 18 years or older who had experienced occupational burns in South Korea over the past 10 years. This time frame was selected to capture a broad range of experiences across various occupational settings. These burns encompassed various types such as scalding, flames, chemical, contact and electrical burns. All cases of occupational burns were included, without restrictions on type or severity. This decision reflects the variability of burn types across different occupational settings. Occupational burns were defined as work-related incidents resulting in burns sustained during the period from the worker’s arrival at the workplace to their departure. Individuals unwilling to discuss their burns were excluded to minimise distress and respect autonomy. No other restrictions were imposed on participants regarding nationality, visa type, time of entry into South Korea, or legal status.

Given that the number of migrant workers with occupational burns in South Korea is unknown, snowball sampling22 was used, with recruitment aimed at including participants with diverse demographic, occupational, and migration factors (eg, legal status). The recruitment materials were translated into 12 languages, listed alphabetically: Bengali, Burmese, Chinese, English, Indonesian, Khmer, Mongolian, Nepali, Russian, Thai, Uzbek, and Vietnamese. These were distributed through migrant labour unions, hospital-based social welfare offices, migrant centres, and migrant communities.

Data analysis

The interviews continued until theme saturation was reached. The strategy for assessing saturation was based on the Comparative Method for Themes Saturation,23 selected for its systematic approach to tracking theme emergence across sequential interviews. This involved sequentially comparing themes from each new interview against those identified in all prior interviews, with a new theme defined as a pattern that could not be absorbed into the existing coding framework. Saturation was assessed at the participant level. Because each participant completed at least two interview sessions, the emergence of new information could be tracked across sessions. When an interview produced no new codes or required no meaningful revision to the thematic structure, saturation was considered reached. The initial assessment was made by the first author and subsequently validated through peer debriefing with all co-authors. During this process, the coding book and thematic structure were reviewed and confirmed. Thematic saturation was reached by the sixth participant, with no new themes emerging in the final sessions. The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist24 was followed to ensure transparent reporting. (online supplemental B).

Considering the use of interpreters and the objectives of this study, we employed thematic analysis following a six-step procedure.25 A general inductive approach was adopted, in which all codes and themes emerged from the data rather than from pre-existing frameworks.26 To preserve linguistic nuance, initial coding and thematic development were conducted in Korean; themes were subsequently translated into English with cross-checks by the research team to ensure conceptual equivalence.

The process of thematic analysis occurred in several steps. First, all interview transcripts were read at least three times to ensure thorough familiarisation with the data. This iterative process led to the development of initial codes. Through constant comparison, codes were grouped into sub-themes and subsequently consolidated into six overarching themes. The co-authors reviewed the coded extracts and coding tree at each analytical stage. Any disagreements over coding or thematic boundaries were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. To enhance analytical transparency, a coding book and analytic memos were developed and maintained to document the progression from raw data to themes.

Rigour and reflexivity

The first author maintained a dual positionality, being familiar enough with the healthcare and occupational contexts to build trust with participants, yet maintaining an analytical distance as an academic researcher. The power imbalances between the research team and participants, who faced considerable structural barriers as migrant workers, were acknowledged and addressed through analytical memoing and a systematic audit trail. Initial sessions were conducted with interpreter assistance to ensure linguistic accuracy for factual details. Subsequent repeated sessions proceeded without interpreters, with targeted clarification sought through interpreter assistance only when needed. This was possible in part because most participants had developed functional Korean proficiency through visa and employment requirements. This progression fostered emotional rapport and unmediated narrative sharing.27

To strengthen rigour, investigator triangulation was employed by involving researchers from both public health and sociology. Beyond thematic validation, multiple peer debriefing sessions were held specifically to examine the researchers’ assumptions and manage the emotional demands of the fieldwork. For language management, conceptual equivalence was prioritised over literal translation. To enhance data credibility, oral member checking was conducted from the second interview onwards. Given participants’ functional Korean proficiency, the first author was able to verbally review the Korean transcriptions with them at a measured pace, allowing for direct verification of narratives without relying on written materials.

Ethical considerations

To minimise the risk of trauma flashbacks (momentary re-experiencing of trauma),28 structured interview questions were reviewed by two trauma specialists beforehand. Criteria for pausing interviews and providing support in the case of flashbacks were established in consultation with the specialists. The first author also received training in ‘Trauma Crisis Counseling and Psychological First Aid’. Follow-up calls were made the day after each interview session to address potential emotional distress arising from the discussion of their burn experiences.

To address the systemic exclusion of migrant populations due to language barriers or legal status, this study prioritised linguistic inclusion and provided diverse participation options. To accommodate undocumented individuals during an active crackdown period, the option to conduct interviews in their dormitories was also provided.

The personal information collected for this study included participants’ names and phone numbers, solely for identification purposes and post-interview check-ins.

Patient and public involvement

Representatives from the Migrants’ Trade Union, the Social Work Team at Hangang Sacred Heart Hospital, Dong Hanh (the Vietnamese community in South Korea), and local migrant community centres participated in the early stages of the study. They provided consultation on the recruitment strategy and helped identify key issues relevant to migrant workers with occupational burns. Their input helped align the research priorities with community needs. However, the study participants (migrant workers) were not involved in the design of the study, the selection of outcome measures, or the writing of the manuscript. The study findings will be shared with the participating organisations and individuals on publication.

Themes and categories

The thematic analysis identified six key themes organised into three phases following Haddon’s temporal framework30: pre-burns, burns-phase and post-burns challenges. Although presented chronologically as distinct stages, the themes are not mutually exclusive; issues such as legal status, financial hardship, and mental health function as cross-cutting factors that intersect and reinforce one another throughout the pathway. These themes included: (1) insufficient safety management and the difficulty of asserting occupational safety, (2) inadequate first aid and treatment delays, (3) systemic gaps in mental health care, (4) premature return to work due to insufficient compensation, (5) inability to return to pre-injury work and limited reemployment due to lasting impairments and a passive job-seeking process, and (6) concerns about legal status. Pre-burns challenges

Insufficient safety management and the difficulty of asserting occupational safety

Occupational burn occurrence among the participants was frequently associated with insufficient safety management in the workplace. This insufficiency included factors such as improper job training (eg, not considering language barriers), a lack of safety training and supervision, and inadequate provision of personal protective equipment (PPE). The participants also described difficulties asserting their safety due to job insecurity, as termination of employment could result in deportation. These dependent positions appeared to prevent them from objecting to employers who hold the power to terminate their employment.

The foreman from the Philippines didn’t explain it verbally; he just gestured with his hands like this (making an ‘X’ with his arms) and it means, ‘Don’t do it like this.’ I couldn’t understand the training content at all. (Participant G, Tap manufacturing factory worker from Nepal)

Participant G was unable to identify which behaviours might be unsafe, as his job training had consisted only of gestures from another migrant worker. Similarly, Participant A attributed her burns to inadequate job training. On arriving at work, she was instructed by her employer to operate a new machine after receiving less than 5 min of instructions in Korean. While operating the unfamiliar machine without proper safety supervision, she immediately sustained full-thickness burns and the loss of two fingertips.

The lack of necessary PPE was frequently mentioned by the participants. Participant G, who handled molten brass exceeding 1000°C, reported that aside from an apron and safety goggles, no other PPE was provided, let alone proper safety training. Following the advice of other workers who started working before him, he had to purchase safety boots and thick gloves at his own expense. Participant B worked on cleaning hot steel plates but was provided with only coarse cotton gloves. She stated that she wore two pairs of these gloves while working because of the heat of the plates. However, this may have contributed to the accident in which her hand was caught between the plates, resulting in major burns.

Even when the participants did not understand the tasks, were not provided with PPE, or were aware of the risks, their marginalised position appeared to make it difficult to advocate for their own safety. For example, Participant D never considered refusing or suggesting an alternative method, even though he believed that the tasks assigned to him were dangerous.

Burns-phase challenges

Inadequate first aid and treatment delays

The severity of burns among the participants was associated with inadequate first aid and considerable delays in initiating treatment. Except for participant G, none of the workers received first aid for their burns; the only first aid provided was an instruction to take a shower.

The coast guards arrived two hours after the accident. I remember it because I was still conscious at that time, but no one on the vessel did anything for me until the coast guards arrived. (Participant C, Offshore fisher from Vietnam)

Participant C took more than 5 hours to reach medical treatment and subsequently underwent 7 months of inpatient care, including 1 month in the intensive care unit. The participants’ treatment duration ranged from several months to over a year. The majority underwent multiple surgeries, including skin grafts and reconstructive procedures, followed by extended rehabilitation.

The delayed initiation of burn treatment among the participants appeared to be linked to two main factors. The first was the distance to medical facilities. Their workplaces were located mainly in suburban industrial areas or offshore fishing zones; the latter could be up to 40 km from the coastline, further limiting access to medical facilities. The second factor was the use of regular vehicles for transportation instead of ambulances. Participant A, who was transported in her employer’s vehicle, stopping first at a local hospital before being redirected to a specialised burn hospital. The journey took 2 hours, compared with a typical travel time of 40 min. Several participants were unaware that ambulance services were available or how to access them, and some employers did not recognise the urgency of burn injuries, resulting in delayed transport to appropriate facilities. In contrast, participant D, guided by paramedic triage, redirected the route from a local hospital to a specialised burn hospital, enabling him to initiate treatment without delay. He also received first aid in the ambulance, which was not provided at the workplace.

Post-burns challenges

Psychological trauma compounded by systemic gaps in mental health care

Migrant burn survivors experienced complex psychological responses, yet these issues were seldom recognised or managed as clinical health concerns necessitating formal intervention. These psychological responses included overwhelming fear (participant A), dissociation (participant E), prolonged sleep disturbances (participants A, D and E), depression (participant G), distress about their condition (participant D), and anger about their physical limitations (participants C and F). All the participants also felt depressed or angry due to the words and actions of their employers, who treated them as incapable individuals. Among the participants, only three (participants A, D and E) with sleep problems were referred for psychiatric treatment during hospitalisation; all other manifestations of psychological distress remained unassessed and unaddressed.

The participants who visited a psychiatrist reported that interpretation services were not available, and felt that their psychiatrist did not fully understand them, which they found unhelpful.

I talked to a psychiatrist using Google Translate and got a prescription for sleeping pills. Since the accident, I’ve been having nightmares and waking up a lot during the night. I’ve been taking the pills for three months now, and it’s getting a little better, but I still can’t stand seeing the machine (from the accident). Additionally, when I run into it in the dorm, my employer keeps giving me a hard time. That’s why I want to move to another factory. (Participant A, Box manufacturing factory worker from Nepal)

Burn trauma was associated with prolonged avoidance of anything that reminded the participants of the incident. For instance, some strongly desired to change workplaces rather than returning to the pre-injury factory (participants A and G), and avoided any kind of fire, even 2 years after the incident (participant E).

Premature return to work due to insufficient compensation

Burn treatment typically requires multiple surgeries, such as skin grafts and plastic surgery, making workers’ compensation particularly important. However, all of the participants, except Participant E, were unaware of workers’ compensation before being admitted to a specialised burn hospital. The two undocumented participants (participants B and F) were particularly unaware of the compensation system, as their employer had covered medical expenses.

Even participants who received workers’ compensation may face financial hardship due to various uninsured items. For example, participant B underwent a total of seven surgeries and paid uninsured costs ranging from US$1200 to US$1500 (approximately 1.7 to 2 million KRW) per surgery. Participant E had to spend nearly US$763 (KRW1 million) per month at the beginning of treatment for hypertrophic scars, which is a partially insured item.

Some workers chose to return to work prematurely due to financial hardship. However, this decision often led to reduced income and worsened the challenges related to discontinued treatment, creating a vicious cycle. Participant C explained the reasons for his premature return to work and the subsequent situation as follows:

When I still hadn’t fully recovered, the captain told me to get back on the vessel quickly. I hadn’t made any money while I was in the hospital for a few months, so I had to go back. But now, I can’t work like before, so I do jobs that even women can handle. I receive less pay than I used to. (Participant C, Offshore fisher from Vietnam)

Inadequate workers’ compensation appeared to push some participants toward another cycle of migrant work. Participant C was billed US$26 000 (approximately KRW37 million) for medical costs not covered by seafarer’s compensation during the first 3 months of hospitalisation. He said that if he could not pay hospital bills while remaining in South Korea, he would have no choice but to return to migrant work, either in South Korea or another country.

The participants also faced non-medical costs, including visa renewal fees, transportation expenses and accommodation costs. The visa renewal fee is US$42 (approximately KRW60 000) per application, and the participants were required to renew their visas every 1–3 months throughout treatment. To renew their visas, they needed to obtain various documents, including medical and balance certificates. This required repeated visits to hospitals, banks, KCOMWEL(Korea workers' Compensation & Welfare service) and immigration offices, adding both administrative burden and cumulative costs to an already demanding recovery process. Some also faced extra rental costs after leaving employer-provided dormitories. For example, Participant B found accommodation with another migrant worker after her employment was terminated, at twice the cost of her previous dormitory fee.

Inability to return to pre-injury work and limited reemployment due to lasting impairments and a passive job-seeking process

Even those who returned to work prematurely could not return to their pre-injury jobs due to termination of employment during the treatment (participants B and D), conflicts with employers over workers’ compensation and treatment expenses (participants C and F) and trauma from occupational burns (participants A, E and G).

Honestly, I can’t do restaurant work anymore. The fire occasionally comes to mind, and when it does, it’s frightening. Now, I just try to avoid fire. (Participant E, Restaurant manager from China)

The participants faced a lack of sufficient time and opportunities to find new employment, leaving them to register on job-seeking lists and await contact from prospective employers. For documented participants, this passive process was further constrained by a 3-month legal deadline under the EPS, within which they were required to secure a new employer to maintain their legal residency. The undocumented participants faced different but equally limiting barriers, including fear of immigration crackdown and physical impairments that restricted their ability to seek employment actively. Participant B described the difficulty of finding employment under these conditions:

The doctor says I can start working little by little, but who will give me work? I can't even hold a broom properly… (Participant B, Food manufacturing factory worker from Nepal)

Concerns about legal status

Employment termination or the inability to secure new work placed participants at risk of losing their legal status, which in turn appeared to obstruct access to adequate physical treatment and negatively impact mental health. Some participants described receiving medical certification to return to light-duty work despite feeling physically and psychologically unprepared. This appeared to be driven by legal pressure to secure employment within a limited timeframe. The case of Participant D demonstrates how difficult it can be for workers to maintain their documented legal status. He was terminated after suffering occupational burns and had to renew his temporary visa while undergoing medical treatment.

At that time, I had just changed my visa to a treatment visa, and my doctor told me that I would need treatment for at least a year. However, KCOMWEL only extended the treatment visa for two to three months at a time. Despite my physical condition, I had to repeatedly visit KCOMWEL and the immigration office. Without proper guidance, sometimes I was sent back multiple times for missing or incorrect documents, and had to visit the office again and again. It was so exhausting and frustrating that I even thought it would have been better if I had died instead of being injured. (Participant D, Foundry worker from Myanmar)

Discussion

This study aimed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the entire pathway of occupational burns among migrant workers. The findings suggest that these experiences are not isolated incidents but represent a cumulative trajectory of vulnerability. Structural inequities across workplace safety, acute medical response, and socio-legal recovery appeared to reinforce one another throughout this pathway. The participants’ narratives illustrate how a single workplace injury can set in motion a prolonged and compounding set of challenges that extend well beyond physical recovery.

Structural factors associated with severe occupational burns among the participants

Our findings align with previous studies, which have underscored that migrant workers are a population susceptible to the interplay of both managerial pressures31 and structural constraints32 due to their employer-dependent status. For instance, orders to work without stopping the machine represent a form of managerial pressure, while the precarious legal status that enhances labour flexibility ultimately constitutes a structural issue. Together, these factors appeared to force migrant workers to work with anxiety or accept unsafe tasks. Although prohibiting workplace changes is a recognised driver of employer dependency and heightened risks of fatal occupational injuries,18 the South Korean employment framework continues to limit migrant workers’ freedom to choose where they work.

The severity of burns among the participants appeared to be compounded by the lack of first aid and delays in initiating treatment. Major burns pose a main obstacle to returning to work, which represents the best functional outcome for burn survivors.6 14 33 The lack of safety training appeared to contribute to an absence of first aid. Appropriate burn first aid involves removing the burn-causing agent and immediately rinsing the burn with cool water to prevent further tissue damage. The injured worker should then be promptly transported to a specialised facility. The long distance to medical facilities in suburban or offshore areas further contributed to delays in initiating treatment. Ambulance transport allows paramedics to triage patients and initiate necessary treatment en route, yet the participants were often unaware of how to access ambulance services. Furthermore, a lack of awareness among employers regarding the urgency of burn care sometimes led to critical delays at non-specialised local hospitals. This is critical because major burns require immediate treatment at specialised burn hospitals to prevent significant cosmetic, functional, or psychological damage.34

Systemic gaps in medical and mental health care

Survivors of occupational accidents not only subjectively suffer from pain but also objectively meet the diagnostic criteria for acute stress disorder or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).35 Mental health care for burn survivors, who are particularly susceptible to PTSD,36 37 is critically important. Psychological pain has been shown to persist beyond the acute treatment phase without diminishing over time.38 The incidence of PTSD among migrant workers who did not receive social support was reportedly 1.62 times higher than that among those who received support.39 Restrictions on family reunification and differing work schedules may limit social support, underscoring the need for public mental health provision. Furthermore, for migrant workers who lack the freedom to choose their employment, the pressure to return to the workplace where the accident occurred has been associated with severe psychological distress.40

Previous studies4143 have emphasised the need for culturally and linguistically appropriate mental health services for migrants. Yet the participants in this study were rarely referred to psychiatric care unless medication was required. This lack of clinical documentation may in turn obstruct access to mental health compensation, as workers are unlikely to meet evidentiary requirements without a formal referral. This gap is widened by the lack of mental health systems that reflect linguistic and cultural needs. Without interpretation services, language barriers appeared to make it difficult for patients to trust their clinicians, contributing to discontinuation of treatment. Previous research44 attributes the focus on immediate medical treatments over deeper consultation to the excessive time and effort required to overcome these communication barriers.

From injury to exclusion: economic hardship and legal precarity among the participants

Beyond the immediate health impact, occupational burns appeared to contribute to a cycle of economic hardship, with some participants choosing between a premature return to work and further labour migration. A report on migrant workers in South Korea45 revealed that income and job security are the main reasons (52.8% of responses) for a premature return to work after occupational injuries, which aligns with the findings of this study. Major burns require an average hospitalisation of more than 4 weeks.46 Treatment often continues for 27 weeks or longer.47 The participants in this study required over a year of treatment, incurring substantial cumulative costs. Since workers’ compensation is often insufficient, achieving a full recovery close to their pre-injury state remains challenging.

Additional non-medical costs, such as visa renewal fees, transportation and accommodation, further exacerbate this financial strain. The administrative burden of frequent visa renewals, often granted for only 2–3 months despite over a year-long treatment need, acts as a persistent barrier to stable recovery. Furthermore, injured migrant workers are frequently evicted from employer-provided dormitories,48 despite legal protections that prohibit termination of employment following occupational accidents.

For non-migrant workers, high burn severity47 49 and mental health status3 49 50 are primary barriers to re-employment. The participants, however, faced structural barriers that extend beyond health conditions, contributing to chronic unemployment. Re-employment is further obstructed by the nature of migrant labour51: these workers are typically concentrated in roles requiring physical strength. Yet burn-induced functional injury and joint contractures from hypertrophic scars appeared to limit their capacity to return to such demanding tasks. Despite the need for job modifications or retraining, tailored vocational rehabilitation programmes and relevant information in their primary languages are absent.52

Furthermore, the return to work is deeply contextualised by legal status. When physicians authorise an impractical return to light-duty work, the worker is often compelled to secure any available employment within 3 months to avoid deportation. This legal precarity was rooted in institutional conditions shared by all participants, including visa dependency and the re-employment deadline. While expressed with particular clarity in some accounts, it formed a common structural backdrop throughout the study. This legal pressure may lead workers to prioritise immediate job security over necessary treatment, potentially exacerbating long-term occupational risks.

Strengths and limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, the study participants were geographically concentrated and their experiences with workers’ compensation were limited, despite the absence of eligibility restrictions. This is partly attributed to migrant workers not considering minor injuries as occupational burns and thus possibly not seeking medical treatment.7 Consequently, those who appealed and were compensated were primarily workers with higher medical expenses. Furthermore, recruitment through migrant support organisations and hospital-based social work offices was more likely to reach workers who had sought formal support, which reinforced this pattern. This means that, despite the diversity in nationalities, occupations and legal statuses among the participants, the sample predominantly reflected the experiences of those with more severe injuries. This pattern itself reflects a characteristic feature of occupational burns among migrant workers, in which only the most severe cases tend to enter formal systems of recognition and compensation.

Second, there may have been limitations in controlling the quality of interpretation for some languages. While interpreters were selected for their contextual understanding of migrant work to ensure the depth of narratives, this approach may have prioritised situational familiarity over the technical standardisation offered by professional interpreters. Additionally, the scarcity of interpreters for certain languages in South Korea (eg, Egyptian Arabic) posed a challenge. However, these risks were mitigated through multiple pre-interview briefings to align on key concepts and ensure thorough preparation.

Third, obtaining in-depth information about specific occupational groups of migrant workers was not possible due to recruitment challenges. Migrant work is highly diverse, and this study focused on a wide range of participants rather than specific occupations to capture extensive experiences related to occupational burns. To understand the burn characteristics of migrant workers accurately, future official statistics on occupational burns and empirical epidemiological studies are required.

Fourth, the timing of the interviews varied among the participants, and the return to work was not reflected in some cases. 1 year may be insufficient to study employment outcomes after burns, as burn survivors often struggle to remain employed or continue working more than 2 years post-injury.33 This study therefore included participants with diverse return-to-work processes and outcomes.

Despite these limitations, this study is the first qualitative study in South Korea to explore the comprehensive pathway of occupational burns among migrant workers.

Conclusions

This study identifies several areas requiring policy attention. Workplace safety training for migrant workers should include multilingual instruction and guidance on accessing emergency services. Employers should also be educated on the importance of prompt ambulance use for occupational burns. Injured migrant workers should have undelayed access to specialised burn treatment. Mental health care should be provided systematically, encompassing interpretation services and psychological screening. Sufficient compensation, extended visa renewal periods for injured workers, and reforms to employer-dependent employment systems would help reduce the structural barriers encountered throughout recovery. Occupational and public health policies must be designed to explicitly recognise and address the distinct vulnerabilities of migrant workers, ensuring their protection and advancing health equity.
