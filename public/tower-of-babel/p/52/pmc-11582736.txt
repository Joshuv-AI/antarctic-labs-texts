Evaluation of a Diabetes Screening Clinical Decision Support Tool
Authors: Eva Tseng, Ariella Stein, Nae-Yuh Wang, Nestoras N. Mathioudakis, Hsin-Chieh Yeh, Nisa M. Maruthur
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe authors evaluated whether an electronic health record clinical decision support system improves diabetes screening across a health system.MethodsStudy population included adults without diabetes attending a visit at 27 primary care clinics. Outcomes included the monthly screening laboratory order rate and completion rate among eligible patient visits. The authors performed logistic regression using a generalized estimating equations model and interrupted time series analysis to evaluate the change in the outcome from baseline to implementation and postimplementation periods.ResultsFrom the baseline to postimplementation period, screening laboratory order rates increased from 53% to 66%, and completion rates increased from 46% to 54%, respectively. The odds of laboratory order and completion increased significantly from the baseline to postimplementation period (test order: OR=3.7; 95% CI=3.4, 4.1, p<0.001; test completion: OR=2.1; 95% CI=2.0, 2.3, p<0.001). In the interrupted time series analysis, laboratory order and completion rates increased significantly from the baseline period (p<0.001 for both).ConclusionsThe authors developed and implemented a clinical decision support system alert that automatically identifies eligible patients and facilitates single-click ordering of a diabetes screening test. An easily implementable and scalable clinical decision support system alert can improve diabetes screening.

INTRODUCTION

The 2021 U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommendation states that adults aged 35–70 years with overweight or obesity should be screened for prediabetes and Type 2 diabetes.1 Unfortunately, national data indicate that <15% of U.S. adults meeting diabetes screening criteria were screened for prediabetes and Type 2 diabetes,2 with estimated rates varying widely across various healthcare organizations.3 As part of a health system diabetes prevention initiative, the authors implemented and evaluated whether an electronic health record (EHR) clinical decision support system (CDSS) improves diabetes screening.

METHODS

Diabetes Screening Electronic Health Record Clinical Decision Support System

The authors developed the EHR CDSS with stakeholder engagement from primary care physicians. The alert was designed to appear only for patients overdue for diabetes screening on the basis of the USPSTF 2021 guidelines (adults with BMI ≥25 kg/m² and aged 35–70 years should be screened every 3 years).1 Patients who had completed glycemic testing (HbA1c or any glucose) in the past 3 years were considered ineligible for screening. Although guidelines specify using a fasting glucose for screening, the CDSS was designed to count all glucoses as an eligible screening test because the laboratory does not collect information on fasting status, and clinicians felt that this alert would otherwise appear too frequently, particularly among patients with previously normal nonfasting glucose values.

The tool, implemented on July 15, 2021, across specified primary care clinics below simultaneously, is an Epic Best Practice Alert appearing on the left side of the screen when a clinician opens a visit encounter (in person or video visit). Clinicians may click on the alert, which causes a pop-up box to open with the title “Diabetes screening overdue: The USPSTF recommends screening for diabetes in adults ages 35 to 70 years whose BMI is 25 or higher.” Below the title is (1) a list of the dates and values for the most recent HbA1c (point of care [POC] and serum if available) and glucose (POC and serum if available), (2) laboratory order options to facilitate single-click ordering (POC HbA1c, serum HbA1c, and serum fasting glucose), and (3) link to the USPSTF website with the guideline recommendation. The clinician may also choose not to place an order and click a reason for not placing an order (address at next visit, disagree with recommendation, does not meet criteria, patient refused or other).

Study Population

The study population included adult patients without diabetes aged 35–70 years who attended ≥1 office or video visit at 27 community and academic primary care clinics across Maryland/Washington, DC, between July 1, 2020, and December 31, 2022. Diabetes status was ascertained by determining whether the patient was active on the diabetes registry in the EHR at the time of the visit. Visits were eligible for the study if the patient had not completed a screening test within the 3 years prior to the visit.

Measures

The primary outcome was the monthly screening laboratory order rate, defined as the percentage of patient visits eligible for screening where a laboratory order from their primary care clinician was placed within ±30 days of their primary care visit that month (time period selected to account for orders that may be placed before visit during precharting). The secondary outcome was the monthly screening laboratory completion rate, defined as the percentage of patient visits eligible for screening where patient completed a screening laboratory test within +30 days of their primary care visit that month. To align with the tests that the CDSS accounts for, the authors defined screening laboratory tests to include HbA1c (serum or POC) and fasting glucose and glucose (panel or POC). The unit of these analyses was eligible visit.

Statistical Analysis

The authors performed logistic regression using a multilevel generalized estimating equations (GEEs) model (accounting for >1 eligible visit per patient in the study and clustering by clinic) to evaluate the change in the aggregated rate for the outcomes from baseline (July 1, 2020–July 14, 2021) to implementation (July 15, 2021–December 31, 2021: period immediately after CDSS start where uptake of tool was starting) and postimplementation (January 1, 2022–December 31, 2022: period of sustainment with greater awareness of the CDSS) periods. Interrupted time series (ITS) analysis employed separate models for the outcomes, allowing for different rates before and after implementation. The models examined whether a significant change in the slope for the outcome rate existed during implementation and postimplementation periods versus baseline. In the ITS model, the authors accounted for clustering by clinic and patient (which accounts for multiple visits a patient may have).

In a sensitivity analysis, the authors restricted eligible screening laboratory orders and results to only include glucose labs labeled as fasting glucose and, additionally, for eligible results, glucose samples drawn between 6:00 and 10:00AM. This time period was selected because the laboratory does not collect information on fasting status and on the basis of an institutional study the authors conducted showing that the majority of patients (80%) coming in for a glucose testing during that time period had reported fasting.4 In an additional sensitivity analysis, the authors adjusted for the overall number of visits a patient had during the study period in the GEE model.

The authors estimated laboratory test results in the glycemic range for diabetes, prediabetes, and normoglycemia using completed HbA1c tests (fasting glucose not used because it was rarely checked) in the 3 study periods. Each patient had only 1 eligible completed test result because thereafter, they were up to date on screening.

In February 2022, a 7-item online survey using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) was sent to all primary care clinicians (PCP) (n=114) to assess reach, accuracy, and utility of the CDSS. The authors collected self-reported demographic data on percentage clinical effort. Survey responses were anonymous and therefore could not be directly linked to the primary outcome. The IRB approved this study.

RESULTS

Between July 1, 2020, and December 31, 2022, there were 18,341 eligible primary care patients seen, contributing 24,488 visits. During the baseline period, average screening laboratory order and completion rates were 53% and 46%, respectively, increasing to 66% and 54%, respectively, in the postimplementation period (Figure 1). In a sensitivity analysis restricting eligible ordered and completed laboratory tests to only include fasting glucose tests and glucose samples drawn between 6:00 and 10:00AM and HbA1c, the authors found similar results for the ITS and GEE models (Appendix Tables 1 and 2, available online). In additional sensitivity analyses, the authors adjusted for the overall number of total visits a patient had during the study period in the GEE model, showing no change in the overall trend and significance (Appendix Table 3, available online).

Among 12,003 eligible screening tests completed during the 3 time periods (61% HbA1c, 4% POC HbA1c, 35% nonfasting glucose, <0.1% fasting glucose), the prevalence of HbA1c test results in the postimplementation period consistent with diabetes, prediabetes, and normoglycemia were 4.7%, 32.0%, and 63.3%, respectively (Appendix Table 4, available online). Across the 3 time periods, there was a small but significant increase in laboratory results consistent with prediabetes (p=0.006).

The primary care clinician survey response rate was 31% (n=35 of 114). Most respondents (94%) felt that the CDSS accurately identifies patients and agreed (51%) that the CDSS is a helpful reminder to identify patients due for screening. There was no significant association between the respondent's percentage clinical effort and whether they found the CDSS helpful.

DISCUSSION

The authors demonstrate that an EHR CDSS may successfully improve diabetes screening. National estimates indicate that 23% of U.S. adults with diabetes and over 80% of adults with prediabetes are unaware of having the condition.5 Prior research suggests barriers to inadequate screening, including low clinician knowledge about screening criteria.6 To overcome such barriers, the authors implemented a simple EHR CDSS alert that automatically identifies eligible patients and facilitates single-click ordering of a screening test, resulting in an absolute increase in screening order rate by almost 13 percentage points.

This study adds to the small number of studies that have focused on diabetes screening.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 The intervention described in this study builds on a quality improvement study that the authors conducted at a single primary care clinic during 2021–2022.11 In that study, the authors tested a manual workflow for identifying patients due for diabetes screening that was later replaced by the EHR CDSS described in this paper, demonstrating a significant increase in weekly percentage of patients screened from 51% to 65% in the preintervention versus intervention phase.11 Among other studies testing interventions to improve diabetes screening, the 2 identified studies conducted in primary care settings7,8 used less robust definitions for the outcome. For example, in a quality improvement study from the Michigan Medicine system, a similar EHR alert was implemented, but the reported outcome was a reduction in the alert-eligible population in primary care instead of actual screening rates.7

Whereas some studies on the benefits of diabetes screening in asymptomatic individuals have been inconclusive,12, 13, 14, 15 there are some notable studies demonstrating the benefits of screening.16,17 In the nonrandomized ADDITION-Denmark study, patients with an elevated diabetes risk score were invited to visit their family doctor for laboratory screening for diabetes.16 Over a median follow-up of 6.2 years, the risks of cardiovascular disease and mortality were 16% and 21% lower, respectively, among patients with diabetes in the screening group versus patients with diabetes in the nonscreening group. The ADDITION-Europe trial demonstrated that intensive treatment of multiple risk factors after diabetes screening led to a small, nonsignificant reduction of incident cardiovascular events and death18 and significant improvements in HbA1c, blood pressure, and cholesterol.19 Diabetes screening has also been shown to be cost-effective20 and associated with few harms.21

Limitations

Strengths include EHR CDSS implementation in community and academic primary care clinic settings. Limitations include the use of glucose from panel orders. However, clinician stakeholders felt strongly that the EHR CDSS would overfire if glucoses from panel orders were not included as eligible screening tests. The authors conducted a sensitivity analysis restricting eligible completed laboratory tests to only include fasting glucose and glucose samples drawn between 6:00 and 10:00AM, finding that results were similar. The clinician survey response rate was low but similar to those of other surveys of general internists that did not provide incentives.22,23 The survey results were anonymous, so the authors could not examine whether outcomes were associated with primary care physician characteristics. The authors cannot exclude the impact of other health system–wide initiatives that may contribute to increased screening.

Future research is needed on how to increase completion of laboratory orders, which were low across all time periods. Additional research on whether follow-up actions were taken on the basis of abnormal results is important in understanding the clinical impact of this EHR CDSS.

CONCLUSIONS

The findings of this study support implementing an EHR CDSS to improve diabetes screening as part of health system initiatives to improve diabetes prevention.
