Representation Transfer via Invariant Input-driven Continuous Attractors for Fast Domain Adaptation
Authors: Tie Xu, Shengdun Wu, Junwen Luo, Yao Yang, Feng Lin, Junjie Jiang, Guozhang Chen, Dongping Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional end-to-end deep neural networks often degrade under domain shifts and require costly retraining when deployed in unpredictable, noisy environments. Inspired by biological brains, we propose a modular framework where each module is a recurrent neural network pretrained via a simple, task-agnostic protocol to learn robust, transferable features. This shapes stable yet flexible low-dimensional representations as invariant input-driven continuous attractor manifolds embedded in high-dimensional latent space across different tasks, supporting robust transfer and resilience to temporal perturbations. At deployment, only a lightweight adapter needs training, allowing rapid adaptation to new tasks. Validated on gesture and rehabilitation action recognition tasks, our framework achieves accuracy competitive with state-of-the-art methods, especially in few-shot settings, while requiring an order of magnitude fewer parameters and minimal training. By integrating biologically inspired attractor dynamics with cortical-like modular composition, the framework offers a practical path toward robust, continual adaptation in real-world information processing.

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems deployed in embodied contexts–such as robotics, autonomous vehicles, and wearable assistants–must operate reliably in open-ended, unpredictable environments. In practice, they routinely encounter sensor noise, adversarial perturbations, data imbalance, and non-stationarity1. Achieving robust performance under such conditions demands rapid domain adaptation2, especially when training data are scarce or environments shift over time. This raises three fundamental challenges2: (1) transferability: how to build representations that generalize across environments; (2) robustness: how to remain stable under noise and corrupted inputs; (3) cross-task adaptability: how to rapidly learn new tasks with minimal data.

Conventional end-to-end deep learning architectures are poorly suited to these challenges. They assume matched training and test distributions3, making their learned task-specific features brittle under distributional shifts. Alternatives such as reservoir computing4 provide faster adaptation by decoupling representation learning from task-specific training, but random reservoirs lack the structured, reusable features needed for transfer across complex domains. Biological cognition, by contrast, achieved remarkable adaptability and generalization from limited supervision5. This flexibility stems from a central organization principle of the brain: the reuse of neural circuitry evolutionarily conserved for many different tasks6,7. Underlying this principle are collective neural dynamics that self-organize into attractors, low-dimensional, stable patterns of neural activity across different input conditions8. Attractors can encode core motion variables such as direction and position8, and have been proposed as reusable cognitive “symbols” for fundamental concepts or operations9,10, enabling circuits to be flexibly recombined across tasks rather than rebuilt each time. Their noise tolerance and representational stability make them well-suited for robust, modular computation and transfer across tasks11,12. Yet it remains unclear how randomly recurrent neural networks (RNNs) can evolve to embed continuous attractors as robust, transferable representations, and how such attractor dynamics can be recomposed for rapid adaptation with minimal supervision.

We address this gap by proposing a brain-inspired modular framework that explicitly embeds continuous attractor manifolds13 as transferable representations for domain adaptation. Drawing on insights from neuroscience and dynamical systems14, we pretrain RNN modules not on task-specific labels, but on simple synthetic sequences that capture fundamental spatiotemporal symmetries. It sculpts each module’s high-dimensional state space into structured, low-dimensional manifolds–rings, cylinders, and tori–that represent interpretable motion primitives such as direction, velocity, and position. Crucially, these input-driven slow manifolds15,16 exhibit smooth and flexible dynamics, remaining stable under noise and perturbations while responding rapidly to changing inputs. This enables robust encoding of continuous input variables under strong distribution shift, preserving performance across tasks.

The framework is validated on the DVS (dynamic vision sensor) gesture benchmark and a custom RGB (red–green–blue) rehabilitation dataset. In both domains, the learned manifolds preserve their structure–anchored to interpretable motion variables–even under severe interruptions such as corrupted or missing frames. The pretrained RNN modules can be flexibly composed within a modular architecture, each specializing in distinct spatiotemporal features. A Hebbian-inspired mechanism enables lightweight reconfiguration at deployment, requiring only a small adapter to be trained. This enables rapid few-shot adaptation in a single epoch without disrupting the pretrained attractor dynamics. The resulting performance is competitive with those of leading deep models, e.g., C3D (convolutional 3D)17 and ViViT (video vision transformer)18, while using an order of magnitude fewer parameters and dramatically reducing training energy demands. By integrating biologically inspired attractor dynamics with modular composition, this work provides a practical path toward robust, efficient, and continually adaptive information processing.

Results

Our framework

Many human actions are dominated by low-complexity, constrained motions that closely approximate rigid-body dynamics19,20. Recognizing such actions can therefore be reduced to estimating a small set of core spatiotemporal observables, including position, direction, and velocity of motion (Fig. 1a). We hypothesize that RNNs pretrained to extract these elementary motion primitives from synthetic inputs can develop internal dynamics that serve as reusable inductive biases, supporting robust generalization to naturalistic tasks. While inductive bias in artificial neural networks has been extensively studied21–24, explicit demonstrations that link learned dynamical structure to transferable inductive biases remain scarce. To test this hypothesis, we pretrain vanilla RNNs on synthetic, task-agnostic video sequences designed to isolate individual motion attributes25 (Fig. 1b). Inspired by developmental learning in biological vision26,27, each module specializes in encoding a fundamental motion primitive–direction, velocity, or spatial salience–by self-organizing into smooth, low-dimensional manifolds aligned with physical motion parameters (Fig. 1c–e). Each module is instantiated as a vanilla RNN with 512 neurons, whose hidden state h(t) evolves via leaky integration of previous state and current input, followed by ReLU nonlinearity and layer normalization for stability (see “Methods” in Section 6). Three such RNNs are pretrained from random initialization, each on synthetic video sequences designed to isolate a single kinematic attribute (Fig. 1b): (1) The Direction RNN is trained on T = 100-frame bar-motion clips spanning 12 directions with sparse Bernoulli noise (p = 0.01), predicting motion angle θ; (2) the Velocity RNN is trained on bars moving at five discrete speeds and 12 directions, jointly encoding both attributes; (3) the Salience RNN is trained on dot-cloud clips with a smoothly drifting Gaussian centroid, estimating (x, y) spatial positions. This pretraining paradigm circumvents difficulties associated with task-specific end-to-end optimization, vanishing/exploding gradients28, and instead induces biologically inspired dynamics aligned with core kinematic primitives (details in Section 6). Among these attributes, direction selectivity is particularly fundamental, consistent with its prevalence in early visual processing across species19,29–31.

Pretraining induces striking geometric organization in low-dimensional subspaces (using principal component (PC) analysis (Fig. 1c–e)): (1) Direction RNN: hidden states self-organized into a smooth ring manifold (effective dimension ≈2.11, measured by participation ratio introduced in Section 6), each point encoding a distinct motion angle–reminiscent of biological ring attractors for head direction or angular velocity8 (Fig. 1c). (2) Salience RNN: hidden states formed a toroidal manifold (effective dimension ≈3.81), where positions on the torus mapped to (x, y) spatial coordinates, embedding 2D locations into a continuous latent structure (Fig. 1d). (3) Velocity RNN: hidden states traced a cylindrical manifold (effective dimension ≈3.46), with angular components encoding motion direction and radial or axial coordinates capturing speed (Fig. 1e). In all cases, the relevant motion features emerged as primary variables, around which activity self-organized into a smooth, low-dimensional manifold. Therefore, synthetic pretraining reliably sculpts modular RNNs into interpretable dynamical systems, where neural activity self-organizes to represent the relevant motion attribute.

The pretrained RNNs are integrated within our pretrained reservoir group (PRG) framework, inspired by principles of cortical modularity and parallel processing. The architecture comprises three components (Fig. 2a): an input alignment module that converts diverse sensory inputs into a unified binary video representation at two spatial resolutions, st1 and st2 (Fig. 2b), ensuring compatibility with the pretrained modules; a set of motion-specialized pretrained RNNs (Fig. 2b, c); and a fusion layer that accumulates evidence across modules. The design is inspired by three well-established neurobiological principles: (1) parallel feature encoding by distinct cortical “mini-columns”; (2) in the primary visual cortex (V1), neurons are organized into mini-columns, each selectively tuned to low-level visual features such as orientation, spatial frequency, or motion direction32,33, while extra-striate areas (e.g., middle temporal area MT/V5) specialize in motion velocity34. (3) Attentional mechanisms in the frontal eye field (FEF) for enhancing signal fidelity while filtering out noise35,36. Mirroring cortical parallelism, we deploy complementary RNNs tuned to distinct motion attributes: a Spatial Module with strong recurrent self-connected (autapse-like) integration for spatial occupancy; a Direction RNN encoding coarse angular features (Z+, Z−); a Velocity RNN encoding discrete speed levels (V+, V−); and a Salience RNN estimating motion centroids (x(t), y(t)) to guide high-resolution processing in downstream modules, e.g., the Direction and Velocity RNNs, yielding refined estimates (ZF+, ZF−, VF+, VF−) (details in Section 6).Fig. 2Implementation of our PRG framework.a Overview. b DVS input processing: at each time step t = 1, . . . , n, preprocessed DVS frames (st1) drive the Salience RNN to estimate focus points (x[t], y[t]), which are used to crop a focused event frame (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\bf{st2}}}}^{{\prime} }$$\end{document}st2′). Decision-making is modeled after parietal-prefrontal circuits that integrate heterogeneous evidence over time37,38. We implement a two-stage fusion strategy: (1) static fusion, in which each module’s hidden state is mapped to class probabilities (via support vector machine (SVM)) and uniformly aggregated (Fig. 2d); and (2) adaptive Hebbian fusion, in which module-specific fusion weights are updated on a small validation set to reinforce correct associations (Fig. 2e; details in Section 6). In comparison, Hebbian fusion yields modest improvements in task performance (Supplementary Table 1), but its primary value lies in demonstrating how simple, local learning rules-akin to those observed in biological systems-can adaptively reweigh module contributions online, providing a biologically plausible and lightweight mechanism for integrating pretrained modules. At deployment, these components are assembled into a task-specific action recognition pipeline. The modular, late-integration design decomposes complex gestures into interpretable components, supports robust cross-domain transfer, and maintains strong performance under distributional shifts and limited supervision, offering a principled route toward brain-inspired generalization.

Probing hidden dynamics

We next examine the internal dynamics of each pretrained RNN under perturbations of both internal state and external inputs (Fig. 3a, b; Section 6). Across modules, the emergent representations form low-dimensional manifolds that are tightly anchored to input and remain resilient to internal noise and input variation. Importantly, the dynamics are fully input-driven: neural activity closely tracks the instantaneous stimulus rather than persisting autonomously. For instance, in the Direction RNN (Fig. 3a), a direction-fixed input (e.g., Iθ = 90∘) places the state at a corresponding location on a ring-shaped manifold, whereas removing the input rapidly drives activity back to baseline. The resulting manifolds consist of input-dependent fixed points and exhibit two core dynamical properties (Fig. 3b–e): (1) Stability: When the hidden state is perturbed away from the manifold under a fixed input, activity rapidly contracts back toward the input-anchored attractor region within 5–10 time steps (Fig. 3c–e, left PC panels). Vector field analyses reveal strong restorative forces pointing inward toward the manifold, with force directions concentrated near 180∘ relative to the outward normal (angle distributions in Fig. 3c–e). Moreover, the magnitude of the restoring force increases monotonically with distance from the manifold (force–distance plots in Fig. 3c–e), using the slow manifold analysis (details in Section 6). Once trajectories return to the manifold, activity evolves slowly along tangential directions, a defining signature of continuous attractors. (2) Adaptability to input drift: When external inputs vary smoothly–such as changes in motion direction (±30∘), spatial location (±1 unit), or velocity level (±1)—neural trajectories exhibit coherent tangential motion along the manifold surface (Fig. 3c–e, right PC panels). High cosine similarity between the neural flow and the local manifold tangent confirms that the encoding geometry is preserved during input changes. This tangential adaptation enables continuous tracking and interpolation of dynamic stimuli without disrupting the underlying representation.

These shared dynamical properties translate into strong generalization well beyond the pretraining conditions (Fig. 3f–h). Specifically, the Direction RNN maintains over 80% decoding accuracy on novel combinations of direction-holding interval and speed (Fig. 3f), substantially outperforming a C3D17 baseline, which incurs over 40% error at high velocities (Fig. 3f); the Salience RNN generalizes robustly across variations in centroid speed and spatial spread σ, again exceeding C3D performance (Fig. 3g); the Velocity RNN preserves high accuracy under changes in velocity-holding interval and exposure to previously unseen speed ranges, demonstrating strong resilience to pronounced distributional shifts (Fig. 3h).

Together, these results demonstrate that each pretrained RNN encodes motion attributes within smooth, low-dimensional, input-driven manifolds–rings, tori, and cylinders–that are both dynamically attractive and geometrically stable under perturbations15. Such a structure constitutes as a powerful inductive bias, enabling reliable interpolation of unseen inputs while avoiding the extrapolation failures commonly observed in conventional feedforward models.

Representation transfer to real-world tasks

Crucially, the manifolds learned through task-agnostic pretraining–input-driven continuous attractors–transfer directly to real-world scenarios while preserving both their geometric organization and dynamical properties. This supports our central claim that these attractors act as robust, reusable computational substrates for naturalistic motion recognition, without requiring retraining. We validate this by deploying the pretrained RNN modules on two real-world datasets: the DVS Gesture dataset and a customized RGB rehabilitation action dataset. For each dataset, neural activity evoked by real inputs is projected into the same PC spaces used during synthetic pretraining, allowing a direct assessment of whether real-task trajectories remain confined to the pretrained manifolds. Despite the substantial distributional shift from synthetic stimuli (isolated direction, salience, and velocity cues) to complex real-world videos (DVS Gesture and RGB Rehabilitation) (Supplementary Fig. 1), the topology and flow structure of the manifolds are largely preserved. This preservation reflects two necessary conditions for effective transfer (Fig. 4). First, dynamical confinement: real input streams generate smooth, low-dimensional trajectories that remain close to the pretrained manifold’s tangent space, ensuring stable and noise-resilient encoding. These principles manifest consistently across all three RNN modules (Fig. 4). In the Direction RNN, clockwise (CW) and counterclockwise (CCW) arm-waving gestures generate oppositely circulating trajectories around the ring manifold (Fig. 4a, left). High cosine similarity between neural flow and manifold tangents confirms preservation of directional encoding (Fig. 4a, middle), yielding near-perfect classification accuracy (97%) and strong robustness to frame dropout (random deletion of a proportion of frames) and temporal interpolation (insertion of random clips between consecutive frames 6), substantially outperforming C3D17 (Fig. 4a, right). In the Salience RNN, left- and right-hand gestures occupy distinct regions on the toroidal manifold (Fig. 4b, left), with clearly separable decoded x-positions (Fig. 4b, middle), demonstrating that spatial structure learned during pretraining is retained under naturalistic inputs and remains robust to noise (Fig. 4b, right). In the Velocity RNN, shoulder movements of varying intensity trace distinct trajectories along the cylindrical manifold (Fig. 4c, left). Decoded speed levels reliably reflect motion strength, including null and full movements in the rehabilitation dataset (Fig. 4c, middle), and remain stable under temporal perturbations (Fig. 4c, right).

Most critically, we find that low dimensionality is essential for transfer. When additional features are entangled during pretraining, the resulting manifolds become higher-dimensional and mixed (details in Section 6). Under these conditions, both geometric alignment and dynamical stability degrade during transfer, leading to sharply reduced task performance (Supplementary Fig. 4). In contrast, low-dimensional attractors that encode disentangled motion primitives preserve topological invariance, ensure smooth flow alignment, and support robust performance under noise and distributional shifts.

Together, these findings demonstrate that input-driven continuous attractor dynamics—when organized in low-dimensional, disentangled manifolds—enable real-time tracking, rapid recovery from corrupted or missing frames, and reliable cross-domain transfer, even when pretraining is performed exclusively on synthetic data. Compared with static feedforward models like C3D, our RNNs continuously integrate information over time and retain coherent representations under substantial distribution shifts (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Performance and ablation studies across two datasets

Now we evaluate the full PRG system on the two real-world datasets. The rehabilitation dataset comprises 15 fine-grained action classes involving upper- and lower-limb joints (e.g., shoulder, elbow, and knee), designed to emulate realistic rehabilitation scenarios with diverse camera viewpoints, motion amplitude, and inter-subject variability (more details in Section 6). Across both datasets, our modular, biologically inspired model–containing approximately 5 million (5M) parameters–achieves strong accuracy, interpretability, and robustness, particularly in data-limited regimes. Despite its compact size, the PRG framework matches or exceeds the performance of substantially larger baselines, including 10- or 50-layer ResNet C3D17 and ViViT18 (more details in Section 6), while requiring only minutes of training time (compared to hours) and an order of magnitude fewer parameters (5M vs. 240–300M).

On the RGB rehabilitation action dataset (Table 2; Fig. 5b), the full model attains 90% macro-class accuracy and 62% overall subclass accuracy across all 15 categories, substantially outperforming both ResNet C3D (10- and 50-layer versions) and ViViT. In few-shot settings, the PRG framework exhibits strong sample efficiency and rapid adaptability. Discussion

We introduced a biologically inspired modular RNN framework where task-agnostic pretraining sculpts reusable input-driven continuous attractor manifolds. These manifolds endow the system with strong resilience to severe input perturbations, including up to 50% frame dropout, while remaining tightly anchored to inputs. Crucially, these pretrained attractors transfer directly to real-world tasks without retraining, allowing the framework to match or exceed the performance of conventional end-to-end deep networks while using orders of magnitude fewer parameters across diverse tasks and sensor modalities (e.g., DVS and RGB). Each RNN module encodes a specific low-dimensional manifold aligned with fundamental sensorimotor primitives–direction, velocity, or spatial position–mirroring canonical computations observed in cortical circuits. Our results suggest that many sensorimotor recognition problems can be reframed as trajectories evolving on a shared set of reusable low-dimensional manifolds. This perspective offers a principled and efficient alternative to monolithic deep models, and points toward self-configuring, resilient agents capable of real-time learning and adaptation with minimal data and computational overhead.

Representation transfer for domain adaptation

Robust domain adaptation remains a central challenge in modern AI. Conventional deep networks tend to learn narrow, task-specific features that degrade sharply under distribution shifts2,39,40. Existing mitigation strategies, such as data augmentation41, contractive objectives42, adversarial alignment43–45, or causal representation learning46, often require massive data, heavy computation, and produce representations that are difficult to interpret or reuse. Reservoir computing partially alleviates these issues by training only lightweight readouts atop fixed recurrent dynamics4,47. Yet, random reservoirs lack structured inductive biases, limiting their effectiveness for complex, structured domains such as video. In contrast, biological brains achieve robust generalization with minimal supervision by reusing evolutionarily conserved, modular circuits that encode stable and transferable priors48,49. These circuits form reusable “basic concepts”50,51 that can be flexibly recombined across tasks, as exemplified by grid cells whose spatial representations generalize to abstract reasoning domains52,53.

Our framework applies these biological principles to AI. By pretraining on task-agnostic synthetic sequences, each module acquires an interpretable and reusable representation of a core motion primitive—direction, velocity, or spatial position—variables that are central to motion perception in biological vision (e.g., MT/V5 neurons)26,34. These representations remain invariant across tasks because they are grounded in fundamental physical symmetries rather than dataset-specific statistics. The use of synthetic proxy tasks allows us to explicitly isolate and embed this invariance into neural dynamics, sculpting compact, low-dimensional, input-driven manifolds that are rarely exposed cleanly in real-world data. As a result, the framework avoids the need for massive labeled datasets, expensive augmentation pipelines, or adversarial training41,43. The learned representations are naturally disentangled54, interpretable, and rapidly adaptable to new domains with minimal additional supervision. Grounding domain adaptation in reusable kinematic primitives thus offers a brain-inspired, data-efficient alternative to black-box transfer learning55, bridging generalization performance with practical deployability.

Invariant input-driven neural manifolds

Our central contribution is the identification of input-driven continuous attractors in pretrained RNNs as a key mechanism for robust representation transfer. Unlike classic autonomous attractors56, which converge to self-sustained states even when inputs vanish, the attractors observed here remain continuously shaped by external inputs. When inputs are static, the network converges to stable, input-anchored fixed points; when inputs vary, neural activity evolves smoothly along low-dimensional manifolds that track the signal in real time. In this way, high-dimensional and noisy input streams are transformed into compact, interpretable trajectories in neural state space57.

This mechanism generalizes traditional continuous attractor models58, which were typically designed for slow, autonomous processes such as spatial integration13,59. Such models struggle to meet the temporal demands of real-world perception, where inputs change rapidly8,59. Input-driven attractors resolve this mismatch by jointly satisfying three properties: stability under fixed inputs, adaptability to continuously varying stimuli, and flexibility through compact low-dimensional manifolds embedded within high-dimensional recurrent dynamics54. This combination enables attractors learned from simple synthetic tasks to generalize robustly to complex real-world settings with minimal supervision.

Although low-dimensional representations can also emerge in feedforward architectures, such as autoencoders60 or CNNs61,62), these models implement static input-output mappings and lack intrinsic dynamical stability. Consequently, feedforward models processing videos rely on fixed temporal windows and are brittle to frame loss or temporal distortions (Fig. 4). In contrast, our input-driven attractors maintain coherent internal dynamics over time, rapidly recovering from missing or corrupted frames and preserving stable representations under severe distribution shifts. Notably, the invariant geometries we observe parallel those reported in biological circuits, including toroidal manifolds in grid cells8 and movement-related trajectories in frontal and cingulate areas63. This convergence suggests that embedding simple low-dimensional manifolds within rich recurrent dynamics is a general neural strategy for balancing stability and flexibility.

Advantages of a brain-inspired framework

Human-like generalization relies heavily on recomposition—the ability to reuse and recombine existing functional components to solve new problems64,65. Our framework instantiates this principle through a modular architecture inspired by cortical microcircuits: parallel processing streams emulate minicolumnar organization, while a salience mechanism supports multi-resolution perception and selective integration of diverse input sources. This design allows incoming data to be decomposed into interpretable “symbols” encoded by pretrained, invariant input-driven continuous attractors, which are then flexibly recombined for downstream tasks. A key practical advantage is that new tasks require updating only lightweight decoders, dramatically reducing training time, data requirements, and computational cost. This plug-and-play modularity makes the framework well-suited for deployment on low-power hardware, including edge devices and neuromorphic platforms, while preserving privacy by keeping raw sensory data local.

Although our experiments focus on relatively simple, rigid-body-like actions, the framework naturally extends to more complex behaviors by adding new modules. As new capabilities reuse the same attractor-based primitives, the marginal cost of expansion remains low. While gesture recognition serves as a proof of concept, the architecture itself is task-agnostic and applicable to broader perceptual, motor, and decision-making domains. Extensions such as scene segmentation, sequence memory, or hierarchical control can be integrated while preserving interpretability and transferability.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the framework depends on a biologically inspired, task-agnostic pretraining protocol, and downstream performance is sensitive to how comprehensively the initial exploration phase covers the relevant motion space; insufficient diversity may yield brittle or incomplete attractor landscapes. Second, the current design is best suited to actions describable by a small set of low-dimensional motion primitives and therefore assumes relatively rigid movements with limited degrees of freedom, limiting direct applicability to highly articulated actions. Third, our experiments focus on single coherent motion streams; more complex scenes involving multiple agents or interactions would likely require additional segmentation or attention mechanisms. Finally, while we evaluate across multiple datasets and sensor modalities, the range of tasks remains limited, and broader benchmarks will be needed to fully assess the generality of the proposed attractor-based inductive bias.

Summary

In summary, we present a biologically inspired modular RNN framework that combines the stability of classical attractor networks with the adaptability of input-driven recurrent dynamics. Through task agnostic pretraining, we sculpt invariant input-driven continuous attractors that maintain stable, low-dimensional representations under abrupt input changes, enabling robust transfer and few-shot adaptation in nonstationary environments. The modular design further allows agents to expand their attractor repertoire through synthetic pretraining–using generative models or physics-based simulators–and to flexibly select and combine modules via lightweight adaptation. Together, these properties provide a foundation for resilient, low-cost embodied AI systems capable of continual learning and real-time adaptation with minimal data and computation.

Manifold analysis

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) can be viewed as nonlinear dynamical systems whose internal states tend to settle near attractor manifold once trained15. We empirically locate these manifold regions by following a systematic process.

Recording internal states

To probe each pretrained RNN’s dynamics, we present two types of stimuli. (i) Artificial sequences: 500 synthetically generated clips (100 frames each) covering the full range of motion parameters. This dataset is used for manifold analysis. (ii) Real sequences: the entire training set of DVS gesture and RGB rehabilitation action videos at resolution st1, used to probe representation transfer. For every frame t, we record the input xt, its ground-truth estimated variable ϕt, and the network’s hidden state h(t), yielding paired datasets {(xt, ϕt, h(t))}.

Decoding test of representation transfer

To test the performance of representation transfer between pretraining task and real task following methods are implemented:

RNN-manifold decoding

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\bf{h}}}}_{t}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{H}$$\end{document}ht∈RH be the hidden state of a pretrained RNN at time t. The same pipeline is applied to all datasets (DVS Gesture and the RGB rehabilitation set).

Robustness to temporal perturbations

To test decoder stability, we apply two temporal corruptions to the input before projection: (1) Frame dropout, which uniformly removes a fixed proportion p of frames; (2) Random interpolation, which inserts stochastic spatiotemporal noise. For the latter, each adjacent frame pair is flattened, concatenated, randomly shuffled to destroy spatial locality while preserving pixel-intensity statistics, then reshaped and inserted between the pair. The SVM is trained and evaluated on both clean and perturbed sequences.

Controlled representation experiment

A natural question is whether transfer performance is preserved when motion attributes (e.g., direction and velocity) are encoded in a more entangled, high-dimensional space. To test this, we designed an alternative pretraining protocol that jointly learns direction and position, yielding a high-dimensional mosaic representation (effective dimensionality 7.7 measured by participation ratio). The same manifold visualizations and representation analyses were performed. While this network achieves comparable decoding accuracy on the synthetic pretraining task, it exhibits markedly reduced transferability to real action recognition, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Hierarchical clustering method

We analyze module representations using the hierarchical clustering method74. The processed video st1 is taken for analysis. These features are transformed by their respective modules into high-dimensional representations. To facilitate analysis, we apply t-SNE to project these representations into a low-dimensional space. We then compute pairwise distances between all data points to form a distance kernel matrix, which serves as input for bottom-up hierarchical clustering. The resulting dendrogram provides a hierarchical structure of the data, allowing us to interpret relationships between instances at different granularity.

C3D and ViViT networks

To benchmark our system, we implement two representative video architectures.

ResNet-50-based C3D

Our 3D CNN backbone is based on an inflated ResNet-5075, where 2D convolutions are extended to 3D. Specifically, the first layer applies a 3D convolution with a kernel size of 7 × 7 × 7 and stride (1, 2, 2), followed by 3D batch normalization, ReLU activation, and a 3 × 3 × 3 max-pooling layer. All weights are initialized using Kaiming initialization. We train the model on both the DVS gesture and RGB rehabilitation datasets for 30 epochs using the Adam optimizer (initial learning rate 10−4, halved every 10 epochs; weight decay 10−5) with a batch size of 16.

ViViT

We implement a vanilla Video Vision Transformer (ViViT) following the “Factorized Encoder" variant proposed by Arnab et al.18, designed to efficiently capture spatio-temporal patterns in video data. Each input video is first divided into non-overlapping tublets using a 3D convolution with kernel and stride size of 2 × 16 × 16, and each tublet is then linearly projected into a 1024-dimensional embedding. A typical video clip is thus transformed into a sequence of 1536 tokens, to which learnable positional embeddings are added to preserve the spatiotemporal order. These token embeddings are then processed by a 12-layer factorized transformer encoder. Each encoder layer consists of pre-normalization LayerNorm, 12-head multi-head self-attention, and a feedforward MLP with GELU activation. The model is trained for 50 epochs on both DVS gesture and RGB rehabilitation action datasets with a batch size of 8 using the AdamW optimizer (learning rate 5 × 10−5, weight decay 10−4).
