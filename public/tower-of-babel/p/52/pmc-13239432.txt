Decreasing patient appointment waiting days for ultrasound diagnosis in Saint Peter Specialized Hospital: a quality improvement project
Authors: Dawit Dagne Moges, Dawit Dereje Leuleberehan, Zenebe Shumeye Dilo, Assefa Tesfaye Menerba, Ibrahim Heyredin Awoll, Biniam Yohannes Wotango

AbstractBackgroundUltrasound examination turnaround time refers to the duration from when a clinician orders the test to when results are reported. In many public hospitals, limited workforce capacity, inefficient workflows and unstructured scheduling contribute to prolonged waiting times for diagnostic imaging. Timely ultrasound diagnosis is essential for appropriate clinical decision-making and patient flow. Introduction

Problem description

Prolonged waiting times for ultrasound examinations are a common challenge in public hospitals and contribute to delayed diagnosis, prolonged hospital stays, patient dissatisfaction and suboptimal clinical outcomes. In diagnostic imaging services, inefficiencies in workflow and scheduling can exacerbate the mismatch between demand and available capacity.1 At Saint Peter Specialized Hospital, the backlog of ultrasound appointments exceeded 28 days in early 2022, resulting in overcrowded waiting areas and delayed diagnoses.

Project context

Saint Peter Specialized Hospital is a public hospital serving a large urban and rural population in Ethiopia. During the project period, the radiology department operated with three radiologists, two ultrasound technologists and two functional ultrasound machines, serving both outpatient and inpatient populations, including emergency referrals. Prior to the intervention, there was no structured appointment system; patients queued physically, staff start times were inconsistent and emergency cases frequently displaced scheduled patients. As a result, effective operational hours averaged only 4 hours per day despite nominal full-day staffing.

Problem statement

From March 2022 to June 2022, radiology department reports indicated that patients were scheduled for ultrasound examinations with an average waiting period of over 28 days. The prolonged appointment time negatively impacted timely diagnosis and treatment, deemed unacceptable for timely care, necessitating a structured QI intervention.

Aim statement

The project aimed to reduce ultrasound waiting times from a median of 28 days to less than 5 days within 4 months (August–November 2022).

Assessment of problem and analysis of causes

A root cause analysis (RCA) was conducted through multidisciplinary meetings involving radiologists, technologists, clerks and hospital management. The key contributors identified were the following:

Workforce capacity: Three radiologists and two technologists were responsible for more than 100 daily ultrasound requests.

Operational inefficiency: Work frequently started after 10:00 AM, with frequent interruptions and average effective working time limited to approximately 4 hours per day.

Absence of structured scheduling: Patients queued physically, leading to duplication, inequitable access and inefficient use of staff time.

Interventions

Workflow optimisation

A clear duty roster was introduced to ensure a standardised start time of 8:00 AM. Supervisors monitored adherence, and peer accountability was encouraged through regular team meetings. Mid-day interruptions were minimised, increasing effective operational hours from 4 to 7 hours per day.

Productivity enhancement

Standardised ultrasound examination protocols were introduced to reduce variability in scan duration. Clerks prepared patient documentation in advance to minimise downtime between scans. Staff were rotated between high-volume and complex cases to balance workload and reduce fatigue.

Onsite ultrasound for emergency cases (POCUS)

A portable ultrasound machine was assigned to the intensive care unit and emergency department. This intervention prevented urgent cases from interrupting scheduled outpatient services in the main radiology unit.

Scheduling system

The existing open queuing system was replaced with a structured, time-slot-based booking register. Patients were assigned specific appointment times, reducing overcrowding, duplication and inequitable access. Although paper-based, the system functioned similarly to simplified appointment scheduling models suited to low-resource settings.

Intervention design

Using the model for improvement, four sequential PDSA cycles were conducted: Cycle 1 tested punctual start times; Cycle 2 piloted extended effective working hours; Cycle 3 implemented point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) for emergency cases; and Cycle 4 refined and stabilised the structured scheduling system.

Results

Operational hours increased from 4 to 7 hours per day. Radiologist productivity increased from an average of 10 to 48 scans per day, while technologist productivity increased from 18 to 65 scans per day. Median waiting time decreased progressively across PDSA cycles, from 28 days at baseline to ≤5 days by Cycle 3 and was maintained through Cycle 4. The appointment backlog was reduced from approximately 380 patients to fewer than 80 by the end of the project period.

Lessons learned

Transparent communication and regular feedback meetings were critical to building trust and engagement. Staff initially expressed concern about longer working hours and increased throughput; however, resistance diminished as improvements in patient flow and reduced overcrowding became evident. Motivation was non-financial and relied on recognition, shared ownership of results and visible patient benefit. Continuous monitoring reinforced accountability and supported sustained adherence to new practices.

Limitations

This project had a relatively short follow-up period of 4 months, limiting assessment of long-term sustainability. Formal quantitative measurement of staff satisfaction was not conducted. While no external funding was received, this did not affect the implementation of core interventions but limited rapid expansion of equipment capacity.

Sustainability

Improvements were embedded into routine departmental operations through standardised duty rosters, structured scheduling practices and continued use of POCUS for emergency cases. Leadership involvement and peer accountability supported adherence. Sustaining gains in this low-resource setting will require institutionalisation of scheduling practices, continued staff recognition and incremental investment in additional ultrasound capacity.

Conclusion

Through structured root cause analysis, iterative PDSA cycles and workflow redesign, ultrasound waiting times were reduced from 28 days to ≤5 days without additional funding. This project demonstrates that meaningful improvements in diagnostic service delivery are achievable in resource-limited settings through staff engagement and organisational change.

Discussion

This quality improvement project demonstrated that substantial reductions in ultrasound appointment waiting time can be achieved through organisational and process-level interventions, even in a resource-constrained public hospital. Reducing the median waiting time from 28 days to ≤5 days, increasing daily scan productivity and shrinking the backlog from 380 to 80 patients underscore how workflow redesign, scheduling discipline and staff engagement can unlock latent system capacity without major financial investment. These findings align with a growing body of evidence suggesting that inefficiencies in diagnostic services are often driven more by process failures than by absolute shortages of equipment or personnel.1 2

Similar improvements have been reported in recent studies. Zhang et al3 described a patient-centred, real-time dynamic ultrasound appointment system that reduced waiting times by 78%–90% while improving resource utilisation and reducing staffing pressure.3 Although our project did not use advanced digital scheduling software, the introduction of structured appointment slots and elimination of physical queuing produced comparable effects, highlighting that even low-technology solutions can yield meaningful gains when thoughtfully implemented. Other studies from middle-income settings have likewise shown that simple appointment systems and workload levelling can significantly reduce diagnostic delays and overcrowding.4

The use of POCUS for emergency and inpatient cases was another critical contributor to success. By diverting urgent cases away from the main ultrasound schedule, service disruptions were minimised, and planned appointments could proceed as scheduled. Evidence supporting the role of POCUS in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is expanding. Pathak et al5 demonstrated that clinicians trained through a blended learning POCUS programme in Ghana used ultrasound frequently in routine practice, often providing the only available imaging for underserved patients.5 Similar findings from sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia indicate that POCUS can improve diagnostic timeliness, reduce unnecessary referrals and enhance patient flow when integrated into routine care pathways.6

Beyond technical interventions, this project highlights the importance of staff engagement and process ownership. Standardising start times, enforcing duty rosters and reorganising task flows parallel observations from studies conducted during the COVID-19 period, where efficient scheduling and attendance systems improved productivity with minimal technological input.7 However, sustaining such gains remains challenging. While short POCUS training courses and workflow interventions have been shown to improve skills, confidence and short-term performance,8 evidence on long-term sustainability is limited.9

Embedding new practices into routine operations, ensuring visible leadership commitment, maintaining feedback mechanisms and gradually expanding resources appear essential for sustaining improvements. Collectively, these findings suggest that pragmatic, context-sensitive quality improvement strategies can meaningfully reduce diagnostic waiting times and may be transferable to other hospitals facing similar constraints, provided local adaptation and continuous monitoring are prioritised.
