Infant Caregiving as a Resilience Factor for Postpartum Substance Misuse: A Scoping Review
Authors: Linnea B. Linde-Krieger, Arushi M. Chalke, McKenna Nelson, Lisa M. Grisham, Lela Rankin, Ann E. Bigelow, Xinger Xia, Stephanie Mallahan, Stacey Tecot, Alicia M. Allen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The postpartum is a period of profound psychological and physiological change characterized by heightened vulnerability to return to substance misuse for individuals with a history of substance use disorder. Much existing research has focused on how postpartum substance use disrupts caregiving behaviors and adversely affects infant outcomes (i.e., risk frameworks). Comparatively little attention has been given to the potential for caregiving behaviors to serve as protective factors against postpartum substance use (i.e., resilience framework). This scoping review synthesized empirical studies examining caregiving behaviors as potential predictors of postpartum substance use outcomes, as thus far the inverse association has received greater attention in the literature. Following a systematic search of PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, and Scopus (2000–2024), 42 studies met inclusion criteria, most of which assessed breastfeeding as a predictor of maternal substance use. Breastfeeding demonstrated consistent protective associations, particularly against postpartum nicotine use, with 28 of 30 studies reporting reduced smoking among breastfeeding mothers. Limited evidence also suggested protective effects of breastfeeding on alcohol, cannabis, and opioid use. Preliminary studies of other caregiving behaviors (i.e., skin-to-skin care, babywearing) indicated potential reductions in substance use urges and cigarette smoking. In several studies, contextual moderators (e.g., partner smoking, socioeconomic status) influenced the strength of these associations. Findings support the hypothesis that caregiving behaviors, especially those involving high physical touch, may reduce risk for postpartum substance misuse through neuroendocrine, psychological, and social mechanisms. However, research remains limited in scope and diversity. Expanding investigations beyond breastfeeding to include varied caregiving practices and caregiver populations could inform strengths-based, family-centered interventions that promote recovery and family well-being.

The postpartum period is a sensitive window of psychological, physiological, and behavioral change for new parents (Tully et al., 2017), many of whom are navigating the challenges of recovery from childbirth while adjusting to the responsibilities of infant care. For individuals with a history of substance use disorder (SUD), this transitional period poses a high risk for return to substance misuse (Amiri & Saadat, 2021; Campbell et al., 2025; Eitel et al., 2024; Meernik & Goldstein, 2015). Although substance misuse decreases significantly during pregnancy for individuals with and without SUD (Kipling, 2024; Peltier et al., 2022), this decrease is followed by a precipitous increase in maternal substance use within the first year after delivery (Bruzelius & Martins, 2022; Forray et al., 2015). Much of the existing research on SUD and addiction during the postpartum period has focused on how substance use disrupts maternal caregiving behaviors and adversely affects infant outcomes (i.e., caregiving risk frameworks; Cataldo et al., 2019). Comparatively little attention has been given to the inverse relationship; namely, the potential for caregiving behaviors to influence maternal postpartum outcomes, including substance misuse trajectories. Identifying whether and how caregiving behaviors may serve as protective factors against postpartum substance use (i.e., caregiving resilience framework; Gavidia-payne et al., 2015; Reupert et al., 2017) can inform the development of effective, strengths-based interventions to improve outcomes for both caregivers with SUD and their infants.

Maternal caregiving in early infancy involves a range of behaviors including feeding, skin-to-skin care, responding to infant cues, and engaging in physical closeness and nurturing. These activities support infant physical and emotional development and have the potential to enhance maternal well-being (Britton et al., 2006; Cong et al., 2021; Rankin & Bigelow, 2025). Biological and psychological feedback loops initiated by caregiving, such as the release of oxytocin during breastfeeding or skin-to-skin care, can modulate stress and foster maternal-infant bonding and physiological co-regulation (Feldman et al., 2007; Moberg et al., 2020), which may in turn reduce the risk of substance use (Strathearn et al., 2019). Caregiving may also promote a sense of purpose and maternal identity that helps buffer against the adverse effects of postpartum stressors that trigger craving and return to substance use (Adams et al., 2021; To et al., 2022).

Whereas prior research has established associations between postpartum stress, depression, and increased risk for substance misuse (Allen et al., 2019, 2020; Choi et al., 2024; Guintivano et al., 2018), less is known about how maternal behaviors that promote closeness and infant engagement might mitigate that risk. Some studies suggest that breastfeeding is associated with a lower likelihood of postpartum return to smoking (Drehmer et al., 2020; Gyllstrom et al., 2012; Kendzor et al., 2010), though less is known about the role of breastfeeding in craving and misuse of other substances. Extant findings suggest caregiving behaviors, particularly those that promote physical closeness and bonding, may represent an underexplored intervention pathway for maternal recovery and relapse prevention. The putative mechanisms underlying this relationship include physiological (e.g., hormonal regulation), psychological (e.g., enhanced maternal self-efficacy), and social (e.g., alignment with pro-health norms) dimensions, all of which merit closer examination.

Despite promising theoretical frameworks and emergent empirical findings, the literature on caregiving and substance use remains fragmented, with few studies assessing infant caregiving behaviors as predictors or moderators of substance misuse outcomes. Most studies have adopted a risk orientation (e.g., documenting how maternal substance use impairs parenting) rather than examining caregiving as a source of resilience, thus limiting our understanding of the full spectrum of interactions between parenting and substance misuse. Moreover, the caregiving behaviors that have been studied are largely limited to breastfeeding, with relatively little empirical attention given to other potentially protective practices such as skin-to-skin care, babywearing, shared caregiving with partners, and responsiveness to infant cues (Bystrova et al., 2009; Grisham et al., 2023; Renbarger, 2024).

Maternal drug overdose deaths represent the leading cause of pregnancy-related mortality in the United States (Azad et al., 2026; Margerison et al., 2022), with most fatalities occurring in the late postpartum period (B. Han et al. 2024a). Population-level studies find that substance misuse is common in the postpartum, with over 25% of postpartum individuals reporting regular tobacco use, unhealthy alcohol consumption, prescription opioid misuse, or other illicit substance use (Stewart, 2023). Given the well-documented consequences of substance misuse for maternal and child health (Bruzelius & Martins, 2022; Conradt et al., 2023; Maher et al., 2024), identifying modifiable behavioral factors that may promote recovery and/or reduce misuse is a public health priority. Targeting caregiving behaviors as part of relapse prevention efforts offers an opportunity to design family-centered interventions that align with caregivers’ intrinsic motivation to care for their infant. Rather than seeking only to reduce or eliminate negative behaviors, such interventions can capitalize on caregiver strengths and promote positive behavioral change. Supporting caregivers with SUD may have intergenerational benefits by reducing early exposure of infants to parental substance misuse and other adversities and promoting secure attachment relationships that buffer against developmental risk (Suchman et al., 2006).

To address gaps in the literature, the current scoping review synthesizes empirical literature examining the influence of caregiving behaviors on substance use outcomes during the postpartum period. While substance use is most often framed as a disruptor of positive caregiving, this review explores whether and how caregiving practices may serve as protective factors against postpartum substance (mis)use. Grounded in a strengths-based, recovery-oriented perspective, this approach may inform clinical practice, policy, and research by highlighting the potential for caregiving practices to support maternal health and family well-being. We restrict this review to studies that measure caregiving behavior as an independent variable (i.e., measured prior to or concurrently with substance use), rather than an outcome, to identify whether caregiver-infant interaction may shape postpartum substance use trajectories. In doing so, this work contributes to a growing body of literature that conceptualizes parenting as a potential source of resilience and recovery (Doty et al., 2017; Gavidia-payne et al., 2015; Reupert et al., 2017). Ultimately, this synthesis seeks to bridge the gap between research and practice by providing healthcare providers, policymakers, and support systems with evidence-based insights into the role of caregiving behaviors in postpartum substance misuse prevention.

Method

This scoping review identified published empirical studies that evaluated concurrent or prospective associations between infant caregiving behaviors and postpartum substance use outcomes (e.g., use, craving). The search strategy was developed in consultation with a health sciences librarian, and the search methodology and results reporting followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) Checklist (Tricco et al., 2018). We reviewed studies published between 2000 and 2024 and limited the search to studies that were published in English and included human participants. We applied the following search criteria in the databases PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, and Scopus.

Two primary search constructs were defined: (1) infant caregiving behaviors and (2) substance use and addiction-related terms. For each construct, we combined keywords and subject headings using the Boolean operator OR, and the two constructs were combined using the operator AND. The infant caregiving construct included the following terms related to caregiving, physical contact, and caregiver-infant interaction: breastfeeding, nurturing care, skin-to-skin, kangaroo care, babywearing, infant/baby carrying, infant/baby holding, consoling, feeding, playing, cleaning, bathing, maternal care, paternal care. The substance use construct included the terms: addiction, substance use, substance misuse, substance use disorder, drug dependency, drug abuse, withdrawal, craving, lapse, relapse, alcohol, opioids, heroin, cocaine, cannabis, marijuana, amphetamine, smoking, nicotine, cigarette, and prescription pain medication.

Database searches also included controlled vocabulary and database-specific indexing terms to increase search sensitivity. This approach captured potentially relevant studies that did not use terminology identical to the search terms in titles or abstracts. For example, in PubMed/MEDLINE, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) included “Skin Physiological Phenomena” combined with “Touch” to capture studies of physical contact. In Embase, the Emtree terms “skin to skin contact” and “kangaroo care” were included. In PsycINFO, subject terms included Substance Use Disorder, Drug Use, Alcohol Use, Smoking, Relapse, Maternal Behavior, Parenting, Infant Care, and Mother-Child Relations. Further, we applied phrase searching to multi-word terms (e.g., “maternal care,” “skin to skin,” “infant carrying,” “substance use”) where supported by database syntax, and truncation symbols captured variations in terminology (e.g., care* to retrieve records for care, caring, caregiver, and caregiving; addic* to retrieve addiction, addictive). The initial database searches targeted article titles, abstracts, and keywords to balance search sensitivity and specificity. A protocol for this review has not been previously published or pre-registered.

Across the four databases, searches yielded 2666 records. We applied a four-level review procedure to determine article eligibility and extract findings (Fig. 1). First, after removal of duplicate records (n = 458), 2208 articles were screened by title and abstract by two assessors, and 2131 were excluded based on the following criteria: did not assess infant caregiving concurrently or prospectively to substance use; reported on non-human research; or not empirical research (e.g., clinical guidelines). If either assessor indicated that a record met initial eligibility criteria (i.e., an empirical study that evaluated concurrent or prospective associations between infant caregiving and postpartum substance use outcomes), the article moved to full text review (step two). Full texts of 77 articles were reviewed for eligibility and data were charted by two assessors using a form that was developed and tested by the research team. Data extracted from each article during step two included publication information (e.g., author last names, publication year), study aims, sample characteristics and demographics, study location and data collection period, study design, substance use and caregiving constructs examined, measurement methods, statistical outcomes and effect sizes (e.g., odds ratios), and qualitative themes/key findings where applicable. In step two, 36 articles were excluded because they did not measure caregiving prior to or concurrently with substance use or did not report postpartum outcomes. In step three, the remaining articles (n = 41) were examined for completeness and consistency with inclusion criteria, and any discrepancies in eligibility determination were resolved by the lead author. Finally, in step four, the reference lists of included articles were reviewed to identify additional potentially eligible articles. This process identified six studies that had not been previously screened. A full text review was completed on these articles, and one met inclusion criteria for the review. In total, 42 studies met the inclusion criteria and were retained in the final synthesis.

Results

Of the 42 studies included in this review, 40 examined breastfeeding as the primary caregiving activity and its relationship to postpartum substance misuse. Most studies focused on a single caregiving activity in relation to a specific substance; only four studies examined more than one relationship (Alshaarawy et al., 2021; Logan et al., 2016; Parackal et al., 2007; Phillips et al., 2012). Use of nicotine via combustible cigarette smoking or e-cigarettes was the most frequently studied outcome (30 studies [all studies evaluated combustible cigarette smoking; 2 studies additionally evaluated other nicotine use, e.g., e-cigarettes, snus]) followed by alcohol (8 studies), cannabis (4 studies), and opioids (2 studies; see Table 1). Only two studies investigated caregiving practices beyond breastfeeding: one evaluated skin-to-skin care (Phillips et al., 2012) and one examined babywearing (Rankin et al., 2023). All studies focused on maternal outcomes, with no studies examining the impact of caregiving on substance misuse among fathers, partners, or other caregivers. Breastfeeding and Nicotine Use

Across 30 studies, breastfeeding was associated with reduced risk of postpartum nicotine use in a large majority. Specifically, 28 studies found a protective association, wherein women who initiated and maintained breastfeeding were less likely to return to nicotine use during the postpartum or reported less frequent nicotine use than those who did not. Most of these studies reported moderate to strong effects (e.g., ORs ≈ 2–9 for abstinence among breastfeeding women relative to non-breastfeeding women). Studies that adjusted for sociodemographic and psychosocial covariates, including maternal age, education, race/ethnicity, income, marital status, parity, pre-pregnancy smoking, and partner/household smoking, found largely consistent protective associations across unadjusted and covariate-adjusted models (see Table 2). Only two studies reported null results (Di Napoli et al., 2006; Simmons et al., 2014).

Research on breastfeeding and nicotine use primarily relied on maternal self-report, though studies differed in how breastfeeding and substance use outcomes were defined. Measures of nicotine use included maternal reports of current smoking status (binary yes/no), cigarettes smoked per day, seven-day point prevalence abstinence, and return to smoking following prenatal cessation. Only six studies confirmed maternal responses using biochemical confirmation (Alshaarawy et al., 2021; Issany et al., 2022; Joseph et al., 2017; Kendzor et al., 2010; Letourneau et al., 2007; Ratner et al., 2000). Breastfeeding measures included maternal reports of breastfeeding initiation, current breastfeeding status (binary yes/no), breastfeeding duration, exclusive breastfeeding versus combination feeding with infant formula, and timing of weaning.

The included studies also varied in the timing of both breastfeeding and substance use outcomes. Some studies assessed breastfeeding and cigarette smoking concurrently at a single postpartum timepoint (e.g., current breastfeeding status and current smoking status at 2–6 months postpartum), whereas others prospectively examined whether breastfeeding predicted later smoking across follow-up periods ranging from several weeks to five years postpartum. In total, 11 of 30 studies measured breastfeeding prospectively to smoking outcomes (see Table 2). These prospective studies generally supported longer-term protective associations, with several reporting that longer breastfeeding duration predicted delayed return to smoking, prolonged abstinence, or lower likelihood of smoking at later postpartum assessments (Caine et al., 2012; Issany et al., 2022; Kendzor et al., 2010; Logan et al., 2016, 2017; Mumford & Liu, 2016; Murakami et al., 2023; Shisler et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2022).

Protective Associations

Although the studies varied in their timing of assessments, definitions of return to smoking, and criteria for breastfeeding, protective effects remained relatively consistent across study designs, including population-based surveys, prospective cohorts, and qualitative investigations. Several longitudinal studies demonstrated that longer breastfeeding duration was associated with longer periods of smoking abstinence (Logan et al., 2016, 2017; Mumford & Liu, 2016; Murakami et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022). In the study with the longest prospective follow-up, Mumford and Liu (2016) followed a nationally representative sample of U.S. mothers (N = 9,050) from 9 months postpartum through 5–6 years postpartum. This study classified participant mothers into five trajectories of nicotine use or non-use, including nonsmokers (70.5% of the sample), persistent smokers (11.7%), temporary quitters (9.4%); delayed initiators (5.1%); or pregnancy-inspired quitters (3.3%). Breastfeeding for > 6 months arose as a protective factor against classification in nicotine use trajectories (persistent smoker: aOR = 0.31, 95% CI 0.19–0.52; temporary quitter: aOR = 0.52, 95% CI 0.31–0.88; delayed initiator: aOR = 0.54, 95% CI 0.30–0.96).

Additional population-level data similarly indicated that breastfeeding mothers reported lower rates of return to smoking compared to non-breastfeeding mothers (Alshaarawy et al., 2021; Gyllstrom et al., 2012; Lauria et al., 2012; Rockhill et al., 2016). For example, a study of postpartum women in the United States (n = 1332) between 2001 and 2018 found that the prevalence of nicotine use among new mothers (< 2 years postpartum) was stable over time as measured via self-report and serum cotinine levels (annual percent change = -1.3; 95% CI = -8.6- 6.7; p = 0.7; Alshaarawy et al., 2021). New mothers who reported breastfeeding were significantly less likely to use nicotine (7.4%, 95% CI: 4.9–10.9%) versus those who were not breastfeeding (25.7%, 95% CI: 22.3–29.3%).

Protective associations were also identified across diverse samples and time points. For example, Caine et al. (2012) found that women who had not initiated breastfeeding by hospital discharge were more likely to return to smoking within three months postpartum. DiSantis et al. (2010) observed that longer breastfeeding during the first postpartum month was associated with delayed return to use, while Drehmer et al. (2020) and Gyllstrom et al. (2012) reported lower return to use rates among breastfeeding mothers in pediatric and community settings. Harmer and Memon (2013) and Simonelli and Velicer (2012) similarly documented a reduced risk of return to smoking by six and eight weeks postpartum, respectively, among breastfeeding mothers. Finally, Parackal et al. (2007) surveyed postpartum (6–24 months) women in New Zealand and found that women who were breastfeeding were less likely to smoke than non-breastfeeding women (13% vs. 27%). Breastfeeding predicted significantly lower odds of smoking in this sample even after controlling for demographic covariates.

Contextual Influences in the Breastfeeding and Nicotine use Association

The influence of contextual moderators was also noted. For example, marital status shaped the relationship between breastfeeding and risk for return to nicotine use in a secondary analysis of the Millennium Cohort Study (n = 1,829; Prady et al., 2012). In this study, participants were stratified by marital status, and return to nicotine use at nine months postpartum was regressed on contextual and behavioral variables, including never breastfeeding and breastfeeding duration. Single women had the highest return to use rates overall (66%), and breastfeeding variables did not significantly predict return to nicotine use among single women. In contrast, having never breastfed was associated with greater likelihood of return to nicotine use for married (OR: 1.61; 95% CI: 1.00-2.59) and cohabitating (OR: 1.67; 95% CI: 1.00-2.78) women, and breastfeeding for only a few days was associated with increased likelihood of return to use only among married women (OR: 2.38; 95% CI: 1.33–4.24). Further, household smoking exposure was identified as a risk factor in several investigations. Studies by Murakami et al. (2023), Kocatas et al. (2020), and Orton et al. (2022) found that partner or household smoking elevated postpartum return to smoking, even among breastfeeding mothers. Orton et al. (2022) additionally observed that breastfeeding and e-cigarette use during pregnancy were inversely associated with return to smoking during the postpartum, but household smoking strongly elevated return to smoking risk (aOR 11.1, 95% CI: 2.47 to 50.2).

Qualitative and intervention studies provided meaning to some of the quantitative findings. Joseph et al. (2017) reported that many women avoided smoking to continue breastfeeding, although others chose not to breastfeed so they could resume smoking. Kocatas et al. (2020) found stress, breastfeeding termination, and partner smoking were key triggers for return to smoking. Ratner et al. (2000) examined factors associated with postpartum return to smoking in a randomized controlled trial of a nurse-delivered postpartum relapse prevention intervention. Women who continued breastfeeding to 12 months postpartum were significantly less likely to be daily smokers (OR: 0.38; 95% CI: 0.19–0.77). In another intervention study, Phillips et al. (2012) showed that promoting skin-to-skin contact alongside breastfeeding support reduced return to nicotine use rates at 8 weeks postpartum.

Null Findings

Despite the overall protective trend of breastfeeding on return to nicotine use, two studies reported null results (Di Napoli et al., 2006; Simmons et al., 2014). One longitudinal study of pregnant/postpartum women in the United States (n = 504) found no statistically significant relationship between breastfeeding at one month postpartum and cigarette smoking at 12 months postpartum follow-up (Simmons et al., 2014). Di Napoli et al. (2006) found that breastfeeding duration (but not initiation) was associated with lower rates of postpartum nicotine use among women in Italy (n = 534; aHR: 3.00; 95% CI: 1.78–5.06), though the association was not significant once parental education levels were considered, and the interaction between smoking status and parental education level was also nonsignificant (p = 0.17).

In sum, the evidence indicates that breastfeeding is generally protective against postpartum return to nicotine use, though this effect may be moderated by social and environmental factors.

Breastfeeding and Alcohol

Eight studies investigated breastfeeding in relation to maternal postpartum alcohol use. All eight measured breastfeeding concurrently with alcohol use. Six identified protective associations, while two reported mixed findings. Murakami et al. (2021) analyzed data from 11,031 women who participated in the Tohoku Medical Megabank Project Birth and Three-Generation Cohort Study in Japan. At one year postpartum, women who reported not breastfeeding were more than six times more likely to engage in hazardous drinking, defined as ethanol intake of ≥ 20 g/day. This finding remained consistent when controlling for education and income levels (OR: 6.72; 95% CI: 5.21–8.67). Similarly, Jagodzinski and Fleming (2007) surveyed women (n = 381) at approximately 14 weeks postpartum who reported frequent and/or risky drinking behaviors before pregnancy. Findings identified a protective effect of breastfeeding, in that breastfeeding within the prior seven days was associated with lower odds of risky drinking behaviors (defined as two occasions within a month where four or more alcoholic drinks were consumed; OR: 0.30, 95% CI: 0.2–0.5). Two additional studies found that breastfeeding was associated with lower odds of moderate-to-heavy alcohol consumption during the postpartum period. Breslow et al. (2007) found that breastfeeding women were less likely to consume two or more alcoholic drinks per week or to increase their alcohol use by 3 months postpartum when compared to non-breastfeeding women (12.0% vs. 17.4%, p = 0.03). Similarly, in a 2024 cross-sectional study (Prior et al., 2024), currently breastfeeding mothers had significantly lower odds of heavy episodic drinking (i.e., ≥ 5 drinks per occasion; aOR = 0.29, 95% CI 0.10–0.80, p < 0.05).

Schroeder et al. (2019) surveyed women (n = 51) before pregnancy and at two months postpartum and found that alcohol and other undefined substance use significantly decreased (i.e., from 59% to 9%) in those who were breastfeeding as compared to no change in use for those who did not attempt to breastfeed (p < 0.001). A limitation of this study was that use of alcohol and drugs was combined into a single variable and drug use was not delineated by category (stimulant, depressant, opioid, etc.), making the interpretation of the results specific to alcohol unclear. Logan et al. (2016) examined concurrent alcohol use and breastfeeding behaviors, including breastfeeding initiation, predominant breastfeeding, and any breastfeeding, among postpartum women (n = 970) in the SPATZ Health Study cohort in Germany. Breastfeeding non-initiation (HR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.19–0.94) and breastfeeding termination over time (HR range: 0.35 − 0.44) were significantly less likely among mothers who remained abstinent from drinking alcohol through 6 months postpartum. In contrast, breastfeeding termination was associated with return to drinking alcohol by 6 months postpartum (HR 1.63, 95% CI: 1.10–2.40).

Two studies found mixed evidence for the protective effect of breastfeeding on alcohol use. Parackal et al. (2007) performed a community-based cross-sectional survey of postpartum (6–24 months) mothers (n = 318) from three cities on New Zealand’s South Island. The authors aimed to assess the prevalence of smoking and alcohol consumption and the influence of sociodemographic factors. The survey found a lower percentage of alcohol consumption in those who were breastfeeding compared to those who were not breastfeeding or were pregnant. While nearly 80% of pregnant and non-breastfeeding participants consumed alcohol (95% CI: 74–85%), 66% of breastfeeding mothers consumed alcohol (95% CI: 54–76%, p < 0.001). However, once sociodemographic variables were considered, breastfeeding was no longer significantly associated with alcohol consumption (p = 0.075), suggesting contextual factors may explain the observed differences. Factors associated with a higher likelihood of alcohol consumption included current cigarette smoking, being Caucasian, having a higher education level, and higher household income. Finally, Maloney et al. (2011) studied a subsample of women who were pregnant and/or breastfeeding in the prior 12 months (n = 915) from a nationally representative survey of Australian households. While 94% of breastfeeding women reported consuming less alcohol compared with when they were not pregnant or breastfeeding, the study also found that the total number of weeks women were pregnant and/or breastfeeding was positively associated with alcohol use during breastfeeding (aOR = 1.06, 95% CI 1.04–1.08), such that the longer a woman had been breastfeeding the more likely she was to report any alcohol use. The authors suggest that this finding may indicate that mothers perceive alcohol use as safer as infants age, or that less frequent breastfeeding over time may make drinking more acceptable.

Overall, results suggest that breastfeeding may reduce alcohol use postpartum, but the association appears to be sensitive to population characteristics and confounding influences.

Breastfeeding and Cannabis

Four studies examined the effects of breastfeeding on cannabis use with mixed results. All four measured breastfeeding concurrently with cannabis use. The first was a population-based study from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey database between 2001 and 2018, in which mothers with children two years old or younger (n = 1332) provided self-reports on cannabis and alcohol use (Alshaarawy et al., 2021). Cannabis findings included a significant increase in use over time, with an average annual percent change of 23.7% (95% CI: 4.8–46.0; p = 0.02). When stratified by breastfeeding status, breastfeeding mothers were less likely to use cannabis (5.7%; 95% CI: 3.2–10.0%) compared to non-breastfeeding mothers (12.2%; 95% CI: 9.3–15.9%). Similarly, a cross-sectional survey of postpartum mothers (n = 3,207) from the 2014–2015 Colorado Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) found that length of breastfeeding was inversely related to cannabis use (Crume et al., 2018). Postpartum cannabis use was higher among women who breastfed for less than 8 weeks (10.2%, 95% CI 7.1%–14.6%) than among those who breastfed for ≥ 9 weeks (4.1%, 95% CI 3.2%–5.2%).

In contrast, a 2017 analysis of PRAMS data from seven U.S. states (n = 4,604) found no significant association between breastfeeding initiation or continuation beyond 12 weeks postpartum and cannabis use (Coy et al., 2021). Roughly 5.5% of women reported using cannabis postpartum, and nearly half of those were still breastfeeding at the time of the survey. However, maternal beliefs strongly influenced both breastfeeding and cannabis use behaviors. Among women who used cannabis postpartum, those who believed it was safe to use while breastfeeding were considerably more likely to initiate breastfeeding (adjusted prevalence ratio = 1.22; 95% CI: 1.04, 1.43) and maintain it for over 12 weeks (adjusted prevalence ratio = 1.57; 95% CI:1.08, 2.27) compared to those who believed it was unsafe.

A qualitative study from Washington state (Kiel et al., 2023) further demonstrated the complexities of decision-making among postpartum mothers with a history of cannabis use. In semi-structured interviews, participants who self-reported marijuana use during pregnancy (n = 15), were asked about their decision-making around using cannabis postpartum, including the role of breastfeeding and lactation. While participants generally felt comfortable with their decisions to use cannabis during pregnancy, there was concern about possible short and long-term effects on their children, particularly when breastfeeding. Some mothers chose to decrease cannabis use or formula feed their infant so the infant would not be exposed via breastmilk. Others chose to continue using cannabis but attempted to minimize exposure to the infant by adjusting their substance use or breastfeeding practices. For example, some mothers timed their cannabis use (e.g., used at night after breastfeeding when the infant would sleep for a long stretch) or chose to pump and discard their breastmilk after cannabis use to mitigate infant exposure. Participants cited conflicting or absent provider guidance and relied instead on advice from peers or online sources, which made it challenging for the mothers to make informed decisions.

Overall, the limited evidence suggests that while breastfeeding mothers may use cannabis at lower rates than non-breastfeeding mothers, beliefs about safety and lack of clear medical guidance substantially influence behavior.

Breastfeeding and Opioids

Two studies examined the effect of breastfeeding on perinatal opioid use, and both found a protective effect. Ray-Griffith et al. (2021) retrospectively examined women (n = 64) with opioid use in pregnancy treated with buprenorphine who were in a treatment retention group versus a dropout group. Treatment retention was defined as attending a postpartum appointment within 10 to 14 weeks after delivery. Breastfeeding rates were significantly higher in the treatment retention group (66.7%) as compared to the dropout group (31.3%, p = 0.019).

A qualitative longitudinal study with mothers with opioid use disorder identified breastfeeding as one of several protective factors against cravings/urges (Rankin et al., 2023). Rankin et al. (2023) surveyed and interviewed mothers (n = 42) with a history of opioid use disorder around the time of birth, 3 months, and 6 months postpartum. Interviews used open-ended questions to assess use, urges, and bonding. Seven themes of opioid relapse risk and protection were found, with one being breastfeeding. Mothers with no urges more often described breastfeeding as a successful and easy experience, whereas mothers with urges did not breastfeed or had negative or difficult breastfeeding experiences.

These findings, though limited in number, point to breastfeeding as a potential contributor to treatment retention and reduced opioid cravings.

Skin-to-Skin Care

Only one randomized controlled trial assessed skin-to-skin care in relation to substance use (Phillips et al., 2012). This trial provided breastfeeding education and support to two groups, with one group also receiving increased support and education on skin-to-skin care and bonding. Results showed that mothers who received enhanced skin-to-skin care education and bonding support, in addition to breastfeeding assistance, were significantly less likely to return to smoking (81% vs. 46%; p < 0.001) and more likely to continue breastfeeding (86% vs. 21%; p < 0.001) at eight weeks postpartum compared to controls. Though preliminary, these findings suggest skin-to-skin care may indirectly support smoking relapse prevention by strengthening maternal-infant bonding and extending breastfeeding duration.

Babywearing

One quasi-experimental study examined babywearing in relation to maternal urges for substance use (including cannabis, cocaine, prescription stimulants, methamphetamine, inhalants, sedatives, hallucinogens, and street or prescription opioids) and found a protective association (Rankin et al., 2024b). This study assigned mothers with a history of opioid use disorder and their newborns to a babywearing (intervention) group or to a baby rocker (control) group. Consistent babywearing (≥ 45 h per week) was associated with substantially fewer urges to use substances at nine months postpartum compared to no babywearing (i.e., 56.5% of mothers in the consistent babywearing group had no urges to use substances compared to only 8.3% of mothers in the no babywearing group). This result suggests that babywearing may reduce urges to use substances and support maternal recovery, though more research is needed.

Summary

Across the literature, breastfeeding emerged as the most studied and consistently protective caregiving activity, particularly with respect to postpartum nicotine use. Evidence regarding breastfeeding and alcohol, cannabis, and opioid use was more limited but suggested possible protective associations. Preliminary findings for skin-to-skin care and babywearing point to the potential for other caregiving practices, particularly those involving high physical touch, to reduce urges to use substances and, subsequently, prevent postpartum return to misuse. Overall, these results highlight caregiving behaviors as an underexplored but promising avenue for supporting postpartum recovery.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesized 42 studies examining the influence of infant caregiving behaviors on caregiver substance use. Thirty-eight studies reported on a single caregiving-substance use relationship, and four studies reported on more than one relationship. Across the literature, breastfeeding was by far the most studied caregiving activity (40/42 studies) and demonstrated the most consistent protective associations, particularly in relation to postpartum combustible cigarette smoking. In contrast, much less research has been conducted on other caregiving behaviors, such as skin-to-skin care and babywearing, despite preliminary evidence suggesting their potential protective effects. In total, 39 studies showed infant caregiving was associated with decreased substance use or a lower likelihood of return to use during the postpartum. Four studies reported mixed or null effects, and only one study provided evidence that alcohol use, while lower during breastfeeding than pre-pregnancy, was higher as breastfeeding duration increased during the postpartum, such that the longer a woman had been breastfeeding the more likely she was to report alcohol use (Maloney et al., 2011). Together, these findings highlight the need to expand conceptualizations of caregiving beyond risk frameworks to consider caregiving as a potential source of resilience in postpartum SUD recovery. Future studies should expand these findings by including caregivers beyond the maternal role (e.g., partners, fathers) and examining the protective role of diverse caregiving practices for substance use and recovery.

Infant Caregiving and Postpartum Substance Use: Protective Effects and Modifiers

The risk of return to substance misuse during the postpartum period is well-documented (Amiri & Saadat, 2021; Campbell et al., 2025; Eitel et al., 2024; Forray et al., 2015; Meernik & Goldstein, 2015), yet there are few effective, evidence-based strategies for relapse prevention. Our review suggests that caregiving behaviors may shape maternal postpartum substance use trajectories, though the evidence on this is largely underdeveloped. Specifically, breastfeeding appears to reduce the likelihood of postpartum cigarette smoking. Though the studies varied on the timing of measures and the criteria for smoking and breastfeeding, 28 of 30 studies found breastfeeding to be protective against nicotine use. Protective associations were also observed, though less frequently, for alcohol, cannabis, and opioid use. While evidence on caregiving practices beyond breastfeeding is sparse, findings from one randomized controlled trial (Phillips et al., 2012) and one quasi-experimental study (Rankin et al., 2024b) suggest that skin-to-skin care and babywearing may similarly decrease urges to use or reduce relapse risk. Future research on these and other caregiving practices beyond breastfeeding is needed.

Several studies found that associations between infant caregiving and postpartum substance use were sensitive to environmental influence. Specifically, the literature on breastfeeding and substance misuse suggests that sociodemographic and contextual factors, including income, educational attainment, and household exposure to use, can serve as moderators or confounders that diminish the benefits of breastfeeding. These findings highlight the complexity of associations between breastfeeding and postpartum substance misuse and suggest that breastfeeding alone may not be sufficient to prevent return to use in the absence of supportive contextual factors. Mothers’ beliefs about safety may also shape both breastfeeding behavior and substance use. For example, both quantitative (Coy et al., 2021) and qualitative evidence (Kiel et al., 2023) indicate that mothers’ decision making around cannabis use is informed by beliefs about the transfer of cannabis to the infant via breastmilk. Further, some mothers report that they abstain from cigarette smoking during breastfeeding to avoid transfer of nicotine through breastmilk, whereas others choose not to breastfeed in order to resume smoking (Joseph et al., 2017; Kocatas et al., 2020). Importantly, maternal beliefs and perceptions of substance use safety were not a focus of this review, and these terms were not included in our search strategy. Therefore, this review does not provide a comprehensive picture of maternal perceptions of the risks and benefits of postpartum substance use, which have been studied elsewhere (e.g., Boerner et al., 2024). To support mothers to make evidence-informed decisions, it may be helpful for providers to offer targeted counseling and education about cannabis and other substance use during the postpartum period. In future work, the protective role of breastfeeding and other infant caregiving activities should be considered in relation to social and environmental features and maternal beliefs and perceptions that may support or undermine recovery efforts. Potential interaction effects and pathways to postpartum return to use are underdeveloped and warrant empirical attention.

Interpretations and Potential Mechanisms

The dominant interpretation in the reviewed literature is that caregiving, particularly breastfeeding, creates a strong behavioral incentive for abstinence, motivated by the mother’s desire to protect her infant from harm (Joseph et al., 2017; Kocatas et al., 2020). While this behavioral-choice explanation is one possible mechanism, the findings from this synthesis suggest the potential involvement of other biopsychosocial processes. Beyond conscious decision-making, caregiving behaviors may operate through neurobiological and relational processes that reinforce abstinence and recovery. Rapid shifts in levels and patterns of hormones during pregnancy and the postpartum enhance neuroplasticity, resulting in both structural and functional brain changes that increase the salience and reward of infant cues (Brunton & Russell, 2010; Hoekzema et al., 2017). As a result, pregnant and postpartum individuals with SUD may experience a biologically-mediated turning point for behavioral change (Li, 2023; Servin-Barthet et al., 2023). Thus, the perinatal period is a time when it becomes possible to modify previously entrenched behavioral patterns, including substance misuse (Bellaert et al., 2022).

By supporting and reinforcing the emerging mother-infant relationship, caregiving activities may play a role in this change process. The caregiving behaviors identified in this review, including breastfeeding, skin-to-skin care, and babywearing, share the element of high physical touch, which is central to mother-infant co-regulation and may help explain observed protective associations. While no studies included in the current review explicitly tested neurobiological variables as explanatory mechanisms, extensive evidence shows that physical touch—and the physiological processes it promotes—can modulate stress and reward sensitivity (Dagnino-Subiabre, 2022; Kidd et al., 2023), strengthen early mother-infant bonding (Fotopoulou et al., 2022; Han et al., 2024; Rankin et al., 2024a), and promote long-term positive outcomes for caregivers and children (Bigelow & Power, 2020; Linde-Krieger & Rankin, 2025). Though less studied, some evidence indicates that supportive physical touch reduces drug craving and substance use vulnerability (Harkki et al., 2025). These physiological and relational interpretations align with broader evidence that affiliative and caregiving behaviors can alter neural reward circuitry in ways that support recovery from addiction (Strathearn et al., 2019).

Physical Touch

Several physiological processes associated with physical touch may serve as mechanisms linking caregiving with substance use reduction. High-touch practices, including breastfeeding, skin-to-skin care, and babywearing, provide continuous sensory input that supports co-regulation of maternal and infant physiology. Touch stimulates parasympathetic activity, lowers maternal heart rate and blood pressure, and promotes emotional regulation, which can mitigate stress-induced cravings (Kidd et al., 2023; Uvnäs-Moberg et al., 2015; Williams et al., 2020). Importantly, postpartum mothers may be uniquely sensitized to infant touch, creating a feedback loop wherein close physical contact enhances maternal identity and feelings of competence (Adams et al., 2021). This closeness and embodied connection may buffer common postpartum stressors (e.g., isolation, pain, mood changes, sleep disturbance) that accompany early parenting. In the context of SUD, the stress-buffering effects of physical touch are also implicated in recovery outcomes (Harkki et al., 2025). In this way, physical closeness may provide substantial benefit to both infant and caregiver by supporting secure attachment and positive infant development, enhancing caregiver neurobiological reward, and reducing substance craving and use.

Neuroendocrine Mechanisms

Caregiving activities that promote close physical contact with the infant also activate neuroendocrine pathways that regulate stress, reward, and motivation. Oxytocin release during breastfeeding and physical touch promotes relaxation, decreases cortisol levels, and enhances affiliative bonding between mother and infant (Feldman et al., 2007; Moberg et al., 2020; Uvnäs-Moberg et al., 2015). Elevated oxytocin may counteract dysregulation in dopamine-driven reward circuits that underlie craving and relapse risk, aligning caregiving with natural reward systems (Galiza Soares et al., 2025; Strathearn et al., 2019). Prolactin, which is elevated during lactation, has also been linked to maternal sensitivity and reduced stress reactivity (Moberg & Prime, 2013), potentially stabilizing mood and decreasing reliance on exogenous substances. In addition, breastfeeding is associated with lower and more stable estrogen and progesterone levels across the postpartum period, which may inhibit the reinforcing effects of nicotine and other substances on brain reward pathways (Forray et al., 2017; Forray & Foster, 2015). Together, these neuroendocrine patterns can restore the stress-reward balance during the postpartum period, decreasing vulnerability to substance use and reinforcing caregiving as an intrinsically rewarding activity.

Strengths and Limitations of the Evidence

This review identifies both the promise and the limitations of existing research. The strongest evidence supports breastfeeding as protective against postpartum cigarette smoking, with consistent findings across large population-based studies, smaller longitudinal cohorts, and qualitative accounts. The convergence of evidence across methods lends credibility to the observed association. However, important limitations constrain interpretations. Most studies focused narrowly on cigarette smoking, and other substances were underexplored. Nearly all of the reviewed studies conceptualized caregiving as breastfeeding, with very few examining other caregiving practices that may be equally or more accessible to families who cannot or choose not to breastfeed. Further, the studies were heterogeneous in the timing and operationalization of caregiving and substance use outcomes, which limits comparability across studies and the ability to draw conclusions about the temporality or direction of the findings. For example, most studies assessed breastfeeding and cigarette smoking concurrently during the early postpartum period, and measures of breastfeeding (e.g., initiation, current breastfeeding status, duration, exclusive breastfeeding, timing of weaning) and smoking (e.g., current smoking status, cigarettes per day, return to use following prenatal cessation, time to relapse, biochemically verified abstinence) differed across studies. Many studies relied on self-reported substance use and caregiving, which introduces the potential for recall bias and underreporting. Definitions of use/relapse and analytic strategies also varied substantially, complicating cross-study comparisons. No studies examined the effects of caregiving behavior on substance use in non-maternal (such as fathers) or non-biological (such as foster parents) caregivers. Moreover, few studies explicitly tested proposed mechanisms, limiting our ability to draw firm conclusions about the pathways through which caregiving exerts protective effects. These limitations underscore the need for more rigorous, diverse, and mechanism-focused research.

Clinical and Policy Implications

The findings of this review have important implications for clinical practice and public health policy. From a clinical perspective, promoting infant caregiving behaviors may represent a novel, culturally acceptable and adaptable, and family-centered approach to relapse prevention. Encouraging breastfeeding when safe and feasible may not only benefit infant health but also serve as a recovery-supportive practice for mothers. When patients cannot or choose not to breastfeed, clinicians should promote a variety of high-touch caregiving practices, such as skin-to-skin care and babywearing, as avenues for bonding and recovery support (Phillips et al., 2012; Rankin et al., 2024a, 2024b). Integrating education on protective caregiving behaviors into perinatal substance use treatment and postpartum care is a low-cost option to promote family-centered models of recovery.

At the policy level, public health systems should recognize caregiving behaviors as potential protective factors and invest in structural supports that make these behaviors possible. This may include providing access to lactation consultation, baby carriers, and family-friendly policies (e.g., paid parental leave). Hospitals and birthing facilities can also provide supportive environments for skin-to-skin care, especially those serving populations at high risk for perinatal substance use. Chronic stress and structural barriers such as poverty, unstable housing, and punitive child welfare policy (e.g., routine removal of infants who screen positive for substances at birth) can undermine the feasibility of positive, high-touch caregiving practices (Linde-Krieger et al., 2025; Renbarger, 2024). Addressing these barriers through integrated services and trauma-informed care can ensure that caregiving-based interventions are accessible to all families.

Future Directions

Future research should aim to move the literature beyond a focus on breastfeeding and combustible cigarette smoking to explore a broader range of caregiving practices and substance outcomes. Rigorous, longitudinal designs are needed to establish causal pathways and clarify the role of biopsychosocial mechanisms, including hormonal regulation, stress physiology, maternal identity, and bonding. Studies should also investigate contextual moderators, such as partner substance use, household environment, and social support, that may influence whether caregiving behaviors exert protective effects. For example, future studies may prioritize examining the protective function of caregiving behavior in contexts where social and/or structural supports are limited, as these settings may reveal unique challenges and intervention opportunities. Additionally, intervention studies that experimentally enhance caregiving behaviors (e.g., randomized controlled trials of babywearing) and assess their impact on substance misuse would provide evidence of efficacy to inform practice and policy.

This scoping review highlights the potential for infant caregiving behaviors—particularly those involving physical touch and closeness—to serve as protective factors against maternal substance use during the postpartum. Whereas breastfeeding has been most extensively studied, emerging evidence suggests that other caregiving practices, such as skin-to-skin care and babywearing, may also support postpartum SUD recovery. More work is needed to identify the physiological, psychological, and social mechanisms underlying these effects, and to test whether benefits of high physical contact caregiving behaviors may extend to caregivers beyond the mother (e.g., fathers, partners, non-biological caregivers). By reframing caregiving as a potential source of resilience, researchers and clinicians can better design interventions that leverage the unique motivations and physiological processes of the perinatal period to promote maternal and family health and well-being.
