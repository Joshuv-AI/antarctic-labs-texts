Human-induced intensification of sea surface temperature regime shifts threatens global Large Marine Ecosystems
Authors: Qinwang Xing, Zihui Gao, Shin-ichi Ito, Haiqing Yu, Wei Yu, Xinjun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal oceans have warmed markedly and will continue to do so. Superimposed on this trend are abrupt, persistent decadal regime shifts between warm and cold phases that alter ocean–atmosphere dynamics and reshape ecosystem composition and function. Despite recognition of the increasing risk of irreversible climate tipping points, the past and future evolution of these decadal warm–cold shifts remains unclear. Here, combining observations and simulations, we show a widespread 130–140% increase in the frequency and amplitude of sea surface temperature regime shifts across global Large Marine Ecosystems over the past 150 years, with asymmetries stronger in the Northern Hemisphere. Climate models attribute these trends to anthropogenic warming, which suppresses cold-regime transitions and reinforces warm regimes. Without mitigation, these shifts are projected to rise 130–180% by the late-21st century relative to recent levels, whereas only the lowest-emission scenario could constrain their intensification—except in the Arctic oceans. Further analyses reveal close links between intensified temperature regime shifts and abrupt ecological and fishery changes, showing 21–46% synchrony that is rising, potentially amplifying decadal ecosystem instability and socio economic challenges. From a marine perspective, these findings underscore the urgency of achieving the Paris Agreement’s target and the Arctic’s marked vulnerability.Subject terms: Biooceanography, Projection and prediction, Ocean sciences

Introduction

The ocean has absorbed approximately 93% of the excess heat from anthropogenic warming, leading to an accelerated rise in sea surface temperature (SST)—from about 0.06 °C per decade in the late 1980s to roughly 0.27 °C per decade during the 2010s1,2. This sustained warming has profoundly intensified ocean stratification, accelerated the decline in polar sea-ice extent and thickness, reduced dissolved oxygen levels and expanded low-oxygen zones, and increased the frequency and severity of extreme climate events3–6. Together, these changes heighten the risk of irreversible ecosystem transformations, posing critical threats to the stability of global marine ecosystems7,8. In addition to these long-term warming trends, SST variability at shorter timescales, such as marine heatwaves, seasonal cycles, the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, and decadal oscillations, also plays a critical role in modulating marine ecosystem dynamics and has attracted increasing scientific attention6,9–11. Variability on these shorter timescales often represents more urgent and less predictable impacts superimposed on the background of global warming12,13.

SST regime shifts (RSs) are pronounced, decadal-scale, abrupt transitions between sustained warm and cold states14,15. These shifts are typically driven by natural climate variability arising from the combined influence of multiple global and basin-scale climate modes16,17. As temperature underlies most chemical, physical, and biological processes, SST teleconnections are closely linked to large-scale oceanic and atmospheric circulation, reflecting the distribution and transport changes of heat and materials and propagating their effects across spatial and temporal scales within the Earth system14,18–20. Consequently, SST RSs are recognized as a key representative of decadal climate variability and are often linked to synchronous, nonlinear changes in marine and atmospheric conditions—including shifts in monsoon intensity, precipitation and drought patterns, polar sea-ice extent, extreme climate events and dissolved oxygen levels4,14,17,19–22. For example, the North Pacific has experienced a series of RSs—1924/25, 1941/42, 1976/77, 1988/89, 1997/98, and 2013/14—driven by alternating warm and cold phases arising from the superimposed effects of the Pacific Decadal Oscillation, Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation, and Victoria Mode21. These abrupt transitions exert profound impacts on marine ecosystem dynamics, as reflected in synchronous changes in phytoplankton and zooplankton biomass and species composition23,24. SST RSs can cascade through higher trophic levels, affecting fish and top predators and driving abrupt fluctuations in growth, recruitment, migration, and body weight, ultimately reshaping ecosystem structure and function13,25–28. Their suddenness renders SST RSs difficult to predict, intermittently triggering unexpected fishery collapses, undermining food security and economic opportunities, and posing major challenges for ecosystem and fisheries management13,26,29,30. For example, SST RSs have been linked to synchronous shifts in the North Sea plankton community between 1959–1980 and 1989–2010; they have also triggered abrupt reductions in phytoplankton and zooplankton driving the collapse of Peruvian anchovy fisheries during 1973–1992 and after 201231,32, and have led to sharp declines in the growth rates of three sea turtle species in the western Atlantic after 199727. Therefore, clarifying the dynamics of decadal SST RSs and their ecological consequences is essential for understanding marine ecosystems and safeguarding human communities in a warming world.

Recent studies have shown that shorter-timescale SST variations, such as marine heatwaves and El Niño–La Niña events, have become more frequent and prolonged under global warming6,33. Concurrently, climate-model experiments indicate that the risk of large-scale, irreversible shifts in multiple climate subsystems, including the collapse of permafrost, the Amazon rainforest, and polar sea ice, is expected to rise sharply with continued greenhouse gas emissions34,35. Although numerous studies have elucidated the mechanisms of decadal SST RSs and their ecological and climatic impacts in regional oceans17,21,36, they also raise the question of how the frequency and amplitude of such decadal warm–cold shifts, largely governed by natural climate variability, might evolve under ongoing anthropogenic climate change, and their implications for decadal changes in ecological variables. In addition, satellite observations revealed pronounced spatial heterogeneity in ocean warming, with intensified warming in western boundary currents, localized cooling in the subpolar North Atlantic gyre, and a marked hemispheric asymmetry favoring the Northern Hemisphere37. However, how these spatial heterogeneities influence global SST RS trends under global warming remains unclear. Given that global warming is expected to continue into the foreseeable future, there is an urgent need for a comprehensive assessment of the global patterns of SST RSs both historical and projected to inform climate adaptation strategies and reduce future risks.

Large Marine Ecosystems (LMEs) are ocean regions defined by distinct bathymetry, hydrography, productivity, and trophic structure, and have been widely accepted since the 1980s as ecologically meaningful units for ecosystem-based management. Although LMEs cover only ~22% of the global ocean, they harbor high biodiversity and sustain ~80% of global marine fisheries catches38. Recent studies have reported synchronous RSs in plankton and fish communities linked to SST RSs across multiple LMEs13,24,39–41, intensifying interest in understanding the past and future occurrence of SST RSs and their ecological consequences under global warming, particularly in LMEs, given the scarcity of such cases documented in the open ocean. Therefore, we address global patterns and trajectories of decadal SST RSs in LMEs under climate change, and their connections to marine ecological variables and fisheries, by integrating five observation-based SST reconstructions over the past 150 years (1870–2019), 51 state-of-the-art climate-model simulations of SST and ecological variables (dissolved oxygen, phytoplankton, and zooplankton), and global fishery catch records. We find a 130–140% increase in SST RSs across global LMEs since pre-industrial times, with asymmetries being stronger in the Northern Hemisphere and in warm regimes. Climate-model experiments attribute these trends to anthropogenic warming, which suppresses cold-direction RSs and reinforces warm RSs, producing stepwise SST increases. Rising SST RSs align with and partly account for amplified ecological and fishery catch RSs, heightening risks to marine ecosystems and fisheries. Under high-emission scenarios, SST RS frequency is projected to increase 130–180% by late century relative to the late 20th century. Fortunately, ambitious emission reductions could reverse trends—though Arctic LMEs remain disproportionately vulnerable, with RSs intensifying even under the Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C target. Unlike previously reported long-term, irreversible shifts or climate tipping points in marine and terrestrial variables12,20,34,42, our results reveal increasingly unstable decadal ocean–climate dynamics, cascading into more frequent and unpredictable ecological and socio-economic shifts, and posing challenges for management.

Results

Global patterns of SST RSs in LMEs related to global warming

We employed multiple advanced RS detection algorithms to quantify total SST RS frequency (number of RS events) and mean amplitudes (absolute SST difference between consecutive regimes) across 66 global LMEs during 1870–2019 using five observed datasets (example in Fig. 1a; see Methods). The spatial distribution of RS metrics exhibits marked heterogeneity, with a global mean of 3.98 events and 0.36 °C, respectively (Fig. 1b–c). Western boundary current regions—hotspots of global warming—exhibit significantly higher RS frequency and amplitude than other regions, with mean values of 4.74 events and 0.43 °C compared with 3.70 events and 0.36 °C elsewhere (p < 0.01, Student’s t test), whereas eastern boundary upwelling systems exhibit significantly more frequent RSs, with mean metrics of 4.27 events and 0.40 °C (Fig. 1d–e). To further resolve SST RS characteristics, we classified them into positive RSs (shifts to warmer states) and negative RSs (shifts to cooler states). Their spatial patterns broadly mirror the total RS distribution, with positive RSs averaging 3.5 events and 0.5 °C amplitude, compared to 1.5 events and 0.4 °C for negative RSs (Fig. S1). Western boundary currents exhibit significantly more frequent and intense positive and negative RSs than other regions (p < 0.01), while upwelling systems show elevated positive RS frequencies and larger negative RS amplitudes (p < 0.05; Fig. 1d–e).

To assess the link between SST RSs and long-term warming, we examined linear SST trends across the 66 LMEs. Over the past 150 years, warming has been most pronounced in western boundary currents and eastern boundary upwelling systems (Fig. S2). Across LMEs, RS frequency and amplitude—both total and positive—scale positively with SST warming rates (p < 0.01), with the exception of negative RS frequency (Fig. S3a–f). Because warming trends may potentially affect RS detection, we repeated the analysis using detrended SST, which yields broadly consistent results (Fig. S3g–l). These findings indicate that faster-warming regions experienced more frequent and intense SST RSs, suggesting that intensified warming in boundary currents and upwelling systems contributed to their elevated RS metrics43.

Global intensification of SST RSs in LMEs driven by anthropogenic warming

To investigate long-term changes in SST RSs, we divided the record into three 50-year periods based on linear trend characteristics (Fig. S4): the pre-industrial (1870–1919), slow-warming (1920–1969), and rapid-warming (1970–2019) phases. Nearly all LMEs (95.45% for frequency; 77.27% for amplitude) show increasing RS frequencies and amplitudes between the rapid-warming and pre-industrial phases (Fig. 2a, b). Bootstrap methods confirm significant differences (p < 0.1) in most LMEs (52 for frequency, 51 for amplitude), underscoring the robustness and consistency between datasets. On average, RS total frequency and mean amplitude are 0.81 events and 0.18 °C during the pre-industrial phase, increasing by 50–70% (1.21 events and 0.31 °C) and 130–140% (1.96 events and 0.42 °C) in the slow- and rapid-warming phases (p < 0.01; Fig. 2c). Spatially, Europe, North America and Asia exhibit larger increases in both metrics (Fig. 2f), driving a pronounced hemispheric asymmetry, with Northern Hemisphere LMEs showing significantly more frequent and intense RSs than those in the Southern Hemisphere (p < 0.05; Fig. 2f and S5).

To test whether long-term warming biases RS detection, we repeated the analysis with nonlinearly detrended SST time series. Although the overall rate of increase is somewhat reduced, both RS frequency and amplitude continue to rise across most LMEs, with hemispheric asymmetry becoming more pronounced: RS metrics increase in the Northern Hemisphere but remain stable or show a slight decline in the Southern Hemisphere (Fig. S6). While recent rapid global warming may enhance the detection of SST RSs, our work focuses on RSs in the original SST, as biological communities and ecosystems respond to the actual thermal conditions they experience. Additionally, we repeated the analysis with three additional RS detection methods to account for methodological biases; all consistently reveal increased RS metrics in most LMEs, along with hemispheric asymmetry (Fig. S7–S9). These independent results reinforce the robustness of our findings.

Different from pronounced increases in the rapid warming phase, increases from the pre-industrial to the slow-warming phase are weaker, though still significant (Figs. S5a, b and 2a, b), consistent with a stronger warming signal during the recent period. To disentangle forcing drivers, we compared 51 CMIP6 historical (anthropogenic + natural) and 14 historical-nature (natural-only) simulations. Consistent and significant increases in RS metrics (p < 0.1) across datasets can be observed across nearly all LMEs (Fig. 2d–e), reinforcing the robustness of the observed RS patterns, whereas historical-nature runs exhibit no significant differences in most LMEs (87% for frequency; 74% for amplitude; Fig. 2g, h). Importantly, even after detrending long-term warming, SST RSs still show significant increases (Figs. 2i and S10), although their increasing trends are attenuated, indicating that anthropogenic warming may, to some extent, amplify the internal climate variability driving decadal SST RSs. Together, these results demonstrate that SST RS metrics have increased 130–140% from the pre-industrial to the recent period, potentially driven by the combined effects of anthropogenic warming and the associated amplification of internal variability.

We further distinguished positive (warming) and negative (cooling) RSs. From the pre-industrial to the rapid-warming phase, negative RSs decline sharply in both frequency (93.9% of LMEs) and amplitude (84.9%), whereas positive RSs increase almost universally in frequency (100%) and amplitude (92.4%), with significant period differences across most LMEs (p < 0.1; Fig. 3a–d). On average, positive RS frequency rises from 0.2 events/50 yrs in the pre-industrial phase to 0.9 in the slow-warming phase and 1.8 in the rapid-warming phase, while negative RS frequency falls from 0.6 to 0.2 and then 0.1 (p < 0.01; Fig. 3e). Positive RS amplitudes also increase significantly, from 0.32 to 0.35 and 0.4 °C (p < 0.1), whereas negative RS amplitudes remain relatively stable (Fig. 3f). This imbalance suggests that SSTs in global LMEs are rising in a stepwise manner during the recent phase.

Directional asymmetry is further confirmed by comparing mean values across all datasets: positive RSs are more frequent and intense than negative RSs (3 vs. 1 events/50 yrs; 0.4 vs. 0.3 °C; Fig. S11). This asymmetry reflects the predominance of warm-phase anomalies, with a cooling signal, often associated with climate variability such as the cold phases of the Pacific Decadal Oscillation, partly masked by anthropogenic warming44. Compared with the results without detrending, repeating the analysis after removing long-term warming shows that the decreasing (increasing) frequency of negative (positive) RSs is largely alleviated (Figs. S11–S12). Specifically, we observe a clear increase (decrease) in negative (positive) RS frequency during the rapid-warming phase, along with the disappearance of the previously intensified positive RS amplitude. These results suggest that anthropogenic warming strengthens warm-direction RSs while constraining cold-direction RSs.

Paris Agreement targets could curb future SST RS increases

We assessed the future evolution of SST RSs across 66 LMEs from 1951–2100 under five Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs), representing increasing levels of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions (see Methods). Using SST outputs from 19 CMIP6 models, we divided the record into three 50-year periods (1951–2000, 2001–2050, and 2051–2100). From 1951–2000 to 2001–2050, nearly all LMEs showed significant increases (p < 0.1) in RS frequency and amplitude across scenarios, with only slight increases under SSP1-1.9, but increases of 60–120% in frequency and 15–20% in amplitude under other pathways (Fig. 4 and S13–S14). However, under the low- to medium-emission pathways (SSP1-1.9–SSP2-4.5), RS metrics recover slightly during 2051–2100, and even show decreases of 10–25% relative to late-20th-century levels under the lowest-emission pathways (Fig. 4e, f). Under high-emission scenarios (SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5), RS frequency surges by 130–180% relative to late-20th-century levels, while mean amplitude increases by 20–40%. These results highlight that only ambitious mitigation (SSP1-1.9 and SSP1-2.6, consistent with the Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C and 2 °C targets) can stabilize and restore RS patterns in the latter half of the century to late-20th-century levels, with SSP1-1.9 uniquely leading to decreases in RSs.

Spatially, Arctic LMEs exhibit the strongest increases in RS metrics across all SSPs (p < 0.05; Fig. 4g, h). Notably, RSs intensify slightly in the Arctic even under SSP1-1.9, despite a global return to late-20th-century levels. Analysis of sea ice projections from 14 CMIP6 models reveals sustained ice loss across scenarios in Arctic oceans, scaling with emissions (Fig. S15). Declines in sea ice are strongly correlated with enhanced Arctic RSs (p < 0.01; Fig. 4i, j), indicating that cryospheric retreat is a key driver of Arctic SST RS variability. Given the irreversibility of sea ice loss, continued amplification of Arctic RSs appears unavoidable, even under the strongest mitigation (SSP1-1.9). Detrended analyses produce overall similar trends (Fig. S16), despite an overall reduction in detected RSs across all climate scenarios, confirming that these patterns are not merely products of long-term warming. Together, these findings underscore the Arctic’s disproportionate vulnerability to climate-driven ocean variability and highlight the pivotal role of cryosphere–ocean interactions in shaping future SST dynamics.

Increased SST RSs may heighten ecological RS risks

Intensifying SST RSs are expected to destabilize marine ecosystems, with cascading ecological consequences26,27,45. To assess their impacts, we detected RSs in pelagic-integrated (0–200 m) dissolved oxygen, phytoplankton, and zooplankton concentrations (1865–2014; 12–17 CMIP6 models) and in fishery catches (1950–2019). Across variables, RS frequency and amplitude increase generally from early (1865–1914) to late (1965–2014) periods across LMEs (Fig. 5a–c and S17–S18), with frequency in particular increasing by 80–150%. The distribution of these increases correlates positively with SST RS frequency and amplitude (r = 0.25–0.45, p < 0.05; Figs. 5i–k and S18e–h)—except for phytoplankton RS amplitude, with dissolved oxygen exhibiting the strongest associations (r = 0.41–0.45). We further quantified the proportion of SST RS events occurring synchronously with RSs in ecological variables, showing ~46% for dissolved oxygen, 21%–24% for phytoplankton and zooplankton (Fig. S19). Importantly, synchrony increases significantly (p < 0.05) over time (Fig. 5e–h), possibly affected by higher-amplitude SST RSs, which exhibit greater synchrony than lower-amplitude events (Fig. S20).

Due to the high diversity of fishery species within each LME, we obtained the species-average RS frequency and amplitude from the normalized catches of each species, exhibiting general ~35% increases in RS frequency across most LMEs, though amplitudes remain stable (Figs. 5d and S18d). Although changes in fishing activities can also contribute to catch RSs, the synchrony of SST and catch RSs increases from an average of 38% (1950–1984) to 47% (1985–2019; Fig. 5h) at a significant level (p < 0.05). Importantly, the distribution of catch RS frequency and amplitude correlates significantly (p < 0.05) with that of SST RSs (Figs. 5i and S18h), implying LMEs with high SST RSs are more prone to catch RSs. Together, these results show that SST RSs do not universally translate into ecological or fisheries RSs, with synchrony limited to 21–46%. Nonetheless, their rising frequency and amplitude are likely amplifying ecological instability and elevating the risk of abrupt shifts in marine ecosystems and fisheries.

Discussion

By integrating multi-source observations, simulations, and detection methods, we show that decadal SST RSs have intensified globally by 130–140% relative to pre-industrial levels, with marked hemispheric asymmetry. Looking forward, SST RSs are projected to persist and intensify under continued warming, but could stabilize under aggressive emission reductions. It should be noted that, owing to limitations in the available observations across the five observational datasets, the relatively coarse spatial resolution (at best a 1° × 1° grid at the highest resolution) and the temporally and spatially uneven distribution of observations may affect our results for some coastal LMEs. However, the consistency between the observational results and those derived from 51 climate-model simulations enhances the robustness of our conclusion regarding the overall intensification of SST RSs across global LMEs (Fig. 2), despite the need for further improvements in climate models’ ability to better resolve coastal processes.

CMIP6 comparison experiments attribute these increases primarily to anthropogenic forcing, whereby the associated warming enhances the detection of RSs. Importantly, intensified RSs persist even after detrending, indicating that anthropogenic warming may amplify natural climate variability, thereby driving increasingly unstable decadal SST variability with profound climatic and ecological implications. These intensified trends are consistent with reports of more frequent and amplified shifts in major climate modes under global warming, including accelerated transitions of the Pacific Decadal Oscillation, earlier and stronger positive Indian Ocean Dipole events, and intensified phases of the North Atlantic Oscillation and Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation46–48. A key mechanism underlying these intensified decadal modes is enhanced ocean stratification induced by global warming, which accelerates Rossby wave propagation and increases the sensitivity of mixed-layer temperatures to heat forcing and transport47,49. In addition, the emergence and strengthening of new decadal climate modes, such as the Pacific quasi-decadal oscillation, may have further enhanced decadal SST variability and increased SST RSs since the 1950s50. Our projections of future SST RS trajectories are consistent with expectations of more frequent phase transitions in major climate modes under global warming48,51. Meanwhile, SST RSs are not shaped by any single mode but rather by the combined effects of multiple modes, such as a series of SST RSs in the North Pacific driven by alternating warm and cold phases arising from the superimposed effects of the Pacific Decadal Oscillation, Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation, and Victoria Mode21. Together, multiple modes regulate regional and global ocean–atmosphere variability and exert cascading influences on SST RSs across global oceans through climatic teleconnections. Therefore, human-induced global warming and the associated acceleration of natural climate variability may have contributed to the intensification of decadal SST RSs.

Our results indicate that stronger anthropogenic warming trends induce frequent and amplified RSs. The spatial patterns highlight western boundary current systems as RS hotspots, where poleward heat transport and strong air–sea coupling sustain intense SST variability52,53 and the warming trends are distinct. A clear hemispheric asymmetry also emerges, with Northern Hemisphere LMEs showing more frequent and intense RSs than those in the south. This contrast reflects faster Northern Hemisphere warming, amplified by stronger air–sea interactions due to cross-equatorial heat transport that enhances water vapor–greenhouse feedbacks and sustains interhemispheric temperature gradients54. Interhemispheric asymmetry of global warming has intensified owing to the uneven spatial impacts of greenhouse warming55,56. In addition, Arctic sea-ice loss plays a critical role by weakening the polar vortex and upper-level zonal winds, reinforcing SST anomalies across the broader Northern Hemisphere57,58. Reductions in sea-ice concentration and thickness also drive strong local SST increases59. Extending this evidence, our findings show that even under stringent mitigation (the Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C target), accelerated ice retreat will continue to amplify SST RSs in Arctic LMEs under all SSP scenarios. Together, these results underscore the tight coupling between SST RSs, ocean–atmosphere feedbacks, and cryospheric change, and highlight the disproportionate vulnerability of Arctic oceans to abrupt warming events despite global mitigation efforts.

Over the past 150 years—and continuing into the future—SST RSs in global LMEs have intensified, indicating increasingly irregular and extreme shifts in ocean temperatures that destabilize the Earth system. This trend parallels the rising frequency, amplitude, and spatial extent of large-scale climate phenomena such as ENSO and marine heatwaves under anthropogenic warming6,9, with cascading impacts on precipitation, heat distribution, atmospheric circulation, sea-ice extent, and extreme events4,14,17,19–22. Intensified SST RSs inevitably propagate through marine ecosystems, driving ecological RSs in plankton, fish populations, and higher trophic levels25,26,41. Our analysis of ecological and fishery records supports this link: RSs in dissolved oxygen, phytoplankton, zooplankton, and fish catch have all increased significantly, accompanied by stronger synchrony with SST RSs, and are amplified by higher SST RS amplitudes. Although the relationships are not strictly deterministic, previous studies show that shifts in community composition and species abundance often track SST RSs24,27,60. For example, the Northeast Continental Shelf LME experienced recurrent zooplankton RSs in the early 1980s, early-2000s, and mid-2000s, coinciding with SST-driven environmental shifts23. Similarly, fish abundance and community structure shifted with SST regimes, with warm-water species dominating during warm phases and cold-water species during cold phases45. Therefore, the risk of these ecological RSs is heightened by increased SST RSs under global warming. In addition, biological and ecosystem RSs are influenced in a nonlinear manner by multiple environmental drivers61, including not only water temperature but also nutrient availability, dissolved oxygen, and human activities, and are further shaped by nonlinear predator–prey interactions and interspecific competition within food webs25,41. Although the statistical tests are significant, the relatively low synchrony and correlation coefficients between SST and ecological RSs suggest that additional environmental and ecological drivers also contribute to RSs in ecological variables and fishery catches. Nevertheless, intensified SST RSs may serve as a proxy for the acceleration of decadal ocean–climate variability, which, together with multiple stressors, poses increasing risks to marine ecosystems.

Additionally, our results also reveal a strong directional asymmetry in SST RSs, with positive (warm) events occurring more frequently and intensely than negative (cold) ones. This asymmetry can be partly attributed to anthropogenic warming, which tends to suppress cold-direction RSs while enhancing warm-direction RSs. Previous work suggests that La Niña–related cooling can be masked during warm phases, allowing positive SST anomalies to dominate14. A notable example is the early-2000s slowdown in global warming, when the cold phase of the Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation was obscured by long-term warming62, leading to a reduced occurrence of negative RSs. This imbalance produces a stepwise rather than linear ocean warming, favoring warm-water species while disadvantaging cold-water species. Unlike the classical view of ecosystem tropicalization under global warming61,63, our findings imply a transition from alternating cold–warm species dominance toward persistent dominance of warm-water taxa, reducing recovery opportunities for cold-affinity species in the face of intensifying positive and declining negative RSs26,45.

Given that abrupt, persistent, and unpredictable RSs can trigger ecological collapses and fishery crises26,30, our finding that rising SST RSs coincide with ecological and fishery RSs highlights mounting risks to marine ecosystem services and the growing challenges for effective management. Unlike short-lived extremes such as marine heatwaves or El Niño, which often allow rapid recovery of partial ecosystems despite their severity31,64, decadal-scale RSs can persist for decades, severely constraining resilience and delaying recovery39. Such persistence has major socio-economic implications, particularly for vulnerable island nations and fishery-dependent economies. For instance, temperature-driven RSs in Peruvian anchovy populations precipitated fishery collapses, reducing national GDP by up to ~1%31. The risks are spatially uneven: LMEs in the Northern Hemisphere—encompassing Asian, European, and Arctic waters—face greater exposure due to larger SST RS increases. This is particularly concerning given that the Northern Hemisphere hosts 87% of the global population, produces 91.8% of global GDP, and supports 70% of marine fishery catches65,66. Consequently, SST RSs pose not only ecological but also far-reaching socio-economic risks, with potential cascades across precipitation, temperature, monsoons, crop yields, and ecosystem dynamics13,19,20,34. Our projections indicate that these risks will intensify if carbon emissions remain unchecked, whereas aggressive mitigation could stabilize RS frequency and amplitude—underscoring both the urgency and efficacy of emission reductions. However, Arctic SST RSs are projected to intensify irreversibly under all SSP pathways, including the stringent SSP1-1.9 scenario aligned with the Paris Agreement target of limiting warming to 1.5 °C67. By contrast, in the global oceans, low-emission scenarios—particularly SSP1-1.9—could maintain SST RS metrics near late-20th-century levels, whereas under SSP1-2.6 (the Paris Agreement’s 2.0 °C target), RS frequency is projected to rise 60% by mid-century before returning to late-20th-century levels by 2100. From a perspective different from previous studies, this highlights the urgent need for achieving the Paris Agreement target and implementing adaptive strategies to confront accelerating sea-ice loss and its cascading impacts on Arctic RSs.

Methods

Observational SST datasets

To assess changes in decadal SST RSs under global warming, we analyzed five spatially infilled, long-term, and regularly updated observational SST datasets spanning 1870–2019, using the most recent versions available at the time of writing. These products, widely applied in climate research, employ complementary reconstruction methodologies that enhance robustness when used in combination.

HadISST1 (Hadley Centre Sea Ice and Sea Surface Temperature; https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/hadobs/hadisst) provides monthly, globally complete fields of SST and sea-ice concentration on a 1° × 1° grid from 1870 to the present68. Large-scale SST fields are reconstructed using reduced-space optimal interpolation (RSOI). Input data comprise in situ observations from the Met Office Marine Data Bank and ICOADS, complemented by satellite retrievals from advanced very high-resolution radiometer (AVHRR) sensors.

ERSSTv5 (Extended Reconstructed Sea Surface Temperature; https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/extended-reconstructed-sst) provides monthly global SST fields on a 2° × 2° grid from 1854 to the present, with statistically enhanced spatial completeness69. Input data include ship and buoy records from ICOADS and Argo profiling floats from the GDAC, improving representation at high latitudes by better capturing spatiotemporal variability. Compared with earlier versions, ERSSTv5 offers improved spatial variability, more realistic magnitudes of El Niño and La Niña events, and a refined depiction of decadal SST changes during the 1930s–40 s, when observing systems underwent rapid transitions.

COBE2 SST (Centennial in situ Observation-Based Estimates, version 2; https://psl.noaa.gov/data/gridded/data.cobe2.html), produced by the Japan Meteorological Agency, provides monthly global SST fields on a 1° × 1° latitude–longitude grid from 1850 to the present70. Each value is accompanied by theory-based analysis errors as a measure of reliability. Biases among different SST measurement types are estimated and corrected prior to analysis, yielding a homogenized long-term record of global mean SST.

Berkeley Earth SST (https://berkeleyearth.org/data) provides monthly gridded temperature fields at 1° × 1° latitude–longitude resolution from 1850 to the present71. The dataset incorporates a larger volume of temperature observations than comparable products, yielding improved spatial coverage. Berkeley Earth uncertainty estimates incorporate contributions from measurement and sampling, coverage, and bias.

Kaplan SST provides monthly SST fields on a 5° × 5° grid from 1856 to the present, derived from UK Met Office data72. Missing values are reconstructed using a combination of EOF projection, optimal interpolation, Kalman filter forecasts and analyses, and an optimal smoother, integrating both spatial and temporal information.

Climate-model simulations

Because observational SST datasets are derived from largely overlapping sources and suffer from limited coverage in the early record, we additionally employed simulated SST data from multiple Earth system models to corroborate our findings on the long-term changes in historical SST RSs and further study the evolutionary patterns under different future climate scenarios. We used simulations from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6; https://aims2.llnl.gov/search/cmip6/), which provides a coordinated multi-model framework to investigate past, present and future climate variability and change73. For historical SST, we analyzed 51 simulations (Table S2) including both anthropogenic and natural forcings over 1865–2014 to support the observational results. The periods differ between observational and simulated results because the historical runs in CMIP6 datasets end in 2014. To separate the contributions of natural variability from anthropogenic forcing, we also analyzed 14 historical-natural simulations (Table S2) over the same period, which include only solar and volcanic forcings.

To examine changes in SST RSs under different carbon emission scenarios, future SST projections for 2015–2100 were obtained from 19 CMIP6 models (Table S2) under five Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs; SSP1-1.9, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5), representing increasing levels of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions and associated greenhouse gas concentrations. Low-emission scenarios (SSP1-1.9 and SSP1-2.6) assume that global CO₂ emissions remain below 2015 levels and decline after 2020, reaching net-zero around 2050 through permanent CO₂ removal. In SSP1-1.9, emissions are projected to become net-negative thereafter, reducing atmospheric CO₂ and limiting global warming to ~1.5 °C in accordance with the Paris Agreement’s ambitious target, whereas SSP1-2.6 stabilizes warming below 2.0 °C by the end of this century, consistent with its basic goal. SSP2-4.5 represents a medium-emission pathway, with emissions rising gradually, stabilizing around mid-century, and approaching carbon neutrality by 2100. It is considered the scenario most consistent with current greenhouse gas trajectories, leading to ~2.7 °C of warming by the end of the century. High-emission scenarios (SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5) feature substantial increases in CO₂ emissions, reaching about two to three times 2015 levels by 2100, and are projected to drive a global temperature rise of 3.6–4.4 °C by the end of the century. To ensure continuity with the historical record, SST simulations for 1951–2014 from the same 19 models were concatenated with the projections (with only 8 models available for SSP1-1.9), producing continuous 150-year time series (1951–2100) for each scenario.

To investigate the relationship between the Arctic sea ice melt and SST RSs in the Arctic LMEs, we obtained sea ice concentration for Arctic LMEs from 12 CMIP6 simulations (Table S2) over the same period and scenarios. Furthermore, to examine the ecological implications of SST RSs, we collected ecological variables, including 12 dissolved oxygen simulations, 16 phytoplankton (expressed as chlorophyll) simulations, and 13 zooplankton (expressed as carbon) simulations from CMIP6 over the period 1865–2014 (Table S2). To assess relationships between SST and these ecological variables, outputs from 5 models (Table S2) providing all variables consistently were further analyzed. All fields were averaged over the upper 200 m of the water column. It should be noted that the number of CMIP6 models used in this study varied by variable, reflecting differences in dataset availability in CMIP6 websites, and all datasets analyzed were selected according to the Variant Label r1i1p1f1.

Fishery catch dataset

Annual fish catch data for all LMEs over 1950–2019 were derived from the Sea Around Us database (www.seaaroundus.org/data/#/lme)74. The Sea Around Us project provides reconstructed time series of global marine fisheries catches from 1950, based on reported catches from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, supplemented by interpolated, country-wide expanded missing data series and considering unreported and discarded catches. For fish catch data, to ensure representativeness, fish species contributing less than 0.1% of the total catch in each LME were excluded, as were species with more than 10% continuous missing data (i.e., absent for over seven consecutive years). We identified catch RSs using normalized time series for each fishery species across LMEs, rather than total catch, which allows characterization of average RS properties within each LME while accounting for all available species irrespective of catch level or species composition. It should be acknowledged that fishery catches were influenced by multiple factors, including fishing pressure and management policies, and that environmental changes such as SST RSs represented only one of the key drivers.

RS detection methods

Before RS detection, all monthly data were converted to annual means and then averaged within each LME to construct time series of SST and other variables. To identify RSs in the annual time series of SST, dissolved oxygen, phytoplankton, zooplankton, and catch data, we applied the Sequential t-test Analysis of Regime Shifts (STARS, https://sites.google.com/view/regime-shift-test/home?authuser=0) method. This approach has been widely used in climate science and oceanography75, and has been applied to multiple physical, ecological, and fishery variables in previous studies. STARS is designed to detect abrupt shifts in time series and does not require a priori specification of shift timing; it can be applied to both anomalies and absolute values. STARS applies a moving two-tailed Student’s t test to successively compare the mean values of two consecutive segments of a time series, identifying statistically significant RSs when the mean levels of adjacent segments differ significantly. In this work, the STARS parameters were set to a cutoff length of 15, a Huber weight parameter of 1, and a significance level of 5%, following previous studies on decadal RSs32,45. The cutoff length defines the number of consecutive observations used to estimate the mean states before and after a potential RS, and regimes shorter than the cutoff length have a proportionally reduced likelihood of detection. A cutoff length of 15 therefore excludes shifts on timescales shorter than ~10 years and better captures decadal-scale regimes. The Huber weight parameter reduces the influence of outliers by assigning weights that decrease with increasing distance from the regime mean and is commonly set to 1.

To account for the potential influence of long-term warming signals on RS detection, analyses were conducted using both the original SST anomalies and detrended SST anomalies with the long-term warming signal removed. The original SST anomalies were defined as the deviation of the SST value from the climatological mean over 1870–2019. Given the long timescales of major climate modes, such as the 60–80-year variability associated with the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation, a 60-year moving-average filter was applied to the original SST anomalies to remove internal decadal and multidecadal climate variability and to extract the long-term, nonlinear warming signal. The detrended SST anomalies were subsequently defined as the residuals obtained by subtracting the nonlinear warming signal (60-year moving average) from the original SST anomalies. It should be noted that the removal of the nonlinear warming signal described above is imperfect due to the limited length of the available time series, which may introduce uncertainty into the detrending, especially near the beginning and end of the SST record.

To address limitations of a single method, we employed three additional approaches—the structural change method (strucchange package in R), the chronological clustering method (rioja package in R) and the non-parametric method (ecp package in R)—to assess the robustness of the long-term changes in RS frequency and amplitude. The structural change method, a widely used univariate change-point detection approach in ecological time series, compares linear and segmented regression models and tests each time step for potential breakpoints76. The chronological clustering method, a hierarchical clustering approach, identifies RSs by grouping similar states in time series, enabling the detection of abrupt changes and patterns without requiring a priori specification of shift timing77. The non-parametric method is designed to detect multiple change points in multivariate time series, which identifies time points where the joint distribution of the series changes, making it suitable for detecting abrupt shifts in ecological or climatic variables without prior specification of the number or location of change points78. For these methods, we imposed a minimum regime duration of 10 years to suppress short-timescale variability and focus on decadal and multidecadal climate variability.

We characterized RSs along two metrics: frequency and amplitude. RS frequency was defined as the total number of detected shifts, and RS amplitude was defined as the mean amplitude, calculated as the average absolute difference between mean values before and after each shift year. RS amplitude for catches was expressed as a relative value by dividing by the mean due to the strong nonstationarity of catch time series.

Change analysis of SST RSs

To examine the spatial heterogeneity of RS total frequency and mean amplitude during 1970–2019, the results of RS detection in the 66 LMEs were grouped into three regions: western boundary current (LME number 6, 7, 8, 14, 15, 30, 41, 42, 46, 47, 49–51), eastern boundary upwelling (LME number 2, 3, 13, 25, 27–32), and others. LME numbers above correspond to those shown in Fig. 1b, c. Statistical significance of differences among regions was assessed using Student’s t tests. We also calculated SST trends for 1970–2019 and examined their relationships with the total frequency and mean amplitude of RSs across each LME using linear regression and Pearson correlation analysis.

Temporal changes in observed SST RS frequency and amplitude were assessed by dividing the time series into three phases based on long-term SST trends (Fig. S4): pre-industrial (1870–1919, –0.051 °C/decade), slow warming (1920–1969, 0.051 °C/decade), and rapid warming (1970–2019, 0.16 °C/decade). Dataset-mean differences were then calculated between the pre-industrial and slow warming phases, and between the pre-industrial and rapid warming phases to quantify RS changes. Bootstrap methods based on results of each dataset were applied to assess differences among phases within each LME, accounting for high inter-dataset variability, with p-values derived from empirical sampling distributions generated through repeated resampling with replacement. Significant differences (p < 0.1) indicated consistent changes across the different datasets. To investigate the spatial distribution of these differences, LMEs were also grouped by continents and latitude zones (Table S1). Comparisons between the Northern and Southern Hemispheres included all LMEs, excluding the Tropical Region. Statistical significance of differences in RS frequency and amplitude changes among regions was assessed using Student’s t tests.

Simulated historical SST RS total frequency and mean amplitude from CMIP6 were calculated and segmented into three analogous periods: 1865–1914, 1915–1964, and 1965–2014. Dataset-mean differences were calculated between 1865–1914 and 1915–1964, and between 1865–1914 and 1965–2014 to quantify RS frequency and amplitude changes. Bootstrap methods based on results of each dataset were applied to assess differences among phases within each LME, accounting for high variation across datasets. Historical-nature simulations were also analyzed based on a similar method to clarify anthropogenic and natural contributions. Projected SST RS frequency and amplitude were computed for each LME to assess potential changes under varying levels of anthropogenic forcing. The projections were divided into three periods (1951–2000, 2001–2050, and 2051–2100), and dataset-mean differences were evaluated between 1951–2000 and 2001–2050, as well as between 1951–2000 and 2051–2100. Detrended results in the projections were detected after applying a 3-year moving average to reduce interannual variability that is amplified by detrending. The 66 LMEs were further classified into Arctic (LME numbers 18–20, 54–58, 63, 66; numbers correspond to Fig. 4a–d) and non-Arctic regions to compare their differences in RS frequency and amplitude changes, with significance assessed using Student’s t tests. Based on the least squares method, we calculated linear trends in the sea-ice concentration across Arctic LMEs from 1951 to 2100 under the five SSP scenarios and examined their linear relationship with SST RS frequency and amplitude changes between 1951–2000 and 2051–2100 to assess the link between Arctic sea-ice loss and SST RS increases using linear regression and Pearson correlation.

Relationships between ecological and SST RSs

We calculated dataset-mean RS frequency and amplitude for dissolved oxygen (12 models), phytoplankton (17 models), and zooplankton (13 models) after applying a 3-year mean filter due to their higher variability, and divided resulting time series into three phases (1865–1914, 1915–1964, 1965–2014) to examine long-term RS changes. Bootstrap methods based on results of each dataset were applied to assess differences among phases within each LME, accounting for high inter-dataset variability. The proportion of SST RSs occurring synchronously with ecological RSs was calculated to assess their relationships based on the same models, which was defined as the number of SST RS events coinciding with dissolved oxygen, phytoplankton, or zooplankton RSs within a 3-year window, divided by the total number of SST RS events. This window accounts for uncertainties in the specific RS years identified by the decadal RS detection algorithm. We further calculated differences in the synchronous proportion across the three phases to quantify temporal changes. For fishery catches, species-specific RS frequency and amplitude were averaged within each LME and divided into two phases (1950–1984 and 1985–2019) to quantify temporal changes. Given the presence of multiple fishery species, the fishery synchronous proportion in each LME was calculated as the proportion of observed SST RSs occurring synchronously with catch RSs in >30% of species to assess their relationships and temporal dynamics. We further calculated RS differences in SST, dissolved oxygen, phytoplankton, and zooplankton between 1865–1914 and 1965–2014, and used linear regression and Pearson correlation to examine their relationships with SST RS changes, based on the same 5 CMIP6 models. Given the shorter time span of fishery data, we calculated the total frequency and mean amplitude of SST RSs and catch RSs, and then assessed their relationship using linear regression and Pearson correlation. In addition, we assessed differences in synchronous proportion between strong and weak SST RSs, with strong events defined as those exceeding the median amplitude (the top 50% of SST RSs) within each LME. Student’s t tests were also used to assess the significance of differences across global LMEs.
