Opioid overdose mortality among black people receiving a community- & theory-based multilevel opioid overdose intervention in Indianapolis, U.S.A., 2022–2025
Authors: Dong-Chul Seo, Dohyun Im, Naomi Satterfield, Jon Agley, Francesca Williamson, Charlotte Crabtree
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe and evaluate a community- and theory-driven multilevel opioid overdose fatality prevention intervention for Black Indianapolis communities for 2022–2025.MethodsA community-driven opioid overdose fatality prevention project was designed based on Community Coalition Action Theory, the Citizen Health Care Model, and Legal Epidemiology. The intervention was implemented in inner-city Indianapolis (zip code areas 46202, 46205, 46208, 46218) with cluster-matched comparison areas in Indiana (46408, 46410, 46628, 46806). Data were collected in Indiana, U.S.A., in 2022–2025. Evaluation included mortality analysis and community probability surveys in Year 1 and 3.ResultsThere was a 45% decrease in Black overdose deaths in the intervention communities versus a 22% decrease in the comparison communities from 2022 to 2024. Probability community surveys showed improvement in overdose knowledge [difference-in-differences (DiD) estimator = 0.68, p = 0.03], perceived competency to manage an opioid overdose (DiD = 0.54, p < 0.01), and accessibility of naloxone (DiD = 1.06, p < 0.01) in the intervention communities compared to the comparison communities between Year 1 (N = 772) and 3 (N = 764).ConclusionsSimilar community- and theory-driven multi-level interventions may be effective in reducing overdose mortalities in the Black communities.Keywords: Opioid overdose, Community coalition, Quasi-experimental design, Black communities, Policy change

Introduction

Opioid overdose is the leading cause of death among U.S. adults aged 18 and 44 (CDC, 2025). Though provisional data indicate a substantial overall decrease in overdose deaths in 2024 (Hedegaard et al., 2021), this progress has not been observed for all races (Smith et al., 2025). The age-adjusted overdose death rates decreased from 35.6 to 33.1 to 24.7 among White people whereas they changed from 47.5 to 48.9 to 33.8 among Black people from 2022 to 2024, showing a persistent discrepancy in prevalence rates (Garnett and Miniño, 2024).

Significant variation between races has been observed in the treatment of opioid use disorder (OUD) and provision of tools to reverse opioid overdose. Medicare data reveal that Black patients receive naloxone at a rate of 14.4% versus 22.9% for White patients, and buprenorphine at a rate of 12.7% versus 23.3% after an OUD event, respectively (Barnett et al., 2023). These patterns potentially reflect differential treatment by, and functioning of, healthcare, criminal justice, and social services systems, underscoring the need for culturally responsive, community-centered approaches (Bailey et al., 2017; Durrance-Bagale et al., 2022).

One such challenge is a documented gap in community-level knowledge about life-saving interventions. A probability-based household survey conducted in 2023 across eight urban Indiana communities found that Black residents scored significantly lower on naloxone knowledge assessments compared to White residents (p < 0.001) (Lee et al., 2025). Despite comprising only 27.2% of the Marion County, Indiana population (according to the U.S. Census data in 2020,) (Marion County Demographics: A map of Marion County's Population by Race, 2020) Black residents accounted for 41% of all suspected overdose deaths in that county in the first quarter of 2024 (King, 2024).

Given the disparate burden of opioid-involved overdose mortalities in Black Indianapolis communities, our team conducted an internal scoping review of frameworks that could inform community-driven approaches to reduce opioid-involved overdose deaths in Black communities. As shown in Fig. 1, the following three frameworks were chosen to inform our project's intervention design and implementation.

Three Theoretical Frameworks That Informed MACRO-B Project, Indianapolis, U.S.A., 2022–2025.

CCAT: Community Coalition Action Theory, CHCM: Citizen Health Care Model, LECM: Legal Epidemiology Competency Model.

The Community Coalition Action Theory (CCAT) framework supports the development of collaborative action plans of coalitions to achieve improvements in their impacted community, highlighting the value of engaging multiple stakeholders to address complex local challenges (Ghaffari et al., 2021). CCAT provides a framework for building organizational capacity through a three-stage developmental framework and 14 core constructs, including member engagement, leadership, and multi-sector partnerships for sustained intervention (Butterfoss and Kegler, 2009; Kegler et al., 2010).

While CCAT offers a framework for building an organizational infrastructure for a coalition, the Citizen Health Care Model (CHCM) emphasizes the transformative potential of community members with lived experience to serve as change agents and peer leaders, recognizing that recovery from substance use disorder is fundamentally a social process requiring authentic peer support (Doherty and Mendenhall, 2006). CHCM comprises six guiding principles and seven core strategies through civic involvement in advancing community well-being. It provides a community engagement framework addressing its iterative challenges—particularly navigating historical mistrust, systemic discrimination, and access to resources and power dynamics between racial minority communities and institutional partners (Mezzina et al., 2022; Berge et al., 2009). The model highlights the importance of collaborative work between professionals and community members with diverse and complementary lived experience and expertise to effect change on a larger scale. The notion of “citizen” refers to individuals and their families, along with their neighbors and others who face similar health challenges, becoming activated to make a difference for a community (Berge et al., 2009).

Additionally, we utilized the Legal Epidemiology Competency Model (LECM) to strengthen the systematic evaluation of policy design and outcomes. In particular, legal mapping and legal evaluation approaches helped compare laws and regulations around naloxone, drug checking equipment, and Good Samaritan laws, which led to our project coalition's legislative education efforts around these topics and, subsequently, the successful amendment of Indiana House Bill 1167.

Methods

Study design and population

The MACRO-B (Multi-Sector and Multi-Level Community-Driven Approaches to Remove Structural Racism and Overdose Deaths in Black Indianapolis Communities) Project was funded by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (CPIMP221346, 2022–2026) to reduce opioid-involved overdose deaths in Black Indianapolis communities. It used a parallel, cluster-matched, quasi-experimental design with pre- and post-measurements and was designed based on the aforementioned three frameworks. CCAT provided infrastructure for community-based research but lacked mechanisms for centering lived experience, whereas CHCM emphasized community wisdom with lived experience but offered limited multi-sector guidance. LECM provided a powerful tool to effect changes to laws and regulations. The mapping of MACRO-B intervention programs corresponding to the constructs of the three frameworks is depicted in Table 1.

Description of Main programming

Within two months of the grant award, we were able to establish a multi-sectoral and multi-level community coalition for the MACRO-B Project, which held monthly plenary coalition meetings as well as numerous ad hoc subcommittee meetings beginning in early December 2022. Nearly all meetings were held in-person to facilitate rapport and interaction among coalition members. Though there were substantial initial disagreements among the members, our perception is that the coalition participants have become deeply engaged and dedicated members (the retention rate was 86% at the end of three years). The coalition comprised 23–25 members, including law enforcement, local emergency medical services, the local fire department, local Black-dominant churches, professional service organizations, decision-makers at local and state levels, staff and leadership from harm reduction programming, and Black community residents who were overdose survivors or relatives of individuals who died from fatal overdoses. Their meeting time was compensated at $50/h for the first year and then at $80/h from the second year forward, except government employees who were unable to receive payment for participation.

To conduct a community-driven assessment of risk factors contributing to the disparate burden of fatal overdoses in Black Indianapolis communities, we completed several research projects. First, we administered community opioid overdose knowledge, attitude, discrimination, and stigma (OKADIS) surveys using community probability samples (N = 772, n = 423 in the intervention and n = 349 in the comparison communities) in Year 1 and in Year 3 (N = 764, n = 417 in the intervention and n = 347 in the comparison communities) to gauge the extent of changes during the two years of the project. The survey was conducted by an independent professional survey institute in March–May 2023 for Year 1 Survey and March–May 2025 for Year 3. Response rates were 23% (2023) and 22% (2025) per the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) response rate formula #4. Details on the survey and results are found elsewhere (Lee et al., 2025). Second, a coalition member with lived experience conducted 50 in-depth interviews and four focus groups (N = 28). Details on the interviews and qualitative findings are found elsewhere (Seo et al., 2025; Seo et al., 2023). Third, we had so-called “neighborhood drive-throughs” where all the coalition members were divided into four groups and each group drove through every corner of one of the four target zip code areas to assess physical environment of the target areas and to identify the places where naloxone boxes should be installed.

Based on the information gathered in the needs assessment, we conducted the following assessment-based structural and individual interventions: First, a total of 1280 community members, the majority of whom were Black, participated in a 90-min overdose prevention education and naloxone administration training, delivered by a professional training institute. Second, we distributed 94,873 naloxone doses, 54,268 fentanyl test strips, and 27,898 xylazine test strips to the target communities and installed 40 naloxone boxes. Usage of naloxone boxes was monitored biweekly to ensure timely replenishment. Third, the Indianapolis Fire Department, one of the MACRO-B coalition member organizations, launched a Naloxone Leave Behind initiative (NLB). Firefighters carry the NLB kits on their apparatus and, when appropriate and the suspected overdose patient has been stabilized, leave the kits with family members, friends, and/or patients. Fourth, with the help of the Mental Health America of Indiana, we provided a 5-day-long (40 h) intensive peer recovery coach training for a total of 17 Black community members with lived experience so that they can serve as change agents in their communities to prevent overdose deaths. Fifth, we developed a MACRO-B website, https://macro-b.org/, to increase awareness and accessibility to overdose prevention resources. Sixth, we designed and distributed >1000 copies of three leaflets with QR codes focused on fentanyl, fentanyl test strips, and naloxone administration. Seventh, we held four town halls to increase overdose awareness and create broad community consensus building to help reduce overdose deaths in Black communities.

Measures

Opioid-involved overdose mortality data were obtained from two sources. The Marion County Coroner's Office provided monthly mortality counts stratified by zip code and race for the intervention communities, while mortality data from comparison communities came from their respective coroner's offices via the Indiana Department of Health.

For OKADIS community surveys, we measured knowledge on opioid overdose and naloxone administration with 10 items adapted from the validated Opioid Overdose Knowledge Scale (OOKS) (Williams et al., 2013). Each correct response earned one point, yielding a total score range of 0–10. Perceived competency to manage opioid overdoses was measured with seven items derived from the OOKS and Opioid Overdose Attitudes Scale (OOAS). It assessed perceived confidence in managing overdose situations on a 5-point Likert scale. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency α=0.86∼0.87. Accessibility of naloxone (e.g., “If an overdosed person is found, naloxone is likely present at or near the scene in my community”) was measured with five items on a 5-point Likert scale (α=0.72∼0.80).

Statistical analysis

For overdose mortality analysis, we compared opioid-involved overdose death count changes between 2022 (baseline) and 2024 (about two years into the project) between the intervention and the comparison communities using two-sample proportion difference z-tests (Fleiss et al., 2013). This allowed us to assess whether mortality changes in intervention communities exceeded those in comparison communities beyond secular trends.

For community survey analysis, we examined descriptive statistics for each survey item and calculated weighted proportions for categorical variables and weighted means for continuous variables for analysis. To account for the probability-based complex sampling design, we applied final respondent weights and specified zip codes as the stratification variable. Since the MACRO-B intervention was implemented at the community level precluding individual-level randomization, we analyzed changes in OKADIS survey outcomes using the difference-in-differences (DiD) methods to compute unbiased estimates (Wing et al., 2018; Angrist and Pischke, 2009). The DiD approach estimates the intervention effect by comparing the change in outcomes over time in intervention communities to the change in comparison communities. Mathematically, the DiD estimator is calculated as: (Intervention2025-Intervention2023) -(Comparison2025-Comparison2023). This method controls for time-varying factors that affect both groups similarly (Wing et al., 2018). Because the counterfactual was not observable, we evaluated whether the assumption of parallel trends of DiD appeared plausible by plotting the overdose mortality rates over time separately for the intervention and comparison communities using pre-intervention periods only. The trends looked approximately parallel before intervention, suggesting that the assumption was not violated. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2 and the survey package (Lumley, 2004).

The necessary ethical approval of the study has been obtained and is on file with the authors' institutions. Informed consent was obtained for all participants and IRB approved waiver of documentation of consent. The research team ensured ethical engagement with community members.

Results

Overdose mortality analysis

The mortality data showed a marked decline in Black overdose deaths within the intervention communities. In Year 1, the intervention area achieved a 27% decrease (the rate per 100,000 from 198.1 to 144.8) in opioid overdose deaths relative to the baseline compared to a 7% increase (the rate per 100,000 from 63.5 to 68.2) in the comparison communities (p < 0.01). In Year 2, the intervention area achieved a 45% decrease (the rate per 100,000 = 108.6) in opioid overdose deaths relative to the baseline compared to a 22% decrease (the rate per 100,000 = 51.7) in the comparison communities (p = 0.02).

Policy change

With the use of LECM, we provided education to decisionmakers around drug checking equipment. We observed a possible reflection of increased policymaker understanding in the passage of an amendment to Indiana House Bill 1167, “Exceptions to Paraphernalia Statutes.” This was following an attempt at a similar amendment (House Bill 1053) in the 2024 legislative session, which passed the House but was not discussed in its assigned Senate committee. HB 1167 took effect in July 2025 and created critical exceptions to state paraphernalia laws, paving the way for expanded access to drug checking equipment such as fentanyl and xylazine test strips.

Community survey analysis

Demographic characteristics were comparable between the intervention and comparison communities at baseline. Table 3 presents the difference-in-differences (DiD) estimates for the three primary outcomes, adjusted for the complex sampling design. The intervention communities demonstrated a significant net increase in opioid overdose knowledge compared to the comparison communities (DiD = 0.68, p = 0.03). Likewise, residents in the intervention communities reported significantly higher confidence in their ability to manage overdose situations (DiD = 0.54, p < 0.01) and higher perceived accessibility to naloxone (DiD = 1.06, p < 0.01) compared to the comparison communities.

Discussion

The MACRO-B project provided evidence that an opioid-involved overdose prevention approach can be successful in affecting community-level overdose rates as well as improving knowledge about opioids and overdoses, confidence in the ability to manage overdoses, and perceived access to naloxone. We hypothesize about, but did not directly test, the underlying reasons for success. Specifically: the project was theory-informed, trust was formed among the community coalition members and the research team, and multi-level interventions were meticulously designed and executed. The MACRO-B project sought to integrate Community Coalition Action Theory, the Citizen Health Care Model, and the Legal Epidemiology Competency Model along with dedication of the community coalition members, and ultimately helped achieve a 23% reduction in overdose deaths among Black residents in the intervention communities beyond what was observed in comparator communities affected only by secular trends. This outcome appears to validate our integrated framework, suggesting that layering structural changes creates a synergistic effect.

MACRO-B coalition members reached a consensus in their evaluation that, of the many intervention components, aggressive distribution of naloxone, fentanyl test strips, and xylazine test strips, implementation of the naloxone leave behind initiative, and installation of naloxone boxes in the intervention communities were among the most impactful interventions that helped reduce overdose deaths by 45% (a net decrease of 23%). This reflects the project's “groundwork first” strategy: prioritizing the physical saturation of the community with harm reduction tools before expecting behavioral change. The logistical feat of distributing massive quantities of harm reduction materials and installing permanent naloxone boxes directly dismantled the “resource desert” phenomenon often found in marginalized neighborhoods (Satcher, 2022). Our results suggest that removing physical barriers can be powerful and expeditious mechanism to improve community readiness to tackle overdose deaths, serving as a necessary foundation for subsequent behavioral and perceptual changes.

While knowledge scores improved, the concurrent rise in perceived competency offers a more nuanced insight into the mechanism of change. Ideally, knowledge should translate into action (Morris et al., 2011), but in marginalized communities, mistrust and fear often complicate this relationship (Seo et al., 2023; Benkert et al., 2019). We attribute the observed increase in perceived competency—the confidence to actually intervene—to the CHCM-guided deployment of peer recovery coaches and community overdose education and naloxone administration training (Razaghizad et al., 2021). By utilizing “citizen change agents” who share lived experiences and racial identities with residents, we believe it is plausible that the intervention may have been responsive to the needs expressed in resident interviews (Seo et al., 2023), and may have helped transform abstract medical knowledge into actionable community norms (although direct measurement of these change pathways was beyond the scope of this study). Though we did not compare CHCM-guided deployment to other methods of building competency, our results vis-à-vis control communities suggest the effectiveness of our intervention, which used cultural and social concordance for converting awareness into self-efficacy in distrustful environments (Lee et al., 2025; Katigbak et al., 2015). In 2026, inspired by the success of the MACRO-B Project, the Indiana Governor's Office has requested and funded a MACRO-B expansion project to six Indiana counties (Allen, Delaware, Elkhart, Lake, St. Joseph, and Vanderburgh) that include the comparison communities. The key approach of the expansion projects is CHCM-guided utilization of citizen change agents.

The sustainability of these community-level changes has been solidified by LECM-guided policy education. The passage of Indiana House Bill 1167 represents a transition from temporary programmatic success to quasi-permanent structural protection. By decriminalizing the possession of tools for testing the composition of controlled substances, this legislation will help save lives by relieving fear of carrying drug testing tools such as fentanyl test strips. Policies that are written with awareness of how intersecting stigmas impact behavior could plausibly continue this momentum (Katigbak et al., 2015; Seo et al., n.d.).

This study has several limitations. First, while the DiD design controls for time-invariant confounders, unmeasured time-varying factors could still influence the results. Second, baseline overdose death rates were different between the target communities and the comparison communities. Though the use of DiD design enabled producing unbiased estimates (Wing et al., 2018; Angrist and Pischke, 2009), we cannot preclude selection biases. Third, the findings may not be generalizable to other communities due to the localization of our study to Indiana. Fourth, the reliance on self-reported survey data may have introduced social desirability bias, although the use of anonymous modes aimed to mitigate this. Fifth, relatedly, self-report measures might be subject to recall bias. However, prior methodology studies indicate recall bias is minimal for surveys on behavioral health (Brener et al., 2003; Darke, 1998). The study was structured to measure the overall impact of a complex intervention, and while we have hypotheses and ideas about why certain changes were observed, the quantitative findings speak to the outcomes from the MACRO-B project as a whole. Additional research is needed to disentangle the specific utility of sub-components of the intervention.

Conclusions

Single-level interventions may not be sufficient for addressing complex, structurally rooted health disparities (Cantu et al., 2023). The MACRO-B project illustrates that structural resource distribution coupled with culturally responsive community engagement, peer leadership, and policy change can contribute to a reduction in the incidence of harmful health outcomes like opioid overdose in communities experiencing disproportionate harms relative to the general US population. This integrated model provides a scalable blueprint for advancing equity in marginalized communities, particularly in terms of substance use or overdose prevention.
