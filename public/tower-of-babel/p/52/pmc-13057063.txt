OsDi19-1 Acts Downstream of OsABI5 to Negatively Regulate ABA-Inhibited Seed Germination and Seedling Growth in Rice
Authors: Yupeng Wang, Chenyan Li, Xinyu Li, Jiaojuan Yin, Lingli Wang, Qi Liu, Zhiquan Wang, Ziming Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Seed germination and post-germination seedling growth are critical agronomic traits in the growth process of rice, determining the seedling emergence rate and seedling quality. Previous studies have indicated that members of the Di19 (Drought-induced 19) gene family are involved in the regulation of abscisic acid (ABA)-mediated seed germination and seedling growth. However, the roles of most members and their molecular connections with ABA signaling are not well understood in rice. Here, we found that the osdi19-1 mutant exhibits enhanced ABA sensitivity during seed germination and seedling growth compared to the wild type. The expression of OsDi19-1 was induced by multiple abiotic stresses and functions as a transcription activator, localized in both the cytoplasm and nucleus. We also found that OsDi19-1 regulates the expression of ABA signaling pathway genes during seed germination, without affecting ABA biosynthesis. Furthermore, the expression of OsDi19-1 was repressed in the aba insensitive 5 (osabi5) mutants under ABA treatment, and OsABI5 could bind to the promoter of OsDi19-1 and activated its expression. Taken together, our findings demonstrate that OsDi19-1 acts downstream of OsABI5 to negatively regulate ABA-inhibited seed germination and seedling growth, thereby establishing the molecular link between the core ABA signaling pathway and the Di19 family.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-026-00896-7.

Introduction

Abscisic acid (ABA), a stress-responsive phytohormone, regulates numerous biological processes, including responses to various abiotic stresses, seed germination, seedling growth, flowering time, and seed maturation (Kavi Kishor et al. 2022). Among these, seed germination and post-germination seedling growth are highly sensitive to ABA; even low concentrations can significantly reduce germination rates and inhibit seedling growth (Lin et al. 2015; Yu et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2021; Li et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023). The canonical ABA signaling pathway consists of ABA, ABA receptors (PYRABACTIN RESISTANCE 1 (PYR)/PYR1-LIKE (PYL)/REGULATORY COMPONENTS OF ABA RECEPTORS (RCARs), clade A type 2 C protein phosphatases (PP2Cs), SNF1-related protein kinases (SnRK2s), ABA-responsive element binding factors (ABFs), and downstream ABA-responsive genes (Fujii et al. 2009; Ma et al. 2009; Melcher et al. 2009; Park et al. 2009). In the absence of ABA, the receptors PYR/PYL/RCARs do not bind to PP2Cs, while PP2Cs interact with SnRK2s and inhibit their kinase activity, preventing the activation of ABA-responsive binding factors and keeping the ABA signaling pathway inactive. In the presence of ABA, the ABA-bound PYR/PYL/RCARs complex interacts with PP2Cs and inhibits their phosphatase activity, thereby releasing and activating SnRK2s. The activated SnRK2s then phosphorylate and activate the ABA-responsive binding factors (ABFs), triggering ABA responses and subsequently regulating various biological processes (Cutler et al. 2010).

The ABA signaling pathway comprises multiple members from each component family, and functional defects in any of these members can alter plant sensitivity to ABA, thereby affecting biological processes such as seed germination and seedling growth (Kobayashi et al. 2004; Ma et al. 2009; He et al. 2014). In rice, the ABA receptor PYR/PYL/RCARs family consists of 13 members. When PYL1/2/3/4/5/6/12 are simultaneously knocked out, the mutant exhibits reduced ABA sensitivity and a significantly increased seed germination rate (Miao et al. 2018). Similarly, PYL12 is specifically expressed in mature seeds of Arabidopsis, and its mutant shows decreased ABA sensitivity during seed germination (Zhao et al. 2020). For Arabidopsis PP2Cs, the triple mutant Defective in HYPERSENSITIVE TO ABA 1 (hab1), ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE 1 (ABI1), and ABI2, and the rice mutant ospp2c49, display significantly enhanced ABA sensitivity, leading to markedly suppressed seed germination and seedling growth (Rubio et al. 2009; Miao et al. 2024). In contrast, the SnRK2s kinase mutant snrk2.2 snrk2.3 snrk2.6 exhibits the opposite phenotype (Fujii et al. 2007). Additionally, loss-of-function mutations in the ABFs transcription factor gene ABI5 result in substantially reduced ABA sensitivity across multiple developmental processes, including seed germination and seedling growth (Finkelstein 1994; Zou et al. 2008; Li et al. 2022).

Di19 (Drought-induced 19) proteins are a class of drought-induced transcription factors that are widely present in plant genomes. Structurally, they contain two conserved domains: a Cys2/His2 (C2H2)-type zinc finger domain that specifically recognizes and binds DNA sequences, and a C-terminal Di19_C domain associated with enhanced plant responses to abiotic stresses, including salt, drought, and cold stresses (Liu et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2014, 2016; Feng et al. 2015; Wu et al. 2019). Previous research has revealed that Di19 family members also participate in ABA-regulated seed germination and seedling growth processes. In Arabidopsis, the Atdi19-3 mutant exhibited significantly higher seed germination rates and cotyledon greening rates than wild-type (WT) plants under exogenous ABA treatment, while its overexpression lines showed the opposite phenotype (Qin et al. 2014). In rice, overexpression of OsDi19-4 significantly inhibited seed germination and seedling growth under ABA treatment compared to WT, whereas OsDi19-4 knockdown lines displayed completely opposite phenotypic responses (Wang et al. 2014, 2016). Similarly, overexpression of cotton genes GhDi19-1 and GhDi19-2, as well as wheat TaDi19A, in Arabidopsis markedly slowed seed germination and cotyledon greening rates, accompanied by significantly increased expression levels of several ABA-responsive genes (Li et al. 2010; Qin et al. 2016). These findings demonstrate that Di19 family members extensively participate in ABA-mediated regulation of seed germination and seedling growth. However, the mechanistic interactions between Di19 genes and ABA signaling pathways remain poorly understood.

Here, we identified the Di19 protein OsDi19-1 as a novel transcription activator that negatively regulates ABA-inhibited seed germination and seedling growth in rice. Furthermore, we found that the ABA-responsive transcription factor OsABI5 can bind directly to the promoter of OsDi19-1 and activate its expression under ABA treatment, thus establishing a molecular link between the ABA signaling pathway and the Di19 genes.

Results

OsDi19-1 Negatively Regulates ABA-Inhibited Seed Germination and Seedling Growth

In our previous screening of ABA-responsive genes (Wang et al. 2023), we observed that mutants with a knockout of the OsDi19-1 (LOC_Os05g48800) gene exhibited delayed germination relative to their WT. Specifically, the seed germination rate of the WT reached approximately 85% on the third day after imbibition, whereas the mutant showed only about 45% (Fig. 1). To investigate whether the slower seed germination rate of osdi19-1 is associated with ABA signaling, germination and seedling growth assays were performed on WT and osdi19-1 seeds using MS medium supplemented with varying concentrations of ABA. Compared to the WT, the seed germination rate of osdi19-1 was significantly slower regardless of ABA treatment, but the difference in germination rates between the WT and osdi19-1 increased with higher ABA concentrations (Fig. 2A–D). Additionally, measurements of post-germination seedling height revealed no significant difference between the WT and osdi19-1 in the absence of ABA (Fig. 2E). However, as ABA concentrations increased, the inhibitory effect on seedling height was more pronounced in osdi19-1 than in the WT (Fig. 2E). These results suggest that OsDi19-1 negatively regulates ABA-inhibited seed germination and seedling growth.

It is widely recognized that ABA signaling positively influences plant responses to salt stress and general osmotic stress. Consequently, we investigated whether OsDi19-1 also plays a role in seed germination and seedling growth under conditions of salt and osmotic stress. To this end, germination and seedling growth assays were conducted on WT and osdi19-1 seeds using MS medium supplemented with 150 mM NaCl or 100 mM mannitol. The outcomes revealed that the disparity in seed germination rates between osdi19-1 and WT seeds was significantly more pronounced under 150 mM NaCl or 100 mM mannitol treatment compared to MS medium alone, with similar trends observed in seedling height (Fig. 3). These results suggest that OsDi19-1 positively regulates seed germination and seedling growth in the presence of salt stress and osmotic stress.

OsDi19-1 is an Evolutionarily Conserved Transcription Factor

Sequence analysis showed that OsDi19-1 encodes a typical Di19 transcription factor, featuring a zf-Di19 domain at the N-terminal and a Di19_C domain at the C-terminal (Fig. S1). BLAST searches of databases revealed the widespread presence of OsDi19-1 homologs in land plants; the protein sequences are highly conserved within the zf-Di19 and Di19_C domains (Fig. S2). Phylogenetic analysis suggested that OsDi19-1 homologs diverged between monocotyledons and dicotyledons, indicating that their functions may have evolved differently (Fig. S3). To determine if OsDi19-1 has autonomous transcriptional activation activity, yeast two-hybrid assays were conducted, which showed that OsDi19-1 exhibits self-activation activity (Fig. 4). Additionally, assays involving truncation and domain dissection of the OsDi19-1 protein demonstrated that both the zf-Di19 (amino acids 41–92) and Di19_C (amino acids 114–223) domains possess self-activation activity (Fig. 4). However, the self-activation activity of Di19_C was suppressed when the region between amino acids 92–114 was fused with Di19_C (Fig. 4).

OsDi19-1 is Induced by Multiple Abiotic Stresses and OsDi19-1 is Transcription Activator

To examine the expression pattern of OsDi19-1 in different tissues, we used reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and found that OsDi19-1 was expressed in various tissues, with the highest level in the sheath (Fig. 5A); Furthermore, OsDi19-1 is expressed in both seeds and seedlings, and its expression level is relatively high in dry seeds (Fig. 5B). To confirm whether the expression of OsDi19-1 is regulated by abiotic stress, we investigated the transcriptional profile of OsDi19-1 under various stress conditions and found that the expression of OsDi19-1 was evidently induced by treatment with NaCl, mannitol, and ABA (Fig. 5C).

To investigate the subcellular localization of OsDi19-1 protein, we fused the full-length CDS of OsDi19-1 with GFP and performed a transient expression assay in rice protoplasts, which showed that the OsDi19-GFP fusion protein was localized in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, and it overlapped with the nuclear marker D53-mCherry in the nucleus (Fig. 5D) (Zhou et al. 2013). A luciferase transient transcriptional assay in rice protoplasts revealed that, compared with the control BD, BD-OsDi19-1 exhibited significant transcriptional activation activity (Fig. 5E, F), indicating that OsDi19-1 functions as a transcriptional activator in the nucleus.

OsDi19-1 Regulates the Expression of ABA Signaling Pathway Genes During Seed Germination

To elucidate the gene regulatory network through which OsDi19-1 modulates ABA-inhibited seed germination, we first conducted a comparative analysis of the expression levels of ABA-responsive genes, including rice Early methionine-labeled 1 (OsEM1), ABA insensitive 3 (OsABI3), OsABI5, and Late embryogenesis abundant 3 (LEA3), in germinating seeds of WT and osdi19-1 mutants. At 2 days after imbibition (DAI), under 0 µM ABA treatment conditions, the expression levels of OsEM1 and LEA3 in osdi19-1 showed no significant difference compared with WT, whereas OsABI3 and OsABI5 exhibited a marked increase (Fig. 6A–D). Under 1 µM ABA treatment conditions, the expression of all tested ABA-responsive genes was significantly up-regulated in osdi19-1 relative to WT (Fig. 6A-D). At 3 DAI, except for OsABI5 under ABA treatment (which showed no significant change compared to WT), the expression levels of the other genes were significantly elevated in osdi19-1 under both 0 µM and 1 µM ABA treatments conditions (Fig. 6A-D). These results suggest that ABA signaling is enhanced in the osdi19-1 mutant during germination.

Furthermore, we examined the expression of other core components of the ABA signaling pathway. The results indicated that under 0 µM ABA treatment conditions, the expression of Stress-activated protein kinase 8 (SAPK8) in osdi19-1 showed no significant difference at 2 DAI or 3 DAI compared with WT, but its transcript levels decreased sharply under 1 µM ABA treatment conditions (Fig. 6E). The expression of the other two SAPK genes (SAPK9 and SAPK10) showed no significant changes in either condition between WT and osdi19-1 (Fig. 6F, G). Compared with WT, the expression of rice phosphatase genes Type 2 C protein phosphatase 49 (OsPP2C49) and OsPP2C06 in osdi19-1 exhibited no difference under 0 µM ABA treatment conditions but were drastically down-regulated under 1 µM ABA treatment conditions (Fig. 6H, I); While the expression of rice ABA receptor genes Regulatory components of ABA receptors 5 OsRCAR5 and OsRCAR6 at 3 DAI in osdi19-1 remained unchanged, the expression of OsRCAR5 slightly increased at 2 DAI under 0 µM ABA treatment conditions, but significantly decreased under 1 µM ABA treatment conditions; In addition, OsRCAR10 showed no difference at 2 DAI but was significantly up-regulated in osdi19-1 at 3 DAI under 1 µM ABA treatment conditions (Fig. 6J-L). These results suggested that OsDi19-1 regulates rice seed germination by modulating the expression of core ABA signaling components.

OsDi19-1 does not Affect ABA Biosynthesis

To investigate whether OsDi19-1 affects the ABA biosynthesis pathway, we examined the expression levels of key ABA biosynthetic genes (OsNCED3, OsABA1, OsABA2, and OsAAO3) in both WT and osdi19-1 mutant seeds. The results showed that the expression levels of OsNCED3, OsABA1, OsABA2 in osdi19-1 were not altered relative to that in WT at 2 DAI or 3 DAI under both 0 µM ABA treatment and 1 µM ABA treatment (Fig. 7A–C), while the exception of OsAAO3 exhibited significantly elevated expression at 3 DAI under 0 µM ABA treatment but significantly decreased expression upon 1 µM ABA treatment (Fig. 7D). To further elucidate whether OsDi19-1 affects ABA biosynthesis, we quantified ABA levels in dry seeds of both WT and osdi19-1 and found that the ABA content revealed no significant difference between WT and osdi19-1 seeds (Fig. 7E). These findings indicated that OsDi19-1 does not regulate ABA biosynthesis.

OsABI5 Activates the Transcription of OsDi19-1

Having confirmed that OsDi19-1 negatively regulates ABA signaling and its expression is induced by exogenous ABA, our aim was to explore the molecular mechanism behind ABA-induced OsDi19-1 expression. Promoter sequence analysis revealed four ACGT motifs (ABA-responsive element, ABRE) on the OsDi19-1 promoter (Fig. S4). Previous studies have indicated that group A bZIP transcription factors (bZIPs), activated by SnRK2 kinases, can regulate the expression of ABA-responsive genes by directly binding to the ACGT motif (Jakoby et al. 2002; Amir Hossain et al. 2010; Nakashima and Yamaguchi-Shinozaki 2013). The rice genome contains eleven group A bZIP transcription factors, specifically OsbZIP09, OsbZIP10, OsbZIP12, OsbZIP23, OsbZIP29, OsbZIP40, OsbZIP42, OsbZIP46, OsbZIP62, OsbZIP66, and OsbZIP72 (Lu et al. 2009). Consequently, we hypothesized that OsDi19-1 might be directly regulated by A-group bZIP transcription factors under ABA induction. To test this hypothesis, quantitative transactivation assays in rice protoplasts were conducted, which showed that various bZIPs could activate the luciferase activity of the OsDi19-1pro: LUC reporter gene, but bZIP10’s activation was significantly stronger than that of other bZIPs (Fig. S5). Intriguingly, bZIP10 is the core ABA signaling transcription factor OsABI5, which positively regulates ABA-inhibited seed germination and seedling growth. Hence, we considered OsABI5 as a potential transcriptional activator of OsDi19-1.

To further verify the transcriptional activation of OsDi19-1 by OsABI5, quantitative transactivation assays were performed in rice protoplasts under ABA treatment. The results indicated that OsABI5 significantly activated the OsDi19-1 promoter, irrespective of ABA treatment, although the activation was more pronounced with exogenous ABA (Fig. 8A). Additionally, we examined the expression level of OsDi19-1 in protoplasts and found it to be significantly higher in those expressing OsABI5-GFP compared to those expressing GFP alone (Fig. 8B). Subsequently, ChIP-PCR assays in rice protoplasts revealed that OsABI5 was significantly enriched in the ABRE-containing region of the OsDi19-1 promoter (Fig. 8C). Next, two osabi5 mutants, osabi5-1 and osabi5-2, were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology (Fig. S6A). We found no significant difference in seed germination and seedling growth between WT and osabi5 mutants in MS medium without ABA; however, the osabi5 mutants exhibited accelerated seed germination and seedling growth in the presence of 2 µM ABA compared to WT (Fig. S6B-E), suggesting that osabi5-1 and osabi5-2 are loss-of-function mutants of OsABI5. We checked the mRNA levels of OsDi19-1 in osabi5-1 and osabi5-2, and found that the expression of OsDi19-1 showed no significant difference between WT and osabi5 without ABA (Fig. 8D). However, under ABA treatment, it was significantly lower in osabi5 compared to WT (Fig. 8D). Taken together, our data demonstrated that OsABI5 can bind to the OsDi19-1 promoter and activate its expression.

Discussion

In this study, we collected several lines of evidence to support the notion that OsDi19-1 acts downstream of OsABI5 to negatively regulate ABA-inhibited seed germination and seedling growth in rice. First, the osdi19-1 mutant displays enhanced ABA sensitivity during seed germination and seedling growth relative to WT (Fig. 2). Second, OsDi19-1 is expressed both during seed germination and early seedling growth, and its expression is induced by exogenous ABA (Fig. 5A-C). Third, the expression of ABA-responsive genes was significantly up-regulated in osdi19-1 relative to WT during seed germination (Fig. 6A-D). Fourth, our molecular and biochemical interaction experiments revealed that OsABI5 can bind to the promoter of OsDi19-1 to activate its expression under ABA treatment (Fig. 8). Based on these findings, we propose that OsDi19-1 acts as a negative regulator in ABA-mediated inhibition of seed germination and seedling growth, and OsABI5, a core transcription factor of ABA signaling components, may weaken ABA response to a moderate level by directly activating the expression of OsDi19-1, thereby enabling rice to maintain an optimal growth state. The Di19 family members have been extensively reported to participate in ABA-mediated regulation of seed germination and seedling growth (Li et al. 2010; Liu et al. 2013; Qin et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2014; Feng et al. 2015; Wu et al. 2019). However, the molecular connections between the Di19 family and the core ABA signaling pathway are still poorly understood. Here, we found that OsDi19-1 negatively regulates ABA-inhibited seed germination and seedling growth, while the ABA core responsive factor OsABI5 can bind directly to the promoter of OsDi19-1 and activate its expression. Our study not only identifies OsDi19-1 as a novel negative regulator of ABA-inhibited seed germination and seedling growth but also reveals the molecular link between the core ABA signaling pathway and the Di19 family, providing new theoretical insights into the regulation of ABA response.

As a stress-response hormone, ABA also inhibits plant growth when its signaling is excessively strong (Lin et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2020; Du et al. 2024). Therefore, precise regulation of ABA signaling is crucial for normal plant growth and development. During the early stages of the stress response, ABA signaling progressively intensifies to enhance the plant’s stress resistance; however, once the stress is alleviated or the plant adapts to the environment, ABA signaling must be suppressed to allow growth recovery (Achard et al. 2006; Tang et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2018a, b). In this study, the expression level of OsDi19-1 showed no significant increase within the first 6 h after exogenous ABA application, but became markedly induced only after 12 h (Fig. 5C). We therefore propose that OsABI5-mediated activation of OsDi19-1 expression primarily occurs during the late phase of the ABA signaling response, which could be a feedback regulatory mechanism for modulating ABA signaling levels in plants. This likely represents an exquisite strategy employed by plants to strictly limit the maximum duration of ABA signaling, thereby maintaining ABA signaling at a moderate level to achieve an optimal balance between stress response and growth. This regulatory pattern resembles the previously discovered feedback mechanism between ABA signaling and other signaling pathways (Hu and Yu 2014; Lin et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2018a, b, 2023). Such feedback regulation of ABA signaling is critically important for seed germination and seedling growth under stress conditions.

It has been reported that OsDi19-4, a member of Di19 family, positively regulates ABA-inhibited seed germination and seedling growth, and it can directly the promoter of two ABA responsive genes, OsASPG1 and OsNAC18, to activate their expression (Wang et al. 2016). We analyzed the protein sequences of OsDi19-4 and OsDi19-1, and found that their homology in sequence is only 46.6%, and they have relatively similar sequences in zf-Di19 domain, while the sequences in other regions show significant differences (Fig. S7), indicating that their functions may be quite different. Additionally, another study has shown that OsDi19-1 and OsDi19-4 can form heterodimers (Wang et al. 2014), which indicated that they may regulate ABA-inhibited seed germination and seedling growth by functioning antagonistically. To explore this possibility, we detected the expression levels of OsDi19-4, OsASPG1 and OsNAC18 in WT and osdi19-1. The results showed that compared with WT, the expression levels of OsDi19-4 was not changed, while the expression of OsASPG1 and OsNAC18 was decreased significantly in osdi19-1 (Fig. S8). This is contrary to the results in the OsDi19-4 overexpression line, suggesting that OsASPG1 and OsNAC18 may not be the common causes of the inhibition of seed germination and seedling growth in osdi19-1 and OE-OsDi19-4 lines. Therefore, whether OsDi19-1 and OsDi19-4 antagonistically regulate ABA-inhibited seed germination and seedling growth requires a more in-depth exploration.

Previous studies have demonstrated that members of the Di19 gene family can enhance plant stress responses by activating downstream gene expression (Liu et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2016). Specifically, OsDi19-4 strengthens ABA signaling responses by directly binding to the promoters of OsASPG1 and OsNAC18 and activating their expression (Wang et al. 2016). In Arabidopsis, Di19 positively regulates drought resistance by directly binding to the promoters of pathogen-related genes PR1, PR2 and PR5, and activating their expression (Liu et al. 2013). In soybean, GmDi19-5 interacts with GmLEA3, though the biological significance of this interaction remains unclear (Feng et al. 2015). In our study, we observed that compared to WT, most positive regulators of ABA signaling showed significantly increased expression in osdi19-1 mutants in seedlings grown on MS medium supplemented with exogenous ABA, while negative regulators exhibited markedly decreased expression (Fig. 6). Notably, ABA content remained unchanged in osdi19-1 (Fig. 7E), indicating that OsDi19-1 does not affect ABA biosynthesis. Since OsDi19-1 functions as a transcription activator, we hypothesize it may promote seed germination and seedling growth by activating the expression of ABA signaling negative regulators. Recently, it has been reported that OsPP2C49 overexpression led to decreased ABA sensitivity, and the opposite phenotypes were observed in ospp2c49 knockout mutants (Miao et al. 2024). Interestingly, we found that the expression of known ABA signaling repressors, phosphatases OsPP2C49 and OsPP2C06, dramatically decreased in osdi19-1 mutants under ABA treatment (Fig. 6H, I). This raises an important question for future investigation: Could OsDi19-1 potentially suppress ABA signaling by directly regulating the expression of these PP2Cs?

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the functions of OsDi19-1, a member of the Di19 gene family, revealing its role in ABA-inhibited seed germination and seedling growth in rice. We have found that OsDi19-1 acts as a transcription activator to accelerate seed germination and seedling growth by regulating the expression of ABA signaling genes in rice, without affecting ABA biosynthesis. Additionally, OsABI5 can bind to the OsDi19-1 promoter and activate its expression, thus establishing a molecular link of feedback regulation between the Di19 family and ABA signaling.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growing Conditions

The mutant materials used in this study, including osdi19-1-1, osdi19-1-3, osabi5-1, and osabi5-2, were generated via CRISPR/Cas9 technology. The osdi19-1-1 and osdi19-1-3 mutants were derived from the japonica rice cultivar Kitaake, while osabi5-1 and osabi5-2 were generated in the Nipponbare background. All phenotypic characterization experiments were conducted using T2 generation transgenic plants or later. For seed germination assays, exogenous ABA treatments, gene expression analyses, transient transformation experiments, and ChIP-qPCR assays, rice materials were cultivated in a growth chamber (PERCIVAL, E-41L2) under controlled conditions: 12-hour light/12-hour darkness photoperiod with corresponding temperatures of 30 °C (light) and 25 °C (darkness), and maintained at 70% relative humidity.

Vector Construction and Rice Transformation

To generate the osdi19-1 and osabi5 mutants, we designed specific 18-bp sgRNAs targeting OsDi19-1 or OsABI5 using the CRISPR target design website (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/cgi-bin/CRISPR/CRISPR). The selected sgRNAs were subsequently cloned into the sgRNA/Cas9 vector following a previously reported method (Miao et al. 2013). The resulting positive constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 and then transformed into calli of japonica rice cultivars Kitaake or Nipponbare, using the method described by Hiei et al. (1994). Positive transgenic progeny were identified through sequencing verification.

Germination and Seedling Growth Assays

Seed germination and seedling growth assays were conducted following previously established protocols (Wang et al. 2023). Briefly, rice seeds were dehulled and then surface-sterilized using 70% ethanol (v/v) for one minute, followed by a single rinse with sterile water. Subsequently, the seeds were subjected to a 30% NaClO (w/v) treatment for 30 min, with gentle shaking every 10 min. After undergoing five washes with sterile water, the seeds were soaked in sterile water for an additional 30 min. The sterilized seeds were then placed on half-strength MS medium (pH 5.7) (M524, Phyto Technology Lab), supplemented with 0.4% Gelzan (w/v; G3251, Phyto Technology Lab) and varying concentrations of ABA. Germination rates were documented starting 48 h post-sowing, with seeds being considered germinated when the coleoptile length surpassed 0.5 cm. Germination counts were examined at 12-hour intervals, spanning a total of seven time points. Seedling height was measured on the sixth day post-sowing. Each experiment consisted of three biological replicates, with 30 seeds per replicate.

RT-qPCR Analyses

Seeds or seedlings were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using the ZYMO RNA extraction kit (ZYMO Research, R2024). The RNA samples were then reverse transcribed into cDNA using dNTPs (TaKaRa, 4019), Oligo (dT)18 primer (TaKaRa, 3806), RNase inhibitor (TaKaRa, 2313 A), and PrimeScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (TaKaRa, 2690 A). Gene expression levels were quantified by real-time PCR using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq reagent kit. The rice Ubiquitin gene (UBQ, LOC_Os01g22490) was used as an internal control. The reactions were performed on either an ABI7500 Real-Time PCR System or an ABI QuantStudio 7 Flex instrument.

Subcellular Localization of OsDi19-1

The coding sequence of OsDi19-1 was amplified and cloned into the N-terminus of GFP in the pAN580-GFP vector to generate the 35 S: OsDi19-1-GFP recombinant construct. The recombinant plasmid was co-transformed with the nuclear marker plasmid 35 S: D53-mCherry into rice protoplasts according to a previously reported method (Zhang et al. 2011). After incubation in darkness at 25 °C for approximately 16 h, fluorescence signals were observed and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM980, Zeiss, Germany).

Luciferase Transient Transcriptional Activity Assay

The transcriptional activity assay was performed according to the method described by Wang et al. (2022). The coding sequence of BD-OsDi19-1 was cloned into the effector vector and fused with the GAL4 sequence via Gateway recombination, generating the recombinant construct 35Spro-GAL4:BD-OsDi19-1. Subsequently, the effector plasmids (35Spro-GAL4:BD-OsDi19-1 and 35Spro-GAL4:BD) were co-transformed with two reporter plasmids (35Spro-GAL4:LUC and 35Spro: REN) into rice protoplasts. After incubation in darkness at 25 °C for approximately 14 h, the activities of LUC and REN enzymes were measured using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (E2920, Promega). Firefly luciferase (LUC) activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase (REN) internal control for quantitative analysis.

UPLC-MS/MS Analysis of ABA

Approximately 0.05 g of dry seeds from both WT and osdi19-1 mutants were ground into powder using liquid nitrogen. The ABA content was then analyzed by UPLC-MS/MS according to the method described by Lin et al. (2015).

Quantitative Transactivation Assays

A 2,000-bp promoter sequence of OsDi19-1 was cloned into the NcoI site of the pGreenII0800-LUC vector to generate the reporter construct (OsDi19-1pro: LUC). The effector plasmids comprised OsbZIPs-GFP fusion constructs, with an empty GFP vector serving as the negative control. The reporter plasmid (OsDi19-1pro: LUC) was co-transformed with either OsbZIPs-GFP or the Empty GFP into rice protoplasts. After 16 h of incubation in darkness at 25 °C, the activities of the LUC and REN enzymes were measured using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (E2920, Promega). LUC activity was normalized to REN as an internal control for quantitative analysis.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR Assay in Rice Protoplasts

The ChIP assay was conducted in rice protoplasts following the protocol outlined by Wang et al. (2023). Approximately 40 µg of either 35Spro: OsABI5-GFP or 35Spro: GFP was introduced into rice protoplasts. Following a 16-hour incubation in the dark at 25 °C, the protoplasts were crosslinked using 1% formaldehyde (final concentration). Subsequently, chromatin was extracted from the samples, and DNA was fragmented to 200–500 bp using sonication. GFP-tagged proteins were immunoprecipitated using an Anti-GFP antibody (Roche, 11814460001) and Protein A magnetic beads (Merck Millipore, 16–661). The ChIPed DNA fragments were then utilized for quantitative PCR.
