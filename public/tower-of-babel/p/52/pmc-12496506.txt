Introduction

Endovascular treatment (EVT) for middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysms is challenging. Currently, for MCA bifurcation wide-necked aneurysms, stent-assisted coiling is still a useful choice [1]. The Neuroform Atlas stent (NAS) (Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, CA, USA) is a self-expanding nitinol hybrid-cell stent that has been widely used to assist in the coiling of MCA bifurcation aneurysms [2]. Uncommonly, during NAS deployment from the MCA upper/lower trunk to the main trunk, proximal migration into the aneurysm may occur due to stent displacement, undersizing, or trans-cell technique manipulation. While this complicates EVT, the migrated NAS can paradoxically facilitate coiling by providing intraneurysmal scaffolding (Fig. 1).

This report delineates the management strategy for this rare complication, providing clinically actionable guidance for practitioners, and is in line with the 2025 SCARE checklist [3]. No artificial intelligence was used in the manuscript development.

Presentation of cases

Patient 1

A 49-year-old female underwent a routine physical examination, and a large aneurysm of the right MCA bifurcation was found via computed tomography (CT) angiography (CTA). She was previously healthy. On physical examination, no neurologic deficit was detected. After one week of dual antiplatelet therapy consisting of oral aspirin (100 mg daily) and clopidogrel (75 mg daily), EVT was subsequently performed.

Under general anesthesia, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) revealed a large aneurysm at the right MCA bifurcation (Fig. 2A). NAS-assisted coiling was performed. After the 6F distal access catheter reached the petrous internal carotid artery. An XT-17 microcatheter (Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, CA, USA) was used to catheterize the upper trunk of the MCA. Afterward, an NAS 4 × 24 mm in size was deployed to cover the aneurysm neck. During NAS deployment, the proximal NAS unintentionally migrated into the aneurysm (Fig. 2B). Salvage coiling began, and an SL-10 microcatheter (Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, CA, USA) was used to deliver 10 mm × 30 cm, 5 mm × 20 cm and 3 mm × 10 cm target coils (Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, CA, USA) to coil the aneurysm (Fig. 2C). The aneurysm was satisfactorily coiled, and the upper/lower and main trunks of the MCA were patent (Fig. 2D).

Following EVT, the patient exhibited no new neurological deficits and was discharged. The patient was administered dual antiplatelet therapy consisting of aspirin (100 mg/day) and clopidogrel (75 mg/day) until the six-month follow-up. At the six-month telephone follow-up, the patient remained neurologically intact and asymptomatic. However, she declined the recommended follow-up angiography. The dual antiplatelet therapy was discontinued and switched to aspirin monotherapy (100 mg/day).

Patient 2

A 58-year-old male presented with sudden, severe headache lasting 5 h. He was previously healthy. On physical examination, his neck was stiff, and he could answer questions correctly. He had grade V muscle strength in the upper and lower limbs. The Hunt–Hess score was Grade I. CT revealed a subarachnoid hemorrhage (Fig. 3A). CTA revealed aneurysmal dilatation of the right MCA with a ruptured bleb (Fig. 3B).

Following the administration of a loading dose of dual antiplatelet therapy with oral aspirin (300 mg) and clopidogrel (300 mg), EVT was performed. Under general anesthesia, DSA confirmed aneurysmal dilatation of the MCA with a ruptured bleb (Fig. 3C). After the 6F distal access catheter reached the petrous internal carotid artery, an XT-17 microcatheter was catheterized into the upper trunk of the MCA to deliver a 4.0 × 24-mm NAS. During NAS deployment, the proximal NAS unintentionally migrated into the MCA aneurysm (Fig. 4A). Another XT-17 microcatheter was catheterized into the lower trunk of the MCA to protect this trunk. An Echelon-10 microcatheter (Medtronic, Irvine, CA, USA) was used to deliver 5 mm × 15 cm, 4 mm × 15 cm, 3 mm × 10 cm and 3 mm × 10 cm target coils to perform the salvage coiling (Fig. 4B). The MCA aneurysm was coiled satisfactorily, and the microcatheter in the lower trunk was removed. The upper/lower and main trunks of the MCA were patent (Fig. 4C). After EVT, the patient recovered well and was discharged.

The patient was administered aspirin (100 mg/day) and clopidogrel (75 mg/day) until the six-month follow-up. At follow-up, he returned to normal life. Magnetic resonance angiography revealed that the aneurysm had complete occlusion. The dual antiplatelet therapy was switched to aspirin monotherapy (100 mg/day).

Discussion

MCA bifurcation aneurysms can be treated with a variety of EVT techniques and devices, including single coiling, balloon-assisted coiling, stent-assisted coiling with the NAS, Enterprise stents (Codman Neurovascular, Raynham, MA, USA), Solitaire (Medtronic, Irvine, CA, USA), low-profile visualized intraluminal support (LVIS) stents (MicroVention Inc., Aliso Viejo, CA, USA), and LEO stents (Balt Extrusion, Montmorency, France) [4]. Stent-like devices, including the PulseRider device (Pulsar Vascular, Los Gatos, CA, USA) and the eCLIPs device (Evasc Medical Systems Corp, Vancouver, BC, Canada), can also be used to assist in the coiling of MCA bifurcation aneurysms [5]. In addition, a flow diverter and a Woven EndoBridge (WEB) device can be used to treat MCA bifurcation aneurysms in select cases (Fig. 5).

Currently, stent-assisted coiling remains popular and is effective for treating MCA bifurcation wide-necked aneurysms. In particular, the NAS, as a low-profile laser-cut microstent, can be delivered through a 0.0165-in. microcatheter, which is optimal for navigation and placement, particularly into MCA branches [2,6]. ‌Given the compliant nature of the NAS, device oversizing or excessive pushing force during deployment may lead to proximal NAS migration into MCA bifurcation aneurysms, as demonstrated in our two clinical cases [7]. To prevent proximal NAS migration, stable anchoring necessitates meticulous selection of both adequate sizing and sufficient landing zone length. In addition, when the trans-cell technique is employed, gentle manipulation is strongly recommended to ensure procedural safety. With respect to planned double stenting procedures, adopting the parallel stenting technique instead of the trans-cell Y-configuration prevents displacement of the first NAS into the aneurysm during microguidewire/microcatheter traversal of the initial NAS (Fig. 6).

In our clinical cases, when the NAS migrated into the aneurysm following complete deployment, retrieval attempts proved both technically challenging and high risk due to the open-cell structure of the stent becoming firmly embedded in the vessel wall. The conventional Solitaire stent retrieval system was ineffective at extracting the NAS, with significant potential for vessel injury during manipulation. While Vora et al. reported successful retrieval of a migrated Neuroform EZ stent and associated coil mass from the vertebral artery using an L5 Merci Retrieve device (Concentric Medical, Mountain View, CA, USA), postprocedural analysis revealed stent fracture with coil integration [8]. Given these outcomes, this technique has not been adopted as standard practice. Retrieval into the delivery microcatheter proved unfeasible even when the NAS remained in a partially deployed configuration. While Kanazawa et al. successfully retrieved the half-released NAS in two patients by keeping the delivery wire locked to the microcatheter, clinical practice does not recommend the retrieval of a partially deployed NAS due to the potential risk of vascular injury from retraction of the NAS [9]. When mandatory stent retrieval is indicated, early surgical vessel opening may ultimately occur [10].

Once the NAS is fully or partially deployed, it must remain in situ regardless of suboptimal positioning. ‌Surgical vessel opening is not routine, as the proximal NAS may serve as an intrasaccular scaffold to protect MCA branches during off-label salvage coiling. ‌In both cases in this report, EVT was completed with salvage coiling. ‌The salvage coiling technique was similar to that of intraneurysmal stenting-assisted coiling. Intraneurysmal stenting to assist coiling is not a new technique.

In 2006, Horowitz et al. proposed a novel application of the Neuroform stent called the waffle-cone technique. This technique involves the placement of the proximal stent into the parent vessel and the distal stent into the aneurysm sac. The stent tip prevents coil extravasation into the parent vessel by providing resistance [11]. Since then, the waffle-cone technique has been used in practice. Subsequently, the pCONus device (Phenox GmbH, Bochum, Germany), which consists of stent-like self-expanding nitinol implants that blossom like a flower inside the aneurysm, was optimized to facilitate the waffle-cone technique. The pCONus device can allow safe coiling of wide-neck MCA aneurysms [12].

Despite successful salvage coiling after proximal NAS migration into the MCA aneurysm in these two cases, the procedure remains high risk and particularly challenging. Proximal NAS tip markers pose a risk of perforating the fragile aneurysm wall, whereas proximal stent deformation may compromise its thrombogenic properties, resulting in thrombosis of the MCA trunk. In addition, ‌coil herniation, specifically loop protrusion into the MCA branch, represents a critical procedural complication [13]. As a last-resort intervention, key technical recommendations for salvage coiling include (1) precise coil deployment control at the MCA bifurcation zone (main trunk junction with upper/lower trunks), where real-time fluoroscopic visualization and vessel protection are mandatory (Fig. 1); (2) standard 6-month dual antiplatelet therapy; and (3) additional coiling for aneurysm recurrence during follow-up.

Conclusion

In the event of unintentional proximal NAS migration into the MCA aneurysm, the malpositioned proximal stent may serve as an intrasaccular scaffold to maintain patency of the upper and lower trunks of the MCA during subsequent salvage coiling procedures. However, recognizing that the NAS is not approved for intra-aneurysmal deployment is imperative, and such off-label use should not be advocated.

Limitations

This study has two notable limitations: Patient 1 declined follow-up angiography, and Patient 2 provided only six months of magnetic resonance angiography data without DSA confirmation. More comprehensive long-term angiographic follow-up is necessary to thoroughly assess the outcomes of salvage coiling after accidental proximal migration of the NAS into an MCA aneurysm. A comparative analysis or follow-up study comparing outcomes between patients who undergo standard procedures and those whose intraprocedural stent migration is managed successfully could further substantiate the clinical utility of this approach. Despite these limitations, this case report highlights the important clinical relevance of an effective bailout strategy for this rare procedural complication.
