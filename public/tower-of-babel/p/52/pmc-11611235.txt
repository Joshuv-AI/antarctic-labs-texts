Spatial transcriptomic analysis deciphers adipocyte-to-fibroblast transformation in bleomycin-induced murine skin fibrosis
Authors: Yixiang Zhang, Jiahao He, Fangzhou Xie, Shengzhou Shan, Jiaqi Qin, Chuandong Wang, Qingfeng Li, Yun Xie, Bin Fang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Scleroderma is characterized by inflammation and fibrosis, predominantly occurring in the skin and extending to various parts of the body. The pathophysiology of scleroderma is multifaceted, with the current understanding including endothelial damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and fibroblast activation in its progression. Nonetheless, the mechanism of cellular interactions and the precise spatial distribution of these cellular events within the fibrotic tissues remain elusive, highlighting a critical gap in our comprehensive understanding of scleroderma’s pathogenesis.Methods:In this study, we administered bleomycin intradermally to the dorsal skin of four individual murine models. Subsequently, skin tissues were harvested at predetermined intervals for comprehensive spatial transcriptomic analysis to determine the spatial dynamics influencing scleroderma pathogenesis. To validate the possible results from bioinformatic analysis, further in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted.Results:Analysis of the spatial transcriptome revealed significant alterations in cell clusters during the progression of scleroderma. Gene Ontology analysis identified disruptions in lipid metabolism as the disease advanced. Pseudotime analysis provided evidence for a phenotypic transition from adipocytes to fibroblasts. In vitro studies demonstrated increased expression of Col1a1 and α-SMA as the disease progressed. These fibroblasts have been identified as key contributors to the increasing inflammation. Co-culturing TGF-β induced adipocytes with RAW264.7 cells resulted in overexpression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the RAW264.7 cells. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed adipocyte loss and fibroblast formation, with transformed fibroblasts showing pronounced pro-inflammatory characteristics, highlighting their crucial role in the disease mechanism.Conclusions:Our study showed the spatial distribution and dynamic alterations of various cell types during scleroderma progression. Crucially, we identified the transformation of adipocytes into fibroblasts as a key factor promoting disease advancement. These emergent fibroblasts intensify inflammation, indicating that research on these cell clusters could reveal key scleroderma mechanisms and guide future therapies.

Introduction

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a rare auto-immune connective tissue disease with a high mortality rate due to fibrosis and vasculopathy. The estimated prevalence is 30–120 cases per million.[1] The disease predominantly occurs in the skin and further extends to internal organs. Thereby, it is further divided into different classifications according to skin involvement, including limited cutaneous SSc and diffuse cutaneous SSc.

One of the main characteristics of SSc is progressive fibrosis of the skin and internal organs. The fibrosis can lead to significant organ dysfunction and even death.[234] The effector cells in the progression of fibrosis in SSc are persistently activated fibroblasts, termed as myofibroblasts.[5] Myofibroblasts further increased the deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) components, most predominantly type I collagen, resulting in skin thickening.

Myofibroblasts are commonly detected in the deep dermis. They are mesenchymal cells of fibroblast lineage that are transiently activated during normal wound healing and should be deactivated shortly afterward.[6] In contrast to fibroblasts, myofibroblasts express the contractile protein alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and form characteristic stress fibers.[7] Current knowledge on their persistent development in SSc showed two key factors such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and mechanical forces.[8] Despite these molecular bases, some of the studies show that myofibroblasts may transform from other cell types, including some progenitor cells and pericytes during disease progression.[9] Further, their interaction with other cells is also critical as they are easily regulated by various cytokines and growth factors released from different cell types.[10]

To study the interaction between myofibroblasts and other cell types as well as their possible origin, we focused on its main location, which is the deep dermis. It is reported that there is one tissue in the deep dermis that is gradually lost during disease progression, which is dermal white adipose tissue (dWAT).[11] dWAT is a unique adipose tissue that lies within the skin, which is distinct from subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), and seems to be required for skin barrier function.[12] Loss of this tissue, which is a prominent feature of human SSc and mouse models of the disease, adversely affects skin homeostasis and promotes dermal fibrosis. More interestingly, studies showed that there is a downregulation of adipocyte markers such as fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ2 (PPARγ2), and adiponectin in the early stages of SSc.[131415] With the help of these studies, we inferred that adipocytes located in the dWAT may be an important source of myofibroblasts. Its transformation from adipocytes to fibroblasts in dWAT may be a disease-promoting factor.

One obstacle to studying adipocytes-to-fibroblasts transformation in dWAT is the isolation of dWAT. Previous studies have only detected all cells in the whole skin tissue using single-cell RNA (scRNA) sequencing. To detect specific alterations in dWAT, we applied spatial transcriptome analysis to study SSc. We injected bleomycin into the dorsal skin of the mouse to induce SSc model.[16] We collected the skin sample with different intervention duration at the same time, applied spatial transcriptome analysis, and showed the specific spatial locations of different cell types in the progression of SSc. Our studies showed the general spatial distribution of different cell types during the progression of SSc. Importantly, we showed the transformation from adipocytes to fibroblasts and the pro-inflammatory profile of these newly transformed fibroblasts. These new findings we studied here may provide new ideas for future clinical treatment.

Methods

Animal experiments

Animal studies were performed in compliance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (No. SH9H-2021-A85-1). Wild-type 8-week-old C57BL/6J strains purchased from the Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital (SYXK2020-0025) were housed under strict pathogen-free conditions. For the experiments, mice received an intradermal injection of 100 μL of bleomycin in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) at a 1 mg/mL concentration into the dorsal skin. Skin samples from the treated area were then collected on the same day with different intervention duration (5 days, 14 days, and 28 days following the injection of bleomycin).[17] Skin from mice that did not receive bleomycin served as the control (designated as day 0). All mice utilized in the study were of identical age, and skin samples were harvested simultaneously to guarantee uniform conditions across all specimens. These samples were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm for further analysis.

Spatial transcriptome analysis

Spatial transcriptome analysis was performed by Shanghai OE Biotech Co. (Shanghai, China). Optimal cutting temperature-embedded mouse back skin was cryosectioned to a thickness of 10 μm. Tissue sections were layered onto a visium spatial tissue optimization slide containing oligonucleotides for mRNA capture (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, USA). To synthesize fluorescently labeled cDNA, a master mix containing reverse transcription reagents and fluorescently labeled nucleotides was added on top of the tissue sections. Fluorescent cDNA covalently linked to oligonucleotides was left on the slide after enzymatic removal of tissues. Fluorescent cDNA was visualized under fluorescence imaging conditions and verified using the visium imaging test slide. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and fluorescence images were compared. The permeabilization time that resulted in maximum fluorescent signals with the lowest signal diffusion was optimal. The libraries were sequenced with paired-end 150-bp sequencing (PE150) using the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The space ranger showed the capture area of the tissue in the slide and differentiated reads for each spot based on spatial barcode information. Spatial transcriptome analysis by RNA sequencing (STAR) was used to assess the sample quality by the total number of spots, the number of pairs of reads in each spot, the number of detected genes, and the number of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs). Loupe Browser (10× Genomics) was used to visually analyze the sequencing results.[18]

Raw sequencing data processing

Output from the visium spatial RNA-seq and images captured using both bright-field and fluorescence microscopy was processed using Space Ranger software (version 1.1.0) (10× Genomics).[19] This analysis involved identifying tissue sections, aligning sequence reads, creating matrices for individual spots, conducting clustering, analyzing gene expression, and mapping the spatial locations of these spots on the slide. This integrated approach leverages algorithms specific to visium alongside STAR, a highly regarded aligner for RNA-seq data.

ST data basic analysis

Initial statistical evaluations were conducted on the gene-spot matrices produced by Space Ranger, including the enumeration of detected UMIs and nUMI and genes (nGene) per spot. Following this, basic quality control (QC) measures were implemented. Specifically, spots characterized by notably low counts of nUMI or nGene, as well as those isolated from the primary tissue segments, were excluded. Additionally, genes that appeared in fewer than three spots were omitted, alongside the exclusion of mitochondrial and ribosomal genes, to refine the data further.

Following QC procedures, we employed the harmony package (version 1.0) (10× Genomics)[20] in R for integrating expression data from distinct sections within each sample and utilized Seurat (version 3.1.5) (10× Genomics)[21] for subsequent basic analysis and visualization. Initially, we aggregated the expression matrices for each sample, subjecting them to normalization, logarithmic transformation, centering, and scaling. Subsequently, we pinpointed 2000 genes exhibiting high variability based on their mean expression levels and variances. Utilizing these genes, we conducted principal components analysis (PCA) to map the spots into a reduced-dimensional space delineated by the leading 20 principal components (PCs). To mitigate batch effects, we designated the section source as the batch variable and applied the “RunHarmony” function for iterative adjustments of the spots’ reduced-dimensional PC representations. Following this adjustment, we used the refined PC matrices for unsupervised shared-nearest-neighbor (SNN) clustering and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization. Moreover, to evaluate clusters at the gene level, we determined differentially expressed genes across all or selected clusters through fold change analysis and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, applying Bonferroni correction for statistical significance.

Cell type scoring by a signature-based strategy

Given the resolution of the current visium spatial transcriptomics, each spot encompasses roughly 8–20 cells, which prevents the precise attribution of specific cell types to each spot. In light of this, to analyze the distribution of cell types within the tissue sections, we introduced a signature-based method for evaluating cell type enrichment within individual spots. Initially, we assembled a collection of gene signatures for prevalent cell types in scleroderma, drawing from xCell signatures[22] and prior biological understanding. Subsequently, we computed cell type scores based on the average log-transformed normalized expression values of signature genes. Utilizing these scores allows for the assessment of relative cell type enrichment across various tissue areas.

Pseudotime analysis

Trajectory analysis was conducted utilizing the Monocle framework employing discriminative dimensionality reduction with trees and preset configurations. The selection of marker genes from the outcomes of Seurat clustering, along with the raw expression data from cells that met the filtering criteria, preceded the use of Monocle. The process involved pseudotime analysis, from which branch expression analysis modeling was employed to examine genes critical in dictating branch destinies.

Cell communication analysis

In the stLearn (v0.4.11) (10× Genomics) framework,[23] cell–cell interaction (CCI) analysis is conducted by integrating various types of data, such as established ligand-receptor pairs, gene expression profiles, spatial positioning, and the distribution of cell types across spatial domains. This approach enables the identification of tissue regions with enhanced cellular communication. The methodology involves several key steps: initially, loading established ligand-receptor pairs and applying the st.tl.cci.run function with standard settings (min_spots = 20, distance = None, pval_adj_cutoff = 0.05) to identify significant ligand–receptor interactions. Subsequently, leveraging cell type abundance in each spot and significant interaction data, cell type interaction networks are deduced. This step utilizes the st.tl.cci.run_cci function’s default parameters (min_spots = 3, cell_prop_cutoff = 0.20). The analysis culminates in the graphical representation of CCI findings via network plots, circos plots, and heatmaps, providing a comprehensive view of cellular interactions within the tissue.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Ontology (GO), and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) analysis

GSVA utilized gene sets from MSigDB[24] to pinpoint pathways and cellular processes that were prominently featured in various clusters, employing the cluster-averaged log-transformed expression matrix for this assessment.

For the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), the Seurat package was employed, setting the significance threshold at a P-value of less than 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change greater than 1 (or 0.58 for a less stringent threshold). Subsequent analyses of DEG enrichment, both in terms of GO and KEGG pathways, were conducted using R, leveraging the hypergeometric distribution to ascertain significant enrichments.

Immunofluorescence

Samples were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde and then subjected to a series of preparation steps including dehydration, clarification, and embedding in paraffin. The sections were then deparaffinized using xylene in two 5-min steps and progressively rehydrated in alcohols of decreasing concentration. Antigen retrieval was carried out in a heating oven, after which the samples were blocked using PBS mixed with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). The sections were incubated with a primary antibody at 4°C overnight (CD86, perilipin, Col1a1, Santa Cruz, USA), then with fluorescently tagged secondary antibodies. Nuclei staining was achieved using 4′-6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) before the sections were covered with an anti-fade mounting solution. Fluorescence microscopy is used for examining and documenting the results.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Tissue specimens were pulverized in liquid nitrogen, then lysed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen Co., Ltd., NY, USA). Following the extraction of RNA as per the provided guidelines, reverse transcription was executed utilizing the PrimeScript Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR analysis was then performed with the SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara Bio Inc.). The primer of each gene is provided in the Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81.

Western blotting

For different groups, tissues underwent lysis in Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay buffer containing protease inhibitors (Servicebio Co., Ltd., China) on ice for 30 min, followed by supernatant collection via centrifugation. Proteins separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis were then transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and blocked with 5% BSA (Servicebio Co., Ltd.) at room temperature for an hour. Overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies (αSMA, Col1a1, Gapdh, cell signaling technology, Inc) was carried out. After 1-h incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies (Servicebio Co., Ltd.), the presence of proteins was visualized using a chemiluminescence detection system.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data were processed and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad software, Boston, USA) and SPSS software version 19.0 (IBM, NY, USA). Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median, and categorical data were expressed in numbers (percentage). Analysis of variance and Mann–Whitney U test were used to compare differences between two or more groups. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Exploration of scleroderma architecture by spatial transcriptomics analysis

To comprehensively study the spatial heterogeneity in scleroderma, our study established a scleroderma mouse model. This model was generated by administering bleomycin injections into the dorsal skin of 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice, a method reported in previous studies.[25] To ensure a consistent physiological status across our specimens and to exclude variations in gene expression or cellular composition that could arise from different temporal sampling points, we collected tissue samples simultaneously from various groups, each subjected to different durations of intervention. Specifically, we collected four tissue specimens at designated intervals post-bleomycin injection: 0 day (control group), 5 days, 14 days, and 28 days. Histological examination via H&E staining shows the hallmark alterations associated with SSc, such as enhanced collagen deposition, skin thickening, and the diminution of adipose tissue [Figure 1A]. Further, we employed spatial transcriptomics using the 10× Genomics Visium platform to study these tissue samples. After degradation and clustering of the data, we generate a UMAP plot that delineates cell distribution changes over time. Each point on this plot represents a single cell, illustrating the dynamic shifts in cell distribution across the studied timeline [Figure 1B]. To assure the quality of our spatial transcriptome data, we assessed percent.mito as a metric to exclude low-quality cells. Moreover, we evaluated the number of detected genes (nGene) to verify sufficient gene representation and quantified UMIs to measure mRNA abundance. Following these rigorous QC measures, we affirmed the reliability of our spatial transcriptome data [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81]. For cell type annotation, we leveraged marker genes indicative of specific cell phenotypes to approximate the cell types on the UMAP plot, thus paving the way for a deeper exploration of cell-specific characteristics [Figure 1C]. To corroborate the congruence between transcriptomic signatures and histological findings, we compared H&E staining images with spatial transcriptomics data, focusing on the expression of selected marker genes. This comparative analysis verified a high degree of alignment between regions demarcated by marker gene expression and those identified in histological examinations.

Different patterns of scleroderma spatial heterogeneities

To delve deeper into the cellular dynamics within our spatial transcriptomics dataset, we utilized the “run_clustering” function within the Seurat package to cluster and categorize the spots across four individual samples. This analysis discerned 10 distinct cell clusters, which were visually represented on a UMAP plot [Figure 2A]. Given the resolution limits of spatial transcriptomics (not single-cell resolution), we were unable to definitively classify cell types. Nevertheless, by employing cell type-specific marker genes, we could approximate the identities of various cell populations. For instance, clusters 3 and 7 showed a pronounced enrichment for adipocyte marker genes, suggesting these clusters predominantly comprise adipocytes. To examine changes in the cellular composition associated with disease progression, we splitted the UMAP plot by group distinctions. Through quantitative comparisons of cell types across these groups, we observed pronounced differences, underscoring the cellular heterogeneity among them, particularly within clusters 1, 2, 8, 9, and 10. Notably, clusters 8, 9, and 10 were exclusive to the bleomycin-treated cohorts [Figure 2B], reinforcing the impact of the disease progression on cellular diversity. This shift in cellular composition was strikingly evident when comparing from the day 0 group to the day 28 group, as demonstrated in a splitted UMAP visualization [Figure 2C]. To gain insights into the spatial distribution of these cell clusters across the four samples and to ascertain whether our inferred cell types aligned with established histological understanding, we mapped the distribution of cell clusters. This approach reaffirmed the consistency of our cell type deductions with prior histological knowledge [Figure 2D]. In pursuit of a more nuanced understanding of the gene expression landscapes characteristic of these cell clusters, we generated a heatmap that shows the gene signature profiles unique to each cluster [Figure 2E].

Group-specific enrichment of gene expression and potential protein–protein interactions (PPI)

Through our investigations into the progression of disease, we observed significant cellular transformations, different cell types exhibit different gene expressions and functions. In this segment, we delved into the general heterogeneity observed at four different time points by using differentially expressed genes (DEG) across varying groups. To elucidate the functional implications and pathways associated with these DEGs, we performed GO functional analysis and KEGG pathway analysis. Intriguingly, our findings highlighted a pronounced downregulation in lipid metabolic processes within the 14-day group when compared with the 5-day group. This trend of suppression extends into the 28-day group when compared with the 14-day group, with notable reductions in the fatty acid biosynthetic process, the fatty acid metabolism, and the consistent lipid metabolic process [Figure 3A]. Conversely, when contrasting the 5-day group with the control, a significant upregulation in immune activation was evident, characterized by heightened processes such as immune system activation, innate immune response, eosinophil chemotaxis, and monocyte chemotaxis. This pattern of immune system upregulation persisted across subsequent comparisons, between the 14-day and 5-day groups, as well as the 28-day and 14-day groups. Furthermore, coinciding with the observed reduction in adipose function at the 14-day and 28-day, there was a concomitant upregulation of fibroblast activity, indicating as extracellular space, extracellular region, and extracellular matrix [Figure 3B]. This correlation furnishes us with insights into a potential interplay between adipose tissues, fibroblasts, and inflammatory processes. To find out the underlying signaling pathways driving these observed changes, we conducted KEGG pathway analysis. The outcomes of this analysis revealed consistent downregulation in the cyclic guanosine monophosphate-protein kinase G (cGMP-PKG), estrogen, and calcium signaling pathways [Supplementary Figure 2A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81], alongside an upregulation in the +hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) signaling pathway, lysosome and phagosome pathways, and so on [Supplementary Figure 2B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81]. Further investigations into PPI underscored the pivotal role of interactions among keratin (Krt) protein family members, particularly Krt7 and Krt25, in the etiology of the disease [Supplementary Figure 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81].

GO analysis of DEGs. (A) GO enrichment analysis of downregulated genes during the disease progression shows downregulated adipose activity. (B) GO enrichment analysis of upregulated genes during the disease progression shows upregulated inflammation and fibroblast activity. GO: Gene Ontology.

Loss of adipocytes, fibroblast activation, and inflammation are the key characteristics during the disease progression

Our bioinformatics analysis elucidated a downregulation in adipose activity, coupled with fibroblast activation and accelerated inflammation. To corroborate these findings, we focused on validating the key genes and proteins implicated in these processes. Initially, we profiled the expression of adipocyte marker genes, Adipoq and Fasn, across four distinct groups to assess changes indicative of adipose tissue loss. The observed downregulation in the expression levels of these two genes across the specimens underscored the diminishing adipose presence. Furthermore, we showed the expression of the fibroblast marker gene, αSMA, noting a gradual increase from the first to the fourth specimen. Additionally, the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL (Interleukins)-1β exhibited a marked escalation throughout disease progression, substantiating the inflammatory surge [Figure 4A]. To validate our bioinformatics insights, we conducted Western blotting analysis on proteins extracted from the injection sites across four different mouse dorsal skins. This analysis revealed a pronounced upregulation in the protein expression levels of the pro-fibrotic marker αSMA and Col1a1 during the progression of the disease [Figure 4B]. Subsequent RT-qPCR analysis on RNA samples from the same injection areas further validated our findings; the expressions of Adipoq and Fasn were significantly downregulated from specimens 1 to 4 (P <0.05), while αSMA and Col1a1 expressions surged notably in the 14-day and 28-day groups (P <0.05) [Figure 4C]. This gene expression trend reinforces the earlier observations of adipose reduction and fibroblast upregulation. To assess the immune landscape within these groups, we employed immunofluorescence using the M1 macrophage marker CD86 across four specimens. This analysis showcased a significant overexpression in CD86 expression from day 0 to day 28, highlighting the progressive immune response [Figure 4D]. Collectively, these investigations characterized SSc by loss of adipocytes, fibroblast activation, and heightened inflammation.

Different patterns of adipocyte and fibroblast subclusters in the different groups

Building on our previous findings that highlighted adipocyte loss and fibroblast activation, we sought to unravel potential interactions between these pivotal cell types. To study these two specific cell types, we isolated the adipocyte and fibroblast populations within our spatial transcriptome data, guided by specific marker genes and the spatial distribution of these cell clusters according to our histological insights. We pinpointed that adipocytes and fibroblasts predominantly occupy clusters 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 9. Subsequent extraction and re-clustering of these specified clusters revealed their subdivision into six distinct clusters, as visualized on a UMAP plot [Figure 5A]. To further elucidate the nature of these clusters, we examined their spatial distribution across four specimens, aiming to enhance their annotation through their spatial location [Figure 5B]. Analyzing the cellular composition of these clusters across the different groups unveiled variations that hint at underlying cellular dynamics. Notably, cluster 6 appeared exclusively in the 14-day and 28-day groups, suggesting the emergence of a potentially novel cell type within these stages. Concurrently, a gradual depletion of cluster 2 was observed from the initial to the final group, with cluster 4 also exhibiting a diminishing trend throughout disease progression [Figure 5C]. These patterns indicate a possible cell type transition occurring from the inception to the later stages of the condition. Moreover, by presenting the gene expression profiles across these clusters through a heatmap, we gained additional insights, aiding in the finer annotation of these cellular congregates [Figure 5D].

Pseudotime analysis showed the transformation of fibroblasts from adipocytes in dWAT during the progression of scleroderma

To corroborate our hypothesis regarding cellular transformation, we performed spatial pseudotime analysis, a technique to predict cell evolution. As depicted in Figure 6A, two distinct differentiation trajectories emerge from pseudotime 0 (blue) to pseudotime 30 (yellow), illustrating the progression of cellular transformation. The positioning of cells along these pseudotime plots, indicative of their differentiation states, allowed for their categorization into 11 distinct states [Figure 6B]. Further dissection of the pseudotime plot, splitted by group, revealed variations in pseudotime situations at different disease stages. In the control group, cells predominantly occupied the lower and right sections of the trajectory. As the disease advanced, an incremental shift toward the left side of the trajectory was observed [Figure 6C], suggesting a dynamic shift in cell types throughout disease progression. To quantify the shifts in cellular states, we employed a pie plot analysis, highlighting a significant expansion in pseudotime state 1, particularly prevalent in the 28-day group. Conversely, states 5 and 6 saw a notable decline [Figure 6D]. Mapping these cellular states onto the four specimens further confirmed that state 1 primarily comprises fibroblasts, while state 6 consists mainly of adipocytes [Figure 6E]. By associating the previously identified cell clusters with specific cellular states, we discovered that clusters 2 and 3 predominantly occupy the initial phases at the bottom of the pseudotime trajectory. In contrast, cluster 6 is situated at the terminal left end of the trajectory [Supplementary Figure 4A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81], elucidating the observed decrease in cluster 2 and an increase in cluster 6. Spatially, cluster 2 corresponds to adipocytes. Whereas cluster 6, although located in fibrotic regions, is distinct from fibroblasts, thereby we further identified them as myofibroblasts. Integrating these findings, we deduce a transformation from adipocytes to a specialized fibroblast (myofibroblasts), occurring deep within the dermis. This transformation signifies the conversion of adipocytes in dWAT into myofibroblasts. To delve deeper into the mechanisms underlying these cellular changes, we utilized beam analysis to study critical signaling pathways behind this differentiation process. The analysis of highly variable genes during differentiation highlighted their aggregation into four modules, as demonstrated in the heatmap [Supplementary Figure 4B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81].

In vivo validation of adipocytes-to-myofibroblasts transformation

Building upon our bioinformatics analysis that suggested a potential transformation from adipocytes in dWAT to fibroblasts, we sought to validate this cellular transformation in vivo. In this regard, we collected dorsal skin samples from mice across different experimental groups and conducted immunofluorescence assays. For this purpose, we utilized perilipin as a marker for adipocytes and Col1a1 as a marker for fibroblasts, examining the specimens under a fluorescence microscope. Consistent with our bioinformatics predictions, we observed a marked decline in the adipocyte-specific marker perilipin (green) from the day 0 group to the day 28 group. Concurrently, the fibroblast marker Col1a1 (red) exhibited a significant increase across the same timeframe. This trend underscores a reduction in adipose tissue alongside an augmentation in fibrotic tissue, aligning with the anticipated progression from adipocytes to fibroblasts [Figure 7A]. To substantiate the hypothesis that adipocytes could be transformed into fibroblasts, we tried to find whether there were adipocytes that are transforming, evidenced by concurrent expression of perilipin and Col1a1, after searching for such transitioning cells. Remarkably, we identified adipocytes displaying both perilipin and Col1a1 markers in the 28-day specimens [Figure 7B]. This finding supports the existence of a cellular transformation from adipocytes to fibroblasts during the progression of SSc, offering a link between our bioinformatics insights and in vivo model.

Enrichment analysis to identify key signaling pathways in adipocyte-to-fibroblast transformation

Given the evidence for the transformation of adipocytes into fibroblasts, our next objective was to elucidate the potential mechanisms underpinning this phenomenon. To this end, we employed beam analysis, as done previously, to find out the key signaling pathways in this transformative process [Supplementary Figure 4B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81]. We focused primarily on lineage states 1–2, where fibroblasts predominantly existed. As there are differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified during the transformation process, we enriched these genes for subsequent analysis. Initial exploration through KEGG analysis revealed several pivotal signaling pathways that were upregulated during differentiation. Notable among these were the insulin, AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase), cGMP-PKG, and HIF-1 signaling pathways, along with the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway [Supplementary Figure 5, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81]. These pathways emerge as potential mechanistic drivers of the adipocyte-to-fibroblast transition, worth deeper investigation and validation. Further enrichment of these DEGs through GO functional analysis revealed a significant alignment of genes in modules 3 and 4 with fibroblast biology–highlighting processes such as extracellular matrix production. This association shows the relevance of these genetic modules to fibroblast development and functionality [Supplementary Figure 6A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81]. Additionally, to dissect the gene expression dynamics across different cell clusters, we conducted a GSVA among the six identified cell clusters. Intriguingly, cluster 6, characterized as newly transformed fibroblasts from adipocytes, exhibited a strong link with the activated immune response. This observation leads us to infer that these nascent fibroblasts contribute to the heightened inflammatory milieu through disease progression [Supplementary Figure 6B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81]. Collectively, these analyses not only reaffirm the conclusion generated in our previous research but also shed light on the potential signaling pathways facilitating the adipocyte-to-fibroblast transformation. Significantly, we discovered that these newly formed fibroblasts display a pro-inflammatory profile, suggesting that an excessive immune response caused by these fibroblasts may account for accelerated disease progression.

In vitro validation of pro-inflammatory profile of adipocyte-transformed fibroblasts and predicted CCI between fibroblasts and other cell types

To investigate the pro-inflammatory characteristics of fibroblasts transformed from adipocytes, we employed a model where RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharides (LPS) for 24 h to induce an M1 macrophage phenotype. Subsequently, these M1 macrophages were co-cultured with adipocytes that had been treated with transforming growth factor (TGF)-β to simulate the transformation process, alongside a control group of untreated adipocytes. After 48 h, RNA was extracted from the RAW264.7 cells for RT-qPCR analysis. This analysis confirmed our bioinformatics predictions, revealing that the expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, and IL-1β) were significantly higher in the group co-cultured with TGF-β treated adipocytes, as opposed to those with untreated adipocytes (P <0.05) [Supplementary Figure 7, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81]. To explore potential pathways of CCI that might underlie this inflammatory signaling, we conducted a CCI analysis. This revealed a notable increase in the number of interactions between cluster 6 (representing fibroblasts) and various other cell types [Supplementary Figure 8A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81]. Further, we identified several critical potential ligand–receptor (L-R) pairs between cluster 6 and other cell clusters, including Col1a2_itga11, Col1a1_itga11, Col1a1_Cd36, and Col1a1_Cd44 [Supplementary Figure 8B–E, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C81]. These interactions suggest a mechanistic basis for the pro-inflammatory profile observed in fibroblasts transformed from adipocytes.

Discussion

SSc is a complex autoimmune disease characterized predominantly by fibrosis and increased immune responses. This study innovatively applies spatial transcriptomics to explore alterations within dWAT and the precise spatial distribution of various cell types during SSc progression. Our bioinformatics analyses revealed a marked decline in lipid biosynthesis and metabolism throughout SSc evolution, underscoring the potentially pivotal role of adipose tissue and adipocytes in the disease process. Subsequent pseudotime investigations suggested a transition of adipocytes into fibroblasts, identifying a previously unrecognized source of myofibroblasts. Immunofluorescence assays further confirmed this significant finding by demonstrating fibroblast markers in adipocytes as SSc progresses. To elucidate whether these adipocyte-derived myofibroblasts contribute to SSc beyond fibrosis, we examined their effects on inflammation. Co-culturing LPS-induced M1 macrophages with TGF-β-stimulated adipocytes, compared to normal adipocytes, revealed an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokine gene expression. This suggests that these newly transformed myofibroblasts exhibit pro-inflammatory characteristics, adding another layer to our understanding of SSc pathogenesis.

Recent investigations have increasingly turned their attention to dWAT, a focus initially sparked by Fleischmajer et al. Through excisional biopsies reaching the muscular layer, they observed dWAT depletion and its substitution with fibrotic tissue in scleroderma patients.[26] While the loss of dWAT predominantly signifies adipocyte reduction, recent findings indicate that the stromal-vascular fraction, particularly adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), also diminishes as the disease progresses. Furthermore, the immune landscape within dWAT undergoes significant alterations. Given the critical need to trace the fate of these lost adipose components and considering dWAT’s cellular heterogeneity, studying dWAT at the single-cell level becomes imperative. Isolating dWAT presents challenges due to its entanglement with skin tissue, leading to reliance on histological studies. In our research, we synergize the strengths of spatial transcriptomics and traditional histology for the first time to dissect SSc, enabling targeted examination of specific cell types within dWAT, distinct from other tissues.

Upon identifying the adipocyte-to-fibroblast transition, a critical question emerges: Can adipocytes truly transform into other cell types, and what are the underlying mechanisms? In 2015, Marangoni et al[27] demonstrated through the application of TGF-β, a conventional pro-fibrotic factor, on human subcutaneous adipocytes, a notable loss of adipogenic traits and a gain of myofibroblast characteristics. This suggests that adipocytes have the potential to transform into fibroblasts, which has already been regarded as a key process in wound healing. Subsequent research indicates that adipocytes might dedifferentiate, reclaiming stemness, and thereafter, transdifferentiate into fibroblasts. Some research reported that inflammatory zone 1 (FIZZ1) has been proposed as one inducer of this transformation.[28] Nevertheless, a deeper understanding of the molecular underpinnings of adipocyte to fibroblast transformation requires further investigation.

Our investigations employing TGF-β on adipocytes to simulate their transformation into fibroblasts revealed a pro-inflammatory profile. This finding diverges from conventional studies that predominantly associate a pro-inflammatory profile with fibroblast formation, while reports on the reverse phenomenon are scarce. In 2021, Venter et al[29] developed a co-culture model involving pro-inflammatory macrophages and myofibroblasts, demonstrating that pro-inflammatory macrophages can alter myofibroblast populations. Further, research on cardiac myofibroblasts highlighted the role of the pro-fibrotic factor connective tissue growth factor, induced by TGF-β1 in cardiac fibroblasts, in enhancing monocyte migration. The capacity of cardiac fibroblasts to attract monocytes via connective tissue growth factor and/or chemokine production and to facilitate their transendothelial migration suggests a self-sustaining loop that fuels both inflammation and fibrosis.[30] Notably, in the context of dWAT, a low-grade inflammation severely disrupts its homeostasis. Given adipose tissue’s protective role in skin integrity, disturbances in its homeostasis could exacerbate disease severity.[31] Thus, our findings indicate that during SSc progression, dWAT adipocytes transform into fibroblasts, adopting a pro-inflammatory role that compromises dWAT homeostasis and worsens the condition.

A limitation of our research is the absence of single-cell RNA sequencing data. This gap stems from the challenges in isolating dWAT and the known difficulties of single-cell RNA sequencing with adipose tissues, which often leads to unsuccessful adipocyte annotation. Reports suggest single-nuclei RNA sequencing as a more viable approach for adipose tissues. Future investigations should seek novel methods to isolate dWAT effectively and develop advanced techniques for adipose tissue single-nuclei/cell RNA sequencing. Additionally, employing transgenic mice with labeled adipocytes could facilitate easier observation of the adipocyte-to-fibroblast transition. Further exploration is required to elucidate the detailed mechanisms of this transformation, focusing on identifying critical genes, proteins, and transcription factors involved in the process.

In summary, our research utilizing spatial transcriptomics on bleomycin-induced mouse models has revealed adipocyte-to-fibroblast transformation alongside a pro-inflammatory profile within these transformed fibroblasts. Furthermore, our findings sketch a comprehensive view of the spatial cell distribution throughout SSc development, hinting at future diagnostic, and therapeutic strategies.
