Past trajectories and future landscapes of urban drought resilience in China
Authors: Huihui Wang, Shuhong You, Yuge Huang, Chengxin Lin, Yuxun Huang, Hui Zhou, Hongguang Zou, Tian Jing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFrequent droughts under global climate change threaten ecological security and sustainable urban development. Drought resilience (DR) denotes the capacity of urban systems to resist, absorb, adapt to, and recover from drought disturbances. This study develops an integrated framework to assess, diagnose, and forecast DR across 288 Chinese cities. Results show that China’s DRI-C increased steadily from 2007 to 2021, with an average annual growth rate of 1.38%, but marked regional disparities persisted, with higher DR in eastern coastal areas and lower DR in the northwest. Significant spatial clustering was observed, including high-high (H-H) agglomerations in Zhejiang, Fujian, and Guangdong and low-low (L-L) agglomerations in the North China Plain, Hexi Corridor, and Northeast Plain. Precipitation was the key climatic driver of DR, whereas temperature and wind speed were statistically insignificant in the panel vector auto regression (PVAR) model. By 2050, DRI-C is projected to increase by 16.6% under SSP1-2.6, while showing slightly weaker growth under SSP5-8.5.

Introduction

Drought is a complex natural hazard with profound impacts on agriculture,1,2 ecosystems3 and socio-economic fields.4,5,6 Under global climate change, droughts are intensifying in frequency and duration.7 The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) further confirms that many regions worldwide are experiencing or will face more frequent and severe agricultural and ecological droughts.8,9,10 This trend poses serious threats to ecosystems and unprecedented challenges to food security, water supply, energy production, and socio-economic stability. As a drought-prone country, China faces growing water resource pressures and drought risks. By 2024, drought had affected 1206.24 thousand hectares of crops in China, with 135.42 thousand hectares completely lost, resulting in grain losses of 1.736 billion kilograms, which had caused water shortages for 1.7455 million people and 1.3622 million head of livestock, resulting in direct economic losses of 8.359 billion yuan.11

Drought management is shifting from crisis response to proactive risk management.12 This shift is enabled by integrated monitoring systems that incorporate remote sensing (RS), geographic information systems (GISs), and climate indices including the standardized precipitation index (SPI), Palmer drought severity index (PDSI), and vegetation condition index (VCI) for dynamic assessment and early warning.13 Risk mitigation and adaptation are central to drought response strategies. China exemplifies this transition through large-scale initiatives such as the South-to-North Water Diversion Project and the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei Desertification Control program. Nevertheless, significant challenges remain in mitigating drought impacts and advancing risk research.

The term “resilience,” which originated from the Latin “resilio,” meaning “to return to the original state,” was later introduced into ecology by Holling14 to refer to the ability of an ecosystem to absorb disturbances and maintain its basic structure and function. Resilience is also interpreted as a method or set of methods capable of coping with the high degree of uncertainty inherent in complex urban challenges.15,16,17 The Fifth Plenary Session of the 19th CPC Central Committee pointed out new pathways and trends for urban development, emphasizing the construction of resilient cities. Against this background, it is particularly critical to improve the resilience of urban infrastructure in the face of the potential risk of drought disasters. Given the complex dynamic interplay between social and natural systems, resource and environmental problems are often seen as complex systemic challenges.18 Hence, by comprehensively considering the interaction mechanisms between social and natural systems, scientifically assessing and enhancing urban drought resilience (DR) not only aids in addressing climate change and sudden disturbances but contributes to the achievement of sustainable urban development goals.19,20

Research on DR assessment has evolved globally from conceptual frameworks to indicator systems: Early studies focused on conceptual definitions and qualitative analysis, while quantitative indicators and models have been progressively developed since the 21st century. Resilience was defined as the level of damage that an area can withstand in the face of extreme natural events, emphasizing improving the overall resilience of the system.21 The framework for the quantitative assessment of community resilience was proposed, incorporating engineering, organizational, social, and economic dimensions into resilience analysis.22 In the 21st century, a more systematic resilience evaluation method will be gradually established. A risk-based community resilience quantification method was developed to assess urban DR from the perspective of population and risk exposure.23 In recent years, scholars at home and abroad began to focus directly on DR evaluation.24 For instance, entropy weight method was introduced to assess vulnerability and identify impact factors for agricultural drought, laying a foundation for quantitative research on DR.25 A DR assessment system in China covering four elements of “social-economic-environment-system” was constructed and the evolution of DR was calculated in each region in recent ten years.2 The drought disaster resilience evaluation system was further constructed, combined the three indicators of pre-disaster resilience, disaster absorption and post-disaster resilience.26 The DR evaluation function for the Yellow River Basin in China was constructed, combined entropy weight method with stability, resistance and resilience indicators to evaluate the DR level and difference of ecosystems in different regions.27 At present, research outcomes on resilience assessments across different regions and industries continue to emerge., while studies focusing on the comparison of multi-indicator systems for DR and the optimization of combination weighting are comparatively scarce.

Current research on the driving mechanisms of DR has largely focused on static analysis. Empirical studies show that adequate precipitation is an important guarantee for drought mitigation, which can directly replenish soil and water, thus enhancing the resilience of the system to drought. For example, Research found that increased precipitation can significantly enhance a city’s DR. Conversely, long-term precipitation deficits weaken the resilience of ecological and social systems to drought.28 Some studies pointed out that the reduction of regional precipitation in the context of climate warming is one of the primary drivers for the increase in frequency and intensity of drought in the future. Rising temperatures typically exacerbate drought severity, thereby diminishing DR. Higher temperatures accelerate water loss from soil and vegetation by increasing evaporation and atmospheric “thirst.”29 Research also demonstrated the amplification effect of high temperatures in a study of extreme droughts in western North America.30 An empirical study is conducted in the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei region, and static results indicate that rising temperatures positively influence urban resilience. However, further dynamic analysis reveals that resilience declines when temperatures become excessively high, exhibiting a “first-strengthening-then-weakening” effect.28

In addition, wind speed variations primarily influence DR by affecting evapotranspiration and vegetation damage. Studies have shown that higher wind speeds have a significant negative impact on drought resistance: A global study found that plants in windy environments tend to evolve higher drought resistance traits, and that excessive wind speeds may exacerbate the adverse effects of drought under climate change scenarios.31 Research on dynamic models linking DR to climatic factors remains relatively scarce. Against the backdrop of climate change, studying the dynamic relationship between DR and climatic elements facilitates a deeper analysis of how each climatic factor influences DR and the underlying mechanisms. Consequently, it represents a key direction for future research on DR.

Based on the recognition of the impact mechanisms of DR, scholars both domestic and international have begun to focus on the evolutionary trends of DR under different climate change scenarios. Climate models and scenario analyses have been extensively employed since the 21st century to forecast future drought risks. Numerous studies indicate that global drought risk will increase overall, with uneven changes across different regions under future climate warming conditions.32 On the global scale, there is a “divergence phenomenon” in the variation of different drought indicators.33 On a regional scale, predictions of future droughts vary from region to region. For example, CMIP6 scenario analysis by Chen et al.34 and Zhang et al.35 indicates that drought may intensify in central and southern China in the future, while drought in parts of northeast and west China is relatively relieved. Furthermore, the evolution trends of DR under different emission pathways also exhibit significant differences.36,37

In order to predict DR under future scenarios, scholars have comprehensively applied various methods such as climate scenario simulation and hydrological models. The CMIP6 climate model is one of the core tools of current research. Researchers usually use the precipitation and temperature data output by climate models to calculate drought indices (such as the standardized precipitation evaporation index SPEI), or drive land surface hydrological models to evaluate soil moisture and runoff changes, thus depicting the multi-dimensional evolution of drought in meteorology and hydrology. For instance, Zhou et al.38 combined 23 CMIP6 global climate models and multiple shared socio-economic pathways (SSPs) to quantify the global meteorological drought risk and its impact on population and economic exposure under different scenarios from 2021 to 2100. This risk assessment, which couples climate scenarios with socio-economic scenarios, provides a more comprehensive perspective for DR studies. Meanwhile, some scholars employ integrated hydrological models and multi-indicator assessment methods to capture the ecosystem’s capacity to respond to drought disturbances. For example, some studies have constructed a vegetation index drought resilience index (DRI) to measure the recovery speed and degree of ecosystems after drought, thus assessing regional ecological resilience.33 Overall, the latest research trend involves integrating atmospheric, hydrological, ecological, and socioeconomic models to predict DR from a multi-system perspective. Although CMIP6 scenarios play a crucial role in drought prediction, inter-model uncertainties persist, such as precipitation simulation biases and vegetation process characterization. Consequently, scholars emphasize the need to enhance prediction robustness through scenario comparisons and multi-model ensembles, while further developing more refined regional climate-hydrological models to support decision-making.

In this study, we adapted a multidimensional systems perspective to construct a DRI system tailored to the prefecture-level city scale, addressing the existing research gap in integrated multi-layer structures. Differing from traditional relative ranking models such as TOPSIS, this paper employed the entropy weight-CRITIC method coupled with a weighted sum model to construct the DRI, ensuring that evaluation results are consistent and comparable across periods. We innovatively introduced the panel vector auto regression (PVAR) model to expand dynamic coupling analysis methods between DR and climate variables, identifying influence pathways and lag effects of climatic factors. Comparing the CMIP6 SSP-RCP scenarios using random forest (RF) and backpropagation artificial neural network (BP-ANN) models, the optimal model is selected to predict future trends in urban DR.

We have solved the following questions in the paper: (1) How about the DR of prefecture-level cities in China? What are the spatial-temporal heterogeneity and spatial clustering characteristics of DR? (2) What are the pathways of climate factors affecting DR? (3) What changes are likely to occur in DR from 2022 to 2050? Simultaneously, our findings enrich the theoretical framework for urban resilience research under climate change. They assist governments in identifying cities and regions at high risk of drought, enabling the scientific allocation of water resources and drought-resilient investments. This provides quantitative evidence for developing region-specific drought adaptation policies, laying the theoretical foundation and technical tools for building resilient cities and climate-adaptive societies.

Taking 2021 as a base year, we conducted comparative analyses between four criterion layers (Figure S1). Social resilience index (DRI-Soc) exhibits a relatively concentrated distribution, with low and extremely low resilience concentrated in the Songnen Plain, the Huang-Huai-Hai Plain, and the Sichuan Basin. In contrast, the northwest, the southeast coastal areas, and certain major cities (such as Beijing) demonstrate higher DRI-Soc, indicating significant progress in their social support systems and emergency management. The economic resilience index (DRI-Econ) is distributed unevenly. High-resilience and extremely high-resilience clusters dominate in East China (Jiangsu-Zhejiang-Shanghai), the Pearl River Delta, and the Shandong Peninsula; whereas most areas in Northwest and Northeast China are classified as low-resilience and extremely low-resilience zones. Next, regions in South China, Southwest China, and northern Northeast China generally exhibit high Environmental resilience index (DRI-Env), while the cities in the arid areas of Northwest China are characterized as low-resilience zones. DRI-Env exhibits a distribution pattern that is higher in the south and lower in the north, indicating that climate and ecological conditions play a significant role in regional DRI-Env. Compared to other criteria levels, the distribution of the Engineering resilience index (DRI-Eng) is more dispersed but still stronger in the east. Parts of East China benefit from robust infrastructure, while many central-western cities remain low, reflecting infrastructure gaps. In summary, cities with high resilience often demonstrate strong performance in DRI-Econ, DRI-Env and DRI-Eng. This indicates that these cities not only possess robust economic foundations but effectively respond to climate change and drought disasters. Cities with low resilience generally exhibit significant shortcomings in infrastructure development and environmental management. This reflects that those cities in central and western regions still face considerable challenges in their comprehensive capacity to cope with drought.

We analyze the temporal dynamic evolution of DR in China’s prefecture-level cities in 2007–2021 across three dimensions: national trend, regional distribution trends, and changes in inter-city score structures. From 2007 to 2021, the National DRI-C average increased from 0.328 to 0.398, having an average annual growth of 1.38% (Figure 1E). All criteria-layer indexes also showed an upward trend, with the most notable improvements observed in DRI-Econ and DRI-Eng. The DRI-Econ index rose from 0.048 to 0.068, while the DRI-Eng index increased from 0.068 to 0.107. This indicates positive progress nationwide in economic adaptability and infrastructure construction.

Figure 1F illustrates the trends, magnitude, and variability in DR across seven regions and nationwide from 2007 to 2021. DRI-C has shown overall growth, with the pattern of higher resilience in southern regions and lower resilience in northern regions remaining pronounced. Central China, East China, and Southwest China have achieved steady growth over the past fifteen years, while regions such as South China and Northeast China have seen relatively slower improvements. DRI-Econ showed the most significant improvement, with the eastern regions holding a clear lead. East China and South China demonstrated outstanding economic performance, primarily due to their solid economic foundations, industrial diversification, and fiscal resilience.

In contrast, central and western regions generally exhibited lower resilience, indicating their continued vulnerability to economic turbulence and disaster impacts.

DRI-Eng has experienced rapid growth across multiple regions, reflecting the effectiveness of infrastructure development efforts. Particularly in areas such as the northwest and southwest, engineering resilience scores have surged from previously low levels, demonstrating significant improvements driven by the “Strengthening Infrastructure” policy. DRI-Soc exhibits relatively small differences across regions and years. The relatively slow improvement in values for Northeast and South China suggests that certain societal issues may constrain regional drought support systems. However, DRI-Env shows a pattern of stronger values in the south and weaker values in the north. Regions rich in ecological resources, such as South China and Southwest China, consistently score higher, while cities in the drought-prone Northwest and North China remain at low levels. Cross-dimension interactions are evident: The improvement rates and pace of both economic and social indicators were largely consistent across most regions, reflecting policy coordination in building DR at the national level, while environmental and engineering dimensions are more influenced by regional resource endowments and institutional foundations, with divergent trends intensifying.

A violin-box-scatter composite shows that the annual distribution of DRI-C exhibits right skewness, with a relatively small gap between the median and the lower quartile, indicating that the resilience levels of most cities remain in the medium-to-low range. The median of DRI-C continues to rise, reflecting an increasing number of highly resilient cities. The density peak shifts upward over time and tends toward concentration in Figure 1H. The overall dispersion of DR among cities remained stable. Between 2007 and 2021, the standard deviation of DRI-C fluctuated between 0.068 and 0.074, showing no significant upward trend in Figure 1G. DRI-Env exhibits the most pronounced inter-city variation, with its standard deviation consistently significantly higher than other criteria layers. DRI-Econ’s inter-city variation has slightly increased in recent years, with the standard deviation rising from 0.0223 in 2007 to 0.0263 in 2021. In contrast, DRI-Soc and DRI-Eng show a more concentrated inter-city distribution. The result indicates that despite the overall improvement in DR in China, differences in resource endowments and foundational conditions still exist among cities over the long term. Particularly in terms of ecological environment and economic development, significant regional imbalances persist.

L-L clusters remain concentrated in North China, the central Northeast, and the northwest arid belt (e.g., the Hexi Corridor). We employ a hotspot analysis method, based on the Getis-Ord Gi∗ index, to examine the spatial clustering and distribution characteristics of urban DR in China. The results are presented in Figures 2E–2H. Hot spots of DRI-C are concentrated in South China and the southeastern coastal urban belts, notably the Yangtze River Delta and Pearl River Delta (e.g., Guangdong, Guangxi, Fujian), forming stable high-resilience clusters. Cold spots are primarily concentrated in North China, the northwest, and parts of the Northeast (e.g., Gansu, Inner Mongolia, Hebei, Liaoning). From 2007 to 2021, three significant shifts emerged: The cold spots in the northern part of Northeast China have shifted from a 90% confidence level to a 99% confidence level, indicating that the spatial clustering characteristics of these low-resilience areas are becoming increasingly pronounced. Overall, the results of Getis-Ord Gi∗ corroborate the LISA patterns, highlighting durable southeast coastal advantages and entrenched low-resilience belts inland.

Dynamic influences of climatic factors

To visually assess the sample distribution, fluctuation range, and potential outliers, we conduct descriptive statistics. The mean, standard deviation, and extreme values for the DRI-C, Temperature, Precipitation, and Wind Speed across 288 cities during the period 2007–2021 are summarized in Table S2. Descriptive statistics for the panel show moderate dispersion with values normalized to [0,1]. Pearson’s correlations indicate DRI-C is negatively associated with temperature (r = −0.4753) and positively with precipitation (r = 0.6679); the temperature-precipitation association is strongly negative (r = −0.7632), while wind speed shows only weak links to another two climate drivers (Table S3).

Unit root testing is a critical step in ensuring the stationarity of time series data. Non-stationary data may lead to spurious regression issues, so we employ two distinct panel unit root tests to examine the stationarity of each variable: the Levin-Lin-Chu test (LLC) and the ADF-Fisher test (Fisher). Table S4 shows that all variables exhibit significant unit roots (non-stationarity) in their original series. In the Fisher test, the original hypothesis is that all individuals in the panel have a unit root (i.e., all series are non-stationary); the alternative hypothesis is that at least one individual’s time series is stationary (i.e., no unit root exists). Stata’s output shows that the Fisher test results for all variables indicate p values significantly less than 0.05 (Table S5). Therefore, in the differenced series, all variables passed the unit root test. After applying first-order differencing to the variables, we re-tested for unit roots. All variables met the stationarity requirements and could be incorporated into the PVAR model for analysis; see Table S6.

In this study, the Kao test indicates that DR exhibits cointegration relationships with temperature, precipitation, and wind speed (Table S7). In addition, three information criteria, AIC (Akaike Information Criterion), BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion), and MQIC (Quasi-Maximum Likelihood Criterion) are used to evaluate the performance of different lag orders (Table S8). MAIC, MBIC, and MQIC all achieve optimal performance at a lag order of 1, indicating that a lag of one order best captures the dynamic relationships between variables.

Before undertaking the PVAR model evaluation, we first perform stability testing to confirm the model’s suitability for long-term forecasting. All eigenvalues lie inside the unit circle, confirming PVAR stability (Figure 3A and Table S9). We analyze the dynamic impact of lag periods after first-order differencing of each variable on changes in current DRI-C using the regression coefficients of the PVAR model and Generalized Method of Moments (GMMs) estimation in Table S10. The change in DR lagging one period has a significantly negative effect on its current-period change (−0.6396, p < 0.001), indicating that DR exhibits significant path dependency, meaning that the current level of DR is influenced by past DR levels. The reduction in precipitation has a significant negative effect on DR (−0.0646, p < 0.001). This shows that reduced precipitation significantly weakens the system’s DR, revealing the precipitation’s crucial dynamic role as a core climatic factor in determining DR levels. Granger tests further show that precipitation Granger-causes DRI-C (x2 = 52.745, p < 0.001). Short-run Granger links from temperature and wind to DRI-C are weak, but given cointegration, temperature contributes to long-run adjustment within the joint system (Table S11).

Orthogonalized impulse response functions (IRFs) (500 Monte Carlo replications; 95% CIs) indicate: The effect of temperature on DRI-C demonstrated a short-lived positive response in the first 1–2 periods that dissipates thereafter (Figure 3B). The effect of precipitation on DRI-C is shown as an initial fluctuation followed by a stable positive effect from approximately the third period onward (Figure 3C), highlighting the central role of rainfall regimes for resilience. However, the influence of wind speed variation on DRI-C is small and transient, remaining within narrow confidence bands (Figure 3D).

Forecast error variance decomposition (FEVD) attributes the largest share of DRI-C forecast-error variance to precipitation. By step 8, precipitation explains roughly 3.0% of DRI-C variability, while temperature and wind speed each contribute less than 1% to the long-term trend (Table S12). In sum, precipitation is the primary exogenous climate driver, with temperature playing a secondary, longer-horizon role, and wind showing limited explanatory power.

Based on the above research of correlations, cointegration, PVAR dynamics, IRFs, and FEVD, we found that precipitation variability is the dominant proximate driver of resilience change; Temperature operates mainly through systemic, longer-run channels (e.g., cumulative heat stress interacting with water availability); and wind exerts minor, short-run effects at the city scale. These patterns align with our spatial diagnostics: Higher-resilience clusters coincide with favorable hydro-climatic regimes and stronger water-related infrastructure.

Interpretable machine learning-assisted scenario analysis

We evaluated the performance of RF and BP-ANN models on both the training and test datasets. The data for RF and BP-ANN were obtained from urban samples collected between 2007 and 2021, and divided into a training dataset (80%) and a test dataset (20%), with an additional 1/6 of the training data allocated as a validation set. As shown in Table S13, based on the comprehensive consideration of the three indices, namely R2, RMSE, and MAE, the BP-ANN is the optimal model in prediction performance across all four criterion layers. Furthermore, we validated the predictive accuracy of the BP-ANN using data from the test dataset, which demonstrated that the BP-ANN has excellent fitting performance in forecasting DR. The results are shown in Table S14.

We conducted SHAP value analysis based on the optimal model (BP-ANN), selecting 1000 samples for interpretation to analyze the contribution of input variables to DR prediction outcomes. According to SHAP analyses in Figure 4A, C2 is the most significant factor influencing changes in DR, followed by A2, B4, and D4. To some extent, this also indicates the critical role of resource endowments in DRI-Soc, and DRI-Econ is more driven by fiscal and macro-output factors. It simultaneously demonstrates the high degree of consistency between the completeness of infrastructure and the enhancement of DRI-Eng. As shown in Figures 5G–5H, by 2030, most cities exhibit robust sensitivity in their DR scenario responses. Only a small number of vulnerable sensitivity areas are distributed in the northeast and scattered across some frontier or higher-elevation regions. By 2050, sensitivity becomes increasingly vulnerable in the central-southern and northeastern parts of the northeast, as well as in western South China.

Despite the robust performance of the RF model in national-scale predictions, a degree of uncertainty persists across different scenario outputs. This paper analyzes the findings from two perspectives: consistency analysis of urban resilience ratings and the sources of prediction bias and model limitations. Research findings indicate that 69% of cities maintain the same rating across four or more scenarios, demonstrating stable model trend assessments. Approximately 90 cities exhibit “rating crossover phenomena” between different scenarios. Cities showing extreme inconsistency account for about 7%, mostly located in border regions, ecologically fragile areas, or regions with data gaps. Moreover, from the perspective of model mechanisms and climate data sources, the primary sources of uncertainty drivers include: Divergences of global climate models in CMIP6 precipitation and temperature data; Excessively high sensitivity of indicators in some cities; Insufficient representation of training data for cities experiencing extreme drought or data gaps; Inadequate response of RF and BP-ANN models to multi-year extreme events.

Based on multi-scenario projections for 2022–2050, China’s urban DR evolution exhibits distinct regional divergence, policy response sensitivity, and pronounced vulnerability in certain cities. Accordingly, a systematic and differentiated resilience enhancement pathway is proposed: High-resilience cities should focus on establishing closed-loop governance systems and leveraging their radiating influence; Medium-resilience cities need to strengthen water resource allocation and climate adaptation capacity building; while low-resilience and high-risk cities should prioritize investments in drought-resistant infrastructure and grassroots emergency response capabilities. This approach will establish a layered, collaborative framework for enhanced DR. Future efforts to enhance DR should prioritize four systematic pathways: establishing a data-driven intelligent drought prevention system to enable early identification and dynamic monitoring of drought conditions; incorporating climate adaptation into urban planning through the designation of sensitivity zones and DR priority areas; developing a multi-scenario policy preparedness framework to strengthen coordinated responses to extreme climate events; and fostering cross-regional collaborative governance to improve overall DR via information sharing, joint emergency response, and financial cooperation.

Discussion

Under global climate change, future water cycles and hydrological processes are undergoing significant shifts.39 Many climate models predict that the frequency and severity of drought events will likely increase in the coming years,40,41 this changing climate pattern directly threatens the achievement of China’s sustainable development goals.42,43 China, as the most populous country in the world, has a water shortage as the current situation in many regions. Drought and water scarcity pose an increasing threat to agricultural cultivation, industrial production, and residential life,44,45,46 drought has become the norm affecting national production and life.47 Therefore, it is of great practical significance to grasp the spatiotemporal distribution characteristics and future evolution trend of DR in time and accurately.

In our study, we reveal the spatiotemporal evolution of DR across Chinese cities from 2007 to 2021, characterized by an overall steady increase accompanied by pronounced regional disparities. The spatial distribution follows a distinct pattern of high in the east and low in the west, strong in the south and weak in the north,2,48,49 which is consistent with the research results of several scholars. Regions such as the southeastern coast and northern Northeast China, benefiting from robust economic growth and favorable natural conditions, showed significant DR improvement. Hexi Corridor, Songlet Plain, and other places still face great challenges due to environmental constraints and weaker socioeconomic foundations. This reflects the positive influence of both natural endowments and economic capacity on DR.2

Urban DR demonstrates significant spatial dependence,50 H-H aggregation areas, such as the southeastern coast, Pearl River Delta, and Yangtze River Delta, can exert a “diffusion-driving effect” by facilitating cross-regional water resource allocation, constructing regional reserve reservoirs, accelerating technology diffusion and industrial upgrading, and promoting the export of intelligent water-saving technologies from high-resilience cities (e.g., Shanghai, Hangzhou) to neighboring areas19 Concurrently, these regions should jointly restore coastal wetlands and mangroves to enhance natural flood storage and DR. In contrast, the L-L aggregation areas, including the North China Plain, Northeast Plain, Hexi Corridor, should leverage policy opportunities to introduce advanced irrigation systems, sustainable agricultural technologies,51,52 reorienting agricultural models to promote drought-tolerant crops49,53 to improve agricultural productivity and resilience to drought and other climate risk challenges. For H-L aggregation areas, such as southern Shaanxi, northern Northeast China, and the Bohai Rim region, optimizing resource allocation and establishing inter-provincial cooperation mechanisms are essential to foster resource sharing and coordinated development. In L-H aggregation areas, such as the Ruyungui Xianggui junction area, infrastructure development should be prioritized, including the construction of intercity water pipelines and shared emergency water sources with high-resilience cities. The adoption of big data technologies including RS, weather radar and seismic monitoring systems should be encouraged,54,55,56 which can significantly improve data collection and sharing, the early warning and response capacity of provinces for drought disasters.

Precipitation serves as a primary climatic driver, exerting a significant positive influence on DR,29,57 temperature and wind speed have a short-term suppression effect on DR,28 and its effects show a time lag, which is consistent with the current research conclusions. In addition, PVAR is used to establish a dynamic relationship model between DR and climate factors, which broke away from the limitations of previous static relationship studies. Future research should incorporate hydrologic variables (e.g., evapotranspiration and soil moisture) and socioeconomic indicators to better capture the integrated “climate-hydrology-society” coupling mechanism.

The frequent occurrence of disaster events under global climate change should prompt us to reflect on how to further improve China’s emergency management capacity to deal with drought disasters and promote regional ecological security and sustainable urban development. First, the current disaster prevention and emergency management system in China has structural weaknesses such as poor coordination between departments and levels and insufficient long-term risk management.58 Hence, it is essential to strengthen cross-departmental, cross-level, and international collaboration mechanisms to establish a unified response system characterized by centralized command, information sharing, and efficient coordination.

Next, the improvement of infrastructure and increased application of modern science and technology are crucial supports for disaster prevention and emergency management. The lack of critical infrastructure (such as water conservancy, transportation, and water supply systems) can amplify the negative impacts of drought disasters. Therefore, it is necessary to increase the water conservancy infrastructure and enhance DR capabilities, such as constructing reservoirs, dikes, and other control projects,51 and developing efficient water-saving irrigation systems.52 The application of modern science and technology plays an irreplaceable role in dealing with major sudden disaster events. Making full use of modern scientific and technological forces, such as RS monitoring, big data analysis, and artificial intelligence, establishing a data-driven intelligent drought prevention system, realizing accurate disaster prediction, early warning, and efficient dispatching of rescue resources, can significantly improve the emergency response capability and resilience level of cities.

Current emergency management systems remain predominantly focused on short-term response, with inadequate attention to long-term risk mitigation. Governments should integrate long-term risk assessments and climate adaptation strategies into national and local sustainable development plans.59 It is essential to establish a comprehensive emergency material reserve system to enable rapid disaster response, enhance training and drills for emergency teams, and strengthen international cooperation to improve relief efficiency. Furthermore, the synergy between ecological conservation and emergency management should not be ignored. When dealing with major sudden disasters, the potential impact on environmental and ecological factors should be considered in advance. Proactive measures such as soil and water conservation, comprehensive erosion control,60 along with forest and wetland protection and restoration to increase vegetation coverage,61 can significantly reduce drought risks and enhance ecological resilience.

Based on differences in urban disaster control and emergency management capabilities, governments should formulate differentiated strategies to enhance urban resilience. High-resilience cities (e.g., Hangzhou) should strengthen the closed-loop governance of “disaster prevention-adaptation-transformation,” promote the shift from engineering disaster prevention to an ecological resilience system, build drought response science and technology demonstration areas, export resilience construction experience, serve surrounding urban agglomerations, and guide diversified capital to participate in green infrastructure construction, so as to realize “eco-society-economy” collaborative resilience enhancement. For moderately resilient cities (such as Changsha), it is necessary to strengthen the basin-scale water resources dispatching mechanism, alleviate the contradiction between supply and demand of water resources at different stages, construct a city-level “climate adaptation plan,” set up a drought event Incident Response Service level and action list, and improve the urban ecosystem service capacity, such as promoting sponge city, landscape forest field lake grass system governance.62 Low-resilience and high-risk cities should be prioritized for enhanced investment in key projects (such as drought-resistant water sources, groundwater protection zones, and water-saving irrigation in farmland), strengthen water conservancy and other infrastructure development, promote multi-level coordination of disaster prevention funds and policy linkage across counties, cities, and provinces, enhance interprovincial collaboration, and establish resource-sharing mechanisms to improve cross-regional disaster prevention and mitigation capabilities.63

Raising public awareness of emergency management is crucial for responding to major sudden disasters. Public participation in emergency management should be encouraged. Regional drought emergency drills and risk education should be conducted to improve people’s ability to identify and act on climate risks.64 The Government and social organizations should enhance public education, training, and participation opportunities to ensure the public can actively engage and play a constructive role in jointly addressing disaster challenges.

In summary, strengthening disaster prevention systems, enhancing infrastructure, leveraging technology, improving emergency reserves, implementing differentiated resilience strategies, and raising public awareness will collectively enhance China’s disaster mitigation capacity and lay a strategic foundation for national security and sustainable development under climate change.

Limitations of the study

Although this study has made systematic exploration in method integration, data processing, and prediction simulation, there are still some limitations. First, the subjectivity of index selection and data constraints. Even if the objective weighting method reduces artificial bias, some variable data are limited by public access and timeliness, which may affect the accuracy of individual city measurement results. Second. Although RF and BP-ANN have strong fitting ability, they are sensitive to the fluctuation of input variables, lack a clear physical modeling process, and have uncertainty in extreme scenario response simulation. In addition, this study provides scenario-based projections under explicitly stated assumptions; therefore, projections that treat some policy-relevant variables as constants or extrapolate them with simplified functions should be interpreted as conditional pathways within the assumed boundary conditions, and may be less responsive to abrupt policy shifts or structural breaks. In future studies, multi-model integrated prediction and scenario simulation platforms (SEIM, LUCC coupled models) will be combined to improve prediction robustness. In addition, because spatial heterogeneity and policy factors have not been fully modeled, this study focuses on natural and social factors and has not systematically considered the influence of “soft resilience” factors such as policy response and governance capacity, nor has it introduced spatial error correction or a panel spatial model. Therefore, spatial econometric models (such as spatial PVAR, spatial Durbin model, and so forth) can be considered in future research to improve the characterization ability of spatial spillover effects, and at the same time, policy response, governance capacity and other factors can be included in the research scope to construct a comprehensive DR assessment system covering social governance-ecological flexibility-engineering system, so as to provide a more comprehensive supporting framework for urban high resilience transformation.

Method details

Research area

China, a nation with both maritime and continental characteristics, is located in eastern Asia on the western coast of the Pacific Ocean. Its territory spans approximately 9.6 million square kilometers, covering a vast expanse. China’s topography is complex and diverse, exhibiting a pronounced west-high, east-low elevation pattern. Its climate is distinctly monsoonal and continental. Aridity is a defining feature of China, influencing population distribution and socioeconomic development. China’s arid zones are widely distributed, primarily concentrated in the inland northwest regions such as Xinjiang, central and western Inner Mongolia, northern Ningxia, most of Gansu Province, and northwestern Qinghai Province. Conversely, China’s eastern coastal and central regions enjoy a humid climate, dense populations, and well-established industrial and agricultural foundations. Nevertheless, drought continues to exert persistent pressure on regional water security, agricultural production, and socioeconomic development. The location of the study area is shown in Figure S2. Currently, China has implemented multiple measures to actively address drought impacts, including optimizing provincial water resource allocation, conducting inter-basin water transfers, planning climate-resilient infrastructure and promoting water-saving irrigation techniques and technical guidance.

Data sources

This study examines 288 prefecture-level administrative units across mainland China, covering most prefecture-level regions except Hong Kong, Macao, Taiwan, and Sansha City. Additionally, the study period is divided into a historical period (2007-2021) and a projection period (2022-2050). Historical period indicator data primarily is sourced from the China City Statistical Yearbook,65 China Regional Economic Statistical Yearbook,66 China City Construction Statistical Yearbook, provincial statistical yearbooks and water resources bulletins,67 China Meteorological Administration (CMA) daily climate data, the Chinese Academy of Sciences Resource and Environment Science and Data Center, DEM raster products, and LUH2 land use data. Some missing data are supplemented by authoritative alternative data sources, bilateral linear interpolation, or mean trend substitution.

Future scenario data acquisition (2022-2050) follows the SSP-RCP scenario combination framework. Climate scenario data originates from five GCM models under the CMIP6 framework (GFDL-ESM4, UKESM1-0-LL, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, IPSL-CM6A-LR, and ACCESS-CM2) (https://aims2.llnl.gov/search). Hydrological scenario data is derived from the standard output products of the WaterGAP2-2e model (https://data.isimip.org/10.48364/ISIMIP.230418.4). Land use data utilizes the long-term land cover and land use projections released by LUH2 v2f (https://luh.umd.edu/). Socioeconomic scenario data is sourced from the 1km-resolution SSP pathway projections published by the research group of Cai Wenjia and Wang Can at the School of Environment, Tsinghua University.68 To achieve seamless data continuity between the historical period (2007-2021) and the future period (2022-2050), this study employs a differential or multiple correction method (Delta Correction) using 2021 as the base year to calibrate the simulation results.

Assessment of drought resilience in 288 prefecture-level cities

DR is defined as the comprehensive capacity and system performance to resist, absorb, adapt to, and recover from drought disturbances. This overall performance depends not on the system’s resource endowment, infrastructure conditions, and governance capacity, but is also jointly constrained by its pre-existing vulnerability and exposure. This study employs systematic literature review (SLR) methodology and multi-source data integration to construct a DR evaluation framework across four criteria levels (societal, economic, environmental, and engineering). The framework is developed based on three dimensions: theoretical logic, practical feasibility, and spatial data adaptability. We utilize databases such as Web of Science, Scopus and CNKI for literature acquisition and screening. A multi-level Boolean search logic is employed to locate literature related to DR assessment indexes. Following a multi-stage screening process (including title, abstract, and full-text review) and the addition of highly cited articles that are initially missed, 105 relevant papers are ultimately identified. To objectively reveal the common knowledge structures embedded within these literatures, we conduct keyword co-occurrence analysis on a subset of them, as shown in Figure S3. The literature pool analyzed here is a rigorously selected, highly targeted collection of documents closely aligned with this research topic. It reflects the mainstream dimensions, hotspots, and interconnections within the specific research theme of DRI assessment.

In addition, by carefully reviewing these 105 literatures, we summarize the assessment indexes and initially extracted 38 high-frequency indicators. To enhance the model’s operational efficiency and interpretability of results, variables exhibiting high redundancy, strong multicollinearity, or weak explanatory power must be eliminated, we adopt a three-stage screening process. First, missing value rate and standard deviation tests are applied to filter out indicators with inadequate information or discriminative power. Next, Pearson correlation tests are used to exclude highly correlated indicators. Finally, data stability is assessed and redundant indicators are merged. This ensures the final indicator set consistently captures long-term DR characteristics and prevents model multicollinearity. Ultimately, 20 core indicators are selected for the system (Table 1), and enabling it to comprehensively characterize the DR traits of China’s 288 prefecture-level cities from 2007 to 2050. The screening process of literatures and indexes is detailed in Figure S4. Furthermore, this paper uniformly applies the Min-Max Normalization method to all DR evaluation indicators, mapping raw values to the [0,1] range to eliminate the influence of unit dimensions and ensure the standardization and comparability of model inputs. Normalization is divided into positive and negative indicators, calculated according to the formula (1)-(3) in Formula S1. Considering that some indicators exhibit significant skewness or extreme values, a Winsorization treatment using the 5th and 95th percentiles as limits was applied to some indicators prior to normalization. This prevents individual observations from exerting unreasonable influence on model fitting and normalization outcomes. Comprehensive evaluation index system of DR in China

The Entropy weight-CRITIC method is a multi-indicator comprehensive evaluation approach. The entropy weight method, which is based on information entropy theory, posits that the greater the dispersion of an indicator’s values across different cities, the greater the information it carries and the stronger its discriminatory power, thus it should be assigned a higher weight. This method can be used to measure the distributional differences between cities. The CRITIC method holds that the importance of an indicator should depend on both its variability (standard deviation) and its conflict with other indicators, and the lower the correlation, the more important it should be, and the CRITIC method emphasizes indicator conflicts and stability. By employing an arithmetic mean fusion method, the advantages of both approaches are preserved during weight integration while avoiding unnecessary fluctuations and excessive influence. The Entropy weight-CRITIC method enhances the objectivity and stability of weighting results, avoids potential biases from single methods, and is suitable for assessing DR levels across Chinese cities in this study. To facilitate the identification of urban DR levels and distribution features, this study employs the natural breakpoint method (Jenks) to classify the DRI-C into five levels: extremely low resilience, low resilience, moderate resilience, high resilience, and extremely high resilience. Quantifying the impact of climate factors on urban drought resilience

To thoroughly examine the pathways through which climate change impacts DR, this study employs the PVAR model, effectively capturing both the long-term and short-term influence pathways of various climate variables on DR, as well as the dynamic causal relationships among these variables.

The model’s key specifications include that we quantify dynamic linkages between resilience and climate by estimating a PVAR on 2007–2021 city-year panels (288 cities) with endogenous variables DRI-C, temperature, precipitation, and wind speed. The fixed effects of cities are eliminated via forward mean differencing (Helmert transform); Optimal lags are selected by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion. The potential endogeneity issues among variables were addressed by employing the GMM approach. We utilize IRF, FEVD, and Granger causality to characterize short-term and long-term impact pathways of climate drivers on DR.

The research methodology steps are as follows: First, descriptive statistics are conducted to preliminarily analyze the fundamental characteristics of the data, including mean, standard deviation, minimum value, and maximum value, ensuring data quality. Second, unit root tests are performed to examine the stationarity of the time series, ensuring the absence of unit roots to avoid the impact of non-stationary data on regression results. Third, cointegration tests are conducted to determine whether long-term stable relationships exist between variables. Fourth, the optimal lag order is selected using AIC and BIC criteria to determine the optimal lag order for the PVAR model, ensuring model fit and interpretability. Fifth, robustness tests are conducted to validate model stability and consistency across multiple scenarios. Sixth, we use Granger causality analysis to examine the causal relationship between climate variables and DR, providing a theoretical foundation for dynamic analysis. Seventh, this study conducts IRF analysis to examine the dynamic response of DR to climate change, revealing both short-term and long-term impacts. Finally, we assess the explanatory contribution of each climate factor to DR variability and analyze their relative importance using FEVD.

The significance and utility of the PVAR model lie in its ability to reveal interactions among multiple variables and their dynamic changes, its applicability to panel data exhibiting temporal and spatial heterogeneity, and to resolve potential endogeneity issues between variables, thereby enhancing the accuracy of assessments. It also clarifies which climate factors directly influence resilience changes and their short-term and long-term effects across different time periods. Furthermore, it can reveal the contribution of variables such as temperature, precipitation, and wind speed to changes in DR.

Interpretable machine learning estimation under CMIP6 scenarios

To comprehensively assess the impact of future climate change on the DR of Chinese cities, this study employs CMIP6 (Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6) models and RCP-SSP (Representative Concentration Pathway-Shared Socioeconomic Pathways) scenario. RCPs describe pathways of greenhouse gas intensities in the atmosphere, while SSPs describe global development trajectories under different socioeconomic scenarios.

In terms of scenario settings, this study selected five representative climate-society pathways scenarios. Among which, SSP1-2.6, a low-carbon development pathway, assumes that the world achieves large-scale emissions reductions and implements robust environmental policies, keeping global temperature rise below 2°C. The world will transition toward sustainable development, promoting a green economy and global cooperation, with climate change effectively curbed; SSP2-4.5, the medium-emission pathway, reflects current policy trends, featuring relatively realistic climate change responses and moderate emission reductions. Greenhouse gas emissions stabilize, with global temperatures projected to rise by approximately 3°C; SSP3-7.0, the high-emission pathway, reflects a pathway of worsening global governance imbalances or regional disparities, lacking effective emission reduction measures, with global temperatures rising over 4°C and severe climate risks and socioeconomic impacts; SSP4-6.0, the uneven development pathway. In this scenario, climate resilience varies significantly across regions, with some areas implementing robust emission reduction measures while others lag behind. Global temperatures are projected to rise by approximately 3.5°C, potentially triggering more frequent extreme weather events; SSP5-8.5, an extreme pathway of high-growth and high-emission, assumes rapid global economic growth accompanied by high emissions, potentially pushing global temperatures beyond 4°C. Greenhouse gas concentrations continue to rise rapidly, representing the most extreme scenario, with climate change impacts becoming exceptionally severe. Each scenario represents distinct climate change pathways and socioeconomic conditions.

This study constructs a double-model comparative prediction framework based on RF and BP-ANN model and the optimal model is selected to predict the DR of 288 Chinese cities from 2022-2050 under five RCP-SSP scenarios. The framework comprises four major steps: Input variable design, output variable design, model construction and training, final model selection and model interpretation, the specific prediction process is detailed in Text S1. First, we select 20 core indexes covering four criterion levels as input variables. All input variables undergo Min-Max normalization prior to modeling to avoid interference in model training caused by differing dimensions and magnitudes. DRI-Soc, DRI-Econ, DRI-Env, DRI-Eng are trained and predicted by RF and BP-ANN as output variables. The final prediction result of DRI-C is obtained by summing DRI-Soc, DRI-Econ, DRI-Env, and DRI-Eng.

The data of RF and BP-ANN was obtained from urban samples collected between 2007 and 2021, and divided into a training dataset (80%) and a test dataset (20%), with an additional 1/6 of the training data allocated as a validation set. We select the root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and coefficient of determination (R2) to evaluate the accuracy of RF and BP-ANN models, these metrics are calculated in Formula S3. Moreover, hyperparameter tuning represents a critical stage in model development. For the RF model, this study employs a 6-fold cross-validation combined with 120 iterations of randomized search. The hyperparameters, including the number of trees, maximum depth, feature sampling strategy, and split criteria, are optimized via RandomizedSearchCV, with the splitting criterion set to minimize the mean squared error. In contrast, the BP-ANN model uses 6-fold cross-validation and a randomized search to jointly optimize the network architecture (e.g., the number of hidden layers and units per layer), regularization parameters (Dropout and L2), and training parameters (e.g., learning rate and batch size). Early stopping and dynamic learning rate reduction are incorporated to mitigate overfitting. The final model selection is primarily based on a comprehensive validation score (Val-score), which is calculated by weighting normalized R2, RMSE, and MAE metrics99; if the score difference between models falls below a threshold, a hierarchical decision strategy is applied by successively referring to the test set score and the validation R2 value (Val-R2), the calculation formula is provided in Formula S4.

In addition, we utilized the SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) method to analyze the complex relationship between four guideline-level indexes and DR. SHAP analysis can enhance the interpretability of machine learning by quantifying the effects of individual features and their interaction patterns.100 The method employs cooperative game theory principles to calculate each feature’s marginal contribution through Shapley values.101 For the jth indicator, the SHAP value is calculated by the formula.(Equation 8)φj=∑s⊆m∖{j}|s|!(|m|−|s|−1)!|m|![f(s∪{j})−f(s)]

φj denotes the SHAP value for jth feature, representing its total impact on the model prediction. m represents the total number of features. s denotes a feature subset. |s| denotes the number of subsets s. f(s∪{j}) and f(s) denote the model results with and without jth feature, respectively. In summary, the SHAP value can measure feature contribution by synthesizing the weighted contributions of all possible feature combinations.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative analyses were conducted using prefecture-level city panel data. Indicator normalization, weight calculation, machine learning prediction, and SHAP interpretation were performed using Python, while PVAR estimation and related statistical tests were conducted using Stata. Spatial statistics and mapping were implemented using GIS software. The drought resilience index was calculated using the Entropy weight–CRITIC method and the weighted sum model, and DRI-C was classified into five levels using the Jenks natural breaks method. Spatial clustering was assessed using Global Moran’s I, local indicators of spatial association, and Getis-Ord Gi∗ statistics. PVAR analysis was used to examine the dynamic relationships between drought resilience and climatic factors, with stationarity, cointegration, lag-order selection, model stability, Granger causality, impulse response functions, and forecast error variance decomposition evaluated. RF and BP-ANN models were assessed using R2, RMSE, and MAE, and their hyperparameters were optimized using randomized search with six-fold cross-validation. SHAP values were used to interpret feature contributions. Statistical significance was evaluated at p < 0.05 for tests reporting p values, unless alternative confidence levels or significance thresholds were specified.
