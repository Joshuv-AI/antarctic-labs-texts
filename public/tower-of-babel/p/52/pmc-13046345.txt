Development and characterization of a hydrogel containing active principles of lotus leaves (Nelumbo nucifera): An in vitro study
Authors: Akshatha Raj, Rohit Prasad, Bhavya Shetty, Dafney Melissa Rodrigues, Debasmita Das, K. Deekshitha, S. Gaanashree
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to formulate and evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of a hydrogel incorporating lotus leaf extract as a potential local drug delivery system for periodontal disease treatment.Materials and Methods:Lotus leaves were extracted using a hydroalcoholic Soxhlet method, and the resulting extract was incorporated into a hydrogel. The antibacterial activity of the hydrogel was assessed in vitro against Fusobacterium nucleatum, a key pathogen in periodontal disease. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) were determined. The gel’s physical characteristics, including pH, consistency, and appearance, were also evaluated.Results:The lotus leaf extract demonstrated significant antibacterial activity with an MIC and MBC of 600 μg/mL (0.6 mg/mL) against F. nucleatum. The hydrogel formulation was green, clear, and semisolid and exhibited a uniform texture with a pH of 7.0, indicating suitability for periodontal application.Conclusion:The lotus leaf extract-based hydrogel showed promising antimicrobial properties and favorable physical characteristics, suggesting its potential as an effective local treatment for periodontitis. These findings highlight the viability of using natural plant extracts in innovative hydrogel formulations to address periodontal disease. Further research is needed to evaluate the lotus leaf extract gel’s efficacy, stability, and safety both in vitro and in vivo and its impact on a broader range of periodontal pathogens.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontal disease is a prevalent and persistent oral health challenge, characterized by inflammation of the gingiva and progressive destruction of the supporting periodontal tissues. The disease is driven by complex interactions between the host immune response and the bacterial biofilm at the gingival margin. The inflammatory process can lead to loss of soft tissue, alveolar bone resorption, and ultimately tooth loss if left untreated.

Existing treatments for periodontal disease, such as scaling, root planing, and periodontal surgery, aim to control the bacterial infection and suppress the host inflammatory response. However, these approaches often fail to achieve complete regeneration of the lost periodontal tissues, and the disease frequently recurs.

Novel therapeutic strategies, such as the use of natural compounds with anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties, have emerged as promising alternatives for the management of periodontal disease. One such compound is the extract of Nelumbo nucifera, a plant that has been traditionally used to treat various inflammatory conditions.

N. nucifera, more commonly known as the lotus or the sacred lotus, is an aquatic perennial plant belonging to Nelumbonacea that has been cultivated for more than 2000 years. Lotus is used in traditional medicine by people for its tremendous health benefits. Many pharmacological activities on lotus leaves, roots, stem, and seeds have proven its anti-inflammatory, antipyretic, hypoglycemic, immunomodulatory, antioxidant, antibacterial, psychopharmacological, anticancer, and tissue regenerative properties.[1]

Numerous flavonoids, including quercetin, have already been isolated from lotus leaves. Quercetin extracted from lotus leaves has been determined to have antimicrobial activity and can be a potential antibacterial agent for periodontitis.[2] Till now, these extracts are used in the form of toothpaste or mouth rinse.[3] There are no studies on using these extracts in the form of a gel. Successful formulation of the gel will open further avenues for in vivo research of this gel as a local drug delivery (LDD) agent in the treatment of periodontitis.[1]

In support of herbal research trends, Sthanislas et al., in their work, focused on nanoparticle production, demonstrating that natural compounds can be engineered into effective therapeutic platforms. The anti-inflammatory and antioxidative potentials reported by Sthanislas et al. align with known phytochemical actions of lotus leaves – particularly flavonoids such as quercetin – suggesting that beyond antibacterial activity, N. nucifera may offer broader biological benefits relevant to periodontitis management.[4]

Therefore, the present in vitro study is designed to formulate and assess the antibacterial efficacy of lotus leaf extract as a potential LDD system for the management of periodontal disease.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of lotus leaf material

A sufficient quantity of leaves of the lotus (N. nucifera) species was collected. The leaves were thoroughly rinsed with water to remove dirt and impurities, following which the leaves were disinfected using 7% hydrogen peroxide to remove fungal infections and fungal gnat infestations. The leaves were then shade-dried for 3–5 days. The dried leaves were coarsely ground using a mortar and pestle. An appropriate amount of lotus leaves powder was weighed using an analytical balance to obtain 100 g of powder [Figure 1]. The collected samples were then subjected to hydroalcoholic (Soxhlet) extraction, following which the active principles, such as flavonoids, glycosides, and alkaloids, will be obtained.

Dried lotus leaf powder

Hydroalcoholic extraction using soxhlet apparatus

The Lotus leaves powder was placed into the thimble of the Soxhlet apparatus [Figure 2]. The round-bottom flask of the apparatus was filled with 70% ethanol till the lotus leaves were completely submerged. The flask containing the solvent was heated to reflux, allowing the solvent vapour to rise and condense in the condenser, dripping onto the Lotus leaf powder in the thimble. Continue refluxing for several hours (typically 6–8 h) to ensure thorough extraction of compounds from the lotus leaves.[5]

Hydroalcoholic extraction using soxhlet apparatus

After extraction, the round-bottom flask containing the extract from the Soxhlet apparatus was removed. The hydroalcoholic extract was evaporated using a rotary evaporator under reduced pressure and controlled temperature to remove the solvent and obtain a concentrated extract. The concentrated extracts yielded a dark greenish in color [Figure 3]. Around 5–7 ml of the extract was then kept in a sterile bottle, refrigerated, and preserved until further experiment.

Leaf extract

Preparation of culture medium to test antibacterial activity of the extract

To prepare the hydro gel, the concentration of the drug had to be determined first; for that, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) test was done to determine the concentration of the gel on the periodontal pathogen.

Bacterial strain: Fusobacterium nucleatum ATCC 25586.

Culture media: trypticase soy agar (TSA).

Incubator set to appropriate temperature and atmosphere for F. nucleatum growth (typically anaerobic conditions at 37°C).

Subculturing procedure

TSA plates were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions and allowed them to solidify.[6] The F. nucleatum ATCC strain was retrieved from the freezer storage at-80°c. The frozen bacterial stock vial was kept in a water bath set at room temperature until completely thawed. Using a sterile inoculation loop, a small portion of the thawed bacterial suspension was streaked onto the surface of the TSA plate in a zigzag pattern. The TSA plates were incubated in an anaerobic incubator at 37°C for 24–48 h, or until visible bacterial growth appeared. After incubation, the TSA plates were examined for characteristic colony morphology of F. nucleatum (typically small, circular, translucent colonies). A single colony was selected using a sterile inoculation loop and streaked it onto a fresh TSA plate to obtain a pure culture. It was repeated as necessary to obtain sufficient bacterial growth for further experiments or storage.

A fresh culture of F. nucleatum was inoculated in trypticase soy broth (TSB) and incubated under appropriate conditions until midlogarithmic phase (OD600 of approximately 0.5).

The density of the bacterial suspension was adjusted to match the McFarland standard 0.5, which corresponds to approximately 1–2 × 108 CFU/mL.

Preparation of extract stock solution

A stock solution of the extract was prepared and tested in a suitable solvent (e.g., DMSO or ethanol) at a concentration of 1–1000 mg/mL. The solution is thoroughly mixed and sterilised.

Preparation of test solutions

The extract stock solution with sterile growth medium (TSB) was diluted to obtain a series of two-fold dilutions in microtiter plates, covering a concentration range of 1–1000 mg/mL. A positive control (bacterial growth without extract) and a negative control (media without bacteria) in each microplate were included. Antibiotic Metronidazole (50 mg/mL) was used as control.

Inoculation of microtiter plates

One hundred microliter of the prepared bacterial suspension (containing approximately 5 × 106 CFU/mL) was added to each well of the microtiter plate containing the test solutions. Proper mixing was ensured by gently pipetting up and down.

Incubation

The microtiter plate was sealed with a sterile lid to prevent evaporation and contamination. The plates were incubated under appropriate conditions for F. nucleatum growth (typically anaerobic conditions at 37°C) for 24–48 h [Figure 4].

Fusobacterium nucleatum cultured plate

Measurement of growth

After the incubation period, bacterial growth in each well was assessed visually and by measuring the optical density (OD) at 600 nm using a spectrophotometer.

The MIC determined was the lowest concentration of the extract that completely inhibits visible growth or reduces the OD compared to the positive control.

Procedure for minimum inhibitory concentration

The purpose of the MIC test is to determine the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial agent that inhibits visible growth of a microorganism.

Various concentrations of the test samples are prepared. The test samples are inoculated with the F. nucleatum ATCC 25586. After inoculation, samples are evaluated for turbidity using the broth method to determine the lowest concentration that inhibits growth.

The microdilution test is performed using 96 well microtiter plate, approximately 50–100 μg of broth medium is transferred into each well. Each well is inoculated with a defined volume of bacterial suspension, typically ~5 × 105 CFU/mL. The plate is incubated at 37°C for 18 h, then examined for growth and turbidity [Figure 5].

Optical density using spectrophotometer

The MIC is the lowest antimicrobial agent concentration with no visible turbidity.

Procedure for minimum bactericidal concentration

The minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) test determines the minimum concentration of an antimicrobial agent required to kill 99.9% of a specific bacterial population within a defined time frame.

Serial dilutions of antimicrobial test compounds are made and are inoculated with test microorganisms.

These dilutions are further transferred into 96-well Microtiter Plates. In parallel, growth control and sterility control are also used in the test procedure. The microtiter plate is further incubated at a 37°C for 18 h. After incubation, bacterial growth is observed. Dilutions showing MIC of 600 μg/mL is plated on an agar medium and incubated to determine the viable cells.

Preparation of Nelumbo nucifera hydrogel and its in vitro characterization

For the preparation of the gel base, the basic method of preparation of hydrogel was followed. Depending on the results of the MIC, the concentration of the hydrogel was determined and was formulated.

To prepare the LDD of lotus leaves extract gel ensuring the bactericidal effect and still within safe phytochemical levels, 600 μg/mL. i.e., 0.6 mg/mL was determined by basic dilution formula C1V1 = C2V2 where: C1 = Initial concentration (stock extract solution), V1 = Volume of stock solution needed, C2 = Desired final concentration (600 μg/mL or 0.6 mg/mL), and V2 = Final total volume of the formulation (e.g. gel base + solvent + extract).

Herbal extracts are poorly soluble compounds and require a carrier to be soluble in the gel base. In the present study, polyethylene glycol (PEG) was used to solubilize the lotus leaf extract into the gel base. PEG also shows high thermal stability.[7]

0.5 g chitosan was dissolved in 10 mL of 2% acetic acid solution with continuous stirring with the help of a mechanical stirrer at 3600 rpm for 30 min.

0.5 g Carbopol 940 was dissolved in 10 mL of water and kept aside to swell for 30 min. It was then constantly stirred using a mechanical stirrer at 3000 rpm for 30 min.[7]

A mechanical stirrer set at 2000 rpm was used to frequently mix 10 mL of water with 0.5 g of polyethylene glycol 6000 (PEG 6000) over the course of 20 min. Next, 60 mg of N. nucifera leaf extract was added, and it was agitated until all of the extract was dissolved. In addition, the lotus leaf extract-containing PEG gel was added to the Carbopol gel and thoroughly mixed. After that, this mixture was added to the chitosan gel and thoroughly mixed for 30 min at a speed of about 5000 rpm using a mechanical stirrer. Triethanolamine was then used to bring the pH to a neutral level. The plasticizer, 2 cc of propylene glycol, was added and slowly mixed. After an hour of mixing, a clear gel was the result. After mixing, the solution was subjected to probe sonication to obtain a homogenous dispersion at 20 s on cycle that was followed by 5 s off-cycle for 10 min.

The gel prepared was subjected to in vitro characterization.[7] The gel formulation was examined for clearness, cooler, pH, consistency, and syringability. The consistency was assessed by pressing a little amount of gel between the thumb and index finger.

Determination of pH

The pH of the gel was determined using a standard buffer solution at neutral pH.[8]

One gram of gel was dispersed in 10 mL of purified water. The electrode was inserted to the sample, for 10 min at room temperature and the reading was noted.

Syringability study

Syringability of the gel formulation was evaluated by injecting the gel through a 26-gauge needle, which is used for oral drug delivery.

Assessment of minimum bactericidal concentration

Results of the MBC test indicated that lotus leaf extract exerted a bactericidal effect against F. nucleatum at a minimum concentration of ~ 600 μg/mL.

This suggests that the plant extract is highly effective against the F. nucleatum with dose-dependent inhibition and bactericidal activity.

In vitro characterisation of prepared gel

The obtained gel was green, clear, and semisolid in consistency. The prepared formulation was smooth and had a uniform surface. The consistency, homogeneity, and physical appearance of the gel were good. The average pH of the gel was 7.0.

DISCUSSION

Antibacterial activity of lotus leaf extract

This study demonstrated that lotus leaf extract exhibited significant antibacterial activity against F. nucleatum, a key pathogen in the initiation and progression of periodontal disease. The MIC and MBC values of 600 μg/mL (0.6 mg/mL) for the lotus leaf extract were comparable to, or greater than, those for the standard antimicrobial agent metronidazole. These observations indicated that lotus leaf extract is a promising and effective local antimicrobial agent for the targeted management of periodontitis. The findings aligned with prior research by Huh and Kim, who reported significant antibacterial activity of lotus leaf extract against Porphyromonas gingivalis, another major periodontal pathogen, with MIC and MBC values of 4 mg/mL.[9]

Lotus leaf extract exhibits strong antibacterial activity against various pathogenic bacteria, including those relevant to periodontal disease. The extract contains bioactive compounds, such as phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and alkaloids, which are believed to contribute to its antimicrobial effects. Studies have reported that lotus leaf extract inhibited the growth of harmful bacteria, such as Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhimurium, Staphylococcus aureus, and Bacillus subtilis.[10] Notably, its antiseptic capacity sometimes surpassed standard food preservatives, highlighting its potency. In addition, flavonoids such as quercetin within lotus leaves have demonstrated strong antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties, further underscoring the extract’s therapeutic potential for oral infections.

Hydrogel formulation and physical characteristics

Hydrogels represented innovative drug delivery platforms capable of responding to specific physiological stimuli, including enzymatic, hydrolytic, and environmental changes, to achieve controlled and site-specific release of active compounds.[11] The formulated hydrogel in this study displayed optimal physical and rheological properties, such as smooth, uniform texture, pH stability, and consistency for periodontal application. Incorporation of chitosan, Carbopol 940, and polyethylene glycol further improved the formulation’s stability and release profile.

A doxycycline proniosomal gel for periodontal developed by Mody et al.’s study emphasized the importance of achieving a stable, biocompatible gel base with sustained drug release,[12] which was also reflected in the present study. The current hydrogel, incorporating chitosan, Carbopol, and PEG, similarly demonstrated favorable physicochemical characteristics such as neutrality of pH, smooth consistency, and syringeability, suggesting suitability for placement in the periodontal pocket. The parallel between our lotus leaf hydrogel and doxycycline proniosomal systems strengthens the evidence that polymer-based gels can efficiently deliver active agents to periodontal pockets.

Relevance in the context of antibiotic resistance

The results underscored the importance of investigating natural, plant-based alternatives for addressing antibiotic-resistant oral pathogens. With the rising prevalence of antimicrobial resistance, herbal formulations validated for antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activity could provide sustainable and effective adjuncts to current periodontal therapies.

Limitations of this study

The main limitations of this study include its in vitro design, meaning that the antibacterial efficacy of the lotus leaf extract hydrogel was only evaluated under laboratory conditions and not within a living biological system. Results obtained from in vitro experiments may not directly translate to clinical effectiveness due to differences in the complexity of the oral environment, host immune response, and periodontal tissue dynamics.

In addition, this study assessed the action of the gel on a single bacterial species (F. nucleatum), whereas periodontal disease is caused by a diverse polymicrobial biofilm. The therapeutic potential of the hydrogel against other key pathogens and its broader spectrum antimicrobial effect remain unexplored. Further, aspects such as long-term stability, optimal dosing, safety, cytotoxicity, biocompatibility, and possible adverse effects of the hydrogel formulation were not addressed in this preliminary investigation.

To fully validate its clinical utility, future studies should include in vivo trials, evaluation against multiple periodontal pathogens, and assessment of patient safety and tolerance.

Future research directions

Future research of lotus leaf extract hydrogel is focused on in vivo studies to evaluate the gel’s antibacterial efficacy, safety, and biocompatibility, followed by clinical trials, which will help to establish the lotus leaf extract gel’s therapeutic range.

CONCLUSION

In summary, the development of a lotus leaf extract-based hydrogel shows significant promise as an adjunctive therapy for managing periodontitis. The formulation demonstrated robust antimicrobial properties and favourable physical characteristics, suggesting its potential to enhance existing treatment approaches. As periodontal care continues to evolve, incorporating effective herbal remedies like this could open new avenues for improving patient outcomes and advancing the field. While the current study was limited to an in vitro setting and a single gel formulation, the findings warrant further exploration. Future research should investigate the gel’s efficacy against a broader spectrum of disease-causing periodontal bacteria to fully capture its therapeutic potential.
