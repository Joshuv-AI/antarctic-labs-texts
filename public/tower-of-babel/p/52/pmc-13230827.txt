CANSD v1.0: the first map of agricultural nitrogen surplus across China from 2000 to 2022
Authors: Yanyi Li, Li He, Mengxi He, Zhenjie Tang, Yuxuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnder the dual challenges of global climate change and agricultural sustainability, spatially precise characterization of agricultural nitrogen surplus has become a key technical bottleneck in balancing food security and water environmental protection. Existing global and national nitrogen datasets remain restricted to individual nitrogen species or loss pathways, with no unified, long-term, spatially continuous gridded product for total agricultural nitrogen surplus—particularly at the national scale for China. Compounding these limitations, current modeling approaches are plagued by coarse statistical resolution, short temporal coverage, and overly simplified process representations that distort realistic nitrogen migration and cycling pathways. To address these critical gaps, this study constructs a 0.5° spatial resolution dataset of agricultural nitrogen surplus in China for the period 2000–2022 (CANSD v1.0). The grid-scale nitrogen surplus (NS) is operationally defined as a spatially continuous variable that quantifies agricultural nitrogen cycling heterogeneity at 0.5° resolution. Gridded values are post-processed by rescaling to align with prefecture-level total nitrogen surplus accounting, while realizing spatially continuous characterization based on the inherent spatial heterogeneity of agricultural nitrogen cycling. This approach overcomes the scale mismatch between administrative statistics and field-scale processes by integrating machine learning with spatial downscaling, thereby mitigating potential circular causality and enhancing the characterization of cross-scale transfer patterns. Our framework provides a practical basis foundation for evidence-based agricultural nitrogen management and environmental risk mitigation.Subject terms: Environmental impact, Developing world

Background & Summary

Reducing agricultural nitrogen pollution is a major challenge due to global climate change, which negatively affects sustainability1. It is estimated that the global annual nitrogen input to agricultural fields exceeds 200 million tons, with an efficiency of utilization below 50%, leading to approximately 100 million tons of reactive nitrogen entering environmental media through surface runoff, leaching, and gaseous emissions2,3. This process triggers a series of environmental risks, including water eutrophication, soil acidification, biodiversity decline, and the exacerbation of the greenhouse effect4. Constructing a high temporal and spatial resolution dataset for agricultural nitrogen surplus (NS) spatial distribution is a critical foundation for understanding the coupled mechanisms of nitrogen transfer across multiple media and formulating precise emission reduction strategies. However, existing data products still face significant gaps in terms of temporal and spatial continuity and mechanistic representation.

Recent advances in nitrogen cycle research have significantly advanced the quantification of individual nitrogen species5,6 and fluxes. State-of-the-art emission inventories such as EDGAR v8.1 (https://edgar.jrc.ec.europa.eu/) provide global small-scale gridded data for atmospheric nitrogen pollutants (NOx, NH3) with long-term coverage, widely adopted by bodies like IPCC (https://www.ipcc.ch/) and UNEP (https://www.unep.org/). Remote sensing products, such as Sentinel-5P (https://sentinels.copernicus.eu), enable high-precision monitoring of atmospheric NH3 and NO2 columns across regional to global scales. Complementary models including DNDC (Denitrification-Decomposition) and SWAT (Soil and Water Assessment Tool) support process-based simulations of agricultural NOX emissions, nitrate leaching, and soil nitrogen transformations at watershed or national levels. FAOSTAT’s updated nitrogen footprint database (https://www.fao.org/faostat/en) further offers consistent national-scale statistics for fertilizer use, crop production, and nitrogen balance accounting. Despite these advances, nearly all mainstream datasets and models remain species or pathway-specific: EDGAR and remote sensing focus exclusively on gaseous nitrogen; DNDC and SWAT excel in leaching or soil processes but demand intensive calibration; FAOSTAT lacks fine-gridded dynamics. None systematically couple gaseous, aqueous, and solid-phase nitrogen fluxes into a unified nitrogen surplus framework.

Traditional NS accounting relies mainly on two approaches: process-driven crop models, which are limited by the difficulty of localizing parameter calibration, thus hindering large-scale spatial application7–9; and statistical models, which can establish relationships between crop yield and predictor variables through empirical relations10,11. However, these models often fail to capture the nonlinear characteristics of nitrogen metabolism due to their linear assumptions, and the spatial transferability of locally calibrated parameters is poor12,13. Although existing spatial downscaling techniques encompass three major paradigms—dynamical, statistical, and hybrid methods—the dynamical downscaling relies on regional climate models constrained by physical mechanisms, resulting in high computational costs14–16. While statistical downscaling offers higher computational efficiency, it is constrained by the quality of observational data and the robustness of statistical relationships17.

This study focuses on China, the largest nitrogen fertilizer consumer globally, and evaluates the predictive performance of 15 machine learning models, including Random Forest, Gradient Boosting Machines, Deep Neural Networks, and Support Vector Regression. Four optimal models were selected based on cross-validation with R² > 0.97 and RMSE < 20%, and integrated into a multi-model framework for spatial downscaling of Nitrogen Surplus (NS). The study incorporates multi-source heterogeneous data: (1) agricultural (nitrogen surplus, cultivated land area, nitrogen fertilizer application), (2) environmental (N2O emissions, CO2 concentration, nitrogen deposition flux, runoff, vegetation cover), (3) soil (moisture, pH, nitrogen content, organic carbon stock, clay content, soil temperature), (4) geographic (elevation, slope), and (5) human activity (population density, electricity consumption, human footprint index). Using a feature importance-guided model selection strategy and residual iterative downscaling, the approach overcomes limitations of traditional models in capturing nonlinear relationships and spatial heterogeneity. The result is a 0.5° spatial resolution NS grid dataset for China 2000–2022 (CANSD v1.0), with supports evidence-based management of agricultural non-point source pollution. The methodology is applicable to other major agricultural regions worldwide.

Methods

Study area

China (73°33′E-135°05′E, 3°51′N-53°33′N), as a major global agricultural producer, boasts rich agricultural resource diversity, a long-standing agricultural civilization, and a large rural population base18–20. Influenced by factors such as its vast latitudinal span and complex topography, China is divided into nine broadly defined agricultural regions (Fig. 1). The Northeast Plain relies on fertile black soil for large-scale mechanized production of maize and soybeans21; the Huang-Huai-Hai Plain, as a historical major grain production area, primarily follows a wheat-maize rotation system22; the middle and lower Yangtze River regions, with a subtropical monsoon climate, have developed a rice-oilseed-aquaculture integrated system23. The northern arid and semi-arid regions rely on water-saving irrigation to develop cotton, fruit crops, and grassland livestock farming24; the Loess Plateau focuses on soil and water conservation, supporting drought-resistant crops and ecological livestock farming; the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, with concentrated cultivation of barley and high-altitude livestock farming in river valleys25, is another distinct agricultural zone. The Sichuan Basin and surrounding areas, characterized by a humid climate to maintain an advantage in integrated rice-pig production26, while the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau utilizes a complex climate to develop unique mountain agriculture27. South China focuses on tropical cash crops and double-cropping rice cultivation28.

Nine major agricultural regions in China.

In addition, China’s vast population (1.4 billion) is unevenly distributed, with higher density in the east and lower density in the west, contributing to a spatial concentration trend29. These factors significantly affect nitrogen surplus. Therefore, in this study, a multi-dimensional variable system was constructed to support machine learning model training, integrating natural geographical patterns (topography, climate), human activity intensity (spatial distribution characteristics of population density, which is higher in the east and lower in the west), and agricultural system dynamic stability (inter-annual fluctuations and long-term trends).

Data collection

The data used in this study comprises the following multi-source datasets (Table 1): (1) Agricultural data: city-level agricultural nitrogen surplus data, national cultivated land area data, and nitrogen fertilizer usage data; (2) Environmental data: N2O emission data, CO2 emission data, nitrogen deposition data, runoff data, and vegetation coverage data; (3) Soil data: soil moisture, soil pH, soil total nitrogen (TN), soil organic carbon, clay content, and soil temperature; (4) Geographical data: elevation and slope; (5) Human activity data: population, electricity consumption, and human footprint. To clarify the causal relationships among these collected variables and avoid potential circularity, we constructed a directed acyclic graph (DAG) based on classic nitrogen cycle theory and relevant high-impact studies (Fig. 2). The DAG explicitly delineates three distinct components-exogenous driving factors, intermediate medium variables governing nitrogen surplus, and indicator validation variables—thereby establishing a rigorous causal foundation for variable selection. Notwithstanding this causal framework, the strict causal independence of individual predictor variables cannot be fully guaranteed, as some environmental covariates may act as proxies for agroecosystem state rather than strictly independent mechanistic drivers. Furthermore, the high predictive performance achieved in spatial disaggregation largely reflects the strong spatial structuring of agricultural systems and administrative statistics, rather than purely mechanistic predictability of nitrogen dynamics.

Agricultural data

In the nitrogen cycle of agricultural ecosystems, the expansion of cultivated land influences nitrogen flux through two primary mechanisms. First, the accumulation of biomass driven by enhanced crop photosynthesis increases nitrogen demand in farmland30. Second, as farming practices intensify, the nitrogen fertilizer compensation mechanisms employed to sustain crop productivity significantly disrupt the system’s input-output balance31,32. The dynamic equilibrium between nitrogen fertilizer application per unit area and crop nitrogen absorption efficiency serves as a critical factor governing nitrogen residue in agricultural soils33,34. When nitrogen application surpasses the crop’s maximum nitrogen utilization threshold, surplus nitrogen accumulates abnormally in the soil35. This nitrogen surplus exhibits notable spatial heterogeneity and environmental mobility, with a portion being released into the atmosphere via key gaseous pathways: N2O emissions from microbial denitrification and NH3 volatilization from soil and fertilizer surfaces36. Atmospheric reactive nitrogen further triggers severe environmental consequences including climate forcing by N2O and aerosol formation via NH3 reactions with atmospheric acids, while the remaining surplus nitrogen enters aquatic systems through nitrification, leaching, and surface runoff transfer. This cross-medium nitrogen diffusion exacerbates a series of secondary environmental issues: atmospheric nitrogen deposition aggravates soil acidification and forest degradation, while aquatic nitrogen enrichment induces eutrophication in freshwater and marine ecosystems and subsequent biodiversity loss37,38.

This dataset features a spatial resolution of 5 km × 5 km and a temporal resolution of one year.

Environmental data

Nitrogen cycling processes, particularly nitrification and denitrification, are core in the soil nitrogen cycle, not only regulates the availability of nitrogen in the soil but also leads to environmental effects such as soil acidification, nitrate leaching, and nitrous oxide emissions49. Excessive nitrogen application in farmland or prolonged soil waterlogging significantly accelerates N2O release, resulting in reduced fertilizer efficiency and contributing to the greenhouse effect. Atmospheric nitrogen compounds enter farmland ecosystems via both dry and wet deposition pathways, and together they constitute a substantial external nitrogen input in intensive agricultural regions50. In this study, only atmospheric inorganic nitrogen wet deposition data were incorporated into the analysis, as dry deposition fluxes are subject to high spatiotemporal variability and lack consistent, high-resolution observational data across the study area-limitations that would introduce significant uncertainty to the quantitative assessment of agricultural nitrogen surplus. Although carbon dioxide does not directly participate in the nitrogen cycle, its increased concentration can enhance the nitrogen utilization efficiency of global farmlands through carbon-nitrogen synergistic effects42. Soluble nitrate fertilizers are regulated by rainfall intensity, soil texture, and irrigation methods, making them more likely to migrate with water51. Surface runoff contributes 50%–75% of total nitrogen and 41%–69% of nitrate nitrogen loss52, while vegetated areas, through root absorption and canopy interception, can effectively control nitrogen migration. Research has shown that mixed planting systems can increase total soil nitrogen compared to monoculture53, and soil nitrogen storage accumulates positively with plant diversity and forest age54. These multiple mechanisms jointly drive the distribution of nitrogen between the land and atmosphere interface. Excess nitrogen diffuses through gaseous volatilization and water infiltration, becoming a key trigger for ecological risks such as eutrophication.

The N₂O and CO₂ data55 are obtained from the EDGAR platform (https://edgar.jrc.ec.europa.eu), which consolidates multiple resources to provide emission estimates of the three major greenhouse gases (CO₂, N₂O) as well as fluorinated gases, categorized by sector and country. These datasets have a spatial resolution of 0.1° × 0.1° and a temporal resolution of one year. The nitrogen deposition dataset56 is provided by the National Ecosystem Science Data Center, National Science & Technology Infrastructure of China (http://www.nesdc.org.cn). This dataset offers a spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km and a temporal resolution of five years. The runoff dataset57 is sourced from the National Tibetan Plateau/Third Pole Environment Data Center (https://www.tpdc.ac.cn/home). After undergoing statistical downscaling and bias correction, the model demonstrates minimal simulation error for climate models in the China region. The Variable Infiltration Capacity model (VIC), a macroscale hydrological model widely applied in hydrological cycle simulation and water resource assessment, driven by observed meteorological data, shows robust runoff simulation results for the historical period. The spatial resolution of this dataset is 0.25°, with a temporal resolution of one year. The vegetation cover dataset58 is also from TPDC. Through time-space trend analysis and verification, this dataset meets both temporal and spatial variation trends. The spatial resolution is 250 m × 250 m, and the temporal resolution is monthly.

Soil data

Soil properties, including physicochemical characteristics, are crucial in regulating nitrogen cycling and residue dynamics in agricultural systems through complex interactions. Soil texture is a fundamental factor: sandy soils, with their high permeability, accelerate nitrate leaching, while clay soils retain ammonium nitrogen more effectively through physical adsorption59. Biochar, a product of biomass pyrolysis, has been shown to adsorb both ammonium (NH₄+-N) and nitrate (NO₃−-N) nitrogen60, thereby significantly influencing nitrogen transformations in soils. Organic matter content regulates nitrogen availability through the mineralization and immobilization balance. A lower carbon-to-nitrogen ratio enhances mineralization, releasing available nitrogen, while a higher ratio leads to microbial nitrogen competition61, with mineralization rates strongly correlated with total nitrogen and organic carbon content62. Soil pH regulates nitrogen transformations, with acidic conditions inhibiting denitrification and enhancing nitrate accumulation, while neutral to alkaline conditions promote ammonia volatilization and denitrification63. Soil water retention, in turn, affects nitrogen migration; higher moisture content can lead to increased N₂O flux due to enhanced nitrification64. Temperature regulates microbial activity and chemical reaction rates, thereby influencing nitrogen dynamics65,66. The combined effects of these factors result in spatial heterogeneity in nitrogen retention and migration in farmland.

The soil moisture dataset67 is provided by the National Cryosphere Desert Data Center (http://www.ncdc.ac.cn) and is constructed using three passive microwave remote sensing products. It boasts high accuracy across the entire time series, with a spatial resolution of 0.05° × 0.05° and a temporal resolution of one month. Soil pH, total nitrogen (TN), soil organic carbon (C), and clay content data68 are supported by the National Earth System Science Data Center, National Science & Technology Infrastructure of China (http://www.geodata.cn). This data is based on a predictive soil mapping paradigm that incorporates an adaptive deep function fitting method, advanced ensemble machine learning techniques, and a high-performance parallel computing environment. It covers basic soil physical and chemical properties across China, with a spatial resolution of 90 m and a temporal resolution of 9 years. Soil temperature data69 comes from NASA (https://data.staging.idas-ds1.appdat.jsc.nasa.gov). This dataset consists of land surface parameters simulated using the Noah 3.6.1 model in the FEWS NET (Famine Early Warning Systems Network) Land Data Assimilation System (FLDAS), adapted from the Land Information System (LIS7). The spatial resolution is 0.1° × 0.1° and the temporal resolution is monthly.

Geographic data

Topographic factors, through their influence on water-heat redistribution and the driving of material transfer70,71, profoundly shape the spatial differentiation of nitrogen cycling. In mountainous ecosystems, slope aspect and altitude serve as key topographic features influencing soil nitrogen dynamics68. Elevation changes restructure the nitrogen transformation environment through vertical climate differentiation. In high-altitude areas, low temperatures inhibit microbial-mediated nitrogen mineralization72, while enhanced precipitation leaching further exacerbates the loss of dissolved nitrogen73. Conversely, relatively warmer environments in low-altitude areas enhance nitrogen transformation activities74. Slope gradient, as a key topographic attribute, directly influences the intensity of surface runoff erosion and the development pattern of subsurface flow75, driving the simultaneous migration and loss of both particulate and dissolved nitrogen. This multi-scale coupling of topography-driven processes ultimately alters the forms and pathways of nitrogen storage and transfer, profoundly affecting the spatiotemporal patterns of nitrogen cycling in agricultural ecosystems.

The elevation and slope datasets are sourced from the National Earth System Science Data Center, National Science & Technology Infrastructure of China (http://www.geodata.cn). These datasets are derived from the Copernicus Global Digital Elevation Model (DEM), released by the European Space Agency (ESA) in 2022. The data has been mosaicked and clipped according to the Copernicus DEM, providing a spatial resolution of 30 m × 30 m.

Human activity data

Agricultural activity data are critical for quantifying human-driven disruptions to the nitrogen (N) cycle. Intensified agricultural production has shifted the N cycle from closed to open systems, directly driving agricultural N surplus76. We therefore selected three agriculturally relevant indicators-human footprint index, agricultural electricity consumption, and land cover change-to characterize human disturbances, with clear mechanistic links to N surplus dynamics. The human footprint index reflects agricultural expansion into natural landscapes: reduced vegetation weakens biological N fixation, while increased surface sealing enhances runoff and erosion, accelerating reactive N migration and surplus accumulation. Electricity consumption serves as a proxy for intensive farming (irrigation, mechanization, fertilizer production)77,78, closely correlating with anthropogenic N inputs to farmlands. Land cover change further alters hydrological pathways and eliminates plant N sinks79 promoting N loss via runoff and regulating surplus distribution. Together80, these variables capture the key agricultural perturbations to N cycling, directly linking human activity to agricultural N surplus and ensuring our predictor set remains focused on farm-relevant drivers.

The population data is sourced from LandScan (https://landscan.ornl.gov/), developed by the Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) of the U.S. Department of Energy and distributed by East View Cartographic. LandScan employs advanced methodologies, including Geographic Information Systems (GIS) and remote sensing, and is recognized as the global standard for population data release. It represents the most accurate and reliable geospatially-based global database on population dynamics, featuring detailed distribution models with exceptional resolution. Electricity consumption data81 can be accessed at 10.6084/m9.figshare.17004523.v1, with a spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km and a temporal resolution of one year. Human footprint data82, derived from research published in Scientific Data, incorporates eight variables that capture various dimensions of human impact, including built environments, population density, nighttime lights, farmland, pasture, roads, railways, and navigable waterways. Developed using the methodology by Sanderson and Venter, this dataset offers a spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km and a temporal resolution of one year.

Data processing

To address temporal discontinuities across 2000–2022, targeted preprocessing was applied to agricultural, environmental, soil, geographic, and human activity data according to their temporal characteristics. Model training was based on fully consistent time-series data, with gap-filling via linear extrapolation for dynamic variables restricted to the early prediction period and partial validation period, so as to avoid artificial distortions of temporal trends. Variables with coarse temporal resolution were held constant within each consistent interval, while quasi-static and static variables (soil properties, topographic attributes, cultivated land area) were kept constant or assigned to the latest reliable observations to maintain natural temporal stability and avoid spurious interannual variability. These procedures are consistent with common practices in agricultural nitrogen cycling and land surface process research, ensuring the temporal consistency and reliability of CANSD v1.0.

Modelling methods

Flowchart of downscaling agricultural nitrogen surplus space in China based on multiple data sources and machine learning models.

The modelling workflow comprises five main steps (Fig. 3). First, multi-source spatial datasets were standardized. Specifically, bilinear resampling is applied to adjust the spatial resolution of the original multi-source dataset, which encompasses 18 parameters across five primary categories: agriculture, environment, soil, geography, and human activity. Second, city-level aggregates were derived by spatially averaging to aggregate these parameters to the city-level administrative boundaries, resulting in a standardized dataset that aligns precisely with the spatial scale. Building on this, a machine learning training set is developed, where city-level agricultural nitrogen surplus serves as the response variable, and a range of multidimensional environmental factors are used as explanatory variables.

The second stage involves the selection of optimal models through a comprehensive multi-algorithm evaluation. The performance of 15 algorithms, including Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Deep Neural Networks (DNN) (Table 2), is systematically assessed. Using ten-fold cross-validation and a range of evaluation metrics, such as R² and RMSE, the most effective model combination is identified, comprising CATBOOST, KNN, RF, and LIGHTGBM. The average R² value for the validation set achieves 0.95 ± 0.04, demonstrating superior model fitting performance.

Third, a multi-model ensemble was used to predict NS at the 0.5° grid scale. Using the optimal model combination, the agricultural nitrogen surplus for the period 2000–2022 at the city level is predicted, and the average of the four model outputs is taken as the baseline prediction result. Following this, reverse spatial disaggregation procedures are applied to refine the nitrogen surplus distribution. Initially, the original data is resampled to a 0.5° × 0.5° spatial resolution, ensuring consistency in the data points for each year. By extracting data points for each grid cell, the nitrogen surplus value and 18 influencing factor parameters are determined. The city-level prediction model is then transferred to the 0.5° grid cells, where standardized high-resolution environmental factor data are inputted to generate preliminary grid-based nitrogen surplus distribution predictions.

Fourth, a residual-based spatial adjustment was applied to align grid estimates with city-level totals. To improve alignment between grid-scale estimates and aggregated municipal accounts, spatial residual correction techniques are applied. The total nitrogen surplus at the city level serves as a constraint, and correction factors are incorporated to refine the initial grid prediction values. This process ensures that the distribution of nitrogen surplus across grid cells is consistent with city-level statistical totals. By reducing local biases introduced during model transfer, this step improves the consistency of nitrogen surplus spatial disaggregation.

Finally, the gridded predictions were integrated to produce the final CANSD v1.0 dataset. The outcomes from spatiotemporal prediction, downscaling modeling, and residual correction are integrated and evaluated for consistency. Ultimately, a 0.5° resolution annual agricultural nitrogen surplus grid dataset for China from 2000 to 2022 is constructed. This dataset provides a more precise representation of nitrogen’s spatiotemporal dynamics and offers essential data support for targeted agricultural non-point source pollution management and spatial governance.

Machine learning models

To fully exploit machine learning models for the systematic exploration of nitrogen migration patterns, 15 representative models with diverse architectures-including ensemble learning, nearest neighbor algorithms, and neural networks-were selected and implemented on the PyCharm 2020 platform. Appropriate parameter settings (Table 3) were carefully chosen to establish a comprehensive multi-dimensional model comparison framework for learning and predicting city-level agricultural nitrogen surplus data in China from 2006 to 2016, alongside the corresponding 18 influencing factors (Table 4). To mitigate overfitting risks, most strategies were implemented in the models, including L2 regularization, hyperparameter tuning, feature scaling, tree complexity control, early stopping, and bagging. The models’performance was rigorously assessed using ten-fold cross-validation. Among these models, CatBoost, KNN, Random Forest, and LightGBM exhibited exceptional predictive performance, with R² values surpassing 0.97 and RMSE values remaining below 15%. To further strengthen the model’s robustness, a weighted averaging approach was applied, effectively mitigating prediction bias inherent in individual models and enabling a more accurate representation of the nonlinear coupling mechanisms governing nitrogen input, transformation, and output within agricultural systems.

CATBOOST83, developed by Yandex, is a gradient boosting decision tree framework with its core innovation centered around native support for categorical variables84. The algorithm utilizes Ordered Boosting technology to restructure the boosting process, effectively addressing the prediction bias caused by target leakage, a common issue in traditional gradient boosting85, thereby enhancing the model’s generalization performance. CatBoost achieves exponential improvements in training efficiency through a symmetric tree structure design and GPU-based parallel acceleration technology86, making it particularly suitable for modeling high-dimensional, heterogeneous data. This algorithm strikes a balance between model accuracy and engineering implementation87, offering an efficient solution for spatiotemporal heterogeneous data analysis in complex scenarios, such as agricultural environmental systems.

KNN88 is a simple and intuitive supervised learning algorithm, widely used in classification and regression tasks89. Its fundamental principle involves calculating the distance between the sample and other points in the training data, then selecting the K nearest neighbors for voting (in classification tasks) or averaging (in regression tasks) to predict the label of an unknown sample. Essentially, KNN is an instance-based lazy learning paradigm that does not require an explicit training process, directly mapping the raw data into decision rules90. Its model-free nature allows it to demonstrate unique advantages on nonlinear separable datasets, making it especially suitable for applications with significant local patterns and complex feature interactions.

Random Forest91, as a representative algorithm in the ensemble learning framework92, optimizes predictions by constructing a collection of heterogeneous decision trees. During the training process, RF uses the Bootstrap method to randomly select samples from the original dataset for training, and at each tree construction step, a random subset of features is chosen, which increases the model’s diversity. Ultimately, RF aggregates the results of all decision trees to obtain the final prediction. This algorithm strikes a balance between model accuracy and interpretability93, making it particularly suitable for nonlinear modeling needs in agricultural environmental systems94, which involve multi-source heterogeneous data.

LIGHTGBM94, developed by Microsoft, is an efficient gradient boosting decision tree algorithm designed specifically for handling large-scale data95. Compared to traditional methods, LightGBM significantly enhances training speed, memory efficiency, and prediction accuracy through the use of histogram algorithms and leaf-wise splitting strategies96. It supports automatic handling of categorical features, effectively processes sparse data and missing values, and accelerates model training through data distribution-based optimizations. Its unique asymmetric tree structure design provides the algorithm with stronger nonlinear expression capabilities, making it exceptionally suitable for modeling multi-source heterogeneous data in ecological environmental systems, demonstrating outstanding spatiotemporal scalability.

Spatial decomposition

Spatial decomposition in this study refers to allocating prefecture-level accounting-based nitrogen surplus to 0.5° grid cells. Grid-scale N surplus is conceptually defined as a spatially continuous variable that characterizes local N balance status. Notably, these grid-scale estimates represent a statistical spatial allocation of administrative nitrogen balances and should not be interpreted as direct measurements of soil nitrogen accumulation or loss processes. Although the spatial decomposition framework is statistically robust, it constitutes a statistical disaggregation procedure rather than a process-based representation of biogeochemical nitrogen fluxes. This decomposition is theoretically supported by the core principle that the spatial pattern of agricultural N surplus is jointly determined by the spatial heterogeneity of environmental and agricultural management covariates governing N input and output processes. The decomposition follows two consistent assumptions: (1) Agricultural N input and output processes are spatially separable at the 0.5° grid scale, with distinct spatial patterns driven by local biophysical and human factors; (2) The selected environmental and human activity covariates are meaningfully and statistically correlated with N surplus spatial distribution, enabling reliable quantification of within-prefecture heterogeneity. We selected 2015 for empirical validation because it features the most complete data coverage and relatively stable agricultural and environmental policies. Using 18 grid-scale covariates, the combined variance explanation rate (R²) for N surplus spatial variation reached 0.9095, and most covariates were significantly correlated with N surplus (P < 0.05), supporting the statistical basis for spatial disaggregation.

Using machine learning models to obtain predicted values of agricultural nitrogen surplus at the municipal level and 0.5° grid scale, a high-resolution spatial distribution simulation map is generated. Based on this, the gridded simulation results are used to construct a spatial weight matrix for the refined spatial allocation of municipal statistical data. Specifically, the time-series nitrogen surplus gridded data from 2000 to 2022 is first normalized to form a dynamic spatial correction coefficient set. This set of coefficients reflects the spatial variation in nitrogen surplus over time and can be used to calibrate municipal-level statistical data for more accurate spatial distribution.

We selected city-level data from 2006 to 2016 for training the model. We evaluated the performance of the regression model using the coefficient of determination (R²), root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean square error (MSE) (Fig. 4). The coefficient of determination (R²) reflects the model’s ability to explain the variation in the data, with values ranging from 0 to 1. The higher the value, the better the model fit. RMSE and MAE quantify the prediction bias in nitrogen residue in actual units, with RMSE being more sensitive to large errors, while MAE provides a direct representation of the average error level. MAPE expresses relative errors as percentages, facilitating cross-regional comparability of the data. MSE amplifies the weight of large errors through squaring, thus providing a gradient direction for model optimization. These five metrics comprehensively analyze the model’s performance from multiple perspectives, including explanatory power, absolute deviation, relative error, and response to outliers, ensuring a rigorous and thorough evaluation. Model fitting

This study conducts a feature importance analysis (Fig. 5) using machine learning models with an R² value exceeding 0.90, revealing a distinct hierarchical pattern in the mechanisms underlying nitrogen residue formation within agricultural systems. The most influential factor is nitrogen fertilizer application, which demonstrates high relative importance across all models. This highlights the important role of human activities in the agricultural nitrogen cycle—particularly the fertilization practices that directly regulate the dynamic equilibrium of soil nitrogen stocks. The efficiency and strategic implementation of fertilizer application are critical in mitigating agricultural non-point source pollution.

The characteristic importance of 18 influencing factors on agricultural nitrogen surplus under various models.

The second-tier influencing factors include N2O, caly, tn, cover, socd, and other parameters. These features are consistently identified as significant by at least five of the eight models, emphasizing the regulatory influence of natural background conditions. Static elements such as soil texture (caly) and tn exert a persistent effect on nitrogen migration and transformation processes, while semi-dynamic factors like cover reflect the regulatory capacity of biogeochemical processes in the environment.

Notably, different algorithms exhibit significant differences in how they represent certain features. The CNN model captures the elevation effect, while KNN emphasizes the impact of arable land area, indicating the varying sensitivities of machine learning models in capturing feature interactions. The KNN algorithm, which is based on local similarity, is sensitive to spatially heterogeneous factors like arable land area, whereas CNN enhances the extraction of spatial features such as terrain elevation through convolution operations. In contrast, tree models and SVM, which rely on global pattern recognition, are better at analyzing the combined effects of broad-area factors such as climate and soil texture. This disparity reflects the complexity of the influence of spatiotemporal dynamic factors (e.g. precipitation, irrigation)—their impact intensity depends on the model’s ability to represent spatiotemporal heterogeneity. In regions with significant seasonal variations, optimizing dynamic factor resolution may require the use of spatiotemporal embedding algorithms.

Spatial verification

To evaluate the spatial generalization ability of the models and avoid spatial overfitting or data leakage, a strict Leave-One-Region-Out (LOBO) cross-validation was conducted based on six agricultural regions (North China, Northeast China, Central China, South China, Southwest China, and Northwest China), where one entire region was used as the unseen validation set and the rest as the training set in each iteration to ensure complete spatial independence. Using R2 as the core evaluation index, the results showed that core models (CatBoost, LightGBM, and RandomForest) performed well in most regions, in North, Central, South, and Southwest China, while all four models exhibited negative R2 (Table 6) values in Northeast China due to extreme agro-environmental heterogeneity (unique cold-climate conditions, single cropping system, and extremely low nitrogen surplus levels), which underscores the distinctiveness of its agricultural regime. Across all six regions, average model performance was relatively low due to the poor results in Northeast China (Fig. 9). After excluding this region, average R2 values improved substantially, and in the four core agricultural zones, CatBoost, LightGBM, and RandomForest all achieved strong predictive performance. These LOBO results confirm the sound spatial generalization ability of the three tree-based models, whereas KNN exhibits weaker spatial adaptability. This result clearly indicates that model transferability is limited in regions with distinct and structurally unique agricultural regimes. Therefore, users should exercise caution and be aware of potentially higher uncertainty when applying the dataset or the methodology in such regions, particularly in Northeast China.

Uncertainty quantification

The uncertainty of the grid-scale agricultural nitrogen surplus dataset arises from three primary sources: (1) Model uncertainty: Variability in predictions from individual models in the ensemble framework and errors of the optimal model; (2) Downscaling uncertainty: Discrepancies in spatially allocating administrative-scale nitrogen surplus to grids and mismatches between driving factors and actual nitrogen cycling; (3) Data uncertainty: Spatial resolution disparities and interpolation of missing values in 18 driving factors.

To provide critical uncertainty estimates for the national-scale dataset and support its reliable reuse, a comprehensive quantification was performed across spatial and temporal dimensions, spanning three scales (grid-cell, municipal, provincial). Spatial uncertainty was assessed using relative uncertainty (RU) and multi-model standard deviation (SD) as core indicators. For 2015, mean RU at the grid-cell level was 19.98% (median = 17.28%, 95th percentile = 42.67%). At municipal and provincial scales, mean RU was 18.49% and 19.11%, respectively, with 95th percentile RU decreasing to 35.25% and 30.02% (Fig. 10), indicating spatial smoothing of local random uncertainty with increasing scale. Temporal trend uncertainty was evaluated by analyzing the significance of inter-annual trends. The proportion of grid cells with statistically significant trends was 49.7%, decreasing to 47.2% (municipal) and 27.3% (provincial). This reduction reflects the offsetting of opposing local trends during spatial aggregation. These multi-scale estimates (RU, SD, trend significance) enable users to assess the statistical significance of observed patterns, addressing the previous lack of uncertainty characterization and supporting reliable dataset reuse.

Ensemble weight sensitivity analysis

To further verify the stability and robustness of the multi-model ensemble prediction, we conducted an ensemble weight sensitivity analysis based on the 2017–2020 time-independent validation set. The baseline ensemble uses an equal-weight arithmetic mean (25% for each of the four models: CatBoost, KNN, LightGBM, and Random Forest). We designed five representative weight perturbation scenarios, including single-model weight adjustments (±5% to ±10%), pairwise weight exchanges, and global minor fluctuations, with all weights rescaled to ensure a total sum of 1.

As presented in Table 7, the equal-weight ensemble achieved a strong baseline performance with an R2 of 0.8887. For all minor and reasonable weight perturbations (Scenarios 1, 2, 3, and 5), the changes in ensemble R2 were negligible (ΔR2 ≤ 0.0017) and MAE variations were minimal. Even in Scenario 4, where the weight of the optimal CatBoost model was reduced by 5%, the ensemble performance improved rather than degraded, which demonstrates the complementary advantages among different models. These results quantitatively confirm that small variations in ensemble weights exert negligible effects on the final prediction, and the proposed ensemble framework is structurally stable and robust.

Key limitations and usage constraints

A core limitation of CANSD v1.0 is that grid-scale nitrogen surplus (NS) is derived by spatial decomposition from prefecture-level administrative totals, rather than being directly estimated at the grid scale, owing to constraints in fine-resolution nitrogen input and output data. Grid-scale absolute values are therefore recommended only for spatial pattern analysis, whereas aggregated values at administrative or regional scales are more appropriate for quantitative nitrogen balance accounting; overinterpretation of individual grid-cell values should be avoided.

Notably, CANSD v1.0 characterizes total unclassified agricultural nitrogen surplus, intended for regional nitrogen management and environmental risk assessment. It does not substitute process-based models for quantifying specific nitrogen species or pathways, and its application must align with appropriate spatial and methodological scales.
