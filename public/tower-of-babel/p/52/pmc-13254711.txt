Data-driven age partitioning for pediatric serum copper: implications for reference interval interpretation
Authors: Hien Thu Pham, Minh Thi Huyen Nguyen, Quyen Hue Luong, Phuong Thi Thuy The, Trang Thi Thu Tran, Mai Thi Chi Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo establish age-specific reference intervals for serum copper in healthy Vietnamese children and adolescents using a data-driven age-partitioning approach and to assess its implications for result interpretation.MethodsApparently healthy participants from birth to <19 years were recruited. Serum copper was measured by graphite furnace atomic absorption spectrophotometry. Candidate age thresholds were explored using recursive partitioning analysis and subsequently rounded to clinically interpretable groups. Final age partitions were determined based on recursive partitioning results, between-group statistical comparisons, effect size, clinical interpretability, and sample size. Differences between adjacent age groups and between sexes were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, Cliff's delta, and the Harris–Boyd method. Outliers were identified using the Horn algorithm. Reference intervals were estimated by the nonparametric percentile method in accordance with CLSI EP28-A3c.ResultsOf 1157 eligible participants, 36 outliers were excluded, leaving 1121 for analysis. Final age groups were 0–<6 days, 6 days to <12 months, 12 months to <7 years, and 7 to <19 years. Significant differences were observed between adjacent age groups, supporting age partitioning. Sex-related differences were small and did not support sex-specific partitions. The derived reference intervals were 1.9–15.4 μmol/L, 6.5–26.9 μmol/L, 9.3–35.8 μmol/L, and 7.9–22.6 μmol/L for the respective age groups.ConclusionsSerum copper concentrations vary substantially with age, particularly in early life, while sex differences are limited. Age-specific, but not sex-specific, reference intervals are recommended. These findings support clinically appropriate age partitioning to improve interpretation of pediatric copper results and reduce potential misclassification associated with overly broad age groups.

Introduction

Copper is an essential trace element involved in numerous biological processes, including oxidative metabolism, connective tissue formation, iron transport and neurological development. It serves as a structural or catalytic component of several key enzymes, such as ceruloplasmin, cytochrome c oxidase, superoxide dismutase and dopamine beta-hydroxylase, which are involved in cellular respiration, antioxidant defense and neurotransmitter synthesis [[1], [2], [3]]. Disruptions in copper homeostasis may lead to clinically important disorders, including copper deficiency and inherited diseases such as Wilson disease and Menkes disease [2,4].

Measurement of serum copper is widely used in the evaluation of disorders of copper metabolism and nutritional status. The clinical interpretation of laboratory results depends on appropriate reference intervals (RIs), which reflect the distribution of analyte concentrations in a healthy population. Establishing accurate RIs is particularly important in pediatric laboratory medicine, where many biochemical markers show substantial age-related variation due to rapid growth, developmental changes and hormonal influences [5,6]. Complex age-dependent patterns have been described for many laboratory measurands in children, emphasizing the importance of appropriate age partitioning to avoid misclassification of results [7].

Previous studies have shown that serum copper concentrations in children are strongly age dependent. Copper concentrations are typically low at birth, reflecting immature hepatic ceruloplasmin synthesis and distinct fetal copper metabolism, and increase during infancy as liver function matures and dietary intake becomes the primary source of copper [2,8]. Concentrations may then stabilize or gradually decline during later childhood and adolescence. Several pediatric RI studies have reported clear differences between age groups, whereas findings regarding the need for sex-specific partitioning have been less consistent [7,9,10].

Despite these observations, pediatric reference intervals for serum copper remain limited in many populations, and copper status may vary according to dietary patterns, environmental exposure and population characteristics [11,12]. Population-specific reference intervals are therefore needed to support accurate interpretation in clinical practice. In pediatric reference interval studies, the use of limited age categories may obscure clinically important age-related variation and may not fully capture the dynamic physiological changes occurring during early life, potentially increasing the risk of misclassification in younger children.

In recent years, data-driven approaches have been increasingly applied to the derivation of reference intervals. Methods such as recursive partitioning analysis may help identify candidate age thresholds objectively, without relying solely on arbitrary age grouping [[13], [14], [15]]. However, statistically derived cut-points do not necessarily correspond to clinically interpretable categories and should be considered alongside physiological plausibility and sample size. The aim of this study was therefore to establish age-specific reference intervals for serum copper in a healthy pediatric population using a data-driven age-partitioning approach integrated with clinical interpretability, and to examine its relevance for routine interpretation of pediatric copper results.

Methods

Study design and population

Apparently healthy children aged from birth to <19 years were recruited between April 2024 and November 2025 during routine health examinations at primary care clinics, kindergartens, and schools. Eligibility was based on good general health as assessed by parental interview, physical examination, and medical history review. Information on recent medication use, mineral or nutritional supplementation, and general dietary practices was obtained during screening interviews, and children with reported use of medications or supplements within the preceding month were excluded. Exclusion criteria included known acute or chronic illness (e.g., liver disease, malignancy, congenital metabolic disorders, or systemic inflammatory conditions), recent hospitalization, use of medications affecting liver function, and abnormal screening findings.

Neonates were recruited from well-baby clinics and post-natal follow-up visits. Only term infants with uncomplicated perinatal courses and no evidence of infection, hypoxia, or congenital anomalies were included; preterm infants and those requiring intensive care were excluded.

Sample collection and processing

Venous blood samples were collected into BD serum separator tubes (Becton Dickinson, USA) in the morning between 7 a.m. and 10 a.m. After allowing clot formation, samples were centrifuged within 2 h of collection to separate serum. The resulting serum samples were transported on ice in batches to the laboratory of the Vietnam National Children's Hospital. Upon arrival, serum aliquots were immediately stored at −80oC until analysis to ensure copper stability.

Measurement of serum copper and analytical quality assurance

Serum copper concentrations were measured by graphite furnace atomic absorption spectrophotometry (GFAAS) on a Shimadzu AA-7000 system equipped with a GFA-7000 graphite furnace and ASC-7000 autosampler. Samples were diluted 1:24 with a nitric acid-Triton X-100 matrix modifier and analyzed using external calibration with copper standards.

Analytical quality was monitored using internal and external quality control procedures. Internal quality control was performed with ClinChek-Control materials (Recipe, Germany). The method showed repeatability CVs of 2.14-3.09%, intermediate precision CVs of 4.68-6.68%, recovery of 94.3-108.5%, and a linear measurement range of 1-50 μmol/L. External quality assessment was performed through the Randox International Quality Assessment Scheme (RIQAS). For copper, the laboratory achieved acceptable performance in RIQAS Monthly Clinical Chemistry, Cycle 22, using the atomic absorption method, with a cycle average absolute SDI of 0.48, within the acceptable limit of <2.0.

Statistical analysis and age partitioning

All analyses were performed in R. Age in days was used as the primary unit throughout the analysis and was converted to months and years for descriptive purposes using 30.4375 days per month and 365.25 days per year.

Candidate age partitions for serum copper reference intervals were initially explored using recursive partitioning analysis (rpart), with age and sex as candidate predictors and serum copper concentration as the outcome. A minimum node size of 40 observations was required, and the tree was pruned by cross-validation to reduce overfitting. Candidate split points suggested by the model were subsequently rounded to clinically interpretable categories. Final age groups were selected by considering the recursive partitioning results together with between-group statistical comparisons, clinical interpretability, and sample size. The final rounded age groups were 0-<6 days, 6 days to <12 months, 12 months to <7 years, and 7 to <19 years.

Before reference interval estimation, records were excluded if age was missing or invalid, sex was unrecognized, copper concentration was missing or non-numeric or age fell outside the predefined age groups. Differences across age groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with Mann-Whitney U tests for comparisons between adjacent groups. Effect sizes were quantified using Cliff's delta and interpreted as negligible (<0.147), small (0.147 to <0.33), medium (0.33 to <0.474), or large (≥0.474). The Harris-Boyd method was also applied as an additional statistical assessment of partitioning. Partitioning decisions were guided by the overall consistency of evidence across significance testing, effect size, and Harris-Boyd evaluation rather than by any single criterion alone.

Evaluation of sex partitioning

Within each age group, the need for sex-specific partitioning was assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test, Cliff's delta, and the Harris-Boyd method. Sex partitioning was considered only when both male and female subgroups contained at least 40 individuals and when the combined statistical evidence suggested clinically relevant between-sex differences. In the analytic workflow, sex partitioning was recommended only when at least two lines of evidence supported separation. Otherwise, combined reference intervals were reported.

Outlier detection

Outliers were identified separately within each final age group using the Horn algorithm with Box-Cox transformation and Tukey fences (1.5 × IQR), applied iteratively until no further outliers were detected or a maximum number of iterations was reached. Observations identified as outliers were excluded before reference interval estimation. Outlier removal was performed once before final reference interval calculation to ensure consistency across analyses.

Reference interval estimation

Reference intervals were estimated using the nonparametric percentile method in accordance with CLSI EP28-A3c. The lower reference limit (LRL) and upper reference limit (URL) were defined as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, respectively. Ninety percent confidence intervals for the reference limits were obtained by bootstrap resampling with 2000 iterations.

Two-sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

Parents or legal guardians provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Vietnam National Children's Hospital IRB (IRB00011976/FWA00028418) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data-driven age partitioning and evaluation of adjacent groups

Recursive partitioning analysis using age and sex as candidate predictors identified several candidate split points in serum copper distributions, with the most prominent changes occurring during the early neonatal period, infancy and later childhood. After consideration of these candidate thresholds and rounding to clinically interpretable categories, the final age groups were defined as 0-<6 days, 6 days to <12 months, 12 months to <7 years and 7 to <19 years. All final groups met the predefined minimum sample size requirement. Serum copper concentrations differed significantly across the four age groups (Kruskal-Wallis p < 0.001). Comparisons between adjacent age groups showed statistically significant differences for all pairs (all Mann-Whitney p < 0.001), with moderate-to-large effect sizes (Cliff's delta 0.45-0.85; Table S2). These findings supported retention of separate age partitions.

Evaluation of sex partitioning

Within each age group, serum copper distributions were compared between females and males. Although nominal statistical significance was observed in the 0-<6 days and 7 to <19 years groups, effect sizes were small or negligible, and the Harris-Boyd criterion was not met in any age group. Overall, the combined statistical evidence did not support clinically meaningful sex-related differences within the defined age groups; therefore, sex-specific partitioning was not applied (Table S3).

The derived reference intervals demonstrated pronounced age-related variation. The neonatal group had the lowest median concentration and the lowest reference limits, whereas higher concentrations were observed during infancy and early childhood. In the oldest age group, both median concentrations and reference limits were lower than those in children aged 12 months to <7 years, consistent with the age-related pattern observed in the scatter plot.

Comparison with published pediatric studies

Comparison with selected published pediatric studies showed a broadly similar age-related pattern, characterized by low copper concentrations in early life, an increase during infancy and childhood, and lower concentrations in older children and adolescents. However, differences in absolute reference limits were observed across studies. Discussion

This study established age-specific reference intervals for serum copper in a healthy pediatric population using a data-driven partitioning approach integrated with clinical interpretability. Serum copper concentrations showed a clear age-dependent pattern, with the lowest values observed during the early neonatal period, followed by higher concentrations during infancy and early childhood, and relative stabilization thereafter. After statistical evaluation and clinical rounding, the final age groups were defined as 0-<6 days, 6 days to <12 months, 12 months to <7 years and 7 to <19 years.

The marked age dependence observed in this study is consistent with the known physiology of copper homeostasis. At birth, serum copper concentrations are relatively low because hepatic synthesis of ceruloplasmin is immature and fetal copper metabolism differs from that in postnatal life. During the first months after birth, circulating copper increases as hepatic ceruloplasmin production rises and dietary intake becomes the primary source of copper. Concentrations then tend to stabilize or gradually decline during later childhood and adolescence as growth and metabolic demands change [1,2,4].

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous pediatric studies. Lockitch et al. reported substantial developmental changes in serum trace elements during childhood and emphasized the importance of age-specific reference intervals for clinical interpretation [8]. Lin et al. similarly demonstrated significant age-related differences in serum copper concentrations, while finding no clinically relevant effect of sex [16]. More recently, Yim et al. established pediatric reference intervals for copper using ICP-MS and also confirmed clear age-related variation across childhood and adolescence [17]. Although the overall age-related pattern in our study was similar to these reports, some differences in absolute reference limits were observed. These differences may reflect variation in analytical methods, population characteristics, nutritional status and environmental exposure (Table S4) [8,[16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. Fig. 3 provides a visual comparison with selected studies for which interval ranges could be directly plotted, whereas Table S4 provides a broader descriptive summary of published pediatric copper RI studies.

An important feature of the present study is the use of recursive partitioning as an exploratory tool to identify candidate age thresholds objectively. In paediatric reference interval studies, limited age categories may not adequately reflect rapid physiological change, particularly during early life. Data-driven approaches such as decision-tree analysis may therefore help identify natural distributional breakpoints without relying solely on arbitrary age grouping [13,[21], [22], [23]]. At the same time, purely statistical cut-points are not always clinically meaningful. Recursive partitioning was therefore used as an exploratory rather than definitive method, and final decisions were based on combined statistical and clinical considerations, with rounded cut-points chosen to improve interpretability in practice. This approach provides a practical balance between statistical objectivity and clinical applicability.

The differences observed between adjacent age groups were not only statistically significant but also associated with moderate-to-large effect sizes, supporting retention of separate age partitions. This was particularly evident across the transition from the neonatal period to infancy and from early childhood to older children. These findings suggest that the use of excessively broad age categories may obscure meaningful developmental changes in serum copper concentrations, especially during periods of rapid physiological transition.

Although the Harris–Boyd criterion did not support formal partitioning between adjacent age groups, statistically significant differences and moderate-to-large effect sizes were consistently observed. This apparent discordance likely reflects methodological differences between approaches. Harris and Boyd (1990) emphasized that statistically significant differences between subgroup means do not necessarily justify separate reference intervals, particularly when subgroup distributions overlap substantially and partitioning results in only limited reduction in overall between-subject variability [24]. Subsequent methodological evaluations by Ichihara and Boyd (2010) and Lahti (2004) further demonstrated that different partitioning approaches may yield discordant conclusions depending on the relationship between subgroup separation and within-group variability [21,25]. In the present study, nonparametric comparisons and effect size measures such as Cliff's delta suggested substantial distributional shifts across developmental stages, particularly across the neonatal-to-infant transition and later childhood. These differences were also biologically plausible and visually evident in the scatter plot. Partitioning decisions were therefore based on the overall consistency of statistical, graphical, and clinical evidence rather than on the Harris–Boyd criterion alone.

Additional partitioning by sex was not supported in this population. Although nominal statistical significance was observed in some age groups, effect sizes were negligible to small and the Harris-Boyd criterion did not support sex-specific reference intervals. Taken together, these findings do not support clinically meaningful sex-related differences within the age groups studied. This is in agreement with previous pediatric studies suggesting that age is the dominant source of variation in serum copper concentrations, whereas sex-related effects are limited during childhood [16,17].

From a practical laboratory perspective, age-appropriate reference intervals are important for interpretation of pediatric copper measurements. Copper testing is used in the evaluation of disorders of copper metabolism, including Wilson disease and Menkes disease, and may also contribute to assessment of nutritional status. Because serum copper changes rapidly in early life, the use of overly broad or non-specific age groupings may obscure meaningful developmental differences and increase the risk of over- or under-flagging of results. Our findings therefore support the use of age-specific reference intervals, particularly in infancy and early childhood where physiological change is greatest.

This study has several methodological strengths. First, age partitions were explored using a data-driven approach rather than relying solely on predefined categories. Second, partitioning decisions were supported by multiple complementary statistical approaches, including significance testing, effect size assessment, and the Harris-Boyd method. Third, outlier detection was performed using the Horn algorithm, and reference intervals were estimated using nonparametric methods with bootstrap confidence intervals in accordance with CLSI EP28-A3c. Together, these features strengthen the reliability and reproducibility of the proposed reference intervals.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The study was conducted in a single population, and serum copper concentrations may vary across regions because of differences in diet, environmental exposure and analytical methodology. In addition, although strict inclusion criteria were applied, the presence of unrecognized subclinical conditions affecting copper metabolism cannot be completely excluded. Pre-analytical factors, including environmental contamination and trace-element handling, may also influence copper measurements. Validation in independent pediatric cohorts would therefore be valuable to assess the broader applicability of these reference intervals.

In conclusion, this study established age-specific reference intervals for serum copper in a healthy pediatric population using a data-driven partitioning strategy. The findings confirm a strong influence of age, but not sex, on serum copper concentrations across childhood. These results support the use of age-specific reference intervals to improve interpretation of pediatric copper measurements in routine clinical practice.
