Comprehensive network pharmacology study on Huangjing Wan in the treatment of ischaemic stroke
Authors: Chen Fan, Yuntian Bi, Jiman Yang, Zhe Luo, Jingxian Bai, Xiangjun Qiu, Hongri Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stroke is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, with ischemic strokes accounting for the majority of cases. Huangjing Wan (HJW) is a classical Chinese herbal formula used in the treatment of stroke, but the precise mechanisms of action remain unclear. We retrieved the active components of HJW, along with their related targets and the targets associated with middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO), by conducting a search across relevant databases. The common targets obtained from the intersection of the 2 target sets were used to establish both a compound-target interaction network and a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. A Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) bioinformatic analysis was ultimately performed to identify the potential signaling pathways associated with HJW in the treatment of MCAO. Through searching and screening a series of databases, we identified 129 active ingredient-associated targets of HJW and 1246 therapeutic targets for MCAO. The intersection of the 2 sets yielded 40 overlapping targets. Based on these 40 common targets, a compound-target interaction network and a PPI network were constructed. Finally, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the key signaling pathways potentially involved in HJW treatment of MCAO primarily include the Hepatitis B pathway, the lipid and atherosclerosis pathway, and the Apoptosis pathway. This study reveals that HJW primarily exerts its therapeutic effects on MCAO through the Hepatitis B virus pathway, the lipid and atherosclerosis pathway, and the apoptosis pathway, providing a theoretical basis for further in-depth research.

1. Introduction

Ischaemic stroke is a condition characterized by the blockage of blood vessels in the brain, leading to oxygen deprivation, necrosis, and impaired function of neurological tissue.[1] It is closely linked with high rates of disability and mortality.[2] Current research indicates that the mechanism by which ischemic stroke leads to neural damage primarily involves the inflammatory response, abnormal energy metabolism, and calcium overload following hypoxia and ischemia in neural tissue.[3] The classical treatment for ischemic stroke in the acute phase includes mechanical thrombectomy and intravenous thrombolysis.[4] The latest research on the treatment of ischemic stroke is predominantly focused on emergency reperfusion to salvage the ischemic penumbra and neuroprotective agents.[5] Currently used neuroprotective agents for post-stroke recovery have limitations in both efficacy and safety.[6] As a result, research into alternative and complementary therapies has become a growing focus.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), as a treasure trove with immense development potential, is gaining increasing attention due to its minimal side effects and high safety profile.[7] Huangjing Wan (HJW) is a classic prescription in TCM, commonly used for the treatment of ischemic stroke. It is recorded in the He Ji Ju Fang, formulated with Huangjing (Polygonatum cyrtonema), Danggui (Angelica sinensis), and Heidou (black beans), prepared with honey (all plant names were verified through MNPS (http://mpns.kew.org) on August 1, 2025). Current studies have confirmed that Danggui possesses anti-inflammatory and antiplatelet aggregation effects,[8,9] while Huangjing demonstrates neuroprotective and immunomodulatory properties.[10,11] However, there is currently a lack of research on the mechanism of HJW in treating ischemic stroke.

Network pharmacology is an emerging interdisciplinary research method used for in-depth analysis of subtle associations between complex diseases and treatment strategies. This has opened up new perspectives for pharmacological exploration using multilevel data mining, comprehensive analysis, and model construction. This innovative method was deeply integrated into a large experimental dataset, which not only meticulously depicted the intricate network graph between candidate targets, but also utilized computational prediction techniques to comprehensively and deeply evaluate the potential side-effect risks between the composite target pathways and phenotypes in 4 dimensions.[12] Patented Chinese drug formulas are usually composed of multiple herbal medicines and have sophisticated interactions with biological systems. Network pharmacology can be used to determine the active components and latent targets of Chinese patented drug compound ingredients, as well as their mechanisms of action, to screen candidate compatibility schemes with high clinical potential and safety.[13] This could effectively solve the multi-objective problem, pathways, and complex compatibility phenomena and significantly improve the diagnostic and therapeutic levels of TCM in network pharmacology applications in the field of TCM.[14]

This study will employ network pharmacology methods to predict the mechanism and key protein molecules involved in the treatment of ischemic stroke with HJW, providing a theoretical foundation for subsequent further research.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Preparation process, quality control (QC) data, and fingerprint spectrum of HJW

HJW was synthesized from Polygonatum sibiricum 250 g and Angelica sinensis 250 g. Polygonatum sibiricum was steamed with 100 g Huangjiu for 48 hours, removed, and dried at a low temperature. Next, added 25 g of Glycine max and boiled twice in water for 3 to 4 hours. The decoctions were then mixed and filtered. The filtrate was concentrated to the appropriate concentration. The concentrated solution was absorbed by Angelica sinensis and dried at a low temperature. Polygonatum sibiricum was crushed into a fine powder after being added, sieved, and mixed well. Then, 100 g of the powder was added to 60 to 70 g of refined honey to prepare large honey pills that yielded HJW. The Chromatographic fingerprint of HJW (high-performance liquid chromatography, HPLC) was shown in the Supplementary Material, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R702.

2.2. Exploration and screening of active ingredients in HJW and prediction of target effects

Active components of HJW and their associated targets were retrieved from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database (TCMSP, https://www.tcmsp-e.com/load_intro.php?id=43, accessed March 8, 2023) using the following screening criteria: oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30%, blood-brain barrier permeability (BBB) ≥ 0.3, and drug likeness (DL) ≥ 0.18. Chemical Abstracts Service (CAS) registry numbers and molecular formulae of HJW-related chemical constituents were systematically retrieved from 2 authoritative pharmacological databases: DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com, accessed March 9, 2023) and PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, accessed March 12, 2023). Gene symbols associated with therapeutic targets were systematically retrieved from the UniProt Knowledgebase (UniProtKB, https://www.uniprot.org, accessed March 14, 2023) under the refined search criteria of Human organisms restriction and inclusion of manually reviewed entries. After establishing the correspondence between active pharmaceutical components and gene symbols, we employed Cytoscape 3.7.1 to construct a compound-gene interaction network. The Network Analyzer tool in Cytoscape 3.7.1 was employed to calculate degree values, with nodes exhibiting higher degree values being identified as central nodes.

2.3. Target mining for middle cerebral artery occlusion

Potential therapeutic targets associated with “Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion (MCAO)” were identified through comprehensive database searches, including: Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, https://omim.org/, accessed March 14, 2023), DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org/, accessed March 19, 2023), GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/, accessed March 16, 2023), Therapeutic Target Database (TTD, http://db.idrblab.net/ttd/, accessed March 18, 2023).

2.4. Construction of “component target” network and “protein-protein interaction” network

The gene symbols of active ingredient targets and disease-related targets were imported into the Venny online tool (https://jvenn.toulouse.inrae.fr/app/index.html, accessed March 19, 2023) to generate a Venn diagram. The overlapping targets identified through this analysis were considered as potential therapeutic targets of HJW for the treatment of MCAO. We constructed the drug-target network of HJW against MCAO using Cytoscape 3.7.1. The topological parameter degree, which was calculated by the Network Analyzer tool in Cytoscape 3.7.1. The PPI network was constructed by importing gene symbols of key targets into the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) database (https://string-db.org/, accessed on March 19, 2023) under multiple selection parameters, with the species restricted to Human to obtain relevant protein interaction data. The resulting data were visualized as a PPI network using Cytoscape 3.7.1, followed by centrality analysis of network proteins through the CentiScaPe 2.2 plugin to generate the final PPI network diagram.

2.5. Construction of the “Target-Function-Pathway” tripartite network

The gene symbols of overlapping targets were imported into the Metascape database (https://metascape.org/, accessed March 20, 2023) with the species parameter set to Human for KEGG pathway enrichment analysis,[15] which identified significant signaling pathways and associated parameters related to HJW’s therapeutic effects against MCAO. The significantly enriched signaling pathways were visualized as advanced bubble plots using the Bioinformatics online platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn, accessed March 21, 2023), while the precise mechanistic roles of relevant targets within these pathways were determined through KEGG Mapper (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/mapper/, accessed on March 22, 2023).

2.6. Ethical approval

Neither informed consent nor approval of the ethics committee is required for this study type, because no human or animal subjects or materials were use.

A total of 129 potential targets associated with the 8 bioactive components were identified from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP) database. 3.2. PPI network analysis

The key targets of HJW in MCAO were analyzed using the STRING database. Through this analysis, 38 of 40 targets were found to have interrelationships. Based on the protein correspondence combined with the score, a PPI was constructed, resulting in an association network consisting of 38 nodes and 263 connecting lines (Fig. 4). In the graph, nodes represent proteins and edges represent associations between proteins. The higher the degree of association, the darker the color of the nodes and the greater the number of edges. 4. Discussion

The primary mechanism driving reperfusion injury in brain tissue is the interplay between oxidative stress and microcirculatory stress, encompassing post-reperfusion inflammation, apoptosis, and intracellular calcium overload. Additionally, diminished antioxidant levels in ischemic cells exacerbate local inflammation and generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to secondary damage.[16] Apoptosis is also an important mechanism of secondary brain tissue damage after cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury (CIRI). Therefore, the subsequent damage caused by CIRI is equally important for the recovery of neurological function in patients. In summary, this study has opened more pathways for the treatment of cerebral ischemia by exploring the mechanism of action of HJW in patients with ischemic stroke.

Currently, there is no quantitative analysis of the various compound constituents of HJW. Based on data retrieved from the Components Contents in Traditional Chinese Medicine (http://www.cctcm.org.cn) database and The Encyclopedia of Traditional Chinese Medicine (http://www.tcmip.cn/ETCM2/front/#/) database,[17,18] the content of several compounds in Polygonatum sibiricum and Angelica sinensis was found to be below 0.01%. Studies have shown that the ketone content in Polygonatum sibiricum ranges from approximately 3 to 5 mg/g, while saponins are present at about 50 mg/g.[19–22] In Angelica sinensis, the Stigmasterol content is reported to be 14.3 mg/g.[23] However, the levels of these compounds can vary considerably depending on the species, geographical origin, and even among individual plants of the same species and region. Studies on the excessive administration of Polygonati Rhizoma components in mice have confirmed that high-dose exposure induces marked behavioral alterations and chromosomal damage. Sub-acute dosing leads to time‑dependent biochemical disturbances as well as structural damage to the liver, kidney and spleen, but the treatment was non-lethal.[22] Data on the oral bioavailability of these compounds and their biological effect concentrations at targets vary considerably across different databases. In summary, the exploration of the mechanisms by which TCM treats diseases still requires more extensive and comprehensive research for elucidation.

The bubble plot constructed by KEGG analysis of network pharmacology results showed that the first and most important signaling pathways are the hepatitis B virus pathway. Although the hepatitis B pathway (hsa05161) was the main focus, it also played a role in the pathological process of cerebral ischemia that some signaling pathways and key molecules altered by HJW: NF-κB, a key transcription factor, plays a critical role in immune and inflammatory responses. Existing studies have demonstrated that both Liquiritigenin and Baicalein can suppress its expression, thereby alleviating inflammatory reactions.[24,25] NF-κB participated in the regulation of inflammatory response by regulating the expression of downstream molecules such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and ICAM-1.[26] In cerebral ischemia, inhibiting the activity of NF-κB could alleviate the inflammatory response to decrease brain damage. Baicalein and sitosterol activate the overexpression of AKT1, thereby enabling protein kinase B (AKT1) to precisely regulate the balance between cell survival and apoptosis through phosphorylation of downstream target molecules.[27–29] Existing studies confirm that diosgenin can influence AKT1, although its precise role remains unclear.[30] Similarly, multiple in silico studies have suggested that AKT1 may be a target of stigmasterol, while there is currently no experimental validation for this.[31,32] In cerebral ischemia, AKT1 activates downstream Bcl2, thereby inhibiting apoptosis through the mitochondrial pathway.[33,34] TP53 is a key tumor suppressor gene. Baicalein can inhibit the expression of TP53, thereby affecting the crucial role of its encoded protein, p53, in apoptosis and growth suppression.[35] In contrast, sitosterol and diosgenin have been shown to upregulate TP53 expression and promote cell apoptosis.[36,37] Existing studies also suggest that stigmasterol may target TP53, although the precise mechanism remains to be elucidated.[38] In cerebral ischemia, TP53 activation can induce cell apoptosis by regulating the Bax/Bcl2 balance; thus, inhibiting P53 may help reduce cell damage induced by cerebral ischemia.[39,40] Nevertheless, the biological effects arising from the simultaneous action of these compounds which regulate TP53 remain to be further investigated. FOS is an immediate early gene that encodes protein c-Fos that forms an AP-1 transcription factor complex with c-Jun. AP-1 plays a crucial role in the inflammatory response.[41] Existing studies reveal that baicalein, sitosterol, and diosgenin reduce the inflammatory response and minimize brain damage in cerebral ischemic stroke by inhibiting the transcription of JUN and FOS.[42–45] Liquiritigenin, stigmasterol, sitosterol, and diosgenin inhibit the infiltration of inflammatory cells by suppressing the expression of MMP9, which degrades the extracellular matrix.[46–49] In cerebral ischemia, inhibition of Matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9) activity can alleviate inflammation and blood-brain barrier damage.[50,51] Baicalein promotes apoptosis by activating CYCS, CASP8, CASP3 and BAX.[25,52,53] Stigmasterol has been shown to upregulate the BCL2/BAX ratio, thereby exerting a protective effect on cells.[38] Conversely, sitosterol and diosgenin promote apoptosis by reducing the BCL2/BAX ratio.[54,55] CASP8 activates CASP3 and induces apoptosis via the mitochondrial death receptor pathway.[56,57] However, the effect of the combined action of the aforementioned compounds on regulating cellular damage induced by cerebral ischemia requires further research. Baicalein, stigmasterol, diosgenin and sitosterol have all been shown to inhibit Transforming Growth Factor beta 1 (TGFB1), which is closely associated with enhancing inflammatory responses.[58–61] Research has revealed that sitosterol induces the overexpression of Protein kinase C alpha (PRKCA),[62] a reduction in the expression of which has been demonstrated to potentially impair synaptic plasticity and learning and memory functions.[63] Although existing *in silico* studies suggest a potential association between stigmasterol and PRKCA, further experimental validation is required.[64] In cerebral ischemia, PRKCA and TGFB1 could regulate various signaling pathways, such as NF-κB, MAPK, and apoptosis pathways.[65,66] Therefore, the modulation of these molecules by compounds in HJW may contribute to alleviating inflammation and cellular damage induced by cerebral ischemia.

In our network pharmacology research, HJW treatment to cerebral ischemia was directly related to the lipid and atherosclerosis pathway (hsa05417) and apoptosis pathway (hsa44210) (Fig. 5), and some key molecules in the above pathways has undergone changes, including RELA, AKT1, TP53, FOS, MMP9, CYCS, HSP90AA1, BCL2, BAX, CASP9, CASP3, CASP8, and PRKCA. In addition to the above factors, HSP90AA1 (heat shock protein 90 α) was a protective protein involved in maintaining protein stability and cell apoptosis inhibition. Cerebral ischemia can lead to increased HSP90AA1 expression, which reduces brain injury.[67]

The activation of RELA can induce MMP9 expression, leading to blood-brain barrier damage. The activation of RELA could also induce the release of inflammatory factors, further leading to cell damage and death; however, this effect could be offset by the activation of AKT1, as AKT1 can inhibit the activation of RELA and reduce inflammation and cell death. AKT1 activation regulates the ratio of BCL2 to BAX and inhibits apoptosis through the mitochondrial pathway. Additionally, AKT1 inhibits the activation of TP53 to reduce cell apoptosis. Activation of TP53 promotes the transfer of BAX to the mitochondria, leading to the release of CYCS, which in turn activates CASP9 and CASP3 for cell apoptosis. Furthermore, TP53 can increase the expression levels of FOS and promote apoptosis. The activation of FOS can induce the expression of MMP9, causing damage to the blood-brain barrier. FOS regulates the expression of BCL2 and BAX to affect cell apoptosis. Activation of MMP9 can disrupt the blood-brain barrier, release inflammatory factors, and further exacerbate the activation of RELA and FOS, forming a vicious cycle. The release of CYCS activates CASP9 and CASP3 during apoptosis. This process can be inhibited by BCL2, as BCL2 can stabilize the mitochondrial membrane and inhibit CYCS discharge. HSP90AA1 expression inhibits cell apoptosis by stabilizing BCL2, BAX, and CASP9 proteins to maintain mitochondrial function. The balance between BCL2 and BAX expression is crucial for apoptosis. BCL2 inhibits cell apoptosis, whereas BAX promotes it. Maintaining this balance can alleviate neuronal damage. CASP9, CASP3, and CASP8 played crucial roles in apoptosis. Their activities are regulated by mitochondrial pathways (such as the release of CYCS) and death receptor pathways (such as FAS). It also alleviates apoptosis by inhibiting the activity of these caspases. PRKCA activation could affect cell apoptosis. PRKCA regulates the expression of BCL2 and BAX, affecting apoptosis via the mitochondrial pathway. Furthermore, PRKCA could also promote the inflammatory response by activating the NF-κB (RELA) pathway.

These molecules and their signaling pathways play a crucial regulatory role in the pathogenesis of cerebral ischemia. Modulating these signaling pathways and molecules may mitigate inflammatory responses and cellular damage induced by cerebral ischemia.

This study employed emerging network pharmacology methodology to elucidate the potential mechanisms of HJW in treating cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury, while predicting key pathways and macromolecular proteins. Our findings provided new directions for the treatment of cerebral ischemia and offer a scientific basis for the clinical application of HJW in other diseases. However, network pharmacology research integrates data from multiple sources, including genomics, proteomics, and chemical informatics, which are not always reliable.[68] Moreover, the complexity of both the components and preparation processes of TCM preparations contributes to uncertainties in data sources, making it challenging to obtain adequate datasets for research.[69] Additionally, the inherent diversity of constituents inevitably leads to variations in the absorption and bioavailability of different compounds, which further compounds the inaccuracies in computer simulations.[70] However, the findings from network pharmacology typically require experimental validation for accuracy. Nevertheless, the complex composition of herbal medicines and their multi-target, multi-pathway mechanisms of action often make it challenging to design appropriate experiments to verify these results.[71] Whilst the Chinese Pharmacopoeia has established quality-standardized production protocols for numerous herbal formulations, however, many herbal preparations are still prepared based on the physician’s clinical experience.[72] This makes it difficult to ensure consistent quality and efficacy across all herbal medicines, thus consequently increasing the challenges associated with network pharmacology research.[73] Therefore, when employing network pharmacology to study TCM, it is advisable to utilize rigorously validated databases with comprehensive screening criteria and to prioritize formulations that have undergone standardized preparation, in order to enhance the accuracy of the research.

5. Conclusions

Using network pharmacology, 40 key targets of HJW against cerebral ischemia were identified. The results of network pharmacology indicate that HJW primarily treats cerebral ischemic stroke through pathways involving Hepatitis B virus, lipids and atherosclerosis, and apoptosis signaling. Key biomacromolecules involved in HJW’s therapy for cerebral ischemic stroke include RELA (NFκB), AKT1, TP53, FOS, among others.
