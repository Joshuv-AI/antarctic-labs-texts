Phage depolymerase modulates virulence and biofilm in hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae by CPS-mediated suppression of type 3 fimbriae
Authors: Zheng Fan, Yuchen Chen, Tongtong Fu, Zhoufei Li, Hongbo Liu, Bing Du, Xiaohu Cui, Lin Gan, Guanhua Xue, Yanling Feng, Hanqing Zhao, Jinghua Cui, Chao Yan, Junxia Feng, Ziying Xu, Zihui Yu, Yang Yang, Yuehua Ke, Jing Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phage-derived depolymerases represent a promising antibiotic alternative for treating Klebsiella pneumoniae infections. Depolymerases can increase the sensitivity of bacteria to the host immune system and complement-mediated killing by specifically degrading capsular polysaccharides (CPS). However, the impact of depolymerases on bacterial biofilm formation remains unclear. This study found that depolymerase treatment significantly enhances the biofilm formation capability of hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (hvKp) strains, which inherently exhibit relatively weak biofilm formation due to their thick capsular polysaccharide (CPS) layers. Further investigation revealed that depolymerase-mediated CPS degradation relieved its repression on the type 3 fimbriae gene cluster mrkABCDF, thereby promoting biofilm formation. In vivo experiments in mice also showed that CPS can inhibit virulence functions associated with type 3 fimbriae. Furthermore, we found that CPS-mediated biofilm inhibition appears to be a common phenomenon among hvKp strains. In summary, by elucidating the dual role of depolymerase in modulating both virulence and biofilm in hvKp, our work reveals a potential interaction between CPS and type 3 fimbriae, providing deeper insights into the pathogenicity of this clinically important bacterium.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a significant pathogenic bacterium, broadly classified into hypervirulent (hvKp) and classical (cKp) strains based on their virulence [1]. cKp typically infects immunocompromised patients, often exhibits multidrug resistance, and is a major cause of healthcare-associated infections. In contrast, hvKp can cause severe diseases such as liver abscess, pneumonia, and bacteremia even in otherwise healthy individuals, posing a serious threat to public health [2,3]. Capsular polysaccharide (CPS) is a key virulence factor of K. pneumoniae, which can enhance the bacteria's resistance to the host's innate immune defenses and promote immune escape [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Specifically, the negatively charged CPS is thought to repel negatively charged phagocytes [6]. Additionally, it physically masks bacterial cell wall structures and membrane-associated proteins, thereby preventing complement-mediated opsonophagocytosis [6,8,9]. In K. pneumoniae, at least 130 capsular types have been identified, with K1 and K2 types being the most common hvKp [3,10]. The overproduction of CPS in hvKp strains, which confers a characteristic hypermucoviscous phenotype, serves as a key indicator for distinguishing them from cKp [11].

Biofilms pose a significant threat to human health, especially when multidrug-resistant bacteria colonize and form biofilms on medical devices, such as urinary catheters, intravenous catheters, and artificial implants [12,13]. These biofilms are highly resistant to antibiotic treatment and are a major cause of recurrent infections in clinical settings [12,13]. Type 3 fimbriae, encoded by the mrkABCDF gene cluster, are filamentous appendages on the bacterial surface that play a critical role in biofilm formation and mediate adherence to host tissues and environmental surfaces [[14], [15], [16]]. Among them, MrkA, the major pilus subunit, forms the structural backbone of type 3 fimbriae and is essential for K. pneumoniae adhesion and colonization [14,16,17]. Although CPS and type 3 fimbriae are the two most important surface-associated virulence factors in K. pneumoniae, it remains unclear whether and how these structures interact to modulate bacterial virulence and biofilm formation.

In recent years, phage-derived depolymerases have been recognized as a promising antibiotic-alternative therapeutic strategy for treating K. pneumoniae infections [18]. They can specifically hydrolyze a given CPS type, facilitating the attachment of phage particles to bacterial outer membrane receptors [[19], [20], [21]]. To date, at least 25 CPS type-specific depolymerases have been identified and characterized in K. pneumoniae [10,22]. These enzymes have demonstrated promising antimicrobial activity, including enhanced susceptibility of bacteria to phagocytosis and increased sensitivity to complement-mediated killing [18]. However, the impact of depolymerases on biofilm formation in K. pneumoniae remains unclear. Some studies have found that CPS is an important component of K. pneumoniae biofilms [23], while others have suggested that CPS inhibits biofilm formation [24]. Investigating the regulatory role of depolymerases in biofilm formation can reveal the functional mechanism of CPS in this process and provide a theoretical basis for assessing the potential risks of depolymerases as an antibacterial strategy.

In this study, we identified a K2-type hvKp strain, A25, exhibiting relatively low biofilm formation capacity. And we identified a novel depolymerase, Dep11, which specifically degraded K2-type CPS. Treatment with Dep11 reduced the virulence of A25 while enhancing its biofilm formation. Further investigation revealed that CPS inhibits biofilm formation by downregulating the expression of the type 3 fimbriae operon mrkABCDF. Concurrently, CPS suppresses the virulence functions associated with type 3 fimbriae. Based on these findings, we propose that CPS acts as a central regulator to fine-tune the transition between a “high-virulence, low-biofilm” state and a “low-virulence, high-biofilm” state in hvKp by inhibiting type 3 fimbriae, and that depolymerase Dep11 treatment can artificially trigger this phenotypic transition.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

All K. pneumoniae strains used in this study were obtained from our laboratory and cultured in fresh Luria–Bertani (LB) broth (Beijing Land Bridge Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, catalog number: CM158). To obtain the gene deletion strains and complementation strains, methods by Link et al. and Pan et al. were used [25,26]. All strains, plasmids, and primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The experiments were performed between November 2024 and May 2025.

Pangenome analysis

Pangenome analysis was performed on the hvKp isolates from our laboratory collection. A presence–absence framework was used, with protein-coding genes clustered based on a minimum BLASTp identity of 95%. Core genes were defined as those present in at least 99% of isolates. Gene integrity and annotation for biofilm-associated loci, including mrkABCDF, treC, and luxS, were assessed within this framework.

Isolation and purification of phage phiA25

Phage phiA25 was isolated from untreated sewage at the Capital Institute of Pediatrics (Beijing, China) using the double-layer agar method [27]. Briefly, 20 mL of untreated sewage was centrifuged at 4400 rpm for 20 min. The phage suspension was collected by filtering the supernatant through a 0.22 μm microfiltration membrane (Pall Corporation, New York, NY, USA). After mixing 100 μL of phage suspension with 100 μL of K. pneumoniae A25 and incubating overnight, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation. Following ten-fold dilution, 100 μL of each dilution was mixed with 100 μL of logarithmic-phase K. pneumoniae A25 in sterile tubes. Subsequently, 4 mL of LB semi-solid medium was added, mixed, and poured onto LB solid medium. Following incubation at 37 °C for 16–18 h, individual phage plaques were incubated in 1 mL SM buffer and eluted with shaking at 37 °C for 4 h. This process was repeated until homogeneous plaques were achieved.

Plaque formation and quantification of phage phiA25

The diameters of the plaques and surrounding halos were measured using the ImageJ software (Version 1.54p) [28]. Each plaque represented one plaque-forming unit (PFU), and titers were calculated as PFU/mL. All measurements were performed in at least three independent replicates to ensure reliability.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Phage morphology was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) as previously described [29]. The phage samples were stained with a 2% phosphotungstic acid solution (pH 7.0) for 30 s, followed by blotting of excess stain from the grid edge, and then observed using TEM at 80 kV.

Bacteria samples for transmission electron microscopy were prepared following an established protocol [27]. Briefly, bacterial cultures were adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.5. Bacterial pellets were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in HEPES buffer, and incubated with ferritin at room temperature for 30 min. Following multiple rounds of centrifugation and washing to remove unbound ferritin, the samples were fixed by gently adding 2.5% glutaraldehyde along the tube wall for subsequent TEM observation.

Host range assay

A volume of 100 μL of the host bacterial suspension to be tested was added to sterile tubes, followed by the addition of 4 mL of LB semi-solid medium. The mixture was thoroughly mixed and poured onto the surface of LB solid medium. After the semi-solid medium had solidified, 10 μL of phage suspension was spotted onto the plate. Following overnight incubation at 37 °C, plaque morphology was examined and recorded. All assays were performed in triplicate to minimize experimental error.

Genome sequencing and analysis

Phage genomic DNA was extracted using the phenol-chloroform method [30]. Whole-genome sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Potential open reading frames (ORFs) were identified using PHASTER [31] and their putative protein products were functionally annotated via BLASTp against the NCBI non-redundant database. Virulence genes and antibiotic-resistance genes were predicted using the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) and Resfinder 4.0 [32], respectively. A genomic map was generated with Proksee. Comparative genomic analysis was conducted using Easyfig 2.2.5.

Expression and purification of putative depolymerases

The fragments of putative depolymerase were amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the following primers. The forward primer sequence was: GTGCCGCGCGGCAGCCATATGATAAAGAACGATTTTAACCAGCC and the reverse primer was CTCGAGTGCGGCCGCAAGCTTTTACAAGAAGTTTACGATAACGTC. The PCR products, flanked by homology arms, were assembled into the NdeI/HindIII-linearized pET-28a expression vector via homologous recombination. The successfully constructed expression vector was then transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 competent cells. Expression of the depolymerases was induced using 0.1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), followed by collection of bacterial precipitate and disruption through sonication. The depolymerase protein was purified using nickel-affinity chromatography. Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA Protein Quantification Kit (catalog no. E112; Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Protein size and purity were verified by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE).

Assessment of depolymerase activity

A total of 4 mL of LB semi-solid medium was added to 100 μL of bacterial cultures, mixed well, and then poured onto the surface of LB solid medium. Then, 5 μL of depolymerase protein (Dep11) at varying concentrations (400, 200, 100, 50, 25, and 10 ng) was applied to the surface of the double-layer agar plates. After incubating at 37 °C overnight, the depolymerization activity was assessed by observing the presence and size of clear or semi-clear spots. Heat-inactivated Dep11 was used as a negative control to confirm the specificity of the observed effects.

Determination of bacterial capsular polysaccharide

The content of uronic acid was quantified to evaluate CPS production as previously described [[33], [34], [35]]. Bacterial cultures were inoculated into the LB broth and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm until OD600 reached 1.0. Subsequently, 1 mL of the bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min. In the depolymerase treatment group, 100 μL of the depolymerase was added to the bacterial suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The bacterial cells were then washed once with 1 mL of sterile water and resuspended in 250 μL of sterile water. Subsequently, 50 μL of a 1% Zwittergent 100 mM citric acid solution was added. The mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 30 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min. The resulting supernatant was mixed with 1 mL of anhydrous ethanol and incubated on ice for 1 h. Finally, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of 100 mM hydrochloric acid. Subsequently, 100 μL of this suspension was mixed with 600 μL of sodium tetraborate sulfuric acid solution. The mixture was incubated at 100 °C for 15 min and then cooled to room temperature. Following this, 20 μL of a 0.125% (w/v) carbazole solution was added to the mixture, which was then incubated again at 100 °C for an additional 15 min. Finally, the content of uronic acid was quantitatively analyzed by measuring the OD520 value and comparing it to a standard curve. Three independent biological replicates, each assayed in triplicate, were included in every experiment. The curve was prepared using D-glucuronic acid (Solarbio, Cat. No. G8930). A high-concentration stock solution was prepared by dissolving the powder in deionized water, and then serially diluted to obtain a standard series covering a concentration range of 0–280 μg/mL.

The string test and low-speed centrifugation were used to assess bacterial capsule formation. In the string test, colonies on LB agar plates were gently picked up with a sterile inoculating loop. The formation of a viscous filament extending more than 5 mm from the colony indicated the overproduction of CPS. In the low-speed centrifugation test, after centrifugation at 1500 × g for 5 min, the sedimentation resistance of bacterial cells was quantified by measuring the OD600 value of the supernatant. The lower the OD600 value, the lower the sedimentation resistance and the lower the capsule content of the strain.

Growth curve and CFU counting assays

Overnight cultures of strain A25 were grown in LB broth at 37 °C with shaking. For growth curve experiments, cultures were diluted 1:100 into fresh LB broth and incubated at 37 °C. OD600 was measured every 2 h to monitor bacterial growth. For the Dep11 group, 1 μg of the depolymerase protein was added at the time of subculture. At the corresponding time points, samples from cultures with and without Dep11 were serially diluted and plated on LB agar to determine CFU counts.

Biofilm formation and quantification assays

Overnight bacterial cultures were diluted 100-fold in LB broth, and 1 mL of the diluted culture was dispensed into confocal dishes. The dishes were incubated statically at 37 °C for 12 h to allow biofilm formation. After incubation, the culture supernatant was removed, and the dishes were gently washed twice with PBS. The adherent biofilms were then fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 3 h. Following fixation, the dishes were gently washed twice with PBS. The biofilms were then stained with 50 ng/mL FITC-conjugated concanavalin A (ConA-FITC) for 20 min at room temperature in the dark, washed gently four times with PBS, followed by staining with 5 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, and finally washed gently four times with PBS. The stained biofilms were observed and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Biofilm formation was quantified using crystal violet staining [36]. Fresh bacterial cultures were diluted in LB broth to reach an OD600 of 0.025 and then dispensed into 96-well plates. The 96-well plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow for mature biofilm formation. The suspensions were then removed, and the wells were washed thrice with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For CFU-based quantification of adherent bacteria, 300 μL of PBS was added to each well, and the biofilm was detached by scraping the well surfaces with a pipette tip. The resulting suspension was serially diluted and plated onto LB agar plates for colony counting after overnight incubation at 37 °C. For crystal violet staining, after removal of the suspensions and washing thrice with PBS, 250 μL of 1% crystal violet solution was added, followed by incubation for 30 min. The staining solution was then removed, and the wells were washed thoroughly with 1× PBS. For quantitative analysis, the crystal violet bound to the adherent biofilm was solubilized by adding 250 μL of a decolorization solution (ethanol:glacial acetic acid:water, 4:1:5 v/v/v) to each well, followed by incubation at room temperature with gentle shaking for 30 min. Finally, the absorbance at 590 nm was measured to quantify biofilm formation. Three technical replicates and three independent biological replicates were involved in each experiment.

Screening and identification of capsule-deficient K. pneumoniae mutants

The screening for capsule-deficient mutants was conducted using a previously described method [27]. Briefly, a 100 μL aliquot of logarithmic-phase K. pneumoniae was mixed with 100 μL of a capsule-specific bacteriophage at a concentration of 109 PFU/mL. This mixture was then incorporated into 4 mL of LB semi-solid medium and poured onto the surface of LB solid medium. Following solidification, the bacterial cultures were incubated at 37 °C for 12–18 h. The growth of single colonies on the top agar layer in the absence of lysis plaques was used to identify putative capsule-deficient mutants under phage pressure. After three rounds of purification, the stable resistance of the isolates was confirmed using a standard double-layer agar assay.

Genomic DNA was extracted from the purified mutants and whole genome sequencing was conducted on an Illumina platform. Sequencing reads were aligned to the reference genome using BWA (v0.7.17) (Parameters: mem -t 4 -k 32 -M) [37]. The duplicate reads were removed using SAMTOOLS (Parameter: rmdup). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were detected and filtered by a minimum read depth of 4 and a minimum mapping quality of 20 using SAMTOOLS (Parameters: -C 50 -mpileup -m 2 -F 0.002 -d 1000) [38]. Small insertions and deletions (InDels) shorter than 50 bp were detected using SAMTOOLS (Parameters: mpileup -m 2 -F 0.002 -d 1000). Copy number variations (CNVs) were identified by CNVnator (Parameter: -call 100). Structural variations (SVs) were detected using BreakDancer (v1.4.4) with a minimum confidence score of 80, based on the alignment of paired-end reads to the reference genome and the actual insert size; SVs supported by fewer than two paired-end reads were discarded. All detected variants were functionally annotated using ANNOVAR 2015Dec14.

RNA sequencing and differential expression analysis

RNA sequencing was conducted in collaboration with Novogene Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Bacterial strains were grown overnight in LB medium, then subcultured to an OD600 of 1.0 in fresh LB medium and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm prior to RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted from bacterial strains using the TRIzol reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Three independent biological replicates were prepared for each experimental group. To eliminate genomic DNA contamination, the samples were treated with recombinant DNase I. Subsequently, rRNA was removed using the Ribo-Zero Magnetic Kit (Epicentre, Madison, WI, USA) to enrich the prokaryotic mRNA. All samples were processed simultaneously in a single batch to minimize technical variation. Enriched mRNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB #7530; New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The resulting cDNA libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (Novogene Co., Ltd.). After sequencing, raw reads underwent quality control, which included the removal of low-quality reads and adapter sequences, as well as checks for sequencing error rate and GC content distribution. This process yielded high-quality clean reads, with an average of 7.8 million reads per sample, a Q30 score >94.8%, and an error rate <0.02%. Clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome of bacterial strains using Bowtie2 (version 2.3.4.3) with default parameters allowing up to 2 mismatches [39]. Gene-level read counts were generated from the alignments using HTseq (version 0.9.1).

For differential gene expression analysis, DESeq2 package (version 1.20.0) was used to compare RNA-seq data between experimental groups [40]. The results were visualized using volcano plots to highlight significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on fold changes and statistical significance thresholds (|log2FC| > 0.5 and p-value <0.05). To analyze co-expression patterns, a heatmap was generated from row-wise normalized expression values of the DEGs, with hierarchical clustering applied to genes and samples separately. In the heatmap, red color indicates high expression and blue color indicates low expression for a given gene, and this color representation is only comparable horizontally across samples for each individual gene. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were performed using ClusterProfiler (version 3.8.1).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Bacterial cultures were grown overnight in LB medium and subcultured to an OD600 of 1.0 in fresh LB medium at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm before harvesting. The bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm, and the resulting bacterial pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of TRIzol reagent. Following the addition of 0.2 mL of trichloromethane, the mixture was vortexed for 15 s and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The aqueous phase was collected carefully and mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). Approximately 1 μg of RNA was utilized for reverse transcription, and cDNA synthesis was conducted using PrimeScript Reverse Transcriptase (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed in a 20 μL reaction volume containing SYBR Premix Ex TaqTM II (TaKaRa, Dalian, China), cDNA template, and gene-specific primers, using the CFX Connect Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, USA). The rpoB gene, which encodes the DNA-directed RNA polymerase subunit β, served as an internal reference.

Serum bactericidal assay

Fresh serum was collected from healthy mice for use in bactericidal assays. Overnight cultures of K. pneumoniae (A25, A25 pretreated with 1 μg of depolymerase Dep11 for 1 h at 37 °C, A25ΔwcaJ, and A25ΔwcaJ/wcaJ) were diluted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL in 1× PBS. A 20 μL aliquot of the bacterial suspension was mixed with 180 μL of serum and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Following incubation, the samples were subjected to a 100-fold dilution, plated, and then incubated overnight. The colonies were then counted. Experiment was performed in three biological replicates for each of three technical replicates.

Bacterial virulence assessment in a murine intraperitoneal infection model

Male C57BL/6J mice aged 6 weeks (Vital River, Beijing, China) were used to develop the experimental model. All animal experiments in this study were performed between January 2025 and April 2025, with approval from the Capital Institute of Pediatrics Animal Care and Use Committee of the Ethics of Animal Experiments (Permission No. DWLL2024022), and in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. Euthanasia was performed by inhalation of isoflurane (3–5% in oxygen) using an induction chamber until complete loss of the righting reflex, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death, in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020).

A total of 192 animals were used to establish an intraperitoneal infection model. A total of 132 animals were used for survival observation, and 60 animals were used for pathological and cytokine examinations. The animals were randomly divided into experimental groups and housed under standard conditions with free access to food and water. (1) For the first survival study, 36 mice were divided into six groups (n = 6). The control group received 1× PBS, whereas the experimental groups received an intraperitoneal injection of A25 (2 × 101 CFU or 1 × 102 CFU) or A33 (2 × 101 CFU, 1 × 102 CFU, or 2.5 × 108 CFU). (2) For the second survival study, 96 mice were divided into sixteen groups (n = 6): control (PBS), A25 (1 × 102 CFU or 5 × 102 CFU), ΔmrkA (1 × 102 CFU or 5 × 102 CFU), A25ΔwcaJ (1 × 102 CFU or 5 × 107 CFU or 1 × 108 CFU), A25ΔwcaJΔmrkA (5 × 107 CFU or 1 × 108 CFU), A25 Dep11+ (1 × 102 CFU, pretreated with depolymerase Dep11 for 1 h at 37 °C), A25ΔwcaJ/wcaJ (1 × 102 CFU or 5 × 107 CFU or 1 × 108 CFU), and ΔmrkA/mrkA (1 × 102 CFU or 5 × 102 CFU). (3) For pathological examination, 60 mice were divided into ten groups (n = 6): control (PBS), A25 (1 × 102 CFU), ΔmrkA (1 × 102 CFU), A25ΔwcaJ (1 × 102 CFU or 5 × 107 CFU), A25ΔwcaJΔmrkA (5 × 107 CFU), A25 Dep11+ (1 × 102 CFU, pretreated with depolymerase Dep11 for 1 h at 37 °C), A25ΔwcaJ/wcaJ (1 × 102 CFU or 5 × 107 CFU), and ΔmrkA/mrkA (1 × 102 CFU). Purified Dep11 was used at a concentration of approximately 500 ng/μL, and 2 μL of this solution (equivalent to 1 μg of protein) was administered to pretreat the bacterial suspensions. Survival was monitored for seven days in the survival studies. In the pathological examination groups, mice were euthanized at 24 h after infection (based on mortality observed in survival studies) to facilitate liver histopathological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and to conduct serum cytokine quantification (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using Student's t-test (for two-group comparisons) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test (for multi-group comparisons). For qRT-PCR analyses involving multiple target genes, statistical significance was first assessed by two-way ANOVA, and P values were subsequently adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. For survival curve comparisons, the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test was used to evaluate the statistical significance between groups, and Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons, with detailed results presented in Supplementary Table S2. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Differences were considered statistically significant at P value < 0.05 (∗), 0.01 (∗∗), 0.001 (∗∗∗), or 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗).

Results

Isolation of A25, a K2-type hypervirulent K. pneumoniae with high capsular polysaccharide production and weak biofilm formation

K. pneumoniae A25, isolated from a patient with a liver abscess, was characterized as the K2-type (capsular serotype) and ST65 (molecular type). Compared to the K30-type classical K. pneumoniae (cKp) A33, strain A25 not only exhibited a thicker capsular polysaccharide but also demonstrated higher viscosity, as evidenced by a positive string test and enhanced sedimentation resistance (Fig. 1A–C). Strain A25 had a genome of 5,394,489 bp and carried a typical hypervirulent plasmid of 236,308 bp, which harbored multiple virulence genes, including mucoid regulators (rmpACD and rmpA2), iron acquisition systems (iucABCD and iroBCDN), and the metabolite transporter peg-344 (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. 1). As expected, strain A25 demonstrated high virulence in an intraperitoneal injection mouse model, with 50% lethal dose(LD50) of less than 102 CFU, whereas the LD50 of strain A33 was close to 108 CFU(Fig. 1E). These findings strongly suggest that A25 is a hypervirulent K. pneumoniae strain (hvKp). Interestingly, despite harboring most of the reported biofilm-associated genes, such as the mrkABCDF operon, treC, and luxS [23,41], strain A25 exhibited a surprisingly weak biofilm-forming capacity (Fig. 1F). Dep11, a new depolymerase capable of specifically degrading the K2-type capsular polysaccharide of K. pneumoniae

To investigate the effect of the depolymerase on the virulence and biofilm formation of strain A25, we attempted to isolate K2 capsular serotype-specific phages, as such phages often encode capsule-specific depolymerases. Luckily, a novel phage, designated phiA25, was isolated from sewage obtained from the Capital Institute of Pediatrics Hospital, using K. pneumoniae A25 as the host. Phage phiA25 formed large and translucent plaques with a diameter of approximately 0.1 cm, surrounded by 0.1-cm enlarged halos (Fig. 2A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that phiA25 is a typical short-tailed phage with an icosahedral head of approximately 62 nm in diameter and a short tail of approximately 15 nm, which was weakly discernible (Fig. 2B). Next-generation sequencing analysis showed that phiA25 exhibited a circular double-stranded DNA genome of 41,030 bp, with 53.11% G + C content and 48 coding sequences (CDS) regions(Fig. 2C). Subsequently, 41 K. pneumoniae strains belonging to 11 different capsular types were used to identify the host range of phiA25. Spot tests revealed that phage phiA25 formed lytic spots on K2-type (A25, A27, and A28) and K30-type (A33 and A34) K. pneumoniae strains but did not form lytic spots on other K-type K. pneumoniae strains, such as K1-type (A1), K54-type (A37), and KL136-type (A40) (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S3). Comparative genomic analysis showed that phiA25 exhibited a genetic structure similar to that of phages K5-2 (NC_047798.1) and K5-4 (NC_047799.1) (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, orf10 of phage phiA25 exhibited 97.22% sequence identity with the K30-type depolymerase encoded by orf37 in phage K5-2, indicating that the protein encoded by orf10 is likely a K30-type depolymerase. Notably, the majority of unmatched genome regions among phiA25, K5-2, and K5-4 function as a “depolymerase-box”. In this region, phage K5-2 encoded K5-type and K30-type depolymerases, whereas phage K5-4 encoded K5-type and K8-type depolymerases. Similarly, the “depolymerase-box” of phiA25 comprised proteins ORF10 (probable K30-type depolymerase) and ORF11 (function unknown) (Fig. 2D). Therefore, we hypothesized that orf11 of phiA25 encoded a novel K2-type depolymerase (Dep11).

The CPS-deficient mutant A25ΔwcaJ was constructed by mutating the capsule synthesis gene wcaJ, thereby serving as a negative control. Dep11 formed clear lytic halos on the wild-type K2 strain A25. This phenomenon was not observed when A25 was treated with heat-inactivated Dep11. Furthermore, no halos were detected on the A25ΔwcaJ mutant or on other K-type strains (Fig. 2E). Both Dep11-treated A25 and the A25ΔwcaJ mutant exhibited almost identical, significant changes in cell morphology and viscosity; both strains completely lost their capsule structure, showed significantly reduced CPS content, and drastically decreased cell viscosity (Fig. 2F–H). But CPS production and all related phenotypes were restored after complementation with wcaJ in A25ΔwcaJ. The results collectively demonstrate that Dep11 is a highly specific and potent K2 capsular depolymerase capable of effectively degrading the bacterial surface capsule.

Depolymerase Dep11 reduces virulence of strain A25

To explore the impact of the depolymerase Dep11 on the virulence of A25, we employed a murine intraperitoneal infection model to compare the virulence of wild-type A25, Dep11-treated A25, and the CPS-deficient mutant A25ΔwcaJ. Growth curve and bacterial counting experiments showed that Dep11 did not affect bacterial growth or viability under the conditions used in this study (Supplementary Fig. 3A and 3B). Survival curve analysis demonstrated that both Dep11 treatment and the A25ΔwcaJ mutant significantly attenuated the virulence of strain A25 (Fig. 3A). Histopathological examination of liver tissues revealed stark contrasts among the groups. Liver tissues from mice infected with wild-type A25 displayed severe pathological changes, including prominent white lesions, indicative of liver abscesses. These lesions were characterized by extensive hepatocyte necrosis featuring nuclear condensation or lysis surrounded by infiltrating inflammatory cells, primarily lymphocytes (Fig. 3B). Additionally, traces of fibrous protein deposition were evident in the portal vein adjacent to necrotic regions (Fig. 3B). In contrast, mice infected with either Dep11-treated A25 or the A25ΔwcaJ mutant showed no signs of liver abscesses. Their liver tissues exhibited preserved hepatocyte integrity and normal histological structure, closely resembling those of the uninfected control mice (Fig. 3B). Consistent with the degree of pathological injury, serum levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were markedly elevated in wild-type A25-infected mice. Both Dep11-treated A25 and A25ΔwcaJ infection groups showed significantly lower cytokine levels, which, although slightly above those of the uninfected controls, indicated a substantially attenuated systemic inflammatory response (Fig. 3C). The attenuation of virulence in strain A25 after Dep11 treatment could be linked to the enzymatic degradation of its CPS, as the CPS is critical for its ability to evade immune-mediated clearance. Depolymerase Dep11 enhances biofilm formation of strain A25

Then, we investigated the impact of the depolymerase Dep11 on the biofilm formation of A25. Compared with the weak biofilm formed by the wild-type A25 strain, the Dep11-treated A25 strain exhibited significantly enhanced biofilm formation. Previous studies have demonstrated that CPS can inhibit bacterial biofilm formation [42,43]. Correspondingly, we found that biofilm formation was significantly enhanced in the A25ΔwcaJ mutant compared to the wild-type strain, and complementation of the wcaJ gene restored biofilm formation to wild-type levels, indicating that Dep11 promotes biofilm formation by degrading CPS (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Fig. 3C and 3D).

Type 3 fimbriae play a crucial role in biofilm formation, with MrkA serving as the primary structural subunit [14,16]. When the mrkA gene was deleted, strain A25 lost its ability to form biofilms, and CPS deficiency failed to enhance biofilm formation in the ΔmrkA strain (Fig. 4C). Subsequently, we expressed either mrkA alone or the mrkABCDF operon in the wild-type A25 strain. Forced expression of mrkABCDF led to a marked increase in biofilm formation in A25, whereas expression of mrkA alone did not significantly alter biofilm levels (Fig. 4D). These results demonstrate that assembling functional fimbriae requires the complete mrkABCDF operon, and that its upregulation can partially overcome CPS-mediated repression.

Since CPS profoundly alters mrk expression and fimbrial function, we next examined the global transcriptomic impact of mrkA deletion in different CPS backgrounds. In the capsule-intact wild-type A25 strain, deletion of mrkA had a relatively minor effect on gene expression, with 87 upregulated and 75 downregulated genes (Supplementary Fig. 5A), consistent with the inherently low expression of the mrkABCDF operon under CPS-mediated repression. In contrast, in the CPS-deficient background, mrkA deletion led to substantial transcriptomic changes, with 689 upregulated and 618 downregulated genes (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Fig. 5B). GO enrichment analysis of these DEGs highlighted distinct functional shifts, revealing that upregulated genes were significantly enriched in DNA-templated transcription and nucleic acid binding, while downregulated genes were primarily associated with organonitrogen compound biosynthesis, ribosome function, and nucleotide binding activity (Supplementary Fig. 5C and 5D). KEGG pathway analysis revealed a metabolic reprogramming pattern of functional differentiation. Upregulated pathways were primarily enriched in central energy and biosynthetic metabolism, such as the citric acid cycle (TCA cycle), carbon metabolism, and the biosynthesis of cofactors. Downregulated pathways were significantly concentrated in translational mechanisms and energy production systems, mainly involving ribosomes, oxidative phosphorylation, and the biosynthesis of O-antigen nucleotide sugar (Supplementary Fig. 5E and 5F). These transcriptomic and pathway analyses suggest that in the absence of both CPS and MrkA, cells may no longer prioritize resources for rapid growth or the construction of complex surface structures, but instead shift to a survival- and adaptation-oriented metabolic strategy.

In summary, the above findings strongly indicate that CPS suppresses biofilm formation in strain A25 by inhibiting the expression of the type 3 fimbriae synthesis gene cluster, the mrkABCDF operon, with transcriptional repression rather than purely physical shielding playing a dominant role in this process.

Given the high virulence observed in the capsule-intact background, we examined whether capsule-deficient strains could be tested at similarly low doses. LD50 analysis of A25ΔwcaJ showed 0% mortality at inocula around 1 × 102 CFU (Supplementary Fig. 6B), indicating that a substantially higher inoculum is required to bring the capsule-deficient strain into a measurable virulence range. Consistent with this, the capsule-deficient mutants, even at a dose 500,000 times higher (5 × 107 versus 1 × 102 CFU), demonstrated a significantly reduced level of virulence. The mortality rate of A25ΔwcaJ was 66.7% (33.3% at 36 h, 50% at 48 h, and 66.7% at 60 h), while the mortality rate of A25ΔwcaJΔmrkA was 0% at all time points (Fig. 5B). Capsule gene complementation (A25ΔwcaJ/wcaJ) restored virulence, resulting in 100% mortality within 36 h at the same 5 × 107 CFU dose (Fig. 5B). This was also observed at a high dose of 1 × 108 CFU; both the capsular complemented strain A25ΔwcaJ/wcaJ (24 h) and the single-capsular mutant A25ΔwcaJ (36 h) exhibited 100% lethality. In contrast, the double mutant A25ΔwcaJΔmrkA showed only a 16.7% mortality rate at 36 h, with a final mortality rate of 33.3% (Supplementary Fig. 6C).

Consistent with the survival curve results, histopathological evaluation showed that the wild-type, ΔmrkA, and ΔmrkA/mrkA (1 × 102 CFU) strains all induced severe liver abscesses, accompanied by necrotic cores, inflammatory cell infiltration, and elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) (Fig. 5C and D). While the A25ΔwcaJ strain at 5 × 107 CFU could still induce liver abscess formation, the A25ΔwcaJΔmrkA strain failed to induce abscesses at all, with cytokine levels consistent with the uninfected control group. Importantly, complementation with wcaJ in the strain A25ΔwcaJ/wcaJ restored both virulence and inflammatory cytokine levels (Fig. 5C and E). These results indicate that in wild-type strains with intact CPS, the absence of type 3 fimbriae has almost no effect on virulence, while in the absence of CPS, the virulence function of type 3 fimbriae is significantly enhanced. This suggests that CPS may have an inhibitory effect on the virulence of type 3 fimbriae.

Subsequently, based on the principle that CPS loss is a key mechanism in bacterial resistance to capsule-specific bacteriophages, we established a rapid screening method to identify CPS-deficient strains (Supplementary Fig. 7D). By selecting phage-resistant mutants from the weak biofilm-forming hvKp strains, we obtained CPS-deficient mutants. Compared to their parental strains, which exhibited a hypermucoviscous phenotype, these mutants tested negative in the string test, confirming CPS loss (Supplementary Fig. 7E). Whole-genome sequencing identified that all resistant mutations were localized within the wcaJ gene. The mutation spectrum comprised insertions, deletions, and point mutations, distributed across various regions of the gene sequence (Supplementary Fig. 8A and 8B). As expected, CPS deficiency significantly enhanced the biofilm-forming capacity of these hvKp strains (Fig. 6B and C). Furthermore, strains treated with specific bacteriophage depolymerases exhibited increased biofilm formation (Fig. 6B and C), whereas depolymerases specific to other K types showed no such promoting effect (Supplementary Fig. 9A). These results clearly demonstrate the widespread inhibitory effect of CPS on biofilm formation among hvKp strains.

Discussion

Hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp) is a highly dangerous human pathogen with a high mortality rate that can cause serious illnesses, such as liver abscesses and meningitis [47]. In hospital settings, bacteria often form biofilms on medical devices (e.g., ventilators, urinary catheters), making them resistant to antibiotic treatment and leading to recurrent infections and clinical treatment failure [48]. In this study, we found that hvKp exhibits a relatively weaker biofilm-forming capacity compared to classical K. pneumoniae (cKp), which is associated with its thicker capsular polysaccharide (CPS) layer. Physical degradation of CPS using phage-derived depolymerases or deletion of CPS synthesis genes (e.g., wcaJ) both enhanced hvKp biofilm formation, indicating that CPS inhibits hvKp biofilm formation. Further investigation confirmed that CPS inhibits biofilm formation by suppressing the expression of the mrkABCDF operon, which encodes type 3 fimbriae—a known key contributor to biofilm formation. In addition to their role in biofilm formation, type 3 fimbriae are significant virulence factors located on bacterial surfaces. We found that the absence of type 3 fimbriae did not affect the virulence of hvKp strains when CPS was present. But the absence of type 3 fimbriae significantly reduced bacterial virulence when CPS was absent. These findings demonstrate the inhibitory effect of CPS on the virulence-related functions of type 3 fimbriae and reveal the complex regulatory interactions among virulence factors in hvKp strains. It should be noted, however, that while our in vitro experiments confirmed that CPS deficiency enhances biofilm formation in hvKp, we were unable to validate the detrimental impact of CPS-deficient hvKp in vivo. This limitation is attributed to the lack of suitable animal models and effective methods for detecting biofilms in vivo.

This study found that the expression of type 3 fimbriae synthesis genes was upregulated after CPS deletion, but the specific mechanism by which CPS regulates these genes remains unclear. Supplementing with exogenous CPS in vitro did not alter the biofilm formation ability or the expression level of the mrkABCDF operon in CPS-deficient strains, suggesting that CPS may not directly inhibit type 3 fimbriae (Supplementary Fig. 8A and 8B), and this may depend on the structural presence of CPS itself. Based on our results, we think that CPS may act as a physical shield, blocking surface structures crucial for biofilm formation, including but not limited to type 1 and type 3 fimbriae. However, our observation that forced expression of the mrkABCDF operon can partially rescue biofilm formation in the wild-type strain demonstrates that this is not merely a physical effect. Instead, transcriptional downregulation is a fundamental mechanism by which CPS limits biofilm formation. To explain this transcriptional repression, we hypothesize that the intact capsule acts primarily as a sensory barrier, insulating the cell surface from external cues. The absence of CPS makes the cell surface more susceptible to external stimuli (such as surface contact, shear forces, or changes in nutrient availability), which activates regulatory networks associated with cell adhesion and biofilm formation [49]. The surface mechanosensing mechanism of bacteria can convert physical contact signals into biochemical signals which can activate the mrk operon [14,50]. Furthermore, the expression of mrkABCDF is finely controlled by multiple factors, including the c-di-GMP-dependent MrkH/MrkI regulators and intracellular c-di-GMP levels [51]. Studies have shown that high c-di-GMP levels can significantly enhance the expression of the mrk gene cluster and promote biofilm formation [14]. CPS synthesis is closely related to the cellular metabolic state, and its deficiency may lead to the remodeling of carbon flux allocation, energy metabolism, or signaling molecule levels, which indirectly affects the c-di-GMP signaling pathway, ultimately promoting the expression of fimbriae-related genes [52]. In summary, CPS inhibits biofilm formation primarily by repressing mrk transcription. The exact mechanisms driving this regulation remain to be determined.

Meanwhile, we propose that CPS in hvKp acts as a molecular switch governing the transition between the planktonic and biofilm states. In the presence of CPS, hvKp strains can maintain high virulence by evading host immune clearance. Conversely, CPS deficiency significantly reduces bacterial virulence while markedly enhancing biofilm formation. A clinical study found that compared to earlier-stage isolates, later-stage hvKp isolates from patients with chronic urinary tract infections exhibited reduced capsule expression, decreased hypermucoviscosity, enhanced biofilm formation, and significantly reduced virulence [53]. Clinical settings present multiple selective pressures that can drive a switch from capsule production to fimbriae-mediated attachment and biofilm formation [54]. Under phage selection pressure, bacteria acquire phage tolerance by reducing or losing capsule expression [43,55,56]. The screening method used in this study to isolate capsule-deficient mutants is a direct application of this principle. Antibiotic stress and the acquisition of resistance plasmids may also promote the shift from a high-capsule phenotype to a low-capsule phenotype [57]. Carrying resistance plasmids is often accompanied by metabolic burden and remodeling of cell surface structures, which in some cases can suppress the expression of genes related to capsule synthesis that can lead to a weakened mucus phenotype [58]. In environments with long-term antibiotic exposure, this shift may make hvKp more inclined to rely on fimbriae and biofilms for long-term colonization. Nevertheless, although CPS-deficient bacteria exhibit reduced virulence and are more easily cleared by the host's immune defense system, once formed, biofilm may encapsulate and protect both CPS-deficient and wild-type hvKp strains, constructing a robust fortress conducive to long-term bacterial survival. Therefore, type 3 fimbriae could serve as a key therapeutic target against biofilm-mediated infections associated with CPS-deficient strains.

In recent years, bacteriophages and their derivatives (such as depolymerases) have been considered promising alternatives to antibiotics for treating bacterial infections [18]. Most of the reported phages capable of killing K. pneumoniae are specific to CPS. These phages typically encode CPS-specific depolymerases, which utilize CPS as a receptor for infection and consequently exhibit specificity in targeting K. pneumoniae strains with particular CPS types [59,60]. At least 25 CPS-specific depolymerases have been identified in K. pneumoniae, including those targeting K1, K2, K5, K8, K20, K27, K47, and K56 [22]. These depolymerases have been shown to reduce virulence and degrade mature biofilms. In this study, we identified a novel depolymerase, Dep11, that specifically degrades the CPS of K2-type K. pneumoniae. Consistent with previous reports, Dep11 effectively reduced bacterial virulence by degrading CPS. However, we also found that Dep11 enhanced the biofilm-forming capacity of low biofilm-forming hvKp strains, challenging the conventional view that depolymerases primarily inhibit or degrade mature biofilms. These findings highlight the complex role of depolymerases in biofilm formation and underscore the need for further investigation into the intricate interactions between phages and bacteria.

In conclusion, by investigating the effects of bacteriophage depolymerases on bacterial virulence and biofilm formation, we explored the interplay between two key virulence factors in hvKp strains: CPS and type 3 fimbriae. These findings reveal the intricate regulatory mechanisms governing the different virulence factors in hvKp and provide deeper insights into the complex interactions between phages and bacteria.
