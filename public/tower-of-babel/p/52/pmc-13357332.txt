Artificial intelligence-based models for colorectal cancer diagnosis using laboratory tests: an exploratory retrospective case-control study
Authors: Jian-Mei Lin, Hai-Yan Huang, Hui-Liu Tan, Shu-Wei Ren, Xue-Ming Lu, Jun Liu, Hui Li, Hui-Zhen Jia, Yan-Hong Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is frequently diagnosed at advanced stages owing to the insidious nature of its early clinical manifestations and the lack of validated screening modalities. This diagnostic delay presents a substantial therapeutic challenge, underscoring the need to develop population-based screening strategies. Based on laboratory data, this study constructed a diagnostic model for CRC to explore a screening strategy for population-based tumor census.MethodsIn this retrospective case-control study, we analyzed anonymized laboratory parameters and baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with histologically confirmed CRC and age-matched healthy controls undergoing routine health checkups at our tertiary care center between January 2021 and December 2022. Non-parametric comparisons between groups were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of candidate biomarkers. Following variable selection via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), the dataset was partitioned into training and internal validation sets via stratified randomization at a 7:3 ratio. Multiple machine learning algorithms were evaluated, and the optimal predictive model for this study was selected based on this comparative assessment. Subsequently, the influence of individual features on the model predictions was analyzed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values.ResultsAfter rigorous screening, this study included 1,068 patients with histologically confirmed CRC and 1,068 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Comparative analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test revealed statistically significant intergroup differences (P<0.05) in age and serum biomarkers. ROC curve analysis identified four predictors with superior diagnostic performances for CRC: total protein (TP), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), age, and apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1). LASSO regression selected nine clinically relevant predictors, and various machine learning models were used to classify the data samples. Based on a comprehensive judgment, the Random Forest model [area under the curve (AUC) =0.946, Brier score =0.101] was the best model for the diagnosis of CRC in this study.ConclusionsAn effective risk stratification model for CRC screening was developed using routine clinical laboratory parameters and the readily available demographic variable of age. However, being derived from a single-center retrospective cohort, the model requires further validation in multi-center, prospective studies to confirm its generalizability and mitigate potential overfitting.

Introduction

According to the latest Global Cancer Statistics released by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), colorectal cancer (CRC) constitutes a major global health challenge. In 2022, an estimated 1.9 million new CRC cases were diagnosed worldwide, representing 10.2% of all malignant neoplasms (1), with approximately 904,000 CRC-related deaths accounting for 9.3% of total cancer mortality (2). These epidemiological data establish CRC as the third most prevalent malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-specific mortality globally (3). National surveillance data from the National Cancer Center of China (NCC) further indicate that CRC holds the second position in cancer incidence and ranks fourth in cancer mortality in the Chinese population (4). These findings underscore the substantial public health burden imposed by CRC both in China and worldwide (5,6).

The insidious onset of early-stage CRC, characterized by non-specific clinical manifestations coupled with suboptimal population-based screening protocols (7), contributes to delayed diagnosis in over 60% of cases at locally advanced or metastatic stages (8). This diagnostic latency significantly compromises therapeutic efficacy, resulting in 5-year survival rates below 15% for stage IV disease versus 90% for localized lesions (9). Current diagnostic paradigms predominantly rely on structural imaging modalities, computed tomography/magnetic resonance imaging (CT/MRI) and histopathological confirmation via invasive biopsy procedures. However, these approaches have inherent limitations, including procedural complexity, substantial healthcare costs, and radiation exposure risks, rendering them unsuitable for large-scale early detection initiatives (10). Regarding serological biomarkers, conventional indicators such as carcinoma embryonic antigen (CEA) demonstrate suboptimal performance characteristics in primary screening contexts. Pooled meta-analytic data revealed that CEA exhibited modest diagnostic accuracy, with a sensitivity of 52.8% and specificity of 59.6% for CRC detection (11), failing to meet the World Health Organization (WHO)-recommended thresholds of sensitivity and specificity of >75% for population-level screening applicability (12).

To overcome the limitations of current screening methods, researchers are actively exploring a range of emerging, non-invasive or minimally invasive diagnostic strategies. These strategies aim to capture the biological characteristics of tumors from different dimensions. For example, metabolomics, through the systematic analysis of all small-molecule metabolites in the body, can most directly reflect an individual’s phenotypic state, providing a new avenue for the early diagnosis of CRC and the discovery of biomarkers. Volatolomics, on the other hand, focuses on analyzing volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in biological samples (such as exhaled breath and urine). These VOCs are products of cellular metabolic activity, and their spectral changes are closely related to disease states, offering great potential for achieving non-invasive and convenient cancer diagnosis (13,14). Furthermore, biophysical technologies based on photoacoustic spectroscopy (15), Raman spectroscopy (16), high-frequency endoscopic ultrasound (17), and optical coherence tomography (18) (OCT) can provide high-resolution microstructural or molecular information from the optical, acoustic, or electromagnetic properties of tissues, enabling real-time, in-situ characterization of lesions. The integration of these technologies with artificial intelligence (especially machine learning) is giving rise to intelligent diagnostic models with high diagnostic performance (19). The machine learning model developed in this study, based on routine laboratory parameters, is an important component of this broad diagnostic ecosystem. By utilizing easily accessible and cost-effective serum markers in clinical practice, it provides a practical tool for large-scale population screening and can complement the aforementioned emerging technologies, jointly constructing a multi-level, precision-oriented early detection network for CRC.

The primary objective of this study was to develop a machine learning-based screening model for CRC by integrating routine clinical parameters with laboratory tests, including traditional tumor markers, inflammatory factors, and serum lipidomic profiles. The proposed model demonstrates the potential to enable cost-effective population-level screening and is applicable to asymptomatic individuals in the general population. These findings support its use as a standardized risk stratification tool for CRC screening. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0535/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

The case group comprised 1,068 patients from The Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University diagnosed with CRC aged 21–96 years [mean ± standard deviation (SD): 59±12 years) who were admitted between January 2021 and December 2022. As controls, 1,068 healthy individuals aged 18–96 years (mean: 44±13 years) undergoing routine medical examinations during the same period were recruited. Pathological confirmation of CRC diagnoses was obtained from the institutional pathology records. Demographic and clinical data, including sex, age, clinical diagnoses, and laboratory parameters, were systematically extracted from the Hospital Information System (HIS) and Laboratory Information Management System (LIS). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The use of this data was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (No. 2024ZSLYEC-216), and the study obtained the exemption for obtaining informed consent.

Case group inclusion criteria

All patients met the diagnostic criteria for CRC specified in the Chinese Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Colorectal Cancer 2023 edition with complete clinical documentation. To establish disease associations, we exclusively enrolled treatment-naïve patients during their initial clinical encounters. The systematic exclusion criteria were: (I) prior oncological interventions, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapies; (II) concurrent malignancies; and (III) metastatic tumors of unknown primary origin.

Control group inclusion criteria

(I) Individuals undergoing routine health evaluations at our institution during the study period; (II) absence of documented CRC or related gastrointestinal pathologies confirmed via a 12-month clinical follow-up; (III) standardized laboratory protocols, including analytical platforms and biomarker detection parameters, were identical to those used in the case group.

Data preprocessing

To evaluate the diagnostic validity of CRC biomarkers and mitigate confounding effects from incomplete datasets and demographic disparities, cases with missing data exceeding the predefined thresholds were excluded. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to address baseline age and sex imbalances between cohorts, yielding matched groups of 1,068 CRC patients and 1,068 healthy controls (Figure 1). Eleven serum biomarkers were analyzed: lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], total cholesterol (CH), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), serum total protein (TP), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 125 (CA125), CA15-3, CA19-9, and white blood cell count (WBC).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R v4.2.3 and Python v3.11.4. Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between the CRC and control groups using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. LASSO regression was employed to automatically eliminate collinear and redundant features. The selected variables were used as input features for four machine learning algorithms: Random Forest, XGBoost, Logistic Regression (LR) and Support Vector Machine (SVM). The best-performing model was selected based on a comprehensive evaluation of area under the curve (AUC), calibration curve, and Brier score.

To further validate the key influencing factors of CRC in this study and to enhance the interpretability of the classification model, this study introduces the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method for feature analysis on the CRC dataset.

Feature screening

The rationale for using LASSO was its ability to handle high-dimensional data and multicollinearity by shrinking regression coefficients to zero for non-informative features, thereby improving model generalizability. A binomial LASSO regression model was constructed for this binary classification task, and the optimal penalty coefficient λ = 0.001 was determined according to the minimum cross-validation standard error. Following dimension reduction, nine variables were retained as independent predictors: age, Lp(a), ApoA1, TP, AFP, CEA, CA125, CA15-3, and CA19-9 (Figure 2).

LASSO regression coefficient path and cross-validation error plot. A total of 13 candidate features were included in the analysis, labeled 1 to 13: sex, AGE, Lp(a), total CH, ApoA1, ApoB, TP, AFP, CEA, CA12-5, CA15-3, CA19-9, and WBC. The final model retained the following variables: AGE + Lp(a) + ApoA1 + TP + AFP + CEA + CA125 + CA153 + CA199. LASSO, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator.

Discussion

CRC demonstrates a significant global disease burden, with epidemiological surveillance data from 2020 documenting 1.93 million incident cases and 930,000 mortality events worldwide (20). Both incidence and mortality rates exhibit persistent annual increases. Prognostic outcomes remained stage-dependent, as evidenced by the 5-year survival rates of 93.2% for stage I, 82.5% for stage II, 59.5% for stage III, and 8.1% for stage IV (21). This stage-specific survival gradient underscores the importance of early detection strategies to reduce mortality. The current screening paradigms predominantly employ fecal immunochemical testing (FIT) and colonoscopy. However, FIT implementation faces diagnostic accuracy limitations, particularly high false-positive rates (20–30%) in population-based screening (22), while the invasive nature and resource intensiveness of colonoscopy constrain its utility for mass screening programs.

Serum-derived laboratory indicators exhibit characteristics conducive to large-scale implementation, including high reproducibility, analytical stability, and reliability. These minimally invasive biomarkers represent a cost-effective screening modality for CRC. Recent research advancements in blood-based markers have demonstrated their promising diagnostic potential. Li et al. (23) established a CRC screening model utilizing serum tumor markers (CEA, CA19-9, and CA125) and achieved an AUC value of 0.774 through immunoassay quantification. However, the diagnostic accuracy of such models is constrained by the limited spectrum of incorporated biomarkers. The systematic integration of novel molecular signatures into existing screening frameworks represents a critical pathway for enhancing early detection rates and facilitating population-wide CRC screening.

The SHAP analysis identified ApoA1, Lp(a) and TP as among the most influential features in our model. Their association with CRC can be interpreted through interconnected pathophysiological lenses (24). Chronic inflammation, obesity, metabolic syndrome, and hyperlipidemia are well-established independent risk factors for CRC (25-27).

The pro-tumorigenic effects of inflammation are predominantly mediated through tumor microenvironment remodeling, with myeloid cell activation and differentiation emerging as central pathological mechanisms (28). Specifically, chronic inflammatory states facilitate CRC initiation and progression via sustained recruitment of tumor-associated macrophages (29) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (30). Clinically, multiple cohort studies have consistently demonstrated elevated WBC counts in CRC patients compared to healthy controls (P<0.01), whereas no statistically significant differences in C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were observed across study populations (P>0.05) (31,32). Concurrently, reduced serum TP often signifies a state of chronic inflammation, chronic malnutrition and cachexia, commonly observed in advanced cancers, reflecting the metabolic toll of the disease (33).

Disorders of lipid metabolism are associated with an elevated risk of tumorigenesis, contributing to accelerated cancer cell proliferation and metastatic lesion formation (6,34). These metabolic dysregulations primarily drive tumor initiation and progression through mechanisms involving heightened oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, both of which are established contributors to CRC pathogenesis (31). Accumulating evidence demonstrates that hypercholesterolemia exacerbates CRC mortality risk by inducing hypoxic signaling in tumor cells via oxidative stress-dependent pathways (35).

Mechanistically, ApoA5 enhances CRC cell survival through PPARγ signaling pathway activation, thereby suppressing reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation (36). A retrospective cohort study conducted at Shijiazhuang Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital further identified Lp(a) as a positive prognostic factor for CRC incidence (37). The underlying mechanism may involve Lp(a)-mediated facilitation of fibrin network formation and thrombogenesis, which collectively promotes cancer cell adhesion, invasion, and metastatic dissemination (38).

Clinically, serum ApoA1 levels are potential non-invasive biomarkers for predicting progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in CRC patients (39). Our model identified ApoA1 as a consistently important protective feature. This finding aligns with established cancer biology. ApoA1, the major protein component of high-density lipoprotein (HDL), is involved in reverse cholesterol transport and has demonstrated anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-tumorigenic properties in the colonic microenvironment (40). Its reduced levels in our CRC cohort may reflect a systemic dysregulation of lipid metabolism favoring a pro-carcinogenic state, consistent with prior research linking hypolipidemia, particularly low HDL-C and ApoA1, to increased CRC risk and progression (39). Thus, the model’s reliance on ApoA1 is not merely statistical but is underpinned by a plausible mechanistic link to CRC pathogenesis.

The biomarkers selected for this study were based on a comprehensive literature review and were validated for clinical applications in medical practice. Our analysis revealed significant differences in the age distribution and serum levels of ApoA1, total CH, Lp(a), TP, AFP, CEA, CA125, CA15-3, and CA19-9 between patients with CRC and healthy controls (P<0.05). Notably, no statistically significant difference was observed in serum ApoB levels. These findings are consistent with previous research (41). The biomarkers integrated into our model reflect key pathophysiological processes underpinning CRC. Elevated CEA aligns with its established role as a tumor-associated glycoprotein. More notably, the inclusion of lipid metabolism components [ApoA1, Lp(a)] and inflammatory markers (TP) is mechanistically grounded. Dysregulated lipid metabolism is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of cancer, contributing to membrane synthesis, energy production, and signaling in proliferating tumor cells. Concurrently, chronic inflammation fosters a tumor-promoting microenvironment. Thus, our multi-parameter panel captures not only direct tumor signals but also the permissive metabolic and inflammatory milieu associated with colorectal carcinogenesis.

Conclusions

Artificial intelligence-based predictive models utilizing laboratory biomarkers have shown significant potential for disease detection, particularly in early-stage tumor screening. In this investigation, the biomarker panel incorporated not only conventional tumor biomarkers, but also inflammatory markers and lipoprotein profiles. Compared to standalone tumor marker panels, the multiparameter diagnostic model demonstrates superior discriminatory capacity for CRC diagnosis. Notably, the predictive model developed using the Random Forest algorithms achieved AUC values that exceeded 95%. Importantly, all biomarkers included in the panel represent routine clinical laboratory tests characterized by high accessibility, cost-effectiveness, and excellent patient compliance rates of >95%. These advantages make this model an ideal and promising risk stratification model for CRC detection.

In conclusion, we developed a machine learning model using routine clinical and laboratory data that shows promising performance for CRC risk stratification in our internal cohort. Its generalizability and true population-level applicability require confirmation through external, multi-center validation. Future integration with emerging modalities like volatolomics could pave the way for robust, multi-layered screening strategies.

This study demonstrates that a machine learning model leveraging readily available demographic and routine serum biomarkers—including parameters of lipid metabolism, systemic inflammation, and nutritional status—can achieve promising diagnostic accuracy for CRC in a single-center retrospective cohort. The primary implication of this work is the demonstration of a feasible and cost-effective approach that warrants further large-scale evaluation. To translate this potential into clinical utility, critical next steps must include:

Firstly, the high performance (AUC >0.95) achieved on our internal validation set must be interpreted with caution, as it may indicate overfitting to the specific characteristics of our monocentric dataset. A monocentric design inherently limits population diversity and compromises external validity, thereby constraining the geographical generalizability of the diagnostic model. Therefore, external validation on independent, multi-center cohorts is imperative to assess the model’s true generalizability.
