Stereotactic radiosurgery for post-herpetic trigeminal neuralgia: results of a case-control study
Authors: Tzu-Chiang Peng, Chun-Fu Lin, Cheng-Chia Lee, Kang-Du Liou, Huai-Che Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesRadiosurgery is a minimally invasive therapy for intractable classical trigeminal neuralgia (TN) with favorable outcomes. However, a little of studies have reported about the effects of stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) on post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN). Herein, we report our institutional experience and aim to compare the outcomes of classical TN versus PHN after SRS.MethodsA case-control study of four patients with PHN and 16 control patients with TN who underwent SRS was performed to evaluate the probability of pain relief, pain recurrence, complications, and medication-free status.ResultsThree of four patients with PHN exhibited significant pain relief and discontinued drug use after radiosurgery. The latent period between SRS and perceived pain improvement ranged from 0.3 to 1 month. Two patients in PHN groups sustained minor recurrent pain along the previously affected location at 2 and 2.5 years after treatment. Compared with patients with TN, those with PHN were less likely to respond favorably to SRS, exhibiting a lower initial pain relief rate (75% vs. 94%), a higher pain recurrence rate (50% vs. 19%), and a higher incidence of post-treatment numbness (75% vs. 38%). However, due to the relatively small sample size in each group, the study was underpowered to detect statistically significant differences.ConclusionsOur preliminary results demonstrated that three of four patients achieved pain palliation following radiosurgery for PHN, with a median latency period of one month. Radiosurgery may be a feasible and safe therapeutic option for these clinically challenging cases of PHN.Keywords: Post herpetic neuralgia, Stereotactic radiosurgery, Gamma knife, Outcomes

Introduction

Post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN) is a condition in which neuropathic pain develops along the distribution of preceding zoster eruption 90 days after the healed eruption [1]. PHN is characterized by burning, persistent, and disabling pain over the trigeminal nerve territory, which significantly affects patients’ quality of life. Approximately every three patients develop herpes zoster virus infection, and about 15% of people who had shingles will experience PHN during their lifetime [2]. Several studies have shown that the risk factors of PHN include advancing age, ophthalmic involvement, prodromal neuropathic pain, and severe zoster rash [3]. The management of PHN primarily focused on pain control while decreasing treatment-related adverse effects. The first-line treatment was the use of medications such as gamma-aminobutyric acid analogs and opioids [3]. However, in some cases, intractable pain was resistant to systemic therapy, and some patients developed drug-related side effects including tremor, somnolence, and rash, leading to long-term therapy.

In 1951, Swedish neurosurgeon Lars Leksell first treated a patient with trigeminal neuralgia (TN) using a prototype device lining to a dental radiography machine [4]. Thereafter, the concept of stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) promoted the revolution of functional surgery. Over time, technical refinement has aimed to improve safety and efficacy. A growing body of literature has shown that SRS is a viable treatment paradigm for TN [5, 6]. Nevertheless, there was a lack of robust scientific evidence validating its application for PHN. Treatment outcomes, durability, and complications had challenged physicians to place radiosurgery in the context of other therapeutic modality. Herein, we report our institutional longitudinal experience in the use of SRS for PHN management to extrapolate the role of radiosurgery in such a serious disease.

Methods

Patient population

This study recruited patients who underwent SRS for intractable PHN at a single academic center between January 2008 and December 2024. The cohort included three female patients and one male patient with a median age of 72.2 (range: 68–76) years. The pain location was as follows: ophthalmic area (V1, n = 3) and hemi-facial area (V1, V2, and V3, n = 1). To compare the effect of SRS on PHN and TN, individual matching of patients in the two groups was performed. The matching paradigm was based on five baseline variables, which included age, sex, comorbidities, interval from pain onset to treatment, and patient-derived pain score. A 1:4 matching ratio was selected to maximize the study power while maintaining two highly homogenous groups.

Definition of outcome measure

The outcome assessment included time to initial pain relief, degree of pain relief, pain recurrence, and post-treatment facial numbness. The measurement of pain degree was scored using the Barrow Neurological Institute (BNI) pain intensity scale (Table 1). Facial numbness was classified using the BNI facial numbness scale (Table 2) [7–9]. Significant pain relief was defined as a BNI pain intensity scale score of I–IIIa. Minor recurrence was defined as a change from a lower BNI pain intensity scale score to a higher score. Major recurrence was defined as the need for one open, percutaneous, or repeated radiosurgical procedure.

Follow-up evaluation

The patients were discharged on the day of the SRS procedure or the day after. Then, they were scheduled to attend clinical visits 1 month after the date of discharge and undergo outpatient surveillance at an interval of 3 months. The examination included facial sensory test and corneal reflex, jaw motility, and pain assessment. The treating physician continued the previous medication for at least 1 month after radiosurgery. The drug dose was tapered gradually if the patient’s pain improved during each clinical evaluation. Patients who did not require medications were instructed to return only if pain recurrence occurred or other untoward conditions developed.

Radiosurgical technique

After stereotactic frame placement, the patients underwent thin-slice magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with and without intravenous contrast enhancement. Standard cerebral MRI sequences were obtained for all patients. The pre-contrast sequences included high-resolution T2-weighted or fast imaging using steady-state acquisition images (with a slice thickness of 1.2 mm, no gap). A 4-mm isocenter was positioned at the root entry zone of the trigeminal nerve. In all patients, the maximal dose was 80–90 Gy.

Results

Characteristics and outcomes of the PHN group

The clinicodemographic profile of the PHN group is presented below. In all patients, the trigeminal V1 nerve was affected. The mean interval from herpes infection to the date of SRS was 69.5 (range: 36–120) months. Two patients preferred SRS due to excruciating pain and two others due to intolerable side effects related to the treatment drug (ataxia, n = 1; dizziness, n = 1). Before treatment, two patients were assigned with a score of IV, one with a score of IIIa, and one with a score of II. The median interval from radiosurgery to pain relief was 1 month (range: 0.3–4 months). During the follow-up period (range: 2.7–13 years), three patients had alleviation of pain and achieved significant pain relief (BNI pain intensity scale score of ≤IIIa). One patient (case 4) reported no significant pain improvement after SRS. Two patients (cases 1 and 2) presented with minor PHN recurrence along the previously affected location at 2 and 2.5 years after SRS. In these patients, the perceived pain sensation was occasional and subtle. Hence, drug treatment was not necessary. Characteristics and outcomes of the TN group

Table 4 shows the basic information of the TN group. The cohort comprised 12 women and 4 men with a mean age of 71 years. The time interval from the development of TN to SRS was 70.1 (range: 24–148) months. In total, 13 patients presented with intractable pain (BNI pain intensity scale score of >3a) before treatment. Three patients received SRS owing to unfavorable medical side effects (rash, n = 1; tremor, n = 1; and dizziness, n = 1). In 16 patients, the median time to pain relief was 1 month (range: 0.5–4.7 months). Fifteen (93.8%) patients achieved significant pain relief. Three patients presented with neuropathic pain recurrence at a median interval of 1.1 years after radiosurgery. At the end of clinical surveillance, 10 patients were free of pain (BNI pain intensity scale score of I, n = 10).

Toxicity

Three patients in the PHN group had post-treatment facial numbness. Among them, two presented with a BNI numbness scale score of II and one with a numbness scale score of III. Six patients in the TN group developed non-bothersome facial numbness after SRS (BNI numbness scale score of II, n = 6). One patient with PHN developed keratitis over the affected side, and two patients in the TN group had mild conjunctivitis probably caused by dry eye syndrome. None of the patients had anesthesia dolorosa, dysgeusia, masseter weakness, or hearing impairment.

Discussion

PHN is one of the most debilitating pain conditions, causing severe pain that markedly impairs functional status and quality of life. It primarily affects aging populations and may persist for years [10]. Pharmacologic safety and compliance are an issue [11]. With respect to patients who do not respond to or cannot tolerate medications, the use of alternative treatments is still under investigation. In contrast, several treatment options are available for TN. Among them, radiosurgery is generally considered a second-line treatment after microvascular decompression, particularly for patients with significant medical comorbidities who may not be suitable candidates for surgery [6, 7]. The initial response rate of TN after SRS ranges from 50% to 100%, with a latent period of approximately 1 month [8, 12]. Although the pain relief efficacy decreased with time, the 10-year pain relief rate was still maintained in 30%–68% of patients [5, 6, 13]. However, there was minimal understanding of the outcomes of PHN treated with radiosurgery. In 2000, Urgošík was the first to show the outcome of SRS-treated PHN [14]. In his study of 16 patients, he placed a single 4-mm isocenter at the cisternal portion of the affected trigeminal nerve with a maximal dose of 70–80 Gy. Results showed that pain significantly improved in 56% of cases. He suggested SRS could be an alternative therapeutic option for patients with PHN. This observation is also supported by the findings of Keep et al. [15] In their study, three patients with medically refractory PHN were treated using a two-target approach, with radiation delivered simultaneously to both the trigeminal nerve and the centromedian nucleus of the thalamus. Two of the three patients experienced significant pain relief. Despite these promising results, no other studies have proceeded to explore the role of SRS in PHN. With the development of technical refinement and technological evolution, re-evaluation of the efficacy and safety of SRS in PHN was required.

In our series, four patients with PHN were treated with radiosurgery due to medically intractable pain (n = 2) or the intolerable side effects of drug treatment (n = 2), which was a situation that physicians commonly encounter when treating these patients. In PHN, pharmacological treatments have been reported to achieve initial pain-relief rates of 63%–91% [16]. Nevertheless, treatment-related adverse events, including somnolence, tremor, rash, and hyponatremia, may adversely affect medication adherence and consequently diminish long-term treatment efficacy [11]. In our cohort, patients were treated using a single-isocenter technique, delivering a maximum dose of 80–90 Gy to the trigeminal nerve root entry zone. Favorable outcomes were observed in three-quarters of the patients (n = 3), all of whom achieved satisfactory pain control following treatment. These patients were able to discontinue medication and experienced only occasional, non-bothersome neuralgic pain. Although favorable outcomes were observed in our series, the results should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size, which precluded robust statistical analysis. Nevertheless, our explorative experience lends support to the hypothesis that SRS may be a feasible treatment alternative for selected patients with these clinically challenging conditions.

The mechanism underlying the therapeutic effect of SRS is believed to involve neuromodulatory effects on small-diameter nerve fibers, resulting in a reduction of abnormal neural impulse transmission [17, 18]. This theory was supported by previous literature revealing that post-SRS dysesthesia is correlated with patient outcomes [12, 19, 20]. Patients who developed post-treatment sensory disturbance were more likely to obtain and maintain excellent outcomes. Some studies have revealed that numbness could be a trade-off for pain relief and that patients should receive counseling before treatment [6, 20, 21]. In line with the query, in our patient population, two patients in the PHN group and six in the TN group developed non-bothersome facial numbness, which did not affect their quality of life. Only one patient in the PHN group had somewhat bothersome facial dysesthesia after radiosurgery. Although post-treatment facial numbness was a common adverse effect, it was generally well tolerated by most patients in our cohort.

The concrete pathophysiology of PHN is debated. However, it is widely accepted that the development of neuropathic pain is primarily attributed to inflammatory damage to both peripheral and central nerve fibers after herpes virus infection [22]. This is supported by neuroimaging findings such as trigeminal nerve atrophy and pontine demyelinating lesions on MRI [23]. Multiple sclerosis-related TN is another clinical entity that might share a similar mechanism with pain development. It accounts for approximately 10% of all TN cases, and its clinical symptoms can be similar to those of PHN with electric shock-like paroxysmal pain [24]. The pathogenesis of multiple sclerosis-related TN is associated with brainstem demyelinating plaques, which can affect the trigeminal pathways and eventually contribute to neuronal hyperexcitability [25]. For this type of condition, expert opinion suggested that pharmacological therapy, such as voltage-gated sodium channel blockers, could be the first-line treatment [24]. Surgical interventions including peripheral lesioning procedures, Gasserian ganglion interventions, and SRS are reserved for medically refractory cases [26–28]. Although each therapeutic approach has its own advantages and limitations, these treatment modalities collectively suggest that pain transmission can be interrupted at multiple levels along the trigeminal pathway. Inspired by this analogous entity with partially shared pathophysiology, the optimal treatment strategy for medically refractory PHN remains to be established and warrants further investigation.

Limitation

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, owing to its retrospective design, the study was inherently susceptible to selection bias and confounding factors, including variations in practitioner experience, clinical judgment, and treatment decision-making. In addition, the reliability of the collected data depended largely on the completeness and accuracy of the medical records, which may have introduced information bias. Second, although most patients underwent routine clinical follow-up, a standardized follow-up protocol was not prospectively implemented. Consequently, variations in the timing and frequency of outcome assessment may have affected the consistency and comparability of the clinical data. Third, the relatively small sample size limited the statistical power of the study and precluded definitive conclusions regarding differences between groups. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Future studies with larger sample sizes, prospective multicenter designs, standardized treatment and follow-up protocols, and clearly defined patient selection criteria are warranted to validate our findings and improve their generalizability.

Conclusions

The role of radiosurgery in the application of PHN remains unclear and controversial. Based on our front-line experience, the current study revealed that SRS could be viable alternative when several lines of treatment had been exhausted.
