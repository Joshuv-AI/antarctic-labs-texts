Von Willebrand, factor VIII, and D-dimer levels as predictors of risk and clinical outcomes in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Hanaa Ali EL-Sayed, Doaa H. Sakr, Mohamed Awad Ebrahim, Reham Alghandour, Eman Eid, May Denewer, Balvinder Saarsalu, Maha Othman, Hanan Azzam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coagulopathies, infection, and CNS infiltration are common complications during induction therapy of acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Early identification of adverse-risk patients may improve outcomes. This study evaluated the predictive value of coagulation markers, von Willebrand Factor antigen (vWF-ag), von Willebrand Factor–ristocetin cofactor (vWF-RCof), antihemophilic factor (FVIII), and D-dimer on risk stratification, severity and outcomes of AML patients. Fifty AML patients treated at Oncology Center Mansoura University hospital, from February 2023 to February 2024 were recruited and stratified into three risk groups favourable, intermediate, and adverse risk groups respectively according to the 2022 European leukemianet (ELN) risk stratification for AML. vWF-ag, vWF: RCof, FVIII, and D-dimer were measured at diagnosis and at remission. Adverse-risk group had the highest median levels of vWF-ag, vWF-RCof, FVIII and D-dimer at, both diagnosis and remission (p < 0.05). All markers significantly declined after remission (p < 0.05). vWF-ag, vWF-RCof, and D-dimer differed significantly among risk groups at both diagnosis and remission. ROC analysis showed that in the adverse-risk group, D-dimer had an AUC of 0.813 (cutoff > 1.6), vWF: Ag 0.780 (> 295), and vWF: RCo 0.761 (> 223). FVIII showed lower predictive value (AUC 0.625). Infection was the most frequent complication, followed by bleeding and thrombosis. Infection correlated with higher vWF: Ag after remission; thrombosis correlated with elevated vWF: Ag at diagnosis and remission and bleeding with lower vWF-ag. CNS infiltration showed no association. vWF-ag, vWF–RCof, and D-dimers may be associated with risk and complications in AML, but these findings require external validation in larger cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-026-02198-0.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the most prevalent acute leukemia in adults, with a median diagnosis age of 68 years. It is a heterogeneous hematologic malignancy that results from the unchecked expansion of clonal myeloid precursors [1]. Although adults’ 5-year overall survival is predicted to be around 30%, results differ by age; for example, those over 65 had an overall survival of less than 10% to 15% [2]. The US Food and Drug Administration has authorized more than 15 new AML agents or combinations since 2017. Advanced molecular understanding of etiology, clonal evolution, progression, and resistance mechanisms, in addition to targeted advancements, have revolutionized AML diagnosis, risk assessment, and treatment management [3].

Significant morbidity and mortality are caused by thromboembolic events in AML, particularly during induction treatment. Predicting the onset or recurrence of thrombosis is generally difficult in hematological malignancies [4]. Myeloid progenitor proliferation results in a hypercoagulable condition and endothelial dysfunction, which in turn alters the complete blood count [5]. The risk assessment methods now in use are not optimal for evaluating consequences in hematological malignancies because they are primarily utilized for solid tumors [6].

Despite the fact that all chemotherapeutics depress the immune system, hematological malignancies have an additional risk of infection, bleeding, or thrombosis because of poor hematopoiesis [7]. Despite being rare, CNS invasion is nonetheless a recognized AML consequence that lacks early warning signs [8]. Routine diagnostics like blood cell counts and coagulation markers only represent current status and are not very useful in predicting future difficulties, making it difficult to predict complications in patients with AML.

By supporting targeted treatment at the time of diagnosis, the identification of biological markers that would offer early insight into AML risk stratification and risk of consequences would improve prognosis. It may seem reasonable to think that von Willebrand factor (vWF), which is produced by both megakaryocytes and vascular endothelial cells, indicates hypercoagulability or injury to these cells. However, vWF may be dysfunctional, which could result in bleeding. An accurate sense of problems may be obtained by analyzing both markers, as vWF-ag determines the amount of vWF released while vWF-RCof determines its functionality. According to reports, vWF-ag may serve as a useful biomarker for stratifying the risk of thrombotic and bleeding complications in patients with AML [9].

When the pretest likelihood is unclear, D-dimer is utilized clinically to diagnose thromboembolic events because it reflects coagulation and fibrinolysis [10]. When AML is diagnosed, elevated D-dimer levels are a robust predictor of symptomatic thromboembolic events after chemotherapy [11]. However, its measurement at remission has not been studied, which could help define predictive cutoffs, at diagnosis, to potentially identify high risk groups and anticipate susceptibility to AML-associated complications.

Due to vWF’s function as a protective carrier molecule that delays the early degradation of procoagulant factor VIII (FVIII), epidemiological studies show that cancer patients have elevated FVIII, which is also independently linked to an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE). It is still unclear what heightened FVIII is based on [12–14].

Each of these markers has limits on its own. Nonspecific, D-dimer is upregulated in cancer, trauma, inflammation, infection, and surgery [15]. Being acute phase reactants, FVIII and vWF may be indicative of underlying disease, with inter-patient release variations impacted by comorbidities, hepatic function, and genetics. Although they might indicate infection, disease severity, endothelial damage, or platelet dysfunction, these are not specific indicators on their own [16].

To improve early prediction of AML risk grade and associated complications, this study examined whether vWF-ag, vWF–RCof activity, FVIII, and D-dimer levels, at diagnosis and remission, can predict outcomes, such as infections, CNS infiltration, thromboembolic events or bleeding, in patients of differing AML risk grades and if so, determine the significant cut off values of these markers at diagnosis and remission.

Patients and methods

Fifty newly diagnosed AML patients were enrolled in the study before the initiation of chemotherapy between February 2023 and February 2024 are the subjects of the current prospective cohort study. Participants were chosen from among the inpatients and outpatients of the Oncology Center, Mansoura University (OCMU). According to the 2022 European LeukemiaNet (ELN) risk stratification system, AML patients are classified into favorable, intermediate, and adverse risk groups which is based on cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities at diagnosis [17]. Prior to research enrollment, all patients provided written consent, and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the study protocol.

Newly diagnosed AML patients who had their diagnosis verified by cytogenetic, flow cytometry, and bone marrow tests met the inclusion requirements. Patients who were on anticoagulant medication or thromboprophylaxis, had incomplete or missing data, or had begun chemotherapy were not included. Age, sex, history of prior VTE, treatment history, related comorbidities, known thrombophilia, and whether bedridden or immobilized were among the clinical and demographic information gathered. In order to evaluate the illness status and identify any associated comorbidities, comprehensive clinical and laboratory evaluations were also carried out. To confirm the diagnosis, all patients had regular peripheral blood film, bone marrow aspiration, flow cytometry, cytogenetic investigations, and complete blood counts. Additional cytogenetic and molecular research evaluated AML risk categorization. The French-American-British (FAB) classification method, which divides AML into subtypes based on the appearance and differentiation of leukemic cells, was also used to classify the patients. These categories range from M0 to M7 [18].

To examine hematological parameters, eight milliliters of whole blood specimens were withdrawn into 0.109 mol/L (3.2% weight/volume [w/v]) buffered sodium citrate collection tubes. Plasma was rapidly separated from whole blood samples at normal temperature and was used for the following laboratory assays: vWF-ag, vWF-RCof, FVIII activity, and D-dimer. Sysmex CS-2500, Kobe, Japan was used to assess FVIII activity via clot-based method, while turbidimetric immune assay provided levels of vWF-ag, vWF-RCof, and d-dimer. These hematological parameters were examined both at diagnosis and after remission in all participants.

Remission was defined as complete blood cell counts reaching reference values and the inability to identify leukemia cells in the bone marrow and blood. Following a conventional chemotherapy course, all patients were monitored for a median of six months to evaluate any problems related to bleeding, infection, thrombosis, or CNS infiltration. They were then reassessed after full remission. Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) was diagnosed by duplex ultrasonography or venography if the patient had symptoms and any other thrombotic event was noted. Depending on the infection site, bacterial, viral, and fungal cultures were used to diagnosis the infections. Imaging was used to diagnose CNS infiltration in patients exhibiting new neurological impairments. Based on the patient’s history and follow-up, bleeding symptoms were documented.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 from IBM Corp [19]. Categorical data comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test to assess the association between variables. The Mann-Whitney U test was applied to compare two independent groups when the data was not normally distributed. The Kruskal-Wallis test, was utilized to compare continuous variables among multiple groups, when the assumptions of normality were not met. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was employed to compare means among three or more groups when assumptions of normality criterion were met. The Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test was used to compare paired samples in which normal distribution assumptions could not be applied. The Spearman correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the strength and direction of the relationship between two continuous variables. ROC curve analysis was used to evaluate the ability of hemostatic parameters to predict adverse-risk AML, with AUC and 95% CIs reported and cut-offs determined by the Youden index. To evaluate predictors of ELN adverse-risk AML, univariable logistic regression analysis was initially performed. To account for the limited number of events and reduce small-sample bias, a penalized logistic regression model using Firth’s correction was subsequently applied. Model variables were selected based on clinical relevance and univariable significance, with restriction of model size to avoid overfitting. Internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations), and bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) 95% confidence intervals were reported. Due to the small sample size and multiple comparisons, analyses were considered exploratory without adjustment for multiple testing.

Results

Patient demographics, stratified risk levels and complications

This study assessed 50 AML patients, 48% males and 52% females, with a mean age of 45.82 years, ranging from 7 to 81 years. The most common FAB classification was M4 (28%), followed by M2 and M3, both 20%. Risk stratification according to the 2022 ELN for AML was distributed, nearly evenly, between favourable, intermediate, and adverse-risk groups, 36%, 36%, and 28%, respectively. Infection was the most common complication at 50%, followed by bleeding 14%, thrombosis 12%, and CNS infiltration was found in 2%. AML complications in association with hemostasis parameters

Thrombosis was significantly associated with a high median vWF-ag at diagnosis (535%) and after remission (392%), compared to patients without thrombosis. Bleeding was associated with the lowest median vWF-ag at diagnosis (85%) and after remission (23%), compared to patients without bleeding. Similar trends were noticed for vWF-RCof, D-dimer and FVIII, with thrombosis consistently associated with higher median values and bleeding with lower values. Infection was associated with a higher median vWF-ag after remission (214%) compared to patients without infection (p = 0.046). CNS infiltration did not show significant associations with all hemostatic parameters. All hemostatic parameters improved significantly after remission when compared to levels at diagnosis in both AML cases with and without studied complications. This data is detailed in (Table 4).

Hemostatic parameters correlations with risk stratification and other patients’ data

At diagnosis and after remission, risk stratification showed the strongest positive correlation with all four hemostatic parameters (p < 0.05 for each). Age also showed a significant positive correlation with D-dimer at diagnosis and after remission (rs = 0.305 and 0.292, respectively, both p < 0.05). Predictive value of hemostatic parameters at AML diagnosis for adverse-risk disease

The D-dimer stands out with an AUC of 0.813, a highly significant p-value (< 0.001), and a cutoff of > 1.6. It demonstrates good sensitivity 78.6% and specificity 75%, indicating its potential as a reliable predictor. VWF-ag demonstrates an AUC of 0.780 at a cutoff value of > 295, sensitivity of 85.71% and specificity of 66.67%, with a significant p-value of 0.003, suggesting good discriminative ability. The vWF-RCof exhibits a notable AUC of 0.761at a cutoff of > 223, sensitivity 85.7% and specificity 75%, with a significant p-value of 0.004. FVIII revealed an AUC of 0.625 with sensitivity of 71.43% and a specificity of 58.33%, and a non-significant p-value of 0.183, suggesting limited discriminatory power for adverse-risk AML. These results underscore the importance of considering both sensitivity and specificity when assessing the predictive value of hemostatic parameters in diagnosing adverse-risk AML, with D-dimer showing particular promise in this context. Discussion

AML is characterized by a complex spectrum of coagulopathies ranging from hemorrhagic to thrombotic symptoms [20]. In this study we investigated the pattern of haemostatic markers, vWF-ag level, vWF–RCof activity, FVIII, and D-dimer, in newly diagnosed AML patients and the change in these parameters after remission.

The predictive value of therapy type (intensive vs. supportive) for DVT in patients with AML was not assessed. In Farge et al. [21] study, patients treated with intensive therapy developed thrombosis more frequently. Intensive chemotherapy can increase the risk of thrombosis via direct endothelial damage, destruction of leukemic cells releasing thrombogenic substances, and reduced synthesis of natural anticoagulants due to liver damage [22–25]. According to the studies conducted in the general population, in patients with AML, CVL insertion is a proven risk factor for thrombosis development [26–30]. Considering that intensive treatment requires CVL insertion and that chemotherapy can be thrombogenic, we decided to include the therapy type in our final model.

Our results show significant differences in vWF-ag, vWF-RCof and D-Dimer across risk groups. In the adverse-risk group, vWF-ag demonstrated an AUC of 0.780, at a cutoff value of > 295, sensitivity of 85.71% and specificity of 66.67% at a p-value of 0. 003.Similarily, vWF-RCof had an AUC of 0.761 at a cutoff of > 223 in the adverse-risk group, with sensitivity of 85.7% and sensitivity of 75% with a p-value of 0.004. D-dimer demonstrated the highest predictive value with an AUC of 0.813 at a cutoff of > 1.6 in the adverse-risk group, with sensitivity of 78.6% and specificity of 75% and a p-value of < 0.001. FVIII was not associated with significant differences amount the risk stratified groups.

These findings are consistent with previously published reports. Elevated vWF-ag and vWF-RCof have previously been documented in hematological malignancies. In AML and ALL patients Liu et al. [9, 31] demonstrated high vWF-ag levels correlated with high-risk phenotypes; another study linked high vWF levels with unfavorable outcomes [32]. Similarly Gavriatopoulou et al. [33] showed that in Waldenstrom’s macroglobulinemia, higher vWF levels corelated to high International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS) score indicating poor prognosis. A pilot study in myelodysplastic syndrome also reported high vWF-ag levels to be associated with high IPSS scores, reduced survival, and poorer response to therapy [34].

The association between D-dimer levels and disease severity in our study parallels prior reports in solid tumors including breast [35, 36], colorectal [37, 38], lung [39, 40] and brain cancers [41]. A metanalysis [42] demonstrated elevated D-dimer reflects enhanced activation of coagulation and fibrinolysis, and independently predicts adverse prognosis, often unrelated to overt thromboembolic events. Previous studies highlighted the prognostic and predictive value of D-dimer in chemotherapy response. According to Inanc et al. [43], lower D-dimer was linked to partial response in colorectal cancer patients while those with progressive disease had significantly higher levels. Moreover, Komurcuoglu et al. [44], reported high levels in lung cancer chemotherapy non-responders compared to responders. Antoniou et al. [45], reported high Dimer levels predicted poor response and tumor progression in advanced lung cancer patients. Alkhoder et al. [46], observed decreased D-dimer after chemotherapy in breast cancer patients. These reports are congruent with the results of this study, which is the first to suggest the clinical significance of D-dimer (and other hemostatic markers) as predictor for therapeutic response in AML patients.

Data regarding the predictive value of the DIC score according to the ISTH criteria are conflicting.Libourel et al. showed that DIC at the time of AML diagnosis was associated with an increased VTE rate. Moreover, D-dimer was marked as individual predictor [11]. Higher D-dimer levels are a widely used biomarker for DVT prediction in patients with cancer [47, 48]. In contrast, Martella et al. failed to prove the predictive value of D-dimer levels [49].

Although the use of prophylactic anticoagulation is not uncommon in patients with AML, the evident bleeding risk could outweigh its clinical benefit [50]. Therefore, developing a model for adequate risk assessment would be useful. The new International Initiative on Thrombosis and Cancer guidelines recommend thromboprophylaxis for patients with cancer at high risk for VTE development and low risk for bleeding [21].

A unique finding in our study is all hemostatic markers significantly decreased after remission, supporting their potential as biomarkers for treatment monitoring. Although some studies have shown evaluated vWF as a biomarker for response, this was mainly in other cancers and few have examined the specific panel we studied. For example, in hepatocellular carcinoma, vWF and ADAMTS13 levels have been proposed to predict response to angiogenesis inhibitors [51], hepatic artery infusion chemotherapy [52] and liver resection [53]. In metastatic colorectal cancer, baseline vWF and angiogenic markers correlated with progression-free intervals and overall survival during treatment [54].

We also observed that thrombosis was more frequent in the adverse-risk group 35.7% compared to the intermediate (5.6%) and low risk group (0%) (p = 0.007). Similar findings have been reported in other studies [55, 56], and [57]. Lee et al. [58], demonstrated VTE incidence increased with advanced molecular and cytogenetic risk. Possible explanations include the use of intensive re-induction chemotherapy in high-risk cases leading to vascular endothelial damage and activation of platelet and coagulation cascade [59–61], and [62]. In addition, disease severity with myeloid precursor proliferation can trigger a pronounced hypercoagulable state.

In our cohort, thrombosis was correlated with elevated vWF-ag, vWF-RCof and FVIII and D- dimer at both diagnosis and after remission compared to patients without thrombosis. This was shown in studies in other solid cancers. For example, high preoperative D-dimer levels predicted postoperative DVT in colorectal surgery [63]. Indeed, existing VTE risk prediction scores for ambulatory solid cancer patients such as Vienna Cancer and Thrombosis Study (CATS) score incorporate D-dimer as a major component [64] and a threshold of ≥ 8% has helped improved efficacy of drug thromboprophylaxis [65].

Beyond D-dimer, both vWF and FVIII correlate with VTE risk within the general population [66]. Interestingly, studies indicate elevated vWF levels as an independent VTE predictor in cancer patients [67–70]. In an assessment of vWF pro-peptide, a marker of vWF secretion, Nossent et al. concluded high vWF correlated with thrombosis via increased endothelial vWF secretion [71]. In Bauer et al. [72], , cultured tumor cell lines stimulate angiogenesis and vWF secretion. Melanoma tumor biopsies revealed ultra-large von Willebrand factor (UL-vWF) multimers within micro vessels. Tumor‐mediated inhibition of ADAMTS13 supported aggregation of UL-vWF multimers. Collectively, outcomes imply tumor cells induce UL‐VWF release, simultaneously, downregulating ADAMTS13. Directly, this rendered to prothrombotic states, with vWF‐platelet aggregates and fibrin deposition in tumor vasculature.

Conversely, patients with bleeding manifestations had the lowest vWF-ag, vWF-RCof, and FVIII levels at diagnosis and remission. This is in keeping with Shen [73], describing nonfunctional VWF multimers in AML [73]. and Federici and D’Amico [74] reporting proteolytic degradation of vWF released from leukemic cells, which reduces vWF–RCof activity and promoting bleeding despite vWF release. Similar findings were shown by others (Abaza et al. [75], and Lethagen et al. [76]).

This study highlights the critical burden of severe bleeding complications in patients with AML, revealing a complex interplay of disease biology, treatment-related factors, and clinical vulnerability. Abdulgayoom et al. [77] reported that among 214 newly diagnosed AML patients treated at our center over a ten-year period, 16.3% experienced Grade ≥ 3 bleeding, with intracranial hemorrhage being the predominant and most devastating manifestation. This aligns with previous studies that have consistently identified central nervous system bleeding as a major source of morbidity and early mortality in AML [78] and is comparable to the 5%-20% incidence reported in other international series [79–81]. Although current guidelines recommend maintaining platelet counts above 10 × 10⁹/L in stable AML and 20–50 × 10⁹/L in high-risk settings, most ICH events occurred below these thresholds (median 11 × 10⁹/L), suggesting that existing practices may not always prevent catastrophic bleeding. Beyond thrombocytopenia, qualitative platelet dysfunction, coagulopathy, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) may further amplify bleeding risk [82].

The study findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating due to small sample size and multiple testing. ROC-derived cut-offs are data-driven and require external validation. The present study demonstrates that vWF: Ag and D-dimer are consistently associated with adverse-risk AML across multiple analytical approaches, including conventional logistic regression, penalized regression, and bootstrap validation. The concordance of these findings supports the robustness of the observed associations despite the limited sample size. Given the exploratory nature of the analysis and the potential for residual confounding, these results should be interpreted cautiously.Limitations include small cohort size, low event rates, and no internal validation. Larger prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings and validate predictive thresholds.

Overall, and despite some limitations related to the relatively sample size and single center research, the study concludes vWF-ag, vWF-RCof, and D-dimer levels are significantly associated with AML risk, thrombotic and bleeding complications, and shows a dynamic pattern with remission, indicating their potential as biomarkers for prognosis, risk stratification, and treatment monitoring in AML. We provide evidence in support of conducting larger studies to validate these observations and to explore integration of these biomarkers into risk stratification models in hematological malignancies.
