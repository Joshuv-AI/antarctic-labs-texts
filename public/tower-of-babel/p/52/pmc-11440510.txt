Coacervate Dense Phase Displaces Surface-Established Pseudomonas aeruginosa Biofilms
Authors: Apoorva Vishwakarma, Amal Narayanan, Nityanshu Kumar, Zixi Chen, Francis Dang, Joshua Menefee, Ali Dhinojwala, Abraham Joy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
For millions of years, barnacles and mussels have successfully adhered to wet rocks near tide-swept seashores. While the chemistry and mechanics of their underwater adhesives are being thoroughly investigated, an overlooked aspect of marine organismal adhesion is their ability to remove underlying biofilms from rocks and prepare clean surfaces before the deposition of adhesive anchors. Herein, we demonstrate that nonionic, coacervating synthetic polymers that mimic the physicochemical features of marine underwater adhesives remove ∼99% of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) biofilm biomass from underwater surfaces. The efficiency of biofilm removal appears to align with the compositional differences between various bacterial biofilms. In addition, the surface energy influences the ability of the polymer to displace the biofilm, with biofilm removal efficiency decreasing for surfaces with lower surface energies. These synthetic polymers weaken the biofilm–surface interactions and exert shear stress to fracture the biofilms grown on surfaces with diverse surface energies. Since bacterial biofilms are 1000-fold more tolerant to common antimicrobial agents and pose immense health and economic risks, we anticipate that our unconventional approach inspired by marine underwater adhesion will open a new paradigm in creating antibiofilm agents that target the interfacial and viscoelastic properties of established bacterial biofilms.

Introduction

Biofilms are organized communities of bacteria within a self-secreted matrix of biomacromolecules.1 They contaminate and proliferate on biotic and abiotic surfaces even under extreme chemical and physical stresses—making biofilms a major scourge in healthcare and industrial settings.2 A recent estimate suggests that biofilms have an economic impact of more than $4 trillion annually, of which $386.8 billion was attributed to the impact of biofilms on medical and human health. Also, over $3 trillion was spent mitigating or managing the consequences of biofilms in industrial settings.3 Given the health, social, and economic implications, numerous research avenues are being pursued for preventing biofilm formation and removing existing biofilms.4 Current strategies for biofilm removal involve combinatorial antibiotic therapies, mechanical disruption using water sprays and jets, and physical debridement from the surface.5 Often, these methods do not remove biofilms altogether, but cause them to slough off and colonize neighboring surfaces, thereby compounding the problem of biofouling and recurring infections.6,7

The recent research efforts on antibiofilm agents have been focused on disabling living bacterial components of the biofilms. For example, the development of novel antimicrobial agents using small molecules,8 peptides,9 polymers,10−13 bacterial signaling inhibitors,14,15 bacteriophages,16−18 and metabolic inhibitors.14,19 Such agents are designed to be effective against metabolically active bacteria and not the dormant bacteria present in biofilms. The mechanical integrity of biofilms persists even after the treatment with antibiotics and biocides because they only disable the metabolically active bacteria, not the nongrowing dormant and persister cells.20−24

The persister cells that are protected by the biofilm matrix can reestablish biofilm formation and lead to recurring infections. Recently, it has been recognized that the viscoelasticity21,25 and surface-adhesive properties26 of biofilms play key roles in their persistence and virulence.27 For instance, the Pseudomonas aeruginosa variants isolated from patients with cystic fibrosis overproduce extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) compared to their isogenic wild-type variant.28 Notably, this EPS overproduction causes changes in the viscoelastic properties of the biofilms and has been suggested to promote prolonged lung infection with reduced cough clearance.21 Despite the recognition that surface adhesion and viscoelastic properties of biofilms are prevalent mechanisms for their virulence, antibiofilm strategies that target these properties are remarkably absent, which can be attributed to the unknowns of the molecular features required for altering the adhesive and viscoelastic properties of biofilm matrices.

Interestingly, for millions of years, marine organisms, such as mussels and barnacles, have found ways to overcome the layers of biofilms found on wet rocks to deposit their adhesive anchors on underwater surfaces. We were particularly intrigued by Fears and co-workers’ observation that the barnacles secrete a phase-separating fluid rich in lipids and phenolic compounds that oxidize and lift off marine biofilms from the targeted surface of their attachment.29,30 It is proposed that in the barnacle adhesive secretion, the lipid-rich phase may act to displace interfacial water, while the phenolic components cause oxidation of surface contaminants by generating reactive oxygen species.29,31,32

Inspired by how marine adhesive organisms deal with biofilm removal, in this study, we postulated an unconventional antibiofilm strategy to remove established biofilms from underwater surfaces. We conceptualized that an aqueous polymer solution that outcompetes biofilm–substrate interactions in wet conditions could be a promising approach to displace existing biofilms. To design such biofilm-clearing materials, we relied on our previously reported liquid phase-separating polyesters (coacervates) that can spread and mediate strong adhesion with substrates underwater.33 To our surprise, we observed that the coacervates efficiently removed surface-established P. aeruginosa bacterial biofilms (biomass removal ∼99%). By testing the biofilm clearance efficiency of this method on biofilms formed by diverse bacterial species, controlled flow rates and surface chemistry, and interfacial adhesion measurements, we discovered that the ability to interfere with biofilm-surface interactions and the optimal viscosity of the coacervate dense phase provides them with a high biofilm clearing capacity. The biofilm-clearing method demonstrated in this study addresses an unmet need in passively clearing established P. aeruginosa biofilms from wet hydrophilic surfaces because it targets the adhesive and viscoelastic properties of the biofilm matrix, in contrast to the antibiofilm strategies that commonly target metabolically active bacteria in the biofilms.

Results and Discussion

The design of a biofilm-clearing material was conceptualized based on the observed physicochemical features of the barnacle underwater adhesion process.29,30 Before the deposition of adhesive cement, barnacles secrete a phase-separating, lipid-rich fluid that displaces biofilms from underwater surfaces. Recently, we reported synthetic polyesters that undergo liquid–liquid phase separation to form coacervates with tunable interfacial and viscoelastic properties.33,34 The optimal viscosity and strong interfacial interactions of these polyesters enable their wetting on surfaces submerged underwater, akin to the lipid-rich secretion of barnacles. Therefore, in this study, we synthesized a similar statistical copolyester using the polyesterification reaction between two N-functional diol monomers and succinic acid (Figure 1A, see the Supporting Information for details).35 To facilitate the visualization under a fluorescence microscope, we covalently tagged the polyester with rhodamine B (<0.05 mol %), giving the polyester solution a magenta color. The first monomer (E, 80 mol %) enables a lower critical solution temperature (LCST)-type phase separation and the second monomer (H, 20 mol %) acts as a hydrophobic group to modulate the LCST of the resulting statistical copolyester. Above their LCST ∼10 °C, the polyester solution phase separates into polymer-rich dense and polymer-poor dilute phases (Figure 1B). The homogeneous aqueous solution of this polyester at an initial concentration of 100 mg/mL (prepared at 4 °C) phase separates at room temperature into dense and dilute phases of ∼54 wt/wt % (polymer/water%) and 2 wt/wt %, respectively (Figure S3). The dense phase can rapidly spread underwater due to its low interfacial energy against water (γDense phase-Water ∼ 0.4 mN/m) and optimal viscosity (η0 ∼ 1.2 Pa-s, 1000-fold higher than water and dilute phase).33,34 This motivated us to evaluate the interactions of the dense phase with the biofilms grown underwater.

Initially, we chose P. aeruginosa as a model organism for growing biofilms on our substrates since it is an extensively studied, biofilm-forming Gram-negative pathogen, which naturally inhabits freshwater.36 We fabricated a simple glass channel (dimensions of channel = 18 mm l × 3 mm w × 0.150 mm d) on glass coverslips as shown in Figure 1C to grow the biofilms and test their interaction with the dense phase. The biofilms grown on these channels were stained with cell-permeable dye DMAO to visualize the bacteria (see the Supporting Information for details). The dense phase (20 μL) was added to one end of the channel underwater (Movie S1). When this system was given a small incline (10°), surprisingly, we observed that the dense phase slowly slides on the surface of the biofilm (sliding speed ∼ 1 mm/min) through the length of the channel, instead of spreading on the biofilm. The observations were unexpected since the dense phase readily sticks on biofilm-free glass surfaces underwater and does not flow through the channel. When visualized under a microscope, we observed a striking absence of both biofilm (green fluorescence) and dense phase (red fluorescence) in the channels, as shown in Figure 1D,E. We used the COMSTAT plugin in ImageJ to quantify and compare biofilm biomass and substratum coverage. Biomass and substratum coverage represent the area-normalized total volume of biofilm and the area occupied by the biofilm on the layer atop the substrate relative to the total area, respectively.37−39 As indicated in Figure 1D, the biofilm biomass in the channels decreased by 99.9% from 13.2 ± 2.1 to 0.004 ± 0.003 μm, while the area occupied by the biofilm (substratum coverage) decreased by 99.9% from 87.1 ± 18.1 to 0.09 ± 0.07% when the biofilm was interfaced with the coacervate dense phase. The dense phase collected after their interaction with biofilms becomes a mixture of the biofilm and dense phase. This biofilm-dense phase mixture does not stick to glass surfaces, indicating that the biofilm and dense phase interact strongly with each other when compared with their interaction with wet glass surfaces.

Inspired by the observations from the dense phase mediated clearance of P. aeruginosa biofilms, we investigated whether the dense phase could similarly displace biofilms made from other bacterial species. With that goal, we tested the dense phase against biofilms of gram-negative bacteria Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli, and Gram-positive bacteria Staphylococcus aureus. All of these bacteria form robust biofilms within 24 h (Figure 2A), which have been implicated in enhancing their virulence during infections.40

Interestingly, as shown in Figure 2B, aside from K. pneumoniae biofilms, we did not observe any significant differences between the biofilms of E. coli and S. aureus before and after the addition of the dense phase. For K. pneumoniae, the biofilm-biomass decreased by 70.6 ± 25.5%, while the differences in the substratum coverage of the biofilms of E. coli and S. aureus before and after the application of the dense phase was not significant.

It should be noted that for each bacterial species, the structure, chemical composition, and mechanical characteristics of their biofilms vary greatly because different bacteria use different biochemical pathways to synthesize and construct their surface adhesions and biofilm matrix components.41,42 The main matrix components of P. aeruginosa biofilms are polysaccharides such as Pel, Psl, and alginate, and proteins like CdrA stabilize the polysaccharide matrices.43−46 Notably, P. aeruginosa and K. pneumoniae(47,48) form polysaccharide-rich biofilms, while E. coli(49,50) and S. aureus(50,51) are known to form biofilms with protein-rich matrices. Aside from the differences in the chemical compositions of the biofilm matrices, the differences in the mechanical properties of the biofilms and their substrate adhesion could also be responsible for the differences in the extent of their displacement by dense phase.27,52 A direct comparison of the mechanical properties of the biofilms of these strains in the specified growth conditions has not been reported in the literature nor has been investigated in this work. However, based on existing reports, biofilms of Pseudomonas (in particular, P. aeruginosa and P. fluorescens), which have polysaccharides as the main matrix material, were reported to have lower yield stresses and lower energy dissipation (more elastic behavior) compared to biofilms of bacteria having proteins as the major matrix material.53,54

The biofilms grown under fluid flow are found to be thinner, but denser, more resistant to external mechanical perturbations, and closely mimic naturally occurring biofilms.55 To test the biofilm-clearing efficiency of the dense phase in real-life mimetic biofilms, we challenged the dense phase against biofilms grown under flowing media in a custom-built setup (Figure 3A, see the Supporting Information for details). This enabled us to grow biofilms for a longer time on different surfaces with continuous nutrient supply and enabled their direct visualization before and after treatments. We used a 3-channel laminar flow setup as shown in Figure 3A to grow biofilms.56−59 After biofilm culture, 50 μL of dense phase was injected into the inlet, inclined at 10°, and allowed to slide down the length of the channel (channel dimensions: 40 mm l × 4 mm w, growth area = 1.6 cm2). The channels were then rinsed with 1 mL of water (flow rate = 1 mL/min), and the biofilms were imaged with a confocal microscope. As seen in Figures 3B and S4, for P. aeruginosa biofilms grown under flow conditions, the dense phase displaced 98.5 ± 0.8% biomass from the surface leading to a 94.0 ± 4.3% decrease in substratum coverage.

In a flow cell, the shear stress induced by a flowing liquid can cause a significant loss of biomass in mature biofilms.20 Such shear stress (S) experienced by the biofilm in response to liquid flow is proportional to the viscosity (η) and the flow rate (R) of the liquid (S ≈ η × R).60 To test if the biofilm removal by the dense phase was solely due to the viscosity-induced shear stress from the dense phase flow, we selected two control liquids with viscosities higher than our polyester coacervate dense phase: control liquid 1 = SYLGARD 184 Part A61 with η0 ∼ 5.1 Pa-s and control liquid 2 = aqueous poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) (20 wt %/v, η0 ∼ 100 Pa-s).62

As shown in Figure 3B, the SYLGARD treatment did not show a statistically significant change in the biofilm biomass. However, the substratum coverage was reduced by 41.7 ± 10.5% when treated with SYLGARD, whereas 20% PVA led to a 70.2 ± 3.1% decrease in total biomass and 56.7 ± 15.8% surface clearance. Even with viscosities 5–100 times higher than the dense phase, the control liquids did not show comparable biofilm clearance to that of the coacervate dense phase (biomass reduction ∼ 99%).

Next, we tested the biofilm-clearing efficiency as a function of the flow rate of liquids with viscosity lower than that of the dense phase. The dilute phase and water were chosen as controls (η0 of dilute phase and water are ∼0.001 Pa-s) to test the effect of shear stress induced by varying flow rates of liquids. Initially, a flow rate of 20 μL/min was chosen, since it matched the estimated flow rate and contact time of the dense phase through the length of the channel at an inclination of 10°. Higher flow rates of 200 and 2000 μL/min were also tested to amplify the shear stress on the biofilm. As shown in Figure 3C, the dilute phase flowing at 20 μL/min led to 63.5 ± 7.1% reduction in biofilm biomass and 68.6 ± 6.9% reduction in substratum coverage of the biofilm, while at 200 μL/min, we did not see statistically significant differences in biomass or substratum coverage compared to 20 μL/min. Increasing the flow rate to 2000 μL/min led to an almost 96.2 ± 1.3% decrease in biomass and a 94.3 ± 0.9% decrease in surface coverage. As shown in Figure S6, flowing water through the channels at 20 μL/min has an insignificant effect on the biofilm and led to a 9.3 ± 29.7% reduction in biomass and a 5.1 ± 15.8% reduction in surface coverage. Increasing the flow rate to 200 μL/min did not lead to statistically significant results. However, at high flow rates of 2000 μL/min, we observed a 78.2 ± 18.3% reduction in biomass and 71.5 ± 14.3% reduction in biofilm surface coverage from the flow of water alone.

Note that the shear stress exerted by the dense phase at 20 μL/min is 10-fold higher than the shear stress exerted by water-only and dilute phase samples at a flow rate of 2000 μL/min (Figure S7). These results indicate that while both water and dilute phase can augment shear stresses strong enough to displace biofilms at extremely high flow rates of 2000 μL/min, the presence of polymer in the dilute phase causes significantly higher displacement of biofilm from the surface compared to that of water (Figures 3C and S7).

Taken together, the lower biofilm clearance behavior of viscous polymer controls (SYLGARD and 20% PVA) compared to the dense phase (Figure 3B) and the biofilm clearance displayed by the dilute phase at low flow rates (Figure 3C) indicate that the shear stress induced by the dense phase viscosity and flow rate alone is insufficient to explain the high biofilm clearance by the dense phase. Thus, we investigated the role of the interfacial properties of the dense phase and surface chemistry of substrates in displacing biofilms from the surface.

To study how the interfacial properties of the dense phase affect biofilm clearance, we tested the biofilm clearance by the dense phase on P. aeruginosa biofilms that were grown on substrates with different surface functional groups. We chemically functionalized glass substrates with self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) as shown in Figure 4A.63 Bare glass (water contact angle ∼0°), PEG-SAMs (water contact angle = 45.7 ± 1.3°), and PETS-SAMs (water contact angle = 80.4 ± 3.7°) were chosen as representative ‘very hydrophilic’, ‘hydrophilic’, and ‘less hydrophilic’ surfaces, respectively (Figure 4B).

The P. aeruginosa biofilms were cultured on bare glass, PEG-SAMs, and PETS-SAMs functionalized glass under flowing media. As shown in Figure 4C,D, we observed that the biofilm biomass and surface coverage are vastly different across bare glass, PEG-SAMs, and PETS-SAMs. The biomass and substratum coverage are the least for biofilms grown on PEG-SAMs. While it is reported in the literature that biofilms are generally denser and more robust on surfaces with lower hydrophilicity,64,65 we did not see a linear trend between biofilm biomass and surface hydrophilicity (Figure 4D, “Before”). The bare glass and SAMs-coated substrates were subjected to a dense phase treatment. In contrast to the biofilms grown on glass, biofilms grown on PEG-SAMs did not show a significant biomass reduction. However, a 60.3 ± 6.7% decrease in biofilm substratum coverage was observed in the case of PEG-SAMs-grown biofilms. We also did not see any significant differences between the biofilms grown on PETS-SAMs before and after the application of the dense phase. With these observations, we can infer that the extent of the biofilm displaced by the dense phase decreases with decreasing surface hydrophilicity. However, the observation that the dense phase is effective for “very hydrophilic” and “hydrophilic” surfaces and not for “less hydrophilic” surface, with all of the conditions equal, is proof that interfacial tension is an important factor in determining biofilm removal. Note that in this process, both polymer–surface interactions (γDense phase-Surface) and biofilm–surface interactions (γBiofilm-Surface) are different across different SAMs-coated surfaces and the resultant trends in biofilm clearance could be a combined effect from both parameters.

To understand the effect of polyester on biofilm-surface interactions (γBiofilm-Surface), we determined the biofilm–substrate work of adhesion (Wad) using a custom-built JKR (Johnson, Kendall, and Roberts) contact model in the water and dilute phase. Figure 5B describes the setup used for calculating the work of adhesion to fracture the biofilm–substrate interface (see the Supporting Information for details).32,66−68 The setup consists of a glass hemisphere (radius of curvature 1.25 mm) attached to a load cell, which is brought in contact (preload = 1 mN) with the biofilm grown on a soft PDMS substrate (storage modulus = 0.7 MPa) in the medium of interest. The contact angle of the glass lens is varied by coating with OTS followed by exposure to vacuum plasma for varying durations (details in the Supporting Information). We chose PDMS as a substrate to grow biofilms because hard surfaces like glass will not deform upon contact and the effective contact area is too small to provide measurable work of adhesion. The presence of biofilms on PDMS was validated using fluorescence microscopy, as shown in Figure S5.

The shear stress induced by the viscosity and flow rate of the coacervate dense phase provides added fracture to the biofilm network. The combined effect provides the dense phase with a higher efficiency in clearing biofilms compared to the dilute phase. Although the interfacial interactions between biofilms and the surface are reduced in the hydrophobic surface, the reduction is not sufficient to cause peeling of the biofilm. This also indicates that the shear stress caused by dense phase viscosity alone cannot remove biofilms from surfaces with high efficiency.

To minimize the viscous dissipative effects originating from the viscoelastic nature of the dense phase, we decided to perform the pull-off measurements in the dilute phase with the assumption that the effect of interfacial properties of the dilute phase on pull-off forces should be similar to that of the dense phase. As shown in Figure 5A, on a representative n-octadecyltrichlorosilane (OTS)-functionalized surface, the interfacial tensions of both the dilute phase and dense phase are comparable, where γDilute phase-OTS = 16.0 ± 0.3 mN/m, γDense phase-OTS = 10.6 ± 1.3 mN/m, γDilute phase-Air = 46.7 ± 2.6 mN/m, and γDense phase-Air = 44.5 ± 5.3 mN/m. Hence, the effects from adhesion measurements originate only from the changes in the biofilm-OTS-SAMs interface.

Having established that the dilute and dense phases have similar surface tensions and comparable interfacial tensions against the OTS-SAMs, we proceeded to investigate the effect of the dilute phase on the Wad to fracture the biofilm–substrate interface. As seen in Figure 5C, the Wad trends almost linearly with the water contact angle of the substrate, implying that the energy required to fracture the biofilm-hydrophobic surface (OTS-SAMs) interface is much higher than that required in the case of a hydrophilic surface (glass). These results support observations made elsewhere in the literature that biofilms stick better to hydrophobic surfaces.64,69,70 Compared to water-only conditions (Wad, Biofilm-OTS-SAMs, in water ∼ 96 mJ/m2), in the dilute phase (Wad, Biofilm-OTS-SAMs, in dilute phase ∼ 48 mJ/m2), the Wad to fracture the biofilm–substrate interface decreases dramatically—indicating that the constituent polyester of the dilute and dense phase is surface active and interferes with the adhesive bonds that the biofilm makes with the substrate (Figure 5D). The loss of biofilm–surface interaction is more pronounced as the hydrophilicity of the surface increases. Notably, we could not detect significant pull-off forces for substrates with water contact angles below 70° in the presence of the dilute phase. To summarize, the dilute phase and, by extension, the dense phase reduce the energy (Wad) required to fracture the biofilm–substratum interface. These results again support our hypothesis that the biofilm removal by the dense phase is mediated via a combination of weakening of the biofilm–substrate interfacial interactions (Figure 5C) and shear stress exerted by the dense phase on the biofilm (Figure 3C).

Conclusions

In conclusion, we report on a novel application of nonionic, polyester coacervates that undergo liquid–liquid phase separation to form a polymer-rich dense phase and a polymer-poor dilute phase. Akin to the cleaning strategies employed by barnacles, we show that the polyester dense phase also possesses unique properties, such as optimal viscosity, density greater than water, and low surface energy, that enable it to sink to the bottom of an underwater surface, rapidly spread on the surface, and displace existing biofilms from the surface. Our data suggest that both the dense and dilute phases reduce the work of adhesion required to fracture the biofilm–substrate interface (Figure 5C). The shear stress induced by the optimal viscosity and flow rate of the dense phase provides an additional fracture mechanism to enhance biofilm clearance (Figure 3C). The combined interfacial weakening and shear stress effect provide the dense phase with higher efficiency in clearing biofilms compared with the dilute phase. However, the biofilms adhere more strongly to hydrophobic surfaces, and hence, it is challenging for the dense phase to displace a significant amount of biofilm biomass from hydrophobic surfaces. The glass surfaces tested here are relatively smoother than most real-life surfaces, where biofilms contaminate. Since surface roughness changes the biofilm adhesion64,71 and fluid flow,72,73 it may affect the efficiency of our polyester coacervates in real-life scenarios.

Note that we do not understand at the molecular level how this polyester influences the interfacial interactions between biofilm and substrate. But this study will inspire the design of a new class of antibiofilm materials that focus on weakening the biofilm-surface interactions. Such future studies may inform us of new material designs and molecular features required to create technologies for removing established biofilms from real-life surfaces with diverse surface chemistry, roughness, and bacterial flora. Nonetheless, we show that in the case of P. aeruginosa biofilms, a tiny amount of dense phase (50 μL) can clear almost 99% of biomass from a 1.6 cm2 area within 10 min under the action of gravity (10° incline). The simplicity of this experiment opens avenues for various applications. Given that our polyester coacervates are biodegradable,74 cytocompatible,75 and can function as sustained release matrices for small molecule76 and macromolecular77 drugs, their surface-cleaning, biofilm-disrupting properties can be used in synergy with antibiotics, or therapeutics in several biomedical applications.
