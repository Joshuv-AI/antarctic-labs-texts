NCKAP1 coordinates focal adhesion and adherens junction dynamics in endothelial barrier maintenance and angiogenesis
Authors: Yunyun Xiao, Paul-Lennard Mendez, Christian Hiepen, Jerome Jatzlau, Petra Knaus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryVascular maintenance relies on a delicate balance between cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions. Both undergo dynamic remodeling through actin cytoskeletal rearrangements. A key mechanism driving these processes is the branched actin networks mediated by the Arp2/3 complex and promoted by the WAVE regulatory complex. However, the contribution of WRC to endothelial barrier maintenance and vascular expansion remains incompletely understood. Here, we show that knockdown of NCKAP1 disrupts WRC and impairs lamellipodia-based protrusions in ECs. This defect suppresses paxillin phosphorylation and delays endothelial barrier formation. Nonetheless, despite the barrier integrity being preserved in fully formed endothelial monolayers, actin dynamics at intercellular junctions are inhibited, and consequently restrain angiogenesis. Moreover, our study also revealed a potential role of NCKAP1 in angiogenic signal transduction through suppression of FAK-paxillin signaling. Our findings identify WRC as a direct regulator of FAs and underscore its essential role in coordinating endothelial barrier function and responsiveness to angiogenic cues.Subject areas: biological sciences, molecular biology, cell biology, biomechanics

Introduction

Lamellipodia, sheet-like membrane rufflings that often exist at the leading edge of migrating cells, are indispensable structures for migration, invasion, and trafficking.1 Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein (WASP)-family verprolin-homologous protein (WAVE) provides pivotal adapter function to transduce extracellular signals to actin-related protein 2/3 (Arp2/3) complex for lamellipodia polymerization.2,3 This takes place in a hetero-pentameric complex, consisting of WAVE1/2/3, CYFIP1/Sra1 or CYFIP2, NCKAP1/Nap1/Hem-2, Abi1/2/3, and HSPC300, known as the WAVE regulatory complex (WRC).4,5 WRC is recruited to patches of the plasma membrane enriched in acidic phospholipids, where it is activated by GTP-bound Rac1 and orchestrates actin filament branching and extension at the cell periphery.4,6 This facilitates the emergence of dynamic protrusions, which are essential for cell migration and related processes. While all WRC subunits contribute to complex stability, NCKAP1 is regarded as a critical scaffold, as its depletion causes the most striking destabilization of this multiprotein complex, highlighting its central role in maintaining WRC integrity and function.7,8,9 In addition, NCKAP1 has gained continuous attention for its role in mediating cell adhesion across species and tissues.7,8,10,11,12 Among these findings, NCKAP1 emerged as a key regulator of focal adhesion (FA) dynamics by modulating focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and paxillin phosphorylation, thereby impacting cell adhesion and invasion.13,14 Mutations of NCKAP1 have been implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders and diverse cancers, highlighting the need to understand its molecular mechanisms in disease pathogenesis.15,16,17,18,19

The endothelium is a semipermeable monolayer of endothelial cells (ECs) that lines the inner surface of blood vessels. As the primary regulator of selective exchange between the bloodstream and surrounding tissues, the endothelial barrier plays a crucial role in maintaining vascular homeostasis.20 Notably, the quiescent state of ECs is not the default but actively maintained.21 This state can be disrupted by diverse physiological and pathological cues that activate small GTPases and induce actin cytoskeletal remodeling.21,22,23

Coordinated cell-cell and cell-extracellular matrix (ECM) adhesions support endothelial barrier integrity by mechanically integrating with the actin cytoskeleton.24 Vascular endothelial-cadherin (VE-cadherin) is the primary adherens junction (AJ) protein in ECs. It mediates homophilic interactions between adjacent ECs via its N-terminal extracellular domain, and its cytosolic tail is connected to actin via catenins to reinforce AJ.24,25,26 At the basal surface, ECs adhere to the ECM through integrin-based FAs, which link to the actin cytoskeleton via adapter proteins and cooperate with AJs to maintain barrier integrity.26,27,28,29,30 FAs and AJs share key regulators, such as vinculin, small GTPases, and associated kinases, underscoring an extensive crosstalk between these adhesive systems.26,30,31,32 In quiescent endothelial monolayers, Rac1-mediated cortical actin network presents in parallel to AJ and supports stable, linear AJ, whereas permeability-inducing factors induce tensile radial actin bundles, enhancing actomyosin contractility via Rho-ROCK signaling.22,23,30 Concurrently, VE-cadherin undergoes phosphorylation-driven internalization, generating transient paracellular gaps.21,30,33 These transient openings facilitate leukocyte transmigration during immune responses and enable angiogenic sprouting30,34 during tissue repair. However, sustained or aberrant activation of ECs disrupts barrier integrity and contributes to pathological outcomes including chronic inflammation, edema, sepsis, and vascular malformations.26,30

Angiogenesis is an important process for new vessel formation in adulthood and especially in cancer and eye diseases.21 FAs function as central signaling hubs that transduce mechanical and chemical signals,34,35,36,37 such as pro-angiogenic vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and induces migration and angiogenesis.34,35,36,37 These processes largely rely on the assembly and disassembly of the FA complex, i.e., its turnover, as well as on actin dynamics.38 FA formation initiates underneath the lamellipodial protrusion of migrating cells, and nascent adhesions can rapidly develop into focal complexes (FXs), which further mature into FAs.38,39 FAK is actively involved in phosphorylating paxillin in this transition.39 Matured FAs connect to stress fibers, which facilitate the retraction of the cell’s rear end as it moves forward. Thereafter, FAs undergo either fibrillar adhesion or disassembly for reassembly at new locations.38,40 Previous studies demonstrated that Arp2/3-mediated lamellipodia formation is crucial for VE-cadherin-based endothelial junction remodeling and cell migration during sprouting angiogenesis.41,42,43,44,45,46 Although WRC is a major upstream activator of Arp2/3, its specific role in endothelial protrusive activity and adhesion remodeling remains poorly defined. In particular, how WRC-dependent lamellipodia coordinate FA assembly with AJ remodeling in barrier integrity and sprouting plasticity has not been addressed.

To address this gap, we examined the role of NCKAP1, a core WRC subunit, in regulating actin-driven protrusions, FA dynamics, and junctional remodeling in ECs, and investigated how its loss influences barrier formation and angiogenic responsiveness.

Results

NCKAP1 depletion disrupts the WAVE complex in HUVECs

The Arp2/3-dependent lamellipodia formation in membrane protrusions is an essential process for many endothelial processes, such as adhesion, migration, barrier formation and maintenance, and angiogenesis.42,44 To evaluate the specific role of the WRC in ECs, we knocked down the structural component NCKAP1 in human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs) using two distinct siRNAs. The knockdown efficiency of NCKAP1 was quantitatively validated at both the transcriptional (Figure S1A) as well as translational levels (Figures 1A and 1B), respectively. Parallel analysis of WAVE2 expression by western blot (Figures 1A and 1B) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining (Figure S1B) revealed a concomitant reduction in WAVE2 protein expression in NCKAP1-deficient ECs. These findings confirm the successful establishment of NCKAP1 knockdown and suggest a consequential reduction of other components of WRC upon NCKAP1 depletion. We further stained for Arp3 and actin in sub-confluent HUVECs (Figure S1C). Upon NCKAP1 knockdown, Arp3 localization at the cell periphery was markedly diminished, consistent with the observed loss of lamellipodial actin at the cell edge. Together, these findings indicate that NCKAP1 depletion impairs membrane protrusion via a WRC-Arp2/3-mediated mechanism.

NCKAP1 deficiency leads to a decrease in focal adhesion number and elongated morphology

Subsequently, the FA complex proteins paxillin and vinculin were co-stained in sub-confluent HUVECs upon NCKAP1 knockdown (Figure 1C). The imaging data revealed a marked alteration of peripheral actin architecture upon NCKAP1 knockdown. Lamellipodial membrane protrusions were largely absent and were partially replaced by filopodia-like structures (Figures 1C and 1D). In parallel, depletion of NCKAP1 in ECs led to a decreased number of FAs and an elongated FA morphology, as determined by quantitative analysis of paxillin signals. This analysis involved the clustering of FAs according to their size and circularity.47 We found that the number of smaller-sized FAs, especially in the range of 0.15–1 μm2, were reduced most strikingly, though numbers in all size categories declined in NCKAP1-deficient cells (Figure 1E). The proportional analysis of circularity distribution indicated that paxillin-positive FAs turned from more round (0.5–0.99) to elongated (0.01–0.5) upon NCKAP1-depletion (Figure 1F). Specifically, siRNA 2 caused a statistically significant decrease in circularity in the 0.5–0.8 category (p = 0.0157) and an increase in circularity in the 0.2–0.5 range (p = 0.0764), indicating that NCKAP1 deficiency leads to a prevalence of FA elongation. Furthermore, proximity ligation assay (PLA) between paxillin and vinculin revealed that the assembly of the FA complex was not significantly affected by NCKAP1 knockdown (Figures 1G and 1H).

Loss of NCKAP1 suppresses paxillin phosphorylation in ECs

Paxillin phosphorylation (pPaxillin) has been observed at nascent FXs, marking the initiation of FA maturation. Impairment of this process has been shown to impede FA turnover, consequently disrupting FA-dependent biological functions.39 As the number of small sized of paxillin positive FAs (0.15–0.5 μm2) was predominantly reduced, we assumed that these structures might represent immature FXs. To evaluate this hypothesis, we examined both total and phosphorylated paxillin in HUVECs depleted of NCKAP1 (Figure 2A) by western blotting. Intriguingly, while the expression of various FA components remained unchanged (Figures S2A–S2E), paxillin expression was elevated upon NCKAP1 knockdown, and the proportion of pPaxillin to total paxillin was reduced (Figure 2B). The elevated paxillin level may reflect a compensatory mechanism response to the reduced proportion of pPaxillin. We further investigated paxillin phosphorylation by IF staining (Figure 2C). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant reduction in the number, area, and intensity of pPaxillin signals in NCKAP1-knockdown ECs (Figure 2D). These results indicate that FA formation and turnover were suppressed in NCKAP1-depleted ECs. However, the activity of associated kinases (pFAK and pSrc) remained unchanged upon NCKAP1 depletion (Figure S2A).

In summary, by visualizing the morphology of the actin cytoskeleton, Arp3, and FA proteins following NCKAP1 knockdown, we demonstrated that NCKAP1 is essential for lamellipodia formation at the peripheral protrusions of migrating cells via the WRC-Arp2/3 axis. Simultaneously, NCKAP1 depletion suppresses new FA formation and turnover, leading to a reduction in FA abundance and an elongated cell morphology due to decreased turnover rates.

NCKAP1 knockdown impairs endothelial barrier formation

WRC-dependent AJ modeling is a vital process for endothelial barrier formation, maintenance, and sprouting angiogenesis. To investigate cell-cell contact formation in NCKAP1-deficient ECs, we applied electric cell-substrate impedance sensing (ECIS) to measure electrical impedance across cell-covered electrodes from cells being seeded into arrays (t = 0). When applying appropriate current frequencies, resistance measurements reflect the integrity of cell-cell junctions, whereas capacitance correlates with cell spreading and substrate coverage (Figure 3A).48,49 Normalized capacitance revealed that cell spreading velocities of HUVECs subjected to NCKAP1 knockdown were hampered, as indicated by elevated normalized capacitance at the time of half capacitance (Figures 3B and 3C). Quantitative analysis of relative barrier resistance at 10 h revealed a significant decrease in NCKAP1 knockdown ECs (Figures 3D and 3E), indicating an impaired barrier formation. Visualization of VE-cadherin and CD31 showed that while control ECs exhibited continuous and robust AJs, NCKAP1-deficient ECs displayed disrupted AJs (Figure 3F). This phenotype indicates that NCKAP1 deficiency compromises barrier formation efficiency as a result of impaired lamellipodia formation.

We also examined AJ status and found that NCKAP1 knockdown did not alter VE-cadherin or CD31 mRNA or protein levels, nor did it affect VE-cadherin membrane localization (Figures S3A–S3E). These results indicate that the delayed barrier formation is unlikely to result from defects in AJ components.

Endothelial barrier integrity remains intact in NCKAP1-deficient monolayers, accompanied by linear vinculin-positive intercellular junctions

To assess barrier function after AJs were fully established, ECs were seeded at a higher density or cultured for an extended period to form confluent monolayers. ECIS measurements of barrier resistance in pre-established monolayers revealed no significant difference between control and NCKAP1-deficient cells (Figure 4A). This finding was further confirmed by an FITC-dextran permeability assay, which showed comparable permeability across all confluent monolayers (Figure 4B), indicating that barrier integrity is preserved in the long term. IF analysis of VE-cadherin demonstrated that NCKAP1-depleted ECs formed mature, continuous linear AJs 48 h post-seeding (Figure 4C). Meanwhile, vinculin localized along these linear intercellular junctions in both control and NCKAP1-deficient monolayers (Figures 4C and 4D). PLA result showed the co-localization between VE-cadherin and vinculin was comparable across control and NCKAP1-deficient monolayers (Figures 4E and 4F). These data suggest that vinculin localized at linear AJs plays a stabilizing role and likely contributes to maintaining endothelial barrier function.

To further assess whether the preserved barrier integrity arises from altered cell-substrate interactions, we applied ECIS modeling, which partitions total impedance into a cell-cell adhesion component (Rb) and a cell-matrix adhesion parameter (Alpha) (Figures S4A–S4D).49 Notably, Alpha values were consistently elevated in NCKAP1-depleted monolayers (Figure S4C), suggesting strengthened cell-substrate interaction.

MTSS1 is upregulated, and Arp3 localization at intercellular junctions is reduced in NCKAP1-depleted endothelial monolayers

Metastasis-suppressor 1 (MTSS1) is a membrane-interacting protein that has been reported to maintain cell-cell junctions associated with WAVE2-Arp2/3 in epithelial cells.50,51 To find how MTSS1 been influenced by NCKAP1 knockdown, we examined MTSS1 and Arp3 localization in confluent monolayers cultured for 48 h and found opposite changes between them (Figure 5A). IF images revealed a marked reduction of Arp3 at AJs in NCKAP1-depleted cells, indicating suppressed actin dynamics at cell-cell contacts, and AJ remodeling might be compromised. Interestingly, increased expression and junctional localization of MTSS1 were clearly observed by IF staining. Further western blot analysis demonstrated upregulation of MTSS1 upon NCKAP1 knockdown (Figures 5B and 5C), which was attenuated by VEGF stimulation (Figures 5C and 5D). Accumulation of MTSS1 at AJs may therefore contribute to the preserved barrier integrity in NCKAP1-knockdown monolayers.

In summary, even though barrier integrity and function are preserved, which may be achieved through vinculin and MTSS1 accumulation at mature AJs in the long-term, NCKAP1 depletion inhibits actin dynamics at cell-cell contacts and strengthens cell-matrix adhesion, suggesting a more rigid junctional phenotype in NCKAP1-deficient endothelial monolayers.

NCKAP1 knockdown restrains endothelial angiogenesis

To evaluate whether the hypothesized rigid junctions affect endothelial angiogenic capacity, we utilized tube formation on Matrigel surface (2D) and 3D bead sprouting assays. Time-course imaging revealed that NCKAP1 deficiency delayed tube formation during the early phase (2 and 6 h) (Figure 6A), accompanied by the persistence of thick junctional plaques. Quantitative analysis of nodes with 3–5 branches showed that NCKAP1 depletion impaired early tube formation (Figure 6B), whereas only siRNA 2 resulted in a sustained reduction in tubular nodes (three branches) at the 24-h endpoint (Figure S5A). In the 3D sprouting assay, HUVEC-coated microcarrier beads were embedded within a fibrin matrix to permit radial sprout formation (Figure 6C). Quantification of the sprouting area surrounding each bead showed that NCKAP1 depletion markedly suppressed sprout formation up to 96 h (Figure 6D), indicating a reduced endothelial angiogenic capacity. Sustained knockdown over this period was confirmed by western blotting (Figures S5B and S5C). This phenotype was recapitulated in human aortic ECs (Figures S5D and S5E), underscoring a conserved role for NCKAP1 in regulating 3D angiogenesis across vascular ECs.

NCKAP1 depletion impairs VEGF-induced focal adhesion activation

Due to the suppressed actin dynamics in endothelial monolayers upon NCKAP1 knockdown, we wondered whether it would affect the angiogenic processes from quiescent ECs. To study this, we applied VEGF as an angiogenic factor to stimulate ECs. First, we found phosphorylated myosin light chain (pMLC) levels changed obviously from normal cultivation conditions to harsher conditions (starvation and VEGF-treatment). In growth medium, NCKAP1 depletion resulted in an increased tendency of pMLC levels, which were suppressed when cells were starved or stimulated with VEGF (Figures 7A, 7B, and S6A), indicating NCKAP1 is critical for actin remodeling and cellular contractility in response to environmental changes. Then we investigated FA activation by assessing phosphorylation levels of FAK and paxillin. Western blot results showed that FAK was activated significantly at 30 min compared to the non-stimulated condition, and NCKAP1-depletion impairs its phosphorylation. The same was seen for paxillin (Figures 7C and 7D). These inhibitory effects of FAK had already appeared in NCKAP1-depleted ECs with VEGF-treatment for 15 min, indicating FAK is the kinase to phosphorylate paxillin (Figures S6B and S6C). These results indicate that endothelial monolayers exhibited impaired responsiveness to external angiogenic cues due to reduced dynamic actin and impaired FA activation in NCKAP1-deficient ECs.

Discussion

WRC is the principal activator of the Arp2/3 complex for branched actin network formation, and plays vital roles in membrane protrusion-dependent cellular processes, including cell adhesion, migration, and invasion across various cell types.7,8,12,13,14 It is known that Arp2/3-mediated actin remodeling is essential in maintaining endothelial barrier integrity and in angiogenic sprouting.42,43,44 However, the specific contribution of WRC and FA regulation in these processes is not understood. Notably, current human genetics resources report no annotated clinical phenotypes arising from NCKAP1 mutations, indicating that potential vascular or barrier-related consequences remain unexplored. In this study, we identified NCKAP1 as a critical regulator of FAs coordinating endothelial barrier integrity and sprouting angiogenesis. In line with previous reports that NCKAP1 depletion destabilizes the WRC,12,14 we also found a marked reduction in WAVE2 expression following NCKAP1 knockdown in ECs (Figures 1A, 1B, and S1B). Similar morphological changes of (i) FA with suppressed paxillin phosphorylation as well as (ii) VE-cadherin structures during the AJ establishment process were exhibited in WAVE2 or CYFIP1 knockdown ECs (Figure S7). These findings support that NCKAP1 modulates endothelial adhesion and actin organization primarily through WRC-dependent mechanism. Additionally, to exclude the compensatory effects from WAVE paralog N-WASP in ECs, we detected endogenous expression of N-WASP upon NCKAP1 knockdown and found its protein levels slightly accumulated (Figures S1D and S1E). Previous studies have shown that N-WASP primarily promotes filopodia formation via the Arp2/3 complex.52,53 Consistent with this, NCKAP1-depleted ECs displayed pronounced filopodia at the cell periphery, replacing typical lamellipodia structures (Figure 1C). Notably, Arp3 was absent from filopodia (Figure S1C), suggesting that N-WASP cannot substitute for WRC in proper protrusion formation,12 but may operate in distinct actin-dependent pathways under WRC deficiency conditions.54

As WRC-Arp2/3-mediated branched actin assembly is of great importance in the protrusion of migrating cells, we investigated FA numbers, morphology, and turnover by staining FA components. We demonstrate that NCKAP1 depletion hampers FA formation and turnover, while not affecting the expression of other components or kinase activity. Elongated FA morphology is associated with low turnover rate,12,13,14,55 supporting our findings in NCKAP1-deficient ECs. Although NCKAP1-deficient ECs did not exhibit overt defects in wound-closure migration (Figures S5F and S5G), this behavior may reflect compensatory protrusive mechanisms. In particular, N-WASP-driven filopodia or formins-mediated linear actin bundles at FAs may partially sustain motility in the absence of WRC.13,56,57,58 Besides, the different coating matrix may also influence the readouts in 2D migration.12 However, we observed that cell spreading, which primarily relies on new FA formation, was delayed in NCKAP1-deficient ECs (Figure 3B), further leading to the barrier formation retardation evidenced by ECIS measuring in real time (Figure 3C). By visualizing the structure of AJs at the earlier stage of barrier formation (24 h post-seeding), NCKAP1-depleted ECs predominantly utilize actin fibers to establish cell-cell contacts, consistent with discontinuous AJ morphology (Figure 3F). In contrast, lamellipodia-driven cell-cell adhesion is more effective and accompanied by branched actin networks that support AJs. Given that phosphorylation of VE-cadherin-based AJ complex, including phosphorylation at Y685 of VE-cadherin,33 Y142 of β-catenin,59 and S721 of vinculin,60 can drive AJ disassembly by disrupting the VE-cadherin-β-catenin complex,60 one potential explanation for impaired barrier recovery would be altered VE-cadherin regulation. However, NCKAP1 depletion did not change VE-cadherin or CD31 expression, membrane localization, or phosphorylation of VE-cadherin (Figure S3), indicating that the delayed barrier establishment does not stem from VE-cadherin phosphorylation-mediated internalization.25,33

Once the endothelial barrier is fully established (48 h post-seeding), barrier integrity remains unaffected by NCKAP1 deficiency (Figures 4A and 4B). Despite NCKAP1 depletion not affecting VE-cadherin statues, reduced Arp3 at cell-cell contacts suppresses VE-cadherin-based AJs remodeling.41 In this study, we have proposed two possible mechanisms for the preserved barrier integrity and function. One possible explanation could be that MTSS1 was increased and localized to AJs (Figures 5A–5C), suggesting a possible compensatory role in maintaining long-term barrier integrity in NCKAP1-depleted endothelial monolayers. The upregulated MTSS1 was restored upon VEGF treatment, which might reflect a disruption of barrier integrity induced by VEGF. MTSS1 has been reported to maintain epithelial barrier integrity by promoting lamellipodial protrusions dynamics with WAVE2-Arp2/3 complex.50 However, it was reported to negatively regulate barrier integrity in ECs.61 In our NCKAP1-depleted ECs, we found a reduction of Arp3 at AJs, but increased junctional localization and protein levels of MTSS1, providing a potential new function of MTSS1 in barrier preservation independent of WAVE2-Arp3. Besides, MTSS1 was identified as a fast-turnover protein,61 and its accumulation upon NCKAP1 depletion might indicate a degradation mechanism directly or indirectly relying on NCKAP1 and/or VEGF. Overall, the interaction between WRC-Arp2/3 and MTSS1 in barrier maintenance remains largely unknown and worth exploring further.

Another factor that may account for this phenomenon is the comparable recruitment of vinculin to VE-cadherin-positive cell-cell junctions (Figures 4C, 4E, and 4F). Although vinculin recruitment to cell-cell junctions is classically considered a force-dependent event that gives rise to FAJ,62 NCKAP1-deficient endothelial monolayers instead displayed continuous, linear VE-cadherin junctions accompanied by junctional vinculin (Figures 4C and 4D). This pattern suggests that vinculin functions here are reinforcing mature AJs and maintaining barrier stability, consistent with ECIS resistance measurements and dextran permeability assay (Figures 4A and 4B). Indeed, vinculin has been reported to play dual roles at endothelial junctions, either marking tension-dependent FAJs or stabilizing linear AJs under barrier-protective conditions.63,64 In contrast to the classically described role of vinculin at force-induced FAJs, vinculin in NCKAP1-deficient endothelial monolayers is distributed along continuous, linear junctions, where it supports junctional stabilization rather than contractility-driven junctional disruption.64

Fully established linear AJ in NCKAP1-deficient endothelial monolayers restore barrier integrity, but the accompanying accumulation of vinculin at cell-cell contacts seems to strengthen cell-substrate adhesion, as reflected by ECIS modeling (Figure S4C). As we demonstrated above, FA turnover was hampered by NCKAP1 knockdown, which means that once matured, FA becomes less dynamic. Indeed, reduction of Arp3 at junctions in confluent monolayer stainings revealed that actin dynamics were suppressed at cell-cell junctions (Figure S3A). As a consequence of this, the restrained cell adhesions suppress angiogenic capacities in both 2D Matrigel tube formation and 3D sprouting (Figure 6). In tube formation assay, the images showed that at the earliest time points, tube formation was inhibited, which is most likely due to the rigid cell-cell adhesion. Node analysis showed a shift from well-organized 3-branching nodes to higher-order (≥4 branch) nodes in NCKAP1 siRNA2-transfected ECs, which might indicate impaired maturation of the vascular network. Given that NCKAP1/WRC signaling has been implicated in Alzheimer’s disease, and abnormal angiogenesis is increasingly recognized as a contributing factor in Alzheimer’s pathogenesis.65 These findings raise the possibility that NCKAP1/WRC defects in angiogenesis may be mechanistically relevant to disease progression and warrant further investigation.

Furthermore, when stimulated with angiogenic factor VEGF, lamellipodia-dependent FA activation is impaired in NCKAP1-depleted ECs (Figures 7C and 7D). In turn, impaired FA maturation under VEGF stimulation further limits stress fiber formation, which normally anchors to mature FAs.34 However, reduced cellular tension preceded the loss of FA phosphorylation in NCKAP1-deficient ECs under VEGF stimulation (Figure 7B), indicating that NCKAP1 regulates intrinsic tension homeostasis, with its effects manifesting differently depending on the environmental context.

Rho GTPases play distinct yet complementary roles in regulating endothelial adhesion dynamics, barrier function, and angiogenesis. These activities are spatially coordinated by specific guanine nucleotide exchange factors, which activate overlapping downstream pathways to regulate cytoskeletal and adhesive dynamics.66 Rac1 is essential for VEGF-induced angiogenesis by promoting WRC-mediated lamellipodia formation and FA assembly.67 In contrast, phosphorylated MLC downstream of Rho-kinase contributes to junctional contractility, which supports basal barrier maintenance but can also drive junctional opening and barrier disruption under elevated actomyosin tension.68 Consistent with observations that quiescent endothelial tubes exhibit higher VE-cadherin-associated contractility than sprouting cells and reducing contractility facilitates Rac1-dependent sprouting,69,70 NCKAP1-deficient ECs showed mildly elevated pMLC under steady-state conditions, likely reflecting enhanced intercellular adhesion. Conversely, pMLC levels decreased upon starvation or VEGF stimulation, suggesting that NCKAP1 loss seems to promote a pro-angiogenic tension state. However, angiogenesis remained impaired, suggesting that proper GTPase signaling is insufficient without WRC-mediated actin nucleation. These findings underscore a coordinated requirement for GTPases and WRC-dependent actin assembly in vascular angiogenesis. Additionally, microtubules have been shown to regulate the balance between Rac1-and RhoA-driven cytoskeletal, which may contribute to the junctional phenotypes observed upon NCKAP1 depletion and warrant further investigation.71,72

In summary, our findings identify the WRC as a critical determinant of endothelial protrusive behavior by coordinating FA formation and turnover. This WRC-dependent actin program supports initial barrier establishment and enables junctional plasticity required for sprouting in angiogenic environments. These findings position WRC-dependent branched actin assembly as a central determinant of endothelial plasticity and a potential therapeutic target for vascular dysfunction and aberrant angiogenesis.

Limitations of the study

While we characterized the alterations in FAs and AJs upon NCKAP1 knockdown and provide evidence that WRC-dependent lamellipodial actin formation is important for endothelial maintenance, several mechanistic limitations remain. In particular, although our data support a role for NCKAP1 in coordinating FA activation and junctional remodeling, the causal relationships among lamellipodial actin dynamics, FA signaling, and AJ plasticity remain largely inferential. This limitation is especially relevant in the context of 3D angiogenic sprouting, where direct mechanistic links between impaired junctional plasticity and altered FA signaling remain unclear. Moreover, upstream signaling events, including potential changes in small GTPase activities during endothelial quiescence and VEGF-induced angiogenic responses, will require further investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture

HUVECs were isolated as previously described.73 Briefly, umbilical veins were rinsed with Hank’s solution to remove any remaining blood, filled with 0.2% collagenase type II, and incubated at 37°C for 15 min. ECs were washed out of the vein with two rinses of Hank’s solution, pelleted, resuspended, and cultured in coated flasks in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. HAoECs were purchased from Cell Applications. ECs were subcultured on 0.1% w/v gelatin (Greiner Bio-One)/PBS-coated cell culture vessels between passages 2 to 5. HUVECs were cultivated at 5% CO2, 37°C in M199 medium (Sigma-Aldrich; with Earle’s salts and NaHCO3), supplemented with 20% fetal calf serum (Biochrom), 50 μg/mL endothelial cell growth factor (Corning), 25 μg/mL heparin (Sigma-Aldrich), 2 mM L-glutamine (PAN-Biotech), 100 U/mL penicillin (PAN-Biotech), and 0.1% mg/mL streptomycin (PAN-Biotech), referred as growth medium. HAoECs were cultured in endothelial cell growth medium 2 (Promocell) supplemented with 2% FCS. All cells were tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Method details

siRNA transfection

Transfection was conducted with Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to supplier protocols. 3–3.5×105 cells/well were seeded in a 6-well plate and cultured overnight. On the following day, cells were transfected with 100 nM siRNA. 6 h later, the transfection medium was replaced with growth medium for further cultivation and experiments. The scrambled siRNA (si-scr) was transfected in parallel as control.

VEGF165 stimulation

HUVECs were washed with PBS and then starved in M199 medium supplied with 100 U/mL penicillin and 0.1% mg/mL streptomycin (starvation medium) for 3 h. Recombinant human VEGF165 (PeproTech) was used at a concentration of 50 ng/mL for the indicated time periods. At the end of VEGF165 stimulation, cells were quickly washed with ice-cold PBS and then lysed or fixed for the subsequent steps.

Immunofluorescence staining

Following overnight incubation post-transfection, cells were digested with trypsin and re-seeded in pre-coated μ-Slide VI 0.4 (ibidi GmbH) at a density of 2×106 cells/mL. Cells were kept in the slide for 1–2 days with daily medium exchange. Then, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS (with Ca2+/Mg2+) and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min at room temperature (RT). Next, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min and blocked with 3% BSA and 5% goat serum in PBS for 1 h at RT. Cells were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Primary antibodies were diluted in the blocking buffer. The next day, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies, mixed with Phalloidin and DAPI. Afterward, cells were washed in PBS and mounted in ibidi mounting medium (ibidi). Images were captured on a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope equipped with a 405-nm diode laser, 488-nm argon laser, 561-nm diode laser, and 633 nm HeNe laser using a 63× (63×/1.40 HC PL APO Oil CS2 WD 0.14 mm) objective.

Paxillin/pPaxillin staining was quantified using self-created scripts in FIJI. Briefly, pPaxillin images were first filtered with median radius = 1 and background subtraction rolling = 5 sliding to eliminate the noise. Then, the threshold was set using the “IsoData” method and manually adjusted. Analyze-Particles function was used to quantify Paxillin or pPaxillin signals by adjusting proper size. The parameters “area,” “mean gray value,” “Centroid,” and “shape descriptors” were measured and summarized.

Western blotting

Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection. For this, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100 (v/v), 1% IGEPAL(v/v), 0.1% SDS), and stored at −20°C. Before electrophoresis, cell lysates were supplemented with Laemmli buffer76 and boiled at 95°C for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein lysates were loaded on SDS gels. The proteins in gels were then transferred onto PVDF membrane (neoLab Migge, Heidelberg, Germany) at 100V for 120 min. Then, the membranes were blocked with 5% w/v BSA (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.1% TBST for 1 h at RT and following incubated with primary antibodies in 3% BSA/TBST overnight at 4°C. On the second day, membranes were washed with 0.1% TBST three times, incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. Western Bright Quantum ECL HRP substrate was added onto membranes, and signals were obtained using a Fusion-FX7 (Vilber Lourmat, Eberhardzell, Germany).

Quantitative real-time PCR

At 48 h post-transfection, cells were harvested and lysed for RNA isolation using Macherey-Nagel kits according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 1 μg RNA was reversely transcribed to cDNA using M-MuLV reverse transcriptase (200,000 U/mL, New England Biolabs). RT-qPCR was conducted with 2.5 ng cDNA, specifically designed primers (100 μM), and Luna PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs), then measured in time with StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The results were shown as relative fold induction calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method relative to the housekeeping gene RSP9.

Surface biotinylation assay

Cell surface biotinylation was performed in 6-well plates at 48 h post-transfection. The entire procedure was performed on ice or at 4°C. First, cells were washed twice with ice-cold 10 mM MgCl2/PBS and incubated with 1 mL 0.5 mg/mL biotin solution (in MgCl2/PBS) for 50 min with light agitation. In parallel, 1 mL MgCl2/PBS was added to control cells. Then, biotinylation was quenched with 1 mL 50 mM Tris pH8 for 10 min. Subsequently, cells were scraped and lysed in 400 μL of RIPA light buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% IGEPAL (v/v), 0.1% SDS) supplemented with PMSF (1 mM), NaVO4 (2 mM), NaF (50 mM), NaPP (20 mM), and proteinase inhibitor (Roche) for 20 min on a rotation wheel, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 10 min. Afterward, 40 μL supernatant was taken out as total cell lysate, and the remaining lysate was incubated with streptavidin beads (GE Healthcare) overnight on a rotation wheel. The following day, beads were washed 3 times with RIPA light buffer and boiled in 40 μL 2x Laemmli buffer. The total lysate and streptavidin pulled-down samples were then subjected to western blot.

Proximity ligation assay

PLA was performed using Duolink In Situ Ligation kit (Sigma-Aldrich) as described previously.75 In brief, cells were seeded into μ-Slide VI 0.4 as described in IF staining. After permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 in TBST, cells were blocked with blocking buffer for 1 h at RT. Next, cells were incubated overnight with the respective primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used for PLA were listed for IF staining above, but at twice the concentration. For control purposes, a single primary antibody was used and subjected to the same experimental procedure. On the following day, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to PLA probes. Subsequently, ligase was added to facilitate circular DNA formation when target proteins were in proximity (<40 nm). The reaction was amplified via rolling circle amplification, producing detectable oligo-DNA signals. Finally, samples were mounted using ibidi mounting medium (ibidi), and images were captured on a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope equipped with a 405-nm diode laser, 488-nm argon laser, and 561-nm diode laser using a 40x (40x/1.30 HC PL APO Oil CS2 WD 0.24 mm) objective.

PLA signals were quantified using a FIJI macro script with modified thresholds based on previously published methods.75

Matrigel tube formation assay

At 48 h post-transfection, cells were digested from 6-well plate using Accutase (PAN-Biotech). Matrigel (Corning) was placed into a 96-well plate according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated at 37°C for 30 min for polymerization. 104 cells in 100 μL growth medium were placed on Matrigel and cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 to form tubes. Images were captured with an inverted epifluorescence microscope (Zeiss) using a 5x objective at 2, 6, and 24 h, respectively. Nodes were quantified as branching points connecting 3, 4, or more than 5 tubular segments.

3D fibrin bead sprouting assay

The 3D fibrin bead assay was performed as described before.77 EBM2 growth medium was used for both HUVECs and HAoECs in this assay. Briefly, on the following day post-transfection, cells were detached with trypsin and incubated with 1000 Cytodex 3 microcarrier beads (GE Healthcare) with intermittent rotation in the incubator for 4 h. EC-covered beads were then transferred to a 6 cm dish and incubated in growth medium overnight to allow EC to spread and adequately adhere to the bead surface. The next day, cell-covered beads were washed gently and resuspended in 200 μL EBM2 growth medium. Then, 90 μL 2.5 mg/mL Fibrinogen (Sigma-Aldrich) solution containing 0.15 U/mL Aprotinin (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10 μL ECs suspension were mixed with 50U/mL Thrombin (Sigma-Aldrich) and distributed to a 96-well plate. Leave the plate for 5–10 min for Fibrinogen polymerization, then transfer to the incubator for a further 30 min. EBM2 growth medium was added to the Fibrin gel matrix and cultivated with daily medium exchange. EC sprouts were captured with an inverted epifluorescence microscope (Zeiss) using a 5x objective. The quantitative results were shown by comparing the area of the sprout relative to the bead area at the indicated time points using FIJI software.

Electric cell-substrate impedance sensing measurements

Cell impedance was measured using an ECIS Zθ system (Applied BioPhysics) according to published protocol.48 The 8W10E array (ibidi) array was employed for ECIS measurements, which was treated with L-lysine and coated with 0.1% gelatin, followed by ECIS destabilization by ECIS until stable impedance was achieved before plating cells. For the spreading and barrier formation assay, cell-free arrays with 300 μL growth medium/well were measured for 30 min to get the stable condition. Then, cells were digested with Accutase (PAN-Biotech), and 5×104 cells in 100 μL growth medium were added into respective wells. For pre-formed monolayer resistance measurement, 1×105 cells were seeded into a pre-treated array with 400 μL growth medium and cultivated in an incubator overnight. The following day, the assay was installed into the ECIS device for measurement. Data was recorded in real time with multi-frequencies 1000, 4000, 16000, and 64000 Hz. The modeling analysis was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

FITC-dextran permeability assay

Following overnight cultivation post-transfection, cells were digested with trypsin, and 1.5×105 cells were reseeded into pre-coated cell culture inserts with a pore size of 0.4 μm (Falcon, Corning). Cells were maintained in phenol red–free growth medium for two days to allow monolayer formation, with daily medium replacement. For the permeability assay, 250 μL of phenol red–free medium containing 1 mg/mL FITC-dextran (70 kDa; Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the upper chamber of each insert, and 500 μL of phenol red-free medium was added to the lower chamber. After 6 h of incubation at 37°C, 100 μL of medium was collected from the lower chamber in triplicate for each condition, and fluorescence intensity was measured using a plate reader (excitation/emission: 485/535 nm).

Gap closure assay

Cells were seeded into 2-well inserts (ibidi) 24 h post-transfection. After overnight cultivation, cells were treated with 5 μg/mL mitomycin for 2 h at 37°C. Then, the inserts were removed, and cells were gently washed with PBS 3 times and replaced in M199 growth medium. Cell migration was recorded every hour at 37°C and 5% CO2 environment with an inverted epifluorescence microscope (Zeiss) using a 5x objective.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Experiments were conducted using a pool of HUVECs that was mixed by three independent biological donors. Each experiment included at least two independent technical replicates using mixed HUVECs pool. Statistical analyses and data visualization were conducted in GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.0, Windows). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two tailed t-tests. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc test was applied; two-way ANOVA was used for analyses involving two independent variables, followed by Sidák’s multiple-comparison test. Statistical significance is indicated by asterisks in the figures (∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).
