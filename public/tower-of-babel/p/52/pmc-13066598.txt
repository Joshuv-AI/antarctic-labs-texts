Design of a Li-ion battery cooling system incorporating PCM, heat pipes, and liquid circuits using marine predator algorithm-enhanced ANN and multi-verse optimization
Authors: Naim Ben Ali, Borhen Louhichi, Waqed H. Hassan, As’ad Alizadeh, Abdullah Abed Hussein, Walid Aich, Khalil Hajlaoui, Sam Aminian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Managing heat generation in lithium-ion batteries remains a major obstacle, as excessive temperatures and non-uniform thermal distribution can significantly undermine safety, operational efficiency, and service life. To mitigate these challenges, this research introduces a structured hybrid methodology for advancing battery thermal management systems (BTMS). The approach integrates machine learning–driven forecasting, optimization techniques, and multi-criteria evaluation within a four-phase framework: preprocessing of data, predictive modeling through hybrid learning algorithms, optimization of design variables, and systematic decision analysis. Two hybrid predictive schemes were constructed, GA-MLPNN and MPA-MLPNN, to estimate critical thermal and performance indices. Comparative assessment revealed that MPA-MLPNN exhibited the highest precision in forecasting temperature differentials (ΔT, R > 0.9985), whereas GA-MLPNN delivered superior predictive reliability for peak temperature (Tmax, R > 0.9986) and energy density (ED, R > 0.999). Multi-objective optimization using the MOMVO algorithm illuminated inherent trade-offs among heat distribution, maximum temperature reduction, and energy storage capacity. For instance, adopting thicker MHPA layers (~ 3.8–3.9 mm) and taller configurations (~ 18–25 mm) effectively reduced hotspots, producing ΔT values of ~ 1.8–2.5 °C and Tmax of ~ 38–39 °C, though at the expense of lower ED (~ 139–144 Wh/kg). Conversely, thinner designs (~ 2.6–2.8 mm) elevated energy density (~ 157 Wh/kg) but impaired thermal stability. Intermediate geometries offered more balanced outcomes, with spacing analyses confirming geometry as a decisive factor in thermal regulation. Finally, multi-criteria evaluation through the CoCoSo method translated Pareto-optimal fronts into practical design recommendations, demonstrating adaptability across diverse operational priorities, including safety-focused, high-capacity, and compromise-oriented battery applications.

Introduction

The accelerating shift toward sustainable mobility has positioned hybrid and electric vehicles (HEVs and EVs) at the forefront of modern energy solutions, primarily due to their ability to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and dependence on fossil fuels1. At the core of these vehicles lies the lithium-ion battery (LIB), a high energy-density storage technology that plays a decisive role in supporting several United Nations SDGs, including SDG 7, SDG 11, and SDG 132,3. However, while LIBs deliver superior performance compared to conventional storage devices, their operation is strongly influenced by thermal conditions4. Heat generation during charging and discharging cycles, combined with external environmental factors, often leads to uneven temperature distribution within battery packs5. This thermal imbalance can reduce electrochemical efficiency, accelerate material degradation, shorten service life, and, in extreme cases, compromise safety through thermal runaway events6. Consequently, the implementation of efficient thermal management strategies is indispensable for ensuring reliability and longevity of EV battery systems7. Broadly, thermal management systems (TMS) for LIBs can be categorized into air cooling8, liquid cooling9, phase-change material (PCM)-based approaches10, and hybrid configurations11. Although air-based cooling provides a low-cost and straightforward solution, its ability to dissipate heat is restricted; in contrast, liquid-based cooling ensures better temperature uniformity and higher efficiency, albeit with greater system complexity and increased power demand12. PCM-based designs provide passive temperature regulation through latent heat absorption but require integration with auxiliary methods to overcome limited thermal conductivity13. Recently, hybrid systems that couple active and passive mechanisms have gained prominence as they offer a balanced trade-off between efficiency, cost, and safety14. In the context of electrified transportation, the optimization of TMS has become critical not only for maximizing battery performance but also for aligning EV technologies with broader global sustainability targets.

A wide range of studies has explored different approaches for improving the thermal regulation of Li-ion batteries, emphasizing the reduction of maximum temperatures, the achievement of more uniform temperature distribution, and the enhancement of overall cooling performance. Sarchami et al.15 experimentally developed a liquid cooling system with wavy/stair channels and a copper sheath, showing that higher alumina nanoparticle concentration and increased inflow velocity significantly lowered maximum temperature and non-uniformity, with stair channels reducing peak temperature by 3.59 K and non-uniformity by 0.65 K under a 5 C discharge. Similarly, Kiani et al.16 proposed a hybrid system combining metal foam–PCM, nanofluid, and a heat sink under a magnetic field, achieving a maximum temperature reduction of 7.5 °C, extending working time by 179% at Re = 1250, and maintaining temperature differences below 4 °C.

Heat pipe–based approaches have also been widely explored. Mbulu et al.17 demonstrated that the use of water-cooled L- and I-configured heat pipes enabled effective heat transfer, removing more than 92% of the produced thermal load, while keeping the peak temperature under 55 °C and restricting the temperature gradient to within 5 °C. Alihosseini and Shafaee18 further confirmed that heat pipes, combined with forced convection, moderated surface temperature rises, kept temperatures below 40 °C, and improved uniformity across consecutive cycles. Zhang and Wei19 reported that employing flat heat pipes led to lower peak temperatures and smaller thermal gradients relative to air convection and aluminum plate cooling, achieving these improvements with only negligible energy consumption. Extending this concept, Lei et al.20 integrated sintered copper-powder heat pipes with a water spray mechanism, achieving a reduction of 29.2 °C in peak temperature and 8.0 °C in temperature difference under high discharge currents.

Alternative compact cooling designs have also proven effective. Yi et al.21 developed an ultra-thin vapor chamber BTMS, which reduced maximum temperature by up to 25.5% at a 2 C discharge rate and maintained temperature differences below 2–3 °C, identifying an optimal filling rate of 120%. Beyond component-level systems, Ma et al.22 implemented a vehicle-integrated system coordinating battery packs, motors, power electronics, and air conditioning, maintaining optimal operating temperatures across all components under real-time conditions. Forthermore, Shen et al.23 developed a digital twin–based thermal model for cell-to-pack battery systems, accurately predicting temperature responses under fast charging and cooling with errors within ± 15%, demonstrating the value of modeling for design and optimization of high-energy-density packs. These studies highlight the effectiveness of nanofluid cooling, heat pipes, vapor chambers, hybrid systems, and integrated modeling approaches in enhancing battery thermal management, providing practical guidance for both experimental and vehicle-level implementations.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have become powerful approaches for enhancing the thermal regulation of Li-ion batteries, providing performance and optimization capabilities that exceed those of traditional modeling and control techniques24. In the context of battery TMS, the primary challenge lies in maintaining the battery within its optimal temperature range while minimizing energy consumption and ensuring uniform thermal distribution across cells, particularly under high charge/discharge rates and variable environmental conditions25. AI and ML techniques are crucial in this domain because they can analyze large volumes of operational data, identify complex nonlinear relationships between battery parameters, and predict temperature evolution with high accuracy26. These predictive capabilities allow BTMS to respond proactively, rather than reactively, to changes in load, ambient temperature, or battery state of charge, thereby preventing overheating, thermal runaway, or uneven aging of cells27. Additionally, ML models, such as neural networks, support vector machines, and ensemble learning approaches, can be integrated with real-time monitoring systems to provide continuous state estimation and anomaly detection, enhancing system reliability28. Optimization algorithms driven by AI, including genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimization, and reinforcement learning, enable the fine-tuning of cooling strategies, flow rates, and phase-change material usage to achieve minimal temperature gradients and reduced peak temperatures while conserving energy29. By continuously learning from historical and real-time battery behavior, AI and ML frameworks can adapt to battery aging and performance degradation, ensuring long-term efficiency and safety30.

In recent years, numerous studies have investigated the development of AI and ML methods to optimize and control BTMS for Li-ion batteries. Liu et al.31 developed a hybrid BTMS combining PCM, air and liquid cooling, optimized via response surface methodology (RSM) for fin height, gap length, and PCM thickness. They further implemented an ANN model based on TCN-BiLSTM to estimate battery temperatures in real time, achieving reductions in energy consumption by 20.87%, maximum temperature by 2.52 K, and temperature variation by 0.05 K, highlighting the effectiveness of AI-driven control. Similarly, Cheng et al.32 employed deep reinforcement learning (DRL) to optimize a PCM-based BTMS with dual central water channels, demonstrating decreases in maximum battery temperature by 0.33 °C, average PCM temperature by 0.3 °C, and improved temperature uniformity relative to classical optimization methods, confirming DRL’s potential for precise BTMS optimization. Afzal et al.33 investigated various coolants and battery spacings, using finite-volume numerical simulations validated against literature data and an ANFIS model to predict the average Nusselt number, achieving R² values of 0.98 and 0.985. Their study emphasized the influence of coolant type and Prandtl number, demonstrating the capability of ML-based predictive models for evaluating thermal performance. Ahmad et al.34 applied an ANN trained on 8,080 simulation cases to optimize dielectric liquid immersion cooling for large-format cells, reducing peak temperature by 7.7% and temperature difference by 86% while maintaining pumping power, highlighting ML’s role in immersion cooling optimization. Khaboshan et al.35 combined PCM, metal foam, and fins to reduce battery temperature by 3 K and temperature differences by up to 75%, with ANN predictions achieving R² of 0.98–0.99 for PCM fraction and battery temperature, highlighting AI’s predictive capability. Further, Zhou et al.36 utilized a Gaussian process surrogate model to optimize 18,650 battery modules with curved cooling channels, improving energy efficiency by 126% while maintaining battery temperatures below 28.4 °C and coolant pressure drops under 3.6 kPa. Gu et al.37 applied the Black Kite Algorithm (BKA) and a BKA-CNN-Self Attention model to optimize air and PCM cooling for electric motorcycles, attaining a remarkably low prediction error of just 0.45 °C. Zhang et al.38 combined PCM with air cooling using Cantor fractal fins and segmented PCM blocks, reducing ΔTmax by up to 21.2% under 4 C discharge and accurately predicting thermal behavior via a backpropagation neural network. Xu et al.39 integrated ResNet-50 ANNs with fluid dynamics to optimize marine battery BTMS, reducing maximum temperature by 10.63 K, temperature difference by 9.41 K, and sound pressure level by 4.6 dB. Finally, Wang et al.40 demonstrated that a PSO-SVM model with NSGA-II optimization can balance heat dissipation and PCM mass, achieving high prediction accuracy (R² = 0.99478 for Tmax and 0.9787 for ΔTmax) and reducing PCM mass by up to 55.3% while maintaining adequate thermal management. Together, these studies illustrate the remarkable role of AI and ML in enhancing the design, optimization, and predictive capabilities of modern BTMS, enabling improved thermal regulation, energy efficiency, and system lightweighting.

Despite the demonstrated effectiveness of AI-driven and hybrid optimization frameworks in BTMS design, several inherent limitations remain insufficiently discussed in existing studies. Most integrated frameworks that combine ML-based surrogates with evolutionary optimization focus primarily on predictive accuracy and thermal performance improvement, while often overlooking issues of data scarcity, model interpretability, and robustness under real-world uncertainties. In particular, many ML–optimization pipelines rely on large, problem-specific datasets or assume idealized operating conditions, limiting their transferability to practical engineering scenarios. Furthermore, the coupling of multi-objective optimization with decision-making methods is frequently treated as a post-processing step, without systematic evaluation of how decision criteria weights influence design robustness or engineering feasibility. Computational burden is another common challenge, as hybrid frameworks may become prohibitively expensive when high-fidelity simulations, complex ML architectures, and large population-based optimizers are combined. These limitations highlight the need for structured, data-efficient frameworks that can operate reliably under limited data availability, explicitly manage trade-offs among conflicting objectives, and provide transparent decision-support mechanisms. The present study addresses these gaps by proposing a modular and scalable hybrid framework that emphasizes data efficiency, controlled model complexity, and explicit multi-criteria decision-making, thereby enhancing practical applicability while maintaining optimization rigor.

Accordingly, this study proposes a comprehensive hybrid framework for BTMS enhancement, comprising four main steps: CFD data analysis, ML-based prediction, multi-objective optimization, and decision-making. Two hybrid ML models, genetic algorithm–optimized multi-layer perceptron neural network (GA-MLPNN) and marine predator algorithm–enhanced MLPNN (MPA-MLPNN), are developed to accurately predict critical thermal and performance parameters of Li-ion batteries. The predictions are then used as inputs for the recently developed multi-objective multi-verse optimization (MOMVO) algorithm, which efficiently identifies optimal trade-offs between maximum temperature, thermal uniformity, and energy density. Finally, the combined compromise solution (CoCoSo) method is applied to select the most suitable design based on weighted priorities for each objective. This integrated approach offers a systematic, data-driven, and practical methodology for enhancing BTMS performance, bridging existing research gaps, and providing a roadmap for future optimization studies (Fig. 1).

Data analysis

Hybrid Li-ion TMS

Data from Xie et al.41 were used in this study as a case example to verify the novel hybrid framework. Their research focused on the development of an advanced hybrid BTMS that combined PCM, a micro heat pipe array (MHPA), and liquid cooling. The central aim of their work was to address the inherent trade-off between effective heat dissipation and the preservation of energy density in Li-ion batteries, particularly under demanding operating conditions. To achieve this, they integrated both experimental investigation and numerical simulation in order to evaluate the thermal performance of the proposed system and to establish a framework for optimizing its structural design.

The methodology employed by Xie et al.41 was multi-layered. At the experimental level, a test platform was constructed with thermocouples and a battery management system to monitor the thermal response of battery modules at discharge rates of 1 C, 1.5 C, and 2 C. The cooling effectiveness was assessed through two key parameters: the maximum cell temperature and the temperature gradient across the battery pack. In parallel, a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model was developed to replicate the thermal behavior of the hybrid BTMS. This model was validated against the experimental data, demonstrating a strong correlation that confirmed the accuracy and reliability of the simulations. With this foundation, the study advanced to a parametric investigation using the Box–Behnken design (BBD), enabling the systematic exploration of critical structural variables and their influence on thermal performance and energy density.

The structural parameters under investigation included the spacing between cells in the X and Y directions (XB and YB), the height of the MHPA (HHP), and the thickness of the MHPA (WHP). Each parameter was varied within a defined range (20–28 mm for XB, 23–29 mm for YB, 30–60 mm for HHP, and 2–4 mm for WHP). The design variable ranges were selected based on physical feasibility, packaging constraints, and thermal–energetic trade-offs reported in Xie et al.41. These bounds consider realistic battery module dimensions, sufficient heat dissipation capability, mechanical robustness of MHPA structures, and acceptable energy density for practical BTMS applications. The outputs of interest were the temperature difference (ΔT), the maximum temperature (Tmax), and the overall energy density (ED). To reduce computational complexity, RSM was employed to build a surrogate model linking these design factors to the performance indicators.

The findings highlighted the effectiveness of the hybrid configuration in improving both safety and efficiency. Compared with a PCM-only module, the hybrid BTMS reduced ΔT from 6.9 °C to 3.9 °C and lowered Tmax by 13.78%, from 46.0 °C to 39.8 °C, during high-rate (2 C) discharging. Sensitivity analysis indicated that the spacing in the Y direction exerted the most pronounced influence on cooling capacity, whereas the MHPA-related parameters played a dominant role in determining energy density. The optimization predicted an increase in energy density by 11.23%, reaching 157.8 Wh/kg, while preserving adequate cooling performance. In summary, Xie et al. demonstrated that the integration of PCM, MHPA, and liquid cooling into a hybrid BTMS, provided a viable strategy for enhancing the thermal stability and energy efficiency of lithium-ion batteries, making the system particularly suitable for electric vehicle and energy storage applications. Machine learning

In the present work, machine learning was applied to construct predictive models linking the structural parameters of the hybrid BTMS to key performance indicators. Artificial neural networks were chosen due to their capacity to model highly nonlinear and coupled relationships inherent in thermal-energy systems. To enhance prediction accuracy, the ANN architectures and hyperparameters were systematically optimized through meta-heuristic algorithms, ensuring that the models effectively captured patterns within the experimental and simulated dataset. Developed ANN configurations were assessed for each target variable, and the most accurate and consistent models were selected to serve as surrogate models for the subsequent multi-objective optimization of the hybrid BTMS.

Multilayer perceptron neural network

Multilayer perceptron neural networks are a class of feedforward ANNs designed to map intricate relationships between input and output variables43. Structurally, an MLPNN consists of an input layer that receives feature data, multiple hidden layers that process signals through nonlinear transformations, and an output layer that generates predictions. Each neuron calculates a weighted input sum, transforms it via a nonlinear function, and propagates the output, which allows the network to represent highly coupled nonlinear dynamics44. MLPNNs are particularly advantageous for regression problems due to their flexibility in modeling intricate dependencies and capturing subtle patterns that conventional linear or parametric models may overlook45. Key structural parameters include the number of hidden layers, the number of neurons per layer, and the selection of transfer functions, while training hyperparameters encompass the learning rate and training algorithm46. In this study, GA and MPA were employed to optimize these hyperparameters. By carefully tuning these hyperparameters, MLPNNs can deliver accurate, robust predictions even in datasets with multivariate inputs or moderate noise levels, making them highly effective and efficient tools for regression modeling in engineering and scientific applications.

Genetic algorithm

GA is an evolutionary computation technique inspired by the principles of natural selection and genetics, first conceptualized in the 1970 s to solve complex optimization problems that are difficult to address with traditional deterministic methods47. By mimicking biological evolution, GA iteratively evolves a population of candidate solutions toward improved performance, making it particularly well-suited for high-dimensional, nonlinear, and multimodal optimization landscapes. Its effectiveness arises from a combination of stochastic exploration and exploitation mechanisms, which enable the algorithm to search broadly across the solution space while simultaneously refining promising regions. The advantages of GA include its robustness in avoiding local optima, its flexibility in handling diverse types of objective functions and constraints, and its adaptability to discrete, continuous, and mixed-variable problems. During the optimization process, an initial population of potential solutions is generated, representing diverse points in the search space. These candidates are then evaluated according to a predefined fitness function, which quantifies their quality with respect to the desired objective. Superior solutions are preferentially selected to propagate their attributes to subsequent generations through recombination and crossover operations, introducing variability and promoting innovation. Mutation mechanisms further diversify the population, ensuring continuous exploration and reducing premature convergence. Over successive generations, the population evolves, with less fit solutions gradually eliminated and high-quality solutions increasingly dominating, leading to the convergence of the algorithm toward optimal or near-optimal outcomes.

Recent research has highlighted the effectiveness of genetic algorithms in optimizing hyperparameters across a range of machine learning models48–50.

Marine predators algorithm

Drawing inspiration from the predatory behaviors of oceanic animals, MPA employs a population-based approach for efficient optimization51. This approach leverages biologically motivated behaviors to achieve an effective balance between global exploration and local exploitation during optimization. Solutions act as dual-role agents (predators and prey) whose interactions drive effective navigation through the optimization landscape. The algorithm adapts movement patterns according to relative velocities, enhancing the efficiency of the search process. The optimization process can be summarized in the following key stages52,53:

Model development and evaluation criteria

Constructing an effective MLPNN requires substantial computational resources due to the wide spectrum of both architecture and training parameters that must be optimized. Traditional approaches, including exhaustive search or trial-and-error techniques, are often impractical because they demand excessive computational effort and time. Metaheuristic optimization methods provide a more efficient alternative by intelligently navigating the hyperparameter space to identify configurations that maximize predictive performance while maintaining manageable computational costs. Optimal MLPNN design depends on careful adjustment of architectural and training parameters. Based on previous research, structurally, important considerations include the number of hidden layers, the quantity of neurons per layer, and the choice of transfer functions in hidden and output layers, whereas training-related factors, such as the training algorithm selection, play a pivotal role in ensuring convergence stability and prediction accuracy55.

The network incorporates a single hidden layer with a maximum of 100 neurons, ensuring sufficient capacity to capture the nonlinear patterns in the data while minimizing the potential for overfitting. This configuration provides a careful compromise between maintaining model expressiveness and addressing the challenges associated with limited training samples. The machine learning model was developed using a relatively small dataset obtained from CFD simulations, consisting of 29 observations, which necessitates careful model design to achieve robust learning. To balance the trade-off between accurate fitting and generalization, the dataset was split with 80% of the samples assigned to training, while the remaining 20% were reserved exclusively for independent evaluation of predictive performance. Despite the limited sample size, this partitioning approach provides a reasonable basis for assessing model reliability. Furthermore, to prevent overfitting, 30% of the training data were set aside for validation and employed within an early-stopping framework, thereby controlling network complexity and promoting stable convergence of the MLPNN. A fixed random seed in MATLAB was used to assign initial weights and biases, and the same datasets were employed for training, validation, and testing to ensure unbiased comparison of MLPNN models optimized via different algorithms. Each metaheuristic method was allowed 2000 function evaluations and repeated five times to account for stochastic variability.

Metaheuristic pareto-based optimization

Designing high-performance BTMS demands careful consideration of multiple conflicting objectives. For example, reducing the spacing between cells or altering the geometrical parameters of the MHPA, including its height and thickness, can effectively minimize temperature difference and enhance thermal uniformity. However, these modifications may simultaneously increase the maximum temperature, thereby compromising safety, or reduce the available active volume, which lowers the overall energy density. Conversely, prioritizing a higher ED often restricts the thermal regulation capacity, leading to elevated thermal stresses. Because these objectives are strongly nonlinear and inherently conflicting, a multi-objective optimization framework is required.

In this study, the MOMVO algorithm is applied to systematically explore these trade-offs. Unlike single-objective methods, MOMVO generates a Pareto front of equally optimal solutions that balance thermal performance, safety, and energy density, rather than converging toward a single compromise point. Multi-objective multi-verse optimization

The multi-verse optimizer (MVO) is a population-based metaheuristic inspired by cosmological theories, where the interaction of universes is abstracted into optimization processes57. Each candidate solution is modeled as a universe, and its evolution occurs through three metaphorical phenomena: white holes, black holes, and wormholes. These mechanisms collectively allow knowledge exchange between solutions, favoring both exploration of the search space and exploitation of the best solutions58. The fitness of each solution is measured by an inflation rate, determining its likelihood of influencing or absorbing information. Building upon MVO, the multi-objective extension (MOMVO) incorporates archiving and leader selection strategies to ensure diverse and high-quality Pareto optimal solutions. The following steps outline its process59:

Initialization: A set of candidate solutions (universes) is generated randomly. Each solution is associated with multiple objective values that define its performance within the search space.Archive formation: The non-dominated sorting procedure identifies Pareto-optimal solutions from the population. These solutions are stored in an external archive, which preserves both quality and diversity along the Pareto front.Fitness evaluation (inflation rate assignment): Each solution is evaluated, and an inflation rate proportional to its performance is assigned. Higher-quality solutions obtain greater inflation rates, increasing their role in information sharing.White hole–black hole operations: Based on inflation rate probabilities, decision variables from strong universes (white holes) are transferred to weaker ones (black holes). This simulates selective information flow and favors the survival of promising solutions.Wormhole mechanism: Independently of fitness, all universes may update their positions by adopting variables from the current elite members of the archive or the best-performing solutions. This is regulated by two parameters:Wormhole existence probability (WEP): the chance that wormhole updating occurs.Travelling distance rate (TDR): the scale of position adjustment. Over time, WEP increases to intensify exploitation, while TDR decreases to refine convergence.Leader selection: To ensure diversity in the Pareto set, guiding solutions are chosen from the archive using crowding distance. A roulette-based mechanism favors solutions from sparsely populated areas, encouraging exploration across underrepresented objective regions.Archive maintenance: At every iteration, the archive is updated: dominated solutions are discarded, non-dominated ones are stored, and if the archive exceeds capacity, the most crowded solutions are removed to preserve diversity.Termination: The algorithm proceeds until a stopping condition (such as maximum generations) is satisfied. Pareto optimal solutions

In this study, the MOMVO algorithm was implemented with a population size of 200 and a maximum of 500 iterations. The Wormhole Existence Probability (WEP) was varied linearly from 0.2 to 1.0 over the iterations, and the Travelling Distance Rate (TDR) was updated according to the original formulation by Mirjalili et al.59, ranging from near 1.0 at the beginning to lower values as iterations progress. These parameters were selected through a trial-and-error process to balance convergence speed and exploration capability of the search space. A brief sensitivity analysis indicated that moderate variations in population size and iterations did not significantly alter the Pareto front distribution, suggesting that the selected configuration is appropriate for reliably identifying non-dominated solutions while maintaining computational efficiency. Regarding WEP and TDR, increasing the WEP rate accelerates exploitation by allowing more solutions to move toward the elite universe, while a lower WEP promotes exploration. Similarly, TDR controls the magnitude of solution perturbations: higher TDR values increase search diversity, whereas lower values refine convergence. The chosen WEP and TDR ranges provided a well-distributed Pareto front with a good balance between convergence and diversity, confirming the robustness of the MOMVO parameters for this problem.

By identifying a wide range of optimal configurations, MOMVO enables designers to select operating strategies tailored to specific priorities, such as maximizing ED or ensuring stricter thermal safety.

However, it can be noted that WHP ≈ 4 mm corresponds to the highest Tmax values (~ 41.5 °C), indicating that excessively thick plates may trap heat and reduce cooling effectiveness. Figure 10(d) examines Tmax against HHP and YB. While HHP exhibits no overall trend, there is a noticeable region between HHP ≈ 30–35 mm where Tmax reaches its lowest values, suggesting an optimal height range for enhancing vertical heat dissipation. For YB, smaller values yield lower Tmax, but as YB continues to increase, Tmax eventually reaches its peak, implying a limit where increased lateral spacing no longer contributes to thermal safety and may hinder airflow effectiveness. Figure 10(e) investigates the effect of WHP and XB on ED. Interestingly, XB shows a consistent negative trend: as the inter-cell spacing increases, ED decreases, which can be attributed to the reduction of active material per unit volume when cells are placed farther apart. For WHP, the relationship forms a V-shaped pattern: initially, ED decreases as WHP increases, but beyond WHP ≈ 4 mm, the data points become widely scattered, suggesting that excessive thickness introduces complex interactions between cooling efficiency and mass contribution, leading to highly variable ED values. Finally, Fig. 10(f) explores ED as a function of HHP and YB. For HHP, no distinct trend emerges, as Pareto points are highly scattered, reflecting its secondary influence on gravimetric performance. However, YB plays a significant role: the highest ED values (> 154 Wh/kg) are concentrated when YB ≈ 25–30 mm, beyond which ED values become widely dispersed and inconsistent, especially around YB ≈ 30 mm, indicating that overly large spacings introduce trade-offs between thermal control and packing efficiency.

The optimization of hybrid BTMS highlights the complex interplay between thermal regulation and energy density, where design variables decisively shape overall system performance. Analysis of the Pareto-optimal solutions demonstrates how geometric and structural parameters influence the trade-offs between minimizing temperature gradients, controlling peak operating temperatures, and maintaining energy storage efficiency. From a thermal perspective, Pareto points indicate that relatively low temperature differences (ΔT ≈ 1.8–2.5 °C) and reduced maximum temperatures (Tmax ≈ 38–39 °C) are closely linked to thicker MHPA structures (WHP ≈ 3.8–3.9 mm) and larger HHP. These configurations effectively suppress hot spots and promote thermal uniformity, which is vital for battery safety and longevity. However, such benefits typically coincide with lower energy density values (≈ 139–144 Wh/kg), highlighting the compromise between thermal reliability and storage capacity. Conversely, designs achieving higher energy densities (up to ≈ 157 Wh/kg) often rely on thinner MHPA thicknesses (≈ 2.6–2.8 mm) and more compact geometries. While these solutions maximize volumetric efficiency, they also correspond to higher ΔT values (≈ 4.0–4.9 °C) and Tmax exceeding 40 °C, reflecting reduced thermal stability. Intermediate configurations, such as those with WHP around 3.0 mm and XB values near 22–24 mm, reveal a balanced profile with moderate ΔT (~ 3 °C), controlled Tmax (~ 39 °C), and favorable energy density (≈ 150–153 Wh/kg).

A closer examination of MHPA thickness reveals three dominant categories that shape performance outcomes. Thin designs (2.04–2.70 mm) constitute 21% of Pareto points, offering material efficiency but limited thermal handling. Moderate thicknesses (2.71–3.35 mm), which represent 39% of solutions, strike a favorable compromise by balancing heat dissipation with energy density. The thick category (3.36–3.99 mm) accounts for 40%, indicating that substantial material deposition is often preferred to enhance heat transfer reliability. This distribution emphasizes the system-wide importance of adequate MHPA thickness for sustaining operational stability.

The influence of MHPA height complements this trend. Compact configurations (7–12 mm) are least common, with only 9% of Pareto points, reflecting spatial efficiency but weak thermal performance. Intermediate designs (12–18 mm) comprise 21% of solutions, offering balanced properties, while Extended heights (18–25 mm) dominate with 55%, confirming their strong suitability for effective cooling without major penalties in structure or mass. Bulky designs (25–35 + mm) make up the remaining 15%, indicating that excessive height, while thermally efficient, is often impractical for weight- or size-sensitive applications. Overall, the prevalence of extended heights suggests a distinct design preference for enhanced heat transfer capacity in hybrid BTMS.

Cell-to-cell spacing in the X direction further illustrates critical design trade-offs. Ultra-compact arrangements (20.00–21.50 mm) are most prevalent, representing 45% of Pareto points and indicating a strong inclination toward maximizing packing density despite higher thermal challenges. The compact range (21.51–23.00 mm) contributes 22%, easing thermal stresses while maintaining efficient spatial utilization. Balanced designs (23.01–25.00 mm) account for 16%, suggesting limited use of compromise spacing, while spacious configurations (25.01–26.50 mm) add 14%, prioritizing heat mitigation over compactness. Highly-spaced solutions (26.51–27.60 mm) are rare at just 3%, reflecting the inefficiency of overly large separations that undermine energy density.

In contrast, spacing in the Y direction demonstrates a much stronger inclination toward larger values. Ultra-compact (25.50–26.20 mm) and compact (26.21–27.00 mm) designs are scarce, accounting for only 4% and 8% of Pareto points, respectively, suggesting that densely packed arrangements are unfavorable under thermal constraints. Balanced spacing (27.01–27.80 mm) contributes 10% and spacious spacing (27.81–28.50 mm) adds 9%, reflecting modest adoption of intermediate geometries. Strikingly, 69% of solutions are concentrated in the highly-spaced category (28.51–29.00 mm), highlighting a clear design preference for wider separation to ensure safety and effective heat dissipation, even when compactness is compromised. This dominant trend underscores the critical importance of YB in achieving robust BTMS optimization.

Given the intricate interplay between thermal regulation, spatial design, and energy density, the reliance on Pareto fronts alone remains insufficient for practical decision-making. Incorporating MCDM is thus crucial to rank alternatives and identify solutions that best align with real-world performance requirements.

Multi-criteria decision-making

In advanced hybrid battery thermal management systems, design optimization often involves multiple conflicting objectives, including thermal uniformity, peak temperature control, and energy density maximization. While multi-objective optimization techniques can generate Pareto-optimal solutions, these results alone do not indicate which configuration is most suitable for practical implementation. The Pareto front captures the trade-offs inherent to the design space but does not directly guide the selection of a single, actionable solution. To overcome this limitation, MCDM methods are employed to systematically evaluate and prioritize Pareto-optimal alternatives based on their performance across multiple criteria. This process assists engineers and decision-makers in identifying designs that best balance competing objectives while meeting application-specific requirements. In this study, the CoCoSo method is applied to rank the Pareto-optimal configurations obtained from the MOMVO algorithm. By consolidating multiple performance metrics into a single evaluation framework, CoCoSo facilitates an informed selection of the most suitable hybrid BTMS design. Consequently, the integration of MCDM provides a practical and structured approach for translating theoretical Pareto-based results into reliable and implementable system configurations, as illustrated by earlier Pareto–MCDM hybrid frameworks60–62.

The CoCoSo approach is a contemporary and cohesive multi-criteria decision-making strategy that integrates both additive and multiplicative evaluation principles to achieve a balanced assessment of alternatives in multi-objective problems63. In contrast to conventional methods that rely on a single aggregation logic, CoCoSo merges the strengths of the simple additive weighting (SAW) and weighted product model (WPM), enabling a more thorough ranking of Pareto-optimal options by capturing both cumulative effects and nonlinear interactions among criteria. Scenario A corresponds to a pure ΔT-focused configuration, with a weight of one assigned exclusively to temperature uniformity. Scenario B emphasizes Tmax, assigning all weight to the reduction of peak temperature. Despite the low ED (139.17 Wh/kg), this scenario is particularly suitable for high-power battery systems where thermal runaway risk must be minimized, such as aerospace or high-speed transportation applications.

Scenario C is designed exclusively for energy density maximization. Scenario C exemplifies designs suitable for applications where maximizing energy storage within limited volume is crucial, such as portable electronics or compact battery modules, with an acceptable trade-off in thermal uniformity.

Scenario D represents a two-criteria compromise between ΔT and Tmax. Here, the MHPA thickness and height mirror those in scenario B (3.96 mm and 28.56 mm, respectively), with XB and YB at moderate levels (26.22 mm and 28.93 mm). Scenario E combines ΔT and ED priorities. The MHPA is moderately thin (3.98 mm) and short (10.22 mm), with XB tight (21.08 mm) and YB wide (28.94 mm). This scenario highlights the potential of designs that prioritize peak temperature suppression while maintaining energy efficiency, useful for systems where thermal safety cannot be compromised, yet energy storage density remains a key performance metric.

Scenario G integrates all three objectives with equal weighting. The MHPA thickness and height are intermediate (3.23 mm and 21.27 mm), with moderate cell spacings (XB = 22.74 mm, YB = 28.90 mm). ΔT, Tmax, and ED are balanced (2.68 °C, 38.24 °C, 149.53 Wh/kg), representing a holistic design solution that ensures a well-rounded compromise across thermal and energy objectives. Scenario G reflects practical applications in which no single criterion dominates, such as hybrid energy storage systems in grid support, where both thermal management and energy density must be simultaneously considered.

Scenario H biases the ΔT objective over the other two criteria by assigning it a higher weight. The MHPA thickness and height are relatively thick and tall (3.81 mm, 19.13 mm), with wider spacing (XB = 25.62 mm, YB = 28.98 mm). ΔT is minimized (2.06 °C), Tmax is controlled (38.04 °C), and ED is moderate (144.16 Wh/kg). This scenario demonstrates the strategic prioritization of thermal uniformity when temperature gradients are critical, such as in high-performance EV modules under cyclic charging/discharging.

Scenario I prioritizes Tmax with triple weight relative to other objectives. Here, the MHPA is moderately thin (2.94 mm) with large height (26.83 mm), and XB is tight (20.96 mm) while YB remains wide (28.94 mm). The resulting ΔT is moderate (3.02 °C), Tmax is minimized (38.04 °C), and ED is relatively high (151.28 Wh/kg). Scenario I is particularly relevant for applications requiring strict control of hotspot temperatures without substantial sacrifices in energy capacity, such as aerospace battery packs or high-density EV powertrains.

Scenario J emphasizes ED with triple weighting. The MHPA is thin and short (2.71 mm, 10.29 mm), with moderate XB and YB spacings (21.69 mm, 27.88 mm). ΔT and Tmax are higher (3.83 °C and 38.90 °C, respectively), but ED reaches 156.37 Wh/kg. This scenario illustrates the design choice for systems where maximizing stored energy is paramount, with thermal considerations maintained at acceptable levels for safe operation, exemplifying portable or compact energy storage devices.

These ten scenarios collectively demonstrate the versatility of the CoCoSo method in evaluating Pareto-optimal solutions under varying criteria weightings. By adjusting the relative importance of ΔT, Tmax, and ED, system designers are equipped with a spectrum of design alternatives, each addressing specific operational priorities. This flexibility ensures that decisions can be tailored to diverse real-world applications, ranging from safety-critical high-power modules to compact, high-energy-density storage solutions. The strategic use of differential weighting allows the exploration of extreme, intermediate, and compromise configurations, thereby promoting informed, data-driven decision-making. Ultimately, these scenarios exemplify how multi-criteria decision-making facilitates the translation of complex optimization outputs into practical design guidance for advanced hybrid BTMS, bridging the gap between theoretical Pareto-optimal solutions and real-world engineering applications.

Conclusion

This study proposed a comprehensive hybrid framework for enhancing battery thermal management systems, integrating predictive modeling, multi-objective optimization, and structured decision-making into a unified methodology. The framework is distinguished by four sequential stages: (1) systematic data preprocessing, (2) hybrid machine learning–based prediction using genetic algorithm–optimized and marine predator algorithm–enhanced multilayer perceptron neural networks (GA-MLPNN and MPA-MLPNN), (3) optimization of key thermal and performance indicators via the recently developed multi-objective multi-verse optimization (MOMVO) algorithm, and (4) selection of the most suitable design alternatives using the CoCoSo method. This integrated approach represents an innovation in BTMS research by simultaneously addressing predictive accuracy, optimization efficiency, and application-oriented decision-making. The key outcomes of the framework at each stage are summarized as follows:

The comparative results of the predictive models confirm that algorithm selection significantly affects accuracy across different objectives. While MPA-MLPNN achieved superior accuracy for predicting temperature difference (ΔT) with a maximum R (> 0.9985), GA-MLPNN demonstrated consistently better generalization for maximum temperature (Tmax) and energy density (ED), achieving higher correlation indices (R > 0.9986 for Tmax and R > 0.999 for ED).The MOMVO-driven optimization revealed the intricate trade-offs between minimizing temperature gradients, reducing peak temperatures, and maximizing energy density. Pareto-optimal solutions showed that thicker MHPA structures (≈ 3.8–3.9 mm) and extended heights (≈ 18–25 mm) effectively suppressed thermal hotspots, achieving ΔT ≈ 1.8–2.5 °C and Tmax ≈ 38–39 °C, but at the expense of reduced ED (≈ 139–144 Wh/kg). Conversely, thinner structures (≈ 2.6–2.8 mm) enhanced energy density (up to ≈ 157 Wh/kg) but raised ΔT (≈ 4.0–4.9 °C) and Tmax (> 40 °C). Intermediate configurations balanced these objectives, suggesting practical design compromises. This structured evaluation equips designers with flexible guidelines tailored to specific operational requirements, thereby bridging the gap between theoretical optimization and engineering implementation.

Despite these contributions, the study acknowledges certain limitations. First, the framework is built upon a specific dataset and surrogate predictive models; expanding the dataset to include more diverse operating conditions and incorporating high-fidelity CFD simulations and experimental validations would further enhance robustness and confidence in the predictions. Second, computational demands of MOMVO and hybrid ML training remain significant, potentially limiting real-time applicability. Third, structural assumptions, such as fixed cooling media properties and simplified cell arrangements, may restrict generalization to more complex battery architectures. Future research should therefore focus on integrating physics-informed ML models to improve extrapolation beyond trained datasets, extending optimization to include reliability and cost factors, and verifying framework outcomes through comprehensive CFD analyses and large-scale experimental BTMS prototypes. Moreover, coupling the proposed methodology with digital twin platforms could enable adaptive, real-time BTMS optimization, further advancing safety, efficiency, and lifespan of next-generation Li-ion battery systems. Furthermore, this study does not account for practical factors such as battery aging, operational condition fluctuations, or manufacturing tolerances, which constitute important limitations and should be addressed in future work.
