Determinants of preterm birth among newborns in resource limited settings
Authors: Teshite Gemechu, Wako Golicha, Berhanu Adula Jara, Angefa Ayele, Alo Edin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe World Health Organization defines preterm birth as any birth before 37 completed weeks of gestation, or fewer than 259 days since the first day of the woman’s last menstrual period. Globally, 1 in 10 babies is born prematurely and more than a million babies are estimated to die annually. The determinants of preterm birth may vary from region to region within the same country due to variation in socioeconomic status and health care service coverage. Therefore, this study was designed to identify determinants of preterm birth among newborns born in selected public hospitals in west Guji, east Guji and Borana zones, southern Ethiopia. A facility based unmatched case control study was conducted on 241 (80 cases and 161 controls) newborns. Among 13 hospitals of study area, 8 hospitals were selected by lottery method and sample size was proportionally allocated for each hospital based on their two month delivery report of cases. For one case two controls were selected consecutively. A pretested structured interviewer administered questionnaire and maternal chart were used to collect data. Then the collected data were coded and entered in to Epi data version 3.1, and exported to the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 25 for analysis. Both bi-variable and multivariable logistic regressions were used for analysis. Finally, adjusted odds ratio together with 95% confidence intervals and p value < 0.05 were used to declare the significance of association between independent variables and dependent variable. All mothers of newborns were included with the response rate of 100%. The result of multivariable analysis showed that; lack of antenatal care visit [AOR = 2.58, 95% CI (1.09–6.10)], ante partum hemorrhage [AOR = 3.46, 95% CI (1.29–9.23)], premature rupture of membrane [AOR = 5.54, 95% CI (1.89–16.20)], pregnancy induced hypertension [AOR = 5.77, 95% CI (1.65–20.21)], and multiple pregnancy [AOR = 5.87, 95 CI (1.6–21.7)] were determinants of preterm birth. The study indicates that a history of premature rupture of membranes, pregnancy-induced hypertension, antepartum hemorrhage, lack of antenatal care, and multiple pregnancies are identified as determinant factors for preterm birth. Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), preterm birth is defined as all births before 37 completed weeks of gestation1. The preterm newborns can be divided as moderately preterm (33–36 completed weeks of gestation), very preterm (28–32 weeks) and extremely preterm (< 28 weeks)2.

Globally, 1 in 10 babies is born premature, and 2.5 million children and more than a million babies are estimated to die in the first month of life, annually3,4. In Africa, the neonatal mortality rate due to preterm is more than six times higher than in developed countries5. In Ethiopia, about 320,000 babies are preterm birth and 24,000 Children Under Five Years (CUFYs) die of direct complication of preterm birth each year6. Prevalence of preterm birth may vary from region to region within the same country due to variation in socioeconomic status and health care service coverage. Studies carried out in Ethiopia indicate that prevalence of preterm birth is 4.4% in Gondar7, 16.5% in Addis Ababa8, and 25.9% in Jimma9.

Preterm birth is associated with poor neurological and cognitive development, childhood morbidity, growth impairment, range of poor health outcomes, chronic diseases later in life and a cause of both short-term and long-term consequences leading to adverse social and economic impacts10.

The global community committed to the sustainable development goals (SDG) and every woman and child initiatives to prevent and improve survival of preterm births; different interventions have been implemented which need huge financial capacity include; antenatal corticosteroid, antibiotic, kangaroo mother care, immediate intensive care unit service, and long-term complex health needs11. These interventions lead to a high economic burden for the family and the community at large through implementations of programs, policies, and strategies9.

Preventing preterm birth is a critical global research priority, yet progress in this area has been limited thus far. Ongoing research is focused on identifying early markers of preterm birth through genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, immunology, and metabolomics during pregnancy, as well as enhancing the accuracy of gestational age assessment12 .

In Ethiopia, the situation is particularly concerning as preterm birth is often underreported and underestimated, despite being the primary cause of neonatal and under-five mortality in the region. The country faces multiple risk factors that are especially prevalent in resource-limited settings, including rural residence, anemia, inadequate antenatal care visits, pregnancy-induced hypertension, and chronic illnesses. There exists a significant research gap regarding the specific determinants of preterm birth in Ethiopia’s healthcare context, where factors such as limited healthcare access, nutritional deficiencies, and socioeconomic constraints may play particularly prominent roles compared to high-income settings. Therefore,, this study aims to identify the determinants of preterm birth among newborns delivered at selected public hospitals in southern Ethiopia.

Method and materials

Study settings

The study was conducted in three zones of South Oromia: West Guji, Guji, and Borana Zones. They are located at the distance of 467 km, 610 km and 568 km from Addis Ababa respectively. There are 4 public hospitals in West Guji zone and Guji zones whereas Borana zone has about five public hospitals. This study involved eight hospitals: three from West Guji, three from Borana Zones, and two from Guji zone.

In West Guji Zone, the selected hospitals provide health services to a population exceeding 1,389,82113. According to the 2021 zonal health department report, these hospitals recorded an annual delivery volume of 3,250 and employ 186 health professionals13. Similarly, in Guji Zone, the hospitals serve approximately 771,879 people and have 132 health professionals, with an annual delivery report of 2362 for 202114. In Borena Zone, the hospitals cater to a population of about 926,690, with a 2021 annual delivery count of 320615. All the aforementioned hospitals are operational and provide delivery services.

Study design and period

A facility-based unmatched case-control study was undertaken in the public hospitals of South Oromia, Ethiopia from May 12 to July 12, 2022.

Population

Cases and controls

All neonates born before 37 completed weeks of gestation were cases while all neonates born between 37 and 42 completed weeks of gestation were considered as control.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria for cases The inclusion criteria for cases included all preterm neonates, defined as births occurring between 28 and 36 completed weeks of gestation.

Inclusion criteria for controls All live term neonates, defined as births occurring at or after 37 completed weeks of gestation (typically 37–42 weeks) were control.

Exclusion criteria for cases and controls Newborns without a confirmed diagnosis of preterm birth (i.e., born before 37 completed weeks of gestation) to avoid misclassification. Mothers critically ill and unable to communicate during the study period were also excluded. Mothers don’t know their Last normal menstrual period (LMP) or not checked early by Ultrasound were excluded because their GA was not known.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

A sample size of the study was calculated using Epi-Info software version 7 with the following parameters for unmatched case–control study. The assumption were: 95% CI, power 80%, adjusted odds ratio 3.6, case to control ratio 1:2 the proportion of control among exposed group (Urinary tract infection) is (7.2%). After adding a 10% of non-response rate, the total sample size was 241(80cases and 161 controls)11.

Among thirteen hospitals of study area, eight hospitals were selected by simple random sampling technique. The two monthly reports of cases and controls were identified from the registration logbook of the selected hospital. The two months report of cases and controls of Yabelo (17 and 510) Moyale (12 and 410), Bule Hora university teaching hospital (17 and 545), Karcha (7 and 180) Mega (14 and 401), Adola (13 and 480) malka Soda (5 and 100) and Nagele hospital are (15 and 570) respectively. Then the calculated sample sizes of cases were proportionally allocated for each hospital based on their two month’s delivery report of cases. Consecutively for each preterm birth case, two controls within the same day were selected from the same facility. As the numbers of cases were rare, all the identified cases were taken from obstetrics ward until the proportionally allocated sample size gets its target during the study period. Controls were also selected by multiplying number of cases by two. Both cases and controls were selected based on fulfillment of case and control definition criteria (Fig-1).

Study variables

Dependent variable

Preterm birth.

Independent variables

Socio-demographic and economic factors (age, residence, marital status, occupation, educational status, and income).

Obstetrics factors like (History of APH, PROM, Parity, Gravidity, Abortion, Hgb of mother, ANC follow up, GA, previous mode of delivery, wanted/unwanted pregnancy, and multiple pregnancy).

Underlying medical condition of mother like History of (DM, obesity, PIH, malaria infection).

Operational definitions

Gestational age Measurement unit of pregnancy duration which is calculated based on last menstrual period (LMP) or early ultrasound16.

Preterm birth Refers to the delivery of a baby between 28 completed and before 37 completed weeks of gestation17.

Term birth Birth of a live baby after 37 and before 42 completed weeks of gestation18.

Congenital anomaly Newborn born with Anencephaly, spina bifida, hydrocephalus and club foot.

Data collection tool and procedures

Data were collected using a questionnaire which was prepared in English and translated to Afan Oromo. The questionnaire was adapted from the previous literatures with little modification19,20. Eight midwives (BSc) for the data collection with four supervisors were recruited. Subsequent to three days training, data collectors started collecting the data. Data were collected by a pretested interviewer structured questionnaire with observation of medical records of mothers and neonates.

Data quality assurance

Training was given for data collectors and supervisors for three days to familiarize with the prepared questionnaire, the data collection procedures, the ethical consideration during data collection, and the objective of the study. A pre-test of this research was conducted on the 5% of sample size (4 cases and 8 controls) at Bore Primary hospital to check consistency. After pre-test some modification was carried out on variables like birth interval, DM and PIH. Close supervision was carried out by the principal investigator and supervisor during data collection to check the completeness, clarity, consistency, and accuracy of the collected data.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data underwent thorough checks for accuracy, consistency, and completeness before being entered into Epi Data version 3.1 and then exported to SPSS software version 25 for further data cleaning and analysis. Descriptive statistics such as mean, frequency, and percentages were utilized to characterize the study population across relevant variables, with results being presented through text, figures, and tables based on the data types.

Binary Logistic regression models were employed for both Bivariable and multivariable analyses. During the Bivariable analysis, the focus was on assessing the association between preterm birth and each independent variables. Variables with a P value less than 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were then included in the multivariable logistic regression model to control for confounding factors and identify the independent predictors of preterm birth. The strength of association was determined using adjusted odds ratios along with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Prior to the final multivariable analysis, multicollinearity among explanatory variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with all VIF values below 3. The goodness of fit of the final multivariable model was evaluated through the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, yielding a p value of 0.87, indicating a satisfactory fit for the logistic regression model. Statistical significance was declared at a P value below 0.05, alongside adjusted odds ratios and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

The mean ages of the cases and controls were 29.14 (± 5.6) and 25.84 (± 5.23) years respectively. The majority of respondents, both among case and control (93.8% and 93.2%), (60% and 61.5%), and (40% and 34.2%) were married, housewives, and no formal education, respectively. More than 60% of the controls and mothers were rural residents (Table 1).

Determinants of preterm birth

The result of bi-variable logistic regression analysis showed that age of mother, residence, house hold food security, ANC follow up, iron supplementation during pregnancy, history of APH, PROM, PIH, Abortion, HIV/AIDS status, multiple pregnancy and parity were candidates of multivariable logistic regression model. In order to identify independent predictors of preterm birth, variables that fulfill the criteria in bi-variable analysis were entered in to multivariable logistic regression model. The multivariable analysis indicated that ANC follow up during pregnancy, APH history before onset of labor, PROM, PIH and multiple pregnancy were significantly associated with the preterm birth.

In this finding, the odds of women who had no ANC follow up were 2.58 higher among PTB than term birth [AOR = 2.58, 95% CI (1.09–6.10)]. On the other hand, women who had PIH were 5.77 times higher odds of PTB than their counterparts [AOR = 5.77, 95% CI (1.65, 20.21)]. This study also showed that, Preterm birth are 6 times more likely to be from multiple pregnancy than term birth [AOR = 5.87, 95% CI (1.6, 21.7)] (Table 4).

Discussion

The study aimed to identify the determinants of preterm birth among newborns delivered in public hospitals within the study settings.

Our findings suggest a significant threefold increase in the likelihood of preterm birth among women who did not receive antenatal care (ANC) follow-up, in comparison to those who did. This finding is consistent with earlier studies, including research conducted in China and Uganda3,21. Previous multiple case-control studies in Ethiopia11,22,23 have also underscored the strong correlation between the absence of ANC follow-up and higher chances of preterm birth. It is evident that the counseling and support offered during ANC visits play a critical role in early detection of risk factors and effective management of potential complications, ultimately leading to a reduction in the prevalence of preterm births.

Antepartum hemorrhage (APH) has been identified as an independent predictor of preterm birth, corroborating findings from studies conducted at Kenyatta National Hospital24, and Jimma University Medical Center25. The underlying mechanism for this association may involve decreased placental blood flow, which adversely affects the exchange of nutrients and oxygen between the mother and fetus26. This compromised blood flow can lead to impaired fetal development, thereby increasing the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth27.

Studies indicates that various factors contribute to the likelihood of preterm birth, and APH is among the significant predictors. For instance, a systematic review highlighted that APH is associated with a higher odds ratio for preterm birth, emphasizing its critical role in obstetric complications28. Additionally, studies from Ethiopia have consistently shown that APH, along with other factors such as pregnancy-induced hypertension and multiple pregnancies, significantly correlates with preterm birth outcomes29,30. In summary, the evidence supports the notion that antepartum hemorrhage is a crucial factor influencing the risk of preterm birth, necessitating careful monitoring and management in pregnant women experiencing this condition.

Mothers with a history of premature rupture of membranes (PROM) are indeed at a significantly increased risk of having preterm births, with studies indicating they are five times more likely to experience this outcome compared to their counterparts. This finding is consistent with research conducted at Kenyatta National Hospital24, and various locations in Ethiopia22,23,25,31,32 which have documented similar associations between PROM and preterm birth rates. The presence of amniotic fluid, with its prostaglandins, can stimulate uterine contractions, potentially leading to preterm labor. Furthermore, PROM has been associated with elevated levels of fetal plasma interleukin-6, an inflammatory cytokine implicated in the initiation of labor and the heightened risk of preterm delivery30.

The study findings also revealed that Pregnancy-Induced Hypertension (PIH) emerged as an independent predictor of preterm birth. Mothers with a history of PIH had 6 times higher probability of experiencing preterm birth compared to their counterparts. This findings is consistent with research conducted in various countries32, including studies in public hospitals in western Ethiopia22, Dilla University in southern Ethiopia11, Butajara hospital in southern Ethiopia31, and other regions of Ethiopia23,25,33. The association between PIH and preterm birth may be attributed to the complications of pregnancy-induced hypertension leading to vascular damage to the placenta or iatrogenic effects resulting from the severity of hypertension or its complications, subsequently triggering oxytocin receptors and culminating in preterm birth34.

In addition, the study revealed that women with a history of multiple pregnancies had nearly 6 times higher likelihood of experiencing preterm birth compared to their counterparts. This finding aligns with studies conducted in24, and Ethiopia23,25,35. The increased risk may be attributed to early uterine contractions leading to excessive uterine distension.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The study possesses both strengths and limitations. The utilization of primary data, along with well-defined and selected cases and controls, enhances its validity and helps in minimizing confounding factors. However, limitations include the potential for recall bias, which could obscure the association between identified risk factors and the occurrence of preterm birth among the study participants when reporting certain issues. Another limitation is the exclusion of deceased newborns from the study, as characteristics of newborns who do not survive may differ from those who do, potentially impacting the study findings.

Conclusions

The findings of this study suggest that factors such as premature rupture of membranes, pregnancy-induced hypertension, antepartum hemorrhage, lack of antenatal care visits, and multiple pregnancies are significant risk factors for preterm birth. Many of these determinants are modifiable, underscoring the importance of focusing on preventing obstetric complications like PROM, APH, and PIH to reduce the incidence of preterm birth. It is recommended to address issues such as promote ANC follow-up, and provide appropriate care for multiple pregnancies during pregnancy to mitigate the occurrence of preterm birth. Health professionals should pay special attention to mothers with multiple pregnancies, PIH, and antepartum hemorrhage. Health extension workers can play a vital role in providing health education to raise awareness among mothers and encourage ANC follow-up. Researchers are encouraged to conduct longitudinal and community-based studies complemented with qualitative methods to further investigate these matters.
