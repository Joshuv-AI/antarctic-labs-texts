Accelerometer-measured sedentary time accumulation patterns and cardiometabolic risk severity in community-dwelling older adults: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Yuri A Freire, Rodrigo A V Browne, Ludmila L P Cabral, Francisco José Rosa-Souza, Raíssa de Melo Silva, Charles Phillipe de Lucena Alves, Marcyo Câmara, Debra L Waters, Tiago V Barreira, Eduardo Caldas Costa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo analyze the association between accelerometer-measured sedentary time accumulation patterns and cardiometabolic risk severity in community-dwelling older adults.MethodsCross-sectional study (n = 248; 78% females; 66 ± 5 years). Sedentary time (ST; <100 counts/min) was assessed by a hip-worn accelerometer for seven days. Total ST (≥ 1 min) and ST in bouts of < 10 (short), 10–29 (medium), and ≥ 30 (prolonged) minutes were registered. Cardiometabolic risk severity was calculated using a continuous metabolic syndrome score (cMetS), comprising fasting glucose, HDL-cholesterol, triglycerides, waist circumference, and blood pressure. Data were analyzed using adjusted generalized linear models.ResultsParticipants spent 10.5 ± 1.6 h/day, 4.2 ± 0.8 h/day, 3.6 ± 0.8 h/day, and 2.7 ± 1.2 h/day, respectively, in total ST, short, medium, and prolonged ST bouts. Adjusted models revealed that total ST (β = 0.08; 95% CI 0.02 to 0.14), and ST in prolonged bouts (β = 0.16, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.27) were positively associated with cMetS, while ST in short bouts was negatively associated with cMetS (β= -0.18, 95% CI -0.31 to -0.05). In addition, older adults in the highest tertile of ST had higher cMetS for total ST (β = 0.24; 95% CI 0.02 to 0.46) and ST in prolonged bouts (β = 0.29; 95% CI 0.03 to 0.56), and lower cMetS for ST in short bouts (β = -0.25; 95% CI -0.46 to -0.04), compared to those in the lowest tertile. There was no association between ST in medium bout and cMetS (p > 0.05).ConclusionST in prolonged bouts was associated with higher cardiometabolic risk severity, whereas ST in short bouts was linked to lower risk in community-dwelling older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06283-z.Keywords: Aging, Sedentary behavior, Sitting, Cardiovascular health

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome refers to the combination of multiple cardiometabolic risk factors, including central obesity, glycemic dysregulation, high blood pressure, low HDL-cholesterol, and hypertriglyceridemia (at least three of these five components) [1]. This syndrome increases the risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, and is considered a strong predictor of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs; e.g., acute myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure) [2–6]. Despite several guidelines to define metabolic syndrome [1], its traditional clinical diagnosis is binary (yes/no). However, a continuous metabolic syndrome score (cMetS) has been proposed to identify the cardiometabolic risk severity, even in those individuals already identified by its traditional binary method [7, 8]. The rationale behind cMetS considers the continuous nature of the components of metabolic syndrome and their linear relationship with MACEs and cardiovascular mortality [8, 9]. Taken together, cMetS may provide a more detailed picture of factors associated with cardiometabolic risk severity.

Sedentary behavior is characterized by any waking behavior in a sitting, reclining, or lying posture with an energy expenditure ≤ 1.5 metabolic [10]. In the last decade, research on sedentary behavior has grown rapidly, with accumulated evidence showing that sedentary time (ST) is a risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [11]. In this context, ST has been linked to incident metabolic syndrome, even when adjusted for physical activity level [12]. The 2020 World Health Organization guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behavior recommends reducing ST to improve cardiometabolic health and prevent cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [13]. This recommendation is especially important for older adults, who spend 65–80% of their waking hours in ST [14] and have a high prevalence of metabolic syndrome [15–17]. The accumulation of sedentary time throughout the day can have different patterns. For instance, a person can accumulate ST in prolonged continuous bouts (“prolonger”), while another accumulates ST with frequent interruptions and in short bouts (“breaker”) [10]. Prolonged bouts of accumulated ST may be associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease [18, 19]. However, to the best of our knowledge, the relationship between sedentary accumulation patterns and cardiometabolic risk severity is still poorly understood in older adults.

Previous studies investigating the relationship between ST and cardiometabolic risk severity in older adults have included small samples and presented conflicting results [20–22]. While the association between total ST and cardiometabolic risk severity has been confirmed in older females [20] and extremely active older adults [21], this association was reported only with ST in bouts ≥ 10 min, and not with total ST, in older females [22]. Regarding sedentary accumulation patterns, Kim et al. [23] examined the association between ST in different bout durations and cardiometabolic risk markers in a sample of 5,917 adults and older adults. The authors found that ST in short bouts (< 10 min) was associated with lower cardiovascular risk levels, whereas time spent in medium (10–29 min) and prolonged (> 30 min) sedentary bouts was associated with higher cardiovascular risk. However, it remains unclear how ST accumulation patterns relate to cardiometabolic risk severity and whether these associations are independent of total ST. A better understanding on the relationship between sedentary accumulation patterns and cardiometabolic risk severity may provide valuable insights about the link between ST and cardiovascular diseases in older adults. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between different sedentary accumulation patterns (total ST and time spent in short, medium, and prolonged sedentary bouts) and cMetS in community-dwelling older adults.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study followed the STROBE guidelines [24] and adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. It was approved by the Research Ethics Board of the Onofre Lopes University Hospital (protocol no. 3.133.164/2019). Data were collected between June 2018 and December 2019 at two sites: the Onofre Lopes University Hospital and the Department of Physical Education at the Federal University of Rio Grande do Norte. Before participation, all individuals were thoroughly informed about the study procedures and provided written informed consent.

Participants

Community-dwelling older adults aged 60 to 80 years were recruited through health units, media advertisements, social media, and community centers. Eligibility criteria included preserved cognitive function, absence of mobility limitations or musculoskeletal injuries, and no clinical history of cardiovascular disease or uncontrolled cardiometabolic conditions. Individuals with incomplete data were excluded (Fig. 1). Detailed recruitment procedures and participant characteristics are reported in a previous publication [25]. The sample size for this study was determined based on a priori sensitivity analysis using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). With 248 participants, 14 predictors in the model, a significant level of 5%, and 80% power, the study can detect incremental effects as small as f2 = 0.03, which is considered a small effect size [26]. For reference, Madden et al. [21], reported a moderate effect size (approximately f2 = 0.20) in a similar model, supporting that the current sample is adequately powered to detect associations of comparable or smaller magnitude.

Outcome ascertainment

Cardiometabolic risk severity

Cardiometabolic risk severity was estimated using a cMetS, based on fasting glucose, HDL-cholesterol, triglycerides, waist circumference, and mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Blood samples were collected after 12 h of fasting, and LDL-cholesterol was calculated using the Friedewald formula [27]. Blood pressure was measured in triplicate by an oscillometric device (Omron HEM-780-E, Kyoto, Japan) after 10 min of rest in the sitting position [28], and waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the iliac crest and the last rib. The procedures used to derive the cMetS followed the methods proposed Wijndaele et al. (2006). Briefly, each variable was log-transformed, and a sex-specific principal component analysis with varimax rotation was performed to extract the components that accounted for the largest portions of variance (Eigenvalue ≥ 1.0). For males, one component explaining 37.7% of the variance was identified, while for females, two components explaining 31.9% and 22.2% of the variance were retained. These components were weighted according to their relative contributions to the explained variance to compute the cMetS. The assumptions for the analysis were met (p < 0.05 for Bartlett’s test of sphericity; Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin index > 0.5). The definition of metabolic syndrome prevalence was based on the criteria established by an international committee [29].

Explanatory variables

Accumulation patterns of accelerometer-measured sedentary time

ST was assessed using an ActiGraph GT3X accelerometer (ActiGraph LLC, Pensacola, USA), which participants wore on their right hip for seven consecutive days. Participants also maintained a logbook to record times when the device was removed during waking hours, as well as bedtime and wake-up times. Data were collected at a sampling rate of 60 Hz and aggregated into 60-second epochs. Non-wear time was defined as periods of at least 90 consecutive minutes of zero counts, allowing for interruptions of up to 2 min with ≥ 100 count/min [30]. ST, light physical activity, and MVPA were defined using thresholds of 0–99 counts/min, 100–1951 counts/min, and ≥ 1952 counts/min, respectively [31–33].

Movement behaviors were analyzed using ActiLife software (version 6.13.3.2), considering the weighted average of weekdays and weekend days. Participants were included in the analysis if they had at least four valid monitoring days—three weekdays and one weekend day—with a minimum of 10 h of wear time per day [34]. Variables derived for analysis included total ST (hours/day), and time spent in sedentary bouts of short (< 10 min), medium (10–29 min), and prolonged duration (≥ 30 min), as well as time spent in LPA (h/day) and MVPA (min/day).

Confounding variables

Data concerning age (years), educational status, smoking history, alcohol abuse (≥ 5 alcoholic drinks for males and ≥ 4 alcoholic drinks for females on the same occasion at least one day in the last 30 days) [35], and lipid-lowering, glucose-lowering and antihypertensive medications were collected through face-to-face interviews with participants. Height (m) was measured using a wall-fixed stadiometer (ES2040, Sanny, Brazil), while weight (kg) was recorded with a digital scale (InBody 270, Ottoboni, Brazil). Body mass index (BMI) was then calculated by dividing weight by height squared (kg/m2). Futhermore, participants’ engagement in regular physical activities not captured by the accelerometer (i.e., resistance training and water-based exercises ≥ 2 d/week) was assessed using the Minnesota Leisure Time Activities Questionnaire [36]. Resistance training considered strength-based activities (i.e., weight training and Pilates), while water-based exercises included swimming and water aerobics.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as absolute and relative frequencies (n and %), for categorical variables, and as means with standard deviations (mean ± SD) for continuous variables. Generalized linear models with a normal error distribution were used to determine the associations between sedentary accumulation patterns and cMetS. A separate generalized linear model was employed for each sedentary accumulation pattern to estimate the coefficient (β) and its 95% Wald confidence interval (CI) for cMetS across tertiles of sedentary accumulation patterns (tertile 1 as the reference group; tertiles 1, 2, and 3 were named low, medium, and high ST, respectively). Tertile or quartile-based approaches have been used to identify which levels of ST are associated with increased cardiometabolic risk [37–39]. All models were adjusted for the following potential confounders [40], based on two levels of adjustment: Model 1 included age, sex, BMI, education, smoking history, alcohol abuse, lipid-lowering, glucose-lowering and antihypertensive medications, MVPA, resistance training, and water-based exercises (physical activities not captured by the accelerometer), as well as accelerometer wear time, while Model 2 included all covariates from Model 1 plus total ST. Effect sizes were estimated using Cohen’s f2, with values of 0.02, 0.15, and 0.35 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively [26]. Normal Q-Q plots were used to assess the normal distribution of residuals. The Omnibus test was conducted to evaluate model goodness of fit, with a well-fitting model defined as having a p < 0.05. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Win/v.27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Discussion

This study investigated the association between different sedentary accumulation patterns and the cardiometabolic risk severity in community-dwelling older adults. Our main findings were: (i) total ST was positively associated with cMetS, a marker of cardiometabolic risk severity; (ii) ST accumulated in short bouts (< 10 min) was negatively associated with cMetS; iii) ST accumulated in prolonged sedentary bouts (≥ 30 min) was positively associated with cMetS; iv) older adults with a high ST (i.e., highest tertile of ST) in short bouts had the lowest cMets; v) older adults with a high total ST or high ST in prolonged bouts had the highest cMetS.

ST is an emerging risk factor for MACEs and mortality. The association of sedentary bout accumulation with markers of cardiometabolic risk severity is still poorly understood in older adults. In the present study, we have demonstrated that different sedentary bout duration ranges modify the direction of the association between ST and cardiometabolic risk severity. The association between accumulated ST in short, medium, and prolonged bouts with the cMetS is, respectively, negative, null, and positive. We found that time spent in short sedentary bouts is associated with lower cardiometabolic risk severity in community-dwelling older adults, independent of MVPA levels and total ST. Using a tertile-based approach, we observed that lower cMetS is found in older adults with high ST in short bouts compared to those with low ST in short bouts. In a mixed sample of middle-aged and older adults, Kim et al. [23] also found a negative association between ST in short sedentary bouts and waist circumference, blood glucose, triglycerides, and other cardiometabolic risk markers. Experimental trials have demonstrated that frequent interruptions of ST throughout the day, with 1–5 minutes of light-intensity physical activity or MVPA, improve cardiometabolic parameters in older adults [41]. This suggests that a ‘breaker’ sedentary behavior pattern may be beneficial for health. The potential cardiometabolic benefits of a ‘breaker’ pattern may arise from the more frequent muscular demand induced by the higher frequency of interruptions in sedentary behaviors, even if they involve only light-intensity ambulation or simply standing activities [42–44]. Regarding medium sedentary bouts, no association was found between this sedentary accumulation pattern and cMetS. It is possible that 10-29-min sedentary bouts are insufficient to trigger physiological impairments (e.g., endothelial dysfunction and impaired glycemic control) [42], which have been observed in studies with high ST accumulated in prolonged bouts [41]. Although medium sedentary bouts represent a third of total ST, this pattern does not seem to influence the association between total ST and cMetS. Our findings highlight the concern regarding ST in older adults, particularly in relation to prolonged sedentary bouts.

Only ST accumulated in prolonged bouts was associated with greater cardiometabolic risk severity. This association was found to be independent of MVPA levels. A systematic review [19] on the relationship between ST patterns and the risk of non-communicable diseases highlighted the association between prolonged bout ST and the risk of various cardiometabolic diseases. The authors noted that, although few studies included total ST as a covariate, these associations often weakened or did not persist after adjusting for total ST. Here, we found that the effect size of the association between ST in prolonged bout and cMetS remained even after adjusting for total ST (β = 1.3 a.u. to 1.6 a.u.). Our findings show the consistent relationship between prolonged bout ST and cardiometabolic risk in older adults.

We also identified that older adults with high ST in prolonged bouts exhibited greater cMetS compared to those with low ST in prolonged bouts. Therefore, it appears that the increased cardiometabolic risk severity associated with ST emerges primarily from a high volume of prolonged bout ST. Studies investigating the risk of MACE’s and mortality associated with mean sedentary bout duration have also found increased risk only in the most extreme groups [37, 45]. Hooker et al. (2022) followed 7607 adults (~ 64 years) for 7.4 years and demonstrated that only the highest tertile of mean sedentary bout duration presented a higher risk for stroke compared to the lowest tertile. Similarly, Belletiere et al. (2019) found that the incidence of MACEs associated with mean sedentary bout duration was stronger and more consistent between the 1 st and 4th quartiles in 5638 older females. Experimental studies have shown a deterioration in cardiometabolic parameters, including increases in blood pressure, blood glucose, and triglycerides, when there is a substantial accumulation of prolonged sedentary bouts throughout the day in older adults [41]. These findings further support the recommendation to regularly interrupt ST as a strategy for cardiometabolic health management.

Total ST is the most commonly used accelerometer-measured sedentary behavior metric [19, 46]. We also found an association between total ST and cMetS, independent of MVPA levels. Additionally, older adults with high total ST (≥ 11.3 h/day) exhibited greater cardiometabolic risk severity compared to those with low total ST (< 9.8 h/day). Several studies have reported a consistent relationship between total ST and the risk of MACEs and mortality [18, 37, 45]. Madden et al. (2021) found an association between total ST and cMetS even in highly active older adults who engaged in 2.6 h/day of MVPA. Total ST included short, medium, and prolonged sedentary bouts. Based on the associations observed in the present study, it is reasonable to consider that the relationship between total ST and cardiometabolic risk severity may be primarily driven by ST accumulated in prolonged bouts. This is further supported by our findings showing that the association between total ST and cMetS disappears (p > 0.05) when prolonged bout ST is included in the regression model, whereas this does not occur when short or medium bout ST is considered.

Our findings are aligned with the previous studies [18, 20–22, 37] regarding the deleterious association of ST and cardiometabolic health in older adults. It is plausible that breaking up prolonged sedentary bouts could help reduce cardiovascular risk, particularly among older adults with high ST in prolonged bouts. Moreover, our findings suggest that the recommendation to “limit and interrupt prolonged sedentary time” should be specifically targeted at older adults with high ST [47, 48]. From a practical perspective, reducing prolonged sedentary bouts by engaging in light physical activity may be a more feasible strategy for daily life in older adults. For instance, walking instead of using motorized transport and participating in active leisure activities such as stretching, walking in pleasant environments, gardening, or playing with grandchildren between prolonged sedentary periods could be effective strategies to reduce ST in prolonged bouts [49, 50]. For instance, on a day with high ST, five minutes of light-intensity walking every 30 min of sedentary behavior improves glycemic and blood pressure control in older adults [51]. Additionally, reducing time spent in sedentary positions (i.e., sitting, lying down, or reclining) by standing more can be another low-effort strategy to reduce ST in prolonged bout [50, 52] Recently, a clinical trial demonstrated that a six-month intervention to replace ST with standing in older adults was able to reduce ST by 31 min/day and systolic blood pressure by 3.4 mmHg [53]. Furthermore, studies consistently demonstrate that higher MVPA levels mitigate the adverse effects of ST on cardiometabolic health and mortality [19, 37, 54]. Therefore, both reducing ST in prolonged bout and/or increasing MVPA levels appears to be alternatives for older adults with high ST.

This study has strengths and limitations that should be mentioned. First, using the cMetS score instead of traditional metabolic syndrome criteria allows for a more precise assessment of cardiometabolic risk severity, considering that the risk progressively increases with the number of metabolic syndrome risk factors and their individual levels [7]. Second, physical activity practices that are not captured by the accelerometer, including water-based exercise and strength training, were recorded via questionnaire and included as a confounding variable in the multiple models. Third, accelerometer-measured movement behavior offers greater precision compared to self-reported measures [55, 56]. However, a known limitation is that the ActiGraph GT3X may overestimate total ST and the number of sedentary breaks [56]. Consequently, ST accumulated in short bouts may be overestimated, while ST accumulated in prolonged bouts may be underestimated. These measurement errors could attenuate or inflate associations between specific sedentary accumulation patterns and cardiometabolic health. Therefore, caution is needed when interpreting the strength of associations involving bout-specific sedentary patterns. The use of medications for cardiometabolic diseases, included as an adjustment in the main analyses, was assessed through medical prescriptions; however, participants’ adherence to treatment is unknown. Finally, our sample consisted of older adults aged 60–80 years, with females comprising approximately 75% of the participants. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution and not generalized to other populations.

Conclusion

In summary, prolonged sedentary bouts (> 30 min) were associated with greater cardiometabolic risk severity in community-dwelling older adults, while shorter sedentary bouts did not. Moreover, older adults with high ST in prolonged bouts had the highest cardiometabolic risk severity. These findings highlight the importance of limiting prolonged sedentary bouts to reduce health risks. Randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these results and explore effective strategies to break up sedentary time.
