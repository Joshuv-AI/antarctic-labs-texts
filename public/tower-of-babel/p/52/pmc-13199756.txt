The Unpaid Infrastructure of Cardiovascular Science
Authors: Wael A Jaber, Issam Motairek, Leslie Cho, Brett W Sperry
Published: 2026

Peer review and professional society service are foundational to cardiovascular medicine, yet they operate almost entirely on unpaid physician labor. Each year, millions of peer reviews are completed globally, representing tens of millions of hours devoted to evaluating manuscripts and maintaining scientific integrity.1 Behind nearly every advance in cardiology—from major clinical trials to imaging innovations and device therapies—lies a network of clinicians voluntarily assessing evidence and refining scientific work before it reaches the clinical community (Figure 1).

The Unpaid Infrastructure of Cardiovascular Science

Volunteer physician labor spans 5 sequential stages of the cardiovascular knowledge-to-care continuum: (1) Evidence generation, including trial investigation, data safety monitoring, registry stewardship, and grant review; (2) Peer review, encompassing manuscript evaluation, editorial coordination, and statistical review; (3) Guideline development, including writing committee service, external review, and dissemination; (4) Professional society activities, including committee membership, abstract grading, award review, and continuing medical education teaching; and (5) Clinical implementation, including quality improvement, pathway development, and trainee mentorship. Each stage depends on unpaid expertise, and downstream stages cannot function without upstream contributions. For decades, this work has been framed as professional obligation rather than compensated labor. Reviewing manuscripts exposes cardiologists to emerging science, sharpens critical appraisal, and provides early-career physicians with mentorship and professional development. For senior investigators, professional service represents stewardship of the field. These rationales remain valid—but the conditions under which voluntary service is expected have changed considerably.

Guideline development: the hidden labor

These contributions are particularly visible in clinical guideline development. Multisociety writing committees synthesize evidence from randomized trials, registries, and observational studies, often devoting hundreds of hours to drafting recommendations and revising documents through multiple rounds of peer review.2 These documents govern the care of millions of patients and shape clinical practice worldwide, yet the intellectual labor required to produce them remains overwhelmingly uncompensated. The aggregate, across dozens of major cardiovascular guidelines updated each cycle, is substantial—an invisible infrastructure that does not appear in any institutional budget.

The changing economics of physician time

The structure of modern cardiovascular practice has evolved in ways that challenge this model. Physician time has become a measurable commodity tied to relative value units and patient throughput, leaving limited protected time for academic activities. As a result, manuscript reviews, editorial duties, and society commitments frequently occur outside the clinical workday—late at night, between patient encounters, or on weekends.

Protected academic time has simultaneously eroded. Faculty physicians are increasingly expected to sustain high clinical volume while continuing to contribute to scholarship and professional service. The expectation of additional unpaid labor is increasingly difficult to reconcile with these demands, particularly as physician burnout remains a pervasive challenge in academic cardiology.

Volunteerism and structural inequity

Reliance on volunteerism also raises equity concerns. Physicians with access to institutional support or protected academic time are far better positioned to participate in peer review, guideline writing, and society leadership. Cardiologists in community settings or under high clinical loads face greater barriers. Physicians with significant caregiving responsibilities—a burden that falls disproportionately on women in medicine—have fewer discretionary hours for voluntary professional engagement.

The consequence is that the individuals shaping cardiovascular research priorities, clinical guidelines, and professional policy may not reflect the full diversity of the workforce or the patient populations it serves. A scientific infrastructure built on uncompensated labor is not merely a sustainability problem—it is a representation problem.

A system built on unpaid expertise

Journals rely on thousands of reviewers and editors to evaluate manuscripts and maintain scientific rigor.2 Professional societies depend on volunteer committees to develop educational programming and policy initiatives. Yet scientific publishing has evolved into a multibillion-dollar industry, with major publishers reporting profit margins comparable to those of technology companies.3 Professional societies also generate substantial revenue through conferences, memberships, and education. In this context, the expectation that physicians will indefinitely donate expertise raises legitimate questions about fairness and sustainability.

Toward sustainable recognition

Recognizing professional service does not require large financial payments. Even modest acknowledgment signals that physician time is valued. Potential approaches include continuing medical education credits for peer reviewers, reduced society membership fees, conference registration discounts, and publication fee waivers. Some journals have begun offering honoraria or formal recognition programs for highly active reviewers. Additionally, some institutions have introduced stipend salaries as a formal incentive for academic activities such as peer review and guideline authorship—an approach that acknowledges the value of these contributions and aligns with the academic expectations of The Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education-accredited training programs.

Institutional recognition matters equally. Incorporating peer review and guideline authorship into academic promotion criteria, and protecting even modest time for these activities, communicates that voluntary service is valued by the institution rather than extracted from personal time. These changes can begin independently at journals, societies, and academic medical centers.

Conclusions

Peer review, guideline development, and professional society engagement remain indispensable to cardiology. They strengthen scientific rigor, improve patient care, and sustain the intellectual culture of the field. But the expectation that physicians will indefinitely provide this labor without meaningful recognition is increasingly misaligned with modern clinical practice. Physician time is one of the most constrained resources in health care. Sustaining the institutions that underpin cardiovascular science may require the profession to reexamine how it values the labor that maintains them.
