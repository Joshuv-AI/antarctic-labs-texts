Menstrual and reproductive factors and risk of Alzheimer’s disease in elderly women: a cohort study in Eastern China
Authors: Fudong Li, Kun Chen, Tao Zhang, Le Xu, Xue Gu, Junfen Lin, Jianmin Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epidemiological studies suggest that proxies of lifetime estrogen exposure are associated with the risk of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). However, results remain limited and inconsistent. This study aimed to examine whether menstrual and reproductive factors, proxies of estrogen exposure, are associated with the risk of AD in Chinese postmenopausal women. Information on menstrual and reproductive factors was obtained through the baseline survey of the Zhejiang Ageing and Health Cohort Study. During the three follow-up waves, AD diagnoses were conducted. We excluded participants with prevalent cognitive impairment or incomplete data of cognitive assessment at baseline, and incident cases of cognitive impairment without AD diagnosis across follow-up. Log-binomial model and the generalized additive model (GAM), controlled for a wide range of potential confounders, were generated to examine the association. Finally, 5606 women were included, and 597 developed AD during follow-up. The age of participants ranged from 60 to 95 years (mean = 65.7 years, SD = 6.5) at baseline. Women with a longer reproductive lifespan (> 34 years, compared with 32–34 years) had a lower risk of AD (RR = 0.81, 95% CI 0.67–0.98). Menopause due to ovariectomy was associated with a higher risk of AD (RR = 2.17, 95% CI 1.16–4.08), compared to those with natural menopause. The shorter length of menstrual cycles (≤ 27 days, compared with 28–30 days) was associated with an increased risk of AD (RR = 1.47, 95% 1.15–1.87), and shorter length of menstrual period (≤ 3 days, compared with 4–6 days) was associated with a decreased risk of AD (RR = 0.73, 95% 0.59–0.90). Women with 2 or more parity showed a higher risk of AD, while women with 2 or more miscarriages and abortions had a lower risk of AD. Older age at first childbirth (> 22 years, compared with 21–22 years) was associated with a decreased risk of AD (RR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.38–0.58). A U-shaped association was identified between mean breastfeeding duration and AD. Oral contraceptive use was also associated with an increased risk of AD. Our results show that menstrual and reproductive factors are statistically significantly associated with the risk of AD in Chinese postmenopausal women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40329-5.

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most common type of dementia, is becoming a prominent health threat with an enormous impact on the quality of life of elders worldwide1. It is estimated that more than 51.62 million people lived with AD and other dementias worldwide in 20192. Based on estimation, the prevalence of dementia and AD among individuals aged 65 years and older in China was around 5.14% and 3.21% in the years 2008 to 2009 respectively3, and is increasing nationwide. The social and economic burden of AD is magnifying, posing major medical and social problems that threaten elders4.

Notable gender disparity exists in both the incidence and prevalence of AD among the elderly, and elderly women are recognized as a high-risk group for AD onset compared with men5. After menopause, ovarian production of sex steroid hormones ceases and the resulting systemic changes in hormone levels are linked to changes in cellular functioning in the brain6,7. Animal and in vitro studies have identified that estrogen has several possible neuroprotective effects on cognitive function, including inhibition of amyloid-β formation8, protection against oxidative stress9, stimulation of cholinergic activity10, and increase in cerebral blood flow11.

The various experiences and events across women’s reproductive lifespan, including menarche, menopause, pregnancy, childbirth, miscarriage, etc., are hypothesized to affect hormone levels over the life course, though direct connections are not always clear12. Due to the diversity in menstrual and reproductive history, and the use of hormone replacement therapy, different lifetime estrogen exposure may lead to discrepancies in women’s cognitive function and AD risk. As a consequence, menstrual and reproductive factors were investigated in previous epidemiological and clinical studies5. However, most previous studies used dementia or cognitive impairment as outcome measures13. Among the limited number of studies focusing on AD, controversy still exists14–16. In addition, several factors such as features of the menstrual cycle (e.g., regularity, length), which possibly affect or reflect the women’s hormonal exposure to estrogen, have previously received little attention in this field.

In this study, we utilized the data from a large population-based cohort study to comprehensively explore the prospective associations of menstrual and reproductive factors with the risk of AD among Chinese postmenopausal women.

Methods

Source of data

The data were from the Zhejiang Ageing and Health Cohort Study, a community-based cohort study focusing on aging and health among the elderly in Zhejiang province, China. It was conducted by Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Seven counties were selected from 90 counties in Zhejiang province, according to local disease patterns, exposure to certain risk factors, population stability, quality of disease registries, local commitment, and the capacity of staff. One town in each county and several communities in each town were then selected. All permanent residents aged 60 years or older in these selected communities were eligible and expected to participate in the study.

The baseline survey was initiated in 2014, and 10,901 elderly individuals were recruited and completed the survey (10,911 were primarily documented in the database, but 10 records were found duplicated later and were ultimately deleted), accounting for 78.1% of eligible residents in selected communities. Three waves of follow-up surveys were administered in 2015, 2016, and 2019–2020, respectively. Following the baseline survey, residents from selected communities who turned 60 years old were invited annually to join the cohort as new participants. 5377 new participants were recruited during 2015–2021. As of 2021, a total of 16,278 elderly individuals were included in the cohort.

A face-to-face interview based on a self-designed questionnaire was performed by trained research assistants at the baseline survey and for each wave of follow-up survey. The questionnaire included information on demographic characteristics, family status, menstrual and reproductive history, medical disease, behavioral habits, diet habits, injury, depressive symptoms, self-care ability, and cognitive function. Data were checked by staff at the Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Missing data and logical errors were fed back to the initial interviewer, who would try to complete the dataset by reinvestigating the participants.

Selection of participants

Among the 16,278 elderly recruited in the Zhejiang Ageing and Health Cohort Study, 7746 men and 123 premenopausal women (female participants who answered “no” to the question “Do you have menopause already?”) were primarily excluded, leaving 8409 postmenopausal women. Meanwhile, 1275 participants with baseline cognitive impairment and 34 with incomplete baseline data on cognitive assessment were excluded, leaving 7100 participants with normal cognitive function at baseline. Then, 1025 participants without follow-up records were excluded. Among the remaining 6075 participants, 1066 were incident cases of cognitive impairment, and 5009 were normal in cognitive function during the follow-up period. 597 out of 1066 were diagnosed with AD. Finally, 5606 participants were included in this study, involving 597 AD cases and 5009 with normal cognitive function during the follow-up period (Fig. 1). The average follow-up time was 3.96 years, and the maximum follow-up time was 7 years.

Cognitive assessment and AD diagnosis

At baseline and each wave of follow-up, cognitive function was assessed using the Chinese version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) within the questionnaire, which includes 30 items. The maximum score is 30 points, with higher MMSE scores indicating better cognitive function. The widely accepted cut-off point to define cognitive impairment in China (MMSE Chinese Standard, MCS) is education-specific17: 17/18 for illiteracy, 20/21 for people with primary education level, and 24/25 for people with higher than primary education level. Cognitive impairment was defined according to this standard in our study.

Participants with cognitive impairment were further examined by the general practitioners or neurologists to diagnose AD or other types of dementia using the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV) and the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer’s disease and Related Disorders Association-Alzheimer’s Criteria (NINCDS-ADRDA)18.

The DSM-IV criteria were applied to confirm the presence of a progressive dementia syndrome affecting memory and other cognitive functions, while the NINCDS-ADRDA criteria were used to specify a diagnosis of “probable AD” by confirming the clinical profile was typical of AD and by excluding other potential causes of dementia.

Menstrual and reproductive factors

Factors that possibly could be related to endogenous and exogenous estrogen exposure across the reproductive lifespan were collected in the menstrual and reproductive history section within the baseline questionnaire: age at menarche (years), age at menopause (years), cause of menopause (“natural”, “ovariectomy”, “hysterectomy”, and “others”), regularity of menstrual cycle (“regular” and “irregular”), length of menstrual cycle (days), length of menstrual period (days), parity, number of miscarriages and abortions, age at first childbirth (years), cumulative breastfeeding duration (months), oral contraceptive use (“ever” and “never”).

Age at menopause was defined as 1 year without menstruation. Reproductive lifespan, an indicator of endogenous estrogen exposure, was calculated as age at menopause minus age at menarche. The regularity of menstrual cycle was based on the length of menstrual cycle. The menstrual cycle was the time from the first day of menstrual period until the first day of the next menstrual period. The menstrual period referred to the menstrual bleeding days. Mean breastfeeding duration was the ratio between cumulative breastfeeding duration (total months spent on breastfeeding) and the parity.

Continuous variables of menstrual and reproductive factors included reproductive lifespan, length of menstrual cycle, length of menstrual period, age at first childbirth, and mean breastfeeding duration. Median of mean breastfeeding duration (12 months) was used as a cut-off point for categorizing the variable. Individuals above and below the cut-off point were both stratified into 2 subgroups. Finally, individuals were divided into 5 groups for mean breastfeeding duration: <6, 6–11, 12, 13–18, > 18 months. For other continuous variables, tertiles (P33.3, P66.7) were used for categorizing. The above factors will be analyzed in the form of both continuous variables and categorical variables.

Covariates

Based on findings reported in the literature19–21, variables described below were considered as potential confounders and were included in our analysis: age, race (“Han ethnicity” and “minority”), education level (“lower than primary”, “primary”, and “junior middle and above”), marital status (“single”, “married” and “divorced/widowed”), family income (“≤10000”, “10001 ~ 20000”, “20001 ~ 50000”, “50001 ~ 100000” and “>100000” Chinese Yuan), body mass index (“<18.5”, “18.5–23.9”, and “≥24.0” kg/m2), alcohol drinking (“never”, “past” and “current”), exercise (“yes” and “no”), tea drinking (“yes” and “no), hypertension (“presence” and “absence”), diabetes (“presence” and “absence”), coronary heart disease (“presence” and “absence”) and depressive symptom (“yes” and “no”). Detailed information on some of these variables was described as follows.

(1) The medical disease section of the questionnaire contained the items on the presence or absence of 16 common diseases, which were supposed to be formally diagnosed by physicians. Hypertension, diabetes (both type I and II), and coronary heart disease (CHD) were considered in this study. (2) Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by the square of height (cm2), and was divided into three categories (“<18.5”, “18.5–23.9”, and “≥24.0” kg/m2) according to the guideline for Chinese22. (3) Depressive symptom was evaluated using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 scale (PHQ-9), and participants who scored 5 or above were defined as having depressive symptoms23.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were applied to illustrate the general characteristics, and menstrual and reproductive factors of the included postmenopausal women. The associations of general characteristics, menstrual and reproductive factors with risk of AD were examined by t-test, Chi-square test, or Kruskal-Wallis test as appropriate to the variables.

We used the log-binomial regression models24 to assess the effect of menstrual and reproductive factors (categorical variables) on the risk of AD. Crude relative risks (RRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and corresponding P values were calculated in model 1. In model 2, we adjusted for age, race, education level, marital status, family income, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, CHD, alcohol drinking, exercise, tea drinking, and depressive symptoms. Each menstrual and reproductive factor was modeled independently of the other menstrual and reproductive factors.

Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) were performed to explore the linear or curvilinear association between menstrual and reproductive factors (continuous variables, including reproductive lifespan, length of menstrual cycle, length of menstrual period, age at first childbirth, and mean breastfeeding duration) and risk of AD, adjusted for all potential confounders same as done in model 2 of log-binomial regression mentioned above. Each menstrual and reproductive factor was modeled independently of the other menstrual and reproductive factors. The restricted cubic spline smoothing technique25 was used for the dependent variables (menstrual and reproductive factors), and the link function was Logit due to the binary outcome. Three knots were set at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles26 according to the distributions of dependent variables. The value of median was used as a reference for displaying the overall trend of AD risk through the range of dependent variables. These menstrual and reproductive factors as continuous variables were also transformed into categorical variables for analyses using log-binomial regression, as mentioned before. The method of categorizing was described in the ‘menstrual and reproductive factors’ section.

Furthermore, we conducted stratified analyses to test whether observed associations of mean breastfeeding duration with risk of AD varied by other menstrual and reproductive factors. Mean breastfeeding duration was analyzed in the form of both a continuous variable and a categorical variable. Meanwhile, interactions between mean breastfeeding duration and other menstrual and reproductive factors were checked through the addition of cross-product terms in the corresponding main-effects models. Cause of menopause, regularity of menstrual cycle, and oral contraceptive use were not considered in this stage due to the inadequate sample size in some levels of those variables.

Finally, sensitivity analyses were adopted to evaluate the robustness of our results: (1) restriction of women ≥ 5 years after menopause, to exclude any potential impact from those in early postmenopausal stage; (2) excluding incident cases of cognitive impairment at first wave of follow-up survey; (3) excluding women with irregular menstrual cycles or unnatural menopause, as these women may have potential diseases in the female reproductive system; (4) adjusting for other menstrual and reproductive factors when analyzing a certain factor.

All statistical analyses were performed by SAS Software Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and the significance level was at two-tailed probability < 0.05. No correction for multiple testing was performed.

Results

Among the 5606 included postmenopausal women, the mean age was 65.7 years, with a higher mean age (69.6 years) in women with AD (P < 0.01). A total of 122 women were minorities in race (2.2%), and the proportions of minorities were not significantly different between the AD group and normal group (P = 0.24). Besides, the majority of variables were significantly associated with the risk of AD, including education, marital status, family income, BMI, alcohol drinking, tea drinking, exercise, and depressive symptoms. Meanwhile, the distributions of hypertension, diabetes, and CHD showed no difference between women with and without AD (Table 1). The reproductive lifespan was used as a main proxy measure for endogenous estrogen exposure and was defined as the length of time between age at menarche and menopause. Compared to the women with 32–34 years of reproductive lifespan, those with > 34 years of reproductive lifespan were related to a lower risk of AD (RR = 0.81, 95%CI 0.67 ~ 0.98). The results of GAM also suggested that a longer reproductive lifespan was associated with a lower risk of AD. Meanwhile, menopause due to ovariectomy was associated with a higher risk of AD (RR = 2.17, 95%CI 1.16 ~ 4.08), compared with natural menopause. The regularity of menstrual cycle was not associated with the risk of AD (P = 0.44). Among the women with regular menstrual cycles, shorter length of menstrual cycles (≤ 27 days, compared with 28–30 days) was associated with an increased risk of AD (RR = 1.47, 95% 1.15 ~ 1.87). Furthermore, a 27% reduction in the risk of AD was observed in women with a shorter length of menstrual period (≤ 3 days, compared with 4–6 days). The results of GAM indicated a similar association between the length of menstrual cycle, the length of menstrual period, and the risk of AD (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Estrogen exposure is expected to be correlated with the number of pregnancies, including parity, miscarriages and abortions. In our study, we tested the relationship between the parity, the number of miscarriages and abortions, and the risk of AD. Compared to women with one parity, an increase in risk of AD was observed in women with 2–4 (RR = 2.08, 95%CI 1.31 ~ 3.30) and ≥ 5 (RR = 2.31, 95%CI 1.41 ~ 3.79) parity, while no significant difference was found in nulliparous women (P = 0.96). The risk of AD in women with ≥ 2 miscarriages and abortions were lower than that of those with one miscarriage or abortion (RR = 0.69, 95%CI 0.50 ~ 0.95). Nevertheless, women without an miscarriage or abortion did not have a significantly increased or decreased risk of AD in contrast with those experiencing one miscarriage or abortion (P = 0.78). Moreover, we found that first giving childbirth > 22 years was associated with a lower risk of AD (RR = 0.62, 95%CI 0.50–0.77) among parous women, compared with those giving birth at 21–22 years (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Compared to the women with 12 months of mean breastfeeding duration, the fully adjusted RRs (95% CIs) were 2.18 (1.73 ~ 2.74), 1.65 (1.32 ~ 2.05), 1.28 (0.99 ~ 1.65), and 1.84 (1.42 ~ 2.38) for women breastfeeding for an average of < 6, 6–11, 13–18, and > 18 months in Model 3, respectively. Moreover, the results of GAM also suggested a U-shaped association between mean breastfeeding duration and risk of AD, using 12 months of mean breastfeeding duration as the reference (Table 3; Fig. 2).

The results of stratified analysis indicated that there was a difference among the subgroups with ≤ 2 and > 2 parity, for the association of mean breastfeeding duration with AD risk. In the subgroup with ≤ 2 parity, > 18 months of mean breastfeeding duration was associated with an increased risk of AD compared to 12 months, while < 6 months, 6–11 months, and 13–18 months were not associated with AD risk. In the subgroup with > 2 parity, both shorter and longer mean breastfeeding duration were associated with AD risk. There was a statistically significant interaction between the parity and mean breastfeeding duration (P interaction < 0.01). In addition, no significant interaction was found between other menstrual and reproductive factors and mean breastfeeding duration (Table S1, Figure S1, Figure S2).

Exogenous estrogen-related factor (oral contraceptive use) was also considered in our study. We observed a statistically significant, almost 50% increase in risk of AD in women who had ever used oral contraceptives (RR = 1.47, 95%CI 1.05 ~ 2.07) (Table 3).

The sensitivity analyses, involving restriction of women ≥ 5 years after menopause, exclusion of incident cases of cognitive impairment at the first wave of follow-up, exclusion of women with irregular menstrual cycles or unnatural menopause, and adjustment for other menstrual and reproductive factors when analyzing a certain factor, yielded consistent results to the main analysis (Table S2).

Discussion

This study explored the prospective relationship between menstrual and reproductive factors and the risk of AD in Chinese postmenopausal women. The results of our study suggested that a longer reproductive lifespan, shorter length of menstrual period, more miscarriages and abortions, and an older age at first childbirth > 22 years were associated with a lower risk of AD. Menopause due to ovariectomy, shorter length of the menstrual cycle, more parity, and oral contraceptive use were associated with a higher risk of AD. In addition, there was a U-shaped association between mean breastfeeding duration and the risk of AD.

The mean reproductive lifespan of participants in our study was 33.2 years, which is similar to that reported in another study of elderly Chinese women27. We found that a longer reproductive lifespan (> 34 years) was associated with a significantly lower risk of AD, consistent with previous cohort studies28,29 and a case-control study30. Meanwhile, another study combining the data from two cohorts reported null associations31. However, a longitudinal study with a mean follow-up of 26.8 years found that a longer reproductive lifespan was associated with an increased risk of AD, particularly for those with AD onset after age 8532. Geerlings et al. reported that a higher risk of AD was associated with a longer reproductive lifespan in APOE ε4 carriers, whereas no association with AD incidence was observed in noncarriers33. Differences in factors, such as the length of follow-up, age at AD onset diagnosis, and APOE carrier status, may lead to the discrepant results across studies. Although our findings supported the protective effect of the reproductive lifespan on the risk of AD, more studies are needed in the future.

It was suggested that cumulative exposure to estradiol would be greater for women with shorter cycles because they experience more cycles during reproductive years34. Furthermore, irregular cycles were thought to be associated with decreased estrogen exposure34. Our study found that women with shorter cycles (≤ 27 days) had an increased risk of AD, and regularity of menstrual cycles was not associated with the risk of AD. Similarly, another cohort study also reported no effect of cycle regularity on the risk of AD28. These results could not be explained through hormonal mechanisms. In addition, our study also found that women with shorter menstrual periods (≤ 3 days) have a lower risk of AD. Unfortunately, no other epidemiological studies have analyzed the association between the menstrual period and AD risk. A previous review pointed out that estrogen levels were relatively low during the menstrual phase of cycle35, which might partially explain our findings. At present, there is still a lack of observational studies exploring the relationship between the length and regularity of menstrual cycle, the length of menstrual period, and the risk of AD. Therefore, we encourage further research into this question.

We explored the effect of both parity and miscarriages/abortions on the risk of AD. Our results suggested that parity and miscarriages/abortions had different effects on cognitive function. We found that multiparous women (2 or more parity) had an elevated risk of AD, compared with primiparous women (1 parity). Furthermore, previous studies found that more parity were associated with a greater risk of AD31,36,37. Estrogens plasma levels, including Estrone Sulfate, Estrone, Estradiol and Estriol, rise markedly during pregnancy. Of the unconjugated estrogens, estradiol had the highest plasma concentration. Besides, Estrone sulfate level was twice that of estradiol38. However, parous women had lower levels of estrogen, estradiol in particular, than nulliparous women over the lifetime, and inverse association was found between estrogen sulfate and parity39,40. As a consequence, the role of parity in late-life risk of AD found by our study supported the protective effect of estrogen exposure. Moreover, our study discovered that women with more miscarriages and abortions had a lower risk of AD, compared with those experiencing only one miscarriage or abortion. A study combined the data from two Korean cohorts and obtained the same results as ours31. Plasma estradiol remained within the range of the normal menstrual cycle till the fifth week of pregnancy, and became two to three times the peak estradiol concentration of the normal menstrual cycle at eighth week38. Although we did not collect the information about when participants experienced the abortions or miscarriages, it was reported that > 90% of abortions41 and > 80% of miscarriages42 occurred within first trimester. Lastly, the association for nulliparous women with risk of AD change direction after adjustment in this study, and the covariate “exercise” drives this change.

Age at first childbirth influences a women’s lifetime estrogen exposure, potentially mediated by the decline in circulating estrogen levels following the first parity. Only one previous observational study focused on AD, while the outcome measures of the remaining studies were dementia or cognitive impairment. Indeed, Fox et al. found that elderly women who had their first child at the age of 21 years or later had a lower risk of AD than those having their first child earlier28. The UK Biobank Study, as well as the U.S. Health and Retirement Study reported consistent associations on dementia43,44. The 10/66 study cohort study45 found no association with dementia, but the diagnosis procedure was different from those of other studies. Our study confirmed that a later age at first childbirth was associated with a decreasing risk of AD. This finding is consistent with the fact that higher lifetime estrogen levels, influenced by later age at first birth, would lead to long-term beneficial effects against AD.

Breastfeeding infants leads to various hormonal changes and consequently affects the health of mothers. One British cohort study46, involving 81 elderly women with 12 months as a maximum mean breastfeeding duration, suggested that a longer mean breastfeeding duration was associated with a reduced risk of AD. Other cohort studies29,32, ignoring the impact of the parity, merely analyzed the total breastfeeding duration, but not the mean duration. The results of our study showed that shorter mean breastfeeding duration was associated with a higher risk of AD compared to those who breastfed for 12 months on average, consistent with the findings of the British cohort study. More importantly, our study possessed a larger sample size and additionally explored the association among women who breastfed for more than 12 months on average. The curvilinear association was found in our study using the GAM model, whose strengths included the ability to determine the natural shape of the nonlinear relationship. Hence, our study might more accurately describe the association. The mechanisms responsible for the effects of mean breastfeeding duration on the risk of AD remains unclear. The endocrine changes of lactation are complex. During breastfeeding, various hormones fluctuate besides estradiol, such as progesterone, oxytocin, and prolactin47. The influences of these hormones on cognitive function are diverse and complex. That may lead to the nonlinear correlation observed in this study, which needs to be confirmed in more observational studies. Furthermore, an interaction was found between the parity and mean breastfeeding duration. A U-shaped association was present among women with > 2 parity, while shorter mean breastfeeding duration (< 12 months) was not associated with AD risk among women with ≤ 2 parity. Future epidemiological evidence is warranted, and the possible mechanism also needs to be studied.

Gynecologic surgery performed before natural menopause can lead to premature cessation of endogenous hormone secretion48. One study, including women from 2 longitudinal studies, reported that earlier age at menopause was associated with increased AD neuropathology49, but the type of surgery was not analyzed. Other studies, with dementia as the outcome measure, supported that hysterectomy44,50 or ovariectomy15was associated with an increased risk of dementia. A combined analysis of data on two cohorts suggested that the risk of dementia was increased in women who had a hysterectomy alone, and further increased in women who had a hysterectomy with ovariectomy51. Our study also found that menopause induced by ovariectomy was associated with an increased risk of AD, supporting the potential negative impact of premature cessation of sex hormones on cognitive function and brain health. Meanwhile, our study found no association between menopause caused by hysterectomy and AD risk. There is no biological plausible mechanism in why a simple hysterectomy without ovariectomy should influence menopausal age. The age at surgery was not collected within the questionnaire, which may affect the results of the association analysis15. Additionally, different outcome measures might also lead to inconsistent results, and studies focusing on the specific type of dementia (e.g., AD) are needed.

Exogenous estrogen uses and related factors may also modify the risk of AD. There are two main types of oral contraceptives: combined (estrogen plus progestin) and progestin-only52. Conflicting results were reported in previous epidemiological studies regarding the effects of oral contraceptives on cognitive function or dementia, and none of them used AD as an outcome variable. Some studies found the protective effects of oral contraceptives on cognitive function53 and dementia29,44, while others found no association for cognitive function54,55. In contrast, our study suggested that oral contraceptive was associated with an increased risk of AD. It is important to note that the particular type and duration of oral contraceptives used by participants were not collected in our study, which were also rarely reported by the aforementioned epidemiological studies. One review has pointed out that continuous progesterone exposure is associated with inhibition of estrogen actions56. Besides the type of oral contraceptive, the short- and long-term effects of oral contraceptives may differ57. Oral contraceptive use in adolescence has the potential to alter the organizational effects of endogenous sex hormones through chronic ovarian hormone suppression58. In adults, even short-term use is associated with gray matter volume changes, though it is unclear whether the magnitude of change tracks with total duration of use over longer timescales58. We encourage more future population-based studies with detailed information on the particular type and duration of oral contraceptives, and using AD as an outcome measure.

There are some limitations and methodological issues: (1) There was no detailed information on the disease of the reproductive system in the questionnaires. For instance, ovarian cancer59,60, breast cancer61, and endometrial cancer62 were found in relation to menstrual and reproductive factors, and estrogen might be involved in the mechanism of these diseases. (2) We did not collect the information on the specific type and duration of oral contraceptive use. Meanwhile, long-acting reversible contraception and other long lasting contraceptive methods were not included in this study, which may impact the results. (3) This study did not analyze the association between estrogen replacement therapy (ERT) use and AD risk. The data on ERT use was actually collected through the questionnaires. However, only 12 (0.3%) reported ever using of ERT. Most of the participants experienced perimenopause before the year 2000. ERT has been gradually accepted and used in recent years in China. However, even in urban areas, there are still many menopausal women who have not received ERT nowadays63. That may explain the low rate of ERT use in our study. We also can’t exclude the possibility of reporting bias of ERT use as well. (4) The results of the mean breastfeeding duration should be interpreted with caution. Although the lowest risk of AD was found at 12 months and 13–18 months, our finding does not mean 10 or 20 months of average breastfeeding is harmful. 1- or 2-month error margin often exists when women recall breastfeeding duration. It supports that the appropriate mean breastfeeding duration was preferable (neither too short nor too long) for women’s brain health. (5) Due to the sensitive nature of reproductive health, some individuals are prone to social desirability bias and may misreport their health conditions to appear more “normal”. (6) The menstrual and reproductive history was obtained from subjective recall. Therefore, potential misclassification cannot be excluded. This misclassification is probably non-differential, resulting the associations to attenuate toward the null. (7) No context was provided when asking regularity of menstrual cycle, the validity of this self-reported variable may be limited. (8) Considering that all the participants were Chinese elderly postmenopausal women, the representativeness of our results might be restricted to Chinese or East Asian women. (9) A number of participants had no follow-up records, so the selection bias could not be entirely excluded. (10) We cannot rule out that some of the significant associations found in this study may be due to multiple testing. (11) We did not consider genetic factors, such as apolipoprotein E (APOE) carrier status, and we have no information on family history of AD. (12) We did not collect the environmental data, many environmental exposures mimic estrogen and can alter reproductive factors as well as AD.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. (1) Prospective study design, and restricting participants to those of normal cognitive function at baseline, allowed us to assess the temporal relationship between former exposure and subsequent outcome. (2) Our study was based on a large community-based sample, and the results were relatively robust and might be potentially correlated with the broader population of elderly women in Zhejiang province. (3) A wide range of covariates were adjusted to control for potential confounding factors. (4) With the application of GAMs, this study could naturally describe the shape of the curvilinear relationship between menstrual and reproductive factors (in the form of continuous variables) and the risk of AD. (5) Many menstrual and reproductive factors have been analyzed in this study, and several of them had not been studied previously, including the length of menstrual cycle, the length of menstrual period, and mean breastfeeding duration. (6) Previous studies indicated that estrogen levels in Chinese women were generally lower than those in Caucasian women in both pre- and post-menopause periods64,65. The limited number of studies based on the Chinese population makes our study a valuable extension of previous work.

In conclusion, our results show that menstrual and reproductive factors are statistically significantly associated with the risk of AD in Chinese postmenopausal women. Several proxies of higher estrogen exposure were associated with lower risk of AD. Future studies are needed to investigate the association of menstrual and reproductive with risk of AD.
