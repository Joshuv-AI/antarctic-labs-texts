Defining Exercise Hemodynamics and Function After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement: The DEFINE-TAVR Prospective Study
Authors: Tayyab Shah, Lissa Sugeng, Amit N. Vora, Yousif Ahmad, Cody G. Pietras, Helen Parise, Daniela Tirziu, Karen Bachand, John K. Forrest, Alexandra J. Lansky
Published: 2026

Background

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has transformed the treatment of severe aortic stenosis; however, up to 20% to 30% of patients continue to experience symptoms that limit quality of life after the procedure.1 As TAVR expands to lower-risk and younger patients, addressing these persistent symptoms is becoming increasingly important. The Small Annuli Randomized to Evolut or SAPIEN Trial (SMART) recently demonstrated that self-expanding valves (SEVs) are associated with lower resting mean gradients than balloon-expanding valves (BEVs), particularly in patients with small annuli.2 Whether these hemodynamic differences translate into symptomatic or clinical benefits remains unknown. Even when resting gradients are normal, some patients may experience exertional symptoms owing to disproportionate increases in mean gradients with exertion—a parameter rarely assessed in clinical practice.3 Indeed, to date, only 2 small retrospective studies have evaluated exercise echocardiogram after TAVR.4,5

Methods

The Defining Exercise Hemodynamics and Function after Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement (DEFINE-TAVR; NCT05567809) study, is a prospective, single-center observational study conducted at Yale New Haven Hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Yale New Haven Hospital institutional review board. All patients provided informed consent. Eligible patients had left ventricular ejection fraction of >35%, underwent successful TAVR without complication, had native annular diameters of 21 to 25 mm on computed tomography, and were able to exercise on a supine bike. Patients presenting with cardiogenic shock or those planned to undergo another cardiac surgery within 12 months were excluded. Valve choice followed standard clinical decision making by the multidisciplinary valve team.

Resting echocardiography was performed before TAVR, prior to discharge, and 30 days and 12 months after TAVR. Supine bicycle stress echocardiography was conducted at 30 days and 12 months using standardized acquisition guidelines. The primary end point was mean prosthetic aortic valve gradient during exercise at 12 months and was assessed by a blinded independent core laboratory. Given an anticipated sample size of 50 patients, no formal hypothesis testing was planned; analyses were descriptive and exploratory, including comparisons by valve type (SEV vs BEV). Secondary end points included other echocardiographic parameters of valvular and myocardial function and VARC-3–defined clinical safety outcomes adjudicated by an independent clinical events committee. P values were calculated using the Fisher exact test for binary variables and Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and are presented for descriptive purposes only. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Between October 2022 and December 2023, 28 patients were enrolled; the study was terminated early because of slow enrollment. By 12 months, 8 patients withdrew consent, 9 patients were unwilling to return for follow-up imaging, and 1 patient died. We report the results of the 10 patients who completed 12-month echocardiographic evaluation (Table 1). The mean age was 76 years, 70% were men, and the mean annular diameter was 23.9 mm, without significant differences by valve type. Six patients received an SEV (29-mm Evolut valves; Medtronic) and 4 patients received a BEV (two 23-mm and two 26-mm SAPIEN S3 valves; Edwards Lifesciences). One patient with each valve type had patient-prosthesis mismatch based on VARC-3 criteria. At 12 months, SEVs had lower mean gradients than BEVs at both rest (6.5 vs 13.8 mm Hg) and exercise (8.0 vs 21.5 mm Hg). Patients with both valve types showed improvements in global longitudinal strain from baseline to 12 months at rest and with exercise (Table 1). Exercise mean gradients were strongly correlated with resting mean gradients (slope, 1.48 ± 0.23; R2 = 0.84); however 1 patient (74-year-old woman with a body mass index of >50 kg/m2 who received a 23-mm BEV that did not have patient-prosthesis mismatch or significant paravalvular leak) was a marked outlier, demonstrating an increase in mean gradient from 11 to 25 mm Hg with exercise (studentized residual = 4.9).

Discussion and conclusion

Although this was a small, nonrandomized, exploratory pilot study with notable attrition, it represents the first prospective evaluation of TAVR valve performance during exercise. The findings raise several important considerations:1.Valve type and hemodynamics: SEVs may have lower echocardiographic exercise gradients compared with BEVs, consistent with resting results from the SMART trial. This could have implications for valve selection in younger, lower-risk patients in whom residual symptoms may be less tolerated.2.Predictive value of resting gradients: Resting gradients predicted exercise gradients for most patients, yet one patient showed marked discordance, suggesting that standard resting echocardiography may miss clinically significant exertional gradients.3.Future directions: Exercise echocardiography may offer a more sensitive assessment of prosthetic valve performance and could help explain persistent symptoms in otherwise “successful” TAVR cases.
