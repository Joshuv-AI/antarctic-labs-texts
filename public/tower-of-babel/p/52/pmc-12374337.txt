Validation and comparison of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 in myopic patients requiring corneal refractive surgery in China
Authors: Gang Liang, Ai-Xue Zhang, Fang-Yu Li, Jian-Hua Liu, Ya-Jie Zheng, Yu Qin, Yue-Zu Li, Rong Ma, Chen-Wei Pan, Dan-Lin Li, Pei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeDifferent health-related quality of life scales are suitable for various populations and scenarios; hence, the selection of measurement tools must be based on research objectives and focal points. To facilitate the choice between EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 in clinical and economic research of myopic patients undergoing corneal refractive surgery, the study aims to assess and compare EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 among the patients in China.MethodsA total of 411 participants with a mean age of 25.6 years were recruited. Information collected including demographic characteristics, EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1. The ceiling effect was evaluated by calculating the proportion of participants who reported the best possible health states. Agreement between EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 health utility scores (HUSs) was tested using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Bland-Altman plot. Convergent validity of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs was evaluated using the Spearman correlation coefficient (r) with VF-14 as a calibration standard.ResultsCeiling effects were 75.2% and 40.4% for EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1, respectively. ICC between the two HUSs was 0.611 (average-measure). Bland-Altman plots showed that the agreement varied across different HUS segments. Correlation between EQ-5D-5L HUS and VF-14 (r = 0.363) was higher than that between SF-6Dv1 HUS and VF-14 (r = 0.226). EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs can only distinguish one clinical condition (i.e., chronic pain symptom); but they can identify health differences across all self-reported known-groups. EQ-5D-5L HUS had greater sensitivity; but SF-6Dv1 HUS was more responsive at follow-ups.ConclusionsEQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUS cannot be used interchangeably in myopic patients undergoing corneal refractive surgery. It appears that EQ-5D-5L HUS performs better in terms of convergent validity and sensitivity; but SF-6Dv1 HUS is more responsive.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-025-02411-w.

Introduction

Myopia is a common visual problem and one of the major causes of vision loss worldwide [1, 2]. Recent trends indicate an escalating prevalence of myopia, which is projected to affect 49.8% of the global population by 2050 [3]. Eastern Asian countries, such as China, exhibit the highest prevalence. For example, it is estimated that about 80% of 18-year-old school children and 17% of adults aged 40 years and older suffer from myopia in China [4]. Corneal refractive surgery is an effective treatment option for myopic patients. It can not only correct the patients’ vision but also improve their health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [5–8].

HRQoL is a comprehensive health outcome measure that reflects an individual’s health status from physical, mental, and social aspects [9]. It is commonly measured using standardized instruments. According to the range of populations to which they are applied, the instruments can generally be divided into two categories: disease-specific and generic [10]. The former is designed to assess HRQoL in specific disease populations and is better able to capture the specific symptoms and problems of patients with that disease. The latter can be applied to a wide range of both general and diseased populations, enabling comparisons and assessment of HRQoL across different populations.

Several disease-specific HRQoL instruments, such as the Quality of Life Impact of Refractive Correction (QIRC) [8, 11] and the Institute for Eye Research multidimensional QOL scale for myopia (IER QOL) [12], have been developed and validated in myopic patients [8, 11–14]. However, there is a lack of validation evidence of generic instruments in the patients. Among the generic HRQoL instruments, EQ-5D and SF-6D are the two widely used [15], mainly because they can convert HRQoL information into health utility score (HUS) for calculating quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). In China, both of them have been adopted in various settings, including clinical trials, population health surveys, and economic evaluations [16–18]. Although EQ-5D and SF-6D are widely used generic Multi-Attribute Utility Instruments (MAUIs) for deriving HUS, they differ structurally in descriptive systems and exhibit different dimension-specific sensitivities. Recent evidence revealed their divergent psychometric performance across different populations in East and South-East Asia, indicating the two instruments may yield conflicting information [19]. Several studies have evaluated the psychometric performance of the EQ-5D and SF-6D in patients undergoing cataract surgery and those with age-related macular degeneration or glaucoma. For example, Ang et al. demonstrated moderate correlations between EQ-5D HUS and Visual Function Index (VF-14) score in cataract patients, suggesting the construct validity of the EQ-5D in the patients with severe visual impairments [20]. Yet, their comparative validity in myopic surgery patients who are typically younger with mild visual impairment remains unexamined.

Hence, the study aimed to evaluate and compare the measurement properties of EQ-5D-5L (the new version of EQ-5D) which has been validated in those with vision impairment in China [21] and SF-6Dv1 (the original version of SF-6D) in myopic patients undergoing refractive surgery in China. Accurately measuring their HRQOL according to generic instruments poses unique challenges due to the characteristics of the patients described above.

Methods

Study design and population

The patients requiring refractive surgery at Yunnan University Hospital between February 2021 to May 2023 were enrolled for the study. The patients underwent a series of ocular examinations to assess the visual acuity and to determine the appropriate approach for surgery (FS-LASIK, FEM-LASIK or SMILE). These examinations included measurement of intraocular pressure, subjective and computerized refractive examinations, corneal topography, and fundus examination. In the current study, the patients had to meet the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 18 and 40 years old; (2) central corneal thickness between 480 and 600 nm; (3) change in ocular accommodation less than 0.5 D in the past two years. The exclusion criteria included: (1) corneal abnormalities such as progressive corneal developmental diseases, history of previous ocular surgeries, glaucoma, and corneal scars; (2) corrected visual acuity below 0.8; (3) systemic connective tissue diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis; (4) breastfeeding or pregnancy; (5) other contraindications for refractive surgery. After confirming that the patients met the aforementioned criteria, the assistant ophthalmologist informed them about the purpose and protocol of the study. All inclusion and exclusion criteria were assessed and confirmed by licensed ophthalmologists based on standard clinical procedures. Subsequently, the patients were required to provide their informed consent. Questionnaire survey was then conducted during the initial consultation, as well as 1 day, 1 week, and 1 month after the surgery, respectively. The questionnaires included the VF-14, EQ-5D-5L, SF-6Dv1, Chronic pain (Headache, backache, chest or abdominal pain, joint pain, etc.), Acute symptoms (Cold, fever, diarrhoea, fall, trauma, etc.), disease conditions (Hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, heart disease, stroke, diabetes, etc.) and socio-demographic information. Socio-demographic information such as gender, ethnicity, age, occupation, education level, and BMI were only collected during the initial consultation through self-reporting.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Soochow university.

HRQoL instrument

Visual Function Index (VF-14)

VF-14 was initially developed to evaluate visual function in cataract patients on the day of interview, and is now widely utilized for patients with other ocular conditions [22]. It comprises 14 visual function items related to daily activities including reading small print, reading a book or newspaper, reading large print, recognizing acquaintances, seeing stairs and curbs, seeing various signs or signboards, performing fine work, completing forms or writing checks, engaging in recreational activities, participating in sports, cooking, watching television, daytime and night time driving [23]. Respondents were firstly required to determine if the activity for each item was impacted or not applicable; and if impacted, they were required to evaluate the level of impact further. The degree of impact was classified into five levels and scored accordingly: 0 (unable to do the activity), 1 (very difficult), 2 (moderately difficult), 3 (a little difficult), and 4 (no difficulty). “Not applicable” were recorded as missing values. Then, all valid item scores were summed to calculate the average score, which was multiplied by 25 to arrive at a total VF-14 score. The total score ranges from 0 (worst condition) to 100 (best condition) [24].

EQ-5D-5L

EQ-5D-5L is a new version of the widely used EQ-5D, assessing the HRQoL of respondents on the day of survey [25]. It consists of two components: a health state descriptive system and a Visual Analog Scale (EQ VAS). The former covers five dimensions: Mobility (MO), Self-Care (SC), Usual Activity (UA), Pain/Discomfort (PD), and Anxiety/Depression (AD). Each dimension has five severity levels (i.e., no problems, slight problems moderate problems, severe problems, and extreme problems), which can be represented by the numbers 1(no problems) through 5 (extreme problems/unable to), respectively. It thus describes 3,125 health states which can be converted into HUSs using the corresponding value set. In this study, the Chinese EQ-5D-5L value set developed by Luo et al. was used [26], ranging from − 0.391 to 1.

SF-6Dv1

SF-6Dv1, developed from the Medical Outcomes 36-item short-form Health Survey (SF-36), is used to assess the health status of respondents over the past four weeks [27]. It includes six dimensions: Physical Functioning (PF), Role Limitation (RL), Social Functioning (SF), Pain (PN), Mental Health (MH), and Vitality(VT). Each dimension contains 4–6 levels that can be represented by 1–6 numbers respectively. It defines a total of 18,000 health states, which can also be converted into HUSs using a value set. In the study, the SF-6Dv1 HUS was calculated based on the SF-6Dv1 value set developed based on the general population in Hong Kong [28], ranging from 0.315 to 1.0 [29].

Clinical information

The preoperative refractive examination results (i.e., preoperative equivalent spherical lens diopters) were used to determine the degree of myopia including mild (−3.00D ~ 0D), moderate (−6.00D~−3.00D) and high (−6.00D or less).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

The characteristics of refractive surgery patients were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables (e.g., EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs, VF-14 overall score) were described using mean and standard deviation (SD), and presented their distributions using histograms. Categorical variables were depicted using frequencies and percentages. We also evaluated the ceiling effect of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 descriptive systems by examining the proportion of respondents who reported the best possible health states. It is exhibited if more than 15% of respondents reported full health [30].

Agreement between EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1

Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Bland-altman plot were used to test the agreement between EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs. The ICC was calculated using a two-way random effects model with an absolute agreement definition, and 95% confidence intervals were reported. According to the guideline proposed by Koo and Li [31], ICC values less than 0.5 indicate poor agreement, values between 0.5 and 0.75, 0.75 and 0.9, and greater than 0.9 indicate moderate agreement, good agreement, and excellent agreement, respectively. Bland-Altman plot can visually show the agreement between EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs across different utility segments. Its horizontal axis means the average value of the two HUS, and the vertical axis refers to the difference between them. When the points in the Bland-Altman plot are evenly distributed around the zero line, it indicates good agreement between the two measurement tools [32].

Convergent validity of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1

Convergent validity was evaluated through: (a) intercorrelations between EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs, (b) their correlations with the VF-14 total score, and (c) associations between conceptually aligned dimensions between the two instruments. Dimension pairs were hypothesized based on established measurement frameworks [33]: MO and SC in EQ-5D-5L with PF in SF-6Dv1; UA with RL and SF; PD with PN; and AD with MH. Meanwhile, VT in SF-6Dv1 was analyzed separately due to the lack of EQ-5D-5L counterpart. Spearman’s correlation coefficient (r) was used to quantify the degree of correlation: r value of > 0.5, 0.35–0.5, 0.2–0.35, and < 0.2 indicate strong, moderate, weak and poor correlations, respectively [34].

Known-group validity and sensitivity of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1

Known-group validity was tested by examining whether EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs could distinguish between subgroups known to have different health statuses [35]. F-statistic value from ANOVA test was used to assess know-groups validity. That is, if the p-value of F-value is less than 0.05, the HUS could possess the known-group validity. In the analysis, nineteen known groups were defined, including 4 groups of clinical indicators: chronic pain symptoms today, acute symptoms in the last week, with or without disease and myopic degree (mild, moderate, high); and 15 groups according to VF-14 questionnaire. They included the VF-14 total score (≤ VF-14 mean score; >VF-14 mean score), and each of VF-14 questions (with and without impact for each of the 14 items). We hypothesized that the patients without symptoms and disease, mild myopia, >VF-14 mean score, no impact for VF-14 items, would report better HRQoL.

Sensitivity was further assessed using relative efficiency (RE) [36], which is calculated according to the ratio of the F-values of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs. In this study, the F-value of EQ-5D-5 L HUS was adopted as the reference, thus EQ-5D-5 L HUS would have higher sensitivity if the RE < 1.

Responsiveness of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1

The analyses were based on the follow-up data of patients for each round, respectively. The patients whose naked eye visual acuity improved to 0.8 or more after treatment were defined as the improved group [37]. The responsiveness of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs in the improved group was assessed using the standardized response mean (SRM) and the standardized effect size (SES). SRM and SES values of > 0.8, 0.5–0.8, and 0.2–0.5 were interpreted as large, moderate, and small responsiveness, respectively [38, 39].

Data were analyzed using STATA 15.0, MedCalc and SPSS 25.0 software. A two-sided approach was used for all statistical tests, and with p < 0.05, the difference was considered statistically significant.

The mean values of VF-14 dimension scores were all above 3.0, with the highest scores for the reading large print dimension and the lowest for driving at night, at 3.97 and 3.01, respectively (Table S3). As a result, the mean (SD) of VF-14 total score at baseline was 85.5 (13.23), and increased over time at follow-up, reaching a maximum of 87.9 (7.66) at 1 month postoperatively.

Compared to EQ-5D-5L, SF-6Dv1 detected more problems on all similar dimensions (Fig. 1 and Table S4). For EQ-5D-5L, AD dimension (17.3%) was reported the most problems; while for SF-6Dv1, VT dimension (45.3%) was identified more problems. The mean SF-6Dv1 HUS was slightly lower than the mean EQ-5D-5L HUS (0.95 ± 0.06 vs. 0.98 ± 0.05). In addition, high ceiling effects were observed in both EQ-5D-5L (75.2%) and SF-6Dv1 (40.4%). As shown in Fig. 2, the VF-14 total score, EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs were all left-skewed distributions with skewness of −2.355, −4.014, and − 1.508, respectively. Compared to EQ-5D-5L HUS, the distribution of SF-6Dv1 HUS was closer to normal distribution.

Agreement between EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1

The ICC between EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs at baseline was 0.439 (95% CI: 0.241–0.582) for single measures and 0.611 (95% CI: 0.389–0.736) for average measures, indicating moderate agreement. The Bland-Altman plot further indicated that the degree of agreement varied across utility segments. Specifically, the agreement was good between 0.9 and 1.0; while they had poor agreement less than 0.9. Furthermore, the agreement of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs improved as the health status of the patients improved (Figure S1).

Convergent validity of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1

EQ-5D-5L HUS was moderately correlated with VF-14 total score (r = 0.363, P < 0.01); whereas SF-6Dv1 HUS was weakly correlated with the score (r = 0.226, P < 0.01). The dimension-level correlations between EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 were generally weak to moderate (r > 0.2) (Table 3). Specifically, the EQ-5D-5L dimensions of MO, UA, and AD showed moderate correlations with the SF-6Dv1 dimensions of PF, SF, and MH, respectively. Among them, the strongest correlation was observed between anxiety/depression in EQ-5D-5L and mental health in SF-6Dv1 (r = 0.381).

Known-group validity and sensitivity of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1

Table 4 and Table S5 display the results of the known-groups validity and sensitivity of the EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs. For clinically defined known-groups, both instruments showed limited discriminative ability, as they could only distinguish the presence or absence of chronic pain symptoms. In contrast, for the self-reported known-groups, both EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 could significantly discriminate all subgroups (P < 0.05 for all). Overall, EQ-5D-5L was more sensitive than SF-6Dv1 in most self-reported groups, except for the daytime and nighttime driving groups; where SF-6Dv1 HUS showed slightly higher RE.

Responsiveness of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1

Table 5 presents the responsiveness of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs in improved group. EQ-5D-5L HUS did not demonstrate responsiveness at any of the three follow-ups (p > 0.05 for all). In contrast, SF-6Dv1 HUS showed responsiveness (p < 0.05) at all the follow-ups, with SRMs of 0.23, 0.26, and 0.36, and SESs of 0.20, 0.23, and 0.30, respectively.

Discussion

To our knowledge, it was the first study to systematically validate and compare EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 in myopic patients undergoing corneal refractive surgery. The findings suggest that both instruments exhibit good convergent validity; they also showed good ability to discriminate HRQoL difference in self-reported groups but their ability in detecting clinically known group difference was limited. In terms of sensitivity and responsiveness, EQ-5D-5L HUS may be more sensitive; while SF-6Dv1 HUS showed better responsiveness.

In this study, we observed that the ceiling effects of EQ-5D-5L (75.2%) and SF-6Dv1 (40.4%) were much higher than the results of previous studies of ophthalmic diseases [40]. This may be due to our participants were younger, with a mean age of 25.6 years whereas cataract patients are predominantly older. Besides, aligning with previous findings [41–44], the ceiling effect of EQ-5D-5L was much higher than that of SF-6Dv1 and the mean EQ-5D-5L HUS was also higher (0.98 vs. 0.95). Two reasons could be given to the difference. Firstly, the recall periods of the two surveys are different. EQ-5D-5L investigates the patient’s self-reported health status on the same day, whereas the SF-6Dv1 covers the past four weeks [45]. Theoretically, a longer survey recall period could detect more health problems. Another explanation is that they differ in the number of dimensions. SF-6Dv1 has one more dimension than EQ-5D-5L, which may capture more of the HRQoL impairments.

Although ICC value indicated moderate overall agreement between the two HUSs, Bland-Altman plot showed that the difference in HUSs was greater along with poorer health status, which is in line with the results of previous disease studies [46, 47]. Moreover, EQ-5D-5L consistently generated lower HUSs when the value was below 0.8. Hence, the two HUSs can not be used interchangeably in the patients.

We found that the correlation between EQ-5D-5L HUS and SF-6Dv1 HUS was stronger than their correlations with the VF-14 total score, which is similar to some prior findings of other diseases [48]. This can be partly due to the fact that the EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 are generic HRQoL scales, whereas the VF-14 is a scale specific to visual disorders. Apart from the vitality dimension, all other SF-6Dv1 dimensions correspond to similar EQ-5D-5L dimensions (Table S4). Additionally, in this study, the SF-6Dv1’s vitality dimension exhibited the highest prevalence of self-reported problems, which could potentially diminish the correlation between the SF-6Dv1 HUS and VF-14 total score.

In contrast, our study showed only weak to moderate associations between the EQ-5D-5L or SF-6Dv1 HUSs and the VF-14 in myopic patients, indicating limited sensitivity of both instruments in a younger, healthier population with relatively mild visual impairment. Similarly, previous research by Khadka et al. and Craig et al. found that while generic utility instruments could capture vision-related quality of life to some extent, they often lacked precision in early-stage or non-blinding conditions, echoing our current findings. These comparisons highlight the need to interpret generic utility scores with caution when applied to non-visually impaired or early-stage ophthalmic populations such as myopia. Importantly, our study is the first to validate both the EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 among Chinese myopic patients, providing novel evidence for the limited convergent validity and responsiveness of these instruments in this specific group.

In terms of known-group validity, EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs showed limited ability to distinguish HRQOL difference between clinically known groups. One potential reason is the younger profile of the patients. That is, they had fewer diseases or acute symptoms. For example, only 3.9% of patients were diagnosed with other diseases such as diabetes and hypertension. As for the self-reported known groups, both EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 could distinguish all of them. Overall, EQ-5D-5L was more sensitive than SF-6Dv1, which is consistent with the results of a previous study in patients with end-stage renal disease, reporting that EQ-5D-5L was more sensitive towards clinical health outcomes [46]. One possible reason is that the two had different utility value sets. Specifically, the EQ-5D-5L HUS ranged from − 0.391 to 1, whereas the SF-6Dv1 HUS ranged from 0.35 to 1. Therefore, when assessing similar health states, the EQ-5D-5L might have shown more significant changes and thus had better sensitivity.

EQ-5D-5L HUS showed no responsiveness at three follow-ups in the improved group. This was probably attributed to the higher ceiling effect of EQ-5D-5L at baseline. As this study focused on the improved group, the higher ceiling effect limited the space for the improvement of the EQ-5D-5L at follow-up. Whereas SF-6Dv1 HUS demonstrated better responsiveness at all three follow-ups. This is consistent with previous findings that SF-6Dv1 shows better responsiveness than EQ-5D-5L [41]. In addition, we observed enhanced responsiveness of SF-6Dv1 with follow-up time. Further analyses showed that this could relate to the extent of changes in each dimension of SF-6Dv1 at different follow-ups. For instance, one day, one week, and one month after surgery, the proportion of PN dimension reporting no problems was 86.4%, 88.9%, and 94.4%, respectively (Figure S2).

From a clinimetric perspective [49], the potential clinical utility of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 in the patients lies in their complementary performance. EQ-5D-5L, with its higher sensitivity in detecting baseline health differences, may be particularly useful for preoperative screening to identify patients with coexisting physical or psychological comorbidities that could affect surgical outcomes. In contrast, SF-6Dv1 demonstrates superior responsiveness, especially in capturing changes relevant to VT and MH dimensions during follow-up (Fig. 1), which is important for the patients whose HRQoL may be disproportionately affected by transient visual disturbances or recovery-related stress. However, both instruments lack sensitivity in capturing vision-specific impacts, particularly on psychological well-being and function (e.g., concerns about visual fluctuations, glare, night vision difficulties, or activity limitations). Hence, the development and use of vision-related ‘bolt-on’ dimension addressing these vision-related aspects to the generic HRQoL instruments would be useful to enhance their content validity and clinical relevance [50].

Our study had the advantage of multiple follow-ups. However, there were also several limitations. Firstly, the respondents were enrolled from one hospital, which may have limited the extrapolation of our findings. Secondly, most of the participants were younger, resulting in smaller sample sizes for some subgroups and which may have affected known-group validity. Moreover, the order of use of the EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 was not randomized, which may have introduced order effect. Additionally, only the VF-14 was used as the anchor for the assessment of convergent validity. VF-14 primarily measures visual functioning, which does not fully capture the full spectrum of HRQoL. Future studies should consider other vision-specific HRQoL tools such as NEI-VFQ-25 or QIRC to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of HRQoL in myopic patients undergoing refractive surgery.

Conclusion

In conclusion, EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs are not interchangeable in Chinese myopic patients undergoing refractive surgery. Both EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 HUSs show acceptable convergent validity and known-group validity. The former seems to perform better both in terms of convergent validity and sensitivity. Whereas SF-6Dv1 HUS had better responsiveness than EQ-5D-5L HUS. The findings suggest that EQ-5D-5 L may be more appropriate for preoperative assessments and distinguishing baseline health differences; while SF-6Dv1 could be better suited for postoperative follow-up and monitoring subtle improvements in HRQOL.
