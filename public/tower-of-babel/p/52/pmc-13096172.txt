The role of preparatory muscle activity in a one-step approach to vertical jumping
Authors: Kiyohiro Konno, Tokiya Noshiro, Atsushi Itaya, Tomohiro Kizuka, Seiji Ono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreparatory actions prior to the intended movements significantly contribute to better performance. Preceding feedforward control, known as preparatory muscle activity (PMA), is crucial for utilizing energy without loss in the main movements. Therefore, the present study attempted to clarify the role of PMA in vertical jumps with preparatory actions such as a one-step motion. Fourteen healthy participants performed vertical jumps with a one-step motion while postural muscle activity was recorded using electromyography (EMG). Kinematics were captured using a 3D motion analysis system. A 500 ms window preceding the onset of the thrust (t0) was defined as the preparatory phase. For each muscle, we detected the EMG activation and deactivation onsets with reference to t0 and integrated the amplitude over that window. Center of mass (COM) velocity, COM anterior–posterior sway range (COM y range), and the change in ankle plantar flexion from heel contact to take‑off (ankle pf rate) were treated as jump‑relevant parameters. Earlier activation onset of tibialis anterior before t0 was associated with larger ankle pf rate, whereas later activation onset of biceps femoris (BF) and medial gastrocnemius (MG) was also associated with larger ankle pf rate. The smaller the amplitudes of erector spinae, rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, MG, and soleus during the preparatory phase, the greater the COM velocity. These results indicate that timing and amplitude modulation of PMA are linked to mechanical conditions that favor converting horizontal approach energy into vertical thrust. Therefore, PMA may contribute to both postural equilibrium and favorable neuromuscular conditions for converting horizontal approach energy into vertical propulsion.Keywords: Preparatory muscle activity, Feedforward control, Jump performance, Electromyography, 3D motion analysisSubject terms: Motor neuron, Reflexes

Introduction

Our body movements can generally perform better when accompanied by preparatory actions. For example, the pre-step approach in jump movements stores kinematic energy that allows the center of mass (COM) to elevate higher. However, the energy conversion from horizontal to vertical during take-off requires complex coordination of muscles, joints, and segments with precise force production and timing. It has been reported that the human motor system has numerous degrees of freedom, and as the number of actions increases, the repertoire of possible movement patterns and their interactions multiply, making control greatly complex1.

When executing upper limb movements in the standing posture (e.g., reaching movements), trunk and other antigravity muscles become activated several hundred milliseconds before the main movement is performed2,3. Such feedforward control, occurring while the body is supported on a surface and serves to preserve whole‑body or segmental stability, has been termed anticipatory postural adjustments (APAs)4,5. This type of muscle activity occurring prior to the main movement plays a crucial role not only in maintaining posture but also in enhancing various motor performances. The term preparatory muscle activity (PMA) is used more broadly to denote any progressive build‑up of muscle activation produced by the central nervous system (CNS) prior to voluntary movements6,7. Therefore, PMA covers APAs when those feedforward bursts occur under standing conditions (ground‑contact), as well as pre‑landing or aerial‑phase activations that adjust stiffness and timing in anticipation of impact7–9. In other words, these underlying functional objectives reflect anticipatory, feedforward commands issued by the CNS, which regulate muscle stiffness, joint kinematics, and intersegmental forces to ensure that forthcoming voluntary movements can be executed effectively without compromising equilibrium.

Muscle activation preceding main movements (e.g., landing and jumping) is modulated primarily by two adjustable control variables. One is the timing of activation (onset), which determines when preparatory muscle activates in response to the expected perturbation. The other is the magnitude of activation (amplitude), which determines the rate of force development and the level of muscle stiffness7. Previous studies on drop-landing and drop-jump have shown a relationship between the timing of PMA and fall duration10,11. Specifically, the timing of PMA is earlier as the drop height increases. This timing modulation is to compensate perturbation due to the impact at the foot contact. On the other hand, findings regarding the amplitude of PMA in drop-landing and drop-jump paradigms are inconsistent across studies. Several studies have reported that the amplitude of PMA is associated with vertical jump performance. The greater amplitude of PMA is associated with improved jump performance (e.g., COM velocity)12,13, while other studies present opposing trends. For example, increases in PMA amplitude do not always translate into greater jump height, and the relationship between PMA amplitude and performance may be task- and context-dependent14,15. Furthermore, the presence of PMA associated with vertical jump has also been demonstrated. It has been reported that deactivation of the soleus (SOL) and activation of the tibialis anterior (TA) occur several hundred milliseconds before the onset of the main activity (i.e., the activation of SOL for the thrust)16,17. An earlier onset of those activities is required to effectively execute voluntary movements without compromising equilibrium. Simultaneously, the inhibition of the vastus lateralis (VL) and the biceps femoris (BF) was observed. Therefore, it is suggested that these inhibitory activities may contribute to upward impulse, such as elastic energy generated through the stretch–shortening cycle (SSC), although the previous study did not evaluate the relationship between the inhibition and jump performance17. Despite extensive work on pre- and post-contact (eccentric phase) muscle activity in various jump paradigms, the relationship between PMA preceding the thrust and vertical jump performance with pre-steps (step-jump) remains uncertain. The step-jump approach requires converting horizontal energy into vertical energy at thrust, whereas drop-jump primarily involves vertical deceleration and rebound from an imposed fall. In addition, the CNS during landing tasks often prioritizes energy dissipation and joint protection, whereas during step-jumps the CNS prioritizes maximizing net vertical energy—as in a vertical jump—while maintaining stability, which may favor different patterns of PMA. Hence, in this study, we aimed to determine the relationship between PMA and vertical jump performance using a vertical jump after a one-step motion.

Based on these findings, we formulated the following hypotheses. In the step-jump, the earlier onset of PMA preceding the thrust would be positively associated with greater vertical jump performance. Furthermore, a larger amplitude of PMA was expected to be associated with improved vertical jump performance. On the other hand, a previous study has suggested that preceding inhibition of agonist muscle activity contributes to energy generation17. Therefore, it is also possible that a smaller PMA amplitude would be associated with improved vertical jump performance. Previous studies have reported that jump‑relevant parameters in the long jump can effectively increase jump performance18,19. However, given that these variables may not necessarily function as jump‑relevant parameters in the present task (i.e., vertical jump with pre-steps) and participants (i.e., non-athletes), we first verified whether they are indeed associated with jump‑relevant parameters before clarifying the hypotheses regarding the onset and amplitude of PMA.

Results

The jump-relevant parameters for the step-jump

The results of Pearson’s correlation analysis are shown in Tables 1 and 2. In the step-jump task, jump height had significant positive correlations with COM velocity and COM anterior-posterior sway range (COM y range) (COM velocity: p < 0.01, r = 0.77, R2 = 0.59, COM y range: p < 0.01, r = 0.68, R2 = 0.46). This r value represents a very large association, which corresponds to a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.59. This indicates that 59% of the variance between these variables can be explained. Additionally, the change of ankle plantar flexion angle from heel contact to take-off (ankle pf rate) had significant positive correlations with COM velocity and COM y range (COM velocity: p < 0.01, r = 0.67, R2 = 0.45, COM y range: p < 0.01, r = 0.78, R2 = 0.61). Therefore, these results support that COM velocity, COM y range, and ankle pf rate are jump-relevant parameters.

Discussion

Previous studies have reported that jump‑relevant parameters in the long jump (i.e., COM velocity, COM y range, and ankle pf rate) effectively enhance jump performance18,19. Furthermore, PMA in vertical jumps is supportive to lift the body against gravity16,20. However, these variables may not necessarily function as jump‑relevant parameters in the present task (i.e., vertical jump with pre-steps) and participants (i.e., non-athletes). In the step-jump, the relationships between PMA preceding the thrust and the jump-relevant parameters remain uncertain. To bridge this gap, this study investigated the relationship between PMA and vertical jump performance using a vertical jump with a one-step motion. To clarify the hypotheses, we first verified whether these variables are indeed associated with jump‑relevant parameters. The results of this study showed that both the timing of PMA preceding the thrust (BF-AO, TA-AO, and MG-AO) and the amplitude of muscle activation during the preparatory phase (ES, RF, VL, MG, and SOL amp) were significantly correlated with COM velocity, COM y range, and ankle pf rate. This was the case that these parameters were confirmed to be jump-relevant parameters. Given the nature of dynamic movements in sports, investigating the specific contribution of PMA in step-jump could uncover new insights into the biomechanical mechanisms that enhance performance. These results are discussed below, considering the possibility that PMA affects jump height in the step-jump task.

We verified key jump-relevant parameters under the step-jump task. Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed a significant and strong positive correlation between jump height and COM velocity under the step-jump task. For the proportion of variance explained (R2), given the inherent inter-individual variability typically observed in biomechanical and neuromuscular data in human movement studies, the level of explanatory power shown by significant relationships in Pearson’s correlation analysis is generally regarded as sufficient21,22. A linear relationship has previously been reported between run‑up speed and jump height when analyzing the best jump of each athlete23. Moreover, the COM velocity at the thrust is a key determinant of the jump height24. In the step-jump, the relationship indicates that a large COM velocity during the thrust is crucial for increasing jump height. Additionally, the results of Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed that significant positive correlations were observed between ankle pf rate and COM velocity and COM y range. Previous studies have shown that from foot contact to take-off, horizontal energy is converted into vertical energy via ankle dorsiflexion-induced forward foot rotation, which is a mechanism confirmed in long jumps, and that a posteriorly shifted COM with a larger leg angle at contact further amplifies vertical energy through increased impact18. Therefore, the results of the step‑jump task in this study support the mechanism that horizontal energy is converted into vertical energy during take‑off, and indicate that COM velocity, COM y‑range, and ankle pf rate are likely to be relevant parameters for increasing jump height in a one‑step vertical jump.

To examine the effects of PMA on the jump-relevant parameters, Pearson’s correlation analyses were conducted. The results revealed a significant correlation between ankle pf rate and BF-AO, TA-AO, and MG-AO. The findings of this study supported the proposed hypothesis. The results indicate that the earlier onset of a specific muscle (i.e., TA) may have improved jump-relevant parameters, whereas the later onset of the other muscles (i.e., BF and MG) just before the thrust may also improve these parameters. A previous study involving detailed segmental analyses in single‑leg running jumps has shown that the forward rotation of stance‑leg foot actively transforms horizontal energy into vertical energy while positive shank power contributes directly to the early take‑off increase in vertical energy19. Moreover, a previous study executing countermovement jumps has demonstrated that ankle flexibility, which is an indicator of the range of ankle dorsiflexion, results in greater jump height25. These findings suggest that in jumping with pre-steps, the forward rotation of the stance‑leg foot is a critical factor for redirecting horizontal energy into vertical energy, and that ankle dorsiflexion preceding take-off particularly influences jump performance. The relationship between the ankle pf rate and the TA-AO suggests that the earlier onset of the TA facilitates the ankle dorsi flexion preceding the thrust. This relationship was consistent with previous studies investigating the functional role of muscle activities preceding the thrust. Specifically, the earlier onset of the TA induced the backward displacement of the center of pressure, providing an effective initial condition for the thrust16,20. In addition, our results showed that the earlier onset of the BF and the MG negatively influenced the change in ankle plantar flexion preceding the thrust. A previous study has reported that the range of the ankle dorsiflexion progressively decreases as the degree of the knee joint changes from 90° flexion to full extension26. Thus, it is possible that a relative delay in the activation of the muscles around the knee joint serves to prioritize the ankle dorsiflexion, which drives TA activity preceding the thrust. Furthermore, a previous study investigating the effects of accentuated eccentric loading (AEL) during the eccentric phase of the SSC on jump heights has reported that increasing the external eccentric load does not reliably increase vertical ground reaction force at the thrust or produce greater elastic energy return, suggesting that simple increases in external load or tendon strain are not sufficient to alter push‑off mechanics in isolation15 Therefore, the earlier onset of the TA combined with delayed activation of the BF and the MG could represent a functional neuromuscular control strategy that, together with sufficient ankle flexibility, optimizes force transmission during the thrust in the step-jump.

For the amplitude of PMA, our results support the latter hypotheses. Muscles (ES, RF, VL, MG, and SOL) obtained significant negative correlations with COM velocity in these results, which serve as prime movers during the thrust motion (i.e., concentric phase). As previous studies have shown, excessive activity in specific muscles leads to a braking effect and inefficient energy dissipation27. Additionally, in vertical jumps, inhibition of muscle activity preceding the thrust was observed several hundred milliseconds17. Therefore, the amplitude of the muscles acting as the prime mover may need to be inhibited during the relatively long preparatory phase (i.e., 500-ms interval). On the other hand, these results do not support the former hypothesis, which is based on analyzing the 100‑ms interval immediately preceding the onset of the primary movement12,13. In this study, the former hypothesis refers to the expectation that a larger amplitude of PMA would lead to improved vertical jump performance. It should be noted that we quantified the total activity over a 500 ms window preceding the thrust to avoid inflating the risk of false‑positive findings through multiple statistical analyses. A previous study suggested that the SSC effectiveness and elastic‑energy utilization can be influenced by muscle activity occurring in much shorter, phase‑specific windows (approximately 50–150 ms) around the lengthening–shortening transition28. Although inhibition of the prime movers during the relatively long preparation phase is important for jump performance, a detailed approach evaluating PMA in each shorter phase may provide clear evidence of the temporal coordination strategies.

This study has several limitations. In this study, we used correlation analyses to investigate the role of PMA in step-jump performance. However, correlation analysis is limited to assessing associations between variables and cannot establish cause/effect. Therefore, future studies should employ experimental manipulations or longitudinal designs to verify cause/effect. A limited number of participants can reduce statistical power and increase the risk of Type II errors, making it more challenging to detect subtle yet meaningful effects. Additionally, the exclusive inclusion of male subjects restricts the generalizability of our findings. The absence of female participants means that potential gender-specific differences in dynamic postural control during the step-jump remain unaddressed. Future studies should aim to recruit a larger and more diverse sample to confirm these findings and explore potential sex-related differences in performance. Furthermore, one potential limitation of this study is the unconstrained use of arm swing, which may have influenced the muscle activity of the upper limb and the ES. Thus, future study should systematically manipulate arm‑swing conditions to isolate their effects on trunk muscle activation and jump mechanics.

Our study revealed that PMA may influence jump height through other factors in the step-jump. These results suggest that body equilibrium compensating for perturbations (upper body tilting forward) caused by thrust and energy conversion, utilizing the energy generated by the step approach for jumping, is involved. To further confirm these relationships, it is necessary to investigate the relationship between the center of gravity and the external work (time integral of the external force) from a step approach to a vertical jump via multiple force plates. Additionally, the observed relationships between PMA and jump‑relevant parameters may reflect better preparatory postural control involved in individual motor strategies and prior learning and sport‑specific experience. Although previous studies have reported differences in optimal PMA timing across skill types and between elite and less‑experienced athletes29–32, this study did not directly assess participants’ training histories or skill‑specific experience. Consequently, we cannot determine whether the PMA patterns reported here are attributable to such factors. Nevertheless, this study provides foundational knowledge of PMA in one‑step vertical jumps. Moreover, because PMA can be modified through task‑specific practice, its patterns may differ according to athletes’ training backgrounds and sport characteristics. Accordingly, future studies should investigate sport‑specific PMA by targeting athletes with distinct competitive and training histories.

Conclusion

The results of the study demonstrated that preparatory muscle activity (PMA) preceding the thrust in vertical jump with a one‑step is associated with kinematic variables that underlie improved jump height. During the preparatory phase, earlier TA activation and phase‑specific delays in BF and MG activation were associated with greater ankle plantar flexion change from heel contact to take‑off, suggesting that the precise timing of distal and proximal muscles may help shape ankle mechanics during energy conversion. Furthermore, reduced amplitudes in prime movers (ES, RF, VL, MG, SOL) were associated with greater COM velocity. This implies that inhibiting excessive pre‑thrust activation of these muscles may prevent braking effects and allow for more effective tendon elastic recoil during the subsequent concentric action. These findings suggest that earlier PMA contributes to maintaining whole‑body equilibrium during the transition from one-step to thrust and to optimizing the neuromuscular conditions for converting horizontal approach energy into vertical propulsion.

Methods

Participants

Fourteen men participated in this experiment (age: 25.6 ± 1.9 years, height: 176.3 ± 5.6 cm, body mass: 76.3 ± 16.8 kg, mean ± standard deviation [SD], including 13 with the right dominant leg). Inclusion criteria required participants to be male undergraduate or graduate students who engaged in physical exercise at least once per week and had no history of lower-limb musculoskeletal disorders or trauma within the past year. The participants were carefully pre-screened to ensure that they had no history of neurological disorders and possessed normal sensorimotor abilities. Specifically, they were required to complete a questionnaire regarding their injury history. Based on the responses, any individuals who reported a neurological condition such as neurodegenerative diseases, or who reported experiencing an injury (e.g., sprains or fractures) within the past year were excluded from the study. To determine the sample size, an a priori analysis was conducted using G*Power software version 3.1.9 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany, http://www.gpower.hhu.de/), and the number of participants was decided based on the results of this analysis33. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation, and the experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health and Sport Sciences, University of Tsukuba (approval number: Tai024-3).

Experimental setup

The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 3. To measure muscle activity data during the trials, an electromyograph (multichannel telemetry system, WEB7000, Nihon Kohden Corporation) was used, with data sampled at a rate of 1000 Hz. Reusable wireless surface active electrodes were used to record muscle activity data, which were attached to the muscle bellies of the erector spinae (ES), rectus femoris (RF), vastus lateralis (VL), biceps femoris (BF), tibialis anterior (TA), lateral gastrocnemius (LG), medial gastrocnemius (MG) and soleus (SOL) on the dominant side of each participant. Prior to data collection, the accuracy of electrode placement for each muscle was verified in accordance with standardized guidelines. Anatomical landmarks were identified according to the SENIAM recommendations34, and electrode positions were confirmed by palpation and visual inspection during voluntary muscle contractions. For each target muscle, participants were asked to perform a brief contraction of the muscle; the resulting muscle activity was observed to ensure that the electrode was placed over the muscle belly—the site of maximal signal amplitude35. This procedure ensured that electrode placement was both accurate and reproducible across sessions. To reduce skin contact resistance during electrode placement, the attachment sites were shaved, abraded with a skin preparation gel for biological signal monitoring (SkinPure, YZ-0019, Nihon Kohden Corporation) and disinfected with alcohol before the electrodes were attached.

Experimental setup. (A) Participants stood at the designated starting positions in each trial of the step-jump tasks. Upon the cue from the examiner, they performed a vertical jump with both feet, with only the dominant foot contacting the force plate. For the laboratory coordinate system in the embedded force plate and the motion capture system, the x‑axis (mediolateral) was positive to the right, the y‑axis (anteroposterior) was positive anteriorly, and the z‑axis (vertical) was positive upward. (B) Placements of wireless surface active electrodes and reflective markers on each participant during the experiment. Eight electrodes were attached to the back and lower limbs on the dominant side of the ES, RF, VL, BF, TA, LG, MG and SOL. Reflective markers were placed at 37 locations on the whole body based on the plug-in gait model.

To measure the kinematic and kinetic data of the participants during the trials, six infrared cameras (Vicon, Vicon Motion Systems) were set up, and an optical reflective motion capture system (Nexus, Vicon Motion Systems) was used. A total of 37 reflective markers were attached to the whole body of each participant based on the plug-in gait model, with data sampled at a rate of 240 Hz. The x-axis was set to the frontal plane, the y-axis to the sagittal plane and the z-axis to the vertical plane for displacement data, whereas the x-axis was set to the flexion‒extension angle, the y-axis to the abduction‒adduction angle and the z-axis to the rotation angle for angle data. To synchronize the muscle activity data and kinematic data in this experiment, a force plate (SS-FP40AO, Sports Sensing, size: 400 mm × 400 mm × 110 mm) was used. The force plate signal was connected to the external stimulus input port of the electromyograph, with data sampled at a rate of 1000 Hz. The force plate signal was input to the PC for motion analysis applications via an A/D converter (32 Ch analog interface, Four Assist), with data sampled at a rate of 1200 Hz.

Experimental procedure

Participants performed 10 trials of the step-jump task. For this task, participants were instructed to perform a one-step approach with their nondominant foot from a designated starting position upon receiving the cue from the examiner. The second step involved landing on the force plate, followed by a vertical jump with both feet on the third step. The participants were instructed to land in the same position as they took off and maintain a quiet stance for at least 2 s after landing. Additionally, they were instructed to ensure that the landing positions of both feet during the second and third steps were as parallel as possible. Before the experiment was conducted, each participant was given a 10-minute warm up to familiarize the experimental setup (i.e. the environment and task). During this period, they were required to perform the tasks set for this experiment moderately. Then, the optimal starting position for the one-step approach was individually determined and set. During each trial, any trial where the participants did not remain stationary for 2 s or where the landing position of the dominant foot or the take-off position did not fall within the force plate was considered invalid, and additional trials were conducted to compensate.

Data analysis

Analysis of kinematic and kinetic data

Identification of the onset of the main movement

Kinematic and kinetic data were smoothed using a fourth-order low-pass Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 20 Hz36. The vertical component data obtained from the force plate were used to identify take-off (< 30 N). When vertical jumping is the task, the initiation of the main movement corresponds to take-off. Previous studies defined the initiation of the main movement as the point at which the vertical component of the ground reaction force exceeded the body weight after unloading16,20. However, upon observing the ground reaction force data during vertical jumping, the take-off begins immediately after the value reaches its minimum (unloading motion). Therefore, in this study, the initiation of the main movement (i.e., t0) was defined as the next data point after the minimum value in the z-axis displacement data of the COM, traced back from take-off (Fig. 4).

Using this threshold, the point exceeding or falling below the threshold before t0 was detected, and the point closest to t0 among the detected points for each muscle was defined as the activation or deactivation onset.

Jump height

For the z-axis COM displacement data, the 1-second window with the smallest standard deviation within the 2-second quiet stance period was identified, and the average value for that window was calculated. The difference between the maximum value in the time series data and this average value was defined as the jump height37.

COM variables

The whole-body COM was calculated by summing the products of each body segment’s estimated mass (15 segments; head, trunk, pelvis, left upper arm, left forearm, left hand, right upper arm, right forearm, right hand, left thigh, left shank, left foot, right thigh, right shank and right foot) and its corresponding COM position, and then dividing this sum by the total body mass. In practice, the COM for each segment was determined based on the spatial coordinates provided by reflective markers placed on standardized anatomical landmarks38. Ankle plantar flexion rate (Ankle pf rate)

For the step-jump task, the heel contact was defined as the point at which the heel displacement data of the dominant foot reached its minimum value when it was traced back from take-off. The ankle joint angle of the dominant foot was computed based on the three-dimensional coordinates of two anatomical landmarks—the lateral malleolus and the foot (toe)40. The vector from the lateral malleolus to the toe was determined by subtracting the lateral malleolus coordinate from the toe coordinate for each respective component (x-axis, y-axis and z-axis). The change in the ankle plantar flexion angle was calculated as the arctangent of the sagittal plane (y-axis) vector and the vertical plane (z-axis) vector. The ankle pf rate was then determined by dividing the change in the ankle plantar flexion angle by the time required from heel contact to take-off.

Analysis of the muscle activity data

The muscle activity data for each trial were processed using a band-pass filter (15–450 Hz) and then full-wave rectified. Furthermore, based on previous studies, the data were smoothed using a fourth-order low-pass Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 50 Hz37.

Timing of PMA onset

To set the baseline values for muscle activities, the 500 ms window with the smallest standard deviation within the 2-second quiet stance period was identified for each muscle, and the average value for that window was calculated. To identify muscle activity preceding t0 (i.e. the next data point after the minimum value in the z-axis displacement data of the COM), the thresholds were set at the first point where the muscle activity exceeded or fell below 2.5 standard deviations (SD) of the baseline value and lasted for 25 ms30. Previous studies have shown that defining the onset threshold as the mean of the 12 lowest values of the signal envelope and 2 SD yields a greater percentage of accurate detections41. Another study reported that using a threshold defined by a 25 ms window, a 2–3 SD above baseline, and a 50 Hz low-pass filter keeps the deviation from visual onset determination under 20 ms42. Therefore, the threshold adopted in the present study (25 ms, 2.5 SD, 50 Hz low-pass) should accurately detect the onsets of postural muscle activation even under dynamic condition (i.e., step-jump task). To evaluate the timing of PMA for each muscle in the step-jump task, the thresholds defined in this study were used to detect the point where the muscle activity exceeded or fell below the threshold within the time window from − 500 ms to t0. This time window included heel contact (mean ± standard deviation: -204.55 ± 0.07 ms). Therefore, the time window set in this study extended from the terminal swing phase of the dominant leg to the next data point after the minimum value in the z-axis displacement data of the COM. The point closest to t0 among the detected points for each muscle was subsequently defined as the activation onset (AO) or deactivation onset (DO) (Fig. 4).

Amplitude of PMA

Due to the variation in gain among participants for muscles, EMG values were normalized based on the maximum EMG elicited across all trials, such that all values were between 0 and 1. To evaluate muscle coordination during preparatory stages, the time window (i.e., -500 ms to t0) under the normalized EMG curve was computed by numerical integration using the trapezoidal rule (i.e., summing trapezoids formed between adjacent samples) and defined as the amplitude of PMA (PMA amp).

For EMG variables, data from one participant were excluded due to values exceeding 2.5 SD from the group mean.

Statistics

All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (SPSS Inc., IL, US). To evaluate the normality of each variable defined in this study, the Shapiro‒Wilk normality test was conducted. This test confirmed normality for all variables used in this study. Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to investigate the relationships among kinematic, kinetic and PMA variables in the step-jump task. When interpreting correlation coefficients, we adopted the guidelines proposed by previous study43. According to this previous literature, the magnitude of the correlation coefficient was considered as small (0.1 ≤ r < 0.3), moderate (0.3 ≤ r < 0.5), large (0.5 ≤ r < 0.7), very large (0.7 ≤ r < 0.9), and nearly perfect (0.9 ≤ r < 1.0). The significance level for all the statistical analyses was set at 0.05.
