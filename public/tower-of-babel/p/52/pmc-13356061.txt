Effectiveness of Micronized Purified Flavonoid Fraction on Quality of Life in Patients with Chronic Venous Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Fabricio Rodrigues Santiago, Denisa Celovska, Sarah Thomis, Vanessa Blanc-Guillemaud, Marie-Blanche Onselaer, Soumeya Serifou, Hurrem-Pelin Yaltirik, Mamuka Bokuchava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveChronic venous disease (CVD) substantially impacts patients’ quality of life (QoL) owing to leg symptoms such as pain, swelling, and discomfort. The aim of this meta-analysis was to evaluate the effects of micronized purified flavonoid fraction (MPFF) treatment on QoL in patients with CVD.MethodsA systematic review conducted in December 2023 identified studies investigating the efficacy of oral MPFF treatment (1000 mg daily for at least 1 month) on QoL in patients with CVD. Eligible prospective studies included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non-RCTs (comparative, single-arm, and observational studies). Medline, Embase, and Cochrane databases were searched. Mean changes (MC) from baseline to the last postbaseline value in the global QoL score (0–100) were estimated using single-group random-effects meta-analysis. Secondary outcomes included all dimensions of QoL score (pain, physical, social, and psychological) and CVD symptom scores (using 10-cm visual analog scale [VAS]) and percentage of patients with complete resolution for heaviness and cramps. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane tools.ResultsIn total, 10 of 317 studies were retained for the analysis. These studies conducted worldwide included 5654 patients allocated to MPFF treatment; nearly all (99.6%) were treated for at least 2 months. Mean (standard deviation [SD]) age was 45.6 (6.7) years, with mostly females (81.3%) and overweight (body mass index [BMI] of 25.9 [3.5] kg/m2) patients. The distribution of Clinical, Etiology, Anatomy, and Pathophysiology (CEAP) classes was: 37.3% (C2), 24.0% (C3), 21.2% (C1), 14.1% (C4), and 3.2% (C0s). Two studies had low risk of bias, four had some concerns, and four had high risk. There was no strong evidence of publication bias (Egger’s test P = 0.083).Patients’ global QoL score significantly improved post treatment: MC of − 17.1 (95% confidence interval (CI) [− 20.0, − 14.1], I2 = 96.3%), with greater reductions observed over longer treatment durations, from − 13.0 (− 17.7, − 8.3) for 1–2 months to − 20.0 (− 24.6, − 15.5) for 6 months treatment (all P < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis excluding studies with a high risk of bias showed consistent results with the main analysis. Subgroup analysis between RCTs and non-RCTs showed comparable reductions across both study designs.QoL dimensions showed improvements in pain, physical function, psychological well-being, and social engagement (all P < 0.001). MPFF treatment also significantly alleviated CVD symptoms such as pain, heaviness, and discomfort, with improvements demonstrated by VAS scores (all P < 0.001). A significant percentage of patients reported complete resolution of heaviness (78.7%) and cramps (95.3%), both P < 0.001. Heterogeneity was high across studies (I2 > 80% in most analyses).ConclusionsThis meta-analysis showed that MPFF treatment was associated with improvements in QoL globally and across all dimensions for patients with CVD. The findings highlight the importance of a patient-centered approach in managing CVD and provide additional evidence of the clinical benefits of MPFF in enhancing patient-important outcomes. However, the exact magnitude of this clinical impact remains to be confirmed through further investigation.Protocol RegistrationInternational Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), CRD42023476525.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40261-026-01563-x.

Key Points

Micronized purified flavonoid fraction (MPFF) treatment was associated with improvements in chronic venous disease‑specific quality‑of‑life scores across ten prospective studies with benefits observed in all dimensions, including pain, physical function, psychological well-being, and social engagement.MPFF also showed clinically meaningful reductions in leg symptom intensity, including pain, heaviness, and discomfort, and high rates of complete resolution for heaviness and cramps.These results, derived from both randomized and non‑randomized studies, add to the evidence supporting MPFF as an effective therapeutic option for improving patient‑reported outcomes in chronic venous disease.

Introduction

The worldwide prevalence of chronic venous disease (CVD) continues to increase, particularly owing to aging populations [1, 2]. Given the chronic progressive nature of the disease, CVD has a significant impact on patients’ quality of life (QoL) [3–5], which refers to the functional impact of the illness as experienced and perceived by the patient. The negative effects of CVD on patients’ well-being are well established and include venous leg symptoms such as pain, heaviness, discomfort, swelling, and burning sensations, and signs such as leg edema, varicose veins, and ulcers among other changes in the skin appearance [6–8]. Venous leg symptoms are common in patients regardless of the stage of the disease, including in very early stages [9]. Notably, patients in the early stages of the disease represent a quarter of the working population, highlighting the importance of addressing this issue [1, 2, 9, 10]. These symptoms can lead to sleep deprivation and depression, thus limiting their ability to work and significantly impacting their daily lives, ultimately leading to a socioeconomic impact [4, 5, 11, 12]. Moreover, recent epidemiological studies showed that patients with venous disease experienced an increased risk of death and cardiovascular event beyond the influence of established risk factors [6, 13–15]. Therefore, the current management of CVD aims to alleviate leg symptoms and signs, as well as improving the patients’ QoL [6]. Multi-dimensional patient-reported outcome measures that evaluate patient well-being and the overall burden of the disease are therefore useful in evaluating the potential benefits of certain lifestyle recommendations or treatments [16–21].

QoL scores are derived from questionnaires completed by the patient, providing a multi-dimensional endpoint for evaluating the efficacy or inefficacy of different treatments in clinical trials and meta-analyses [16, 18–21]. Several QoL scales have been specifically validated for CVD (on the basis of their psychometric properties) and span physical, psychological, and, in some cases, social dimensions of the patient’s life [16, 19, 20, 22]. The Aberdeen Varicose Vein Questionnaire (AVVQ) and the Chronic Venous Insufficiency Quality of Life Questionnaire (CIVIQ-14 and CIVIQ-20), each using different scales and dimensions (e.g., pain, physical, social, psychological), are the most commonly used outcomes in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies involving patients with CVD [16, 22]. Notably, the CIVIQ questionnaire has been validated in patients with CVD up to Clinical, Etiology, Anatomy, and Pathophysiology (CEAP) class C4, supporting its use across a broad spectrum of clinical severity. Several studies have shown that QoL worsens as the disease progresses, starting from the early CEAP stages [23–26]. In addition, the number of vein-specific complaints has been correlated with impaired QoL [5, 23, 24, 27, 28].

A previous meta-analysis of double-blinded, placebo-controlled trials by Kakkos et al. suggested that micronized purified flavonoid fraction (MPFF) is highly effective in improving leg symptoms, edema, and QoL in patients with CVD [29]. Ulloa et al. highlighted in a systematic literature review and expert consensus (comprising both RCTs and real-world studies) that MPFF has a particularly strong evidence base, which supports its effectiveness in improving patients’ QoL [17]. However, no meta-analysis has been conducted to quantitatively estimate the effect size of MPFF’s effectiveness on QoL in patients with CVD, regardless of study design. To address this gap, the aim of the present work is to provide an overview of the benefits of MPFF treatment on QoL in patients with CVD on the basis of data from various types of prospective studies, including real-world evidence that reflects routine clinical care of patients with CVD.

Methods

Protocol and Registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and meta-Analysis (PRISMA) statements [30]. The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (no. CRD42023476525).

Study Eligibility Criteria

Study characteristics of eligible studies were specified using the “review PICOS” statement (online resource Electronic Supplementary Material Supplementary Table S1). Participants were adults with CVD, regardless of the CEAP class, and treated with MPFF, excluding those undergoing venous procedural intervention. The dose of MPFF was 1000 mg daily, with a treatment duration of at least 1 month. The primary outcome was the global QoL score assessed using CVD-specific questionnaires. Secondary outcomes included QoL score by dimension (pain, physical, psychological, and social) and CVD leg symptoms (pain, heaviness, cramps, discomfort). The following prospective study designs were included: RCTs with placebo or active comparators, non-randomized comparative studies, single arm studies, and observational studies.

Report Characteristics

There were no restrictions on the year of publication or the publication language (articles were translated into English when required). Eligible publication types included clinical studies and conference abstracts containing results. Excluded publication types were reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, study protocols, preclinical studies, case reports, letters, notes, and editorials. Unpublished data were not eligible.

Information Sources

Systematic literature searches were performed by M.B.O., V.B.G., and C.D. They were first conducted in the Medline database (via PubMed; search completed in October 2023) and then in the Cochrane database (including Embase; search completed in December 2023). A manual reference search was performed in the Servier database (December 2023) to identify additional studies. A manual search based on recent reviews and meta-analyses was conducted to retrieve any previously unidentified studies (December 2023).

Search Strategy

The search strategy integrated Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free‑text terms using Boolean operators. Search terms are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Selection Process

Two reviewers (M.B.O. and C.D.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the retrieved records to identify studies relevant to the search criteria. Reasons for exclusion included the report or study design not meeting eligibility criteria (e.g., publication type, indication, product, treatment regimen, treatment duration, outcomes) or duplication of another study. Subsequently, the two reviewers independently assessed the full texts of selected reports for inclusion using the same criteria and on the basis of the availability of the data. Consensus on the inclusion or exclusion of reports was reached through discussion between the two reviewers. A third reviewer (V.B.G.) validated the final list of selected reports without interfering in the literature-search process.

Data Collection Process

Efficacy data were collected for the intention-to-treat population when available and for the per protocol population otherwise. This meta-analysis examined changes from pre-treatment baseline to the last post-treatment timepoint. The baseline timepoint was defined as the last value recorded before the administration of the first dose of the study drug. In cases where multiple post-intervention timepoints were available, the last post-baseline value was retained from each study. The two reviewers (M.B.O. and C.D.) independently extracted data from the reports using separate data extraction forms specifically designed for this systematic review and meta-analysis. Extracted data were then compared, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Data Items

Patient characteristics including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), CVD etiology, CEAP class, and concomitant medication were collected. The primary outcome was the global QoL score. As outlined in the search strategy, we considered all questionnaires specific to venous disease, including the CIVIQ-14, CIVIQ-20, AVVQ, Veines-QOL, Ve-QoL, or any other questionnaire [16]. To ensure cross‑study comparability, all instruments were expressed on a 0–100 scale, this allowed pooling data within a unified metric. Regarding CIVIQ scores, since CIVIQ‑14 and CIVIQ‑20 global scores have different theoretical ranges (14–70 and 20–100, respectively), the Global Index Scores (GIS) (0–100 scale) were considered. Secondary outcomes were QoL score by dimension (pain, physical, psychological, and social), CVD symptom scores (leg pain, heaviness, and discomfort), and complete resolution of symptoms (leg heaviness and cramps). For all QoL scores, an improvement in the patient’s QoL was represented by a decrease in the score.

Study Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias was evaluated independently by two reviewers (S.S. and M.B.O.) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tools [31], RoB2 [32] for RCTs, and ROBINS-I [33] for non-RCTs. In addition, publication bias was evaluated using a funnel plot and the Egger’s regression test.

Effect Measures

The following effect measures were analyzed on the basis of data retrieved from the included studies from baseline to the last post-baseline value. For the primary outcome, the effect measure was the mean change (MC) in patient’s global QoL score. Secondary outcomes included the MC in the patient’s QoL score by dimension. In addition, secondary outcomes encompassed the MC in CVD symptom scores assessed using a 10-cm visual analog scale (VAS), as well as the percentage of patients experiencing complete resolution of heaviness and cramps.

Synthesis Methods

Random-effects meta-analysis models, employing the restricted maximum likelihood method, were used to estimate the overall MC from baseline to the last post-baseline value in outcome measurements, in line with Cochrane recommendations. The estimate of the overall treatment effect, along with its corresponding 95% confidence interval (95% CI) and P-value, was provided. The overall treatment effect represented a weighted mean of the individual study treatment effects, with weights determined by the inverse-variance method (the inverse of the sum of the variance of the treatment effect estimate within each study), considering interstudy variation. A forest plot illustrated the estimated treatment effect for each individual study and the overall treatment effect, including the corresponding 95% CI. Significance was declared at a 5% two-sided level.

For quantitative variables, the following were used when available: number of observations (n), number of missing data (n missing), mean and/or median, standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM), first and third quartiles (Q1 and Q3) or interquartile range (IQR), and minimum and maximum. For qualitative variables, the following were used when available: number of observations (n), number of missing data (n missing), absolute frequency (n), and relative frequency (%). Percentages were calculated on the basis of the number of participants with available data, unless specified otherwise.

For the meta-analysis of continuous outcomes, the required parameters included the MC from baseline with SD and the sample size. These parameters were directly extracted when available. However, in many cases, they were not reported directly in the articles, necessitating derivation on the basis of Cochrane’s recommended rules. If the parameters could not be derived using these rules, imputation methods recommended by Cochrane were employed [34]. For missing MC data, outcome estimates were not imputed. For missing SD data of the MC, the following approach was adopted: if the derivation rule required an estimate of the correlation coefficient between baseline and post-intervention outcomes, and no such information was provided, the mean of reported correlation coefficient from other studies was used. For the main analysis, a default value of 0.75 was used [35]. If the above rules were not sufficient to estimate the SD, the mean of the reported (or derived) SDs from other studies using the same scale was used as an estimate. The number of studies used for this imputation and their associated descriptive statistics (mean, median, IQR, minimum, maximum) for the SD were documented. For missing sample size information, the sample size for the change from baseline was approximated using the post-baseline sample size or, if that was unavailable, the baseline sample size. In instances where studies reported results from subgroups separately, a combined mean and SD might be required. For meta-analysis models of binary outcomes, the number of patients experiencing the event of interest (complete resolution of symptoms) and the sample size were required.

Statistical Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity within the meta-analysis was identified and quantified using several key metrics: Cochran’s Q heterogeneity statistic, I-squared (I2) statistic, and tau-squared (τ2) [36, 37]. The assessment of heterogeneity was completed through visual inspection of forest plots, which graphically represented the variability among the study findings. In addition, both fixed-effect and random-effects analyses were performed. All analyses reported the random-effects model as main analysis model.

Sensitivity Analysis and Subgroup Analyses

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the synthesized results for the MC in the patient’s global QoL score, using the same main analysis model but excluding studies identified as having a high risk of bias.

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore the MC in global QoL score, using the main analysis model stratified by MPFF duration (short-term treatment: 1–2 months, medium term: 3–5 months, long term: 6 months or more) and by study design (RCTs versus non-RCTs). In addition, a meta-regression was used to evaluate the overall impact of the covariate: study design on the primary outcome.

Statistical Software

The statistical analyses were conducted using the R statistical software (version 4.2.2), with the metafor package (version 4.4.0) [38].

Results

Study Selection

The literature search identified a total of 317 studies: 211 from Medline, 77 from Cochrane, and an additional 29 obtained through manual search (Fig. 1). After eliminating 31 duplicates, the titles and abstracts of 286 reports were screened, resulting in the exclusion of 202 publications and the identification of 8 unavailable reports. Ultimately, 10 reports out of the 76 assessed for eligibility were retained for the analysis [39–48]. Among the selected studies, two were placebo-controlled RCTs [39, 44], two were comparator-controlled RCTs [41, 43], and six were non-RCTs: five interventional studies [42, 45–48] and one observational study [40]. Of the four RCTs, two studies compared MPFF with placebo [39, 44] and two studies compared different formulations of MPFF [41, 43]. Of the six non-RCTs, one was an observational study performed in C4 patients [40], four studies evaluated MPFF in patients with situational reflux [45–48], and one study compared participants with venous reflux with those without venous reflux [42].Fig. 1Preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram depicting the selection process of studies. Study Characteristics

Characteristics of the ten included studies are presented in Supplementary Table S3, with details on the population of interest extracted for the present meta-analyses. The included studies were conducted in Brazil, Russia, or internationally, and the data were published between 2002 and 2022. Studies enrolled at least 26 and up to 4527 patients allocated to MPFF treatment group. Almost all patients (99.6%) were treated with MPFF for at least 2 months.

Most reports provided global QoL scores as GIS from CIVIQ scales ranging from 0 to 100. Five studies utilized CIVIQ-20 GIS [41, 45–48]. In the study by Jantet [42], details on CIVIQ-20 scores were obtained from an internal report provided by Servier. Rabe et al. [44] used the reciprocal of the CIVIQ-20 GIS, meaning that higher scores indicate better QoL. Mansilha et al. [43] and Bogachev et al. [40] employed the CIVIQ-14 GIS. In the study by Belczak et al. [39], the Ve-QoL score was used and consisted of ten items assessing the daily and personal impact of venous disease. Ve-QoL provides a total score (0–100) that has been shown to correlate positively with ambulatory venous pressure [49].

Regarding the secondary outcomes, the CVD symptom scores retrieved from six studies [40–43, 45, 48] were all reported on a continuous VAS ranging from 0 (absence of symptoms) to 10 cm (most severe level of symptom). For the complete resolution of CVD symptoms, the number of patients with symptoms at baseline and the number of patients with resolved symptoms post treatment with MPFF were collected from four studies [42, 46–48].

Patient Demographics and CVD History

The global QoL scores were extracted from an overall population of 8069 patients (81.3% female), with a mean (SD) age of 45.6 (6.7) years (N = 8069) and a BMI of 25.9 (3.5) kg/m2 (N = 2411). The etiology of CVD was classified as primary in six studies [40, 41, 43, 46–48], as both primary and secondary in three studies [39, 42, 44], and unidentified in one study [45]. Four studies focused exclusively on a single CEAP class: two on C1s [46, 47], one study on C0s [45], and one study on C4 [40]. The six remaining studies evaluated broader CEAP classes: C0s–C2 [48], C0s–C4 [41–43], C2–C5 [39], and C3–C4 [44]. In a Belczak study [39] involving patients with C2–C5 disease, only a subset of the cohort was clearly defined (e.g., 12 patients treated with MPFF were classified as CEAP C3). However, the CEAP classification for the remaining 16 patients (0.2% of the overall population included in our analysis) was not reported. Overall, the distribution of patients categorized by CEAP classes (N = 7529) across the studies was as follows: 37.3% of C2, 24.0% of C3, 21.2% of C1, 14.1% of C4, and 3.2% of C0s. Concomitant CVD treatments were reported in only one study [40], in which a total of 335 patients assigned to the MPFF treatment group received adjunctive compression therapy, topical treatments, or both. It can therefore be hypothesized that only 4.5% (n/N = 335/7433) of patients in the present meta-analysis were treated with MPFF in adjunction to other conservative approaches at baseline.

Risk of Bias of Included Studies

Two RCTs were assessed as having a low risk of bias [41, 43], one raised some concerns [39], and one was judged to have a high risk of bias [44] (Fig. 2a). Regarding non-RCTs studies, three raised some concerns [40, 46, 48] and three were deemed to have a high risk of bias [42, 45, 47] (Fig. 2b). The risk of bias of the report by Tsukanov and Nikolaichuk (B) [47] was downgraded (from “some concerns” to “high risk”) to reflect a potential risk of data duplication with the report by Tsukanov and Nikolaichuk (A) [46]. Results of Individual Studies and of Data Synthesis

Primary Outcome: Global QoL Score

Summary statistics for each selected study, including sample size, MC with SD, and effect estimate with its precision (MC with 95% CI), are available in Supplementary Table S4. The main analysis of the primary outcome included ten reports (four RCTs [39, 41, 43, 44] and six non-RCTs [40, 42, 45–48]), encompassing a total of 5654 patients with C0–C4 venous disease, treated with MPFF. There was a significant reduction in the global QoL score, with an MC of − 17.1 (95% CI [− 20.0, − 14.1], P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S4 and Fig. 4), representing an improvement in patients’ QoL. There was a high level of heterogeneity between these studies (Q = 129.2, df = 9, P < 0.01; I2 = 96.3%; τ2 = 18.8). The sensitivity analysis, excluding studies with a high risk of bias (six studies included [39–41, 43, 46, 48], N = 2139), showed consistent results with the main analysis, reporting a significant and even greater reduction in the estimate of the MC of the global QoL score (− 19.4 [− 22.5, − 16.3], P < 0.001; high heterogeneity: Q = 31.8, P < 0.01; I2 = 93.0%; τ2 = 11.9; Supplementary Fig. S1).

The subgroup analysis stratified by MPFF treatment duration (Supplementary Fig. S2) showed significant reductions in the MC of the global QoL score in the 1–2 months (seven studies [39, 41–46], N = 5190), 3–5 months (four studies [42, 44, 47, 48], N = 3535), and 6 or more months (two studies [40, 42], N = 3533) treatment groups, with MCs of − 13.0 (− 17.7, − 8.3), − 15.5 (− 20.2, − 10.8), and − 20.0 (− 24.6, − 15.5), respectively (all P < 0.001). These results suggested that longer exposure to MPFF leads to greater improvement in patients’ QoL. However, interstudy heterogeneity remained high regardless of MPFF treatment duration, and no meta-regression was performed to assess this difference.

The subgroup analysis stratified by type of studies (RCT versus non-RCT) considered four RCTs (N = 1937) [39, 41, 43, 44] versus six non-RCTs (N = 3717) [40, 42, 45–48] (Supplementary Fig. S3). Significant improvements in patients’ global QoL were observed, with comparable reductions across both study designs: − 16.7 (− 21.6, − 11.8) for RCTs versus − 17.2 (− 21.4, − 13.1) for non-RCTs (both P < 0.001). The interstudies heterogeneities were high: for RCTs, Q = 56.3, df = 3, P < 0.01; I2 = 96.4%; τ2 = 22.5; and for non-RCTs, Q = 71.8, df = 5, P < 0.01; I2 = 95.7%; τ2 = 20.8. No difference between subgroups was detected by meta-regression (P = 0.87).

Secondary Outcomes: Pain, Physical, Psychological, and Social Dimensions of the QoL Score

Data on the pain, physical, and psychological dimensions of the QoL score were retrieved from four studies [40–43] (N = 5204), comprising two RCTs and two non-RCTs. The pain dimension of the QoL score showed a significant reduction, with an MC of − 27.5 (95% CI [− 30.0, − 25.0], P < 0.001; mild heterogeneity: Q = 70.3, P < 0.01; I2 = 93.5%; τ2 = 5.9; Fig. 5). Secondary Outcomes: CVD Symptoms

Five studies [40–43, 45] (N = 5083), comprising two RCTs and three non-RCTs, were included in the analysis of the leg pain score, showing a significant reduction with an MC of − 2.9 cm (95% CI [− 3.3, − 2.4], P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. S4) on the 10-cm VAS. The heterogeneity between studies was high (Q = 134.5, P < 0.001; I2 = 96.7%), even though the interstudy variance was low (τ2 = 0.2).

Patients also experienced relief from the leg heaviness symptom after treatment, with data from four studies [41, 43, 45, 48] (two RCTs and two non-RCTs, N = 1590) showing a significant reduction of − 3.4 (− 3.5, − 3.3) cm (P < 0.001), without heterogeneity between studies (Q = 4.6, P = 0.20; I2 = 6.1%; τ2 = 0.0; Supplementary Fig. S5). Furthermore, this symptom was completely resolved on average for 78.7% of the patients (95% CI [59.0%, 93.2%], P < 0.001; N = 3332; Supplementary Fig. S6), with a high heterogeneity between studies (Q = 102.4, P < 0.01; I2 = 94.8%), even though the interstudy variance was low (τ2 = 0.1).

A significant reduction in the leg discomfort score from baseline to the last post-baseline value was observed, with an MC of − 3.4 [− 3.7, − 3.2] cm (P < 0.001; two studies [41, 43], N = 1518; Supplementary Fig. S7), suggesting that MPFF treatment alleviated the symptom.

Finally, cramps were completely resolved in 95.3% of the patients (95% CI [78.9%, 100.0%], P < 0.001; four studies [42, 46–48], N = 3272; Supplementary Fig. S8), with a considerable heterogeneity between studies (Q = 63.9, P < 0.01; I2 = 94.2%) even though the interstudy variance was low (τ2 = 0.1).

Discussion

Summary of Evidence

The main objective of this study was to provide an overall perspective of the treatment effectiveness of MPFF at 1000 mg per day on QoL in patients with CVD, by including data from various types of prospective studies irrespective of their design.

The main findings included a significant reduction in the global QoL score from baseline to the last post-baseline value, with an MC (95% CI) of − 17.1 (− 20.0, − 14.1) (P < 0.001), reflecting an improvement in the patients’ QoL, although a high heterogeneity between studies was revealed. All dimensions of QoL were significantly improved, with − 17.5 (− 19.3, − 15.8) for the psychological dimension, − 20.7 (− 23.4, − 18.0) for the social dimension, − 25.9 (− 34.0, − 17.8) for the physical dimension, and − 27.5 (− 30.0, − 25.0) for the pain dimension. In addition, CVD symptom intensity decreased, with significant reductions in the 10-cm VAS: − 2.9 (− 3.3, − 2.4) cm for leg pain, − 3.4 (− 3.5, − 3.3) cm for heaviness, and − 3.4 (− 3.7, − 3.2) cm for discomfort (all P < 0.001). Some symptoms even completely disappeared post treatment, with 78.7% (59.0%, 93.2%) of patients reporting complete resolution of leg-heaviness symptom, and 95.3% (78.9%, 100.0%) of cramps (both P < 0.001).

Relevance of the Findings

According to available literature, this meta-analysis represents the most extensive study conducted on CVD with a focus on QoL in patients receiving MPFF treatment, encompassing ten studies for a total of 5654 patients. It is also the first meta-analysis to assess all dimensions of QoL questionnaires, including physical, psychological, and social dimensions. In 2018, Kakkos et al. performed a meta-analysis of 1692 patients from seven RCTs (conducted between 1982 and 2015), which showed that MPFF significantly improved CVD symptoms and signs and patients’ QoL [29]. However, unlike the present work, that meta-analysis focused exclusively on RCTs. In 2023, Ulloa et al. performed a systematic literature review that included both RCTs and real-world evidence to review the impact of various VADs on patients’ QoL. The analysis revealed that MPFF was the most frequently represented VAD, appearing in 12 out of 19 studies (two RCTs and ten observational studies, accounting for 12,622 patients with MPFF at baseline). The authors observed that MPFF was significantly superior to placebo in the two RCTs for improving QoL [17]. MPFF was also associated with an improvement in all aspects of QoL in each of the ten observational studies across all CEAP clinical classes, either as monotherapy or in combination with other conservative therapies. In the present work, the global QoL score was significantly improved in both RCT and non-RCT studies, and the meta-regression comparing results across both study designs did not show any differences. In addition, a recent large-scale observational study, VEIN STEP [51], including 6236 patients with CVD, reinforced evidence that MPFF-based conservative treatment was associated with a reduction in the intensity of the main CVD symptoms. This reduction reflected improvements in QoL both overall and across the three CIVIQ-14 dimensions: pain, physical, and psychological.

Seven QoL questionnaires specific to CVD have been described [16, 18–21]. Some of them consider the global impact of CVD progression on the patients’ QoL, such as CIVIQ-20 and CIVIQ-14, Venous Insufficiency Epidemiological and Economic Study—Quality of life/Symptom (VEINES-QoL:Sym), and Specific Quality of life and Outcome Response—Venous (SQOR-V). Other questionnaires focus on certain aspects of CVD, including Aberdeen Varicose Vein Questionnaire (AVVQ), VARIShort (patient-reported outcome measure for superficial venous insufficiency), Venous Leg Ulcer Quality of Life questionnaire (VLU-QoL), and Charing Cross Venous Ulcer questionnaire (CCVUQ). CIVIQ-20 and CIVIQ-14 are among the most extensively validated and have been translated into multiple languages. Of the ten reports selected for this meta-analysis, seven used CIVIQ-20 and two used CIVIQ-14, while one study used a 0–100 scale. The significant reductions observed in the global QoL score, as well as in the QoL dimensions, suggest that it is important for the physician to take into consideration the patients’ perception of the disease.

An interesting study has been published that investigated how physicians and patients perceive the impact of CVD on QoL [52]. It appears that in the early stages of CVD, physicians may focus primarily on symptoms, potentially overlooking the fact that patients’ QoL may be affected, while patients may already perceive negative impacts on various aspects of their lives [52]. For physicians, well-being is primarily related to symptomatology and secondarily to patient’s abilities to perform daily activities or concerns with cosmetic aspects. In contrast, patients prioritize their overall well-being and the impact of CVD on various aspects of their lives. In particular, sleep disturbance, which is often caused by venous symptoms that worsen at night, such as cramping or tingling, is an important factor that negatively affects patients’ daily activities, their condition at work, and their relationships. The clinical severity of CVD is closely linked to a progressive decline in QoL [23–26, 53, 54], with physical functioning being one of the earliest and most significantly affected domains. Carradice et al. demonstrated that the negative impact of uncomplicated varicose veins on QoL was comparable to that of varicose veins with complications (e.g., edema, skin changes, or healed ulcer), highlighting the importance of disease management even in the earlier stages [26]. Indeed, the high prevalence of venous symptoms in patients at early stages of the disease directly affects their daily activities, overall lifestyle, and thus their QoL. This underscores that CVD is not merely a cosmetic problem but a serious medical condition with a negative QoL impact starting from early CEAP stages and comparable with other chronic diseases. For instance, patients classified under CEAP class C3 exhibit a QoL similar to those with diabetes or cancer, while those in class C4 experience QoL levels that are worse than those observed in cancer or chronic pulmonary disease [54]. Moreover, individuals with CEAP classes C5–6 have a markedly diminished QoL, comparable to that of patients with heart failure [54]. The diverse clinical manifestations of CVD make it difficult for clinicians to fully understand the impact of the disease on patients’ QoL. Therefore, it is crucial to encourage the use of QoL questionnaires in clinical practice to guide the selection of the most appropriate therapeutic options, without underestimating the impact of venous symptoms on both physical and emotional aspects.

In the present meta-analysis, as expected, we observed a concordance between the reduction in symptom intensity or their resolution and the improvement in QoL. In patients treated with MPFF, a reduction in the intensity of pain assessed with VAS translated into an improvement in QoL in the pain dimension. This dimension also encompasses questions related to sleep and work impairment, considering not just pain intensity but also the significant impact that pain has on daily activities. Addressing both the severity of pain and its impact on key functions, such as sleep and work, enables a more comprehensive understanding of the individual’s well-being. Overall, the significant improvement of CVD symptoms—including leg pain, heaviness, and discomfort—observed in this meta-analysis may contribute to the comprehensive enhancement of patients’ physical function, psychological well-being, and social life. In addition to symptoms, it is important to recognize that the cosmetic manifestations of CVD (such as telangiectasias, varicose veins, etc.) can also have significant psychological effects. Many patients report feelings of self-consciousness or embarrassment about the appearance of their legs, which can negatively impact their self-esteem and overall mental well-being.

The physical dimension is also crucial. The study performed by Andreozzi showed that the progressive impairment of QoL primarily affects the physical aspects [54]. Indeed, CVD significantly limits patients’ lifestyles, reduces their mobility, and impacts their overall health [55]. Regular physical activity is essential both for the prevention and the management of cardiovascular diseases, as it has been shown to significantly improve cardiovascular health by mitigating risk factors such as hypertension, obesity, and insulin resistance. This is of particular concern given that several recent cohorts have shown that CVD predicts all-cause mortality and increases the risk of major adverse cardiac events, independently of traditional risk factors [13, 14, 56]. Consequently, improving the QoL in patients with CVD, particularly in the physical dimension, may have additional benefits for cardiovascular health.

Limitations of the Study

Several factors may have contributed to heterogeneity among the studies, such as sample size and treatment duration. Treatment duration varied, with most patients receiving MPFF for at least 2 months but ranging from 1 to 6 months. The sub-analysis performed according to treatment duration—short-, medium-, and long-term—showed a progressive improvement in the global QoL score. In addition, at baseline, patients with different stages of CVD were included, which may have impacted the results, as the response to treatment can differ depending on the severity of the disease. However, patients with the most severe disease classifications (CEAP C5 and C6) were not represented in the included studies, highlighting the need for future studies to assess QoL in patients across all stages of the disease, including the more severe stages.

This meta-analysis also has a limitation associated with its single-arm design, meaning that the effectiveness of MPFF was not compared with a placebo treatment. However, this comparison was previously addressed in two meta-analyses, Kakkos et al. [29] and Martinez-Zapata et al. [57], both of which showed significantly improved QoL associated with MPFF treatment compared with placebo, on the basis of RCTs data.

Despite significant heterogeneity among the studies in the present meta-analysis, all selected studies reported a reduction in the disease’s impact on patients’ QoL and improvements in symptoms with MPFF treatment. To mitigate the impact of different assessment tools (CIVIQ-14 and CIVIQ-20), the GIS was considered. However, although GIS enables standardization on a 0–100 scale, it does not overcome the structural differences between the CIVIQ-14 and CIVIQ-20 questionnaires. In addition, although the questionnaire used by Belczak et al. [39] covered several dimensions, it differed from the CIVIQ questionnaires. This limitation should be acknowledged. Missing data were imputed or derived as necessary, and the impact of these imputations was assessed using sensitivity analyses, which raised no concerns (data not shown). Estimates from fixed-effect models were similar to those from random-effects models, suggesting that the heterogeneity among studies did not substantially impact the overall results. Although Servier supported this work, several measures were implemented to limit potential bias, including the use of predefined protocols, registration in PROSPERO, and the independent selection of studies. In addition, an external contract research organization carried out data extraction and analysis. Even with these safeguards, the possibility of some residual influence cannot be entirely ruled out. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution given the inherent limitations of the analysis.

Strengths of the Study

This meta-analysis provides significant added value to current literature. The main strength of this meta-analysis lies in its comprehensive approach, including both real-world studies and RCTs, combining data in the same analysis on QoL regardless of patient profile. The expanded sample size enhances the robustness and reliability of the results, providing a more generalizable understanding of MPFF’s impact on QoL across diverse patient populations. In addition, by integrating data from real-world settings alongside rigorously controlled trials, the analysis offers a broad assessment of MPFF’s effectiveness. In addition, a subgroup analysis stratified by study design showed similar results from RCTs (MC from baseline in global QoL score of − 16.7 [− 21.6, − 11.8] versus non-RCTs − 17.2 [− 21.4, − 13.1]). This suggests that the beneficial effects of MPFF observed in controlled environments are replicable in everyday clinical practice, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the results.

To address any potential confounding due to the heterogeneity of the studies, a sensitivity analysis was also conducted, excluding studies with a high risk of bias. The results of this analysis were consistent with the main findings, confirming the robustness of the primary outcome and reinforcing confidence in the overall conclusions drawn from the data. In addition, funnel plot inspection and a nonsignificant Egger’s test (P = 0.083) indicated no strong evidence of publication bias, although the limited number of studies may reduce the sensitivity of these methods.

Importantly, this meta-analysis emphasized the patient perspective by evaluating not only the global QoL score but also specific domains such as pain, physical functioning, social interactions, and psychological well-being. This multidimensional approach provides a comprehensive understanding of the impact of MPFF on patients’ daily lives and demonstrates significant improvements in all these domains, highlighting the overall benefits of MPFF treatment on patients’ QoL.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis, which includes data from 5654 patients, is the first meta-analysis to specifically evaluate the effect of MPFF on disease-specific QoL scores across a broad range of prospective study designs. MPFF treatment was associated with improvements in QoL in patients with CVD. Improvements were observed across all dimensions, including pain, physical functioning, psychological well-being, and increased social engagement. These findings highlight the importance of a patient-centered approach with a particular focus on QoL in the management of CVD. While evidence suggests that MPFF improve patient-reported outcomes, the study limitations necessitate further research to precisely quantify the magnitude of the clinical benefits.
