Danon Disease Diagnosed by Multimodal Imaging
Authors: Xuanxuan Li, Wenxue Yang, Xin Zhang, Hongzun Xu, Renping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDanon disease is a rare X-linked dominant disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in lysosome-associated membrane protein-2 (LAMP2).Case SummaryWe report an 18-year-old male patient in which multimodal imaging revealed left ventricular hypertrophy, reduced myocardial perfusion, electrocardiographic evidence of pre-excitation, abnormal serologic parameters, and histopathologic abnormalities. Genetic testing identified a variant in LAMP2, confirming the diagnosis of Danon disease. Testing of his mother and 3 children also revealed the LAMP2 variant.DiscussionPatients with Danon disease primarily present with cardiac manifestations, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, and heart failure.Take-Home MessagesYoung men exhibiting myocardial hypertrophy, elevated plasma creatine kinase, pre-excitation on electrocardiography, and/or characteristic late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac magnetic resonance should be evaluated for Danon disease. Multimodal imaging (echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance) combined with genetic testing is key to accurate diagnosis. Family screening and genetic counseling are crucial given the X-linked dominant-inheritance pattern.Key words: Danon disease, LAMP2 gene, WPW syndrome

History of Presentation

A previously healthy 18-year-old man was admitted with a 2-year history of chest tightness and shortness of breath. One week before admission, his symptoms worsened, with increased chest tightness, inability to lie supine, and anuria. He denied any family history of genetic disorders or similar conditions. Physical examination revealed hypotension (blood pressure: 98/66 mm Hg). Development, intelligence, consciousness, skeletal muscle, and retinal findings were normal. On initial hospitalization, limb muscle strength was graded 4/5, with normal muscle tone.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial work-up included electrocardiogram (ECG), transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR). After confirming the hypertrophic cardiomyopathy phenotype, evaluation focused on storage diseases (light chain and transthyretin amyloidosis, PRKAG2 syndrome, Fabry disease, Anderson-Fabry disease) and common genetic causes.

Investigations

Laboratory tests on admission revealed elevated alanine aminotransferase (235 U/L, reference: 0-40), aspartate aminotransferase (353 U/L, reference: 0-40), lactate dehydrogenase (2,139 U/L, reference: 120-250), creatine kinase (2,086 U/L, reference: 24-190), high-sensitivity troponin T (87 pg/mL, reference: 0-0.02), and B-type natriuretic peptide (954 pg/mL, reference: 0-125). ECG showed left ventricular hypertrophy and pre-excitation (Figure 1). TTE demonstrated concentric left ventricular and right ventricular hypertrophy, with the thickest wall measuring 22 mm (Figures 2A and 2B). Tissue Doppler imaging indicated increased end-diastolic filling pressure (E: 90 cm/s, septal e′: 3 cm/s, lateral e′: 4 cm/s). Myocardial strain analysis revealed segmental left ventricular strain reduction (Figure 2C). Coronary computed tomography angiography excluded acute coronary syndrome and confirmed coronary patency. CMR validated morphological and functional abnormalities. Delayed-enhancement imaging showed extensive myocardial enhancement in the biventricular walls and the ventricular septum (Figures 3A to 3C). T1-mapping was elevated (Figure 3D). These findings suggested a decompensation phase of inherited metabolic cardiomyopathy.

Histopathology revealed cardiomyocytes with glycogen inclusions and cytoplasmic vacuoles (Figures 4A to 4C). Genetic testing identified a hemizygous LAMP2 mutation (c.865-2A>G, exon 7, NM_002294), affecting splicing (Figure 2A) and classified as pathogenic according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines (PVS1+PS2+PS4+PM2). This confirmed the diagnosis of Danon disease. The mutation was also detected in the patient's mother (heterozygous) but not in his father (Figure 5). His mother was subsequently diagnosed with cardiomegaly and heart failure.

Management

The patient initially received standard heart failure therapy with oral spironolactone (20 mg once daily), irbesartan (150 mg once daily), and bisoprolol (5 mg once daily), which improved his symptoms. Over a 12-year follow-up, his condition progressively deteriorated, and therapy was escalated to sacubitril-valsartan, dapagliflozin, and vericiguat. Laboratory investigations and cardiac function gradually declined over the course of the illness (Table 1). Holter monitoring revealed frequent ventricular premature beats and ventricular tachycardia, prompting the addition of amiodarone. He was advised to undergo implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement or heart transplantation but declined surgical intervention.

Outcome and Follow-Up

As an adult, the patient had 3 daughters and 1 son without prior genetic counseling. All initially asymptomatic daughters were later diagnosed with Danon disease. The patient died 12 years after his diagnosis, while his mother died 3 years after her diagnosis. The children remain under follow-up. Discussion

LAMP2 gene variants cause left ventricular myocardial scarring, accompanied by autophagy defects, vacuolization, and infiltration from epicardium to endocardium, which may explain the reduced longitudinal strain in the epicardial layer.1 ECGs may show various arrhythmias, most commonly pre-excitation.1

In other myocardial hypertrophy disorders, cardiac amyloidosis exhibits homogeneous left ventricular thickening and granular hyperechogenicity on echocardiography; CMR shows no perfusion defects but extensive delayed enhancement. ECGs show low-voltage, pseudoinfarct Q waves as well as ST-T changes. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy demonstrates asymmetric left ventricular hypertrophy, small chambers, and heterogeneous echogenicity on echocardiography; MRI late gadolinium enhancement shows delayed enhancement in the hypertrophied myocardium, and ECG shows left ventricular hypervoltage. Fabry disease presents with centripetal symmetric left ventricular hypertrophy with reduced left ventricular global longitudinal strain on echocardiography; MRI shows decreased T1 values and prominent late gadolinium enhancement, and ECGs show ST-segment depression.2

In Danon disease, LAMP2 deficiency impairs macrophage function, causing intracellular vacuole accumulation and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy.3 Defective autophagy leads to cell death, diffuse fibrosis, arrhythmias, and ultimately a hypodynamic, hypertrophied heart with wall thinning and dilation.1 Glycogen degradation defects cause glycogen accumulation, while LAMP2 defects result in latent glycogen accumulation, myocardial hypertrophy, and electrophysiologic abnormalities.4 Other contributing mechanisms include increased mitochondrial oxidative stress, altered metabolism, and impaired contractile function.

Extracardiac manifestations of Danon disease include elevated liver and muscle enzymes. It remains unclear whether cholesterol accumulation in the liver of LAMP2-deficient animals results from liver injury or elevated right-heart pressures.1 Some elevated aspartate aminotransferase levels may reflect skeletal muscle injury. Further studies are needed to determine whether liver enzyme levels can track disease progression. Skeletal myopathy is typically mild, associated with myotonic discharges, and may be exacerbated by various factors. Elevated creatine kinase and transaminases in males suggest myocyte damage.3

While cardiac transplantation remains the only therapeutic option with demonstrated survival benefit, a phase I AAV9 gene therapy trial for Danon disease has been completed.5 However, a subsequent phase II AAV9 gene therapy trial6 did not show positive results. Major challenges in designing a comparative effectiveness trial for Danon disease with a traditional control group include low disease prevalence, infrequent outcomes, and modest treatment effect sizes.5

Conclusions

Patients with Danon disease often progress from being asymptomatic or having imperceptible symptoms to rapid deterioration and heart failure. In the current case, although diagnosis occurred during the decompensated stage, it still allowed for initiation of guideline-directed medical therapy and consideration of device-based or surgical interventions to manage disease progression and complications. A thorough understanding of the disease's distinctive clinical and imaging features, particularly using CMR, is essential.
