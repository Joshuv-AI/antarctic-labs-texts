Intrinsic capacity and stroke risk in a multiple cohort study
Authors: Ying Li, YiLin Chen, Yin Chen, YiYang Pan, Yuan Chen, LiJuan Huang, Wei Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intrinsic capacity is a concept established by the World Health Organization (WHO). The concept aims to represent the total of a person’s physical and mental abilities, measured across five domains: cognition, psychological, sensory, vitality, and locomotor that were identified as crucial for healthy aging. The aim being to shift focus from disease to function to predict health, disability, and frailty. In this large-scale cohort study, we aim to investigate the association between intrinsic capacity and incident stroke. The study includes 184,219 participants aged 40 years or older. Intrinsic capacity is assessed across five domains according to the WHO Integrated Care for Older People framework. We use Cox proportional hazards regression models to examine this association and perform stratified and sensitivity analyses to evaluate domain-specific contributions and robustness. Here we show that both overall intrinsic capacity and each of its five domains are significantly associated with stroke incidence, with the strongest association observed among adults aged 80 years or older. These findings highlight intrinsic capacity as a promising, multidimensional target for primary stroke prevention in an aging population.

Introduction

Over the past three decades, substantial progress has been made in stroke prevention and management, supported by advancements in medical science and public health systems1. From 1990 to 2019, the global stroke mortality rate decreased by 36.4%2. Nevertheless, recent epidemiological data indicate that stroke remains a leading contributor to global morbidity and mortality3. By 2021, the global prevalence of stroke reached 93.8 million, with a 70% increase in incident cases. Stroke-related deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) rose by 44% and 32%, respectively. Currently, the global economic burden of stroke exceeds USD 890 billion annually, and in the absence of effective mitigation strategies, it is projected to double by 20504. Thus, addressing the modifiable risk factors for stroke has become a critical priority in global public health.

Well-recognized risk factors for stroke include hypertension, diabetes, tobacco use, physical inactivity, and environmental exposure. The Global Burden of Disease data from 1990 to 2021 highlighted notable shifts in the distribution of these risk factors. For example, salt intake and tobacco use decreased by 4.6% and 12.8%, respectively5. However, there have been marked increases in the prevalence of high body mass index (BMI), elevated fasting plasma glucose levels, increased blood pressure, and exposure to extreme ambient temperatures. Of particular concern, stroke-related DALYs attributable to heat exposure among individuals aged 65 years and older increased by 72.4% between 2017 and 2021 compared to 2000–20046. Despite the overall decline in stroke mortality, population aging has led to a nearly 200% increase in stroke incidence among adults aged 80 years and older, substantially contributing to the global stroke burden7. Age and gender are commonly regarded as non-modifiable risk factors; however, the increasing age-related stroke burden poses substantial and growing challenges for global health systems.

The rapid demographic shift toward an aging global population has altered the epidemiology of non-communicable diseases, thereby necessitating novel approaches for risk assessment and health promotion8. To address this issue, the World Health Organization (WHO) introduced the concept of intrinsic capacity in 2015, a cornerstone of its subsequent “Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030)” global strategy9. While often related, intrinsic capacity, as defined by WHO, differs from functional decline by focusing on the individual’s inherent physical and mental capacities rather than solely their ability to perform daily activities. This framework emphasizes maintaining and enhancing the physical and mental capacities that support healthy aging, thereby enabling older adults to live independently and preserve their functional abilities. This evolution necessitates a paradigm shift from managing single diseases toward delivering comprehensive, people-centered primary and long-term care services, with a central focus on preserving intrinsic capacity. Empirical evidence supports the utility of the five-domain model of intrinsic capacity as a valid construct for assessing functional health in older adults10,11. The decline in intrinsic capacity has been associated with an increased risk of frailty, falls, cardiovascular events, and all-cause mortality. However, there remains a paucity of research examining the relationship between intrinsic capacity and clinically relevant outcomes such as stroke. Strengthening the evidentiary foundation in this area is essential for informing public health interventions, supporting clinical decision-making, and guiding policy development to delay the onset of age-related conditions12,13.

This study is the first to use a large population cohort to identify the association between the development trajectory of intrinsic capacity and the incidence of stroke. The complexity of assessing the correlation between intrinsic capacity development and clinical outcomes has led to a widespread concern that has not been well addressed, particularly amid a growing stroke burden due to population aging. Our findings are expected to support population-based stroke prevention, provide effective interventions to address the rising burden of stroke associated with population aging, and ultimately contribute to reducing the burden on healthcare systems.

Figure 2 illustrates the associations between changes in intrinsic capacity and stroke risk. Compared with participants who maintained moderate intrinsic capacity at baseline, midpoint, and endpoint, those with persistently low intrinsic capacity had an increased risk of stroke (HR 2.11, 95% CI 1.95–2.19), whereas those with consistently high intrinsic capacity had a reduced risk (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.71–0.93). Conversely, participants with high intrinsic capacity at baseline who experienced a progressive decline by the endpoint had an increased risk compared with those who consistently maintained high capacity (HR 1.65, 95% CI 1.37–2.08).

Discussion

In this large-scale cohort study, we identified a significant negative association between intrinsic capacity and stroke incidence. Individuals who maintained stable and high levels of intrinsic capacity exhibited a substantially reduced risk of stroke. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these associations across multiple models. Furthermore, a clear dose-response relationship was observed, with dynamic changes in intrinsic capacity over time closely paralleling variations in stroke risk.

Advances in healthcare have profoundly altered population structures, posing substantial challenges for clinicians and healthcare systems worldwide14,15. By 2050, it is estimated that the population aged over 80 will double, in conjunction with an approximate 50% increase in stroke-related mortality. Consequently, clinical researchers have advocated for a more comprehensive approach to primary stroke prevention that targets individuals across all risk levels and integrates prevention strategies for other diseases that share common risk factors. In line with this perspective, the World Stroke Organization (WSO) has proposed the concept of “primordial stroke prevention,” which underscores the importance of early intervention before the clinical onset of stroke16. The WHO has defined intrinsic capacity, a core concept in healthy aging, as “a composite of all physical and mental capacities that an individual can draw on at any point in time.”17 In our study, intrinsic capacity among individuals aged 80 years and older exhibited a markedly stronger association with stroke incidence compared with younger age groups. The findings are particularly crucial in addressing the projected rise in stroke incidence among adults aged 80 years and older. Our findings suggest that intrinsic capacity is a practical intervention target for the WSO concept of primordial stroke prevention.

Vitality is considered a central domain of intrinsic capacity, reflecting the physiological state arising from the dynamic interplay among multiple physiological systems18. Assessing vitality provides critical insights into an individual’s vulnerability to health risks by capturing the physiological reserves and functional status. Although some studies have indicated that vitality may favorably impact physiological function and overall health, empirical research in this area remains limited19. Our study provides population-based evidence suggesting that maintaining a high level of vitality plays a critical role in preserving neurological function and reducing the incidence of stroke. Thus, enhancing vitality represents a strategic target for preventing age-related conditions, including stroke. Gaining a deeper understanding of how to augment vitality and identify effective long-term interventions to bolster vitality reserves is crucial for mitigating age-associated disease risks.

Locomotor capacity serves as a key measure for assessing age-related mobility decline and functions as both a predictive and modifiable indicator of frailty risk20. Clinical and epidemiological studies have underscored frailty as a significant public health concern, with previous research identifying it as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular events21,22. Our study further reinforces the importance of preserving locomotor capacities before the onset of stroke, which can substantially lower the risk of stroke. These findings emphasize the need for early risk-assessment strategies. Evaluation of locomotor capacity performance in routine screening protocols may enable healthcare professionals to identify early functional decline and implement timely interventions, thereby reducing or delaying the incidence of stroke.

Cognitive impairment is a well-recognized and prevalent sequela of stroke, affecting approximately 30% to 50% of stroke survivors23,24. More recently, individuals with mild cognitive impairment were suggested to have an elevated risk of subsequent stroke, which is potentially linked to underlying vascular dysfunction or lifestyle-related factors. A longitudinal study reported that following a diagnosis of cognitive impairment, the incidence of ischemic stroke reached 30% within one year and increased to 40% within two years. Cognitive dysfunction may reflect the systemic activation of endothelial injury pathways and thus serve as a prognostic marker for stroke risk. The pathological processes driving endothelial dysfunction not only play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of dementia but also contribute to blood-brain barrier disruption, chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, accelerated neurodegeneration, cerebral microbleeds, and ischemic neuronal injury25,26. Our large-scale cohort study provided robust evidence supporting these mechanistic links.

Recent studies have reported a marked increase in both hearing and vision impairments among individuals aged 80 years and older, accompanied by an elevated risk of stroke27. To date, only a limited number of studies have demonstrated a notable association between sensory impairment and stroke occurrence. This relationship is commonly attributed to shared risk-factor mechanisms. For instance, carotid artery plaques can induce hemodynamic disturbances and constitute a common risk factor for both stroke and sensory impairments. Vascular risk factors, such as hypertension and diabetes, not only elevate the risk of stroke but may also provoke cochlear or retinal ischemia, resulting in hearing and vision impairments28. In addition, studies have shown that hearing loss can exacerbate perceived and actual social isolation, reducing both the quantity and quality of social networks29. Similarly, individuals with visual impairment experience lower social participation, poorer quality of life, greater social isolation, and reduced medical adherence due to the loss of certain functional abilities. Declines in physical activity and the presence of depression may further contribute to frailty. Together, these direct and indirect factors can increase the risk of stroke30. Although the precise pathophysiological mechanisms linking sensory impairments and stroke remain incompletely elucidated, our findings underscore the potential value of early population-level screening for hearing and vision impairments as a critical component of preclinical prevention and management of stroke.

Although studies examining the relationship between depressive symptoms and stroke risk have yielded inconsistent results, a growing body of evidence suggests that depression can markedly increase the risk of cardiovascular events in older adults31. Evidence indicates that depression contributes to endothelial dysfunction, promotes chronic inflammation, and accelerates vascular injury through increased release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby fostering atherosclerotic plaque formation and increasing the risk of stroke. Depression is also associated with enhanced platelet reactivity and thrombosis, which may result in cerebral embolism. Conversely, cardiovascular events represent major traumatic stressors that can precipitate or exacerbate depressive disorders32,33. Differences in the sample size, demographic characteristics, and ethnic composition may partially explain these inconsistencies. In our study, depressive symptoms were strongly associated with an increased incidence of stroke. Notably, with advancing age, the influence of socioeconomic factors on the risk of stroke appears to diminish, whereas psychological and social support factors play increasingly prominent roles in modulating stroke susceptibility in older individuals.

Despite the growing interest and research driven by the WHO advocacy over the past decade, substantial gaps remain in understanding the relationship between intrinsic capacity and clinical outcomes to effectively guide diagnosis, treatment, and preclinical prevention. A study used four domains of intrinsic capacity measurement tools to explore the association between intrinsic capacity and cardiovascular disease mortality, including long-term stroke conditions34. This result would overestimate the association between intrinsic capacity and stroke. Intrinsic capacity is a concept of healthy aging, but this study only included individuals aged 37 to 76 years. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a robust association between intrinsic capacity and stroke incidence. Our study is based on four aging cohorts with a follow-up period of 28 years, and includes participants aged 80 years and older. Moreover, we assessed intrinsic capacity across all five domains to strengthen the reliability and validity of the findings. Our findings advocate a comprehensive risk assessment approach that incorporates the patients’ global functional status to better guide stroke risk management among older patients. Specifically, maintaining and enhancing intrinsic capacity has emerged as a feasible strategy for the preclinical and primary prevention of stroke in older adults. Frailty has long been recognized as a clinical condition associated with multiple diseases35. It is also described as a common manifestation of chronic disease, contributing to an increased risk of falls and mortality in the short term. Although the management of frailty involves diagnosis and treatment, accurate assessment and effective care are often challenged by complex presentations and multi-morbidity. By contrast, the concept of intrinsic capacity emphasizes preclinical functional reserves, is positively framed, and remains highly modifiable. Decline in intrinsic capacity represents the precursor and principal pathway to frailty. Intervening at this stage, before the diagnostic threshold for frailty is crossed, provides an opportunity for effective and cost-efficient prevention.

The WHO has progressively introduced the concepts of frailty, multimorbidity, and intrinsic capacity. Previous studies have reported the predictive roles of frailty and multimorbidity in stroke events. Research indicates that frailty is an independent predictor of stroke incidence; however, evidence also underscores that frailty is particularly well suited for predicting adverse post-stroke outcomes and for identifying risk among high-risk older individuals36,37. Other studies have demonstrated that patients with multimorbidity, such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, have a significantly higher risk of stroke compared to those without these conditions38,39. It is well-established that these chronic clinical diseases are also independent risk factors for stroke. Compared to early stroke prevention, multimorbidity has greater clinical significance in predicting post-stroke functional recovery, complications, and mortality. In the present study, we have developed a model using intrinsic capacity to predict stroke incidence in the general elderly individuals. Our findings suggest that intrinsic capacity may serve as a promising tool for the early prediction of stroke.

This study has several limitations. First, although the WSO reports that ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes account for 65.3% and 34.7% of global stroke cases, respectively, detailed clinical and pathological classifications were not available in the four population-based cohort studies, limiting our ability to examine associations between intrinsic capacity and specific stroke subtypes. Although ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes have overlapping risk factors, they are characterized by distinct pathological mechanisms. This distinction may lead to an underestimation of the association with ischemic stroke and an overestimation with hemorrhagic stroke. Second, despite the WHO’s identification of five domains of intrinsic capacity, a unified assessment framework has yet to be established globally. It is important to clarify that the intrinsic capacity measurement tool used in this study is only valid for the present study population. Third, in this study, participants aged 80 years and above had the highest proportion of missing values. As intrinsic capacity generally declines with age, this pattern suggests that the missing data were likely MNAR. Under such circumstances, multiple imputation based on the missing at random assumption could introduce additional bias. In the absence of strong prior knowledge to explicitly model MNAR mechanisms, we considered complete case analysis to be a more transparent and conventional approach. To mitigate potential bias, we also performed stratified analyses. Nonetheless, the missing data may have introduced exclusion bias, which should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. In addition, the absence of data on specific medication classes and other unmeasured covariates may introduce residual confounding, potentially leading to biased effect estimates. Fourth, although the trajectory analysis provides dynamic insights, the primary analysis depends on baseline intrinsic capacity, which may not fully capture longitudinal changes throughout the follow-up period in all participants. Fifth, self-reported assessments of cognitive, sensory, and psychological capacity may be subject to reporting bias. Nevertheless, the WHO has convened experts to evaluate these measures and has confirmed their applicability and effectiveness in primary health care at the community level.

Stroke remains a leading cause of mortality and a major contributor to long-term disability worldwide. High-quality prevention, acute treatment, and rehabilitation services are essential components of stroke management, and prevention is of paramount importance. However, current primary prevention strategies have failed to comprehensively reduce the incidence of stroke, particularly among individuals aged 80 years and older, who face higher incidence rates, more severe sequelae, and challenges in pharmacological management. Our findings provide compelling evidence that stabilizing and maintaining a high level of intrinsic capacity is associated with a lower risk of stroke. This insight has profound implications in stroke prevention and clinical practice. Future research should focus on identifying effective interventions to enhance intrinsic capacity, including cognitive training, targeted exercise programs, improved nutrition, and promotion of social participation, as well as exploring the application of artificial intelligence in clinical and community practice for continuous monitoring and evaluation.

Methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study employed publicly available aggregated data without individual-level identifiers. Ethical approval was not required as the utilized data were already anonymized and in the public domain. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design and participants

This large-scale cohort study pooled data from four longitudinal studies: the Health and Retirement Study (HRS)40; the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA)41; the Survey of Health, Aging, and Retirement in Europe (SHARE)42; and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)43. The HRS utilized a multistage probability design based on the US population. The SHARE covered 29 countries using probability sampling. The ELSA is an annual cross-sectional survey designed for the English population. The CHARLS employed a multistage probability design across China. Data coverage spanned 1992–2020 for HRS, 2002–2019 for ELSA, and 2004–2020 for SHARE (with the exception of 2008). Data collection follows a biennial schedule. CHARLS contributed data from the 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018 waves. Response rates varied by study design: HRS (85–90%, steady-state design), CHARLS (85%, at baseline), ELSA (82% in Wave 2, post-technological update), and SHARE (61% in Wave 1, due to multinational complexity). This study utilizes data from four large-scale, nationally representative cohort studies, each of which provides a sample size sufficient to ensure adequate statistical power. In total, 234,340 participants, including their spouses, were surveyed biennially between 1992 and 2020. Supplementary Tables 1, 2 summarize the baseline characteristics of participants. This study utilized publicly available data; therefore, no pre-specified exclusion criteria were applied.

Intrinsic capacity measures

Intrinsic capacity was measured in accordance with the WHO guidelines and assessed across five domains: cognitive, locomotor, vitality, sensory, and psychological capacity44. Intrinsic capacity scores were derived for all the participants based on five domains, with total scores ranging from 0 to 51 and higher scores indicating greater intrinsic capacity.

Cognitive capacity was evaluated dimensions using two subscales: memory and orientation. The word recall test, comprising 20 items, assessed immediate and delayed memory, whereas the orientation test included four items related to time orientation. The scores ranged from 0 to 24, with higher scores indicating better cognitive function. Locomotor capacity was assessed using the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) scale and the chair stand test. Participants who reported no difficulty in tasks such as using a phone, managing money, shopping, preparing meals, and taking medications were assigned 5 points; otherwise, 0 points were assigned. In the chair stand test, participants who were able to rise from a chair without using their arms scored 1 point, whereas those requiring assistance scored 0. Vitality, a physiological component of intrinsic capacity, reflects an individual’s energy balance and metabolic status. In the Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) screening framework, nutritional assessment is applied to evaluate vitality. BMI is a widely accepted, simple, and practical measure of nutritional and metabolic status in community-based primary healthcare45. Vitality was evaluated using BMI based on the WHO criteria. BMI categories were scored as follows: 18.5–24.9 kg/m² = 3 points; 25.0–34.9 kg/m² = 2 points; <18.5 kg/m² = 1 point; and >35.0 kg/m² = 0 points. Participants from CHARLS were categorized according to Asian-specific criteria, with BMI scored as follows: 18.5–22.9 kg/m² = 3 points; 23.0–29.9 kg/m² = 2 points; <18.5 kg/m² = 1 point; >30.0 kg/m² = 0 points. Sensory capacity was assessed using self-reported eyesight and hearing rated on a five-point scale from poor (1 point) to excellent (5 points). Although grip strength is commonly employed as a proxy indicator of vitality, it was not included in this study due to the lack of corresponding measurements in the earlier cohorts. Eyesight scores were calculated as the average of the distance and near-vision ratings. The highest score for sensory capacity is 10 points. Psychological capacity was measured using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), which comprised eight items assessing the participants’ feelings over the previous week. The total score was calculated after reversing the relevant items, with values ranging from 0 to 8. The detailed measurement methods for intrinsic capacity are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Covariates

Covariates were assessed during each observation period from 1992 to 2020. Participants self-reported their demographic characteristics, including gender, marital status, educational level, and individual income. Age was calculated from the reported date of birth. Smoking status was assessed by asking participants whether they currently smoked; those who reported ever smoking were further asked about the age and year at which they started and stopped smoking. Alcohol use was assessed by asking, “During the past year, did you drink any alcoholic beverages, such as beer, wine, or liquor?” Respondents who reported drinking provided additional information on beverage types, frequency of consumption, and subjective experiences related to drinking. Physical activity was evaluated according to type, intensity, and frequency. General health status was self-reported on a five-point scale ranging from very good to very bad. Participants also reported their medical history. However, data on specific medication classes, detailed dietary habits, nuanced social network characteristics, and direct measures of cognitive reserve were not available in this study.

Outcomes

At baseline, 2850 participants with a prior physician-confirmed diagnosis of stroke were excluded from the outcome assessment. The remaining eligible participants were continuously monitored for their health status and survival throughout the follow-up period. Stroke was diagnosed by physicians based on clinical assessment; however, detailed classification into ischemic or hemorrhagic subtypes was not consistently available across all cohorts, representing a limitation in our outcome ascertainment.

Statistical analysis

A total of 184,219 participants aged ≥40 years were included in the analysis. The cohort comprised 81,754 (44.4%) men and 102,465 (55.6%) women, with a mean baseline age of 61.4 years. Gender-based differences were carefully considered in this study. Participants’ gender was self-reported, and analyses were stratified accordingly. Baseline characteristics across the different cohorts are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Person-years of follow-up were calculated from the baseline to either a physician-confirmed stroke diagnosis or the end of the observation period. Sensitivity analyses suggested that the data were likely missing not at random (MNAR); therefore, we employed complete case analysis to address the missing data. We restricted our analysis to complete cases by excluding observations with missing data.

Cox proportional hazards (CPH) regression models were used to examine the association between intrinsic capacity levels and incident stroke. Intrinsic capacity scores were categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4), with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group, and three dummy variables were created to represent different exposure levels. The proportional hazard assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals and graphical diagnostics (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). Person-years exposed to relevant risk factors were calculated, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated to quantify stroke risk relative to intrinsic capacity levels. Stratified CPH models further assessed the association between each domain of intrinsic capacity and stroke incidence. All models were adjusted for potential confounders, including age, gender, marital status, individual income, educational level, smoking status, alcohol use, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes, and self-reported health status.

We performed formal interaction tests to assess effect modification by age, gender, and cohort. The tests revealed significant interactions (P for interaction <0.001), indicating that the association between intrinsic capacity and stroke differed by age groups, gender, and cohorts.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the association between intrinsic capacity and incident stroke. First, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to compare the intrinsic capacity levels across the four cohorts. Within each cohort, CPH models were used to evaluate the association between intrinsic capacity and the risk of stroke. Second, we performed an age-stratified analysis by CPH models for individuals aged <80 years and those aged ≥80 years to assess the association between intrinsic capacity and stroke across different age groups. Third, intrinsic capacity was assessed at baseline, midpoint, and endpoint, and classified into high, medium, or low categories at each time point. Based on intrinsic capacity levels at baseline, midpoint, and endpoint, three control groups were established to examine the association between intrinsic capacity trajectories and incident stroke. Fourth, to verify the robustness of the hypothesized association between intrinsic capacity and stroke, a directed acyclic graph (DAG) was developed (Supplementary Fig. 4). Additionally, intrinsic capacity scores were included in the model as a continuous variable (Supplementary Table 4).

All statistical tests were two-sided, and P-values < 0.05 were deemed statistically significant. Data analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R (version: 4.1.2). Schematic diagrams and mechanistic models were developed using Adobe Photoshop 2023 to visually summarize key findings.
