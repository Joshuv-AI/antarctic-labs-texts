Long-range circuit of the mouse spinal trigeminal nucleus caudalis
Authors: Leili Tang, Tao Jiang, Anan Li, Yong Deng, Xiangning Li, Hui Gong, Miao Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe spinal trigeminal nucleus caudalis (SPVC) is a key central relay in the trigeminal circuit that processes orofacial nociceptive and somatosensory information. However, its long-range connectivity patterns remain poorly characterized.MethodsUsing viral tracing and fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (fMOST), we obtained three-dimensional datasets of long-range input-output circuits of the SPVC in adult male C57BL/6J mice. An orofacial pain model was established by injecting formalin into the whisker pad of adult male C57BL/6J mice, followed by pain behavior assays and cfos immunohistochemical staining. Using adult male Fos-CreER mice combined with viral tracing and fMOST imaging, we mapped the projection patterns of pain-activated neurons in the SPVC.ResultsWe systematically mapped the long-range connectivity patterns of SPVC in adult male mice. High-resolution, three-dimensional whole-head imaging with the fMOST system revealed substantial neuronal input to the SPVC from the trigeminal ganglion (TG) neurons innervating the facial skin and vomeronasal glands. Cortical input neurons exhibited clear subregional preferences. Whole-brain and spinal cord continuous imaging further demonstrated that the SPVC projects centrally via two distinct circuits that target the thalamus and the parabrachial nucleus (PB), respectively. The thalamic projections were further subdivided into dorsal and ventral circuits and exhibited clear subregional specificity within the thalamus. Additionally, the SPVC extended to the spinal cord through three major projection circuits, reaching as far as the lumbar segments. Along the whole-brain and spinal projection circuits, synaptic connections were identified in major target regions, including the submedial nucleus of the thalamus (SMT), ventral posteromedial nucleus of the thalamus (VPM), PB, and the C1 segment of the cervical spinal cord. Furthermore, tracing of SPVC pain-activated neurons revealed that they do not project to cerebellar crus 2 of the ansiform lobule (ANcr2) and paraflocculus (PFL).ConclusionsHigh-resolution 3D imaging reveals the SPVC long-range connectome across head, brain, and spinal cord. Together, these findings establish a comprehensive connectivity framework for understanding the neural mechanisms underlying orofacial pain and provide a foundation for advances in basic sensory research and disease prevention and treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02380-5.

Introduction

The precise processing of orofacial sensory information, particularly the transmission and integration of nociceptive signals, is fundamental for organisms to perceive potential threats and execute adaptive behaviors. As the primary pathway conveying orofacial sensory information to the central nervous system, the trigeminal system plays a central role in this process. The spinal trigeminal nucleus caudalis (SPVC), the caudal component of the trigeminal complex, is a continuous structure that extends from a level slightly rostral to the obex to the spinal cord and exhibits a laminar organization similar to that of the cervical dorsal horn [1]. The SPVC is involved in multiple functions, including orofacial pain processing and pain-induced aversive emotions [2–4]. The SPVC is well-established as a core relay station for orofacial nociceptive information ascending to the brain. Nevertheless, its long-range connectivity remains poorly characterized, which limits our understanding of the neural circuit mechanisms underlying orofacial pain and its associated behaviors.

Previous studies have shown that the SPVC not only receives nociceptive inputs from the trigeminal ganglion (TG) neurons innervating the facial skin but also integrates descending modulatory inputs from multiple brain regions, including the somatosensory cortex (SS), agranular insular area (AI), hypothalamus, and rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM), forming a complex input circuit that provides the structural basis for fine-tuned modulation of sensory information [5–8]. The SPVC also directly projects to higher-order brain regions involved in pain processing, such as the thalamus, hypothalamus, and parabrachial nucleus (PB) [9, 10]. The PB is considered a key relay for transmitting nociceptive information to pain modulatory circuits and is involved not only in pain processing but also in sleep-wake regulation and anorexia [11–13]. A recent study revealed that specific subtypes of SPVC neurons project to the ventral posteromedial nucleus of the thalamus (VPM), PB, and spinal dorsal horn, participating in diverse physiological processes including sensory processing, emotional regulation, and orofacial self-grooming behaviors [14]. Notably, neurons projecting to the PB and spinal cord exhibit lamina-specific distributions within the SPVC and are largely spatially segregated, suggesting marked projection specificity among functionally distinct neuronal populations. However, these findings have largely relied on traditional tracing methods, such as local injection of dyes or viruses into peripheral tissues or the SPVC followed by two-dimensional sectioning [15]. Such approaches have inherent limitations: they cannot achieve continuous large-volume tracing across the whole head, whole brain and spinal cord; lack three-dimensional spatial information necessary to resolve precise projection trajectories and target topography; cannot effectively distinguish projection patterns of functionally distinct neuronal subpopulations (such as pain-activated neurons and non-pain neurons); and provide limited validation of synaptic connections. These constraints have hindered our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying orofacial pain processing. Therefore, a systematic mapping of the long-range input-output connections of the SPVC is urgently needed.

In this study, we systematically mapped the long-range connectivity of the SPVC by combining viral tracing strategies with high-resolution, three-dimensional continuous imaging using the fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (fMOST) system [16]. Continuous whole-head imaging in mice revealed neuronal input to the SPVC from the TG neurons innervating the facial skin and vomeronasal glands. Cortical input exhibited subregional specificity. Continuous whole-brain and spinal cord imaging further demonstrated that the SPVC sends distinct projections within the brain and spinal cord, with thalamic projections showing clear subregional preference. Synaptic connections were observed in all major downstream targets along the SPVC projection circuits. Furthermore, this study revealed that SPVC pain-activated neurons do not project to the the cerebellar crus 2 of the ansiform lobule (ANcr2) and paraflocculus (PFL). This comprehensive connectivity map of the SPVC provides crucial anatomical insights into the neural mechanisms underlying orofacial pain and establishes an essential foundation for future functional studies targeting specific circuits.

Methods

Animals

All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology. Adult male C57BL/6J mice (2–6 months old, Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.) and Fos-CreER mice (2–6 months old, Jackson Laboratory, 021882) were used. Mice were group-housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-hour alternating light/dark cycle, controlled temperature (22–24 °C) and humidity (40–60%), and provided ad libitum access to food and water. For input and output tracing experiments, n = 3 per group; for synaptic experiments, n = 3 per group; for immunohistochemistry, n = 3 per group; for behavioral experiments, n = 10 for the control group, n = 9 for the saline group, and n = 10 for the formalin group.

Virus and stereotactic injection

For retrograde tracing of inputs to the SPVC, rAAV-EF1α-EGFP (AAV2/11, 5.03 E + 12 vg/ml, BC-0012/11) was used. For anterograde tracing of SPVC outputs, rAAV-hSyn-EGFP (AAV2/9, 2.04 E + 12 vg/ml, BC-0020/9) or rAAV-EF1α-DIO-EGFP (AAV2/9, 4.2 E + 12 vg/ml, BC-0015/9) was injected. For presynaptic terminal labeling, a mixture of rAAV-hSyn-flex-mGFP-T2A-Synaptophysin-mRuby (AAV2/9, 2.50 E + 13 vg/ml, BC-0464/9) and rAAV-hSyn-SV40 NLS-Cre (AAV9, 5.01 E + 12 vg/ml, BC-0159/9) was used. These above viruses were purchased from BrainCase.

Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (1% in saline, 10 µL/g body weight) and placed in a stereotaxic frame (RWD). For retrograde tracing, 100 nL of rAAV2/11-EF1α-EGFP was injected into the SPVC (AP, − 7.76 mm; ML, − 1.86 mm; DV, − 3.8 mm) at a rate of 20 nL/min. Three weeks later, mice were perfused. For anterograde tracing, 100 nL of rAAV2/9-hSyn-EGFP or rAAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-EGFP was injected into the SPVC at a rate of 20 nL/min. Three weeks later, mice were perfused. For presynaptic terminal labeling, 100 nL of rAAV2/9-hSyn-flex-mGFP-T2A-Synaptophysin-mRuby and rAAV2/9-hSyn-SV40 NLS-Cre (mixed at a ratio of 2:1) was injected into the SPVC at a rate of 20 nL/min. After surgery, mice were allowed to recover for 3 weeks before perfusion.

Orofacial model of pain

Formalin (4%, 10 µL) or saline (10 µL) was subcutaneously injected into the right whisker pad of mice. Pain-evoked wiping behaviors were recorded using a video camera. A single wiping behavior was defined as a gentle, single stroke of the forepaw across the cheek from the caudal to the rostral direction [17].

Pain behavior assays

All mice were handled daily for at least 3 days before the behavioral experiment. On the day of the behavioral test, the mice were habituated to the test room conditions for at least 1 h prior to experimentation. All behavioral procedures were performed during the day cycle.

Activity-dependent labeling of pain-activated neurons

Fos-CreER mice received SPVC injections of 100 nL of rAAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-EGFP. Fourteen days later, 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT, Sigma-Aldrich, T5648) was administered intraperitoneally to competitively bind the ER receptor, inducing the fused Cre protein to dissociate from its anchoring protein HSP90 via conformational change, translocate into the nucleus, recognize LoxP sites, and mediate recombination [18, 19]. To minimize stress, all experimental animals were handled daily for at least three consecutive days prior to 4-OHT injection. Once the animals had fully adapted to the experimental environment, 4-OHT was administered intraperitoneally at 20 mg/kg, followed 30 min later by subcutaneous injection of formalin (4%, 10 µL) or saline (10 µL) into the right whisker pad. Mice were perfused three weeks after viral expression. The 4-OHT solution was formulated in corn oil (Aladdin, C116025).

Histology

Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium and perfused with 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Sigma-Aldrich, P3812) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Sigma-Aldrich, P6148). After perfusion, the intact head, brain, and spinal cord were carefully removed and post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for at least 12 h.

For samples used to obtain input neurons of the intact head, LR-White resin (Ted Pella Inc, Redding, CA, USA) was used for embedding. LR-White resin readily penetrates large-volume samples and provides excellent sectioning properties after polymerization. PFA-fixed intact mouse heads were rinsed multiple times in 0.01 M PBS. The samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 85%, 95%-I, 95%-II, 95%-III), with each step lasting 12 h. Subsequently, the samples were infiltrated with a graded series of LR-White resin solutions (50%, 70%, 85%, 100%), each for 36 h, and finally embedded and polymerized in molds.

For all samples used to obtain whole-brain and spinal cord input neurons and axonal outputs, Lowicryl HM20 resin (Polysciences, Inc., 15924–1) was used for embedding [20]. PFA-fixed mouse brains and spinal cords were rinsed multiple times in 0.01 M PBS, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series (50%, 75%, 95%) for 2 h each, and three additional 100% ethanol washes for 2 h each. The samples were then infiltrated with Lowicryl HM20 resin at 50%, 75%, and 100%. All steps were performed at 4 °C. Finally, the samples were embedded and polymerized in 100% Lowicryl HM20 resin.

For all samples used to obtain presynaptic terminal distributions in the whole-brain and spinal cord, agarose embedding was used. PFA-fixed mouse brains and spinal cords were rinsed multiple times in 0.01 M PBS. Agarose (Sigma, A0576) was oxidised by stirring in 10 mM sodium periodate (NaIO4, Sigma, 311445) solution for 2 h at room temperature (20–25 °C); then, the oxidised agarose was washed three times in 0.01 M PBS and resuspended in 0.01 M PBS to bring the final concentration to 5%. Prior to embedding, the agarose was heated in a microwave, then dropped into molds, and the surface-dried brains and spinal cords were quickly placed into the agarose. After complete solidification, the samples were removed and stored in 0.01 M PBS until further use.

For immunohistochemistry, PFA-fixed mouse brains were rinsed multiple times in 0.01 M PBS and then embedded in agarose for sectioning. Freely floating sections were washed with 0.1 M PBS and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin and 0.3% Triton X-100 in 0.1 M PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were incubated with a rabbit polyclonal antibody against cfos (1:1000, diluted in PBS with 10% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100, Synaptic Systems, 226008) for 48 h at 4℃. Sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 488, donkey anti-rabbit IgG, 1:1000, Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 2 h at room temperature. Sections were washed three times with 0.1 M PBS between the changes of antibody incubations. Each section was counterstained with DAPI (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA).

Imaging and preprocessing

For whole-head samples, dual-color fluorescence images were acquired using the fMOST system (equipped with a 20x water-immersion objective (XLUMPLFLN 20XW, Olympus) with a numerical aperture (NA) of 1.0) at a resolution of 0.32 μm × 0.32 μm × 5 μm. For whole-brain and spinal cord samples, images were acquired at a resolution of 0.32 μm × 0.32 μm × 2 μm. Resin-embedded samples were mounted on the stage and immersed in propidium iodide (PI, Thermo Fisher Scientific, P21493) solution to simultaneously capture cellular architecture [21]. Imaging was performed by moving the stage so that the objective scanned the sample surface, after which the imaged portion was removed with a diamond knife, enabling serial sectioning and imaging. This process was repeated until the entire sample was imaged, yielding complete and continuous datasets [22].

For agarose-embedded samples, sections were obtained using a vibration microtome (Leica, VT1200S). For samples used to examine presynaptic terminal distributions in the whole-brain and spinal cord, mouse brains and spinal cords were coronally sectioned at 70 μm and the resulting sections were subsequently imaged using a virtual slide scanning system (Olympus BX61VS) with a 10× objective. For high-magnification imaging of presynaptic terminals, a 63× oil-immersion objective (63×/1.46, alphaPlan-Apochromat, DIC slider EC PN 63 × 1.25 III, CA 63×/1.2 W III) on a confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM710) was used. For immunohistochemistry samples, mouse brains were coronally sectioned at 70 μm using a vibration microtome, and sections were imaged on a virtual slide scanning system with a 10× objective.

Data processing

Registration

To obtain the input and projection patterns in the SPVC, we registered our data to the Allen Mouse Brain Common Coordinate Framework version 3 (Allen CCFv3) [23]. The registration method was as previously described [24]. The image stack of labeled neural structures was resampled to isotropic 2 μm voxels, the brain outline was segmented, and the intensity outside the outline was set to zero. Using the transformation parameters from the registration step, the data were warped to the Allen CCFv3 at 2 μm scaling. Injection sites were manually segmented on registered coronal sections.

Quantification of whole-brain inputs

For input neurons, we firstly used NeuroGPS to automatically identify and locate the somata of these neurons [25, 26]. Then, we manually examined all neurons in the upstream input areas to avoid unrecognized errors and registered the coordinates of these neurons to Allen CCFv3 [24]. The neurons at the injection site were eliminated, and the input neurons from other different regions were combined together, which was called the total input neurons. In order to reflect the connection strength of different input regions to the injection site, the percentage of input neurons in a single region to the total input neurons was presented in this study. Subsequent analyses were based on the connection strength values of different brain regions.

Quantification of whole-brain projections

Projection signals were detected and quantified as previously described [27]. Briefly, each registered section was background-subtracted, Gaussian-filtered, and threshold-segmented. The whole brain was divided into 10 μm × 10 μm × 10 μm grids to calculate signal density per grid, generating a 10 μm voxel-resolution signal density matrix. Voxels that could not be back-tracked to the injection site were removed. All results were manually inspected, and the projection signal volume in each brain region was normalized by the total whole-brain signal volume (excluding the injection site) to represent projection strength.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v.8.02. Statistical significance was assessed by t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. All three-dimensional visualizations were generated using Amira software (v5.2.2, Mercury Computer Systems, San Diego, CA, USA). Final figures were compiled and exported using Adobe Illustrator (Adobe, 2022).

Results

Whole-head input distribution of SPVC

The SPVC receives orofacial nociceptive information and transmits it from the peripheral nervous system to higher-order brain regions. To map the peripheral inputs to the SPVC, we injected a retrograde tracer, rAAV2/11-EF1α-EGFP into the SPVC of C57BL/6J mice (Fig. 1A and Fig. S1A). The whole mouse head was resin-embedded and imaged using the fMOST system to acquire a continuous dataset at a spatial resolution of 0.32 μm × 0.32 μm × 5 μm (Fig. 1B). Three-dimensional reconstruction of the serial coronal sections demonstrated the well-preserved architecture of the whole mouse head (Fig. 1C). Using this approach, we systematically mapped the input circuits to the SPVC. Whole-head three-dimensional mapping revealed that these inputs could be broadly classified into two categories: intracranial and peripheral (Fig. 1D). Consistent with classical neuroanatomy, we observed neuronal input to the SPVC from the TG neurons innervating the facial skin (Fig. 1E and F). This alignment with previous findings validates the reliability of our methodological approach [15, 28].

We next characterized the peripheral inputs in detail. Peripheral afferents were primarily conveyed via the maxillary nerve (V2), a branch of the trigeminal nerve (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1B). In addition to TG neurons that innervate the facial skin and input to the ipsilateral SPVC (Fig. 1E and F), we also observed nerve terminals in the vomeronasal glands (Fig. 1G). These neurons entered the cranium via the trigeminal nerve tract (Vn) and input to the ipsilateral SPVC (Fig. 1H). Notably, the terminals originating from the vomeronasal glands far exceeded those of the previously reported facial skin inputs.

The use of the fMOST system for three-dimensional continuous imaging enabled serial data acquisition from the whole-head. Compared to conventional two-dimensional sectioning, this approach preserves the integrity of cranial structures while enabling precise reconstruction of their three-dimensional morphology and spatial location.

Whole-brain and spinal cord input distribution of SPVC

The SPVC also receives inputs from the central nervous system and participates in the processing of nociceptive information. To map the whole-brain and spinal inputs to SPVC, rAAV2/11-EF1α-EGFP was injected into the SPVC of C57BL/6J mice (Fig. S2A). The brain and spinal cord samples were resin-embedded and imaged using fMOST system to acquire a continuous dataset with a spatial resolution of 0.32 μm × 0.32 μm × 2 μm (Fig. 2A and Fig. S3). Three-dimensional visualization of the calculated whole-brain input neurons revealed that SPVC input neurons were primarily located in the cortex, medulla, pons, and midbrain, with a smaller population in the hypothalamus (Fig. 2B).

We next quantified the distribution of SPVC input neurons across brain regions. The proportion of input neurons from each brain region relative to the total number of input neurons was used to represent the input strength of different upstream regions (Fig. 2C). SPVC receives extensive inputs from the cortex and medulla, as well as from other brain regions including the striatum, hypothalamus, midbrain, and pons (Fig. 2C). To further compare the SPVC circuit with different brain regions, we analyzed the ipsilateral and contralateral connectivity (Fig. 2C). SPVC received bilateral inputs from most regions. Notably, contralateral cortical inputs to SPVC were more numerous than ipsilateral ones, while ipsilateral medullary inputs were slightly more abundant than contralateral inputs. Within the striatum, inputs originated exclusively from the central amygdalar nucleus (CEA) with inputs being predominantly ipsilateral and only sparse contralateral innervation. As an additional approach to visualize the organizational principles of whole-brain inputs, input neurons were mapped onto coronal sections of a standardized brain atlas, generating a continuous whole-brain input map (Fig. 2D). Consistent with the quantitative analysis, SPVC inputs primarily originated from the cortex, medulla, pons, and midbrain.

The cortex was identified as the major input region to the SPVC. Cortical input neurons were primarily located in layer V (Fig. 3A). Consistent with the whole-brain analysis, SPVC received bilateral cortical input, with contralateral inputs outnumbering ipsilateral ones. Input neurons were distributed across both dorsal and ventral cortical regions, with a bias towards lateral and anterior cortical regions (Fig. 3B). To further examine the organization of these cortical inputs, we performed three-dimensional visualization of cortical subregions with relatively abundant inputs (Fig. 3C), including the anterior cingulate area (ACA), secondary motor area (MOs), primary motor area (MOp), primary somatosensory cortex (SSp), supplemental somatosensory area (SSs), visceral area (VISC), gustatory area (GU), and AI. We next quantified the input strength from different cortical subregions to the SPVC by calculating the percentage of input neurons originating from each cortical subregion relative to the total number of input neurons (Fig. 3D). Cortical inputs to the SPVC exhibited marked regional preferences. The SPVC received the strongest inputs from the SSp and MOs. Within the SSp, the primary somatosensory area, barrel field (SSp-bfd) subregion contributed a higher proportion of input neurons compared to other SSp subregions. This was followed by inputs from the MOp and SSs. The dorsal anterior cingulate area (ACAd) provided significantly stronger input than the ventral anterior cingulate area (ACAv). While inputs from the dorsal agranular insular area (AId) and the caudal agranular insular area (AIc) showed no significant difference, the ventral agranular insular area (AIv) contributed almost no input neurons. In contrast, several other cortical regions, including frontal pole (FRP), auditory areas (AUD), visual areas (VIS), orbital area (ORB), retrosplenial area (RSP), posterior parietal association areas (PTLp), perirhinal area (PERI), and ectorhinal area (ECT), provided negligible input to the SPVC. To further elucidate the connection strength between individual cortical subregions and the SPVC, we analyzed the input distribution patterns (Fig. 3D and E). Consistent with the aforementioned findings, the SSp-bfd subregion contributed the highest number of input neurons.

We next examined whether the SPVC received inputs from the spinal cord. Using the same labeling method described previously, we obtained mouse brain samples with whole spinal cords and imaged them with the fMOST system. This approach revealed that spinal cord neurons indeed input to the SPVC. Input neurons from the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar segments were presented separately (Fig. 3F). Among cervical segments, C1 contained the highest number of input neurons, primarily localized in the dorsal horn. In thoracic (T7) and lumbar (L3) segments, the number of input neurons gradually decreased. No input neurons were observed in the sacral segment.

Whole-brain and spinal cord output distribution of SPVC

To systematically map the distribution of SPVC projection fibers throughout the whole brain and spinal cord, we injected the anterograde tracer rAAV2/9-hSyn-EGFP into the SPVC (Fig. 4A and Fig. S2B). Reconstruction of the continuous high-resolution images, acquired by the fMOST system, produced a three-dimensional dataset of SPVC outputs throughout the whole brain (Fig. 4B). From a whole-brain perspective, the SPVC primarily projects to the medulla, cerebellum, pons, and midbrain, with the most distal projections reaching the thalamus and hypothalamus (Fig. 4B and Fig. S4).

Based on the distribution of projection fibers, we quantitatively analyzed the projection intensity to different regions (Fig. 4C). Due to interference from labeled neuronal dendrites and somata signals at the injection site, local output signals were excluded from analysis, and only long-range output circuits were considered. The results showed that SPVC projected to 48 brain regions, including the thalamus, hypothalamus, midbrain, pons, medulla, and cerebellum, with distinct differences in fiber distribution across these areas. The medulla and cerebellum were the primary projection regions of SPVC, with the majority of fibers distributed ipsilaterally. In contrast, rostrally-directed projections were predominantly contralateral, targeting regions such as the pons, midbrain, and thalamus. A sparse fiber distribution was also observed in the zona incerta (ZI) of the hypothalamus.

To further analyze the projection patterns, we traced continuous sections and identified two main intracerebral circuits: the SPVC–PB circuit and the SPVC–thalamic circuit (Fig. 4D). In the SPVC–PB circuit, SPVC projected to multiple ipsilateral cerebellar targets and rostrally to the ipsilateral principal sensory nucleus of the trigeminal (PSV) and PB, with collaterals via the lateral PB projecting to the hypothalamus (Fig. 4D and E). In the SPVC–thalamic circuit, two main projection routes were identified, both ultimately terminating in the submedial nucleus of the thalamus (SMT). In the first route, SPVC projected to the contralateral inferior olivary complex (IO) and facial motor nucleus (VII), then rostrally to the contralateral ventral tegmental area (VTA) and periaqueductal gray (PAG), and finally to contralateral ZI and SMT. Interestingly, after projecting to the contralateral ZI, branches also projected to the ipsilateral SMT. In the second route, SPVC projected to contralateral hypoglossal nucleus (XII) and intermediate reticular nucleus (IRN), rostrally to contralateral inferior colliculus (IC) and motor-related superior colliculus (SCm), and finally reached the contralateral VPM and SMT (Fig. 4D and E).

We next performed a detailed, subregional analysis of SPVC projections within the thalamus. Our three-dimensional data showed that SPVC axons were primarily concentrated in the lateral and ventral thalamic areas (Fig. 5A). Quantitative analysis of projection intensity of different thalamic subregions demonstrated a clear subregion-specific preference, particularly in the VPM, posterior triangular thalamic nucleus (PoT), medial geniculate nucleus, medial part (MGm), and SMT (Fig. 5B).

Using the same labeling method, we imaged whole spinal cord samples and found that SPVC projected to different spinal segments (Fig. 5C). Axon terminals were primarily observed in the ipsilateral cervical spinal cord, with dense axon terminal distribution in the C1 segment, mainly in the dorsal horn. Fewer axon terminals were observed in the thoracic (T7) and lumbar (L3) segments, and no axon terminals were found in the sacral segment (Fig. 5C). Analysis of SPVC–spinal projection patterns identified three main projection pathways (Fig. 4D). First, SPVC projected to the ipsilateral nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS) and contralateral SPVC, then caudally to the contralateral cervical, thoracic, and lumbar segments. Second, SPVC projected to the contralateral XII and contralateral SPVC, then caudally to the contralateral cervical, thoracic, and lumbar segments. Third, SPVC projected directly to the ipsilateral cervical, thoracic, and lumbar segments.

Together, these data delineated the major long-range output pathways of the SPVC, revealing distinct projection patterns to both supraspinal and spinal targets.

Whole-brain and spinal cord synaptic connectivity of SPVC

To verify the presence of synaptic connections, in the primary projection target regions of SPVC pathways, we injected a mixture of rAAV2/9-hSyn-flex-mGFP-T2A-Synaptophysin-mRuby and rAAV2/9-hSyn-SV40 NLS-Cre into the SPVC of C57BL/6J mice at a ratio of 2:1. This strategy enabled simultaneous visualization of SPVC neuronal axons (GFP) and presynaptic terminals (mRuby labeling synaptophysin) (Fig. 6A). Whole-brain and spinal cord imaging of axons and presynaptic terminals revealed that SPVC axons and presynaptic terminals projected broadly to multiple target regions throughout the brain and spinal cord (Fig. 6B and Fig. S5A), extending rostrally to the SMT and caudally to the lumbar segments of the spinal cord.

To further clarify the synaptic connectivity within the major intracerebral pathways, we performed confocal microscopy imaging of axons and presynaptic terminals in the primary projection target regions along the two main intracerebral pathways described earlier (Fig. 6C and Fig. S5B). In the SPVC–PB circuit, SPVC projected to multiple target regions in the ipsilateral cerebellum, such as the uvula (UVU) and copula pyramidis (COPY). Axons and presynaptic terminals of Purkinje cells in these areas exhibited significant varicosities, and the projections continued rostrally to densely innervated regions including the ipsilateral PSV and ipsilateral PB, where large clusters of presynaptic terminals were formed (Fig. 6B and C). In the SPVC–thalamic circuit, the ventral thalamic projection pathway showed a large accumulation of presynaptic terminals in VII and SMT, while fewer presynaptic terminals were observed in VTA and ZI. In the dorsal thalamic projection pathway, there were more presynaptic terminals in XII, IC, and VPM, but fewer in SCm (Fig. 6B and C).

We next examined whether synaptic connections were present between SPVC and the spinal cord. Imaging of axons and presynaptic terminals in the spinal cord revealed that SPVC projected as far caudally as the lumbar segment (Fig. 6B and D). Dense accumulations of axons and presynaptic terminals were observed in the ipsilateral cervical C1 segment, predominantly distributed in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. In the thoracic T7 and lumbar L3 segments, the distribution of axons and presynaptic terminals gradually decreased.

Distribution of SPVC pain-activated neuronal outputs

To investigate the downstream circuits through which SPVC contributes to orofacial pain, we first established an orofacial pain model by subcutaneously injecting formalin into the right whisker pad of C57BL/6J mice (Fig. 7A). Formalin injection elicits biphasic pain sensations: Phase I (0–10 min post-injection) is primarily mediated by nociceptive fibers such as C-fibers, while Phase II (10–60 min post-injection) is mainly driven by peripheral inflammation and central sensitization [29]. The experiment was divided into three groups: the experimental group (Formalin), the control group (Saline), and an untreated group (Control). Pain-induced wiping behavior was recorded by a camera within 1 h after the pain stimulus. Subcutaneous injection of formalin into the whisker pad significantly induced facial wiping behaviors in both Phase I (0–10 min) and Phase II (10–60 min), with data plotted at 5-minute intervals (Fig. 7B and C).

We next examined whether SPVC neurons were activated by this pain stimulus. Subsequently, we performed cfos staining on the post-experiment mice. In the central nervous system, induced expression of cfos protein largely reflects stress-induced neuronal activity and is commonly regarded as a marker of neuronal activation. Compared to the Saline and Control groups, the Formalin group showed a significant increase in the number of cfos-positive cells in the SPVC, confirming that neuronal responses in the SPVC were indeed increased during the formalin-induced pain in mice (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, we found that cfos expression in the SPVC was predominantly localized to the ipsilateral side of the pain stimulus and mainly distributed in the superficial layers.

To further investigate the distribution of SPVC pain-activated neuronal outputs, we used the Fos-CreER mice combined with an anterograde tracing viral labeling strategy (Fig. 7E). Fos-CreER mice express the recombinase CreER in Fos+ neurons, allowing for activity-dependent labeling of pain-activated neurons. First, to validate the effectiveness of 4-OHT, an anterograde tracer virus was injected into SPVC followed by formalin pain stimulation, but without 4-OHT administration. As expected, no labeled neurons were detected at the injection site (Fig. S6A). Consistent with our cfos staining results, pain-activated neurons at the injection site in the formalin group exhibited the highest number and brightest labeling of neuronal somata, and were predominantly localized to the ipsilateral superficial layers (Fig. S6A). The Formalin group samples were then embedded in resin and imaged using the fMOST system, obtaining continuous datasets at a resolution of 0.32 μm × 0.32 μm × 2 μm (Fig. S2C). We found that the SPVC pain-activated neurons exhibited a subregional projection preference in the cerebellum (Fig. 7F and Fig. S6B). Compared to non-specific neuronal types in C57BL/6J mice, SPVC pain-activated neurons did not project to the ANcr2 and PFL regions of the cerebellum, and only a few fibers were observed in the paramedian lobule (PRM) and COPY. The three-dimensional results showed that SPVC pain-activated neurons primarily project to the medulla, cerebellum, pons, midbrain, thalamus, and hypothalamus (Fig. 7G). Interestingly, through quantitative analysis, we found that pain-activated neurons projected heavily to the IC and SCm, suggesting that these may be specific output targets of SPVC neurons that convey pain information.

Discussion

Through a systematic approach combining mouse genetics, viral tracing, and the fMOST system, we systematically mapped the connectivity of the SPVC, providing a comprehensive description of its long-range input-output organization. High-resolution, three-dimensional continuous whole-head imaging revealed neuronal input to the SPVC from the TG neurons innervating the facial skin and vomeronasal glands. Continuous whole-brain and spinal cord imaging showed that the SPVC projects along distinct circuits in the brain and spinal cord, with synaptic connections present at the major target regions. Furthermore, our analysis of pain-activated neurons revealed a distinct projection feature: these neurons specifically do not project to the cerebellar ANcr2 and PFL. This high-resolution connectome provides a crucial anatomical framework for illustrating the neural mechanisms underlying orofacial sensation and pain.

In the input circuit, the nose is richly innervated by trigeminal sensory nerve endings, and future studies should aim to compare SPVC neuronal responses to nociceptive stimuli applied to these distinct peripheral territories, such as nose versus whisker pad, to determine if they are encoded by different neuronal subpopulations or exhibit unique response profiles, thereby further elucidating the functional specialization within the SPVC. The TG-SPVC circuit is known to be involved in the initial integration and transmission of orofacial nociception, thermal sensation, and partial tactile information, as well as in mediating certain important reflex activities. It is noteworthy that previous studies have shown that TG neurons innervating the orofacial masticatory muscle provide input to the spinal trigeminal nucleus, interpolar part (SPVI)/SPVC transition zone, and that TG neurons innervating the cornea provide input to both the SPVI/SPVC transition zone and the SPVC/C1 transition zone, rather than to the SPVC proper [15, 30].

Continuous whole-brain and spinal cord imaging revealed that SPVC afferents were concentrated in the cortex, hypothalamus, midbrain, pons, and medulla, as well as the spinal cord. Among these regions, the SSp, SSs, AI, paraventricular nucleus (PVH), lateral hypothalamic area (LHA), PAG, RVM, and NTS have been most frequently reported as SPVC input regions [31–33], and our data not only corroborate these findings but also extend them by providing a quantitative, whole-brain perspective. We found that the cortex was the predominant source of descending input, with cortical neurons primarily located in layer V (Fig. 3A), in agreement with previous observations [34]. Importantly, our quantitative analysis uncovered clear subregional preferences within this cortical input (Fig. 3D and E), suggesting that top-down modulation of SPVC function is highly specific and likely routed through discrete sensorimotor and limbic cortical areas. We speculate that spinal cord inputs to the SPVC may be involved in the integration of somatosensory and facial sensory information.

In the output organization, our whole-brain and spinal cord imaging findings were consistent with previous reports of SPVC projecting to XII, VII, PB, PAG, SCm, IC, VPM, and C1 [35, 36]. More importantly, our detailed tracing allowed us to parse these outputs into distinct pathways: the SPVC–parabrachial nucleus circuit and the SPVC–thalamic circuit. It is generally accepted that the SPVC-thalamic circuit conveys nociceptive information through the lateral pain system to the cortex, mediating the sensory-discriminative aspects of orofacial pain, whereas the SPVC-PB circuit is involved in emotional-affective and homeostatic responses [37]. Our structural data provided a high-resolution anatomical substrate for this functional dissociation. The SPVC-spinal cord circuit is involved in orofacial self-grooming behavior and may play an important role in sensorimotor coordination [14, 38]. The SPVC–thalamic circuit was further subdivided into dorsal and ventral routes, both ultimately projecting to the SMT. In the ventral thalamic circuit, SPVC projected to the contralateral IO and VII, continued rostrally to the contralateral VTA, and then to the contralateral ZI and SMT. Interestingly, after projecting to the contralateral ZI, branches also extended to the ipsilateral SMT. In the dorsal thalamic circuit, SPVC projected to the contralateral XII and IRN, continued rostrally to the contralateral IC and SCm, and further rostrally to the contralateral VPM and SMT. Given that the SMT has been implicated in nociception and pain-induced aversion [39, 40], we hypothesize that these two thalamic projection circuits may subserve distinct but related functions: the dorsal route, via VPM, may be more specialized for relaying sensory quality and location, while the ventral route, via SMT and its limbic-associated targets, may contribute more to the affective dimension. This hypothesis is further supported by our observation that SPVC projections exhibit a clear subregional preference within the thalamus, with dense innervation of VPM, PoT, MGm, and SMT (Fig. 5A and B).

To gain insight into the circuits specifically activated by pain, we analyzed the projections of SPVC neurons activated by formalin-induced orofacial pain. Interestingly, while the overall projection pattern of these pain-activated neurons was similar to that of the general SPVC population, a distinct feature emerged in their cerebellar projections. Notably, these neurons did not project to the ANcr2 and PFL, and showed sparse projections to PRM and COPY (Fig. 7F and Fig. S6B). This observation suggests a functional segregation within the SPVC-cerebellar pathway. We hypothesize that superficial SPVC neurons project preferentially to PRM and COPY, whereas deeper neurons target subregions such as ANcr2 and PFL. The cerebellum is involved in sensorimotor coordination, emotional responses, and reward processing [41, 42]. Based on the distinct spatial distribution of neurons within the SPVC, we speculate that the different organizational patterns of SPVC inputs and outputs may originate from distinct neuronal subpopulations within the SPVC. Future research should aim to distinguish different neuronal types within the SPVC and employ single-neuron techniques to dissect co-projection patterns and the relationship between dendritic complexity and target preference, thereby providing circuit-level insights that eliminate the potential confound of fibers of passage inherent in population-level tracing.

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive, high-resolution map of the SPVC long-range connectome. We have delineated parallel and segregated ascending and descending pathways, and uncovered a unique projection signature for pain-activated neurons within the cerebellum. These findings provide a new and detailed neuroanatomical basis for understanding the neural circuits underlying orofacial pain, its associated negative emotions, and sensorimotor regulation. This work establishes a circuit-level theoretical foundation for the future development of more selective and effective therapeutic strategies for orofacial pain.

Conclusions

In conclusion, high-resolution 3D imaging reveals the SPVC long-range connectome across head, brain, and spinal cord, Together, these findings establish a comprehensive connectivity framework for understanding the neural mechanisms underlying orofacial pain and provide a foundation for advances in basic sensory research and disease prevention and treatment.
