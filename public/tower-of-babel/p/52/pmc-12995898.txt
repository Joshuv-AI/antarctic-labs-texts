Stress fiber traction force reshapes chromatin accessibility and YAP binding to direct diverse transcriptional programs in mesenchymal stem cells
Authors: Jiacheng Lei, Yicen Long, Xiaojing Liu, Zhiqin Chu, Qiang Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStress fiber–generated traction forces critically regulate mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) behavior, yet how mechanical cues are integrated across transcriptional programs remains unclear. Here, we attenuated actomyosin contractility in human MSCs and performed parallel Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (ATAC-seq), YAP-targeted Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation sequencing (CUT&Tag) and RNA-seq profiling. We show that reduced stress fiber traction force selectively reorganizes chromatin accessibility into coherent functional modules, resulting in diverse transcriptional programs. The mechanosensitive co-activator YAP functions as a parallel force-responsive regulatory layer coordinating with chromatin accessibility changes. Integration of chromatin accessibility, YAP occupancy, and transcriptomic profiles reveals pathway-specific regulatory responses, identifying focal adhesion and PI3K-Akt signaling as central mechanosensitive pathways coordinated across layers. Together, these findings establish a modular framework for force-dependent gene regulation, demonstrating how mechanical signals are integrated across epigenomic and transcriptional networks to shape MSC transcriptional programs.Keywords: Mechanotransduction, Stress fiber traction force, Chromatin accessibility, YAP, Transcriptional regulation

Introduction

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are highly sensitive to mechanical cues from their microenvironment, which critically regulate their lineage commitment, self-renewal capacity, and regenerative potential.1,2 Extensive studies have demonstrated that diverse mechanical cues—including extracellular matrix stiffness,3,4 topographical features,5,6 and cyclic stretching7,8—regulate MSC differentiation,9,10 metabolism,11,12 immunomodulation,13,14 and therapeutic efficacy.15,16 Stress fiber–generated traction force senses these extracellular cues and transmit them through integrin-mediated adhesions and cytoskeletal networks, converting physical forces into intracellular regulatory signals.17,18 Acting as a central integrator of extracellular mechanics and intracellular signaling, stress fiber traction force links the mechanical microenvironment to downstream gene regulatory processes, thereby shaping MSC identity and functional states.

Although recent studies highlight the importance of mechanical regulation in MSC biology, most work has concentrated on specific phenotypic outcomes or individual mechanosensitive pathways. Consequently, a comprehensive view of how intracellular traction forces orchestrate transcriptional programs across diverse functional networks in MSCs is still missing.

Emerging evidence suggests that mechanical cues influence MSCs fate through both chromatin-based mechanisms and mechanosensitive transcriptional regulators.19,20 Mechanical forces can remodel chromatin accessibility,21,22 reshape epigenetic landscapes,23,24 and reorganize nuclear architecture,25,26 while transcriptional regulators such as YAP/TAZ link traction force to downstream gene expression.27 However, most existing studies have examined these mechanisms in isolation, focusing on chromatin accessibility, transcription factor activity, or transcriptional outcomes. Consequently, how these regulatory layers are coordinated under defined mechanical conditions in MSCs remains unclear.

In this study, we developed an integrative strategy to investigate how intracellular traction force regulates transcription in human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hASCs). By using blebbistatin to pharmacologically reduce actomyosin contractility, we established a controlled model of decreased intracellular traction force. We then combined ATAC-seq, YAP-targeted CUT&Tag, and RNA-seq to profile changes in chromatin accessibility, YAP genomic binding, and gene expression. This multi-layer approach allowed us to examine mechanical regulation across chromatin, transcription factor occupancy, and transcriptional output, revealing coordinated responses in metabolic, immunomodulatory, and epigenetic gene programs. Overall, our results provide a comprehensive view of how intracellular traction forces shape gene regulatory landscapes in MSCs.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hASCs) were cultured in α-MEM (Gibco) supplemented with 8% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were passaged using trypsin-EDTA, counted with a hemocytometer, and seeded at densities of 5,000 cells per well in 24-well plates or 1.5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates. Cells were allowed to adhere overnight under standard culture conditions prior to subsequent treatments.

Drug treatment for intracellular traction force attenuation

To attenuate intracellular traction force, hASCs were treated with blebbistatin (MedChemExpress), a selective myosin II ATPase inhibitor. Blebbistatin stock solutions were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), aliquoted, and stored in the dark to prevent photoinactivation. Working solutions were freshly prepared by diluting the stock in culture medium immediately before use. Cells were treated with blebbistatin at a final concentration of 20 μM for 48 h unless otherwise specified. Based on published dose-response analyses, 20 μM blebbistatin was selected as it lies within the effective inhibitory range (<50 μM) and corresponds to near-maximal suppression of myosin II activity.28 Control cells received equivalent volumes of DMSO.

Following overnight adhesion, blebbistatin was added directly to the culture medium, and cells were maintained under standard culture conditions for subsequent analyses. All treatments were performed in parallel using matched biological replicates.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining of cultured cells

Cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with cold PBS. Permeabilization was performed with 0.25 % (v/v) Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with PBS. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubation of the samples with 1 % (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBST overnight at 4 °C. After a brief rinse with PBST, samples were incubated with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: phospho-myosin light chain 2 (Thr18/Ser19) (Cell Signaling Technology, #95777; 1:400), Lamin A/C (Cell Signaling Technology, #4777; 1:400), and YAP (Cell Signaling Technology, #4912; 1:500). After washing twice with PBST and three times with PBS, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11011, 1:400) or Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-32728, 1:400) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Merck), and F-actin was labeled using Alexa Fluor 488-phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A12379) for 60 min at room temperature. Samples were washed twice with PBST and three times with PBS prior to imaging. Immunofluorescence images were captured using a Leica DMi8 fluorescence microscope and a Leica STELLARIS 5 laser scanning confocal microscope. Quantitative image analysis, including measurement of fluorescence intensity and spreading area, was performed using Fiji (ImageJ).

Chromatin condensation parameter (CCP) analysis

Cells were stained with DAPI to visualize nuclear DNA and imaged using a Leica confocal microscope equipped with a × 63 objective. Chromatin condensation was quantified by calculating the chromatin condensation parameter (CCP), defined as the edge density within individual nuclei. Briefly, DAPI-stained nuclear images were processed using a gradient-based Sobel edge detection algorithm in MATLAB R2016a to extract intensity gradients corresponding to chromatin boundaries. The density of detected edges was used as a quantitative proxy for chromatin compaction, with higher CCP values indicating increased chromatin condensation.29,30

Actin-FRET - based stress fiber traction force measurement

The cells were stably transfected with plasmid Actin-cpstFRET-Actin (Addgene plasmid # 80643) and expressed β-actin-CFP/YFP-β-actin fusion protein at sufficiently high levels for easy visualization. Cell suspensions were obtained using trypsin (Gibco) digestion and seeded on the material surface at a suitable density. cells were imaged using a confocal microscope (Leica). FRET results (530 nm) were captured to excite β-actin-CFP (corrected by YFP with a 488 nm laser). At least 20 cells were quantified for each condition. The cell images used for all values shown in the figure were acquired under the same imaging condition.

Actin-cpstFRET-Actin was obtained as a gift from Fred Sachs (Addgene plasmid #80643), which encodes a β-actin - CFP/YFP - β-actin tension sensor.

Plasmids were transfected into human adipose-derived stem cells (hASCs) using X-tremeGENE HP DNA transfection reagent (Roche) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection, and stable Actin-cpstFRET-Actin expressing cells were established. After trypsinization, cells were reseeded at an appropriate density and allowed to adhere prior to imaging.

FRET imaging was performed using a Leica confocal microscope. CFP was excited at 405 nm, and the resulting YFP emission (∼530 nm) was collected as the FRET signal. YFP fluorescence was also directly excited at 488 nm, and the resulting emission (∼530 nm) was used to normalize for differences in sensor expression between cells. The final FRET/YFP ratio was calculated for each cell as the FRET signal (405 nm excitation) divided by the directly excited YFP signal (488 nm excitation), which inversely correlates with actin cytoskeletal traction force, as previously described.31

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of hASCs

RNA-seq and downstream analyses were performed by Duolaimi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Total RNA was isolated and purified using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher, cat.15596018) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Poly(A) mRNA was captured by two rounds of purification using oligo(dT) magnetic beads (Dynabeads Oligo(dT), Thermo Fisher, cat.25-61005, USA). The captured mRNA was fragmented using a magnesium ion-based RNA fragmentation module (NEBNext Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module, NEB, cat.E6150S, USA) at 94 °C for 5-7 min.

First-strand cDNA was synthesized using SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, cat.1896649, USA). RNA-DNA hybrids were then converted to double-stranded cDNA using E. coli DNA polymerase I (NEB, cat.M0209, USA) and RNase H (NEB, cat.M0297, USA). dUTP was incorporated during second-strand synthesis (Thermo Fisher, cat.R0133, USA) to maintain strand specificity. The resulting cDNA ends were blunted, A-tailed, and ligated to T-tailed sequencing adapters, followed by size selection and purification using magnetic beads. Second-strand digestion was performed with USER enzyme (NEB, cat.M0280, USA).

Strand-specific libraries (∼300 bp ± 50 bp) were amplified by PCR (initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 8 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 15 s, 72 °C for 30 s; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min). Paired-end sequencing (PE150) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 according to standard protocols.

Assay for Transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq)

ATAC-seq and downstream analyses were performed by Novogene Technology Co., Ltd. Briefly, nuclei were isolated from cells and incubated in Tn5 transposase reaction mixture for 1 h at room temperature. Equimolar adapters 1 and 2 were then added, and the transposed DNA fragments were amplified by PCR. Libraries were purified using AMPure beads, and library quality and concentration were assessed using Qubit.

Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) sequencing

CUT&Tag assays were performed following standard protocols. Briefly, 70,000 cells per sample were collected, washed once with 500 μL washing buffer, and bound to ConA beads for 10 min at 25 °C. Cells were then incubated with 1 μg YAP antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #14074) overnight at 4 °C. For the negative control group, rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, #2729) was used instead of the primary antibody. All experimental groups and controls were prepared in two biological replicates.

After thorough washing, anti-mouse IgG was added and incubated for 1 h at 25 °C. Cells were washed three times with DIG wash buffer and incubated with 0.04 μM pA/G-Tn5 for 2 h at 25 °C. Subsequently, cells were washed three times with DIG 300 buffer, resuspended in labeling buffer, and incubated for 2 h at 25 °C. Labeling was terminated by addition of Proteinase K, LB buffer, and DNA extraction beads, followed by incubation at 55 °C for 10 min. Samples were placed on magnets, and unbound liquid was removed. Cells were rinsed twice with 80% ethanol, and DNA was eluted in nuclease-free water.

Libraries were constructed using the TD904 Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina and TD202 TruePrep Index Kit V2 (Vazyme Biotech) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Libraries were sequenced by Berry Genomics Co., Ltd.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Experiments included at least two biological replicates (n ≥ 2). Differences between the two experimental groups were analyzed using a two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated prior to the t-test, and appropriate transformations or non-parametric alternatives were applied if necessary.

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. In figures, statistical significance is indicated using asterisks: p < 0.05 (∗), p < 0.01 (∗∗), p < 0.001 (∗∗∗), and p < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗).

Results

Stress fiber traction force attenuation alters nuclear lamina structure and chromatin organization

To establish a controllable intracellular traction force attenuation model in human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hASCs), we inhibited actomyosin contractility using blebbistatin, a myosin II inhibitor (Fig. S1).28,32 Blebbistatin-treated cells were defined as the Force(−) group, whereas DMSO-treated cells served as control (Ctrl) (Fig. 1a). As expected, Force(−) cells exhibited a marked reduction in phosphorylated myosin II levels compared with Ctrl cells, confirming effective suppression of actomyosin activity (Fig. 1b).

(b) Immunofluorescence (n = 50 cells; three technical replicates) of P-myosin IIa (red) in Ctrl group and Force(−) group. (c) Schematic illustration, representative images, and quantification of cellular traction forces measured using FRET-based actin tension probes. Higher FRET/YFP ratio indicates reduced cellular traction force (n = 20 cells; two technical replicates). (d) Immunofluorescence of Lamin A/C (cyan) to visualize nuclear lamina structure; fluorescence intensity was quantified (n = 50 cells; three technical replicates), and lamina wrinkles were counted (n = 30 cells; three technical replicates) to assess structural changes in the nuclear lamina. (e) DAPI-stained nuclei (blue) and F-actin (green); chromatin condensation parameter (CCP) calculated as nuclear edge density (edge%) using Sobel algorithm (n = 30 cells; three technical replicates). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Scale bar, 100 μm for b, 50 μm for c, 5 μm for d and 10 μm for e. Two-sided Student's t-test was used.

To further validate stress fiber traction force attenuation, we quantified cytoskeletal traction force using an actin-based FRET tension sensor.31 Blebbistatin treatment resulted in a significant increase in the FRET/YFP ratio, indicating decreased stress fiber tension in Force(−) cells (Fig. 1c). Together, these results confirm successful establishment of an intracellular traction force attenuation model.

Using this validated model, we next examined nuclear structural responses to reduced stress fiber traction force. As cytoskeletal forces are transmitted to the nucleus through the LINC complex to regulate nuclear lamina mechanics, we first analyzed Lamin A/C organization.33,34 Immunofluorescence revealed decreased Lamin A/C intensity accompanied by increased lamina wrinkling in Force(−) cells relative to controls (Fig. 1d), indicating altered nuclear lamina structure under attenuated intracellular traction force.

Given the established coupling between nuclear lamina mechanics and chromatin organization,35,36 we further assessed chromatin structural changes. Quantification of chromatin condensation parameters (CCP),29,30 calculated as nuclear edge density from DAPI-stained nuclei revealed elevated nuclear edge density in Force(−) cells, suggesting increased chromatin condensation following cytoskeletal force attenuation (Fig. 1e). Together, these findings indicate that reduced cytoskeletal traction force is associated with coordinated remodeling of nuclear lamina structure and chromatin organization, motivating subsequent genome-wide analyses to determine how intracellular mechanical attenuation impacts chromatin accessibility and transcriptional regulation.

Stress fiber traction force reshapes chromatin accessibility landscapes

Given that chromatin accessibility represents a fundamental prerequisite for transcriptional activation and is highly sensitive to nuclear mechanics,19,20 we next examined whether attenuation of cytoskeletal traction force induces genome-wide changes in chromatin accessibility. ATAC-seq was performed to profile open chromatin regions based on preferential Tn5 transposase insertion37 (Fig. 2a). Analysis of accessibility around transcription start sites (TSS ± 3 kb) revealed a global reduction in accessible chromatin peaks in Force(−) cells compared with Ctrl cells (Fig. 2b), indicating widespread chromatin compaction following intracellular traction force attenuation.

(b) Heatmaps of genome-wide ATAC-seq peaks at transcription start site (TSS) proximal regions (TSS ± 3 kb) in Ctrl group and Force(−) group. To functionally interpret these accessibility changes, enriched Gene Ontology (GO) pathways were organized into six biologically relevant clusters reflecting key aspects of hASC physiology, including mechanosensing and cytoskeletal organization, metabolic homeostasis, stress and adaptive responses, cell fate regulation, stemness and differentiation potential, and microenvironmental signaling (Fig. 2c). Notably, the mechanosensing cluster encompassed pathways related to cytoskeletal organization, cell migration, and junctional dynamics, whereas metabolic and stress-response clusters highlighted force-associated regulation of intracellular homeostasis. In parallel, pathways linked to developmental programs and lineage commitment were also prominently represented, suggesting that cytoskeletal force attenuation broadly impacts both functional and identity-related regulatory networks (Fig. 2d).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that reduced intracellular traction force drives extensive remodeling of chromatin accessibility across diverse biological programs, raising the question of how mechanical cues are coupled to specific transcriptional regulatory machinery.

Stress fiber traction force regulates YAP nuclear localization and genomic occupancy

To explore whether these force-sensitive chromatin regions are actively engaged by mechanosensitive transcriptional regulators, we focused on Yes-associated protein (YAP), a well-established mechanosensitive transcriptional co-activator whose activity is governed by actomyosin-driven traction force.27 A schematic overview illustrates YAP localization under Ctrl and Force(−) conditions together with the CUT&Tag workflow used to profile genome-wide YAP chromatin occupancy38 (Fig. 3a). Consistent with reduced traction force, immunofluorescence analysis revealed decreased nuclear YAP localization in Force(−) cells compared with Ctrl cells (Fig. 3b). CUT&Tag profiling further demonstrated a marked reduction in YAP binding at TSS proximal regions in the Force(−) group (Fig. 3c).

Green indicates F-actin; and blue indicates DAPI. (c) Heatmaps of genome-wide YAP CUT&Tag peaks at transcription start site (TSS) proximal regions (±3 kb) in the Ctrl and Force (−) groups. To assess the functional consequences of altered YAP chromatin engagement, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed on YAP-bound regions. Enriched pathways were mapped onto the six functional clusters defined by ATAC-seq to enable direct comparison between chromatin accessibility and YAP occupancy (Fig. 3d). Notably, YAP-associated pathways broadly overlapped with accessibility-defined clusters, spanning mechanosensing and cytoskeletal organization, metabolic homeostasis, stress and immune responses, cell fate regulation, stemness and differentiation programs, and microenvironmental signaling (Fig. 3e). Representative pathways within these clusters highlighted YAP involvement in cytoskeletal dynamics, nucleotide and energy metabolism, inflammatory signaling, proliferation control, and developmental processes, indicating coordinated regulation of diverse cellular programs by YAP under force-modulated conditions.

Together, these results indicate that cytoskeletal force attenuation markedly alters YAP nuclear localization and chromatin engagement across multiple functional clusters. The observed changes in chromatin accessibility and transcriptional regulator occupancy provide a potential mechanistic link between intracellular traction force and gene regulatory programs; however, whether these alterations are fully realized at the transcriptional level remains to be determined.

Stress fiber traction force attenuation rewires transcriptional programs across functional clusters

To examine whether the force-dependent changes in chromatin accessibility and YAP occupancy are reflected at the transcriptional level, we performed RNA sequencing of Ctrl and Force(−) cells. Heatmap visualization revealed widespread transcriptional alterations at the genome-wide level under cytoskeletal force attenuation (Fig. S2). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was then applied to map the differentially expressed genes onto the six functional clusters previously defined by ATAC-seq, allowing comparison of transcriptional responses with chromatin and YAP occupancy changes.

GSEA revealed coordinated yet cluster-specific transcriptional responses in Force(−) cells relative to Ctrl cells. In the mechanical sensing and cytoskeletal organization cluster, pathways related to actin filament bundle assembly, actin cytoskeleton organization, and actin filament binding were negatively enriched, consistent with reduced cytoskeletal traction force (Fig. 4a). The metabolic and intracellular homeostasis cluster showed mixed enrichment, with negative enrichment of amino acid biosynthesis pathways and positive enrichment of steroid metabolic and NADP binding pathways (Fig. 4b). The stress, immune, and adaptive responses cluster exhibited divergent trends, including negative enrichment of wound healing-related pathways and positive enrichment of immune response-related pathways (Fig. 4c). Pathways associated with cell fate and survival decision networks, such as cell cycle regulation and kinetochore organization, were uniformly negatively enriched (Fig. 4d). Similarly, the stemness maintenance and lineage differentiation potential cluster displayed negative enrichment for pathways involved in muscle cell development, glial differentiation, and angiogenesis (Fig. 4e). Finally, the microenvironmental interaction and signal integration cluster exhibited negative enrichment of pathways regulating gene expression, extracellular vesicle-mediated processes, and chromatin remodeling (Fig. 4f).

For each functional cluster, three representative GSEA plots are shown, with corresponding p values and normalized enrichment scores (NES) indicated.

Collectively, these transcriptional changes broadly align with the force-dependent alterations in chromatin accessibility and YAP occupancy, motivating further analyses to identify upstream regulatory pathways that coordinate activity across multiple functional clusters.

Synergistic regulation of signaling pathways by altered chromatin accessibility and YAP targeting

To identify upstream regulatory pathways that may coordinately control multiple functional modules under reduced cytoskeletal force, we systematically selected KEGG pathways that were highly significant in both ATAC-seq and YAP CUT&Tag datasets, excluding disease-associated pathways (Fig. 5a). This integrative approach allowed us to focus on pathways consistently and robustly marked at both the chromatin accessibility and transcription factor binding levels.

The x-axis represents −log10(p value) and bubble color intensity denotes −log10(classic Fisher) values. (b) Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of RNA-seq for KEGG pathways intersected in (a) (p value < 0.05), highlighting transcriptional changes in the focal adhesion and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways. (c) Volcano plot of RNA-seq differentially expressed genes enriched in representative KEGG pathways shared between ATAC-seq and CUT&Tag analyses, with the x-axis representing log2(fold change) and the y-axis representing −log10(p value).

To assess whether these shared pathways were accompanied by transcriptional changes, we intersected RNA-seq - enriched KEGG pathways with the overlapping ATAC - CUT&Tag pathways. This analysis highlighted focal adhesion and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways as significantly downregulated at the transcriptional level in Force(−) cells relative to controls (Fig. 5b). Differential expression of representative genes within additional pathways, including Wnt, JAK-STAT, and TNF signaling, was visualized using volcano plots, showing both up- and downregulated genes in Force(−) cells (Fig. 5c and Fig. S3). Expression changes for genes associated with the remaining shared pathways are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Taken together, the combined analysis of ATAC-seq, YAP CUT&Tag, and RNA-seq identifies regulatory pathways that are consistently altered when cytoskeletal force is reduced, pointing to potential upstream mechanisms orchestrating force-dependent transcriptional responses.

Discussion

Mechanical forces critically shape cytoskeletal organization, nuclear lamina mechanics, and gene regulation, thereby controlling stem cell behavior. In this study, we investigated how attenuation of cytoskeletal traction force impacts chromatin organization and transcriptional programs in human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hASCs). By integrating ATAC-seq, YAP CUT&Tag, and RNA-seq data, we show that reduced intracellular traction force induces coordinated and selective regulatory changes, forming a structured, force-dependent landscape.

At the mechanical level, inhibition of actomyosin contractility decreased cytoskeletal traction force and altered nuclear lamina structure, evidenced by reduced Lamin A/C intensity and increased lamina wrinkling. These findings support the concept that the nucleus acts as a mechanosensitive organelle, with stress fiber traction force transmitted through the LINC complex to modulate nuclear lamina mechanics.39 Such structural changes provide a physical context for downstream chromatin and transcriptional regulation.

Although blebbistatin efficiently suppresses myosin II-dependent contractility, it may induce broader cytoskeletal remodeling and potential off-target effects, particularly under prolonged inhibition, introducing secondary biochemical changes beyond pure force attenuation.40,41 In parallel, we assessed nuclear mechanical state indirectly through Lamin A/C organization, which correlates with nuclear membrane tension but does not substitute for direct mechanical measurements.42,43 Future studies employing more precise genetic or molecular perturbations of intracellular force-generating machinery, such as targeted knockdown of myosin light chain or other key contractility regulators, together with quantitative approaches such as atomic force microscopy or nano-indentation will help more precisely define traction force-dependent regulation of nuclear mechanics and chromatin accessibility.

Reducing cytoskeletal force led to selective changes in chromatin accessibility. ATAC-seq revealed clustered alterations corresponding to functional modules, including cytoskeletal regulation, metabolism, stress responses, cell fate, stemness, and microenvironmental signaling. Similar force-dependent chromatin remodeling has been observed in fibroblasts,44,45 epithelial,46 and endothelial cells,47,48 where cytoskeletal tension or substrate stiffness reshapes enhancer accessibility.49,50 Our findings extend this principle to hASCs and further suggest that mechanical cues target genomic regions linked to specific biological processes, channeling intracellular traction forces into context-dependent regulatory networks.

Mechanosensitive transcriptional regulators translate these chromatin changes into gene regulatory programs. YAP, a co-activator whose nuclear localization depends on cytoskeletal traction force,27 showed reduced nuclear accumulation and diminished chromatin engagement under low-force conditions. YAP-associated pathways largely overlapped with ATAC-defined chromatin modules, suggesting preferential engagement of mechanically sensitive regions. Notably, although chromatin accessibility and YAP occupancy changed in a coordinated manner, their causal relationship remains unresolved: accessible chromatin may permit YAP binding,51,52 whereas YAP itself may contribute to chromatin remodeling.53,54 Time-resolved perturbation or live-cell imaging approaches will be required to clarify their dynamic and reciprocal interplay.

Analysis of functional pathways indicated that responses occurred in a modular and directionally coherent manner. Pathways related to the cytoskeleton, cell cycle, and lineage commitment were consistently suppressed, while metabolic and stress-response pathways exhibited more variable patterns. These findings imply that YAP not only senses changes in chromatin accessibility but also plays an active role in converting stress fiber traction forces into specific transcriptional programs.

Although chromatin accessibility and YAP occupancy define regulatory potential, transcriptional output is only partially aligned. RNA-seq revealed that force-sensitive programs such as cytoskeletal organization and cell cycle control were downregulated as predicted, whereas metabolic and stress pathways displayed mixed responses. This suggests that chromatin-level changes establish a permissive or restrictive landscape that may not be fully realized at the transcriptional level, emphasizing the importance of multi-layer integration. Together, these findings indicate that stress fiber traction force directs a diverse spectrum of transcriptional programs spanning cytoskeletal regulation, metabolism, immune responses, cell fate decisions, and microenvironmental signaling.

Across datasets, focal adhesion and PI3K-Akt signaling emerged as robustly regulated pathways, exhibiting coordinated alterations in chromatin accessibility, YAP chromatin engagement and transcriptional output. Notably, ATAC-seq and YAP CUT&Tag represent parallel and cooperative upstream regulatory layers, whereas RNA-seq reflects downstream transcriptional outcomes. These multi-layer consistencies indicate that concurrent remodeling of chromatin accessibility and YAP binding is required to effectively propagate mechanical signals to gene expression, highlighting focal adhesion and PI3K-Akt signaling as pathways tightly coupled to intracellular mechanical states and candidate upstream regulators of force-dependent MSC programs. Previous studies have provided evidence that focal adhesion55,56 and PI3K-Akt signaling57,58 participate in mechanotransduction, suggesting their potential roles as mechanosensitive regulatory nodes. Our multi-layer analysis extends these observations by demonstrating coordinated regulation of these pathways across chromatin accessibility, transcription factor occupancy, and transcriptional output.

In summary, cytoskeletal traction forces influence nuclear lamina mechanics, chromatin accessibility, and transcription factor activity to direct selective transcriptional programs in MSCs. These results offer a systems-level framework for understanding how mechanical cues integrate across multiple regulatory layers to shape mechanically defined cellular states. Given that mechanical dysregulation characterizes pathological contexts such as fibrosis, aging, and tumor-associated stromal remodeling16—conditions in which mesenchymal stem cell function and epigenomic states are frequently altered—defining traction force as an upstream regulator of chromatin accessibility and YAP-dependent transcription offers mechanistic insight into how aberrant mechanical microenvironments may reshape stem cell behavior. This mechanogenomic coupling may ultimately inform strategies to modulate stem cell fate in regenerative medicine and to target mechanically altered stromal niches in disease.

Conclusions

This study shows that reducing stress fiber traction force in hASCs triggers coordinated changes across nuclear lamina mechanics, chromatin accessibility, and transcription factor activity. Notably, focal adhesion and PI3K-Akt pathways display consistent changes across chromatin structure, transcription factor binding, and gene expression, highlighting their tight linkage to intracellular mechanical states. By integrating data across multiple regulatory layers, our findings establish cytoskeletal traction force as an upstream regulator of chromatin-mediated gene control and provide a framework for understanding how mechanical signals guide selective transcriptional programs in stem cells. These results enhance our understanding of mechanotransduction in MSCs and may inform approaches to engineer force-responsive cellular behaviors.
