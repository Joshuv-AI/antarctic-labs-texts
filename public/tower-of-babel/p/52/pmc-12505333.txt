Twenty-Year Clinical Experience of the Korea Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry
Authors: Doo Sun Sim, Myung Ho Jeong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Author's summaryDespite significant global advances in acute myocardial infarction (AMI) care, unmet needs remain in understanding region-specific epidemiology, risk profiles, and treatment responses in East Asian populations. The Korea Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry (KAMIR), over 2 decades, has addressed these gaps by providing large-scale real-world evidence. Key findings include unique risk factor patterns, validated risk prediction tools, optimized reperfusion and pharmacotherapy strategies, and outcome improvements. These insights have informed Korean and Asian guidelines, highlighting the scientific value of tailored evidence and the clinical impact of registry-driven continuous quality improvement in AMI management.

INTRODUCTION

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. The Korea Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry (KAMIR), launched in November 2005, was the first nationwide, prospective, multicenter registry of patients with AMI in Korea.1) KAMIR has been conducted in six phases over the past 2 decades and remains an ongoing, continuously updated cohort. By October 2018, more than 70,000 patients had been enrolled,1) and by mid-2025 the cumulative enrollment reached approximately 87,000 (Figure 1). This extensive real-world registry was established to document patient demographics, risk factors, treatments, and outcomes, thereby identifying gaps in care and opportunities to improve AMI management in Korea. Importantly, KAMIR provides insights into how Korean patients with AMI differ from Western cohorts in terms of risk profiles, disease presentations, and therapeutic responses. KAMIR’s impact is further evidenced by its prolific research output, with more than 230 publications arising from the registry by 2018,1) now totaling 468 papers (428 journals indexed in the Science Citation Index) as of early August 2025. This substantial body of work highlights the importance of region-specific data in refining global cardiovascular practice and has disseminated KAMIR’s findings to inform clinical guidelines2)3)4) and quality-improvement initiatives.

In this state-of-the-art review, we synthesized 2 decades of clinical experience from KAMIR. We detailed KAMIR contributions across epidemiology, risk-factor evaluation, risk stratification, acute interventional management, and medical therapy of AMI. The accumulated evidence demonstrates that large-scale registry data, such as KAMIR, are essential for understanding AMI in specific populations and for optimizing care beyond what can be learned from international trials alone.

EPIDEMIOLOGY

Acute myocardial infarction trends in Korea

KAMIR has enabled a detailed examination of AMI incidence and subtype distributions over time. One striking trend is the declining proportion of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) accompanied by a concomitant rise in non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI).1)5)6) In 2005, approximately 64% of AMI presentations in KAMIR were STEMI, but by 2018 STEMI comprised only about 48%.1) This shift is attributed in part to the widespread adoption of high-sensitivity cardiac troponin assays, which increased NSTEMI detection that previously might have been classified as unstable angina or missed.7) The STEMI-to-NSTEMI ratio in KAMIR inverted around 2012 (Figure 2). By the late 2010s, NSTEMI slightly exceeded STEMI, aligning Korea with patterns observed in North America and Europe.1)8) During the 2005–2018 observation period of KAMIR, clinical outcomes improved substantially, with progressive declines in in-hospital mortality and 1-year major adverse cardiac events (MACE); notably, 1-year all-cause mortality decreased by approximately 39% in relative terms (Figure 3).1) These improvements paralleled the near-universal adoption of primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), the expansion of PCI-capable regional networks, and greater adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), reflecting system-level advances in the timeliness and quality of care. In complementary nationwide analyses, shorter door-to-balloon times were independently associated with lower long-term mortality, reinforcing that process improvements translated into meaningful survival benefits at the population level.9)

Patient demographics

The mean age of patients with AMI in Korea increased steadily from approximately 63 years in 2005 to about 65 years in 2018.1) This pattern reflects population aging and improved survival among individuals with ischemic heart disease, resulting in more patients living long enough to present with AMI at older ages. Notably, KAMIR data revealed an age-related survival gap: octogenarians with AMI had a 1-year mortality of approximately 22%, compared with about 6.5% among patients aged <80 years.10) Nonetheless, studies have shown that, when treated aggressively, very elderly patients can derive substantial benefit from reperfusion therapy.11)12)13) For example, patients with AMI aged ≥75 years with cardiogenic shock experienced survival improvements with PCI comparable to those of younger counterparts,11) and even nonagenarians have been managed with PCI with acceptable outcomes, although absolute risks remain higher.12)

In terms of sex distribution, men have consistently outnumbered women in KAMIR,1)14) with the male proportion increasing from approximately 67% in 2005 to about 78% in 2018.1) Early KAMIR analyses reported that women experienced slightly worse short-term outcomes, including lower PCI success rates in NSTEMI and higher 30-day complication rates,14)15) whereas subsequent multivariable analyses demonstrated that female sex was not an independent predictor of higher mortality after AMI.16)17) The initially observed differences were largely explained by women being older at presentation and having a higher burden of comorbidities or hemodynamic instability. After adjustment for these factors, female sex was no longer associated with higher in-hospital mortality.16)18)

RISK FACTORS

Assessment of patient-level risk factors has been a central aim of KAMIR. The registry’s large-scale, prospective data collection enables comprehensive evaluation of both conventional cardiovascular risk factors—hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking, and dyslipidemia—as well as clinical risk markers including admission glucose, blood pressure, and renal function. KAMIR findings generally corroborate established associations but also provide several important observations in the Korean context. Temporal analyses from 2005 to 2018 demonstrated substantial increases in the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, accompanied by a notable decline in current smoking (Figure 4), likely reflecting successful nationwide public health initiatives. The cardiovascular risk profile of Korean patients with AMI evolved markedly over the KAMIR study period, reflecting societal changes in diet, lifestyle, and healthcare access. In KAMIR, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and renal dysfunction consistently emerged as strong predictors of adverse outcomes, whereas dyslipidemia showed distinct patterns compared with Western cohorts.1)36) Notably, a substantial proportion of patients presented without any identifiable risk factors, highlighting the importance of population-wide preventive strategies beyond traditional high-risk groups.35)

RISK ASSESSMENT

Risk stratification of patients with AMI is crucial for guiding management intensity and follow-up. Early analyses led to the development of a dedicated risk score specific to the Korean AMI population, reflecting regional epidemiologic and clinical characteristics. More recently, KAMIR has advanced the field by applying machine learning and artificial intelligence (AI) approaches to enhance the precision of outcome prediction. This section summarizes key KAMIR findings in risk assessment, ranging from conventional clinical risk scores to emerging AI-driven prognostic models.

Risk stratification using risk scores

One of the landmark contributions of KAMIR has been the development of risk scores tailored to Korean patients with AMI. The KAMIR risk score, derived from data on 5,458 patients, incorporated age, Killip class, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), no in-hospital PCI, admission glucose and serum creatinine,37) demonstrating superior calibration and discrimination for long-term mortality compared with widely used Western models such as thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) and Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) risk scores.

In addition, a supplementary VAMIR score was developed to predict ventricular tachyarrhythmias in patients with AMI and preserved ejection fraction (EF).38) This model included clinical variables such as Killip class, STEMI diagnosis, conduction abnormalities, smoking, and incremental EF reductions, and showed good discriminatory ability for arrhythmic risk.

Beyond KAMIR-derived tools, the registry has served as a valuable platform for validating and extending the application of established risk scores. For example, the CHA2DS2-VASc score, originally designed to estimate stroke risk in atrial fibrillation (AF), was shown in KAMIR analyses to predict both in-hospital mortality39)40) and 24-month adverse cardiac events39) after AMI, even in the absence of AF. Similarly, the GRACE score41) for ischemic risk and the ACUITY–HORIZONS score42) for bleeding risk were used in combination to stratify patients after PCI, providing a framework for individualized tailoring of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) duration.43)

Building on these insights, investigators proposed the KAMIR-DAPT score, specifically calibrated to East Asian clinical characteristics.44) This score, derived from 10,687 patients with AMI, integrated ischemic and bleeding risks to guide both the choice of P2Y12 inhibitor and the duration of therapy. It identifies which East Asian patients derive net ischemic benefit from potent P2Y12 inhibitors such as prasugrel or ticagrelor compared to those at higher bleeding risk best treated with clopidogrel. Unlike prior risk tools based on Western populations,45)46) this scoring system reflects the unique ischemic–bleeding risk profile of Korean/East Asian patients,47) complementing global risk models with region-specific guidance.

Collectively, these findings highlight how KAMIR has advanced risk stratification not only for prognostic assessment but also for guiding therapeutic decision-making in routine clinical practice.

Clinical and biomarker-based prognostic factors

In addition to formalized risk scores, KAMIR has identified multiple clinical and biomarker predictors of outcome after AMI. LVEF remains a powerful prognostic determinant: even mildly reduced EF (40–49%) was associated with significantly higher long-term cardiovascular mortality compared with preserved EF,48) while patients demonstrating EF recovery during follow-up had better outcomes than those with persistently impaired EF.49) These observations support reassessing EF at 6–12 months after AMI to refine risk stratification and guide decisions such as implantable cardioverter–defibrillator candidacy and the intensity of medical therapy. AF also emerged as an important prognostic factor. KAMIR data showed that new-onset AF during AMI was independently associated with increased short-term mortality, reflecting adverse hemodynamic effects and its relation to atrial stretch and heart failure.50) This emphasizes the need for vigilant rhythm monitoring and timely arrhythmia management in the acute phase. Elevated N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide at admission in STEMI was associated with adverse short-term outcomes including the need for inotropic support and 30-day mortality.51) Similarly, elevated baseline high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) independently predicted long-term mortality, particularly in the first 6 months after AMI.52) Notably, high-intensity statin therapy appeared to attenuate the excess risk associated with elevated hs-CRP, and reductions in hs-CRP during follow-up were associated with lower stent thrombosis and recurrent events.53)

Machine learning and artificial intelligence approaches

Beyond conventional scores and biomarkers, KAMIR data have also been employed for the development of next-generation risk prediction tools. A recent study applied a dynamic machine-learning algorithm including multiple clinical variables to predict longitudinal outcomes after AMI.54) Unlike static, one-time risk scores, this model recalculated risk at defined follow-up time points (1 and 3 years), thereby improving prognostic accuracy and supporting individualized surveillance and treatment adjustment. Another group trained an AI model using admission electrocardiograms to identify patients with AMI who would benefit from urgent revascularization.55) The model demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in detecting subtle ischemic changes that might escape human interpretation, suggesting a potential role in rapid triage and early intervention.

ACUTE INTERVENTIONAL MANAGEMENT

Rapid reperfusion therapy remains the cornerstone of AMI management, and KAMIR has played a pivotal role in documenting temporal trends and advancing the use of reperfusion strategies in Korea. This section summarizes how the registry has shaped contemporary interventional practice and enhanced our understanding of evidence-based strategies for AMI care.

Primary percutaneous coronary intervention and reperfusion trends

In 2018, KAMIR analyses reported the use of primary PCI in 99.1% of STEMI cases and drug-eluting stent (DES) implantation in 99.6%.1) This remarkable achievement indicates that effective reperfusion therapy has become accessible to nearly all STEMI patients nationwide. Concomitant with the widespread adoption of PCI, door-to-balloon (D2B) times improved significantly. Several analyses also examined treatment delays. In a nationwide cohort of 5,243 STEMI patients, shorter D2B time showed a continuous association with lower 1-year mortality—even within the 60–90-minute window—supporting an approach that prioritizes the shortest possible in-hospital delay.9) By contrast, onset-to-door (O2D) time was not an independent predictor after multivariable adjustment. These findings imply that public education to reduce prehospital delay is as critical as maintaining rapid in-hospital response times.

Thrombolysis versus percutaneous coronary intervention

KAMIR provided an opportunity to compare real-world outcomes of different reperfusion strategies. In a propensity-matched analysis of STEMI patients, outcomes of primary PCI were compared with a pharmaco-invasive approach (fibrinolysis followed by routine early PCI).56) Outcomes were broadly similar to primary PCI when timely PCI was available. However, when PCI was expected to be significantly delayed, a pharmaco-invasive approach was a reasonable alternative. These findings were consistent with international trial evidence,57)58)59) but in the context of Korea’s dense hospital network and improved interhospital transfer systems, primary PCI can now be delivered to the vast majority of patients, and fibrinolysis has become infrequently used.

Multivessel disease in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction

A long-standing controversy in STEMI management has been the optimal approach to significant non-culprit coronary lesions. Observational data from KAMIR indicated that leaving significant non-culprit lesions untreated was associated with higher late event rates.60)61)62) One KAMIR study specifically evaluated STEMI patients with cardiogenic shock, comparing culprit-only PCI with multivessel PCI.62) Immediate multivessel PCI was not associated with improved short-term survival compared with culprit-only PCI, consistent with the CULPRIT-SHOCK trial.63) However, among survivors after initial stabilization, staged PCI of non-culprit lesions was associated with better outcomes. For STEMI patients without shock, KAMIR findings—together with randomized trial evidence,64)65)—have supported a more aggressive approach favoring complete revascularization.60)61)66) One analysis demonstrated that patients who underwent treatment of all significant lesion—either during the index procedure or as staged PCI during hospitalization—had superior 6-year outcomes, including lower rates of reinfarction and heart failure, compared with culprit-only PCI.66) Consequently, clinical practice in Korea has evolved toward a strategy of multivessel PCI in stable STEMI, with the timing of non-culprit vessel intervention individualized according to patient condition.3)

Intervention timing in non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction

For high-risk NSTEMI, current guidelines recommend an early invasive strategy, typically within 24 hours.67)68) A recent analysis demonstrated that in high-risk patients without hemodynamic instability, a very early invasive approach (within 12 hours of admission) was not associated with improved outcomes and was linked to higher 1-year mortality compared with intervention after 12 hours.69) Another study found that invasive management within 48 hours of symptom onset was associated with significantly lower 3-year mortality than intervention after ≥48 hours (7.3% vs. 13.4%).70) Furthermore, an earlier KAMIR report indicated that immediate PCI within 4 hours of presentation did not improve 12-month outcomes compared with PCI performed after 4 hours, although immediate PCI was associated with less major bleeding and a shorter hospital stay.71) Taken together, these findings suggest that, in high-risk NSTEMI, an early invasive strategy within 24 hours is advisable, whereas immediate intervention within a few hours in otherwise stable patients does not necessarily confer additional survival benefit.

Drug-eluting stent in acute myocardial infarction

KAMIR analyses demonstrated consistent reductions in repeat revascularization with DES compared with bare-metal stents (BMS), without an increase in death or reinfarction.72) In larger vessels, DES lowered target lesion revascularization (TLR) and target vessel revascularization (TVR) at 6 and 12 months, while mortality and myocardial infarction (MI) remained similar to BMS.73)

Among first-generation DES, direct comparative analyses revealed clinically relevant intraclass differences primarily driven by repeat revascularization rates rather than hard endpoints. In STEMI cohorts, sirolimus-eluting stents (SES) yielded lower rates of repeat revascularization and MACE compared with paclitaxel-eluting stents (PES), with the difference attenuated among patients with diabetes.74) Endeavor-zotarolimus-eluting stents (E-ZES) were also less effective than SES at 12 months in terms of MACE, TLR, and TVR, while death and reinfarction rates were comparable.75)

The transition to second-generation DES consolidated gains in both efficacy and safety. In a large propensity-matched analysis of 2,686 patients with AMI, everolimus-eluting stents (EES) reduced target lesion failure (TLF) compared with E-ZES (6.5% vs. 8.7%, p=0.029) and markedly lowered definite/probable stent thrombosis (0.3% vs. 1.6%, p<0.001).76) Compared with PES, EES further decreased reinfarction, repeat revascularization, and stent thrombosis without an increase in mortality.76) Against SES, EES showed approximate parity, with signals of incremental benefit in adjusted TVR reduction.77)

With newer-generation DES, outcome differences have largely converged, reflecting advances in stent architecture and polymer biocompatibility. Resolute-zotarolimus-eluting stents (R-ZES) and EES demonstrated comparable 6-month device- and patient-oriented outcomes,77) biolimus-eluting stents showed similar 12-month composite outcomes to EES.78)

Analyses comparing polymer strategies also suggested practical equivalence when modern engineering standards are met. In a propensity-matched cohort, 2-year outcomes were similar between biodegradable-polymer DES and second-generation durable-polymer DES, with MACE rates of 10.7% and 9.9% and stent thrombosis rates of 0.8% and 0.9%, respectively.79)

Adjunctive devices and techniques

In KAMIR, routine manual thrombus aspiration during primary PCI in STEMI did not improve overall 12-month outcomes, although signals of benefit were observed in patients presenting 4–6 hours after symptom onset80) or in prespecified subgroups receiving glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors or with left anterior descending artery culprit lesions.81) These data support current guidelines, which do not recommend routine aspiration,67)82) but suggest selective use in carefully chosen patients.

Intravascular imaging has shown consistent prognostic value. Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) guidance reduced 3-year TLF, driven primarily by reductions in cardiac death and target vessel MI.83) Optical coherence tomography (OCT) guidance was associated with 1-year TLF comparable to that observed with IVUS,84) supporting OCT as a reasonable alternative in selected patients.

Mechanical circulatory support has also been examined. Intra-aortic balloon pump therapy did not confer survival benefit in AMI with severe cardiogenic shock,85) whereas early extracorporeal membrane oxygenation initiation (before or without cardiac arrest) was associated with substantially better survival compared with delayed initiation.86) These findings highlight the potential role of proactive mechanical support in selected high-risk patients.

Acute myocardial infarction in special situations

Among patients with AMI and non-obstructive coronary arteries, KAMIR analyses demonstrated event rates comparable to those with 1- or 2-vessel disease, indicating that this entity is not benign and warrants mechanism-directed management.87) In vasospasm-related AMI, the 2-year incidence of MACE was significantly lower than in atherosclerotic AMI, primarily driven by fewer repeat revascularizations, while total mortality and nonfatal MI were similar, underscoring the importance of vasospasm-targeted therapy.88)

STEMI without chest pain remains a clinical challenge. In the KAMIR STEMI cohort of 7,288 patients, about 11% presented without chest pain, experiencing longer D2B times (133 vs. 88 minutes) and worse outcomes after primary PCI, including higher in-hospital mortality (5.9% vs. 3.6%) and 1-year MACE (26% vs. 19%).89) Another analysis found that 14% of STEMI patients presented with non-chest pain complaints and similarly had longer delays across the care pathway, yet propensity-matched analyses showed no significant differences in mortality or MACE through 2 years.90) Collectively, these data indicate that atypical or pain-free STEMI presentations are not uncommon, often incur clinically meaningful delays, and require heightened vigilance and streamlined pathways.

Misdiagnosis of STEMI as NSTEMI was uncommon (1.4%) but associated with substantial treatment delays, with median D2B times of 264 minutes versus 61 minutes in correctly diagnosed cases.91) Although overall adjusted 1-year mortality was not significantly different, patients with left anterior descending artery culprit lesions had higher adjusted 1-year mortality (hazard ratio [HR], 1.84). This reinforces the importance of accurate early diagnosis to minimize reperfusion delay.

MEDICAL THERAPY

Beyond interventional treatment, optimal medical therapy is crucial for secondary prevention and improvement of long-term outcomes after AMI. Evidence from KAMIR has provided comprehensive insights into the real-world prescription patterns and prognostic impact of various pharmacologic strategies in Korean patients. These include antithrombotic agents, lipid-lowering therapies with statins and adjunctive agents, beta-blockers, and inhibitors of the renin–angiotensin system. Moreover, KAMIR findings have helped tailor these therapies to East Asian populations by accounting for differences in pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and baseline risk profiles.

Statin therapy and lipid targets

Analyses from KAMIR have provided compelling evidence for the benefits of statin therapy even in patients presenting with very low baseline low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). In a cohort of 1,054 patients with AMI and LDL-C <70 mg/dL, statin therapy at discharge significantly reduced 12-month MACE (HR, 0.56), primarily through reductions in cardiac death (HR, 0.47) and repeat revascularization (HR, 0.45).92) Extending to 1,048 patients with LDL-C ≤50 mg/dL, statin therapy remained beneficial, lowering 12-month MACE (HR, 0.60) and decreasing the need for coronary artery bypass grafting (HR, 0.27).28)

Beyond baseline thresholds, KAMIR also addressed the optimal treatment target for secondary prevention. In a study of 1,305 patients with AMI and baseline LDL-C ≥70 mg/dL, achieving a ≥50% reduction in LDL-C at 1 year was associated with significantly fewer 2-year MACE compared with <50% reduction (8.2% vs. 11.9%; HR, 0.53). In contrast, attaining an absolute LDL-C <70 mg/dL provided no incremental advantage compared with ≥70 mg/dL (10.7% vs. 10.6%; HR, 0.96).93) A subsequent analysis of 2,198 patients reinforced this paradigm: those achieving the combined target of LDL-C <70 mg/dL and ≥50% reduction experienced fewer 3-year MACE than non-achievers (9.8% vs. 13.0%; HR, 0.73). Within achievers, however, event rates were similar whether the achieved LDL-C was <55 mg/dL or 55–70 mg/dL (8.1% vs. 10.0%; HR, 0.75), suggesting only modest incremental benefit from lowering below 55 mg/dL.94)

More intensive statin therapy also showed consistent benefit even in patients with very low LDL-C. Among 1,086 patients with AMI and baseline LDL-C <70 mg/dL, more-intensive therapy (expected LDL-C reduction ≥40%) versus less-intensive therapy reduced 12-month major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE: 7.0% vs. 11.6%; HR, 0.59), particularly by lowering TVR (HR, 0.41) and TLR (HR, 0.35). The benefit was more pronounced in patients with elevated hs-CRP, without excess hemorrhagic stroke or major bleeding.95)

Nationwide registry data further highlighted secular trends. In 26,751 post-AMI patients over a decade, the use of high-intensity statins increased markedly (12.8% in 2011 to 69.3% in 2020; p<0.001), paralleled by growing ezetimibe adoption (4.5% in 2016 to 22.5% in 2020; p<0.001) and improved LDL-C goal attainment (<70 mg/dL from 41.4% in 2012 to 62.5% in 2019; ≥50% reduction from 20.8% to 39.5%). High-intensity statins were independently associated with greater likelihood of achieving both absolute and relative lipid targets (HR, 2.97 for <70 mg/dL).96)

In comparative analyses, high-intensity statin therapy was superior to a simvastatin–ezetimibe combination, with significantly lower 12-month MACE (4.0% vs. 10.4%; HR, 3.09), largely driven by reduced repeat revascularization (HR, 3.94). All-cause mortality and recurrent MI, however, were not significantly different between the groups. Importantly, outcomes were broadly consistent across high-risk subgroups, including patients with diabetes, advanced age, or reduced LVEF,97) suggesting that in patients unable to tolerate high-dose statins—particularly those at high risk—moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe may represent a practical alternative.

Taken together, these observations confirm that the benefit of high-intensity statins extends to Korean patients, consistent with global evidence that LDL-C lowering improves outcomes. However, KAMIR data also indicate that lipid profiles and the magnitude of statin benefit in East Asian populations may differ from those in Western cohorts. The prevalence of dyslipidemia in Korean patients with AMI has historically been lower, and their dyslipidemia is characterized by lower LDL-C levels and higher rates of hypertriglyceridemia and low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C).1)6) In one KAMIR analysis, a high triglyceride (TG)/HDL-C ratio (≥3.35) independently predicted 12-month MACE, highlighting the adverse impact of this atherogenic profile.98) In another KAMIR cohort, the benefit of statin therapy on 2-year MACE was evident in patients with favorable lipid profiles (HDL-C ≥40 mg/dL and TG <150 mg/dL), but was not significant in those with low HDL-C and/or high TG,99) suggesting that residual risk from atherogenic dyslipidemia may persist despite statin therapy in East Asian patients.

In summary, while intensive statin therapy provides consistent benefit across populations, East Asian patients often present with an atherogenic dyslipidemia phenotype (high TG/low HDL-C), in which residual risk may persist despite optimal LDL-C control.

Antiplatelet therapy

Antiplatelet therapy is central to AMI management. In East Asian populations, distinct pharmacodynamic responses—such as a higher prevalence of CYP2C19 polymorphisms associated with clopidogrel resistance—and a higher bleeding propensity, collectively termed the “East Asian paradox,” pose challenges for the optimization of DAPT strategies.2)4)

Triple antiplatelet therapy (TAPT) with cilostazol: In an analysis of 4,203 STEMI patients, TAPT significantly reduced 8-month cardiac death, total death, and MACE compared with DAPT (MACE: 7.6% vs. 9.3%), without increasing major bleeding.100) Subgroup analysis suggested particular benefit in older patients, women, and those with diabetes. Subsequent analyses confirmed improved outcomes in high-risk subsets, supporting selective use of cilostazol in the pre-potent P2Y12 inhibitor era.101)102)103)

Ticagrelor and prasugrel: In patients with AMI undergoing successful PCI, ticagrelor and prasugrel demonstrated efficacy comparable to clopidogrel but were associated with higher in-hospital bleeding. Ticagrelor use was independently linked to increased bleeding risk, especially in elderly or low-weight patients.104) Similarly, prasugrel showed no ischemic advantage over clopidogrel but increased bleeding events, with risk factors including transfemoral access and glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor use.105) These findings diverge from Western trials106)107) and exemplify the “East Asian paradox,” wherein East Asian patients tend to experience less ischemic benefit but greater bleeding risk on intensive antiplatelet therapy, warranting tailored strategies in East Asians. In STEMI patients, comparative analysis showed no significant differences between ticagrelor and prasugrel in ischemic or bleeding outcomes, suggesting that either agent may be used appropriately in Korean practice.108) For extended secondary prevention, continuation of ticagrelor or prasugrel beyond 24 months yielded no overall ischemic advantage over clopidogrel (3-year MACE: 20.5% vs. 21.6%), although the need for recurrent PCI was lower with the potent P2Y12 inhibitors (7.6% vs. 12.2%, p=0.006).109)

Dose adjustments of prasugrel: Pharmacodynamic studies have shown that East Asians achieve greater platelet inhibition at lower prasugrel doses.110) In a joint analysis of KAMIR and the Japan Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry, adjusted-dose prasugrel (20-mg loading, 3.75-mg maintenance) was associated with reduced in-hospital major bleeding compared with the standard 60/10-mg regimen, while 12-month MACE rates were similar (4.70% vs. 4.70%).111)

DAPT duration after DES in AMI: In 6,199 event-free patients AMI treated with second-generation DES, extending DAPT beyond 12 months did not reduce ischemic events (MACCE: 1.3% vs. 1.0%), and major bleeding rates were similarly low (0.1% vs. 0.1%).112) In a validation of Academic Research Consortium–High Bleeding Risk in 10,291 patients with AMI undergoing PCI, 32.8% met high bleeding risk criteria, experiencing markedly higher 1-year Bleeding Academic Research Consortium type 3/5 bleeding (9.8% vs. 2.9%; HR, 3.69) and all-cause mortality (22.8% vs. 4.3%; HR, 5.84),113) highlighting the need for individualized treatment strategies. Furthermore, in a study of 4,604 patients with AMI who were event-free at 1 year, transitioning to single antiplatelet therapy between 12 and 36 months significantly reduced MACCE compared with continued DAPT (4.2% vs. 8.5%), without excess stroke or stent thrombosis.114)

In summary, while universal principles of DAPT optimization—such as balancing ischemic and bleeding risks—apply globally, evidence from East Asian registries including KAMIR highlights unique regional features. These include the “East Asian paradox” of heightened bleeding susceptibility despite lower ischemic event rates, the influence of CYP2C19 polymorphisms on clopidogrel response, and enhanced platelet inhibition at lower doses of prasugrel. Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of tailoring antiplatelet therapy in Korean and East Asian patients, whereas the broader therapeutic paradigm remains consistent with global practice.

Beta-blockers

In a large KAMIR cohort (n=12,200; 2011–2015), beta-blocker therapy at discharge was associated with a significantly lower 1-year rate of MACE after multivariable adjustment (HR, 0.84), mainly by reducing cardiac death (HR, 0.74).115) The effect varied according to LVEF (p for interaction=0.002): patients with reduced (≤40%) or mildly reduced (40–49%) LVEF derived significant benefit (HR, 0.63 and 0.69, respectively), whereas those with preserved LVEF (≥50%) did not (HR, 1.16). These findings emphasize the importance of prioritizing beta-blockers in patients with reduced or mildly reduced EF, while suggesting individualized use in preserved EF. By contrast, in an earlier STEMI cohort (n=8,510) undergoing primary PCI, discharge beta-blocker therapy was similarly associated with improved survival at 1 year (HR, 0.46), but no interaction with LVEF was observed.116)

Two complementary comparative analyses favored vasodilating beta-blockers (carvedilol, nebivolol) over conventional agents (bisoprolol, metoprolol).117)118) In a study on 7,127 patients with AMI undergoing successful PCI, vasodilating agents were associated with a significantly lower risk of cardiac death (HR, 0.63) and of the composite endpoint of cardiac death, MI, or heart failure hospitalization (HR, 0.67).117) A subsequent study of 7,269 patients with AMI, with 3-year follow-up, similarly demonstrated a reduction in the composite of cardiac death, MI, or heart failure hospitalization with vasodilating agents (7.6% vs. 9.8%; HR, 0.76), driven mainly by lower cardiac mortality (HR, 0.73).118) Collectively, these data support EF-guided initiation, preferential selection of vasodilating agents, and long-term continuation in appropriate candidates.

Renin-angiotensin system inhibitors

In STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI with preserved systolic function (LVEF ≥40%), a large KAMIR prospective cohort demonstrated that angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB) improved outcomes compared with no renin-angiotensin system (RAS) blockade and were comparable to angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI) after propensity matching.119) In matched analyses, cardiac death or MI occurred significantly less frequently with ARB than with no RAS inhibitor (1.7% vs. 3.1%; HR, 0.35), whereas the rates were similar between ARB and ACEI (1.8% vs. 2.0%; HR, 0.65). These data support the routine use of RAS inhibition after STEMI, even in patients with preserved EF, and indicate that ARB represent an acceptable alternative to ACEI.

Not all ARB appear equivalent. In a study of 2,740 patients with AMI, surmountable ARB (losartan, eprosartan) were associated with higher 12-month MACE compared with insurmountable agents (valsartan, candesartan, irbesartan, telmisartan, and olmesartan), a difference that remained significant after propensity matching (14.9% vs. 11.4%; HR, 1.36),120) providing consistent, though hypothesis-generating, evidence favoring insurmountable agents post-AMI.

Further, when combined with statins after DES-treated STEMI, ACEI may confer a survival advantage over ARB. In a propensity-matched analysis of 11,706 patients with 2-year follow-up (2,729 pairs), ARB therapy was associated with higher risks of all-cause death (3.1% vs. 2.0%; HR, 1.55) and cardiac death (2.4% vs. 1.3%; HR, 1.85) compared with ACEI, while the rates of MACE, recurrent MI, and revascularization did not differ significantly.121)

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors

In a cohort of diabetic AMI survivors, outcomes were compared between patients discharged on metformin plus a sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor (n=186) versus a dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitor (n=593). At 1 year, the incidence of major adverse composite events (all-cause death, nonfatal MI, any revascularization, stroke, or rehospitalization) did not differ between groups after inverse probability weighting, and individual endpoints were similarly neutral. Notably, an exploratory analysis demonstrated greater recovery of LVEF with SGLT2 inhibitors compared with DPP-4 inhibitors over 1 year (ΔLVEF +6.9% vs. +3.1%, p=0.027).122)

CONCLUSIONS

Over the past 2 decades, KAMIR has substantially advanced our understanding and management of AMI in Korea. Through meticulous data collection and analysis, the registry has documented major improvements in outcomes, including faster reperfusion, broader adoption of GDMT, and marked reductions in mortality and complications. KAMIR has also bridged the gap between Western trial evidence and real-world practice by revealing distinctive characteristics of Korean and East Asian patients, thereby informing tailored treatment strategies. Key findings, such as the benefit of intensive lipid-lowering even at low baseline LDL-C levels, the need to balance potent antithrombotic therapy against bleeding risk in East Asian populations, and the value of intravascular imaging and complete revascularization in selected patients, have been incorporated into national guidelines and daily clinical practice.
