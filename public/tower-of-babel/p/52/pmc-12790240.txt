Surgical Retrieval of Protective Guidewire That Spontaneously Fractured in the RCA and Penetrated the Vessel
Authors: Xue Li, Hao Wang, Hong Qiu, Ji-Hong Zou, Guang-Sheng Cai, Xiao-Juan Pan, Meng-Yun Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuidewire fracture is a rare and serious complication of percutaneous coronary intervention, especially when it is “standing by” as a protective one.Case SummaryWe present a case in which a protective guidewire in the right coronary artery spontaneously fractured during percutaneous coronary intervention for chronic total occlusion lesion of the left anterior descending artery. The fractured guidewire pierced the vessel and entered the pericardium before any measures could be taken, and a percutaneous retrieval attempt using the twisting wire technique failed. The patient developed cardiac tamponade, and emergency surgery was performed to remove the fractured guidewire.Take-Home MessagesGreater prudence is required when choosing guidewires during percutaneous coronary intervention. Coiled guidewires may not be suitable for use as a protective guidewire.Key words: cardiac tamponade, chronic total occlusion, complication, guidewire fracture, percutaneous coronary intervention

Timeline of Case Presentation

CTO = chronic total occlusion; IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; LAD = left anterior descending artery; PCI = percutaneous coronary intervention; RCA = right coronary artery.

History of Presentation

A 72-year-old man was admitted to our hospital owing to stable angina pectoris for 2 months. Coronary angiography indicated 80% stenosis in the midsegment of the left main artery, 100% stenosis in the midsegment of the left anterior descending artery (LAD), 80% stenosis in the both the first and the second diagonal branches, and 50% stenosis in the right coronary artery (RCA). The coronary artery was right dominant, and the RCA provided collateral circulation to the distal segment of the LAD.

We performed percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) to recanalize the chronic total occlusion (CTO) of the LAD under the reverse guidance provided from right to left. A 7-F EBU 3.5 guide catheter (right hand) and a 6-F JR 3.5 guide catheter (left hand) were used. A balance middle weight (BMW) Universal II guidewire was advanced to the distal segment of the RCA for protection, and a Sion Blue guidewire was advanced to the second diagonal branch. Firstly, bilateral angiography was performed to guide the distal vessels; this significantly improves the success rate and safety of CTO-PCI (Figure 1).1,2 With the support of a Kaneka dual-lumen catheter, an ULTIMATEbros 3 guidewire was able to pass through the occluded segment of the LAD. Under intravascular ultrasound guidance, 4 stents were implanted in a series from the distal segment of the LAD to the open segment of the left main artery. This process lasted for a total of 80 minutes. After the intervention procedure, the BMW Universal II guidewire in the RCA was planned to be withdrawn, but it was found spontaneously fractured within the coronary artery (Figures 2A and 2E). The fractured end was at the first bending of the RCA. Before any measures could be taken, the broken end of the guidewire spontaneously pierced the vessel and entered the pericardium with the beating of the heart, while the distal end of the guidewire was still located in the RCA (Figures 2B and 2C). An attempt was made to retrieve the guidewire by winding it with the other 2 Sion Blue guidewires within the coronary artery, but it was unsuccessful. Intravascular ultrasound indicated that the fractured guidewire pierced the vessel at the first turning of the RCA, and there was a local hematoma (Figure 2D). There was no obvious stenosis in the RCA, with TIMI flow grade 3. The patient had no discomfort symptoms and had stable vital signs. Echocardiography showed no pericardial effusion. We decided to terminate the operation.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and long-term smoking.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included coronary artery dissection and acute coronary artery occlusion.

Investigations

Computed tomography examination after PCI confirmed that the distal end of the guidewire was in the RCA, and the broken end was located at the lower edge of the aortic arch (Figure 2F). In addition, we used a JSM-IT200 electron microscope (JEOL Ltd) to scan the broken end of the guidewire (Figure 3).

Management

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for close monitoring. Subsequent echocardiography indicated that the pericardial effusion was increasing significantly, and electrocardiography on the next day showed ST-segment elevation in the inferior leads. The patient underwent emergency surgery. There was about 200 mL of old bloody pericardial effusion. An approximately 10-cm guidewire could be seen penetrating from the starting point of the atrioventricular groove in the proximal segment of the RCA (Figure 4). The guidewire was pulled out, and the penetration site was continuously sutured, with no obvious bleeding observed.

Outcome

The surgery was successful, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Discussion

Guidewire fracture is a rare complication of PCI, especially when the guidewire is “standing by” as a protective guidewire. The total incidence of guidewire fracture is approximately 0.1% to 0.2%.3,4 However, according to the literature, only our hospital has reported another case of a BMW guidewire fracture when it was used for protection.5 Therefore, the fracture of a protective guidewire is an even rarer complication of PCI that is worthy of attention. The management of guidewire fracture is challenging, as inappropriate operations may result in fatal clinical outcomes.6

In the current case, a BMW Universal II guidewire serving as a protective guidewire in the RCA spontaneously fractured during PCI, and the fractured end of the guidewire penetrated the vessel from the proximal segment of the RCA. The attempt to retrieve the guidewire by winding it with the other 2 Sion Blue guidewires failed, and the fractured segment was removed through emergency surgery, as the patient developed cardiac tamponade. The patient subsequently recovered without further incident.

In previous case reports and meta-analyses on guidewire fractures, the potential causes include excessive angulation, excessive rotation and forceful traction of the guidewire, the use of rot ablators and thrombus aspiration, and the shedding of the guidewire polymer coating.3,7,8 Our center has previously reported a case where a BMW guidewire used to anchor in the RCA spontaneously fractured during CTO-PCI of the left circumflex artery.5 The reasons for the guidewire fracture were considered to be related to the shedding of the polymer coating and the non-coaxial guiding catheter.5

Unlike our previous case, the BMW Universal II guidewire spontaneously fractured at the first bend of the RCA without being subjected to any external force in this case. This guidewire was used for anchoring and protecting, not being repositioned or having instruments inserted into or removed from the RCA. The occurrence of guidewire fracture typically involves 3 factors: fatigue limitation of the material, local stress concentration, and repeated bending.3 We believe that the mechanism of the current guidewire fracture may be related to the following factors. First, there was a large-angled curve at the first bend of the RCA. At this turning of the tortuous RCA, the guidewire repeatedly endured pressure and was bent for a long time (80 minutes) following the rhythm of the heartbeat. This caused the pressure to be fixed at a single position of the guidewire, which eventually reached its fatigue tolerance and fractured. In other words, a guidewire that has been indwelling for a long time undergoes repeated bending due to the beating of the heart and experiences local stress concentration, which is one of the factors leading to guidewire fracture.

The design of the BMW Universal II guidewire may also be related to its breakage.9 The BMW Universal II is currently a first-line working guidewire during PCI, using nitinol as its metal core.10 In this case, the fracture point was 14 cm away from the distal end of the guidewire. This location is relatively far from the tip, resulting in a decrease in the antifracture properties of the metal core. Under electron microscope scanning, the surface of the metal core fracture was very flat and smooth. However, the edge of the peripheral polymer coating showed tear-like irregularities, indicating that the antifracture performance of the metal core at this point was highly likely to be poor. Spontaneous fracture of the metal core may have occured first, followed by the tearing of the polymer coating, which ultimately resulted in the complete breakage of the guidewire. Also, the guidewire fracture was 14 cm from the distal end, in the middle of the metal core. The metal core of the BMW Universal II guidewire has a progressive taper.9,10 At the position 14 cm from the distal end, the core is in the middle of the taper, where the core is still relatively thin, resulting in poor fracture resistance performance.9,10 The guidewire is more likely to reach the fatigue limitation at this location. Therefore, the structural and material characteristics of the guidewire also contributed to the guidewire fracture.

In conclusion, we believe that the angulation at the first curve of the RCA and the repeated bending and pressure concentrated on this position of the guidewire, along with the structural characteristics of the guidewire, jointly led to the guidewire fracture. The location where the fracture occurred, in the middle of the metal core of the BMW Universal II guidewire, happens to be a part with relatively poor fracture resistance.9,10 Moreover, this location is exactly at the angled position of the RCA. The guidewire at this location was subjected to repeated bending and pressure during the beating of the heart, which ultimately resulted in guidewire fracture.

The BMW Universal II guidewire may not be suitable for use as a protective guidewire for CTO-PCI. As a coiled guidewire, the proximal support and fatigue resistance performance of the BMW Universal II guidewire were insufficient when it was used as protective guidewire for CTO-PCI.9,10 There is also a stress concentration point at the junction of the core wire and the coil layer, which makes the guidewire prone to breakage at this point.9 The tip of the BMW Universal II guidewire has a thinner metal core compared with guidewires with a core-to-tip design because it uses a shaping ribbon design.10 Therefore, if the BMW Universal II guidewire is left in a tortuous coronary artery for a long time, it may reach the fatigue limit owing to repeated friction. For the protective guidewire in CTO-PCI, guidewires with stronger proximal support and better fatigue resistance are preferred, such as the Sion Blue guidewire.9,10 It may help avoid the risk of guidewire fracture when left in a tortuous coronary artery as a protective guidewire during a prolonged PCI procedure.

Conclusions

We presented a rare and serious case of a protective guidewire spontaneously fracturing in the coronary artery. The guidewire fracture and serious cardiac tamponade were well treated, and the patient recovered. The potential causes and prevention methods were analyzed. Coiled guidewires may not be suitable for use as a protective guidewire.
