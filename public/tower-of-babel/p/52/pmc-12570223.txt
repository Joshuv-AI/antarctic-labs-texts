The complete plastid genome of Crepidium calophyllum (Orchidaceae: Malaxidinae) and phylogenetic analysis
Authors: Chunxia Li, Wei Han, Baiyin Yu, Ran Yu, Kaiqi Huang, Lisha Lu, Shengxin Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrepidium calophyllum is a terrestrial orchid native to tropical forests in southern China and Southeast Asia. Once classified under Malaxis, it has since been reassigned to Crepidium. This study presents the first complete plastid genome (plastome) of C. calophyllum, which is 158,884 bp in length. Its gene content and order are highly conserved compared to other autotrophic species within Malaxidinae. Phylogenomic analyses using both maximum likelihood and Bayesian inference, based on complete plastomes, place C. calophyllum within Crepidium, as sister to Dienia. Interestingly, these analyses also reveal the polyphyly of Malaxis and Liparis, suggesting unresolved taxonomic issues within Malaxidinae.

Introduction

Crepidium calophyllum (Rchb. f.) Szlach. 1995 (Szlachetko 1995) is a terrestrial orchid species distributed mainly in tropical forests of southern China (Hainan and Yunnan) and Southeast Asia. It is notable for its compact growth, ornamental foliage with white-striped brown leaves, and dense terminal inflorescences bearing small flowers (Chen et al. 2009).

Historically, C. calophyllum was placed in the genus Malaxis, subtribe Malaxidinae, based on earlier morphological classifications (Dressler 1993). However, taxonomic revisions by Szlachetko (1995) transferred it to Crepidium, as it shares key morphological traits with that genus, such as prominent above-ground pseudobulbs and lateral or terminal racemes (Szlachetko 1995). This reassignment has since (Kumar et al. 2022) been supported by molecular phylogenetic analyses using nuclear ITS and plastid matK markers, which resolved Crepidium and Malaxis as separate lineages.

Given its compact growth, ornamental foliage, and dense inflorescences, C. calophyllum has potential value in ornamental horticulture (Hossain 2011). Moreover, Crepidium species are known to produce diverse bioactive compounds with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties (Prabha and Kushwaha 2024). Although direct ecological data on C. calophyllum are lacking, studies on terrestrial orchids, including other Crepidium species, indicate that habitat quality strongly affects orchid diversity and population viability (Timsina et al. 2021; Crain et al. 2025). Given its dependence on forest understory habitats, C. calophyllum warrants conservation attention.

Despite its taxonomic importance, no complete plastome has been reported for C. calophyllum to date. Here, we present the first complete plastome sequence of C. calophyllum, assembled using Illumina sequencing. The plastome structure and gene content were annotated and compared with other Malaxidinae taxa, and a phylogenetic analysis was performed to evaluate its systematic position within the subtribe.

Material and methods

Sample collection, DNA extraction, and sequencing

C. calophyllum plants were collected from Wenshan, Yunnan Province, China (latitude: 23°24’02.05ʺN, longitude: 104°15′18.94ʺE), and subsequently transferred to the greenhouse facilities at Shaoguan University for cultivation (Figure 1). A specimen was deposited at the Collection of School of Biology and Agriculture, Shaoguan University (http://www.sgu.edu.cn/swynyxy/, contact person: Chang Shengxin, changsx@sgu.edu.cn) under the voucher number SG-CC-2022-06. Total genomic DNA was extracted from fresh leaves of C. calophyllum using the CTAB method and treated with RNase (Qiagen). High-quality DNA was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., USA), generating 10,017,077 paired-end reads (2 × 150 bp). After quality filtering, the complete plastome was assembled using NOVOPlasty v4.3.1 (Dierckxsens et al. 2017), and reads were mapped back using Bowtie2 v2.3.5 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012) to confirm IR and single-copy region boundaries. The final average sequencing coverage reached 623.2 (Figure S1).

Genome annotation

Annotation was performed in Geneious Prime® v2023.0.1 using the ‘Live Annotation & Predict’ function with a 70% similarity threshold. CDS regions were manually curated based on sequences from autotrophic orchids. tRNA genes were annotated using tRNAscan-SE v2.0 (Chan et al. 2021). A circular genome map was generated using OGDRAW v1.3.1 (Greiner et al. 2019).

Phylogenetic analysis

Plastomes of all available Malaxidinae species were downloaded from NCBI, along with two outgroup taxa (Dendrobium officinale and Bulbophyllum reptans). One IR copy was removed prior to alignment. Whole-genome alignments were done using MAFFT v7.511 (Katoh and Standley 2013) and refined with Gblocks v0.91b (Castresana 2000) under codon settings (min conserved block = 9; max non-conserved = 6; no gaps allowed).

ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) in IQ-TREE v2.2.0 (Minh et al. 2020) identified the best-fit nucleotide substitution model (TIM+F + R4) under the Bayesian Information Criterion. Maximum likelihood phylogenetic inference was performed with 5000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (Minh et al. 2013). Bayesian inference was conducted in MrBayes v3.2.6 (Ronquist et al. 2012) using the GTR+I + G + F model selected by ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017). Two independent MCMC runs were performed for 2,000,000 generations with sampling every 1000 generations, and the first 25% of samples were discarded as burn-in.

Results

The complete plastome of C. calophyllum is 158,884 bp in length and exhibits the typical quadripartite circular structure found in most angiosperms. It comprises a pair of IRs of 27.0 kb, separated by a large single-copy (LSC) region of 86.6 kb and a small single-copy (SSC) region of 18.2 kb (Figure 2). A total of 133 genes were identified, including 87 protein-coding genes, 38 transfer RNA genes, and 8 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes (Figure 2). Among them, 19 genes are duplicated, comprising 7 PCGs (rpl2, rpl23, rps7, rps12, ndhB, rps19, and ycf2), 8 tRNA genes (trnA-UGC, trnH-GUG, trnI-CAU, trnI-GAU, trnL-CAA, trnN-GUU, trnR-ACG, and trnV-GAC), and all 4 rRNA genes (rrn16S, rrn23S, rrn4.5S, and rrn5S). The remaining genes are present as single copies.

Most genes are intronless, whereas 10 PCGs (atpF, petB, petD, ndhA, ndhB, rpl2, rpl16, rps12, rps16, and rpoC1) and 6 tRNA genes (trnA-UGC, trnG-UCC, trnI-GAU, trnK-UUU, trnL-UAA, and trnV-UAC) each contain a single intron (Figure S2). Two PCGs (ycf3 and clpP) contain two introns. The matK gene is located within the intron of trnK-UUU, and rps12 is a trans-spliced gene, with its 5′ exon located in the LSC region and its two 3′ exons in the IR regions (Figure S2). The overall gene content and order in C. calophyllum are conserved, showing no differences compared to other Crepidium and Malaxis plastomes (data not shown). Notably, the gene composition is also consistent with that of typical autotrophic angiosperms such as Nicotiana tabacum and Arabidopsis thaliana.

The phylogenetic trees reconstructed using maximum likelihood (ML) and BI methods yielded consistent topologies (Figure 3). C. calophyllum was recovered within a moderately supported clade (UFBoot = 87; PP = 0.72) that included three other Crepidium plastomes, with Dienia ophrydis resolved as its closest sister lineage. Interestingly, the two available M. monophyllos plastomes showed discordant placements: one (GenBank accession NO.: PQ571154) clustered with L. petiolata and L. auriculata, while the other (RefSeq accession NO.: NC_083997), currently listed as the reference genome on NCBI, was placed outside the Malaxidinae clade (Figure 3). Additionally, Liparis species were scattered across multiple clades, indicating polyphyly. One lineage clustered with Crepidium and Dienia, another grouped with Malaxis and Oberonioides, and a third included species from Oberonia and Stichorkis (Figure 3).

Discussion and conclusion

Compared to heterotrophic orchids, C. calophyllum and other species within Crepidium and Malaxis possess structurally conserved plastomes with intact gene complements, reflecting their autotrophic lifestyle and dependence on photosynthesis (Zeng et al. 2024). Most members of the Malaxidinae subtribe are terrestrial and autotrophic (Collobert et al. 2023), and their plastomes generally show high structural conservation (Zeng et al. 2024). In contrast, plastome gene losses—particularly of ndh genes—have been reported only in a few epiphytic or lithophytic taxa, such as Oberonia and certain Liparis species (Zeng et al. 2024), suggesting a potential correlation between ecological habit and plastome stability.

The plastome of C. calophyllum exhibits a closer phylogenetic relationship with Crepidium than with Malaxis (Figure 2), supporting its current generic assignment. However, our phylogenetic analyses also reveal notable inconsistencies within Malaxidinae. The two M. monophyllos plastomes were placed in divergent positions: one grouped with Liparis species, while the other fell entirely outside the Malaxidinae clade. If sequencing or taxonomic misidentification can be excluded, these results strongly suggest that Malaxis is a non-monophyletic genus. Although previous studies have proposed splitting Malaxis into multiple lineages—one aligning with Crepidium and another with Liparis (Ya et al. 2021)—all lineages were nonetheless retained within Malaxidinae.

Likewise, Liparis was found to be polyphyletic, consistent with earlier studies (Li et al. 2020; Kumar et al. 2022). In our analysis, Liparis species were clearly resolved into at least three separate clades (Figure 2). Compared to earlier studies, our results provide clearer evidence of the close phylogenetic relationships between Liparis and several genera including Dienia, Oberonioides, and Stichorkis, raising doubts about current generic boundaries.

Notably, several Malaxidinae genera (e.g. Alatiliparis, Crossoglossa, Crossoliparis, Hammarbya, Hippeophyllum, Orestias, and Tamayorkis) still lack complete plastomes, and even basic molecular data such as ITS or barcode markers remain unavailable for many of them. Comprehensive genomic sampling is therefore critical for resolving taxonomic uncertainties and improving our understanding of phylogenetic relationships within Malaxidinae.
