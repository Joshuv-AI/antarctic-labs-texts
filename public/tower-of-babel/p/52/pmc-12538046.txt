Characteristics and outcomes associated with multiple triage visits among patients with suspected preterm labor or preterm premature rupture of membranes
Authors: Jameaka L Hamilton, Paula L McGee, George R Saade, Rebecca G Clifton, Michael W Varner, Alan TN Tita, Monica Longo, Edward F Bell, Barbara J Stoll, Donna Allard, Ashley Salazar, John M Thorp, Uma M Reddy, Maged M Costantine, Emily S Miller, Dwight J Rouse, Hyagriv N Simhan, Jennifer L Bailit, Lorraine Dugoff, George A Macones, Baha M Sibai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospital-based obstetrical triage units frequently serve as an extension to outpatient care. Evaluation of the burden of preterm birth (PTB) typically focuses on the delivery and neonatal periods, while antepartum health care utilization related to risk of PTB is seldom reported.ObjectiveTo identify the characteristics and outcomes associated with multiple triage visits among patients with suspected preterm labor (PTL) or preterm premature rupture of membranes (pPROM).Study DesignSecondary analysis of a retrospective cohort study of all triage visits from 20 0/7 through 34 6/7 weeks gestation on randomly selected dates in 2019 at 34 hospitals across the US. Those in whom the reason for the visit was suspected PTL or pPROM and who were subsequently discharged were eligible for this analysis. Patients with ≥3 triage visits were compared with those who had <3 visits in the index pregnancy visits. Data were abstracted by certified research personnel using predefined criteria. The primary outcome was a composite of severe neonatal morbidity or mortality before 120 days. Secondary outcomes included PTB (<37 weeks gestation), a composite of respiratory neonatal morbidities, and maternal outcomes. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were used for the maternal outcomes, and generalized linear and logistic models were used for the neonatal outcomes to account for the correlation among twins.ResultsA total of 1764 mother/newborn dyads were included. Patients with more frequent triage visits (≥3) were more likely to self-identify as Black, be younger, not married/living with a partner, unemployed, have government-assisted insurance, or have less than a college degree compared to those individuals with less frequent triage visits (P<.05). Additionally, individuals in the more frequent triage visits group were more likely to be multiparous, have obesity, and pre-existing medical conditions, including kidney or liver disease requiring treatment (P<.05). There were no significant differences between groups for the primary composite neonatal outcome (1.6% in the more frequent group vs 2.4% in the less frequent group, adjusted odds ratios [aOR] 0.69, 95% CI 0.35–1.39) or secondary neonatal outcomes including PTB less than 37 weeks gestation (25.2% vs 23.2%, aOR 0.94, 95% CI 0.68–1.30), composite of respiratory morbidity (11.8% vs 11.0%, aOR 1.01, 95% CI 0.71–1.43), SGA (10.5% vs 10.6%, aOR 0.92, 95% CI 0.66–1.29), or birthweight (3056±639 g vs 3048±678 g, LS mean 27, 95% CI –27, 82). Similarly, there were also no statistically significant differences between groups for the secondary maternal outcomes including composite of severe maternal morbidity (8.1% vs 8.7%, aOR 1.00, 95% CI 0.69–1.44), composite of maternal infection (5.8% vs 5.1%, aOR 1.11, 95% CI 0.72–1.73), or delivery via cesarean section (31.2% vs 35.2%, aOR 0.86, 95% CI 0.69–1.07).ConclusionIn this multisite registry of triage visits with rigorous ascertainment and data collection, multiple visits (≥3) for suspected PTL or pPROM were not associated with differences in maternal or neonatal outcomes.Key words: adverse pregnancy outcomes, obstetric triage utilization, pregnancy, prenatal care

AJOG Global Reports at a Glance

Why was this study conducted?

To evaluate obstetric triage utilization related to antepartum risk of preterm birth in pregnant patients with suspected preterm labor or preterm premature rupture of membranes and its association with adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Key findings

Patients with more frequent triage utilization (≥3 visits) were younger and more likely to be unmarried, Black, obese, unemployed, have preexisting medical conditions requiring treatment, less than a college degree, a history of prior preterm delivery, and government-assisted insurance compared to those with less frequent utilization (<3 visits).

The frequency of obstetric triage visits for suspected preterm labor or preterm premature rupture of membranes was not associated with differences in maternal or neonatal outcomes.

What does this add to what is known?

This study enhances the understanding of obstetric triage unit utilization among pregnant patients with suspected preterm labor or preterm premature rupture of membranes.

Introduction

Hospital-based labor and delivery triage units not only provide emergency services to pregnant patients experiencing acute and critical conditions, but also serve as extensions of outpatient clinics to evaluate common complaints in pregnancy such as threatened preterm labor (PTL), preterm premature rupture of membranes (pPROM), vaginal bleeding, signs and symptoms of preeclampsia, decreased fetal movement, or labor at term.1 Obstetric triage volumes can be high, exceeding overall birth volumes of a hospital by 20% to 50%, and require adequate resource allocation to improve efficiency, reduce length of stay, and improve screening and evaluation.2,3 Several risk assessment tools have been developed and validated to standardize the assessment of the urgency of an obstetric patient’s condition to increase the proportion of high-acuity patients seen in an appropriately urgent fashion.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9

The utilization of obstetric triage units by patients and providers is common in at-risk individuals with suspected PTL or concern for pPROM, with some patients utilizing these units frequently in the course of a pregnancy. These units are often utilized to initially evaluate patients and ultimately stabilize them in preparation of a final disposition, which could include discharge home, transfer to another facility, or admission to an inpatient unit, with the goal to reduce maternal and neonatal morbidities associated with preterm birth (PTB).10,11 The evaluation of the burden of PTB on the US healthcare system has typically focused on the circumstances surrounding delivery and neonatal periods, often excluding the increased health care utilization related to antepartum risk of PTB with triage visits for threatened PTL or suspected pPROM. The objectives of this article are to evaluate factors associated with higher triage resource utilization and to compare maternal and neonatal outcomes among patients who present more frequently (defined in this analysis as ≥3 visits in an index pregnancy) or less frequently (fewer than 3 visits) to triage for suspected PTL or concern for pPROM.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a secondary analysis of data from a retrospective cohort study of pregnant individuals who delivered a singleton or twin gestation at one of 34 Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development Maternal-Fetal Medicine Units (MFMU) Network hospitals across the US following triage evaluation between 20 0/7 and 34 6/7 weeks gestation on randomly selected dates from January through April 2019. Patients were included in the original study if they had a triage evaluation during the study time period for one of four conditions: suspected PTL or pPROM, suspected fetal growth restriction, suspected preeclampsia or gestational hypertension, or antenatal bleeding. Individuals were excluded from the original study if they delivered at a non-MFMU network hospital, participated in an antepartum intervention study where management was determined by a study protocol rather than standard clinical practice for that site, had a twin gestation with delivery of the twins more than 72 hours apart, or had a prior triage visit that already met eligibility criteria. Detailed electronic medical record abstraction was performed by certified research personnel to obtain all clinical data and outcomes prospectively at the time of the original study. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval for the prematurity registry was obtained at each MFMU site. As the current analysis had no interventions and used deidentified patient data collected through the Data Coordinating Center, it was viewed as “minimal risk” and therefore IRB-exempt at our institution.

In this analysis, we included all participants in the original triage cohort study who presented to triage for evaluation of suspected PTL or pPROM at least once, and excluded those who were admitted to the hospital following the index triage visit. Patients were then categorized as those who had more frequent triage visits (≥3 visits) or less frequent (<3 visits) with the goal to identify factors associated with higher triage resource utilization and to compare maternal and neonatal outcomes in these groups. A cutoff of 3 triage visits in an index pregnancy for suspected PTL or pPROM was determined a priori as a clinically meaningful exposure and used in this analysis.

Study outcomes

Differences in sociodemographic characteristics were assessed among patients with more frequent (≥3 visits) vs less frequent (<3 visits) triage utilization for suspected PTL or concern for pPROM. Our primary outcome was a composite of severe neonatal morbidity and mortality defined as any one of the following: fetal or neonatal death, severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia (grade 3),12 intraventricular hemorrhage grades III to IV,13 necrotizing enterocolitis (proven—Bell Stage 2A or greater),14 periventricular leukomalacia, retinopathy of prematurity stage III to V, or proven sepsis (early and late) before 120 days. Secondary neonatal outcomes included spontaneous PTB less than 37 weeks gestation; a respiratory morbidity composite defined as respiratory distress syndrome or transient tachypnea of the newborn, defined clinically; small for gestational age (<10%), and birthweight. Maternal outcomes included a composite of maternal severe morbidity and mortality defined as maternal death, ICU admission, transfusion of one or more blood products, postpartum hemorrhage, maternal sepsis, stroke, or thromboembolism during delivery hospitalization; a maternal infectious composite defined as chorioamnionitis, postpartum endometritis, maternal sepsis, or surgical site infection during delivery hospitalization; and mode of delivery.

Statistical analysis

Research personnel collected information on the mother’s baseline demographic features, medical and social history, pregnancy complications, and subsequent triage visits, in addition to neonatal baseline characteristics, including mode of delivery, gestational age at the triage visit, and estimated fetal weight at the time of the triage visit. A maternal denominator was used to evaluate maternal obstetric characteristics and outcomes, and a neonatal denominator was used to evaluate neonatal characteristics and outcomes. Baseline maternal and fetal/neonatal characteristics of patients with ≥3 or <3 triage visits in the index pregnancy were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon test for continuous variables. For neonatal baseline characteristics, a generalized estimating equation model with an independent covariance structure and a within-subject effect was assessed to compare the groups to account for correlation between twins.

Multivariable analysis was performed to quantify the relationship between the triage visits groups and the primary and secondary outcomes. Logistic regression analyses were used for maternal outcomes, and generalized linear models were used for neonatal outcomes with an unstructured covariance matrix to account for correlation between twins. For the continuous outcome of birthweight, normality was assessed, and the difference in the adjusted least squares means is reported. Models were adjusted a priori for maternal age, delivery BMI (>30 kg/m2), receipt of antenatal corticosteroids, prior preterm delivery, and preexisting medical conditions; and results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS statistical software (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, NC) version 9.4. All tests were two-tailed, and no imputation for missing values was performed.

Results

A total of 1764 pregnant individuals were included in this analysis, with 1110 (62.9%) in the “less frequent triage visits” group and 654 (37.1%) in the “more frequent triage visits” group, representing 1764/3103 (56.8%) of the full triage cohort in the original study. Patients with more frequent triage visits (≥3) were more likely to self-identify as Black, be younger, not married/living with a partner, unemployed, have government-assisted insurance, or have less than a college degree compared to those individuals with less frequent triage visits (P<.05, Table 1). Additionally, individuals in the more frequent triage visits group were more likely to be multiparous, have obesity, and pre-existing medical conditions, including kidney or liver disease requiring treatment (P<.05, Table 1). Overall, individuals in the more frequent triage visits group had a higher number of total triage encounters compared with those in the less frequent visits group (median IQR 4 [3,6] vs 1 [0,2], P<.05; Table 2).

There were statistically significant differences between groups in prior preterm delivery history (P<.001) and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (P=.014), Table 2. There were no statistically significant differences between groups in obstetric or medical characteristics, including the gestational age at the index triage visit, number of prenatal visits, frequency of multifetal gestation, pregestational or gestational diabetes, placental abruption, oligohydramnios, chorioamnionitis, and pPROM. Discussion

Principal findings

This study of a multisite registry of obstetric triage visits for suspected PTL or pPROM analyzed the differences in sociodemographic characteristics and maternal and neonatal outcomes among patients with more frequent (≥3 visits) vs less frequent (<3 visits) triage utilization. We found several factors associated with more frequent triage utilization, including Black race, younger age, marital status, obesity, unemployment, lower education levels with less than a college degree, preexisting medical conditions, a history of prior preterm delivery, a known diagnosis of a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy, and Medicaid insurance status. However, the frequency of triage visits, after excluding those who were admitted during the initial triage visit, was not associated with prespecified maternal or neonatal outcomes.

Results in the context of what is known

Our analysis found several characteristics that were more frequent among patients with higher frequency of obstetric triage visits prior to 35 weeks for suspected PTL or pPROM without any associated differences in adverse maternal or neonatal outcomes. Previous studies reviewing patient demographic, clinical, and healthcare characteristics related to obstetric triage utilization in diverse hospital settings have noted factors associated with more frequent triage use including minoritized race/ethnicity, younger age, multiparity, pre-existing medical conditions, Medicaid insurance status, low income, high-risk pregnancy, and a history of prior PTB.15,17 Of note, prior studies have also shown worse maternal and neonatal outcomes among individuals with higher obstetric triage utilization including increased risk of PTB, low birthweight, and cesarean delivery.15,16 Monti et al17 found that patients with any antepartum emergency department visit experienced an increased risk of severe maternal morbidity, long delivery length of stay, and higher postpartum emergency department use compared to those patients without any antepartum hospital use. In contrast to these studies, we did not find an association between higher triage utilization and adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Clinical implications

There were no differences in adverse maternal or neonatal outcomes seen in our study among patients with multiple obstetric triage visits for suspected PTL or pPROM, suggesting that traditional hospital-based obstetric triage remains an effective means of patient stabilization to avoid risks related to spontaneous PTB. There has been active discussion regarding the development of alternative obstetric care models with more efficient utilization of hospital-based obstetric triage units traditionally managed primarily by registered nurses. Paul et al18 assessed the utility of a certified nurse-midwife-managed model of care in a level III hospital-based obstetric care triage unit and found improved patient satisfaction and reduced length of stay in the triage unit before and after implementation.

The development of an obstetric-specific emergency department (OBED) has also been proposed for specialized assessment of pregnant patients in conjunction with the rise of Obstetric hospitalist programs.19, 20, 21 Emerging data on this model of continuous on-site availability of an OB/GYN specialist with specific focus on L&D suggests improved patient safety outcomes and reduced cesarean delivery rates.22, 23, 24, 25 Additionally, implementation of validated acuity-based screening algorithms in these OBED care delivery spaces, such as the Association of Women’s Health, Obstetric, and Neonatal Nurses’ Maternal-Fetal Triage Index which is endorsed by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, have been shown to reduce adverse events, optimize staffing, and improve OBED space utilization.1,26,27

The use of telehealth triage, which became popular during and following the COVID-19 pandemic, has been associated with comparable health outcomes, enhanced patient satisfaction, safety, and engagement, but the suitability of this model for after-hours obstetric triage to direct more acute pregnant patients to a hospital-based triage unit requires further evaluation.28,29 The development of an after-hours obstetric-specific urgent care clinic may be another alternative care delivery model for further consideration given the promise seen among targeted oncology and ophthalmology specific urgent care clinics in healthcare cost savings, improved patient satisfaction due to reduced wait times, and reduced ED utilization.30,31 For patients identified as more frequent obstetric triage users, policies to ensure these patients have early outpatient referrals to social work to connect them with services to address any unmet social needs and access to regular prenatal care should be implemented to reduce preventable hospital utilization.

Research implications

This study reveals the need for further evaluation of the optimal care site for obstetric triage, as more frequent triage utilization in the current model of care used in our analysis was not associated with differences in adverse maternal or neonatal outcomes for patients presenting for evaluation of suspected PTL or pPROM. A systematic review assessing the use of technology-enhanced health care delivery found that such care was associated with improved obstetrical outcomes, perinatal smoking cessation, breastfeeding, early access to medical abortion services, and a decreased need for high-risk obstetric monitoring office visits, but further studies are needed to guide implementation protocols of this care model into Obstetric practice.32 The Obstetric hospitalist group model has had promising results thus far, but more evidence is needed to assess both the positive and negative attributes of this model of obstetric care.33

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study lies in its inclusion of contemporary data collected from 34 hospitals across the country with well-defined patients from diverse racial, ethnic, and geographic backgrounds enrolled in a multi-site registry to assess adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes related to specific obstetric triage. While prior registries may have been limited by their focus on neonatal outcomes, this registry was primarily designed to assess maternal burden of prematurity. In addition, data were abstracted by trained research personnel using standardized definitions, enhancing internal validity of this analysis. Our study is not without limitations. First, there is potential for selection bias as our analysis was limited to only those patients receiving an obstetric triage visit for suspected pPROM or suspected PTL prior to 35 weeks gestation. This cohort of patients included in our study also had a notably high average PTB rate of 24% in both groups, surpassing the national average of 10%.10 Second, our findings apply only to patients presenting to triage specifically with concerns for suspected PTL or concern for pPROM and therefore may not be generalizable to the experience of patients presenting to triage with other complaints. Third, we excluded patients who were admitted following the index triage visit, who may have been discharged and re-presented to triage for evaluation of suspected PTL or pPROM, which could have impacted the PTB rate seen in our cohort. Fourth, though there was a lack of association between frequency of triage visits and adverse outcomes, we could not assess the utility of various triage interventions, which may have contributed to, or prevented, these outcomes. Finally, this is a secondary analysis of a retrospective cohort study, which does not include a power analysis for the composite outcomes and their components.

Conclusions

In conclusion, using a multisite registry of triage visits, we identified several patient characteristics associated with more frequent triage utilization for common obstetrical complaints during pregnancy. However, we did not find any differences in adverse maternal or fetal/neonatal outcomes in patients with three or more triage visits for suspected PTL or pPROM compared to those with fewer visits. These findings suggest that hospital-based obstetric triage remains an effective model of care for prompt evaluation and patient stabilization to reduce risk of spontaneous PTB.
