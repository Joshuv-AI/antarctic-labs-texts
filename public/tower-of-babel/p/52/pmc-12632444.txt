Biomimetic Carbon‐Based Nanomaterials: From Design Strategies to Next‐Generation Biosensing and Theranostic Applications
Authors: Marzieh Ramezani Farani, Ashkan Zandi, Fatemeh Shojaeian, Mingzhen Zhang, Danial Khorsandi, Fariba Mollarasouli, Iraj Alipourfard, Amin Farahani, Yu Jung Choi, Heemin Kang, Yun Suk Huh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarbon‐based nanomaterials (CBNs), including graphene oxide (GO), carbon nanospheres (CNSs), carbon nitrides (CNs), carbon nanotubes (CNTs), carbon dots (CDs), nanoporous carbon, and nanocomposites, possess exceptional thermal, mechanical, electrical, and optical properties with highly versatile surface chemistries. Their tunable size, shape, and surface functionalities facilitate strong π–π interactions and semiconductor‐like behavior, enabling efficient light absorption and intimate biomolecular interfacing. These attributes have positioned CBNs as leading candidates in biomedical engineering, inspiring biomimetic designs that integrate organic and inorganic functions within unified architectures. This review highlights recent advances in the use of CBNs for next‐generation biosensing and theranostics. Approaches such as physicochemical engineering, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) origami templating, peptide‐ or enzyme‐assisted assembly, polysaccharide anchoring, and lipid modification have enhanced their biocompatibility, selectivity, and catalytic activity. Innovations in fluorescence switching, aptamer–CNT photophysics, and spacer‐controlled energy transfer enable ultra‐sensitive detection of metal ions, metabolites, neurotransmitters, pathogens, drugs, and cancer biomarkers. Furthermore, integrated CBN‐based platforms, including field‐effect transistors and laser‐scribed graphene electrodes, demonstrate capabilities for single‐virus or single‐cell diagnostics and responsive therapeutic intervention. Finally, translational challenges related to scalable synthesis, biosafety, regulatory harmonization, and public acceptance are discussed, and interdisciplinary strategies combining flexible electronics, organ‐on‐chip models, and AI‐guided design to advance clinical translation of CBN‐enabled precision diagnostics and personalized theranostics are proposed.Keywords: biomedical sensing, biomimetic carbon nanomaterials, design strategies, photophysics of aptamers, switching fluorescence

Introduction

Nanotechnology is rapidly evolving across diverse fields, ranging from industrial production to healthcare. With the rapid progress in nanotechnology, nanomaterials have been extremely attractive due to their exceptional physicochemical properties, including size‐dependent effects at the nanoscale, quantum behavior, high reactivity, and large surface area.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] In multidisciplinary fields, particularly those incorporating nanomaterials in biological and biomedical applications, nanotechnology has made significant progress.[ 4 , 5 ] Biomimetic nanotechnology focuses on the fundamental features of living systems and their translation into human applications. The term “biomimetic” derives from the Greek words bios (“life”) and mimetikos (“imitative”).[ 6 ] According to the literature, biocompatible materials are generally classified as either 1) synthetic or 2) biological materials.[ 7 , 8 ] Biomaterials may consist of entire living structures or partial components designed to perform, enhance, or replace natural functions.[ 9 , 10 ] Typically, these biomaterials are designed for medical biotechnology and pharmaceutical applications. Biological materials, shapes, and functions are mostly dependent on nanoscale functions. From the nanoscale to the microscale, to the mesoscale, and eventually to the macroscale, these nanoscale functions are frequently laced with additional components that are beautifully interwoven in hierarchical layers. This is relevant to a number of other domains, including smart materials science, medicine, physics, sensor technologies, and materials science. Biomimetics of nanoscale living system characteristics is very difficult, fascinating, and gratifying.[ 11 , 12 , 13 ]

For example, medical devices made of biomimetic materials are artificial substances that have been created to be biocompatible or biodegradable. Numerous biomimetic medical materials with cutting‐edge properties have been introduced recently due to advances in nanotechnology. Biosensing, bioimaging, and other domains have benefited greatly from the development of biomimetic nanomaterials and have gained increasing attention. For example, free radicals and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generate significant redox‐regulated alterations in a cell environment, which emphasizes the value of biomimetic nanoantioxidants in the biomedical sciences. Redox reactions are crucial in regulating key biological processes, such as cellular signaling. Investigating the effects of elevated levels of free radicals and ROS is particularly compelling, as it may lead to an imbalance between beneficial and harmful compounds, which can lead to severe cellular toxicity.[ 14 ] The researchers are eager to create a novel material type that is stable and long‐lasting for free radical scavenging. In this regard, different carbon‐based substances, such as CNs, CDs, CNTs, and graphene‐based substances, have strong free radical scavenging properties.[ 15 ]

Many CBNs with antioxidant properties have been discovered in recent years to control inappropriate ROS levels in organisms due to their effective catalytic activities, similar to enzymes. Excellent thermal stability, optical characteristics, cheap cost, ease of manufacture, great chemical resistance, biocompatibility, and high electron affinity make CNs stand out. It is also a naturally abundant conjugated organic semiconducting polymer consisting of nitrogen and carbon. As a result, organic synthesis and photocatalysis frequently employ conjugated organic semiconductor polymers. In addition, the doped CNs show enhanced free radical scavenging activity compared with pristine materials.[ 16 , 17 ]

In addition to antioxidant applications, carbon‐based nanoenzymes, such as CD nanozymes, have anti‐inflammatory potential due to their high efficiency in enhancing electron transport for ROS scavenging.[ 18 , 19 ] Furthermore, it has been demonstrated in related research that CDs have the features of selectively activatable enzymatic activity due to the abundance of groups surrounding the sp2 hybrid carbon center. One example is a biocompatible ROS‐scavenging nanozyme created by Dong and colleagues for the treatment of inflammation. This nanozyme efficiently destroyed a wide range of free radicals, such as •OH, O2 −•, and ABTS+•, and reduced the levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, yielding a high anti‐inflammatory activity, through multi‐enzyme activity against ROS.[ 20 ]

CNTs, as another carbon‐based biomimetic substance, have several uses in non‐invasive disease diagnosis, biological contrast agents, and biosensors, owing to their outstanding electrical properties and luminescence capabilities.[ 21 , 22 ] Single‐walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) and multi‐walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) are the two main kinds of CNTs.[ 23 ] SWCNTs are 1D cylinders composed of graphene sheets.[ 24 ] MWCNTs have strong electrical conductivity and are made up of many layers of concentric cylinders. SWCNTs have better structural, mechanical, and optical properties compared to MWCNTs.[ 25 ] The sidewalls or ends of CNTs can be functionalized by covalent or non‐covalent bonding to increase their biocompatibility.[ 26 ]

Besides CNTs, graphene and graphene derivatives have also attracted immense attention for biosensing and biomedical applications owing to their 2D hexagonal carbon lattice structure, high thermal and electrical conductivity, superior mechanical stability, and satisfactory optical properties. These properties make them notably ideal for disease biomarker detection at ultralow concentrations.[ 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 ] To provide a consolidated perspective on the biomedical applications of carbon‐based nanomaterials, we summarized their performance metrics in Table 1 .

In this review, we provide an innovative biomimetic design approach and comprehensive CBN analysis, focusing on theranostics, systems that integrate diagnostic and therapeutic functions into a single nanomaterial platform, an approach increasingly recognized as critical for personalized medicine. This research highlights the significance of tailored nanomaterial features in improving biomedical outcomes by assessing novel biomimetic techniques. Such biomimicry aligns with recent literature that emphasizes nature‐inspired nano‐bioengineered materials for enhanced functionality in clinical settings. Furthermore, this review addresses current challenges and outlines future opportunities, reinforcing the collective efforts within the research community to harness the full potential of CBNs. By synthesizing recent advancements and providing a coherent perspective on biomimetic design and theranostic applications, we aim to bridge existing knowledge gaps and offer practical insights into the translation of CBNs into clinical practices.

Design Strategies

Biomimetic synthesis has emerged as a promising approach for designing and producing functional nanomaterials with tailored properties, particularly in the development of carbon‐based nanomaterials such as graphene, GO, GQDs, carbon quantum dots (CQDs), CNTs, and CDs.[ 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 ] For instance, biomimetic graphene‐hydroxyapatite (HA) materials have been explored for multifunctional applications in biomedicine, demonstrating the potential of biomimetic strategies in diverse fields.[ 57 ] Furthermore, the biomimetic synthesis of carbon nanotube modified poly(dimethylsiloxane) (CNT/PDMS) bilayer structures has shown promise in creating multistimulus responsive actuators for flexible and smart devices, highlighting the potential for biomimetic design in advanced material fabrication.[ 58 ] Additionally, a biomimetic mineralization strategy has been proposed for the synthesis of ferrite quantum dot/graphene heterostructures, showcasing the versatility of biomimetic approaches in material synthesis.[ 59 ] Moreover, biomimetic nanomaterials have been explored for various applications, including photothermal and photodynamic therapy of cancers, electrochemical glucose sensors, and high‐potential electrocatalytic reduction of hydrogen peroxide.[ 60 , 61 , 62 ] Extending this versatility, CQDs, a distinct class of biomimetic carbon‑based nanomaterials, have also attracted significant attention.[ 63 ] CQDs, derived from different carbon sources, have shown promise in antibacterial applications, exhibiting higher quantum yields and the generation of reactive oxygen species under photoexcitation.[ 64 ] Furthermore, functional CQDs have been highlighted for their versatile applications in chemosensing and biosensing, including selective assays of enzyme activity and fluorescent identification of cancer cells and bacteria.[ 65 ] In addition, carbon nanodots have been developed as peroxidase nanozymes for biosensing, showcasing substantial advancements in the design of functional nanoscale materials with biomimetic properties.[ 66 ] These examples underscore the potential of biomimetic design strategies in advancing the synthesis and application of carbon nanomaterials, offering a versatile approach for the development of functional nanomaterials with diverse properties and applications.

Physicochemical Properties of Carbon‐Based Nanomaterials

A unique element in the periodic table, carbon can organize its four valence electrons in sp, sp2, and sp3 hybridization states, allowing for strong covalent bonds and π─π interactions. Carbon atoms can build a variety of structures through these hybridizations, ranging from small molecules to long chains.[ 67 ] CBNs display a mix of features that make them particularly successful in biomedical applications. Their high surface‐to‐volume ratio allows for efficient loading and delivery of drugs, imaging agents, and biomolecules to targeted regions, and provides multiple functionalization opportunities to enhance solubility, stability, and targeted efficacy. Moreover, owing to their robust sp2 hybridized carbon networks, CBNs exhibit exceptional mechanical strength and chemical stability under physiological conditions, maintaining integrity even in challenging biological environments.[ 68 ] Their electrical conductivity and photoluminescence are valuable in biosensing and bioimaging, which require high sensitivity and precision. CBNs interact with biomolecules via π─π stacking, covalent bonding, and ionic interactions, enabling selective targeting and cellular engagement. When functionalized, CBNs like carbon nanotubes and graphene quantum dots exhibit low toxicity and high biocompatibility, making them ideal for in vivo diagnostics, therapy, and regenerative medicine.[ 56 ]

Closely related, GO and its oxidized derivatives have excellent mechanical strength, flexibility, and charge carrier mobility. The amphiphilic nature of GO makes it suitable for both hydrophobic drug loading and dispersion in aqueous conditions. The inclusion of carboxyl and hydroxyl groups improves its hydrophilicity, colloidal stability, and bioconjugation capacity. Other carbon‐based nanostructures, such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs), make excellent biosensing transducers due to their high aspect ratio, huge surface area, and conductivity exceeding 5000 S cm−1. Their sensitivity to surface contacts enables the detection of biomolecules at extremely low concentrations, albeit functionalization is required to improve their solubility and biological compatibility. Beyond tubular structures, spherical carbon allotropes also play a key role in biosensor design.[ 69 ] Fullerenes, including C60, are chemically stable and highly symmetrical molecules with antioxidant and photostable properties. Their solubility improves after functionalization, making them suitable for reactive oxygen species scavenging and tailored drug delivery applications.[ 70 ]

DNA Origami‐Protected Carbon Nanomaterials

DNA origami (DO)‐protected CBNs are a new class of hybrid nanomaterials that have garnered considerable interest because of their distinctive attributes and potential applications in multiple areas, such as biomedicine, electronics, and energy storage.[ 71 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 ] DO is a powerful method for shaping and assembling DNA into specific patterns. The combination of DO and CBNs offers several advantages over traditional methods of creating hybrid nanomaterials. These include improved stability, biocompatibility, and functionalization.[ 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 ] DO acts as a protective layer around the CBNs, preventing their aggregation and improving their solubility in water and other biological fluids.[ 80 ] This property renders them highly suitable for use in biomedical fields, including imaging techniques and drug delivery systems.[ 78 ]

These materials can be designed and programmed to exhibit a variety of functions and properties. One of the key design strategies for DO‐protected CBNs is the precise management of the dimensions, morphology, and surface chemistry of the CBNs.[ 81 ] This makes it possible to rationally design hybrid materials with specific qualities and functions. Another design strategy for DO‐protected CBNs is the use of DO as a scaffold to control the spatial arrangement and orientation of the CBNs (Figure 2A).[ 82 ] This method enables the development of complex hierarchical structures with multiple layers of functionalization. For example, a study demonstrated the fabrication of a DO‐protected antenna array using gold nanoparticles, which exhibited tunable emission wavelength and polarization.[ 83 ] While this work focused on plasmonic nanoparticles, the same design principles could potentially be extended to carbon‐based nanomaterials such as CNTs, opening new avenues for programmable photonic devices based on CBN architectures. In addition to controlling the spatial arrangement and orientation of the CBNs, DO can also be used as a platform for the functionalization of the nanocomposites with various molecules and biomolecules. For example, the attachment of DNA aptamers to the surface of DO has been demonstrated for the specific detection of cancer cells, targeted drug delivery, and sensing applications.[ 84 , 85 , 86 ] Similarly, the covalent attachment of enzymes to DO‐protected GO has been used for the development of biosensing applications.[ 87 , 88 ]

A powerful design strategy involves using DNA as a programmable molecular switch to modulate the chemical and physical properties of DO‑protected CBNs. By integrating programmable DNA elements into the protective scaffold of carbon‐based nanomaterials such as CNTs or graphene, it becomes possible to exert fine‐tuned control over their physicochemical properties. In this approach, dynamic DNA motifs, such as hairpin loops, strand‐displacement domains, or aptamer sequences, act as molecular switches that respond to specific environmental stimuli, including changes in pH, temperature, or the presence of complementary nucleic acid strands or small molecules.[ 79 ] Upon activation, these DNA switches undergo predictable conformational changes, which in turn modulate key properties of the associated CBNs, such as electrical conductivity, fluorescence emission, or surface reactivity.[ 79 ]

The utilization of DNA as molecular switches empowers researchers to achieve fine‐grained and reversible manipulation of CBNs' properties, thereby enabling the development of highly customizable materials for applications in diverse fields, including electronics, sensing technologies, drug delivery systems, and nanotechnology. For example, the hybridization of complementary DNA strands can be utilized to trigger conformational alterations in the DO scaffold, and these changes can impact the characteristics of the CBNs.[ 80 , 82 , 91 , 92 ] This approach has been demonstrated for the control of the fluorescence emission of DO‐protected CNTs.[ 80 , 82 ] In another important example, Sun et al. presented a novel and efficient method for controlling the placement, spacing, and alignment of SWCNTs using DO (Figure 2B).[ 49 ] They employed nanotrenches created from DNA brick crystals to spatially limit the alignment of CNTs through DNA hybridization. This led to the development of the spatially hindered integration of nanotube electronics (SHINE) method for constructing uniformly spaced arrays of CNTs. Using the SHINE method, Zhao et al. demonstrated the integration of nanometer‐scale biomolecular assemblies into large arrays of high‐performance solid‐state electronics. Their model system utilized parallel arrays of semiconducting CNTs assembled using this technique.[ 89 ]

Key to this precise organization is the inherent affinity between DNA and carbon nanotubes. Specifically, the strong π–π stacking interactions between ssDNA bases and the graphitic surface of CNTs not only facilitate individual nanotube solubilization and stabilization in aqueous environments but also ensure their stable integration within the DNA origami framework, making them ideal platforms for targeted drug delivery and biosensing.[ 80 , 93 , 94 ]

Therapeutic agent distribution is another possible use for DO‐protected carbon nanostructures. The DO scaffold can be functionalized with various molecules, such as peptides, aptamers, or antibodies, for the precise delivery of therapeutic drugs or nucleic acids to specific cells or tissues.[ 95 , 96 , 97 ] For example, Li and colleagues reported the development of a DO nanorobot for the targeted transport of anticancer agents to cancerous cells.[ 98 ] The functionalization of the DO scaffold with tumor‐targeting peptides allowed for the selective binding and internalization of the nanocomposites into cancer cells. DO‐protected CBNs represent a promising platform for the development and fabrication of nanocomposites with tailored properties and functions. For example, Pei et al. pioneered a precise arrangement of SWCNTs by covalently attaching DNA strands to their ends and then using DNA origami templates to position each nanotube at specific loci on a 2D DNA raft. This approach not only preserves the conductive sidewalls of the nanotubes but also enables the construction of complex, multilayered architectures. By leveraging programmable DNA hybridization on the origami scaffold, the study achieved ordered SWCNT arrays, demonstrating promising applications in electronics and biomedicine (Figure 2C).[ 90 ] These advancements collectively unlock broad opportunities for innovation across biomedicine, electronics, and materials science. By integrating programmable DNA features into CBN frameworks, whether as scaffolds for structural control or as molecular switches for dynamic responsiveness, researchers can engineer multifunctional nanocomposites tailored to specific applications. DNA origami offers a powerful and programmable platform for organizing CBNs with high precision. By leveraging the structural versatility of DNA, researchers can control the spatial arrangement[ 82 ] and properties of CBNs such as CNTs and graphene. This approach enhances the stability, solubility, and biocompatibility of nanomaterials, enabling their use in advanced applications such as targeted drug delivery, biosensing, and nanoelectronics.[ 76 , 77 , 78 ] The integration of DNA as a molecular switch or scaffold introduces a new level of control, allowing for dynamic and responsive nanomaterials. These advancements highlight the potential of DNA origami to transform the design and functionality of next‐generation biomimetic nanocomposites.

DNAzyme‐Conjugated Carbon Nanomaterials

One possible method for improving the performance of CBNs in a variety of applications, including drug delivery, biosensing, and biomedical imaging, is to conjugate them with useful biomolecules like DNAzymes. DNAzymes, also known as catalytic DNA or deoxyribozymes, are synthetic DNA sequences that possess enzymatic activity, enabling them to catalyze specific chemical reactions.[ 99 ] DNAzyme molecules can be engineered to cleave specific RNA molecules in a sequence‐specific manner.[ 100 , 101 ] They have been used in various applications, including biosensing,[ 102 ] gene regulation,[ 103 , 104 ] and therapeutics.[ 105 , 106 , 107 ] DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs are a promising category of hybrid materials that display unique properties for a wide range of applications. The conjugation of DNAzymes to CBNs results in a hybrid system that leverages the mechanical and electronic stability of the nanomaterial with the catalytic precision of DNAzymes. This integration opens up possibilities for different applications, such as biosensing, targeted drug delivery, and environmental remediation.[ 108 , 109 ]

The design strategies for DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs involve a systematic approach to engineering hybrid materials with enhanced catalytic activity and specific functionalities. Key design considerations include the selection of suitable DNAzymes and CBNs, surface functionalization to enable conjugation, optimization of conjugation chemistry, characterization of the resulting hybrid materials, and incorporation of biocompatible features. By carefully addressing each of these design strategies, researchers can create DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs with tailored properties for a wide range of applications.[ 108 ] DNAzyme selection is a critical aspect of designing DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs. It involves choosing DNAzymes that possess specific catalytic properties suitable for the desired enzymatic reaction. Various factors, including substrate specificity, reaction rate, and stability, are taken into consideration during the selection process. DNAzymes can be engineered through sequence design, allowing for modifications that enhance their catalytic efficiency or expand their substrate range.[ 110 ] Substrate specificity refers to the ability of the DNAzyme to recognize and bind to a specific target substrate molecule. It is crucial to ensure that the selected DNAzyme exhibits high specificity toward the desired substrate while minimizing off‐target effects.

Reaction rate is another important consideration, as it determines the speed at which the enzymatic reaction occurs. DNAzymes with fast reaction rates are desirable for efficient catalysis.[ 111 ] Stability is a key characteristic of DNAzymes, as it ensures their longevity and functionality under various environmental conditions. Stability considerations include resistance to degradation by nucleases, temperature stability, and resistance to chemical denaturation.[ 112 , 113 ] By selecting DNAzymes with high stability, the durability and effectiveness of the DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs can be enhanced. DNAzymes can be engineered through rational sequence design to enhance catalytic efficiency, broaden substrate specificity, or improve overall performance. Such modifications may involve introducing targeted mutations or structural motifs to fine‐tune enzymatic behavior for specific applications. In this regard, Adornetto and colleagues used a general strategy to design nucleic acid catalysts that are triggered by certain binding proteins. The two peroxidase‐like DNAzymes that the authors created to illustrate this strategy bind to either the microphthalmia‐associated transcription factor or the TATA‐binding protein. DNAzymes and ribozymes can be allosterically controlled using this method.[ 114 ]

In the design of DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs, selecting the appropriate CBNs is a crucial step. CNTs and graphene derivatives are commonly chosen due to their distinct attributes, such as a large surface area, high electrical conductivity, and mechanical stability.[ 108 ] The large surface area of CBNs provides ample space for the attachment of DNAzymes, allowing for efficient conjugation and maximizing the catalytic potential of the hybrid material.[ 108 , 115 ] The high surface area also enhances the interaction with target molecules, improving sensitivity and performance in various applications.[ 116 ] The high electrical conductivity of CBNs, including CNTs and graphene derivatives, enables efficient electron transfer during catalytic reactions facilitated by the DNAzymes.[ 107 , 108 , 117 ] This conductivity is advantageous for electrochemical sensing applications, where the catalytic activity of the DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs can be precisely measured or utilized for signal amplification.[ 118 , 119 ]

Mechanical stability is another important property of CBNs. They possess exceptional strength and resilience, allowing them to maintain their structural integrity under various conditions. This stability ensures the robustness of the DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs during handling, conjugation, and subsequent applications, enhancing their overall performance and durability. The specific choice between CNTs and graphene derivatives depends on the requirements of the application. CNTs, with their tubular structure, offer unique properties such as 1D electron transport and selective permeability.[ 120 ] Graphene derivatives, on the other hand, possess a 2D structure with a single layer of carbon atoms, providing excellent electrical conductivity and a large surface area for functionalization.[ 121 , 122 ] Considering these properties, the selection of CBNs is essential for attaining the expected performance and functionality in DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs. A nuanced understanding of how surface area, conductivity, and mechanical integrity influence material performance enables fine‐tuning of DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs for specific tasks, including therapeutics, sensitive biosensing, efficient catalytic reactions, and robust energy‐storage applications.[ 123 , 124 ]

Surface functionalization plays a significant part in the design of DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs. It entails adding particular functional groups to CBNs' surfaces to produce reactive spots where DNAzymes can connect. Commonly used functional groups include carboxyl (─COOH), amino (─NH2), or hydroxyl (─OH) groups.[ 124 , 125 ] The functionalization process begins by modifying the surface of the CBNs to expose or introduce the desired functional groups. Carboxyl groups can be introduced through oxidation or treatment with oxidizing agents like nitric acid or potassium permanganate. Amino groups can be introduced through reactions with amine‐containing compounds, such as 1,3‐diaminopropane or ethylenediamine. Hydroxyl groups can be generated by treating the CBNs with strong acid or base solutions.[ 125 ] Once the functional groups are introduced, they act as anchor points for subsequent conjugation reactions. For instance, carboxyl groups can react with amino groups in DNAzymes through amidation reactions, forming stable amide bonds. Amino groups can also react with carboxyl groups in DNAzymes through amidation reactions.[ 126 ] Hydroxyl groups can undergo various reactions, including etherification or esterification, to enable conjugation with DNAzymes.[ 124 , 127 , 128 ] The selection of specific functional groups depends on the conjugation chemistry employed and the desired properties of the DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs. These functional groups provide reactive sites for the attachment of DNAzymes, ensuring stable and efficient conjugation. The presence of these functional groups also facilitates the subsequent attachment of targeting ligands, polymers, or other molecules, expanding the versatility and functionality of the hybrid materials. Surface functionalization enables the control and customization of the CBN's surface properties. It enables the incorporation of particular functional groups needed for subsequent conjugation reactions, enhancing the stability and functionality of the DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs.[ 124 ] This strategy opens up opportunities for tailoring the properties and interactions of hybrid materials, making them well‐suited for applications in biosensing, drug delivery, and catalysis.[ 124 ]

Conjugation chemistry is another critical step in attaching DNAzymes to the functionalized surface of CBNs. Covalent conjugation methods are typically employed, ensuring stable and long‐lasting attachment.[ 129 ] Among the commonly utilized conjugation reactions are amidation, esterification, and click chemistry.[ 130 , 131 ] Amidation involves the reaction between carboxyl groups present on the surface of the functionalized CBNs and amino groups present on the DNAzyme. This reaction forms stable amide bonds, resulting in a strong attachment between the DNAzyme and the nanomaterial surface. Amidation offers versatility and robustness in conjugation, contributing to the stability and functionality of the DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs.[ 124 , 129 ] Esterification, on the other hand, relies on the reaction between hydroxyl groups on the nanomaterial surface and carboxyl groups on the DNAzyme. This reaction forms ester bonds, providing a covalent linkage between the DNAzyme and the nanomaterial surface. Esterification is widely utilized for conjugating DNAzymes to CBNs functionalized with hydroxyl groups, allowing for effective and stable attachment.[ 124 ]

Click chemistry reactions, particularly azide‐alkyne cycloaddition, offer highly efficient and selective conjugation. This reaction involves the reaction between an azide group, typically present on the nanomaterial surface, and an alkyne group, present on the DNAzyme. The reaction proceeds rapidly and under mild conditions, resulting in a stable triazole linkage. Click chemistry reactions are known for their high yield, bioorthogonality (compatibility with biological systems), and selectivity, making them a valuable tool for DNAzyme conjugation to CBNs.[ 132 , 133 ] The choice of specific conjugation chemistry depends on the functional groups available on both the nanomaterial surface and DNAzyme, as well as the desired characteristics and stability of the formed conjugate. These covalent conjugation methods ensure a strong and enduring attachment between the DNAzyme and the CBNs surface, enabling the efficient transmission of catalytic properties and functionalities. Characterization techniques are important in assessing the properties and confirming the successful conjugation of DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs. Several techniques are commonly employed for this purpose. UV–vis spectroscopy is employed to track variations in the absorption spectra of the hybrid materials.[ 134 , 135 ]

By comparing the spectra before and after conjugation, any shifts or alterations in absorption peaks can indicate the successful attachment of DNAzymes to the CBNs. This technique provides valuable information about the conjugation efficiency and the stability of the hybrid materials.[ 136 ] Fluorescence spectroscopy is employed to examine changes in fluorescence intensity. By labeling either the CBNs or the DNAzymes with fluorescent tags, changes in fluorescence emission can indicate successful conjugation. This approach facilitates the identification and quantification of DNAzyme attachment, providing insights into the hybrid material's characteristics and performance.[ 137 , 138 , 139 , 140 , 141 ] Electrochemical methods are utilized to assess the catalytic activity of DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs. These methods involve measuring the electrochemical response of the hybrid material in the presence of the target substrate or analyte. By monitoring the fluctuations in current or voltage, the catalytic effectiveness of the DNAzyme can be evaluated. Electrochemical techniques offer real‐time and quantitative analysis, allowing for the assessment of catalytic efficiency and sensitivity.[ 108 , 120 , 142 , 143 ]

Imaging approaches such as atomic force microscopy (AFM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) are used to look at the nanostructures and confirm the success of the conjugation process.[ 144 , 145 , 146 , 147 ] TEM provides high‐resolution images, enabling the visualization of the nanomaterial morphology and the presence of DNAzymes attached to the surface.[ 147 , 148 , 149 , 150 ] AFM allows for topographic imaging, giving insights into the surface characteristics and confirming the presence of DNAzymes on the CBNs.[ 151 ] These characterization techniques provide valuable information about the successful conjugation, stability, and performance of DNAzyme‐conjugated CBNs. They make it possible for scientists to measure catalytic activity, verify DNAzyme attachment, gauge conjugation efficacy, and see the nanostructures. By utilizing a blend of these techniques, researchers are able to gain a comprehensive understanding of the properties and behavior of the hybrid materials, aiding in the optimization of their design and performance for various applications.

One promising design strategy for DNAzyme‑conjugated carbon‑based nanomaterials is to employ DNA origami as a programmable template to organize DNAzyme catalytic units in spatially defined patterns on the CBN surface. While direct demonstrations on CNTs or graphene oxide are still forthcoming, analogous systems provide proof of principle: DNA origami has been established as a highly precise scaffold for positioning G‑quadruplex/hemin DNAzymes with controlled density and orientation,[ 152 ] and separately for aligning carbon nanotubes via site‑selective DNA docking sites.[ 153 ] This suggests that DO‑protected CBN assemblies can be engineered where DNAzymes are anchored at predetermined locations on the carbon structure, enabling both catalytic function and nanoscale programmability. Additionally, DNAzymes can be covalently attached to the surface of CNTs via amide or carbodiimide chemistry.[ 123 , 124 , 154 ] Alternatively, DNAzymes can be adsorbed onto GO via π–π stacking interactions and then reduced to graphene.[ 155 ] These approaches have been shown to produce stable conjugates with retained catalytic activity. Another design strategy is to use protein scaffolds as a template for conjugating DNAzymes onto CBNs. For example, CNTs have been functionalized with streptavidin, which can then be used to bind biotinylated DNAzymes.[ 159 , 160 ] This approach allows for the use of a wide range of DNAzymes and provides a highly specific and stable platform for sensing applications.

DNAzyme‐conjugated carbon nanomaterials combine the enzymatic specificity of DNAzymes with the structural and electronic advantages of CBNs, such as carbon nanotubes and graphene. This hybridization allows for precise catalytic activity, robust signal amplification, and tailored sensing platforms. The design process involves selecting suitable DNAzymes with high specificity and stability, functionalizing CBN surfaces with reactive groups, and employing covalent attachment methods like amidation and click chemistry.[ 128 , 131 ] Characterization techniques, including UV–vis, fluorescence, and electrochemical analysis, confirm successful conjugation and activity.[ 134 , 135 ] Notably, spatial control using DNA origami or protein scaffolds enhances targeting accuracy, while DNA‐based molecular switches enable programmable sensing and drug delivery. These versatile materials have demonstrated applications in biosensing, gene silencing (e.g., BCL‐2 mRNA targeting), and molecular diagnostics, showcasing their broad utility in biomedical nanotechnology.

Protein/Peptide–Templated Carbon Nanomaterials

Among different protein templates, bovine serum albumin (BSA) has been widely used for the synthesis of CBNs due to its high biocompatibility, stability, and abundance. Bovine serum albumin, a globular plasma protein, has been effectively used as a green precursor and template for the synthesis of carbon‐based nanomaterials. Hydrothermal carbonization of BSA yields stable, highly luminescent carbon dots with tunable surface chemistry and excellent biocompatibility. This makes BSA an attractive template for designing CBNs with controlled size, morphology, and functionality for sensing or biomedical applications.[ 161 , 162 , 163 , 164 , 165 , 166 ] BSA contains many functional groups, such as amino acids. These serve as sites where carbon structures can start growing or attach.[ 167 ] Additionally, high solubility of BSA in aqueous environments and its surface‐exposed amino acid residues (e.g., histidine, lysine, leucine) provide multiple reactive functional groups, making it particularly suitable as a protein template for biomedical nanomaterials.[ 157 , 168 , 169 , 170 ] These features enable uniform dispersion, strong interactions with carbon precursors, and effective conjugation in biologically relevant conditions.[ 171 , 172 ] Design strategies for protein/peptide‐templated CBNs, specifically using BSA, encompass various approaches to control the synthesis, assembly, and functionalization of these carbon‐based structures. These strategies aim to tailor the properties and functionalities of the resulting nanomaterials for specific applications.[ 120 , 167 , 173 , 174 ]

One design strategy for protein/peptide‐templated CBNs is to modify or functionalize a template to introduce specific functionalities (Figure 3B).[ 157 , 175 , 176 , 177 , 178 ] For example, researchers have utilized BSA modification by incorporating additional chemical groups or molecules onto its surface. One such approach is the conjugation of BSA with hydrophobic molecules, such as fatty acids or lipid moieties.[ 120 ] This chemical alteration enhances the hydrophobicity of the BSA template, allowing for better interactions with carbon precursors during the synthesis of CBNs. By modifying BSA with hydrophobic molecules, the resulting protein/peptide‐templated CBNs exhibit improved dispersibility in nonpolar solvents or hydrophobic environments. This strategy can be particularly advantageous for applications in which hydrophobic interactions or stability in non‐aqueous environments are desired.[ 120 , 179 ] For instance, in the field of nanoelectronics, the hydrophobic BSA‐modified CBNs can be used as building blocks for constructing devices with enhanced charge transport properties or improved compatibility with hydrophobic organic semiconductors.[ 120 ] Moreover, the functionalization of the BSA template can be extended to introduce other desired properties or functionalities. For example, by incorporating functional groups such as amine (─NH2) or carboxyl (─COOH) onto BSA‐derived carbon nanomaterials, researchers enable downstream conjugation with biomolecules, including DNAzymes, antibodies, or targeting ligands, via standard bioconjugation chemistries like EDC/NHS coupling or Schiff base reactions.[ 120 , 180 , 181 , 182 , 183 ] This functionalization strategy enhances the versatility and applicability of the resulting protein/peptide‐templated CBNs. These materials serve as valuable tools for biomedical applications, such as drug delivery systems, biosensors, or tissue engineering scaffolds.

Control over the choice and properties of carbon precursors is a critical design strategy in the synthesis of protein/peptide‐templated CBNs. Carbon precursors refer to the molecules or sources that provide the carbon atoms for the growth of the nanomaterials.[ 120 ] By selecting and manipulating the carbon precursors, researchers can exert control over the size, structure, and properties of the resulting CBNs.[ 184 , 185 ] For example, in the synthesis of protein/peptide‐templated CNTs, researchers can employ carbon precursors such as acetylene, methane, or other hydrocarbon gases. The concentration and flow rate of the carbon precursor gas during the synthesis process can influence the diameter, length, and chirality of the CNTs.[ 186 , 187 , 188 , 189 ]

By precisely controlling these parameters, researchers can obtain CNTs with specific dimensions and structural characteristics for different applications.[ 190 ] Similarly, in the synthesis of protein/peptide‐templated CDs or GQDs, carbon precursors can include carbon‐containing molecules such as glucose, citric acid, or other organic compounds.[ 191 , 192 , 193 , 194 , 195 ] The dimensions, surface functional groups, and optical characteristics of the resulting CDs may all be affected by the choice of carbon precursors and the modification of reaction parameters, including temperature and reaction duration.[ 196 ] These parameters can be tuned to tailor the fluorescence emission wavelength, quantum yield, or stability of the CDs for specific applications in bioimaging, sensing, or optoelectronics.[ 197 ] In addition to the carbon precursors themselves, the presence of protein or peptide templates can also influence the growth and assembly of CBNs. The functional groups and conformations of the template molecules can interact with the carbon precursors and influence their deposition, orientation, or crystal structure. The protein/peptide template can act as a nucleation site or provide steric constraints, leading to the formation of unique CBN structures.[ 198 , 199 ] By carefully selecting and manipulating the carbon precursors, along with the presence of protein/peptide templates, researchers can precisely control the growth process and properties of the resulting protein/peptide‐templated CBNs. These design strategies enable the production of CBNs with tailored dimensions, structures, and properties for various applications, like electronics, energy storage, and biomedical engineering.

One of the design strategies for BSA‐templated CBNs is to control the reaction conditions during synthesis, like pH, temperature, and reaction time. For example, Laptinskiy and colleagues synthesized BSA‐templated CDs by controlling the reaction pH and temperature, which significantly influenced the size, morphology, and luminescence properties of the resulting CDs.[ 200 ] Past investigations have demonstrated the use of BSA as a template for the synthesis of various nanoparticles, highlighting the potential for BSA‐templated CDs.[ 161 , 167 , 201 ] In addition, Guo and coworkers reported a solvothermal synthesis method for Pt(IV)‐coordinated CDs and their BSA complexes, showcasing the versatility of BSA in tailoring CDs with specific properties (Figure 3C).[ 158 ] Furthermore, Zhang's group achieved significant enhancement in multiphoton fluorescence using BSA composites with red emissive CDs, underlining the tunability of luminescence properties in BSA‐templated CD composites.[ 202 ] These studies highlight the potential for controlling the size, morphology, and luminescence properties of CDs through BSA templating, supporting the assertion that reaction pH and temperature play important roles in determining the characteristics of the resulting CDs. Another approach is to modify BSA with functional groups, such as amino or carboxylic groups, to enhance the interactions between BSA and carbon precursors. For instance, Rajasekar et al. reported the synthesis of BSA‐templated GO by modifying BSA with amino groups, which improved the dispersion of GO in aqueous solution.[ 203 ]

In addition to modifying BSA, other strategies involve incorporating additional precursors or additives during the synthesis process. For instance, BSA‐templated CDs can be synthesized by adding glucose as a carbon source and citric acid as a passivation agent.[ 195 , 204 , 205 , 206 , 207 ] In these works, citric acid and glucose improved the optical properties of CD while also increasing carbon yield. In another method, mesoporous carbon materials have been successfully synthesized using protein–phenol–formaldehyde polymer blends, such as gelatin or BSA combined with phenol–formaldehyde resin, as the templates. These hybrid templates govern the resulting carbon architectures, enabling tunable structural features (e.g., pore size, morphology, and surface chemistry). This templating strategy underscores the potential to extend similar design principles toward protein/peptide–templated carbon nanotubes or carbon dots for customized nanomaterial synthesis.[ 208 , 209 ] Furthermore, the design of BSA‐templated CBNs can be achieved by using distinct methods, including hydrothermal, sol‐gel, and microwave‐assisted synthesis methods.[ 210 , 211 , 212 , 213 , 214 , 215 , 216 ] The properties and applications of BSA‐templated CBNs can be further enhanced by functionalization with various molecules such as drugs, proteins, or other nanomaterials.

Protein‐ and peptide‐templated carbon nanomaterials, especially those synthesized using BSA, offer a biologically compatible and structurally versatile route to create functional nanomaterials.[ 176 , 177 , 178 ] BSA serves not only as a structural scaffold but also introduces functional groups that enhance dispersion, biocompatibility, and surface reactivity. By modifying BSA with hydrophobic or reactive groups, researchers can tailor the interface between the protein and carbon precursors, enabling fine control over size, morphology, and optical properties of the resulting nanostructures, such as CDs, GO, and BSA‐Pt complexes. Reaction conditions, such as temperature, pH, and carbon source, play a critical role in dictating the final material characteristics. Additionally, BSA can serve as a nucleation site or structural template, directing the formation of carbon nanomaterialsthrough hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, or covalent attachment. The section highlights a variety of synthesis approaches, including hydrothermal, sol‐gel, and microwave‐assisted methods, along with examples of multifunctional applications ranging from fluorescence imaging to immunogenic cell death for cancer therapy. These design strategies make protein‐templated CBNs highly promising for biomedical, electronic, and catalytic platforms.

Among different types of polysaccharides, chitosan, cellulose, and pectin have been extensively explored as effective anchoring agents for CBNs.[ 218 ] These biocompatible and biodegradable polymers can provide both chemical and physical interactions with the CBNs, resulting in enhanced stability and functionality.[ 218 , 221 , 227 , 228 , 229 , 230 , 231 ] One of the main design strategies for polysaccharide‐anchored CBNs is to control the surface chemistry of the CBNs through the modification of the polysaccharide anchors. Through surface modification, CBNs like CNTs or graphene can be chemically altered with functional groups to improve their compatibility with polysaccharides. Functionalization methods include covalent bonding, non‐covalent interactions, or physical adsorption. Covalent bonding involves forming strong chemical bonds between the nanomaterials and functional groups, providing a stable attachment.[ 174 , 232 , 233 , 234 , 235 , 236 ] Polysaccharides can be covalently attached to CBNs by utilizing reactive functional groups present in both components. For example, amine groups in chitosan can react with carboxylic acid groups on the CBNs, forming stable covalent linkages. This covalent bonding ensures a strong interface between the polysaccharides and CBNs, leading to improved mechanical and chemical stability. Another design strategy involves covalent crosslinking within the polysaccharide matrix itself. Crosslinking agents such as glutaraldehyde and genipin induce inter‑chain covalent bonds, creating robust, 3D polymer networks with enhanced structural stability and functional integrity.[ 237 ] The CBNs can be incorporated into this network during the crosslinking process, resulting in a 3D composite material. Crosslinking strengthens the material's mechanical strength and stability, which qualifies it for uses that call for durability. For non‐covalent interactions to generate a reversible coating, weak forces such as electrostatic forces, van der Waals forces, and hydrogen bonds are necessary.[ 238 , 239 , 240 ]

Physical adsorption relies on weak electrostatic interactions for direct attachment. This modification facilitates the subsequent attachment of polysaccharides and improves the overall stability and dispersibility of the nanomaterials.[ 218 , 241 ] Polysaccharides can form electrostatic interactions with charged CBNs. By controlling the pH level or ionic concentration of the solution, the electrostatic forces between the negatively and positively charged components can be utilized to anchor the polysaccharides onto the CBNs' surface. This method provides a simple and reversible way to form composite materials.[ 242 , 243 , 244 , 245 ] For example, the surface charge of chitosan‐modified CNTs can be modified by varying the level of deacetylation of chitosan (Figure 4B).[ 224 ] In addition, adding functional groups to the cellulose backbone can enhance the dispersion and biocompatibility of CBNs.[ 246 , 247 , 248 , 249 ] Cellulose can be chemically tailored through substitution of its hydroxyl groups with functional moieties, such as specific acids, acid chlorides, or oxidized functionalities (e.g., aldehydes or carboxyls), enabling the development of derivatives with new and enhanced physicochemical properties.[ 249 , 250 , 251 ] Based on these morphological characteristics, cellulose fibers can be transversely dissociated at the amorphous areas, resulting in nanoscale, highly crystalline rod‐like fragments known as cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs).[ 248 ] Pectin has been used as a versatile polysaccharide anchor for CBNs, with the ability to form both electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions.[ 218 , 221 , 231 , 252 ] Hydrogen bonding is a dipole–dipole interaction between a partially positive hydrogen atom and an electronegative atom (e.g., oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, or fluorine) that is not covalently bonded to the hydrogen.[ 253 ]

Another important design strategy for polysaccharide‐anchored CBNs is to optimize the synthesis parameters to achieve desired properties. For instance, the size, shape, and surface charge of chitosan‐coated carbon nanoparticles (CNPs) can be controlled by varying the pH, reaction time, and temperature.[ 253 , 254 , 255 ] The addition of surfactants or co‐solvents can also enhance the stability and dispersibility of cellulose‐functionalized CNTs.[ 256 ] In addition, the use of different types of crosslinkers, such as glutaraldehyde, can improve the stability of pectin‐coated CNPs.[ 257 , 258 , 259 ] Polysaccharide‐anchored CBNs have also shown promising applications in various fields. For example, chitosan‐functionalized CBNs have been used for drug delivery, tissue engineering, and biosensing.[ 260 , 261 , 262 ] Cellulose‐coated CNTs have shown potential for environmental remediation and energy storage.[ 263 , 264 ] Pectin‐modified CNPs have been explored for drug delivery (cancer therapy) and biomedical imaging.[ 231 , 264 , 265 , 266 , 267 ]

Layer‐by‐layer (LbL) assembly is a technique used for the construction of multilayered structures by sequentially depositing alternating layers of oppositely charged materials (Figure 4C).[ 225 , 268 ] In the context of polysaccharide‐anchored CBNs, such as chitosan, cellulose, and pectin, the LbL assembly method can be employed.[ 269 , 270 ] The process begins by immersing the CBNs, which may have been functionalized or modified to possess a particular charge, in a solution containing one of the charged polysaccharides, such as chitosan. Due to electrostatic attractions between the oppositely charged surfaces, the polysaccharide layer adheres to the nanomaterials, forming the first layer. Next, the material is rinsed to remove any unbound or loosely adhered polysaccharide molecules. Then, the substrate is submerged in a solution with the second charged polysaccharide, such as cellulose or pectin, with an opposite charge to that of the first layer. This results in the deposition of another layer onto the previously coated surface. The process can be repeated with additional layers of alternating charged polysaccharides, creating a multilayered structure. The number of layers can be monitored to attain the required thickness and composition of the film. Each layer contributes to the overall properties and functionalities of the composite material.[ 270 ] The LbL assembly technique offers several advantages. It allows for precise control over the layer thickness, composition, and overall structure. This control enables tailoring of the material's features, like mechanical strength, biocompatibility, or permeability, to specific applications. Furthermore, the LbL assembly technique is adaptable and suitable for a range of materials, such as polysaccharides and CBNs.[ 271 , 272 ]

Overall, the design strategies for polysaccharide‐anchored CBNs can be tailored to achieve specific applications, with the potential for further development in the future. Polysaccharide‐anchored CBNs represent a biologically inspired approach for developing multifunctional nanocomposites with improved biocompatibility, structural stability, and application versatility. Natural polysaccharides such as chitosan, cellulose, and pectin serve as anchoring frameworks that interact with CBNs through covalent bonding, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic forces, or physical adsorption.[ 227 , 228 , 229 , 230 ] These interactions enable the formation of stable, functional nanohybrids. Chitosan contributes amine‐based electrostatic interactions and structural integrity for drug delivery and biosensing. Cellulose, often chemically modified, enhances dispersibility and mechanical strength for environmental and energy applications. Pectin provides additional hydrogen bonding capacity useful in biomedical settings. Strategies such as crosslinking and LbL assembly allow for further structural tuning and multilayer film formation. Control over reaction parameters, such as pH, ionic strength, and precursor chemistry, enables precise modulation of nanomaterial properties. These materials have demonstrated potential in tissue engineering, cancer therapy, imaging, and sustainable material design, underscoring their importance in both biomedical and industrial domains.

Lipid‐Modified Carbon Nanomaterials

Lipid modification has emerged as a versatile strategy for enhancing the properties and functionalities of CBNs. By incorporating lipid molecules onto the surface of CBNs, researchers aim to improve biocompatibility, stability, and functionalization capabilities, enabling diverse applications across multiple disciplines. One of the most common strategies for preparing these materials is through the covalent attachment of lipids to the surface of CBNs, such as GO and CNTs.[ 273 , 274 , 275 ] The covalent attachment of lipids to CBNs, such as GO and CNTs, involves a series of chemical reactions. The process begins by functionalizing the nanomaterial surface with appropriate functional groups, which can react with lipid molecules. Coupling agents or crosslinkers, such as N,N'‐dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC), and N‐hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), are commonly employed to activate the functional groups on the nanomaterial surface and facilitate the formation of stable covalent bonds with the lipid molecules. The reaction occurs under specific conditions, such as in organic solvents or aqueous solutions, with factors like temperature, pH, and reaction time optimized for efficient attachment. This covalent attachment results in the formation of stable bonds, ensuring long‐term stability and preventing the detachment or loss of lipid molecules during subsequent applications. Relevant studies, such as the work by Tao's group[ 276 ] on the covalent functionalization of lipids on GO, and the research by Dayani's group on lipid modification of CNTs, provide insights and methodologies for achieving lipid modification on CBNs.[ 275 ] Numerous techniques, such as amidation, esterification, and thiol‐ene chemistry, can be used to link lipids to CBNs.[ 172 , 277 , 278 , 279 , 280 , 281 ] For example, Zhao et al. demonstrated a temperature‐sensitive lipid‐coated CNT system designed for synergistic photothermal therapy (PTT) and gene therapy (GT) (Figure 4D).[ 226 ] This innovative approach utilizes peptide lipid and sucrose laurate coatings to achieve controlled siRNA release, enhancing therapeutic efficacy through temperature‐triggered gene silencing and photothermal effects.

Amidation is a common method used to covalently attach lipids to CBNs.[ 282 , 283 ] This process comprises the reaction between carboxylic acid groups on the nanomaterial surface and amine groups present in the lipid molecules. The reaction is typically mediated by a coupling agent, like DCC and NHS, which activates the carboxylic acid group and facilitates the formation of an amide bond with the lipid's amine group.[ 277 ] Esterification is another approach employed for lipid attachment.[ 284 , 285 ] This reaction occurs between the hydroxyl groups on the lipid molecules and carboxylic acid groups on the nanomaterial surface. In this process, an acid catalyst is often used to promote the esterification reaction, leading to the formation of ester bonds between the lipid and the nanomaterial.[ 284 ] Beyond amidation and esterification, other chemical strategies have been explored to diversify the lipid conjugation toolbox. Thiol‐ene chemistry is a versatile method that involves the reaction between thiol groups present in the lipid molecules and unsaturated carbon‐carbon double bonds on the nanomaterial surface.[ 278 , 286 , 287 , 288 ] This reaction is typically initiated by a photoinitiator or a radical initiator, allowing the formation of thioether bonds between the lipid and the nanomaterial. Based on the particular needs of the research or application, any of these attachment techniques may be used, each offering distinct benefits. Selecting the suitable method is influenced by various factors, including the chemical composition of the lipid molecules, the functional groups present on the nanomaterial's surface, and the required stability and compatibility of the resulting lipid‐modified nanomaterials.[ 289 , 290 ] By utilizing these covalent attachment strategies, researchers can effectively tailor the properties and functions of lipid‐modified CBNs. These modified materials exhibit improved stability, compatibility with biological systems, and functionality, making them valuable for various applications, including drug delivery, biosensing, and biomedical imaging.

Another approach to the design of lipid‐modified CBNs is through the use of self‐assembled monolayers (SAMs). Using non‐covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces, a layer of lipids is adhered to the CBN's surface in this manner.[ 291 , 292 , 293 , 294 ] This method is effective in improving the biocompatibility and stability of CBNs. The integration of lipids with CBNs has opened up opportunities for applications in drug delivery, tissue engineering, biosensing, energy storage, and catalysis. Ongoing research aims to explore new lipid molecules and optimize attachment methods to further advance the field of lipid‐modified CBNs. In the past decade, novel lipid architectures such as dendritic lipids and lipopeptides have emerged as advanced modifiers for nanomaterials. For instance, dendritic guanidinium‐based lipopeptides have been designed to enhance targeting capabilities in liposomal systems, indicating the potential utility of these structures for the surface modification of carbon‐based nanomaterials.[ 295 , 296 , 297 , 298 , 299 ] These new materials offer improved biocompatibility and functionality over traditional lipid

Lipid‐modified CBNs present a promising route for enhancing the biological performance and application flexibility of nanostructures such as graphene oxide and carbon nanotubes.[ 273 , 300 , 301 , 302 ] By covalently attaching lipid molecules via amide, ester, or thiol‐ene chemistry, often employing coupling agents such as DCC and NHS, researchers can significantly improve the stability, dispersibility, and functionalization potential of CBNs in aqueous and biological environments. These modified materials have been successfully applied in areas such as photothermal therapy, gene delivery, and biosensing. An alternative design strategy involves the formation of SAMs, where lipids attach non‐covalently to the CBN surface through van der Waals and hydrogen bonding forces, offering a simpler and reversible functionalization method.[ 293 , 294 ] Recent developments include the integration of dendritic lipids and lipopeptides, which further expand the range of biological and therapeutic applications. Control over surface chemistry, lipid structure, and bonding type enables the fine‐tuning of nanomaterial properties for targeted use. These advances position lipid‐modified CBNs as versatile platforms for future innovation in drug delivery, diagnostics, nanoelectronics, and responsive nanodevices.

Emerging Approaches in Biosensing

In this section, we delve into pioneering methodologies that are reshaping the capabilities of biomimetic CBNs for sensing applications. These approaches represent the forefront of research, introducing innovative techniques that challenge conventional boundaries. By harnessing the unique properties of biomimetic CBNs, researchers are pushing the boundaries of what is possible in sensing technology.

Switching Fluorescence Turn‐On via a Spacer Sequence

Biomimetic CBNs, inspired by natural structures and processes, hold significant promise for sensing applications. One such emerging approach involves utilizing a spacer sequence to achieve switching fluorescence turn‐on. The method entails designing a fluorescent probe with a fluorophore and a quencher separated by the spacer sequence. When the target molecule binds, the spacer is cleaved, releasing the fluorophore. This leads to visible fluorescence.[ 303 , 304 ] This approach allows for the development of sensitive and selective sensing platforms for various target molecules.

Switching fluorescence turn‐on via a spacer sequence offers precise control over the fluorescence output of biomimetic CBNs. By incorporating the spacer sequence, researchers can regulate the fluorescence traits of the nanomaterials.[ 145 ] Changes in the spacer's length, structure, or makeup affect how the fluorophore and quencher interact. These changes can turn the fluorescence on or off. This strategy opens up new possibilities for using biomimetic CBNs in biosensing, imaging, and other research and technology applications, providing greater control over their fluorescence output.

The spacer sequence confers several key advantages to biomimetic CBN‐based fluorescent sensors. First, the spacer sequence offers high molecular specificity. It can be engineered to selectively recognize and respond to a particular molecule or protein, ensuring that the fluorescence of the CBNs is activated only in the presence of the intended target. This high selectivity enhances the accuracy of biosensing applications. Second, the spacer sequence is reversible. The interaction between the spacer sequence and the target molecule or protein can be broken, so the fluorescence of the CBNs can be turned off. This makes it possible to use the CBNs to monitor the concentration of a target molecule or protein over time. Third, the spacer sequence is stable. The interaction between the spacer sequence and the target molecule or protein is relatively stable, so the fluorescence of the CBNs will stay on for a long period of time. This makes it a valuable tool for long‐term sensing applications.[ 305 , 306 , 307 ]

Fluorescence sensing using CNPs encompasses a range of mechanisms, primarily centered around three distinctive processes: photoinduced electron transfer (PET), Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), and the inner filter effect (IFE).[ 308 , 309 ] These mechanisms offer distinct ways to facilitate fluorescence‐based sensing applications with remarkable sensitivity and specificity. We will explain each of these mechanisms in detail to develop a comprehensive understanding of their functionality and significance.[ 310 , 311 , 312 , 313 , 314 ]

In the PET mechanism, the sensing paradigm involves a composite structure comprising a receptor, spacer, and fluorophore. This construct engages in an intriguing electron transfer phenomenon. When irradiated, the receptor relinquishes electrons to the attached fluorophore, leading to a dampening of fluorescence emission, termed the “off‐mode.” However, when an analyte of interest binds to the receptor, this electron transfer process is disrupted, resulting in an augmentation of the fluorophore's emission intensity, triggering the “on‐mode.” This mechanism, hinging on the manipulation of electron transfer dynamics, forms the foundation for designing highly responsive fluorescence sensors, tailored to specific analytes.[ 315 , 316 , 317 ] For instance, Lan and colleagues developed a novel CD‐based fluorescence turn‐on sensor for hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) monitoring in aqueous solutions. The sensor operates via a PET mechanism, with a diphenylphosphine group covalently attached to the surface of the CDs, functioning as both the PET donor and acceptor. The fluorescence of the obtained CDs is quenched through PET, and the addition of H2O2 triggers the release of the diphenylphosphine moiety, leading to fluorescence turn‐on. The sensor has excellent selectivity, sensitivity, and rapid response to H2O2, making it a valuable tool for detecting abnormal levels of ROS in living organisms and various fields of application. The CDs were synthesized using a microwave‐assisted hydrothermal approach, utilizing citric acid and ethylenediamine as starting materials. Their fluorescence characteristics were observed to be responsive to changes in the solvent and the pH of the surrounding medium.[ 318 ]

In an outstanding study, Shekarbeygi and colleagues reported the synthesis of a hybrid nanosystem comprising fluorescent silver (Ag)–CNPs. These nanoparticles were synthesized through a hydrothermal method using a mixture of citric acid, ammonium sulfate, and various metals. This synthesis aimed to create a detection method for gemcitabine, a well‐established chemotherapeutic agent. The interaction between the nanosensor and gemcitabine resulted in the quenching of the sensor's emission, a phenomenon attributed to the PET mechanism. This interaction effectively dampened the emitted fluorescence. Within the concentration range of 0.003 to 0.1 µM, the response demonstrated a linear correlation, showcasing the potential of this nanosystem as a quantitative detection tool. Impressively, the reported limit of detection stood at 0.002 µM, underlining the sensitivity of this approach. It's worth noting that the application scope was expanded beyond the lab environment. The hybrid nanosystem was successfully employed for gemcitabine detection in real samples, specifically human plasma and urine. This extension into real samples underscores the practicality and potential clinical relevance of this innovative nanosensor approach.[ 319 ]

FRET sensors work on a unique basis in which energy is non‐radiatively transferred from a donor fluorophore to an acceptor chromophore. The spectrum overlap between the acceptor's absorption and the donor's emission is a crucial need for FRET. The donor typically exhibits spectral characteristics at higher energy levels (blue‐shifted), contrasted by the acceptor's lower energy absorption spectra (red‐shifted). This energy transfer mechanism enables sensitive probing of molecular interactions and binding events,[ 320 , 321 , 322 ] making FRET‐based CNP sensors a versatile tool for various applications (Figure 5A).[ 323 ] For example, Wang et al. described the advancement of a fluorescent nanoprobe designed for the sensitive detection of hydrogen sulfide (H2S). This fluorescent sensor capitalizes on the integration of CNPs that have been modified with silver nanoparticles (SNPs). This innovative hybrid nanosystem demonstrated a highly linear response in the detection of H2S within a concentration range of 1–1900 nM, exhibiting an impressively low detection limit of 0.4 nM. The CNPs used in the probe were synthesized using tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane. The introduction of SNPs onto the carbon surface led to a quenching of fluorescence through a process known as the FRET mechanism. Notably, the fluorescence of these individual nanoparticles could be efficiently recovered upon interaction with H2S, thereby producing an emission signal attributed to the formation of Ag─S bonds. The proposed sensing mechanism was validated and reinforced by density functional theory (DFT) and time‐dependent DFT (TD‐DFT) experiments. In addition, this innovative sensor showcased its applicability in real‐world scenarios. The detection of H2S was extended to the cerebral system, demonstrating the sensor's capacity to provide valuable insights in complex biological contexts (Figure 5B).[ 324 ]

The IFE introduces another avenue for fluorescence sensing. It results from the fluorescence excitation being attenuated because of an absorbing species that is present in the measurement cuvette. In the primary version of IFE, the excitation or emission energy of the fluorophore is absorbed by a distinct chemical entity, commonly referred to as an absorber. By carefully modulating the concentration of either the absorber or the fluorophore, the sensor's response can be finely tuned to detect specific analytes with exceptional precision.[ 327 ] For example, Li and colleagues introduced a nanosensor tailored for detecting Cr(VI), also known as hexavalent chromium. This innovative sensor was created by synthesizing water‐soluble nitrogen‐doped nanoparticles (N‐CNPs) through the calcination of diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA). The N‐CNPs were engineered to enable the rapid identification of Cr6+ ions, effectively utilizing the phenomenon known as the IFE process to achieve fluorescence quenching. The sensing approach displayed a linear correlation within a concentration range of 0.5–160 µM L−1. Impressively, the calculated detection limit stood at a remarkable 0.15 µM L−1, showcasing the high sensitivity of this nanosensor. Furthermore, the fluorescence intensity could be restored, and the IFE process was halted by the introduction of ascorbic acid. This addition prompted the reduction of Cr6+ to Cr3+, resulting in the revival of fluorescence. In light of these capabilities, the researchers proposed a dual application for this system. Beyond its primary function of detecting chromium ions in water samples, it could also be harnessed for the identification of ascorbic acid in human serum samples. This versatility underscores the potential of the Cr(VI) nanosensor to contribute to analytical chemistry, environmental monitoring, and clinical diagnostics.[ 328 ]

Likewise, a novel carbon nanosphere (CNS)‐based fluorescence aptasensor was introduced for targeted detection of breast cancer cell MCF‐7. The technique identifies MUC1 protein overexpression on the surface of MCF‐7 using FAM‐labeled mucin1 aptamer P0, producing a highly effective and non‐toxic detection approach with a low limit of detection. The fluorescence was engineered using CNSs derived from the carbonization of glucose, which exhibit strong fluorescence quenching capability and minimal cytotoxicity. The CNSs‐based fluorescent nanoprobes were developed to enhance the fluorescence signal from target cells while reducing background fluorescence (Figure 5C).[ 325 ]

On the other hand, Li's group investigated to demonstrate the viability of using CNPs as a fluorescence sensing platform for nucleic acid detection. The technology achieved exceptional selectivity, even down to a single‐base mismatch. The fluorescence has been designed by adsorbing a fluorescein‐based dye onto the CNPs, which can effectively quench the fluorescence emission. The P‐HIV‐CNP complex demonstrates a considerable boost in fluorescence upon incubation with complementary target nucleic acid, which results in fluorescence recovery. The CNPs utilized in this investigation are predominantly spherical, measuring between 25 and 40 nm in size, and possess a zeta potential of −7.19 mV. They are effectively dispersed in a mixture of water and ethanol. This approach holds significant promise for a variety of applications, including gene expression profiling, clinical diagnostics, and therapeutic interventions.[ 329 ]

Because of its hydrophobic nature, achieving dispersion of untreated graphene in water presents a significant challenge. To address this issue, GO emerges as a practical solution for establishing connections with biomolecules. The utilization of GO in DNA‐related optical sensing is particularly compelling, rooted in two pivotal aspects. First, GO exhibits a notable ability to adsorb ssDNA through a compatible affinity, fostering a strong molecular interaction. Second, GO showcases a versatile and highly effective capability to quench fluorescence, bolstering its role as a reliable and comprehensive fluorescence quencher.[ 330 ]

The inherent hydrophobic properties of pristine graphene give rise to difficulties when attempting to disperse it within aqueous environments. This intricate challenge is circumvented through the incorporation of GO, an advanced material that bridges the gap between graphene and biomolecular interactions. A striking advantage of employing GO in the domain of DNA‐centric optical sensing stems from its innate propensity to establish a binding rapport with ssDNA molecules. This interaction is characterized by a tailored affinity that supports robust and specific ssDNA adsorption onto the GO surface, laying the foundation for accurate and responsive molecular recognition. However, GO's significance transcends its role as a mere binding partner. The versatile and wide‐ranging quenching abilities of GO in the realm of fluorescence present a key attribute that enriches its utility in biosensing applications. By acting as a potent fluorescence quencher, GO introduces a dynamic modulation of fluorescence signals, rendering it an invaluable tool for precise signal control. This dual nature of GO, as a proficient ssDNA adsorbent and a proficient fluorescence quencher, underpins its prominence in advancing DNA‐based optical sensing methodologies. In essence, the adoption of GO as an intermediary in the interaction between biomolecules and graphene‐derived materials bridges the divide between hydrophobic graphene and aqueous environments. The synergy of tailored ssDNA binding and potent fluorescence quenching, inherent to GO, empowers its role in DNA‐driven optical sensing endeavors, thereby contributing to enhanced molecular recognition and versatile fluorescence modulation.[ 326 , 331 ]

Recently, Shin and coworkers demonstrated the creation of a GO‐based fluorescent nano‐sensor for highly sensitive miRNA detection, achieved through a strategic design involving a duplex DNA molecular beacon. The sensor's architecture incorporates a shorter upper strand for direct GO binding and a complementary dye‐labeled bottom strand. Through the toehold‐mediated strand displacement (TMSD), an extended single‐stranded region functions as a toehold, starting interactions with the target miRNA and causing the dye‐tagged bottom strand/miRNA duplex to separate from the GO surface. This separation allows fluorescence recovery with heightened sensitivity. The sensor's performance is assessed by monitoring the fluorescence increase of GO‐LMB‐21 in the presence of miR‐21. Initially quenched by FRET due to proximity to GO, the fluorescence intensity of FAM‐labeled LMB‐21′s bottom strand experiences gradual recovery upon miR‐21 addition, stabilizing after 2 h, offering a potent avenue for enhanced miRNA‐based disease diagnostics and research applications.[ 331 ]

Rising antibiotic resistance in infectious diseases underscores the urgency of swift and accurate detection of antibiotic resistance genes. Meanwhile, Kang et al. introduced a pioneering method that capitalizes on programmable DNA‐binding domains known as transcriptional activator‐like effectors (TALEs) and 2D‐nanosheet GO to establish a rapid and sensitive system for antibiotic resistance gene detection. The method avoids the requirement for DNA denaturation by designing TALEs to specifically target particular double‐stranded DNA regions in the tetracycline resistance gene. Adsorbed TALEs close to the GO surface facilitate fluorescence quenching through Förster resonance energy transfer (RET), which is made possible by the employment of GO as an efficient signal quencher in conjunction with QD‐labeled TALEs. TALEs are released from GO, and the fluorescence signal is restored upon conformational changes brought about by binding to the target dsDNA. The system detects minimal dsDNA sequences from the tetM gene within a 10 min incubation, achieving an impressive 1 fM limit of detection for Staphylococcus aureus genomic DNA. The study highlights the potential of TALEs and GO for sensitive, rapid, and direct antibiotic resistance gene detection, circumventing the need for DNA amplification or labeling (Figure 5D).[ 326 ]

Growing interest has been sparked by the urgent requirement for sensitive and selective uranyl ion (UO2 2+) detection in environmental water due to its extreme toxicity and radiotoxicity. While precise UO2 2+ determination is achievable in well‐equipped laboratories, on‐site analysis remains challenging. In response, Cheng and colleagues proposed a smart nanosystem integrating DNAzyme and GO for turn‐on fluorescent UO2 2+ sensing. This innovation capitalizes on DNAzyme's UO2 2+ selectivity, GO's potent fluorescence quenching, and GO's affinity for diverse DNA structures. The DNAzyme/GO nanosystem exhibits exceptional analytical performance with a broad dynamic range (0.1–40 nM), a low limit of detection (25 pM), and remarkable selectivity over other metal ions (MIs). Successful water sample testing underscores its potential for environmental monitoring, addressing the pressing need for portable and accurate UO2 2+ detection.[ 332 ]

The switching fluorescence turn‐on approach leverages spacer sequences to control the spatial relationship between a fluorophore and a quencher, enabling precise and selective detection of target molecules. Upon recognition of the analyte, the spacer is cleaved or undergoes conformational change, restoring fluorescence and producing a measurable signal. This strategy offers high specificity, reversibility, and signal stability, making it suitable for real‐time biosensing applications. Several fluorescence mechanisms underpin this approach, including PET, FRET, and IFE, each offering unique sensitivity and detection capabilities.[ 308 , 309 , 310 , 311 ] Real‐world implementations have demonstrated the detection of hydrogen peroxide, gemcitabine, hydrogen sulfide,[ 324 ] and Cr(VI),[ 328 ] as well as the targeting of cancer cells and viral nucleic acids. Graphene oxide further enhances sensing performance by serving as a universal quencher and ssDNA binder, enabling advanced applications like miRNA and antibiotic resistance gene detection. Altogether, this spacer‐based fluorescence modulation technique offers a robust and adaptable platform for next‐generation biosensing.

Fluorescence Regulation via DNA Template Manipulation

DNA template modification offers a reliable and flexible platform to develop fluorescent probes with tunable sensitivity and selectivity, enabling precise control over signal activation in response to target molecules. Building on this versatility, DNA serves as a structurally programmable scaffold that enables the precise spatial arrangement of fluorophores, facilitating efficient fluorescence signaling upon interaction with specific analytes or environmental changes. Integrating CBNs, such as CDs or GQDs, with DNA templates empowers researchers to achieve fine‐tuned control over fluorescence output by manipulating the structure and properties of the DNA scaffold. In this context, DNA templates not only act as programmable scaffolds but also function as versatile spacers that support the stable attachment and spatial organization of CBNs. This structural control enables precise modulation of fluorescence emissions by influencing interparticle distances and photophysical interactions.[ 333 , 334 ] By carefully manipulating the DNA template, adjusting sequence length or hybridization architecture, researchers can precisely control inter‑CBN distances and intermolecular orientations, thereby modulating interparticle interactions and tuning the fluorescence emission characteristics of carbon‐based nanomaterials (e.g., DNA‐templated carbon dot arrays with defined spacing ≈8 nm).[ 335 ] The fluorescence regulation achieved through DNA template manipulation holds great promise for diverse scientific applications, including biosensing, imaging, and drug delivery, where precise control over fluorescence output is pivotal for accurate detection and analysis.

One instance of controlling fluorescence by DNA template alteration is the application of DNA‐templated silver nanoclusters (AgNCs) as fluorescent probes. These nanoclusters can be produced on DNA templates, and by modifying the DNA sequence or the surrounding environment, their fluorescence properties can be adjusted.[ 336 , 337 ] AgNCs have emerged as novel fluorescent labels with applications in biomedical imaging and biosensing. By utilizing the high affinity between cytosine (C) in DNA bases and Ag+, fluorescent AgNCs can be formed through a reduction reaction, making DNA/AgNCs scaffolds advantageous due to their ease of fabrication, lower cost, reduced toxicity, and biocompatibility.[ 336 , 337 , 338 , 339 ] Moreover, through interactions with neighboring DNA strands or hybridization with complementary DNA strands, the fluorescence emission of DNA/AgNC can be tuned over a wide range, turned on or off, or even changed in color. This makes it a highly versatile and easily adaptable sensor for miRNA detection in clinical applications.[ 340 , 341 , 342 , 343 ] For instance, a new DNA/AgNC‐cDNA probe was designed by Fang and coworkers to detect miR‐223, a potential biomarker for inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), using a fluorescence‐enhancing (turn‐on) strategy. The probe showed improved sensitivity (10 times) and excellent specificity compared to traditional fluorescence quenching (turn‐off) approaches. The mechanism of the probe involved the competitive binding of miR‐223 to the cDNA, leading to the release of the DNA/AgNC and an increase in fluorescence, addressing the limitations of current probes for miRNA detection in the diagnosis of miR‐223‐associated diseases like IBD.[ 344 ]

Another approach is the use of DNA‐templated QDs as fluorescent probes. QDs are semiconductor nanocrystals that exhibit strong and stable fluorescence and can be conjugated to DNA to form QD‐DNA hybrids. DNA‐templated QDs are a type of QD that is synthesized using DNA as a template. They have unique attributes that render them suitable for use as fluorescent probes in biological sensing applications. These properties include high stability, long‐term fluorescence, and the ability to detect multiple signals simultaneously. QDs can be conjugated with antibodies and other biomolecules to create new reagents for fluoroimmunoassay. QD‐based biosensors are made more sensitive and specific by utilizing energy transfer processes such as FRET, chemiluminescence resonance energy transfer (CRET), and bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET).[ 345 , 346 , 347 ]

One example involves using QDs as fluorescent probes to detect specific DNA sequences. QDs can be functionalized with single‐stranded DNA that is complementary to the target sequence. In the sensing setup, a fluorophore‐labeled DNA strand is hybridized to the QD‐conjugated probe, bringing the fluorophore into proximity with the QD. This proximity enables FRET from the QD to the fluorophore, leading to fluorescence quenching. When the target DNA is introduced, it hybridizes with the QD‐bound strand, displacing the labeled strand and disrupting the FRET interaction. This results in a measurable fluorescence recovery, indicating the presence of the target DNA.[ 348 , 349 , 350 , 351 ] Another example is the use of QDs as fluorescent probes for the detection of proteins or biomolecules. Target protein or biomolecule‐binding ligands or antibodies can be used to functionalize QDs. Upon binding, the QD‐protein or QD‐biomolecule complex exhibits a change in fluorescence intensity or wavelength, which can be identified and quantified. There is also some potential use of QDs as fluorescent probes for the detection of pathogens, environmental pollutants, and other analytes.[ 352 ]

Moreover, DNA aptamers can be used as molecular recognition elements to selectively bind to specific target molecules and also serve as a template to regulate the fluorescence of certain molecules or materials. Aptamers are widely recognized as versatile chemical entities with extensive applications in biosensing. They possess programmable functional sites that exhibit structural and conformational transitions upon analyte binding. These dynamic changes can be exploited as readout signals, manifesting as fluorescence on/off modes or colorimetric responses.[ 353 , 354 , 355 , 356 , 357 , 358 , 359 , 360 ] An aptasensor based on FRET was developed by Liu et al. consisting of an ATP aptamer and its complementary sequence on the surface of QDs. The proximity between Cy3 and the QDs allowed for RET, resulting in fluorescence signals at 525 nm (QDs) and 560 nm (Cy3). The addition of ATP disrupted the duplex formation, leading to an increase in the distance between Cy3 and the QDs and a loss of the RET signal. Even in the presence of other nucleotide triphosphates, the method's excellent sensitivity (detection limit of 0.1 nM) and selectivity enable the measurement of ATP, exhibiting its simplicity and specificity.[ 361 ] In addition, it was also reported on a novel method for the selective detection of ATP using a fluorescence sensor based on the zeolite‐imidazolate framework‐67 (ZIF‐67). The system demonstrated a ′turn‐off‐on response″ where the fluorescence of FAM (6‐carboxyfluorescein)‐tagged DNA was quenched upon binding to ZIF‐67. The addition of ATP competed with ZIF‐67, leading to the removal of FAM‐DNA from the surface and resulting in a significant fluorescence recovery (turn‐on) up to 24‐fold. Notably, other phosphate anions did not induce such fluorescence enhancement. The sensor exhibits a high selectivity for ATP detection, with a detection limit of 29 nM, even in the presence of serum, highlighting the importance of careful material design for achieving ultrahigh selectivity in molecular recognition.[ 362 ] In a recent study by Kim et al., DNA‐wrapped single‐walled CNTs were developed as nanosensors to monitor autophagy‐associated lysosomal acidification in vivo.[ 363 ] These sensors exploit the near‐infrared optical properties of organic color centers covalently attached to the CNTs. The sensors' emission band alters in response to local pH differences after they are localized to lysosomes, allowing for the spatial, dynamic, and quantitative mapping of minute lysosomal pH alterations. This application underscores the utility of DNA‐templated CBNs in real‐time, high‐resolution biomedical monitoring and diagnostics (Figure 6A).[ 363 ]

In an interesting study, Shamsipur and colleagues presented a novel approach to biosensing using DNA‐stabilized fluorescent AgNCs and GO. The researchers designed and synthesized DNA aptamers for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and cytochrome c, and used them to create fluorescent DNA‐stabilized AgNCs. They then formed nanohybrids with GO to enhance the fluorescence intensity of the nanoclusters. The resulting biosensor showed high sensitivity, specificity, and ease of use for label‐free detection of ATP and cytochrome c in cell lysates in less than 20 min, using only 2 µL of the cell lysate. This approach offers great opportunities for the high‐sensitivity identification of diverse biological molecules and biomarkers within clinical and biological samples.[ 365 ]

Similar to DNA templates, RNA templates offer a versatile platform for controlling the fluorescence properties of molecules or materials. RNA aptamers, in particular, have been developed to regulate the fluorescence of small organic molecules. Upon binding to the aptamer, the molecule undergoes conformational changes that can either enhance or suppress its fluorescence. This demonstrates the potential of RNA templates in modulating fluorescence and expanding the toolbox for fluorescence regulation.[ 366 , 367 , 368 , 369 ] To dive more deeply into the area, a study by Jepsen and colleagues developed an RNA aptamer‐based FRET system called apta‐FRET, which enables the detection of conformational changes using fluorescent RNA aptamers. The system utilizes ssRNA origami scaffolds, where the aptamers are positioned closely together, and a new fluorophore is synthesized to enhance spectral overlap. The researchers successfully established that the system is genetically encodable and that RNA nanostructures fold correctly by expressing apta‐FRET constructs in Escherichia coli. This suggests potential uses as ratiometric sensors for real‐time experiments in cell and synthetic biology.[ 370 ]

Overall, fluorescence regulation via DNA template manipulation is an emerging approach for developing highly selective and sensitive fluorescent probes. By using DNA or RNA templates, the fluorescence properties of certain molecules or materials can be regulated and tuned for specific detection applications. This approach has great potential for developing new diagnostic tools for medical and environmental monitoring purposes, as well as for fundamental studies in biophysics and chemistry.

Photophysics of Aptamer–Carbon Nanotubes Complexes

The photophysics of aptamer–CNT complexes encompasses the intricate interplay of fluorescence quenching and enhancement phenomena. Fluorescence quenching arises as a result of non‐radiative energy transfer processes, including FRET and charge transfer, between the aptamer and the CNT. Proximity within a few nanometers enables FRET, leading to the transfer of excitation energy from the CNT to the aptamer, thereby reducing CNT fluorescence. Charge transfer results in a charge‐separated state and a decrease in the radiative recombination process, leading to quenching. In contrast to quenching, fluorescence enhancement arises when aptamer binding induces changes in the local photonic environment of the CNT, such as altered surface states or electronic coupling, which promote radiative recombination and intensify emission.[ 371 , 372 ] These alterations, caused by purification effects, localized electronic states, efficient electronic coupling, and charge transfer states, promote radiative relaxation processes and intensify fluorescence emission. Both quenching and enhancement responses depend on the spatial arrangement, electronic properties, energy level alignment, and specific interactions between the aptamer and the CNT. This intricate interdependence necessitates meticulous experimental and theoretical investigations, holding promise for tailored biosensing and nanophotonic applications.[ 321 , 373 , 374 , 375 , 376 , 377 ]

The photophysics of aptamer–CNT complexes has emerged as a promising field for developing highly sensitive and selective biosensors, leveraging the unique electronic and optical properties of CNTs that make them attractive for optical biosensors, and more.[ 190 , 371 , 378 , 379 , 380 , 381 , 382 , 383 , 384 ] The incorporation of aptamers as recognition components in CNT‐based biosensors offers advantages over traditional antibodies, such as low cost, ease of synthesis, and high stability.[ 385 , 386 ] Here, we reviewed recent approaches in the photophysics of aptamer–CNT complexes. One emerging approach in the photophysics of aptamer–CNT complexes is the use of time‐resolved spectroscopy techniques to study the interaction between aptamers and CNTs.[ 387 , 388 ] This technique allows the identification of the energy transfer process between the CNT and the aptamer, which is critical for the design of sensitive and selective biosensors. For example, fluorescence anisotropy has been employed to investigate the binding mechanism of the thrombin‐binding aptamer (TBA) with its target protein, providing valuable insights into aptamer–protein interaction dynamics. While this approach has not yet been extensively explored for aptamer–SWCNT systems, its principles could be adapted to study the photophysical behavior of such complexes.[ 389 , 390 ]

A further application of the photophysics of aptamer–CNT complexes is near‐infrared fluorescence imaging for bioimaging purposes. Because of their remarkable optical and electrical characteristics, CNTs are great options for near‐infrared fluorescence imaging.[ 391 , 392 ] Recent studies have shown that aptamer–CNT complexes can be used as contrast agents for bioimaging, as they exhibit strong near‐infrared fluorescence.[ 84 , 85 , 393 , 394 , 395 ] In addition, these complexes have high biocompatibility and low toxicity, making them attractive for in vivo applications.[ 394 ] In a significant advancement in the field of aptamer–CNT complexes, Lee et al. demonstrated the use of carbon‐nanotube‐based single‐molecule field‐effect transistors (smFETs) to dynamically monitor aptamer‐ligand interactions at the single‐molecule level (Figure 6B).[ 364 ] This study highlights the innovative application of DNA aptamers attached to CNTs to detect and quantify serotonin. It also demonstrates real‐time shape changes in the aptamers. The integration of smFETs allowed for the detection of discrete changes in electrical conductance caused by the binding of serotonin to the aptamer, which led to significant shifts in the conformational state of the aptamer. These findings offer deeper insights into the photophysical dynamics of aptamer‐CNT complexes, underscoring the potential of such technologies in enhancing the sensitivity and specificity of biomolecular detection in nanoscale devices.[ 364 ]

Surface‐enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) is another emerging approach in the photophysics of aptamer‐CNT complexes. SERS is a powerful technique for detecting and analyzing biomolecules, and CNTs have been shown to enhance the SERS signal of aptamers.[ 396 , 397 ] For example, Zou et al. presented a strategy for efficient cancer cell recognition using SERS technology. They designed SERS‐encoded nanoparticles (NPs) by synthesizing isotopic cellular Raman‐silent graphene–isolated‐Au‐nanocrystals (GIANs) and attaching phospholipid‐polyethylene glycol‐linked aptamers to target overexpressed proteins on cancer cell surfaces. GIANs exhibit stable optical properties and strong Raman signals due to their graphitic nature. The Raman shift of GIANs' 2D‐band is controlled by isotopic modification, maintaining molecular structure. These isotopic GIAN‐aptamer‐encoders enable multiplexed Raman imaging in vitro and in vivo, providing low background interference. This approach demonstrates potential for efficient cancer cell identification and diagnosis, with simplified fingerprint spectra.[ 398 ]

Aptamer–CNT complexes have also been used in electrochemical biosensors. Recent investigations have shown the application of aptamer–CNT complexes for the detection of a variety of targets, including ATP, dopamine,[ 399 ] and thrombin.[ 400 ] In these biosensors, the binding of the target analyte to the aptamer–CNT complex results in a change in the electrochemical signal, which can be quantified. Finally, the development of hybrid materials, such as GO‐CNT–aptamer hybrids, has emerged as an approach in the photophysics of aptamer–CNT complexes. These hybrids combine the unique properties of CNTs and GO, and can be used for various applications, such as biosensing, drug delivery, and tissue engineering. For example, a recent study demonstrated the application of a GO–CNT‐aptamer hybrid for the detection of interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), a cytokine that is associated with various diseases (Figure 6C).[ 11 ] The hybrid exhibited high sensitivity and selectivity for IL‐6 detection, making it a promising biosensor for clinical applications.

Aptamer–CNT complexes offer a powerful platform for optical biosensing by coupling the molecular recognition capabilities of aptamers with the unique photophysical properties of CNTs. These hybrid systems operate rimarily through mechanisms such as FRET and photoinduced charge transfer, wherein the binding of a target molecule induces aptamer conformational changes that modulate fluorescence output. The efficiency of this modulation is influenced by multiple factors, including CNT structural characteristics (e.g., diameter and chirality), surface functionalization, and environmental conditions such as pH and ionic strength. These complexes provide label‐free, highly sensitive detection capabilities and enable real‐time monitoring, making them highly valuable in biomedical diagnostics, environmental sensing, and molecular imaging. Ongoing research is focused on deepening the understanding of photophysical behavior and fine‐tuning aptamer‐CNT interactions to enhance performance in targeted sensing applications.

Biosensors Applications

A pioneering platform for the creation of extremely sensitive and selective biosensors is the use of biomimetic CBNs.[ 401 ] These materials have special qualities and functions that make them incredibly well‐suited for a variety of sensing applications. They are inspired by the complex patterns seen in nature.[ 402 , 403 ] Biomimetic CBN‐based biosensors excel in detecting MIs,[ 404 , 405 , 406 ] neurotransmitters,[ 407 , 408 ] pathogenic microbes,[ 409 ] pharmaceutical compounds,[ 410 ] and cancer biomarkers[ 411 ] with enhanced precision. These tools are essential for refined diagnostics,[ 412 ] environmental monitoring,[ 413 ] and medical research advancements.[ 414 ]

Metal‐Ions Sensors

Metal ions (MIs) include heavy elements typically characterized by atomic weights above 63.5 g mol−1 and a density greater than 5 g cm−3. These can follow various biological routes, leading to toxicity in animals and humans and long‐term ecosystem damage.[ 415 ] Key MIs, like mercury, copper, lead, cadmium, chromium, thallium, and zinc, are recognized as notable environmental pollutants.[ 416 ] As a result, international bodies, including the European Medicines Agency (EMA), World Health Organization (WHO), European Environment Agency (EEA), and the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), have set strict regulations on their maximum permissible concentrations.[ 417 ]

For the last ten years, the field of bio‐ and biomimetic electrochemical sensors for MI monitoring has advanced significantly.[ 418 ] These sensors rely on either biological or biomimetic recognition elements, such as enzymes,[ 419 ] antibodies,[ 420 ] aptamers,[ 421 ] or molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs),[ 422 ] which are positioned close to the electrode surface. When the target ions are present, these receptors detect changes on the surface, making it easier to monitor target molecules compared with detection in bulk solution.[ 423 ] These sensors are noted for their sensitivity, accuracy, and cost‐effectiveness.[ 424 ] Consequently, they are well‐suited for on‐site monitoring of various environmental pollutants.[ 425 ] The surge in interest in these sensors is due to advancements in recognizing suitable receptors, in understanding their interactions, and in developing tools like screen‐printed electrodes, miniaturized instruments, and portable potentiostats.[ 426 ] Moreover, CBNs play a pivotal role by providing high surface area, excellent electrical conductivity, and facile surface functionalization, thereby significantly improving the sensitivity and selectivity of metal‐ion biosensors.[ 405 ] Table 2 provides a summary of applications of CBNs in biomimetic biosensors for detecting MIs.

Additionally, in a recent investigation, a unique and effective approach was established to fabricate a composite of nickel cobaltite (NiCo2O4) and a SWCNT.[ 437 ] This composite was specifically engineered for the electrochemical detection of the widely observed pesticide, paraoxon‐ethyl (PEL), to achieve ultra‐sensitive detection thresholds in the picomolar range (Figure 7B).[ 437 ] Through techniques such as Raman spectroscopy and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy, a pronounced electronic interaction between the composite components was confirmed. Interestingly, when compared to the standalone NiCo2O4, the NiCo2O4‐SWCNT‐modified glassy carbon electrode (GCE) displayed a significantly reduced detection limit (2.03 pM). This performance not only exceeded that of the sole NiCo2O4 (33.7 nM) but also surpassed the capabilities of many other metal‐oxide‐based composites documented previously. The inherent PEL sensing capability of this composite was attributed to the robust coordination between its components. Furthermore, the sensor was observed to maintain impressive stability and reproducibility, showing potential for simultaneous detection of PEL and dopamine in real‐time samples, such as cabbage and banana.

In another study, the creation of a selective sensor specifically for a particular MI was explored, acknowledging the challenges associated with specificity.[ 438 ] Polymeric membrane electrodes (PMEs), renowned for their adaptability and analytical simplicity, are often employed in environmental studies. A pioneering PME was introduced, aimed at detecting bismuth ions with heightened sensitivity. Breaking conventional norms, GFP was utilized as an ionophore in crafting the PME electrodes (Figure 7C). Recognized for its biocompatibility, GFP has seen widespread application in fields like bioimaging, sensors, and optical devices, due to its distinct benefits. The research embarked on formulating a high‐efficiency PME through the solvent casting technique, amalgamating GFP‐infused elastomeric MWCNTs within a conductive plasticized polyvinyl chloride (PVC) matrix. With GFP's inherent metal‐binding propensity, it presented as a potential ionophore within the PME framework. The nuanced structures of the resultant GFP‐integrated MWCNT were elucidated using HRTEM. Notably, the electrode manifested a Nernstian response with a gradient of 19 ± 1 mV per decade and showcased a commendably low detection threshold of 4.3 × 10−6 M. Furthermore, the response time of the electrode was clocked at a swift 40–45 s.

The advances in biomimetic CBNs have provided remarkable strides in the detection and monitoring of MIs; however, their versatility extends further. Despite environmental concerns and MI pollutants, these nanomaterials also serve a fundamental function in the medical and health sectors. With their intricate designs and nano‐scaled features, they are increasingly being harnessed to detect and analyze small biomolecules, substances critical for understanding biological systems and diagnosing diseases.

While these analytical tools show great potential, consistent monitoring of MI levels in the environment and food remains challenging.[ 439 ] Biomimetic CBN sensors face numerous challenges, such as low selectivity due to interference from coexisting ions in highly complex matrices, which often necessitates extensive sample pretreatment;[ 440 , 441 ] batch‐to‐batch variability in nanomaterials synthesis and functionalizationreduces reproducibility and hinders performance standardization.[ 442 ] Surface biofouling and compromised long‐term stability on actual samples can lead to progressive degradation of sensor response over time, necessitating periodic calibration or antifouling.[ 443 ] Furthermore, the Debye length constraint in high‐ionic‐strength media restricts signal transduction efficacy to a few nanometers of the electrode surface.[ 444 , 445 ] Finally, most platforms rely on complex multi‐step fabrication processes, which impede scalability and increase production costs, preventing commercial translation.[ 446 , 447 ]

Detection of Small Biomolecules

Small biomolecules refer to low molecular weight organic compounds that play critical roles in the biochemistry of living organisms.[ 448 ] Examples include nucleotides, amino acids, simple sugars, fatty acids, vitamins, and hormones such as thyroid and steroid hormones.[ 448 ] Detecting small biomolecules is of principal importance because of their crucial roles in various physiological, pathological, and environmental processes.[ 449 ] Their concentrations and activities can provide important information about the state of health, disease progression, or environmental contamination, among others.

Biomimetic CBNs have many applications in sensing small biomolecules, spanning medical diagnostics, pharmaceuticals, and food science. For instance, graphene‐based materials are fundamental in diabetic care,[ 450 ] owing to their high sensitivity in detecting glucose levels.[ 451 ] CNTs find relevance in biomedical research, particularly in translating cellular signaling through the detection of nitric oxide (NO).[ 452 ] Fullerene derivatives play a role in monitoring oxidative stress by sensing hydrogen peroxide in biological systems.[ 453 ] GO has been employed to gauge the content of ascorbic acid,[ 454 ] vital for determining vitamin C concentrations in various products. Meanwhile, carbon QDs (CQDs) are at the forefront of neuroscience, especially in research related to dopamine levels,[ 455 ] which are crucial in understanding conditions like Parkinson's disease. Recent advancements in CBN‐based sensors include sophisticated functionalities for small biomolecule detection. These novel developments, often marrying the unique attributes of various carbon structures with innovative biochemical techniques, showcase promising directions in biosensing. One particularly intriguing example is the utilization of hemin‐SWCNTs in colorimetric nanosensing, which leverages the unique characteristics of ssDNA‐specific nuclease (S1) and peptide nucleic acid (PNA) for efficient and label‐free SNP identification. These hemin‐SWCNTs showcased inherent peroxidase‐like activity, providing a foundation for the sensor's functionality (Figure 8A).[ 461 ] PNA, an artificial DNA analog known for its remarkable resistance to enzymatic breakdown, effectively shielded perfectly matched DNA within DNA/PNA pairings from nuclease degradation. On the other hand, when mismatches occurred in the DNA/PNA complexes, the DNA was observed to fragment into smaller segments. This differential behavior was visually manifested through a distinctive color transformation in the TMB/H2O2 system, a change that was attributed to the varying reactions of the hemin‐SWCNTs when interacting with ssPNA or the DNA/PNA complex. Notably, this approach, as described by Xu et al. was adept at distinguishing a variety of single‐base mismatches. It sidestepped the requirement for complex equipment or the need for intricate probes or nanomaterial modifications. Its promising results when analyzing standard cellular extracts and PCR products from standard cell cultures hint at its potential in broader bioanalytical applications and biosensing technologies.[ 461 ]

In a study by Jain and colleagues, an innovative electrochemical biosensor was developed using MWCNTs embedded with nickel nanoparticles (NiNPs) and anandamide (AEA) molecularly imprinted polymer.[ 476 ] The synthesized NiNPs were combined with MWCNTs and an MIP, resulting in enhanced sensitivity and selectivity of the sensor. A distinct method was adopted by Sacramento et al., where a novel biomimetic material was created, reminiscent of an antibody.[ 477 ] The objective was to develop a composite imprinted material, previously unexplored, incorporating highly conductive support nanostructures and a conductive polymeric layer produced at a low temperature. ACh was chosen as the target analyte, given its significance in conditions like Alzheimer's disease. The biomimetic material was crafted through bulk polymerization, introducing ACh into a composite matrix of MWCNTs and aniline (ANI). The resultant polymerization produced a polyaniline (PANI) structure, which served as the composite's physical support. Characterization of these materials was undertaken using techniques such as RAMAN and FTIR, TEM, and SEM.[ 477 ] The capabilities of this biomaterial to bind ACh were ascertained by incorporating it into a PVC/plasticizer mix. Membranes containing the imprinted material and an anionic additive showcased superior analytical attributes, registering a sensitivity of 83.86 mV per decade and a detection limit of 3.45 × 10−5 mol L−1 in HEPES buffer pH 4.0. Noteworthy selectivity against substances like creatinine, creatine, glucose, cysteine, and urea was recorded. The applicability of these electrodes was further demonstrated on synthetic serum samples, proving their potential as a tool for ACh detection in such samples (Figure 9B).[ 477 ]

Shifting the focus to the detection of dopamine, a neurotransmitter closely linked to neurological disorders like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, a study by Shin and coworkers highlighted the limitations of traditionally used metal materials like gold and platinum in electrochemical detection methods.[ 478 ] A novel biosensor was designed after it was realized that limited sensitivity and linearity at low dopamine concentrations posed issues. An indium tin oxide (ITO) electrode was changed by adding SNPs to its surface for the first time. GO was then electrochemically deposited on top of the modified surface. The outcome was a biosensor that showcased amplified electrochemical signals at low dopamine concentrations, suggesting its potential applicability in monitoring neurological conditions (Figure 9C).[ 478 ]

The evolution of CBNs has significantly enhanced our capabilities in neurotransmitter detection, marking a fundamental shift in the approaches and methodologies used in diagnosing and understanding neurological diseases. The forward momentum in this domain not only heralds more precise diagnostic tools but also suggests a brighter horizon for patient care and therapeutic interventions.

The search for innovative approaches to antimicrobial treatments and diagnostic tools has intensified due to the rising prevalence of antimicrobial resistance and the slow pace of novel antibiotic development.[ 479 ] Over the last decade, CBNs have emerged as promising materials for theranostic applications with strong antibacterial efficacy and diagnostic potential.[ 409 , 480 , 481 ] These benefits are because of their unique structural properties, affordable production methods, user‐friendly interfaces, adaptable physicochemical characteristics, compatibility with biological systems, and various modes of action, positioning CBNs as a strong category of theranostic nanomaterials and an ideal alternative to traditional diagnostic methods.[ 482 ] Their robustness, combined with extensive surface areas for functional group attachment, renders them especially suitable for diagnostic applications.[ 483 ] When paired with metallic nanoparticles, these nanomaterials exhibit remarkable sensitivity and boast impressively low detection thresholds.[ 484 , 485 , 486 ] While there is a wide spectrum of biomarkers for pathogenic microorganisms, spanning from bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa, CBNs have emerged as a favored choice.[ 487 ] Their unparalleled ability to engage in “nano‐bio” interactions with biomolecules at a diagnostic platform's sensing interface solidifies their standing in modern diagnostics.[ 487 ] Table 5 summarizes applications of CBNs in biomimetic biosensors for detecting pathogenic microorganisms.

A recent study showcased the potential of CBNs in bacterial detection applications.[ 495 ] In this research, an electrochemical immunosensor was devised for the prompt identification of pathogenic bacteria in food and environmental samples. The objective was to create a microwire‐based electrochemical immunosensor (MEI) specifically for recognizing E. coli and S. aureus in mixed microbial samples, utilizing dielectrophoresis (DEP) for cell concentration.

Additionally, a tungsten microwire coated with gold was prepared through successive layering of polyethylenimine, a suspension of SWCNT, streptavidin, biotin‐tagged antibodies, and subsequently, solutions of BSA (Figure 10A).[ 495 ] It was determined that a double layer of SWCNTs combined with a 5% BSA solution optimized the enhancement of the signal and reduced nonspecific interactions. By generating an electric field in the solution with the bacteria at a frequency of 3 MHz and 20 Vpp, specific capture of E. coli K12 or S. aureus cells was achieved. As the concentration of E. coli increased within a certain range, a consistent rise in electron transfer resistance was noted (R2 = 0.976).[ 495 ] Similarly, an MEI sensor is tailored with anti‐S. aureus antibodies indicated a resistance increase with varying concentrations of S. aureus, exhibiting a correlation of R2 = 0.983. To confirm the specificity of these MEI sensors, other bacteria like Salmonella typhimurium and Listeria monocytogenes were tested. The methods employed in crafting the MEI sensor hint at its potential utility for detecting various harmful microbes in both the food and environmental sectors.[ 495 ] Likewise, Figure 10Ai highlights how coating the microwire with SWCNTs amplifies the electron transfer resistance (ΔRet) resulting from the antibody–bacteria interaction. ΔRet is calculated by taking the difference between the resistance values of the antibody (Ret (antibody)) and the combined resistance of the antibody and antigen (Ret (antibody‐bacteria)). This difference essentially represents the resistance produced when E. coli binds to the E. coli MEI sensor.[ 495 ]

The advancement in CBNs has opened new avenues for efficient viral detection, especially in urgent scenarios like the recent SARS‐CoV‐2 outbreak. A study spearheaded by Liang and colleagues delved into this potential by introducing a multi‐functionalized floating gate CNT field effect transistor (FG‐CNT FET) biosensor.[ 496 ] This innovative tool is crafted for single virus‐level detection of both SARS‐CoV‐2 virus antigen and RNA, offering results within a minute using a mobile sensing platform. As shown in Figure 10B, the sensors, enhanced with aptamers, have shown the capability to identify SARS‐CoV‐2 antigens directly from raw nasopharyngeal swab samples in under a minute.[ 496 ] Furthermore, by employing a multi‐probe approach, this FG‐CNT FET biosensor can detect longer RNA chains, even down to the level of a single virus, again within just a minute. In practical clinical tests, the device successfully differentiated between 10 COVID‐19‐positive patients and ten healthy individuals, recognizing both RNA and antigens. Using a combined detection approach, the overall accuracy was ≈100%. These findings not only bolster the potential of FET‐based sensors in detecting nucleic acids but also establish the FG‐CNT FET biosensor as a promising integrated platform for highly sensitive multi‐biomarker detection. Its efficiency in delivering results without the need for amplification accentuates its potential for point‐of‐care clinical applications.[ 496 ]

Furthermore, the use of CBNs, especially in the identification of fungal pathogens, presents valuable opportunities for ensuring environmental and food safety. A particularly challenging fish ailment, the epizootic ulcerative syndrome (EUS), is primarily attributed to the oomycete fungus Aphanomyces invadans. To address this, an innovative label‐free immunosensor was developed. This sensor employed a graphene‐gold nanoparticles composite (G‐AuNPs) and was denoted as G‐AuNPs/SAM‐Ab‐BSA/GCE for the precise determination of Aphanomyces invadans. The electrode's fabrication involved the immobilization of anti‐mycelium antibodies onto the G/GCE, which was further modified with a SAM and then enhanced using N‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl)‐N‐ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), BSA, and NHS. After optimizing the procedure, several key parameters were identified: a 90 min immersion time of the SAM‐modified electrode in the anti‐mycelium solution, a concentration of 0.20 µg mL−1 for the anti‐mycelium solution, and 10 min for the immunoreaction. Under these conditions, the immunosensor showcased a notable detection limit of 309 ng mL−1 and demonstrated consistent reproducibility. This advancement underscores the adaptability and potential of CBNs‐based tools in the vital field of fungal pathogen detection.[ 497 ]

The promise of CBNs in diagnostics has been evident in their applications for bacteria, viruses, and fungi. Looking ahead, the potential of these materials to revolutionize the detection of protozoa cannot be overstated. Protozoa, being complex microorganisms, pose unique challenges in their detection, especially in environments where rapid and accurate identification is crucial for public health.[ 498 ] Harnessing the innate attributes of CBNs, such as their adaptability, high surface area, and compatibility with biological systems, could pave the way for developing advanced sensors capable of pinpointing protozoal pathogens with precision. As research continues, it's anticipated that the integration of these nanomaterials in diagnostic platforms will significantly enhance our ability to combat infections, ushering in a new era of improved public health measures and disease control.

Pharmaceutical Compound Sensors

As pharmaceuticals become increasingly complex and diverse, the need for accurate, rapid, and efficient detection methods grows.[ 499 ] Biomimetic CBNs, inspired by nature's intricate designs, have emerged as potent tools in this arena. Mirroring certain biological structures and processes, these nanomaterials offer both heightened sensitivity and specificity in the detection of various drug compounds. For instance, opioid analgesics, including medications like tramadol, codeine, and oxycodone, have revolutionized pain management for countless patients.[ 500 ] Yet, the administration of such drugs is not without complications. Different analgesics can induce a myriad of side effects, potentially compromising patient well‐being. Among these opioids, nalbuphine hydrochloride (NAL), a specific phenanthrene derivative, has been employed to alleviate pain from various severe and persistent conditions, ranging from surgical pain to migraines.[ 500 ] Its mechanism of action entails binding to kappa‐opioid receptors and finds application in obstetric analgesia. A recent study introduced a screen‐printed electrode (SPE) tailored for the potentiometric determination of nalbuphine (NAL).[ 507 ] In this research, the electrode was enhanced using a nanocomposite of carboxylated MWCNTs and PANI (f‐MWCNTs/PANI) as an ion‐to‐electron transducer. This was overlaid by a selective PVC membrane, which was integrated with MIP beads to recognize nalbuphine potentiometrically. When evaluated, the SPE demonstrated a commendable Nernstian response and a notably swift response time. Chronopotentiometry (CP) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were utilized to determine the interfacial capacitance of this innovative SPE. Importantly, the f‐MWCNTs/PANI nanocomposite layer was observed to enhance the electrode's interfacial capacitance and durability. Such advancements not only signal the potential of biomimetic CBNs in pharmaceutical sensing but also indicate their utility in real‐world applications, such as rapid overdose diagnosis in medical settings and ensuring drug quality in the pharmaceutical sector (Figure 11A ).[ 507 ]

Another compelling example that underscores the potential of biomimetic CBNs in pharmaceutical sensing involves the detection of acetaminophen (APAP), a commonly used antipyretic medication that can be hepatotoxic at elevated levels. In a notable study, Balram's group developed a novel nanocomposite exhibiting exceptional electrocatalytic properties for the detection of APAP at nanomolar concentrations. This innovative composite was based on amine‐functionalized MWCNTs (NH2‐MWCNTs) adorned with zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs).[ 508 ] Figure 11B illustrates the synthesis of begonia flower‐like ZnO NPs facilitated by arginine, alongside the process of surface functionalization employed in the preparation of NH2‐MWCNTs. The creation of this sensor involved surface functionalization of CNTs with amine groups, achieved through an ultrasound‐assisted approach. Concurrently, begonia flower‐like ZnO nanoparticles were synthesized using an arginine‐assisted aqueous solution method. To analyze and characterize these synthesized materials, various techniques were employed, including UV‐Vis spectroscopy, micro Raman spectroscopy, FTIR, XRD, elemental mapping, and SEM.[ 508 ]

Electrochemical investigations revealed the superb electrocatalysis of APAP at low potentials, evidenced by well‐defined redox peak currents on the ZnO NPs/NH2‐MWCNT‐modified screen‐printed carbon electrode (SPCE). This resulted in an ultralow detection limit of just 1 nM and a remarkably high sensitivity of 89.64 µAµM−1cm−2, showcasing the sensor's exceptional performance. Further evaluation of this APAP sensor revealed commendable stability, reproducibility, repeatability, and excellent resistance to interference.[ 508 ] In addition, the practical applicability of this sensor was validated through experiments conducted on real samples, including APAP tablets, human urine, and human serum.

In another innovative study, Rovina and colleagues explored the utilization of a unique nanocomposite film comprising chitosan/GO (CHIT/GO), MWCNTs, and AuNPs. This composite was employed in the development of a GCE (CHIT/GO/MWCNTs/AuNPs/GCE) aimed at detecting Tartrazine (TZ), a synthetic dye commonly found in food products. This electrochemical sensor demonstrated heightened sensitivity, benefiting from the signal amplification attributes of GO and the superior electronic and antifouling properties of MWCNTs. The CHIT/GO/MWCNTs/AuNPs/GCE, serving as an efficient electron transfer material, showcased enhanced current signals due to its intercalation properties. Under optimal conditions, the modified GCE offered a linear response between 10 and 100 mg mL−1, with a sensitivity of 0.018 µA µM−1. Detection and quantification limits were observed at 1.45 and 4.83 mg mL−1, respectively. Furthermore, the application of this sensor for TZ determination in various commercial food items yielded satisfactory and reliable results, with recovery percentages ranging from 94.52% to 109.0%.[ 509 ]

In a parallel advancement, Moradi's group reported the in situ synthesis of palladium/ZnO loaded on GO (PZGO) nanocomposites, which were characterized using techniques like SEM, EDS, and powder‐XRD.[ 510 ] This newly synthesized nanocomposite was applied to modify the surface of a GCE for the detection of nitrite ions (NO2 −) in food products. The synergistic effect of different layers was examined by modifying the GCE surface with both PZGO nanocomposites and GO nanoparticles. Voltammetric analysis revealed that the nanocomposite exhibited commendable catalytic activity toward nitrite oxidation. The proposed sensor demonstrated the ability to selectively detect NO2 − within a linear range from 3.17 to 1111 µM, with a notable detection limit of 2.39 µM. Notably, the synthesized nanocomposites maintained their stability for at least nine months, indicating the long‐term viability of the developed sensor.[ 511 ] This research not only highlights the versatility of biomimetic CBNs in detecting pharmaceutical compounds but also demonstrates their practical application in monitoring drug levels in various matrices, thereby contributing significantly to both clinical diagnostics and patient safety.

The diverse and innovative applications of biomimetic CBNs, as exemplified in the studies highlighted above, underscore their significant potential in the field of pharmaceutical sensing and beyond. From enhancing the detection of complex drug compounds to ensuring safety in food products, these nanomaterials are paving the way for more accurate, rapid, and efficient analytical methods. Their ability to mimic biological structures, coupled with their enhanced sensitivity and specificity, positions them at the forefront of scientific innovation. These materials are expected to become more and more important as we investigate and utilize their potential. They will eventually help create a safer and better‐informed society by improving environmental monitoring, food safety, and healthcare.

Cancer Biomarker Detection

The detection of cancer biomarkers is a critical step in diagnosing and managing cancer, yet it presents several challenges. Conventional techniques frequently have drawbacks such as poor specificity, low sensitivity, and the need for large sample quantities. In addition, early‐stage detection, which is crucial for successful treatment, is particularly challenging as biomarker levels can be extremely low.[ 512 , 513 ] Furthermore, the complexity of biological samples and the presence of interfering substances add to the difficulty of accurate detection. Biomimetic CBN‐based sensors have emerged as a potentially effective way to get around these challenges in this setting. These sensors leverage the exceptional properties of CBNs, such as high surface area, excellent conductivity, and the capacity to be easily functionalized. Intriguingly, a recent study has marked a breakthrough in the detection of C‐reactive protein (CRP), which the liver produces in response to systemic inflammation caused by bacterial infections, trauma, or organ dysfunction.[ 526 ] CRP's role extends beyond indicating cardiovascular diseases, type‐2 diabetes, and hypertension; it has also emerged as a potential marker for various cancer types. Elevated serum levels of CRP often signal these pathological states, underscoring its importance in early detection and management. The study unveils the development of a remarkably sensitive and selective immunosensor for CRP, based on CNT field‐effect transistors (CNT‐FET). For instance, Rabbani and colleagues skillfully layered CNTs on a Si/SiO2 substrate, situated between the source and drain electrodes, and further refined it with the renowned linker PBASE. A pivotal step involved the attachment of anti‐CRP, leading to a CNT‐FET immunosensor specifically designed for CRP. This innovative sensor demonstrates an impressive capacity for detecting CRP, covering a range from 0.01 to 1000 µg mL−1. Notably, it offers a quick response time of just 2–3 min and maintains consistency with a variation of less than 3%. These attributes make the sensor highly suitable for clinical use, particularly for the early diagnosis of diseases such as coronary heart disease (CHD).[ 527 ] From a cancer detection standpoint, this sensor's precise measurement of CRP levels is especially vital. The sensor offers itself as a viable tool for ongoing cancer surveillance and early cancer identification, given the association between high CRP levels and different forms of cancer. Its effectiveness was further confirmed through tests using CRP‐enriched serum samples and comparisons with the enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), a traditional diagnostic approach.[ 528 , 529 ]

This CNT‐FET immunosensor is a potential game‐changer. It stands out as a streamlined and cost‐effective alternative to the complex, traditional laboratory‐based procedures for CRP diagnosis typically employed in hospitals. By facilitating the early detection of increased CRP levels, this sensor not only helps identify cardiovascular and metabolic disorders but also positions itself as an invaluable instrument for the early diagnosis and monitoring of various cancer types. The development of CBNs‐based biomimetic sensors is a major accomplishment in the field of medical diagnostics.[ 530 ] With the ongoing advancement and integration of these technologies into clinical practice, there is significant potential to transform cancer management, resulting in enhanced patient outcomes and offering renewed hope in the battle against this complex disease.

Conclusion and Future Perspectives

CBNs have sparked widespread interest in nanotechnology owing to their distinctive characteristics and variable dimensional structure. CBNs offer exceptional electrical, thermal, and optical features, making them ideal for biosensing, bioimaging, tissue engineering, and drug delivery applications. CBNs are already available in a variety of forms, including nanotubes, nanosheets, nanoribbons, and quantum dots, with many more under development that offer intriguing future uses. By altering both physical and chemical properties, CBNs' surface functionalization improves their dispersibility, appropriateness for use in biological systems, and capacity for drug loading and release at specified places. As a result, CBNs have been utilized in several biological systems with success. In addition, because of their optical properties, high biocompatibility, and adjustable systemic toxicity, they have been used extensively in photodynamic treatment procedures. Nevertheless, some restrictions may still make it difficult for them to be used in specific drug delivery applications in biomedical settings. The current focus of study is on enhancing the surface chemistry of CBNs in order to decrease toxicity and boost bioavailability, biocompatibility, and dispersibility. Enhancing the toxicity and solubility of CBNs by conjugation and grafting more suitable materials onto their surface may facilitate the synthesis of more biocompatible and soluble products based on CBNs. As their limitations are addressed and their suitability for human applications improves, we anticipate a growing utilization of CBN‐based composite materials.

Commercializing biomimetic CBNs remains a serious hurdle, preventing large‐scale production and widespread biomedical applications. Complex synthesis processes, including chemical vapor deposition and sol‐gel processes, frequently encounter scalability challenges when transitioning from laboratory to industrial settings, affecting the uniformity of size, shape, and functional characteristics critical for biosensing and therapeutic applications. Another barrier is the high cost of production, driven by expensive precursor materials and specialized equipment. Balancing these expenditures with competitive market pricing is critical to successful commercialization.

Another major hurdle is regulatory compliance, as medical applications demand rigorous, costly, and time‐intensive evaluations of safety, toxicity, and biocompatibility. This process is made more difficult and may take longer to get market approval if there are no established procedures for production, testing, and quality control. Adoption is also influenced by public perception, in addition to regulatory barriers; patients and healthcare professionals may be hesitant due to inaccurate information or a lack of knowledge about the efficacy and safety of nanomaterials. Thus, it's critical to show the distinct, observable benefits of biomimetic CBNs over current technology. It takes constant research and strategic innovation to meet these difficulties. Future projects should focus on increasing the scalability of synthesis, improving cost‐effectiveness, creating strong regulatory frameworks, and bolstering public trust and knowledge. By solving these challenges, biomimetic CBNs will be able to realize their full potential and have a significant impact on biomedical categories.

To create more effective and stable systems, additional studies into novel materials with high surface areas, active sites, and co‐reactive groups (binding and catalytic groups) may be conducted in the future with the aim of creating biomimetic nanomaterials. Future research must overcome the following obstacles: i) A comprehensive evaluation of the bioavailability of CBNs in relation to their functionalization, size, purity, and purifying techniques is required. ii) It is necessary to assess the impact of functionalizing CBN surfaces on efficacy and toxicity; iii) the development of straightforward synthetic techniques for CBN modification is necessary for biomedical application treatment. In light of the concerns, some recommendations are given: 1) To comprehend the catalytic mechanism of nano‐enzymes and elucidate the connection between the structure of biomimetic nanomaterials and catalytic activity, a logical design of nano‐enzyme materials is necessary. It is possible to prevent the limitations of nanozymes by carefully designing materials to improve their sensing capabilities. 2) Modify the biomimetic nanoparticles' size to enhance their catalytic activity. In terms of practicality, the market still lacks items and technologies that are relevant. It is envisaged that combining biomimetic nanoparticles with a range of existing market technologies would successfully boost their early deployment and commercialization. Looking ahead, the convergence of carbon‐based nanomaterials with cutting‐edge technologies such as flexible electronics, organ‐on‐chip systems, and artificial intelligence presents an exciting frontier. Smart biosensors embedded with CBNs could offer real‐time monitoring, adaptive feedback, and personalized therapeutic interventions. Furthermore, the development of hybrid platforms integrating CBNs with peptides, DNA origami, and responsive polymers will enable multifunctional, programmable biomedical systems. To fully realize these possibilities, interdisciplinary collaboration across materials science, biotechnology, and computational modeling will be essential in translating lab‐scale innovations into clinically viable and scalable devices.
