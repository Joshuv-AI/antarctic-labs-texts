Dietary index for gut microbiota: A new frontier in sarcopenia prevention
Authors: Jiajia Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research examines the correlation between the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) and the incidence of sarcopenia. A cross-sectional analysis was conducted using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2011–2016) involving participants aged 20 years or older. The DI-GM, comprising 14 dietary components (10 beneficial and 4 detrimental), was evaluated. Weighted logistic regression models were used to assess the relationship between DI-GM and sarcopenia, adjusting for various covariates. In addition, restricted cubic spline analysis was performed. Subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted to explore whether any factors modified this relationship. Among 5908 eligible participants, 474 were diagnosed with sarcopenia. Individuals with sarcopenia exhibited significantly lower DI-GM scores compared with healthy counterparts. A consistent inverse association was observed between DI-GM and sarcopenia across all models. Participants in the highest DI-GM quartile (score ≥ 6) demonstrated a 54% reduced prevalence of sarcopenia (odds ratio = 0.46, 95% confidence interval = 0.28–0.75, P = .008) relative to the lowest quartile, with a significant dose–response trend (P for trend = .044). Subgroup analyses corroborated these findings. Higher DI-GM scores are associated with reduced sarcopenia prevalence. These results suggest that dietary interventions targeting gut microbiota modulation may serve as a feasible strategy for sarcopenia prevention and management.

1. Introduction

Sarcopenia, a syndrome associated with aging, is defined by the gradual deterioration of skeletal muscle mass and functionality.[1,2] Its pathogenesis is multifactorial, encompassing neuromuscular dysfunction, chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired anabolic signaling.[3–7] These mechanisms converge to disrupt muscle innervation, protein synthesis, and metabolic homeostasis, thereby accelerating muscle wasting.

The gut microbiota has emerged as a pivotal regulator of host metabolism and immunity, a role first uncovered in foundational studies. Early work by Ley and colleagues showed that obesity is associated with shifts in gut bacterial composition – specifically a reduced ratio of Bacteroidetes to Firmicutes – that can revert toward lean-like profiles alongside weight loss.[8,9] Turnbaugh et al further demonstrated in mice that an “obese microbiota” enhances energy harvest from the diet and, when transplanted into germ-free mice, leads to increased fat accumulation.[10] Building on composition-based insights, a twin study introduced the concept of a “core microbiome,” highlighting that shared microbial functional genes, rather than individual taxa, may be central to host metabolic regulation.[11] The MetaHIT consortium then established a comprehensive gut microbial gene catalogue comprising over 3.3 million non-redundant genes, laying the foundation for in-depth functional metagenomic analyses.[12] Subsequent research has detailed the taxonomic composition – including dominance by Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes – and clarified mechanisms such as polysaccharide fermentation into anti-inflammatory short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like acetate, propionate, and butyrate, as well as the impact of dysbiosis on immune homeostasis and metabolic disorders.[13–16]

Notably, gut dysbiosis has been linked to sarcopenia. Observational studies in elderly populations reveal reduced microbial diversity in sarcopenic individuals, marked by diminished abundances of butyrate-producing taxa (e.g., Roseburia, Eubacterium) and enrichment of pathobionts like Bacteroides.[17] A large-scale community study (n = 1417) utilizing metagenomic sequencing identified elevated Desulfovibrio and Clostridium species in sarcopenia, correlating positively with disease severity.[18] A meta-analysis of 17 randomized trials (n = 4307) confirmed reduced α-diversity in sarcopenia cohorts, with depletion of beneficial genera (e.g., Ruminococcaceae, Faecalibacterium) and expansion of pro-inflammatory taxa (e.g., Parabacteroides).[19] Mechanistically, gut microbiota may influence sarcopenia via systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation, as evidenced by cytokine-mediated crosstalk between dysbiosis and muscle wasting.[4]

Kase et al created the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM), which measures dietary trends that alter the composition and function of gut microbes.[20] By integrating 14 dietary components (10 beneficial, 4 detrimental), DI-GM predicts gut health outcomes and guides personalized nutrition strategies. Previous studies associate higher DI-GM scores with reduced risks of constipation,[21] sleep disorders,[22] and diabetes,[23] underscoring its utility in evaluating microbiota-related health interventions. However, the relationship between DI-GM and sarcopenia remains unexplored. Investigating this association could elucidate novel microbiota-muscle crosstalk mechanisms and inform preventive strategies. Leveraging data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 2011–2016), this study aims to bridge this critical knowledge gap.

2. Methods

2.1. Research framework and participants

Publicly available data from the 2011 to 2016 cycles of NHANES, a nationally representative survey that uses a multistage stratified probability sampling design to evaluate the health characteristics of non-institutionalized US adults, were used in this cross-sectional study. From 29,902 participants enrolled in NHANES 2011 to 2016, we excluded 15,723 individuals with missing data required for the diagnosis of sarcopenia and 5594 individuals aged <20 years, leaving 8585 eligible participants. Of these, 1550 were further excluded due to missing dietary information for the calculation of the DI-GM score, and 1127 were excluded due to missing covariate data. The final analytic sample comprised 5908 participants (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study. DI-GM = Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota, NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.2. DI-GM assessment

The DI-GM score was derived from dietary intake data collected via 24-hour dietary recalls in NHANES. Following the methodology established by Kase et al, the index integrates 14 dietary components (10 beneficial, 4 detrimental) associated with gut microbiota diversity and health, including dietary fiber, prebiotics, and fermented foods. Each food group was assigned a binary score (0 or 1) according to whether individual intake was below or above the sex-specific median. The total score was obtained by summing across all components. Higher DI-GM scores indicate greater dietary alignment with gut microbiota-promoting patterns. The grading criteria and comprehensive component definitions are given in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1.

2.3. Sarcopenia definition

Sarcopenia was defined using appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). All NHANES participants included in our analysis (2011–2016) were assessed using DXA, and the measurement protocol was consistent across survey cycles. Bioelectrical impedance analysis data were not used in this study. The sarcopenia index was calculated as follows:

Sarcopenia index = ASM (kg)/body mass index (BMI, kg/m2)

Participants were classified as having sarcopenia if their index fell below sex-specific thresholds: <0.789 for males and <0.512 for females.

2.4. Covariate assessment

We adjusted for a wide range of covariates, including demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors (e.g., age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, smoking, drinking, BMI, comorbidities, and laboratory biomarkers). Detailed definitions and coding of all covariates are provided in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Analyses incorporated NHANES sampling weights, strata, and clusters to generate nationally representative estimates. Survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression models evaluated the independent association between DI-GM (quartiles) and sarcopenia across 3 models:

Model 1: unadjusted.

Model 2: adjusted for sex, age, and race.

Model 3: adjusted for sex, race, marital status, smoking, educational level, drinking, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, BMI, poverty income ratio, albumin, calcium, creatinine, phosphorus.

A two-tailed P < .05 defined statistical significance. Analyses were performed using R 4.2.1 with the survey package.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

The analytical cohort comprised 5908 participants, representing 118,512,241 non-institutionalized US adults. Among them, 474 individuals (8.02%) were diagnosed with sarcopenia. Compared with non-sarcopenic individuals, sarcopenic participants exhibited significantly lower mean DI-GM scores (Table 1). Statistically significant differences (P < .05) were observed between groups for albumin, calcium, creatinine, age, poverty income ratio, gender, and marital status. No significant differences were detected for phosphorus, race, or other covariates (P > .05). In addition, the prevalence of sarcopenia appeared particularly high among Mexican Americans compared with other racial groups.

3.2. Association between DI-GM and sarcopenia

Higher DI-GM scores were inversely correlated with sarcopenia risk in fully adjusted models. When analyzed as a continuous variable, each unit increase in DI-GM corresponded to a 12% reduction in sarcopenia prevalence (odds ratio [OR] = 0.88, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.78–0.99). Participants in the highest DI-GM quartile (Q4, score ≥ 6) had a 54% lower risk of sarcopenia compared with the lowest quartile (Q1; OR = 0.46, 95% CI = 0.28–0.75; P = .008), with a significant dose–response trend (P for trend = .044; Table 2). Restricted cubic spline suggested a steep decline in sarcopenia risk with increasing DI-GM up to approximately 4 points, after which the curve plateaued. Although the threshold effect model indicated a potential inflection point at DI-GM ≈ 4, this finding was not statistically robust, and overall no definitive threshold effect was established (Fig. 2). Finally, we assessed the discriminative power of model 3 using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, yielding an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.80–0.83; Fig. 3). The fully adjusted model showed an AUC of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.80–0.83), suggesting good in-sample discriminative performance for distinguishing participants with and without sarcopenia. However, because the model was developed and evaluated within the same cross-sectional dataset, this finding should be interpreted as exploratory and does not establish clinical prediction utility.

4. Discussion

Evidence on diet quality and sarcopenia remains mixed. A recent meta-analysis found no overall association between healthy dietary patterns and sarcopenia risk (pooled OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.85–1.06), although benefits were observed for gait-speed preservation.[24] In contrast, more recent large-scale observational studies have reported protective associations for established dietary indices. Analysis of NHANES 2005 to 2018 showed that higher adherence to healthy eating index-2020, alternate Mediterranean diet, and dietary approaches to stop hypertension dietary patterns was inversely associated with sarcopenia risk, with each standard deviation increment linked to 18%, 16%, and 14% reductions in odds, respectively.[25] Similarly, in Chinese elderly populations, a 1-unit increase in healthy eating index corresponded to a 1.2% reduction in the prevalence of sarcopenia.[26] Against this backdrop, each 1-point increase in the DI-GM was associated with a 12% lower prevalence of sarcopenia. In addition, the fully adjusted model yielded an AUC of 0.81 in the present NHANES sample, indicating good in-sample discriminative performance. Nevertheless, this result should be interpreted cautiously because the model was developed and evaluated within the same cross-sectional dataset. Therefore, the observed discrimination should be regarded as preliminary, and external validation in independent prospective cohorts is required before any clinical or population-level prediction utility can be established.

Growing research underscores a strong link between gut microbiota and sarcopenia.[27] However, because the present study did not directly assess gut microbial composition, microbial function, or circulating/fecal metabolites, the mechanistic pathways discussed below should be interpreted as biologically plausible explanations based on prior literature rather than mechanisms directly demonstrated in our study. The “gut-muscle axis” may facilitate interactions between gut microbiota and sarcopenia through several pathways, including systemic inflammation, SCFA production, intestinal barrier function, and nutrient metabolism.[4,27–30] Previous studies have reported reduced gut microbiota diversity in individuals with sarcopenia, lower abundances of beneficial bacteria such as Roseburia and Faecalibacterium, and enrichment of potentially pro-inflammatory taxa.[17,28,31] These alterations may contribute to muscle loss by promoting chronic low-grade inflammation, impairing nutrient metabolism, and reducing microbial metabolites that support muscle homeostasis.[4,28–30] Nevertheless, these pathways require confirmation in future studies incorporating microbiome sequencing, metabolomic profiling, and longitudinal assessment of muscle outcomes.

The development of sarcopenia is influenced by various risk factors. Age is an important factor, as gut microbiota diversity and stability decline with age, reducing beneficial bacteria and increasing harmful bacteria, while muscle mass and function also gradually deteriorate, raising the risk of sarcopenia.[32] For example, a study on elderly Japanese individuals found lower abundances of Bifidobacterium and Firmicutes in the gut of sarcopenic patients, which were positively correlated with muscle strength and physical function.[33] In populations with other chronic diseases, such as diabetes, the relationship between gut dysbiosis and sarcopenia becomes more complex. In diabetic patients, a hyperglycemic environment may alter gut microbiota composition and function, while microbiota changes may exacerbate sarcopenia development through mechanisms such as insulin resistance.[6] Moreover, gender may influence the relationship between gut microbiota and sarcopenia. Some studies suggest that men and women may exhibit differences in microbiota composition and the pathogenesis of sarcopenia, though the exact mechanisms require further exploration.[34] In addition, medication use may affect both gut microbiota and muscles. The use of antibiotics disrupts gut microbiota balance, reducing beneficial bacteria and increasing antibiotic-resistant bacteria, which can impair gut barrier function and muscle metabolism.[35,36] Studies have also shown that lifestyle factors, such as lack of exercise, smoking, and alcohol consumption, are associated with gut dysbiosis and sarcopenia. A lack of exercise may slow gut motility, affecting the distribution and function of gut microbiota, which is also detrimental to muscle maintenance and growth.[29]

The disproportionately high prevalence of sarcopenia observed among Mexican Americans in our study is consistent with prior NHANES-based reports. Although earlier research reported disparities at the broader Hispanic level,[37] Mexican Americans constitute the largest subgroup within the US Hispanic population, which may partly explain the consistency between our findings and prior reports. More recent NHANES analyses have also identified Mexican Americans specifically as a high-risk group.[38,39]

Several factors may contribute to this pattern. Sarcopenic individuals among this group more frequently exhibit central obesity and metabolic comorbidities – including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and liver disease – accompanied by higher waist-to-height ratio and lower physical activity.[38] Functional measures further reveal that Hispanic individuals experience significantly worse muscle function, with up to 2.6-fold and 3.2-fold increased odds of low grip strength and slow gait speed, respectively.[37] These risks are exacerbated by systemic disadvantages: Hispanic individuals, including those with chronic kidney disease, are markedly more likely to be uninsured or lack a regular source of healthcare (ORs 2.52 and 1.68, respectively),[40] and face linguistic, cultural, and socioeconomic barriers that limit access to high-quality nutrition and preventive care. These intersecting sociodemographic, behavioral, and structural factors likely contribute to the elevated sarcopenia burden in Mexican American populations and highlight the need for culturally tailored, equity-oriented interventions.

Higher DI-GM scores were associated with a more favorable gut microbiota profile and a lower prevalence of sarcopenia. This highlights diet as a modifiable factor for improving gut health. Modulating the gut microbiota can enhance barrier function, reduce systemic inflammation, and potentially alleviate muscle damage. Probiotics may increase beneficial bacteria, suppress harmful species, and reduce inflammation, thereby supporting muscle mass and function.[41,42] Prebiotics promote the growth of beneficial bacteria by supplying essential substrates. Fecal microbiota transplantation, which transfers a healthy donor’s microbiota into the patient’s gut, has shown promise in treating dysbiosis-related diseases.[13]

Gut microbiota modulation also influences nutrient metabolism and absorption, providing better nutritional support for muscles. Dietary interventions that alter microbial composition promote the production of SCFAs, which improve energy metabolism and insulin sensitivity.[30] Fiber-rich diets enhance SCFA production and improve muscle health. For example, resistant starch type 4 increases Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium abundance and elevates fecal butyrate and propionate levels in individuals with metabolic syndrome, changes associated with improved host metabolism and potentially lower sarcopenia risk.[43]

The Mediterranean diet is another pattern linked to both gut and muscle health. It emphasizes vegetables, fruits, whole grains, olive oil, and fish, and provides abundant nutrients and antioxidants.[44] Studies indicate that adherence to this diet modulates gut microbiota composition, increases beneficial bacteria, reduces inflammation, and preserves muscle function.[45,46] Observational evidence further suggests that higher adherence to the Mediterranean diet is associated with lower rates of sarcopenia.[47–49]

In contrast, diets high in sugar and fat but low in fiber disrupt microbial balance, reduce beneficial species, and increase inflammation, all of which may impair muscle health.[50,51] For instance, long-term intake of excessive saturated fat leads to dysbiosis, chronic inflammation, and accelerated muscle atrophy through impaired protein synthesis and degradation pathways.[52] Overall, adjusting dietary patterns, increasing fiber intake, and adopting healthy dietary models can improve gut microbiota and may help prevent or alleviate sarcopenia.[53]

This study offers a number of noteworthy benefits. First, it is the first to show a significant correlation between DI-GM and sarcopenia after adjusting for confounding variables, with increased DI-GM linked to a progressive decline in sarcopenia risk. Second, the study shows a consistent protective effect of DI-GM against the development of sarcopenia by establishing a nonlinear exposure–response connection between DI-GM and sarcopenia risk. According to this research, dietary changes that raise DI-GM may help lower the incidence of sarcopenia. In addition, the use of a large, nationally representative NHANES sample enhances the generalizability of our findings to the broader US adult population.

This study has several limitations. First, owing to the cross-sectional design of NHANES, temporal and causal relationships between DI-GM and sarcopenia cannot be established. Second, DI-GM was derived from 24-hour dietary recalls, which may be affected by recall bias and may not fully reflect habitual dietary intake. Third, selection bias may exist because participants with missing DXA, dietary, or covariate data were excluded; these individuals may differ from those included in age, health status, nutritional status, physical function, or socioeconomic characteristics. Fourth, sarcopenia was operationally defined using DXA-derived ASM/BMI and did not include muscle strength or physical performance measures such as grip strength or gait speed; thus, our findings mainly apply to low muscle mass–defined sarcopenia. Fifth, although extensive covariate adjustment was performed, residual confounding from medication use, dietary supplementation, chronic inflammatory conditions, frailty status, genetic susceptibility, and other unmeasured lifestyle factors cannot be excluded. Sixth, no direct microbiome sequencing, metagenomic profiling, or metabolomic analyses were performed; therefore, microbiota-related mechanisms should be regarded as biologically plausible hypotheses rather than directly verified pathways. Finally, although the ROC analysis showed good in-sample discrimination, the model was developed and evaluated within the same cross-sectional dataset and lacked external validation. Independent prospective studies are needed to confirm causality, validate risk discrimination, and assess the clinical relevance of DI-GM for sarcopenia.
