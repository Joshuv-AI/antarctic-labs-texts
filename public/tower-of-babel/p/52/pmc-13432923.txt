Mental Health and Serious Drug Crime: A Case–Control Study
Authors: Morten Hesse, Julie Elizabeth Brummer, Freja Ilsing Magnussen, Erik Simonsen, Thomas Friis Søgaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSerious drug crime involves a high level of risk and is often characterised as requiring a network of suitable and trustworthy collaborators. Although prior studies have focused on the importance of economic, social and work‐related factors for involvement in such crime, there has been less research into the mental health of the people involved.AimsTo assess the prevalence of mental disorders in a full nationwide cohort of individuals convicted of serious drug crimes in Denmark (2001–2020), who were born after 1984 and had a personal national identification number.MethodsWe compared four groups: large‐scale serious drug crime offenders sentenced to ≥ 5 years' imprisonment (n = 232); moderate‐scale serious drug crime offenders sentenced to under 5 years' imprisonment (n = 2955); offenders convicted only of small‐scale drug offences, such as low‐level supply, but never of serious drug crime (n = 7630); and sex‐ and age‐matched general population controls who had never been convicted of a serious drug crime (n = 3187). Mental disorders were identified using hospital and pharmacy registers.ResultsThe adjusted prevalence of any mental disorder was similar across offender groups (large‐scale: 32%, 95% CI [27%–38%]; moderate‐scale: 36%, 95% CI [34%–38%]; small‐scale: 36%, 95% CI [35%–37%]) and, in all three groups, was substantially higher than among controls (23%, 95% CI [22%–25%]). A similar pattern was observed for specific disorders.ConclusionsOur results challenge the assumption that engagement in serious drug crime requires skills and behaviours that are incompatible with having a mental disorder. We conclude that people with serious drug crime convictions are as likely as minor drug offenders to need mental health services and that people with mental disorders are vulnerable to recruitment into serious drug crime.Keywords: drug crime, drug trading, mental health, psychiatric disorders, register data

Introduction

The Mental Health of Serious Drug Crime Offenders

Understanding the mental health of people involved in serious drug crime, including individuals engaged in the manufacturing, smuggling and wholesale of illegal drugs, is crucial for tailoring preventive measures, developing resocialisation interventions and ensuring the provision of adequate healthcare. Nevertheless, prior researchers have rarely investigated mental disorders specifically among these individuals and, even when they have, they have primarily done so through studies of the broader category of organised crime offenders (Adamse et al. 2024; Comunale et al. 2020). This category also includes individuals involved in, for example, human smuggling and trafficking, fraud and money laundering (Calderoni et al. 2024), and such studies tend to focus on psychological traits rather than mental disorders. Although serious drug crime is not synonymous with organised crime, there is overlap, and the literature on organised crime can offer insights into serious drug offenders—both because serious drug crime can be considered a subtype of organised crime (see, e.g., F. Desroches 2007) and because the requirements for involvement in organised crime are considered to be similar to those for serious drug crime (Adler 1993; van Koppen 2013).

Existing research on mental health problems among people involved in organised crime has predominantly focused on antisocial traits generally associated with all types of criminal behaviour, leading Comunale et al. (2020) to suggest that future studies should consider including other types of disorders. Relatedly, although some studies suggest that low self‐control (i.e., being risk‐seeking and action‐oriented) can be an asset in achieving success in organised crime (Morselli and Tremblay 2004), others identify personality traits, such as being calculating, careful and disciplined, as factors that play a role in the selection of collaborators and are conducive to a career in organised crime generally and serious drug crime specifically (Bovenkerk 2000; F. J. Desroches 2005). Furthermore, a recent study by Gotzsche‐Astrup et al. (2022) found that organised crime offenders differed from the general population not only in traits such as excitability and mischievousness but also in terms of lower emotional stability, indicating a high propensity for mental disorders.

Thus, although there has been research into the association between personality traits and organised crime, even here studies on the prevalence of mental disorders remain scarce. Prior research has generally focused on mental disorders in relation to crime more generally and has found a high prevalence of mental disorders compared to the general population (Emilian et al. 2025; Hensel et al. 2020). Given this, and the findings reported by Gotzsche‐Astrup et al. (2022), it might be expected that mental disorders are not absent among serious drug offenders. Serious drug crime, however, has also been characterised as an activity with certain prerequisites for involvement that may be incompatible with having a mental disorder, such as being seen as a reliable collaborator, having organisational skills and behaving in ways that inspire trust from co‐offenders (Adler 1993).

One limitation of prior research on organised crime and personality traits is the lack of distinction regarding the level of organised crime involvement. With respect to drug crimes specifically, researchers have divided people involved into three categories (Pearson and Hobbs 2003) based on their position within the supply chain: (1) those involved in street‐level drug crime, such as small‐scale drug sales (Coomber and Moyle 2014); (2) those involved in moderate‐scale activity, which entails organisational skills, albeit not a central organisational role; and (3) those involved in large‐scale crime, including the handling and management of often large quantities of drugs (Pearson and Hobbs 2001).

Assuming that involvement in organising and planning serious drug crime requires better psychological functioning than involvement in small‐scale drug crimes, one would expect that the prevalence of mental disorders would differ across subpopulations of drug offenders, with those who are involved at the top level being less likely to suffer from psychopathology.

Aims and Hypotheses

Our aim was to assess the prevalence of mental disorders across four samples: large‐scale serious drug crime offenders; moderate‐scale serious drug crime offenders; offenders convicted of small‐scale drug supply but never of serious drug crime; and controls from the general population who were sex‐ and age‐matched to the large‐ and moderate‐scale drug crime offenders and who had no recorded involvement in serious drug crime.

We hypothesised that people involved in large‐scale drug crime would have a lower prevalence of mental disorders than those involved in moderate‐scale drug crime or small‐scale drug supply, but that their prevalence would not differ significantly from that of the general population. In addition, we hypothesised that the moderate‐scale drug crime group would have a higher prevalence of mental disorders than the general population.

Methods

Ethics and Data Security

This study was registered with Aarhus University. Danish law does not require an ethics evaluation or individual informed consent for register data to be used for research if the data are used solely for statistical and scientific purposes. All study data were stored on secure servers.

The Samples

The four samples included in this study are as follows:

Large‐scale serious drug crime offenders: individuals who had been sentenced to five years or more of imprisonment for a violation of Danish Penal Code § 191.

Large‐scale serious drug crime offenders: individuals who had been sentenced to five years or more of imprisonment for a violation of Danish Penal Code § 191.

Moderate‐scale serious drug crime offenders: individuals convicted of any drug crime under Penal Code § 191 who had never received a prison sentence of five years or more for a § 191 offence.

Moderate‐scale serious drug crime offenders: individuals convicted of any drug crime under Penal Code § 191 who had never received a prison sentence of five years or more for a § 191 offence.

Small‐scale drug supply offenders: individuals convicted of the sale of narcotics under the Euphoriants Act but never convicted of offences under Penal Code § 191.

Small‐scale drug supply offenders: individuals convicted of the sale of narcotics under the Euphoriants Act but never convicted of offences under Penal Code § 191.

A control group: individuals from the general Danish population, who had not been tried for a serious drug crime during 2001–2020. Each control was matched by birth month, birth year and sex to each member of the serious drug crime groups. Control sampling was done with replacement. For inclusion in this group, we also required that the individual was alive and living in Denmark at the time that their matched case was tried for their first serious drug crime.

A control group: individuals from the general Danish population, who had not been tried for a serious drug crime during 2001–2020. Each control was matched by birth month, birth year and sex to each member of the serious drug crime groups. Control sampling was done with replacement. For inclusion in this group, we also required that the individual was alive and living in Denmark at the time that their matched case was tried for their first serious drug crime.

The offender samples were identified using the national police case register (POLSAS); information was supplemented with Danish national crime registers (i.e., the registers of criminal charges, decisions and related cases) and the Danish population register. The POLSAS dataset contained all individuals tried for serious drug crimes (covered by Penal Code § 191) in Denmark between January 1, 2001, and December 31, 2020, and all individuals charged with a small‐scale drug supply offence (covered by the Euphoriants Act) in Denmark between January 1, 2003, and December 31, 2020. The offender populations were mutually exclusive, with each offender classified according to their most severe type of crime. We excluded all those born before 1985 and those without a valid Danish personal identification number to ensure full coverage by the relevant registers.

In the Danish legal system, for drug crimes, the type and quantity of the drug constitute the main criteria for determining the criminal code under which an individual is convicted (Penal Code § 191 or the Euphoriants Act; see Supporting Information S1: Appendix Table S1). In our study, we used a minimum prison sentence of 5 years to distinguish large‐scale from moderate‐scale serious drug crime based on sentencing guidelines and precedents. Examples of large‐scale drug crimes include trading large quantities of drugs, such as 1000 kg of hash, 1 kg of heroin or cocaine and 2 kg of amphetamine (Director of Public Prosecutions 2022, 2024). Most criminal justice systems give priority to drug type and quantity/weight as indications of seriousness, but having a coordinating or leadership role is often considered an aggravating circumstance (Director of Public Prosecutions 2006; Fleetwood 2011; Hughes et al. 2014), resulting in a harsher sentence. Thus, for our purposes, we considered longer prison sentences for such crimes to be a proxy for higher levels of involvement. Among the moderate‐scale offenders, the median maximum prison sentence per criminal career for serious drug crime was 270 days (IQR = 480 days). Supporting Information S1: Appendix Figure S1 shows the distribution of prison sentence lengths for moderate‐scale offenders.

Mental Disorder Measurement

The outcome variables were the lifetime prevalence of mental disorders prior to study entry, defined as the date of the first supply‐related drug crime or, for the control group, the date of the first supply‐related drug crime for their matched case. Mental disorders were identified using the National Patient Register, which records both public and private hospital admissions (Schmidt et al. 2019), and the Danish National Prescription Registry, which records prescriptions redeemed at community pharmacies (Pottegård et al. 2017). Thus, either a psychiatric diagnosis recorded in the hospital register or a redeemed prescription for a psychiatric medication, as specified below, was sufficient to classify an individual as having a mental disorder. Record linkage was made using the personal identification number.

An individual was defined as having had a lifetime history of any mental disorder if, prior to study entry, they had received any of the following ICD‐10 (World Health Organization 1993) diagnoses in the National Patient Register: F20–F29 (schizophrenia, schizotypal, delusional and other non‐mood psychotic disorders), F30–F39 (mood/affective disorders), F40–F48 (neurotic/anxiety disorders), F50–F59 (physiological/physical disturbances), F60–F61 (personality disorders) and F90–F99 (disorders with onset usually occurring in childhood and adolescence and unspecified mental disorder). We excluded the following disorders: F0 (organic), F7 (mental retardation) and F8 (disorders of psychological development). An individual was also considered to have had a mental disorder if they were recorded in the National Prescription Registry as having redeemed a prescription drug for psychotic symptoms (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) code N05A), mood or anxiety disorders (ATC N06A) or attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD, ATC N06B).

We also examined, separately, the prevalence of the following four categories of disorders after combining diagnostic data from hospital registers and pharmacy data: (1) mood/anxiety disorders, except bipolar; (2) severe mental illness (including bipolar disorders and schizophrenia, schizotypal, delusional and other non‐mood psychotic disorders); (3) ADHD; and (4) conduct disorder and dissocial personality disorder. We pooled mood/anxiety disorders together based on theoretical perspectives and empirical evidence linking both to an internalising spectrum of disorders and bipolar and schizophrenia spectrum disorders together based on both belonging to a thought disorder spectrum (Ringwald et al. 2023). Similarly, we combined conduct disorder with adult dissocial personality disorder because dissocial personality disorder generally emerges from conduct disorder (Chiocchetti et al. 2022; Feeney 2003). Individuals could be assigned to multiple diagnostic categories, and we considered comorbidity in the analyses.

Control Variables

Substance Use Disorders

Because of the likely overlap between substance use and mental disorders (Arendt 2009; Mcgorry et al. 2025; Ricard et al. 2025; Ringwald et al. 2023; Voce et al. 2021), we controlled for a history of substance use disorders using three sources: (1) diagnoses in the hospital register (F1*, excluding F17), (2) registrations of community‐based publicly funded substance use treatment and (3) prescriptions for the treatment of substance use disorders (ATC N07BB, N07BC02, N07BC01 and N07BC51).

Socioeconomic Status

In addition, we controlled for socioeconomic status, childhood adversity and ethnicity. Socioeconomic status was determined using the Employment Classification Module (Petersson et al. 2011; Statistics Denmark 2023), with individuals being coded as not working or studying if they were socioeconomically inactive and not enrolled in an education programme. Childhood adversity was indicated by whether an individual spent at least one year from birth until the age of criminal responsibility (age 14 from July 2010 to February 2012 and age 15 during all other periods) living with neither parent, according to the population register. Ethnicity was dichotomised as Western/non‐Western based on Statistics Denmark's classification system. Finally, we also controlled for the number of years during which the person was not living in Denmark from birth until the age of criminal responsibility.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were first calculated. Effect size was measured using Cramér's V. We considered values of V with two degrees of freedom to be small at 0.07, medium at 0.21 and large at 0.35. For comparisons with three degrees of freedom, we considered effect sizes to be small at 0.06, medium at 0.17 and large at 0.29. Adjusted prevalence with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was given as the marginal proportion of individuals in each group after logistic regression, adjusting for covariates.

Prevalence of Specific Mental Disorder Categories and Comorbidity

The prevalence of the four categories of mental disorders is shown in Table 3. Across all groups, the control group had the lowest prevalence of mental disorders. Among the offender groups, the large‐scale drug crime group had the lowest prevalence, but the differences were not substantial. A similar pattern was observed for comorbidity. In the control group, under five per cent had more than one disorder, but 15%–18% of the offender groups had at least two major mental disorders (Cramér's V = 0.2128). The highest percentage was in the small‐scale supplier group (18%), with the moderate‐scale drug crime group being very similar (17%) and the large‐scale drug crime group only slightly less affected in this regard (15%) (Cramér's V = 0.0176) (see also Supporting Information S1: Appendix Table S3).

Discussion

Our hypothesis that large‐scale drug crime offenders are less likely than moderate‐scale drug crime offenders or small‐scale suppliers to experience a mental disorder was not supported. All offender groups were, however, significantly different from the general population comparison group. For specific categories of mental disorders, a similar pattern emerged, with prevalence appearing to decrease with increasing drug crime scale, although effect sizes were small. Again, the crime groups, separately and collectively, differed more substantially from community controls.

Approximately 4%–5% of our sample with a history of drug crime had previously been diagnosed with either conduct disorder or dissocial personality disorder across the small‐, moderate‐ and large‐scale drug offender groups. Although this is a small fraction, compared to the general population controls, where less than 1% had a similar diagnosis, it is a remarkable difference. Previous studies have reported a considerably higher prevalence of conduct disorder among offenders using diagnostic assessments conducted for research purposes; for instance, a recent review found a prevalence of 43% among young people entering custody (Murie et al. 2026). In contrast, our study used administratively recorded histories of conduct disorder, making direct comparisons difficult. Our finding may nevertheless reflect substantial underdiagnosis, as no treatment programmes exist within mental health services in Denmark for either conduct disorder or dissocial personality disorder, and, thus, mental health services have little incentive to assess these conditions. In a study of the population prevalence of diagnosed conduct disorder in Denmark, Germany, Norway and the USA, Denmark had the lowest prevalence (< 1%), whereas the highest prevalence was observed in Germany (3%) (Bachmann et al. 2024).

The prevalence rates of specific diagnoses among large‐scale drug offenders were similar to those reported in Emilian et al. (2025)'s review of mental health among incarcerated individuals in general, as approximately one in five people involved in large‐scale drug crime in our study had a history of mood/anxiety disorders, compared to 17% in the review. Severe mental disorders, which we defined as diagnosed bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, schizotypal, delusional or other non‐mood psychotic disorders, or redeeming an antipsychotic medication, were, however, more common in our samples, with between 13% and 19% of drug offenders having a history of these conditions, compared to 4% with psychosis, 2% with bipolar disorder and 4% with schizophrenia spectrum disorders in the review. In the review by Emilian et al. (2025), prevalence figures varied substantially across studies (see also Edwards et al. 2025). The high prevalence observed in our study may reflect methodological differences, including our use of both diagnostic and prescription records, or the potential misdiagnosis of drug‐related psychotic episodes. Furthermore, although Emilian et al. (2025) considered only incarcerated offenders, incarceration was not required for inclusion in either the small‐ or moderate‐scale offender groups in our study.

Strengths and Limitations

We used police registers to identify people involved in serious drug crime. If people without mental disorders are more organised, they may be better at avoiding police detection and thus more likely to have been excluded from our study samples, resulting in an overestimation of the prevalence of mental disorders in our cases. Furthermore, we acknowledge that our reliance on recorded offences and prison sentence length to distinguish between populations may have resulted in misclassification. However, given that we drew on both sentencing guidelines and precedents in categorising our populations, we have used the best approximation that is available based on Danish register data alone. Future studies using a more direct measure of role and degree of involvement in drug crime would complement findings from our register‐based study. A further limitation is that our use of register‐based data, which are linked via a Danish personal identification number, meant the exclusion of foreign nationals who have committed drug crimes in Denmark but did not have a valid Danish residence permit.

Another limitation is that diagnoses were not made using structured diagnostic interviews. Although such interviews do not guarantee high validity (Chmielewski et al. 2015), they are generally considered superior to diagnoses made without the aid of an interview guide. Further, we may have failed to capture some diagnoses, such as neurodevelopmental disorders, that are more likely to be made in the school system than in the health system. Future studies using a range of clinical assessments and tests could add to the clinical picture of our sample, as could the inclusion of self‐report assessments, clinical observation and tests.

We note that pharmaceutical treatment as a proxy for mental disorders could include the off‐label use of drugs, such as the use of antipsychotic medications as sleeping medications (Cinar et al. 2025; Hojlund et al. 2019) or the use of some antidepressants for neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia or migraines (Skånland and Cieślar‐Pobuda 2019). Sleep disorders are, however, closely linked with other mental health problems, including psychosis (Merrill et al. 2023).

The main strength of this study is that we used complete data from hospitals, pharmacies and community‐based treatment throughout an entire country on a full cohort of people convicted of drug crimes and a full set of matched general population controls.

Implications for Practice

Our findings show that many drug offenders have mental health problems, thereby highlighting that a focus on mental health problems should not be limited to offenders whose crimes are less organised and goal‐directed. This finding is particularly relevant for the treatment of mental disorders in prison, as, similar to the situation in other countries (Shammas et al. 2014), people involved in serious drug crime currently constitute the largest group within the Danish prison population (24%) (Danish Prison Service K 2023). The high prevalence of mental disorders among them points to a possible need for dedicated assessment and treatment for these individuals in all custodial settings (Emilian et al. 2025).

Our findings may also highlight the importance of considering the degree to which people with mental disorders are vulnerable to exploitation by seasoned serious drug offenders (Harding 2023; Moyle 2019).

Conclusions

We found a high prevalence of mental disorders among serious drug crime offenders, including severe mental illness, although there was a surprisingly low prevalence of conduct disorder/dissocial personality disorder. The latter is likely to reflect under‐recognition of personality disorder in the registers we used but serves to underscore that most of the disorders that were recognised are well within the scope of mainstream treatment. Our findings challenge the notion that mental disorders are incompatible with engagement in serious drug crime, a category of offending that has been characterised as requiring a high degree of organisational skill and reliability as a collaborator. Mental health professionals should be aware of the risk of patients being recruited into drug crime—by definition, all those identified as having a mental disorder had been in some contact with services—whereas criminal justice services should assess people with histories of serious drug crime involvement for mental disorders.
