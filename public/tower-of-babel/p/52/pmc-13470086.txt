The Humidifier Disinfectant Disaster in Korea: Implications for Preventive Medicine and Public Health
Authors: Sue K Park, Woojin Lim, Dong-Wook Lee, Mina Ha, Chang Hyun Lee, Hae-Kwan Cheong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe humidifier disinfectant disaster in Korea was one of the most severe environmental health disasters of recent decades. It showed that indoor household chemical products used in daily life can cause fatal health effects at the population level when inhalation risks are not adequately assessed before marketing. The disaster also reaffirmed the central role of epidemiology in detecting emerging environmental health threats and guiding public health responses. Fifteen years after the disaster was recognized, major challenges remain, including defining the full spectrum of health damage, providing compensation, and ensuring long-term support. The Special Act on Remedy for Damage Caused by Humidifier Disinfectants, fully amended in 2026, represents an important institutional advance. By explicitly defining the joint liability of the state and relevant companies, the amended Act reflects a shift toward a more public, state-administered system of compensation and health support. This disaster also highlights the roles of public health professionals in generating epidemiological evidence on delayed or newly recognized diseases and sequelae, applying that evidence in clinical and policy contexts, and establishing life-course–based lifelong health management programs. For the 2026 comprehensive amendment to produce meaningful progress, continued efforts are needed to clarify long-term health effects and refine health data–based surveillance systems so that scientific evidence can be translated effectively into policy.Keywords: Humidifier disinfectant disaster, Environmental health, Epidemiology, Household products, Causality, Precautionary regulation

INTRODUCTION

The humidifier disinfectant disaster in Korea is regarded as one of the most serious environmental health disasters of recent decades [1–3]. In 2011, cases of unexplained severe lung injury were reported among infants and pregnant women, and subsequent epidemiological investigations confirmed a causal relationship with the use of humidifier disinfectants [1–3].

Subsequent clinical, epidemiological, and exposure studies demonstrated a strong association between exposure to humidifier disinfectants and acute progressive lung injury, later termed humidifier disinfectant-associated lung injury (HDLI) [1–5]. Further epidemiological and toxicological studies have since shown that exposure to humidifier disinfectants is associated not only with HDLI but also with a broader range of outcomes, including asthma and other respiratory diseases, lung cancer, tuberculosis, dermatologic diseases, ocular diseases, and fetal loss, including miscarriage and stillbirth [1–5].

Humidifier disinfectant products had been widely used in Korean households for more than a decade before the disaster was recognized, and substantial inhalation exposure is therefore considered to have occurred continuously in ordinary indoor environments [4,5]. POLICY LESSONS FROM THE DISASTER OF HOUSEHOLD CHEMICAL PRODUCTS

The major policy lessons derived from this event are as follows [1,4,5,8]: First, the disaster showed the need for stronger precautionary regulation of household chemical products. The main causes of the humidifier disinfectant disaster included the failure to ensure safety with respect to inhalation toxicity and the use of inappropriate labeling that suggested the products were harmless to the human body [1,4,5]. To prevent similar disasters from recurring, consumer chemical products such as biocides should be placed on the market only after safety evaluations that consider the intended methods and amounts of use [4,5,8]. In Korea, following the humidifier disinfectant disaster, the enactment of the “Act on the Safety Management of Household Chemical Products and Biocides” established a system under which biocidal products such as humidifier disinfectants must be approved before market release [9].

Second, the disaster demonstrated the central role of epidemiology in identifying emerging environmental health threats. Detection of unusual disease clusters and subsequent epidemiological investigations made a decisive contribution to identifying potential exposure sources and guiding public health action [1–3,5]. Without these epidemiological efforts, identifying humidifier disinfectants as the cause of the cluster would have taken much longer, and the response would have been more delayed and less effective than the response ultimately achieved. In particular, the exposure–disease–policy pathway demonstrated by the humidifier disinfectant disaster provides an important conceptual framework for understanding the interplay between health impacts and policy responses in environmental and public health (Figure 1).

Third, the disaster showed the complexity and difficulty of causal inference when relief policies are established for environmental damage. Even when strong evidence exists at the population level, establishing causality in individual cases remains challenging because exposure patterns vary across households and individuals [1,4–6]. Nevertheless, as illustrated by the United States Superfund program, damage-relief systems should be designed to provide timely support to affected individuals [5,8]. Through previous partial amendments, the former law shifted from a framework in which causality was presumed on the basis of “substantial probability,” established through both general and individual causation, to a framework in which causality is presumed when exposure occurred, disease developed after exposure, and an epidemiological association exists between exposure and disease, unless the business operator rebuts this presumption by showing that the disease was due to other causes [8].

THE 2026 COMPREHENSIVE AMENDMENT AND FUTURE TASKS

Although 15 years have passed since the sale of humidifier disinfectants was suspended, the disaster remains ongoing. The fully amended “Special Act on Remedy for Damage Caused by Humidifier Disinfectants” (hereafter, the fully amended Act), scheduled to take effect on October 8, 2026, strengthens institutional mechanisms for victim recognition, compensation, and medical monitoring while improving the existing relief system [9]. Above all, the fully amended Act moves away from the previous relief approach, which relied on company contributions, and establishes a compensation system that assigns responsibility for the cause of harm and provides reparation for damages by explicitly defining the joint liability of the state and companies related to humidifier disinfectants, thereby creating a more robust legal structure [10]. In addition, the Act places greater emphasis on compensation, memorial projects, health monitoring, and institutional support [10]. This can be interpreted as an effort to recognize the humidifier disinfectant disaster as a major environmental and social failure requiring a strong public system and to respond accordingly. Future policy efforts should focus on ensuring meaningful and substantive support for victims of the humidifier disinfectant disaster. First, continued research is needed on secondary, delayed, and newly recognized diseases associated with exposure to humidifier disinfectants [5,6,8,9]. The scope of recognized damage has expanded from the initial focus on HDLI to a broader range of diseases reviewed and recognized through the statutory epidemiological-association review process. However, further research is still needed on fetal toxicity and carcinogenicity, as well as on the burden of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases that may arise from direct toxicity or from other related diseases. Therefore, a sustained and stable support system should be established and operated for epidemiological and toxicological research on secondary, delayed, and newly recognized diseases.

Second, further scientific evidence is needed to determine whether secondary, delayed, and newly recognized diseases should be regarded as caused by humidifier disinfectants. In environmental exposure events, even when population-level evidence is sufficiently persuasive, establishing causality in individual cases often remains difficult [1,5,6,8]. The amended Act is noteworthy because it attempts to integrate epidemiological evidence systematically into the legal framework [6,10]. As scientific knowledge accumulates, determinations of whether humidifier disinfectants are related to specific health effects may change over time. In particular, the effects of exposure may not be immediately apparent and may instead manifest only after a prolonged period. Reflecting these characteristics, the amended Act has, to some extent, a structure that allows compensation and recognition systems to be continuously re-evaluated on the basis of new scientific evidence rather than remaining fixed. In addition, the government has indicated that, considering the characteristics of the disaster, in which health damage may appear gradually over a long period, additional applications for compensation may be allowed when causality is identified retrospectively or when related diseases newly develop after the application period. This approach can be understood as an attempt to recognize such damage as “delayed damage,” in which chronic effects may appear over time, and to expand both the period for exercising rights and the concept of damage accordingly. Accordingly, a scientific basis is needed to guide the Epidemiological Association Review Committee in determining whether diseases that appear after a prolonged latency should be regarded as an extension of existing damage or as new damage, and in evaluating the causal relationships of such delayed diseases.

Third, attention should be paid to victims who have not been included in the existing damage-relief system. Some individuals may not have recognized that they were exposed, even if exposure occurred, whereas others may have been excluded from registration; in addition, health problems may emerge only after a long latency period. Therefore, although the fully amended Act includes certain exceptional provisions, the application procedure should ensure sufficient inclusiveness so that individuals are not excluded because recognition of health damage is delayed.

It is therefore reasonable to build a system in which epidemiological findings can contribute more directly to legal decision-making [6]. However, the true success of the system depends not merely on the wording of legal provisions, but on whether newly generated scientific findings can continue to influence damage recognition and compensation decisions over time. In this regard, a sustained and stable support system for epidemiological and toxicological research should be established.

Finally, the amended Act aims to establish a systematic support system for long-term follow-up management [6,8]. It provides health monitoring, treatment support, and rehabilitation services as part of long-term follow-up management for victims. These forms of support should not be treated as secondary issues, but should instead be regarded as core elements of an effective response to the disaster. The actual value of such support ultimately depends on faithful implementation of the law. Because the law provides only an operational framework, this system must be sustainable, highly accessible, and flexible enough to respond promptly to changing circumstances if it is to provide practical benefits to victims.

TASKS FOR PREVENTIVE MEDICINE AND PUBLIC HEALTH

Several tasks are proposed for the fields of preventive medicine and public health. Through accumulated efforts to date, the association between exposure to humidifier disinfectants and HDLI, as well as various respiratory diseases including lung cancer, has been clearly established. However, uncertainties regarding various cancers, subclinical abnormalities, and diseases affecting other organ systems should be further reduced [1–5,7,11].

Long-term diseases, the course and prognosis of diseases resulting from exposure to humidifier disinfectants, and especially the effects of exposure during developmental periods on adult health remain ongoing issues that require diverse and continued research. In addition, more precise elucidation of health effects according to the components and types of humidifier disinfectants, the impact of mixed exposures, and factors related to susceptibility or resilience is important not only as a basis for causal inference, but also for the long-term health management of affected individuals. Therefore, multidisciplinary approaches and long-term follow-up should be maintained as key elements of future responses.

The humidifier disinfectant disaster continues to convey a sobering but realistic message: such problems can recur. Health problems may continue to occur among exposed individuals who fall outside the current administrative framework, potentially imposing a substantial burden not only on individual households but also on the national healthcare system through excess incidence of specific diseases. To detect new disease clusters caused by chemical products and toxic substances at an early stage, surveillance systems are needed that encompass not only officially recognized victims but also the broader population with potential exposure. The comprehensive report of the Special Investigation Commission on Social Disasters also recommended establishing a national poison center for nationwide toxic surveillance. Similar to post-marketing surveillance of pharmaceuticals, an integrated system should be established to detect signals of new diseases or syndromes using real-world healthcare data and national health big data.

Furthermore, efforts are required to translate scientific research findings into actual policy instruments. These include developing risk assessment guidelines for hazardous environmental factors, improving regulatory systems, and strengthening collaboration among health researchers, policymakers, and healthcare providers in environmental health. Ultimately, preventive medicine and public health should not only respond to environmental health crises, but also play a more proactive role in preventing such crises in advance.

CONCLUSION

The humidifier disinfectant disaster demonstrates the serious health damage that can result from insufficient regulation of chemical exposure in everyday environments. Korea’s experience suggests the need for stronger precautionary regulation, confirms the decisive role of epidemiology in identifying and elucidating environmental disasters, and underscores the importance of inclusive compensation systems and continuous health monitoring to address long-term health effects.

The fully amended Act represents a meaningful advancement. However, it should not be regarded as the final solution to this disaster. Continued efforts by the academic community are required to translate scientific evidence into policy.
