Effect of TR Band™ radial artery compression device—screw and air types on bleeding severity after radial arterial line removal in open-heart surgery: A randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Ali A. R. Al-Anbaki, Wafaa Abd Ali Hattab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:A safe and dependable operation that is frequently carried out in the intensive care unit and cardiac operating room is percutaneous radial arterial line cannulation. In critically ill or surgical patients, it directs fluid management and offers continuous blood pressure monitoring and blood collection for artery bypass grafting analysis. After radial line removal, there are serious consequences. China uses the TR Band™, invented by Terumo in Japan, for radial artery hemostasis following transradial surgeries. The purpose of this study was to assess how the screw and air varieties of the TR Band™ radial artery compression device affected the degree of bleeding in patients undergoing open-heart surgery following the removal of their radial artery lines.MATERIALS AND METHODS:After radial arterial line removal, 97 patients undergoing open-heart surgery were divided into three groups for the study: 35 were placed in the control group (10–15 min of manual pressure or bandage), 29 were placed in the TR Band™ screw type group (4–5 min of mechanical compression), and 33 were placed in the TR Band™ air type group (5–6 min of pneumatic pressure). The Validated Intraoperative Bleeding Scale and a Patient Identification Form (which includes clinical and demographic information) were used in a researcher-administered technique to gather data. “Early complications” were defined as local vascular issues following the removal of a radial artery line. Hemostasis times and access-site issues were noted, and the extent of the bleeding was evaluated right after the procedure.RESULTS:In terms of hemostasis following radial arterial line removal in patients undergoing open-heart surgery, the study demonstrated no discernible difference in efficacy between the TR Band™ compression device-screw type and the TR Band™ compression device-air type. When it came to bleeding severity, the screw-type device and the air-type device had the same mean rank of M.W.U. =31.03 and 31.91, respectively. A P = 0.846 > 0.05 showed that there was no significant difference, suggesting that both devices were effective in lowering bleeding severity.CONCLUSIONS:The TR Band™ radial artery compression device, both screw and air types, effectively lowers the chances of bleeding after the radial arterial line is taken out during open-heart surgery and is recommended for use by nurses.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) encompass a group of illnesses that influence the heart and blood vessels.[12345] These diseases include ischemic heart disease (IHD), coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, valvular heart disease, rheumatic heart disease, and heart failure.[67] Globally, noncommunicable diseases, especially CVDs, are the leading cause of early mortality.[89101112] According to the World Health Organization, in 2019, about 17.9 million deaths were caused by CVD, which accounts for 32% of the total worldwide mortality rates; 85% were caused by myocardial infarction and stroke.[13] According to the American Heart Association, CVD caused about 19.1 million deaths globally in 2020.[14] According to the Iraqi Ministry of Health, coronary heart disease is the most common health problem in Iraq and ranks as the leading cause of mortality.[15] IHD alone accounts for 11.2% of all deaths in the country.[16] In extreme situations, open-heart surgery is one of the most used procedures to treat IHD, such as coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and valve replacements for improving coronary circulation and lowering risks.[1718] The Bentall treatment, which includes implanting an artificial aortic valve, is another example of how heart surgery has changed over time, moving from early practices like Billroth’s pericardiectomy in 1882 to more contemporary methods.[192021]

Since the first successful heart surgery performed by Ludwig Rehn in 1896, open-heart surgery has made significant progress.[22232425] However, hemodynamic instability is a common concern in patients undergoing open-heart surgery and can lead to serious health issues and even death.[26] Several studies have identified potential contributing factors. One major cause is prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass time, which increases physiological stress and can negatively affect blood pressure regulation.

Anesthesia drugs also play a significant role, as certain agents or high doses may lead to hypotension or bradycardia. Intraoperative blood loss and inadequate volume replacement are additional factors that can compromise hemodynamic stability. Electrolyte imbalances, particularly involving potassium and calcium, can further disrupt cardiac function. Moreover, preexisting cardiac dysfunction, such as left ventricular failure, increases vulnerability. Valve-related issues and complex procedures involving prosthetic valves may also contribute.[2728]

One of the critical procedures for managing hemodynamically unstable patients is the insertion of an arterial line. This procedure is safe and secure for continuous blood pressure monitoring and for guiding therapeutic interventions.[29] The European Society of Cardiology recommends radial access because it is associated with better results for high-risk cardiac patients and has fewer complications than femoral access.[3031] Although the radial artery is preferred due to its ease of access and reduced risk of complications, patients who have hemodynamic instability, changed vascular anatomy, or obesity may face difficulties.[32]

Since radial arterial line removal frequently results in postremoval consequences, such as pain, bleeding, and hematoma, healthcare providers have a major duty to prevent these issues.[33] Usually, manual compression is a nonpharmacological approach, which is a safe technique for reducing bleeding following the removal of a radial arterial line.[34]

However, this technique may not always prevent complications, such as thrombosis, radial artery occlusion, bleeding severity, and hematoma formation, and it can be uncomfortable.[35363738] With the improvement of the quality of healthcare and medical technology, Terumo created the TR Band™ compression device, which aids in hemostasis, guarantees comfort, and reduces complications.[39] The use of compression devices, like the TR Band™, is one of the novel strategies being investigated to address these issues.

In this context, although these devices assist in preventing bleeding severity, hematoma formation, and other issues, no previous studies have been conducted on determining the effectiveness of these techniques and how well they work when radial arterial lines are removed during heart surgery.[1440]

Therefore, it seems necessary to find a suitable, effective, and safe solution for the patients undergoing radial arterial line removal.[4142] This study aims to fill this gap by comparing two techniques to reduce bleeding severity after radial arterial line removal in open-heart surgery patients: the TR Band™ compression device with a screw and the TR Band™ compression device that uses air. Identifying the most effective strategy for managing early complications compared to the conventional manual pressure method, frequently used in clinical practice, will provide valuable insights for improving patient care and outcomes. The study’s main question is: Does using a TR Band™ radial artery compression device-screw type or air type help reduce early complications and make patients more comfortable after removing a radial arterial line following open-heart surgery, compared to regular care?

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

To evaluate the impact of the TR Band™ radial artery compression device (screw and air types) on bleeding severity following radial arterial line removal in patients undergoing open-heart surgery, a prospective, interventional, parallel-group, multicentric study was carried out on 97 participants. From November 25, 2024, to February 5, 2025, the study was carried out at four different locations in Baghdad and Karbala. In Baghdad, these facilities included the Iraqi Center for Heart Disease, which is under the Medical City Directorate, and the Ibn Al-Bitar Center for Cardiac Surgery, which is under the Al-Karkh Health Directorate. Al-Imam Al-Hassan Al-Mujtaba (A.S.) Teaching Hospital, which is connected to the Karbala Health Directorate, and Ibn Al-Nafees Teaching Hospital for Cardiology, Thoracic Surgery, and Vascular Medicine, which is a part of the Rusafa Health Directorate in Baghdad, were also the sites of the study.

Adult male and female patients from the cardiac intensive care unit who satisfied the following requirements were included in the study: They had to be willing to participate, have had their radial arterial line removed following open-heart surgery, have no visible signs of necrosis, scarring, incision, or infection at the site of insertion, and have not taken any analgesics for at least 4 h before the study. Patients with a history of severe bleeding or clotting problems, arterial line cutdown, substance allergies, hemodynamic instability, reintubation, cognitive impairments, communicative impairments, or peripheral vascular disease were excluded [Figure 2].

The CONSORT flowchart of the study

Study participants and sampling

To allocate participants to a specific group of patients having radial arterial line removal following open-heart surgery, the simple random sample method was chosen. By choosing a colored card, white, pink, or green, participants in this study were randomized into one of three groups. One of the three groups, such as Control, TR Band™ screw type, and TR Band™ air type, was represented by each hue. Equal selection possibilities were guaranteed by the straightforward random sampling technique, which also made group comparison possible.[43] For a homogeneous population, this strategy worked well, guaranteeing representativeness and ease of use.[44] G-power was used to calculate the sample size, which included a probability level of 0.05, a target statistical power level of 0.8, and an expected effect size of 0.8.

Intervention

The study tool was divided into two parts. The first part included seven components: age, sex, education level, body mass index, smoking habits, surgical procedure type, and procedure time. It was centered on the sociodemographic and clinical information of patients having radial arterial line removal after open-heart surgery. In the second section, the severity of bleeding during the procedure was evaluated using the Validated Intraoperative Bleeding Severity Scale (VIBe Scale),[45] created in reaction to the US Food and Drug Administration’s demand for a validated scale for use in hemostatic agent clinical trials. Reduced patient risk is the goal of the VIBe Scale. Using precise blood loss predictions, it assesses the extent of bleeding depending on the type and rate of blood loss after radial arterial line removal.

The bleeding severity scale has five levels, ranging from no bleeding (Grade I: ≤1 mL/min) to potentially fatal blood loss (Grade V: >50 mL/min). Here are the grades: Grade I (<1 mL/min, no bleeding), Grade II (more than 1 to 5 mL/min, mild bleeding), Grade III (5 to 10 mL/min, moderate bleeding), Grade IV (10 to 50 mL/min, severe bleeding), and Grade V (more than 50 mL/min, life-threatening bleeding). Each lesion was sampled using preweighed gauze to assess blood loss. The weight difference was then calculated and multiplied by 10 to determine the rate in milliliters per minute, with 1 g of blood loss equal to 1 ml.

Its reliability, with a coefficient of r = 0.97, is backed by strong intra- and interobserver agreement, and its construct validity is validated by its usability, clarity, and relevance. The majority of surgeons concurred that the scale was pertinent for monitoring hemostasis and accurately represented the spectrum of bleeding site sizes and severities after Siegel et al.[46] evaluated surgical procedural films and coagulopathic circumstances to validate the scale’s reliability. Before utilizing the scale, the researcher had authorization from the scale’s designer. As a result of its exceptional use and dependability, the VIBe Scale is a validated and clinically relevant tool for evaluating intraoperative bleeding in clinical investigations looking into hemostatic drugs [Supplementary Figure 1].

Data collection tool and technique

Utilizing the researcher-administered method, the researcher collected data. Following an explanation of the study’s goals and methods, as well as an assurance that their information would be kept private and used only for research, all participants gave their agreement. Following their signature on the consent papers, individuals were randomly assigned to the experimental and control groups. The intervention group underwent preoperative testing, including the Allen test, and oxygen saturation levels in each finger of the hand were monitored during customized testing, whereas the control group’s patients received conventional regular care. The Allen test[47] was used to evaluate complications following radial artery installation and to determine the hand’s vascular patency before cannulation, while the participants were asked to remain seated at the edge of the bed.

The radial and ulnar arteries were then blocked after participants were told to grip their fists tightly. When the hand was reopened, it looked pallorous, or white. Within 15 to 20 s of releasing pressure on the ulnar artery, the skin should become hyperemic. Edgar Allen first proposed the Allen test in 1929 as a way to test both hands at the same time and assess the radial artery’s contribution to the distal circulation.[48]

Control group

To achieve hemostasis and stop bleeding at the puncture site, manual compression or a gauze pad was used after the radial artery line was removed. To control bleeding, the practitioner used their hand or fingers to provide direct, hard pressure to the puncture site. They had to keep up this pressure for 10–15 min. To help absorb any blood and provide extra control to maintain hemostasis, a sterile gauze pad or bandage was applied with mild pressure over the puncture site. The gauze or pad was then secured in place with a bandage or compression dressing.

Interventional groups

The radial arterial line removal postprocedural patent hemostasis approach was developed based on the researcher’s experience, prior research, manufacturer advice, and a survey of scientific literature. Proper hand alignment, cautious patient placement, and privacy preservation by merely exposing the wrist and forearm were all part of the protocol. In accordance with usual procedure, the patient was observed, clean gloves were used to prevent infection, and an absorbent pad was positioned beneath the puncture location to preserve the bed linens. To keep the puncture site clean, povidone was used once the dressing was removed.

TR Band™ radial artery compression device—Screw type

A mechanical radial compression device called the TR Band™ screw type is intended to maintain the radial artery’s patency while hemostasis is being performed. Through a mechanical screw thread and an adjustable screw cap, the device precisely regulates the pressure exerted on the radial artery and puncture site by increasing or decreasing compression. Among its key features are a mechanical screw cap that allows for precise pressure adjustment, total transparency for the best possible view, a design that guarantees the preservation of radial artery patency, and a sizable silicone pad (1 × 1.75 inches) that distributes pressure uniformly over the radial artery. After finishing the procedure, the researcher put on the TR Band™ compression device, which is a mechanical screw-type tool designed to keep the radial artery open while stopping bleeding, and pulled back the introducer radial arterial line by two to three centimeters. The transparent gadget had an adjustable screw cap to regulate pressure and provided excellent visibility of the puncture site. To keep the band from spinning, the strap was fastened to the wrist with an adjustable fastening. The radial artery line was concurrently withdrawn, and the mechanical screw cap was slowly rotated to change the compression. During the process, the transparent mechanical screw cap was adjusted to rapidly raise or lower pressure while preserving the patent hemostasis technology. If bleeding happened while the pressure was being gradually decreased, the mechanical screw cap pressure was then restored. The radial pulse was verified, and the reverse Barbeau’s test was used to assess the patency of the radial arteries. To track pulsatile waveforms, an oxygen saturation probe was applied to the thumb or index finger of the afflicted upper extremity. At the wrist level, the ulnar artery was squeezed, and the waveform behavior was seen. Hemostasis was maintained by lowering the hemostatic compression pressure until the plethysmographic waveform returned, demonstrating antegrade radial artery flow, because its absence suggested a disruption in radial artery flow. It was verified that the bleeding had ceased after the pressure was completely relaxed. To stabilize the access site, the adjustable band was undone, and light pressure was applied. After that, the band was gradually taken off by raising it toward the palm. A sterile gauze bandage was applied, being careful not to cover the entire wrist, and any blood that had pooled under the device was cleaned with a sterile swab or cotton. Finally, we evaluated the hand’s perfusion once more. The Terumo logo was placed closest to the patient’s little finger on the support plate when the device was worn on either the left or right wrist. The position of the device was changed to guarantee that the silicone pressure plate covered the puncture site and radial artery with consistent pressure. The idea was to make sure the bleeding stopped as soon as the sheath was taken off entirely.

TR Band™ radial artery compression device—Air type

The TR Band™ compression device (air type) was placed once the treatment was finished, and the researcher removed the introducer radial artery line by two to three centimeters. The compression balloon’s green marker was positioned 1–2 mm close to the puncture point. The adjustable fastener was then used to attach the strap on the wrist, making sure the device was snug enough to keep from spinning. The TR Band™ Inflator syringe was used to progressively inflate the TR Band™ compression balloon with 15–18 mL of air as the radial artery line was gradually withdrawn. The arterial line was fully withdrawn before the air was fully introduced. The patent hemostasis technique was used to titrate the air in the compression balloon by extracting 1 mL/min while monitoring the access site for bleeding symptoms. The researcher could also progressively deflate the TR Band™ compression device in a single move to expedite the process. If bleeding happened after removal, 1–2 cc of air was administered to verify patent hemostasis and reestablish hemostasis. The radial pulse was verified, and the reverse Barbeau’s test was used to assess the patency of the radial arteries. To track pulsatile waveforms, an oxygen saturation probe was applied to the thumb or index finger of the afflicted upper extremity. After compressing the ulnar artery at the wrist level, the waveform was examined. The radial artery flow was disrupted, as evidenced by the lack of a plethysmographic waveform. To preserve hemostasis and confirm antegrade radial artery flow in these situations, the hemostatic compression pressure was reduced until the waveform recovered. The bleeding was verified to have ceased after the band’s air was completely evacuated. After unfastening the adjustable band, the access site was stabilized with light pressure. The band was then carefully raised toward the palm to be taken off. A sterile gauze bandage was applied, being careful not to cover the entire wrist, and any blood that had pooled under the device was cleaned with a sterile swab or cotton. They reviewed the hand’s perfusion. Depending on whether it was used on the left or right wrist, the device’s position was changed to guarantee that the silicone pressure plate on the radial artery applied consistent pressure across the puncture site and radial artery. Throughout the postoperative phase, patients in both groups were monitored and followed up with to complete the data.

Statistical analysis method

Descriptive and inferential statistical measures were used in the analysis of the data using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA was used for statistics and the Microsoft Excel Worksheet (2010). The demographic and clinical data features of the participants, such as the number of participants (N), frequency (F), percentage (%), mean score (mean), and standard deviation (SD) for the control and study groups, were described using descriptive statistical analysis. The nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was utilized for inferential statistics to identify statistically significant variations in bleeding severity between the study and control groups. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was also used to evaluate the data’s normality.

Discussion

After removing a radial arterial line, nurses play a critical role in radial artery hemostasis. Essentially, nurses apply hemostasis techniques and monitor the access site using clinical assessments, such as visual examination and palpation. The purpose of this study was to assess the effects of the air and screw types of the TR Band™ device for radial artery compression on the degree of bleeding in patients undergoing open-heart surgery following the removal of a radial arterial line. The absence of previous studies on this topic shows that this is the first research to look at bleeding control methods after taking out a transradial arterial line in patients who have had open-heart surgery.

The results of this study show that mechanical and pneumatic compression devices, especially the TR Band compression device, can effectively decrease the severity of bleeding after open-heart surgery when radial artery lines are removed. These findings are consistent with our study aim and hypothesis, which posited that such a device would improve outcomes related to bleeding severity in this patient population. These devices take away the uncertainty of stopping bleeding in patients, provide a clear view of where the line was inserted, make it easy to monitor the process, and let you adjust the pressure when needed.[49] This is consistent with recent studies that show that mechanical and pneumatic compression devices, especially the TR Band™, are the most often used hemostasis techniques for the radial artery after coronary angiography.[5051]

After taking out the radial arterial line in patients who had open-heart surgery, the amount of bleeding was compared between a control group and three groups that used different types of the TR Band™ compression device: screw-type, air-type, and screw and air type. The control group had average bleeding ranks of 47.00 and 51.00, while the groups using the TR Band™ compression devices had average bleeding ranks of 15.00 and 17.00. The control group had average bleeding ranks of 47.00 and 51.00, while the groups using the TR Band™ compression devices had average bleeding ranks of 15.00 and 17.00. The control group experienced mean bleeding ranks of 47.00 and 51.00, while the TR Band™ compression device groups experienced mean bleeding ranks of 15.00 and 17.00. A statistically significant difference (P = 0.000 < 0.05) was seen between the control group and the TR Band™ compression device-screw and air-type groups. Furthermore, the TR Band™ compression device air-type had a mean rank of 31.91. The mean rank of bleeding for the screw type of the TR Band™ compression device was 31.03. This study suggests that postradial arterial line removal bleeding was reduced in patients undergoing open-heart surgery who participated in the TR Band™ compression device screw and air types. As demonstrated by a P = 0.846 > 0.05, there was no discernible difference between the efficacy of the TR Band™ compression device air-type and the TR Band™ compression device screw-type in lowering the severity of bleeding. These results indicate that the TR Band™ compression device works well to stop bleeding, as both the screw and air types help patients who have open-heart surgery experience much less bleeding after their radial artery lines are taken out. According to Wu et al.,[52] using pneumatic compression significantly reduces bleeding at the wrist area for patients undergoing transradial coronary intervention. These results are consistent with their findings. They differ from the results of Cong et al.,[53] who found that manually applying gauze compression and using compression band devices had similar rates of early access-site bleeding. Also, a study by Cubero et al.,[54] showed that mechanical compression devices worked just as well as manual compression for keeping blood vessels closed.

Both mechanical and pneumatic compression devices are equally safe and effective, as seen by the reassuringly identical rates between the two techniques. This research is unique because there haven’t been any previous studies that directly compare how well the TR Band™ compression device screw-type and the TR Band™ compression device air-type work to reduce bleeding after removing a radial arterial line following open-heart surgery. Statistical analysis, however, showed no significant difference between the two groups’ mean bleeding reduction rankings of 31.03 and 31.91, respectively (P = 0.846 > 0.05). This suggests that both devices can achieve hemostasis, a crucial aspect of patient safety and recovery, with comparable effectiveness. This conclusion suggests that the choice between screw and air types may not have a significant impact on clinical outcomes. These results agree with Ramos HJ et al.,[55] who discovered that the TR Band types worked just as well to manage bleeding in patients having transradial coronary intervention. Additionally, even though there were significant differences between the studies, a meta-analysis by Costa et al. and Bardooli et al.[5657] combined the results and found that there was no major change in how often bleeding occurred with improved radial artery procedures. However, some studies indicate that the effectiveness of compression devices may differ, and there is continuous discussion over the necessity and usefulness of compression devices in various situations, underscoring the need for more investigation.[58]

In general, there is not a noticeable distinction in the effectiveness of the two types of medical devices (TR Band™ Radial Artery Compression Device screw and air types) when compared to the control group. This is because mechanical compression exerts a more constant and stable pressure on the artery. Theoretically, mechanical compression is superior to manual compression in terms of hemostasis duration and bleeding intensity, which could produce more consistent and dependable outcomes. The idea behind this notion is that consistent, continuous pressure improves vascular occlusion and lowers the risk of recurrence, which is a problem that manual compression frequently faces.[59] Confirming this result in a bigger randomized experiment is recommended. Shortening the time to hemostasis may result in substantial financial savings and less nursing supervision. Busy cardiac intensive care facilities might not be able to accommodate manual compression, which calls for specialized staff. These parameters are even more important given the increasing trend of same-day discharge after radial artery line removal.

Limitation

There are certain restrictions on the study’s design (randomized clinical trial). The study gathered the sample over a specific period, potentially limiting the broad applicability of the findings. Furthermore, the sample’s four locations in Iraq may limit the generalizability of the results to other areas or populations. In addition, not all factors that affect the development of hematomas were considered in the study, including differences in surgical methods or comorbidities specific to each patient. Additionally, the brief follow-up period might not detect long-term issues.

Conclusions

The TR Band™ Radial Arterial Compression Device, whether using a screw or air, works similarly to lessen bleeding after a radial arterial line removal in open-heart surgery, according to this study. In particular, this method is useful because it reduces the amount of time and effort required to erase lines. Overall, the study indicates that this safe, low-cost medical device could increase patient satisfaction. It also provides nurses with practical methods for managing early problems, which ultimately enhances the long-term care of patients following open-heart surgery. To support the future execution of research projects, this study provides a valuable resource for the Ministry of Health and research stakeholders, including academics and medical professionals. Additionally, it promotes the inclusion of nonpharmacological, noninvasive techniques, such as cutting-edge hemostatic devices, in medical education programs by the Ministry of Higher Education to enhance the treatment of early issues in patients having open-heart surgery.
