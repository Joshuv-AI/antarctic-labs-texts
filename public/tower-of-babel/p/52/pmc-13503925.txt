Electro-optical nonlinearity and Kerr effect engineering in SnO2-doped nematic liquid crystal for advanced photonic applications
Authors: Mahsa Khadem Sadigh, A H Moayednia, Amid Ranjkesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study reports a concentration- and temperature-dependent analysis of the Kerr effect and third-order nonlinear optical response in SnO2 NP-ML-1102 composites (0.1–0.9 wt%). Ordinary and extraordinary refractive indices, birefringence, normalized polarizabilities, and Kerr constants were analyzed to investigate the optical and electro-optical characteristics of the host medium. Positive birefringence was maintained across the investigated temperature range for all compositions. The Kerr constant and the derived third-order susceptibility χ(3) exhibited a clear dependence on nanoparticle loading, with the highest values observed near the nematic-isotropic transition for the 0.7 wt% composite. However, compositions outside this concentration range showed comparatively reduced nonlinear response. The pre-transitional temperature (T*), determined from the inverse Kerr constant behavior, displayed a non-monotonic variation with nanoparticle concentration, consistent with phenomenological expectations for fluctuation-mediated electro-optical effects. The results demonstrate that nanoparticle incorporation provides a practical route for adjusting nonlinear electro-optical behavior in nematic liquid crystal systems.Keywords: Kerr Constant, Liquic Crystal, Nanoparticle, Pre-transition, Third order susceptibilitySubject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Liquid crystals (LCs) are a group of materials that exhibit orientational order combined with fluidic behavior, leading to remarkable electro-optical properties such as field-induced birefringence and refractive index modulation. These characteristics have made LCs essential components in modern photonic technologies including displays, optical switches, spatial light modulators, and adaptive optical systems1–7. In addition to their linear electro-optical response, liquid crystals also demonstrate pronounced nonlinear effects such as the Kerr effect, which is crucial for next-generation photonic applications8–10.

The Kerr effect is a quadratic electro-optical phenomenon in which an external electric field induces birefringence in an initially isotropic medium. In this effect, the induced refractive index change is proportional to the square of the applied electric field, and its magnitude is described by the Kerr constant. This phenomenon is of particular importance in nonlinear optics due to its role in optical modulation, optical switching, and tunable photonic applications11,12. This effect has also been extensively studied in liquid crystal systems, where the induced optical anisotropy under an external electric field can be much stronger than in ordinary isotropic media.

However, the Kerr constant and nonlinear susceptibility of conventional nematic liquid crystals are often insufficient for advanced applications. This limitation has motivated extensive research aimed at enhancing and engineering their nonlinear responses13,14. Early studies demonstrated that under specific conditions, nematic liquid crystals can exhibit significantly enhanced Kerr responses. Notably, the Jannossy effect and colossal optical nonlinearity provide examples where the Kerr effect is amplified by several orders of magnitude. These pioneering observations, reported more than two decades ago, underscore the remarkable tunability of nonlinear optical properties in liquid crystals and provide an important context for contemporary research efforts aimed at tailoring Kerr nonlinearity.

One effective strategy to improve the electro-optical behavior of liquid crystals is the incorporation of nanoparticles into the liquid crystal matrix. Nanoparticle-doped liquid crystals have been widely reported to exhibit modified dielectric anisotropy, threshold behavior, birefringence, and response dynamics, depending on nanoparticle type, concentration, and dispersion characteristics. The presence of nanoparticles can alter molecular alignment and introduce additional polarization mechanisms, thereby amplifying nonlinear electro-optical effects including the Kerr response15–18. Various nanomaterials such as metallic nanoparticles19,20, metal oxides21–23, magnetic nanoparticles24, and quantum dots25,26 have been explored as dopants in liquid crystals. In particular, tin oxide (SnO2) nanoparticles have attracted growing attention due to their unique characteristics12. Despite the extensive use of SnO2-based nanomaterials in photonic applications, systematic investigations of their influence on electro-optical nonlinearity and Kerr behavior in nematic liquid crystals are still scarce. Although enhancement of electro-optic nonlinearity through nanoparticle doping has been explored in many liquid crystal systems, the underlying mechanisms are not universally identical and often depend strongly on dopant concentration as well as thermodynamic conditions near phase transitions. Reported behaviors frequently deviate from simple monotonic trends, indicating that nanoparticle inclusion may simultaneously promote local ordering while disturbing long-range orientational correlations. Under such conditions, a systematic concentration-dependent investigation becomes crucial for distinguishing physical effects from generic additive interpretations. In particular, nonlinear optical responses observed near the nematic-isotropic transition are highly sensitive to fluctuation dynamics, meaning that even small modifications of intermolecular interactions can lead to qualitatively different electro-optic behavior.

Engineering the Kerr effect via SnO2 nanoparticle doping provides an effective platform not only for enhancing third-order nonlinear optical responses but also for systematically probing the fundamental electro-optical mechanisms governing liquid crystal nanocomposites.

In this study, the primary objective is to quantitatively determine the ordinary and extraordinary refractive indices of a pure nematic liquid crystal and SnO2 NP-ML-1102 composites with different weight fractions. Furthermore, the normalized ordinary and extraordinary polarizabilities of these systems are evaluated as a function of temperature in order to determine the influence of nanoparticle incorporation and thermal effects. Based on these measurements, the Kerr constant and the third-order nonlinear susceptibility (ꭓ(3)) are subsequently extracted over a range of temperatures, thereby enabling a comprehensive analysis of concentration- and temperature-dependent nonlinear electro-optic behavior in SnO2/LC composites. Rather than presuming a uniform increase of nonlinear response, the present work emphasizes the identification of concentration-dependent regimes and the correlation of multiple experimental observables to clarify the physical origin of Kerr nonlinearity in SnO2 NP-ML-1102 composites.

Experimental Section

Materials

In the present work, the nematic liquid crystal ML-1102 was used as the host due to its stable mesophase characteristics and a well-defined nematic-isotropic phase transition at approximately 80 °C. Tin oxide (SnO2) nanoparticles and ML-1102 liquid crystal were supplied by Merck. According to the information reported by the company, the SnO2 nanoparticles have a spherical shape, an average particle size of 20 to 30 nm, and high purity (99%). These characteristics make them suitable for incorporation into liquid crystal hosts, as the small and uniform nanoparticles are less prone to aggregation and can interact effectively with the surrounding molecules. To prepare the composites, SnO2 nanoparticles were first dispersed in ethanol at a 1:20 nanoparticle to solvent ratio to reduce the risk of agglomeration. The suspension was subjected to ultrasonication for six hours at room temperature to ensure uniform distribution. To prepare the liquid crystal- nanoparticle composites at the desired weight fractions, measured portions of this nanoparticle suspension were added to the ML-1102 host. Controlled heating was applied during the ethanol evaporation to maintain a homogeneous dispersion. Visual inspection and reproducible optical measurements confirmed the stability of the dispersion, even at the highest loading of 0.9 wt%. All electro-optical measurements were conducted at temperatures above the nematic-isotropic transition (⁓80 °C), where the liquid crystal is in the isotropic phase. In this uniform fluid phase, the likelihood of nanoparticle sedimentation or aggregation affecting the results is minimal.

Refractive index and polarizability measurements

The ordinary (no) and extraordinary (ne) refractive indices of both pure and SnO2 NP-ML-1102 composites were determined using an Abbe refractometer (Bellingham Stanley Abbe 60ED) equipped with a temperature-controlled stage. Measurements were carried out at several temperatures within the nematic phase region, extending toward the nematic-isotropic transition temperature. Based on the experimentally measured refractive indices, the normalized ordinary and extraordinary polarizabilities were calculated for each sample. Their temperature dependence was analyzed in order to assess the influence of nanoparticle concentration and thermal variation on molecular ordering and optical anisotropy within the nematic host.

Experimental setup for measuring the Kerr constant

The electro-optical response of pure and SnO2 NP-ML-1102 composites in the isotropic phase was studied using a null-intensity optical configuration (Fig. 1)27–29. This experimental setup was used to measure the field-induced birefringence. In the absence of an electric field, the cell remained, optically dark; however upon application of an external field, measurable intensity modulation was observed due to induced birefringence. A continuous-wave solid-state laser with a wavelength of 532 nm and an output power of 5 mW served as the probe beam. In the isotropic phase, the linear electro-optical response is negligible. Therefore, variations in the transmitted intensity were monitored to evaluate the Kerr-type electro-optical response. Measurements were performed using a quartz cell with an optical path length of 10 mm, assembled following the Haltner-Konski arrangement30.

Experimental set up.

An alternating electric field at 1 kHz was applied via stainless-steel electrodes separated by a Teflon spacer, forming a parallel-plate geometry, providing a uniform electric field calculated as E = V/d. Owing to the small electrode gap relative to the lateral dimensions of the electrodes, the field distribution within the measurement region can be reasonably treated as uniform. The excitation frequency was chosen to minimize ionic and electrodynamic contributions, thereby ensuring that the observed response reflects a genuine Kerr-type behavior. Temperature control was achieved using a PT-100 sensor with an accuracy of ± 0.1 °C. The polarizer and analyzer were arranged in a crossed configuration and oriented at 45º relative to the electric field direction. A rectangular voltage waveform was supplied by a high-voltage source. The transmitted light, after passing through a quarter-wave plate, was detected by a photodetector and recorded using a digital oscilloscope (GPS1202XE).

Results and discussions

Linear refractive index of LC-NP composites at different temperatures

The temperature dependence of the ordinary (no), extraordinary (ne), and isotropic (niso) refractive indices was investigated for the pure ML-1102 nematic liquid crystal and for SnO2 NP-ML-1102 composites with different nanoparticle weight fractions. As shown in Fig. 2, for all studied composites and across the entire nematic temperature range, the extraordinary refractive index (ne) remained higher than the ordinary refractive index (no), confirming that the system exhibits positive birefringence (Δn = ne-no) (Fig. 3). This behavior is consistent with the optical anisotropy expected in calamitic liquid crystals, where molecular polarizability along the director exceeds that perpendicular to it. With increasing temperature, a gradual decrease in the extraordinary refractive index was observed for all samples, while the ordinary refractive index showed an opposite increasing trend. This behavior can be attributed to the progressive reduction of orientational order within the nematic phase as thermal fluctuations become more pronounced. As the temperature approaches the nematic-isotropic phase transition, both refractive indices converge toward the isotropic value, reflecting the progressive loss of long-range orientational order. The incorporation of SnO2 nanoparticles significantly modifies the refractive index values, and this modification strongly depends on the nanoparticle concentration. Among the investigated compositions, the sample containing 0.7 wt% SnO2 exhibited the highest refractive index values, while deviations at both lower and higher concentrations suggesting a balance between nanoparticle-induced ordering and disordering effects. At moderate concentrations, interfacial interactions may influence local molecular organization, whereas excessive loading may introduce structural perturbations or aggregation effects.

Such controllability is particularly advantageous for temperature-sensitive photonic and electro-optical applications.

Kerr constant and pre-transitional temperature of LC-NP composites

The Kerr constant of pure ML-1102 and SnO2 NP-ML-1102 composites was determined using a null-intensity optical technique in the isotropic phase. In this method, the electrically induced birefringence generates a phase difference δ between light polarized parallel and perpendicular to the applied field. By plotting Δn as a function of the square of the applied electric field (E2), a linear relationship is obtained, and the slope of this line provides a direct measure of the Kerr constant (B) (Δn = λBE2). This procedure enables reliable evaluation of the electro-optical nonlinearity over the investigated temperature. To further support this analysis, the variation of induced birefringence with the square of applied electric field is presented for the pure liquid crystal and SnO2-doped composites near the transition temperature. As shown in Fig. 5, Δn increases approximately linearly with E2 over the studied field range for all samples, confirming the quadratic electro-optical response expected for the Kerr effect. The differences in slope among the compositions indicate a clear dependence on nanoparticle concentration and temperature. In particular, the doped samples exhibit higher slopes compared to the pure liquid crystal, reflecting an enhancement of the field-induced response. This behavior can be associated with nanoparticle-induced modifications of local molecular environments and intermolecular interactions, which increase the susceptibility of molecular clusters to the applied electric field. At low concentrations, the slope becomes more pronounced, suggesting an optimal balance between enhanced local ordering and minimal structural disturbance, while deviations at higher concentrations may arise from aggregation or increased disorder.

Near the nematic-isotropic transition, a noticeable enhancement of Kerr response is observed in the SnO2 NP-ML-1102 composites compared to the pure liquid crystal. At temperatures close to the transition, the largest Kerr constant is recorded for the 0.1 wt% doped sample, followed by 0.3 wt%, 0.7 wt%, 0.5 wt%, 0.9 wt%, and finally the pure liquid crystal (Fig. 7(b)). 0.1 wt% sample exhibits the highest Kerr constant, whereas the differences among samples gradually diminish at higher temperatures due to the dominance of thermal disorder. The observed decrease in the Kerr constant with increasing temperature can be attributed to the gradual weakening of short-range orientational order of thermal fluctuations. The reduction of Kerr constant with temperature can be associated with gradual weakening of short range orientational correlations in the isotropic phase. As thermal fluctuations intensify, the field induced alignment of liquid crystal molecules becomes less effective, resulting in smaller induced birefringence. The concentration-dependent behavior further indicates that nanoparticle incorporation affects the balance between local ordering and structural perturbations. At low to moderate nanoparticle concentrations, interfacial interactions and local effects may enhance molecular correlations, whereas higher concentrations can introduce aggregation or excessive local disturbances. This delicate balance explains the non-monotonic variation of the Kerr constant with both temperature and nanoparticle concentration.

Reported Kerr constants span several orders of magnitude depending on molecular structure, phase symmetry, and measurement conditions1–5. It is important to emphasize that direct quantitative comparison between different studies is intrinsically limited, since Kerr nonlinearity is highly sensitive to experimental parameters such as wavelength, temperature, electric field strength, and cell configuration. For this reason, comparison at the level of order of magnitude provides a more physically meaningful assessment. Within this established framework, the Kerr constants measured for the SnO2/ML-1102 composites lie well within the characteristic range previously reported for nanoparticle-modified nematic liquid crystals. The comparison further indicates that differences between material systems primarily reflect variations in dominant physical mechanisms rather than implying absolute superiority of any specific composition.

The pre-transitional temperature (T*) was determined from the temperature dependence of the inverse Kerr constant (B− 1) within the Landau-de Gennes framework. The linear behavior of B− 1 versus temperature in the isotropic phase allows reliable extraction of T* (Fig. 8). The obtained T* values display a non-monotonic dependence on nanoparticle concentration, with a maximum near 0.5 wt% SnO2. This behavior suggests that nanoparticle incorporation modifies short-range orientational correlations governing the pre-transitional regime. Lower or higher nanoparticle loadings result in slightly reduced T*, which may reflect weaker interfacial coupling or enhanced structural perturbations. The pure liquid crystal exhibits the lowest T*, consistent with the absence of nanoparticle-induced effects. Overall, the combined analysis of the Kerr constant and pre-transitional temperature demonstrates that SnO2 nanoparticle incorporation provides an effective route for tuning electro-optical nonlinearity. The observed concentration-dependent behavior highlights the sensitivity of fluctuation-mediated nonlinear response to nanoscale inclusions.

Conclusions

This experimental work shows that the incorporation of SnO2 nanoparticles can effectively modify both the linear and nonlinear optical characteristics of ML-1102 nematic liquid crystals. The combined analysis of temperature-dependent refractive indices, normalized polarizabilities, and Kerr constants reveals a clear concentration-dependent quadratic (Kerr) electro-optical response, while linear electro-optic contributions are negligible under the experimental conditions. Among the investigated compositions, intermediate nanoparticle concentrations, particularly 0.7 wt%, exhibit the strongest nonlinear behavior in the vicinity of the nematic-isotropic transition. The observed non-monotonic variation of the Kerr constant and third order nonlinear susceptibility with nanoparticle loading indicates that the quadratic electro-optic (Kerr) response is governed by a balance between nanoparticle-induced effects and concentration-dependent structural influence. Changes in the pre-transitional temperature further suggest that nanoparticle incorporation alters the effective pre-transitional behavior of the host liquid crystal. Overall, these results highlight the sensitivity of quadratic (Kerr) electro-optical nonlinearity to controlled nanoscale inclusions and provide an experimental framework for tuning nonlinear optical responses in nematic liquid crystal systems.
