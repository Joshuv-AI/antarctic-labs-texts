A finite element reliability analysis platform for damage index-based fragility assessment of RC bridges
Authors: Hyeonsung Song, Minsun Kim, Young-Joo Lee, Tae-Hoon Kim, Seungjun Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, a computational platform is developed for fragility assessment of reinforced concrete (RC) bridges using a damage index (DI)-based approach. The platform integrates a reliability analysis in conjunction with sophisticated nonlinear finite element (FE) analysis. In this process, the first-order reliability method enables efficient probabilistic evaluation, while the FE analysis provides DI values that directly quantify bridge damage condition. The platform was applied to a hollow RC bridge pier under cyclic loading, and fragility curves were derived for multiple damage levels calibrated by experimental thresholds. Compared with Monte Carlo simulation, the proposed platform achieved similar accuracy with far fewer analyses, requiring only 80 runs instead of 2,711. A parametric study further investigated the effect of threshold uncertainty on fragility curves. These results demonstrate the potential of the proposed platform as an efficient tool for reliability-based vulnerability assessment and the derivation of seismic fragility curves.

Introduction

Earthquakes pose a significant threat to bridges because of the risk of catastrophic structural failures, economic disruptions, and enduring social impacts1–3. Therefore, seismic risk assessments and mitigation strategies have become essential components of structural engineering, urban planning, and infrastructure management. Fragility curves play a crucial role in evaluating structural vulnerabilities and guiding decision making for effective disaster mitigation4,5. For example, seismic risk assessment software such as HAZUS-MH6 systematically employs fragility curves to predict bridge performance and estimate post-earthquake losses. Similarly, IN-CORE7, SYNER-G8, and OpenQuake9 integrate fragility curves into their analytical frameworks. These risk-assessment tools enable stakeholders to assess potential bridge damage scenarios and optimize mitigation measures. Given their importance in critical infrastructure management and disaster mitigation strategies, extensive research continues to focus on refining the methods for developing accurate and computationally efficient fragility curves10.

Seismic fragility curves quantify the probability that a structure will reach or exceed specific damage states, given an intensity measure of the hazard, while explicitly accounting for uncertainties in the structural responses. Fragility curves are typically derived using three primary methods11: (1) judgment-based, (2) empirical, and (3) analytical methods. Judgment-based and empirical methods rely mainly on observational information, such as expert opinions or statistical analyses of the observed damage from historical earthquakes. However, these approaches have inherent limitations including subjectivity, variability in expert judgments, and reliance on limited or inconsistent post-earthquake damage data. Analytical approaches employ numerical simulations grounded in structural modeling and have been widely adopted for their applicability to diverse structural systems. In recent years, these methods have increasingly been integrated with surrogate models and advanced artificial intelligence techniques, including machine learning and deep learning12–15. Nevertheless, their accuracy remains highly dependent on the underlying modeling assumptions and simulation strategies16,17.

Each of these methods has distinct strengths and limitations. Thus, selecting an appropriate methodology requires careful consideration of data availability, computational resources, and the desired level of accuracy. In particular, analytical methods require sophisticated structural analyses, such as finite element (FE) analysis, to achieve accurate fragility assessments18,19. Several studies have demonstrated the importance of detailed structural modeling for deriving the seismic fragility curves of bridges. For instance, Zhang and Huo20 developed analytical fragility curves based on bridge displacement to quantify seismic vulnerabilities. Nielson and DesRoches21 used the drift ratios as indicators to evaluate the seismic performance of highway bridges. Similarly, Kumar and Gardoni22 adopted strain measures obtained through FE simulations to assess structural vulnerabilities. These studies collectively illustrate that an accurate fragility curve derivation can be achieved through detailed structural modeling combined with appropriate response indicators.

Detailed structural modeling is essential for accurately capturing responses in seismic fragility assessments. However, the reliability of these assessments also depends on how key uncertainties, such as structural capacity and associated thresholds, are characterized and incorporated. Although structural response parameters such as ductility, drift ratios, and displacement are widely used, they do not directly correspond to damage states23–25. Consequently, fragility analyses often depend on predefined capacity thresholds derived from preliminary simulations, such as pushover analyses18,26. However, the seismic capacity is highly sensitive to variations in material properties, structural configurations, and loading conditions, which can introduce substantial epistemic and aleatory uncertainties into the assessment process. For instance, HAZUS-MH assigns a coefficient of variation (COV) of 0.3 to account for the variability in structural capacity6. However, the uncertainty in capacity thresholds used to determine structural damage levels was reported to vary depending on structural parameters and damage levels19. Therefore, using a fixed COV regardless of damage level may provide inaccurate seismic fragility curves, as the introduced index cannot represent structural damage directly.

To address these limitations, several studies have adopted damage index (DI)-based approaches that aim to quantify structural damage directly. Unlike conventional methods, which often rely on uncertain capacity thresholds, DI-based approaches define damage states on the basis of physically observable and experimentally calibrated criteria. This approach can potentially reduce the epistemic uncertainty associated with structural damage. Singhal and Kiremidjian27 developed fragility curves for reinforced concrete (RC) frames by employing nonlinear time-history analysis with DIs and incorporating ground motion uncertainty through Monte Carlo simulation (MCS). Dumova-Jovanoska28 generated fragility curves for RC buildings by applying a modified Park and Ang damage model23 to pushover analysis results under incrementally increasing lateral load patterns. Jeong and Elnashai29 introduced a three-dimensional spatial DI and conducted bidirectional nonlinear time-history analyses to capture the torsional effects in irregular RC buildings. Kappos and Panagopoulos30 employed a hybrid methodology based on nonlinear static and dynamic analyses to construct fragility curves for representative RC typologies in Greece. Zhang et al.31 proposed a material-based three-dimensional DI for RC columns, providing physically grounded measures that can inform refined fragility assessments. Although DI-based approaches offer a physically meaningful representation of structural damage, their practical application in reliability-based fragility assessments remains limited. This is primarily due to the lack of systematic integration with probabilistic analysis frameworks and the substantial computational cost required for repeated nonlinear simulations.

To bridge this gap, this study develops a computational platform that integrates a recently proposed and experimentally validated DI for RC structures32,33 with the probabilistic analysis software FERUM35. The DI defines multiple damage states, from initial cracking to ultimate fracture, on a continuous scale from 0 (undamaged) to 1 (collapsed), and is implemented in the nonlinear FE software RCAHEST32–34. While RCAHEST can quantitatively evaluate progressive structural damage, its potential for probabilistic applications has not yet been fully explored. In the proposed platform, FERUM iteratively calls RCAHEST to compute DI values during reliability analysis, thereby enabling fragility curves to be derived directly from a physically meaningful damage measure rather than from indirect response parameters. To demonstrate its applicability, the platform was applied to a previously validated hollow RC bridge pier. Seismic fragility curves were derived based on the DI thresholds and compared with those obtained from MCS to verify accuracy and efficiency. In addition, a parametric study was conducted to investigate the influence of structural capacity uncertainty on the fragility estimates.

Proposed computational platform

Several computational platforms that integrate sophisticated FE models with reliability analysis methods have been developed to efficiently evaluate structural reliability and derive fragility curves. Since the early efforts of Der Kiureghian and Taylor36, who first established the link between reliability algorithms and structural analysis, extensive efforts have been made to combine specialized structural analysis software with reliability assessment tools. For instance, computational platforms such as FERUM–ABAQUS37,38, FERUM–ZEUS18,19,39, the Python-based Interface for FERUM and ABAQUS (PIFA)40–42, and FERUM–MIDAS43 have been developed to leverage advanced structural analysis capabilities and accurately capture complex structural behaviors in reliability assessments. These integrated computational platforms primarily rely on structural response-based criteria such as ductility, drift ratios, or displacements to define limit states. In contrast, the platform proposed in this study directly incorporates an experimentally validated DI into the reliability analysis, providing a physically based alternative to conventional response measures. This section describes the architecture of the proposed FE model-based reliability analysis platform developed for deriving seismic fragility curves of RC bridges.

Reliability analysis method

This conditional probability is typically quantified using sampling-based methods such as MCS, which estimates the failure probability by performing numerous structural analyses on randomly generated samples. Although this approach is robust and accurate, its computational cost becomes prohibitive when dealing with time-consuming simulations or low-probability events18. Therefore, to address these computational challenges efficiently, this study adopts the first-order reliability method (FORM), a non-sampling-based analytical approach.

To derive the fragility curves, this study employs FORM, which significantly reduces computational effort by linearizing the limit-state function. This linearization is performed at the most probable point (MPP), a critical concept in reliability theory and conceptually illustrated in Fig. 1. Nonlinear FE analysis using RCAHEST

RCAHEST (Reinforced Concrete Analysis in Higher Evaluation System Technology) is a nonlinear FE analysis program specifically developed to evaluate the structural performance of RC structures under various loading conditions, including seismic actions. It was originally built on the general-purpose FE analysis platform FEAP ver. 7.2, developed by Taylor at the University of California, Berkeley45, and has since been extended with specialized modules such as RC plane-stress elements, interface elements, and joint elements. RCAHEST incorporates advanced constitutive models for both concrete and reinforcing steel, employs non-orthogonal fixed-crack models based on the smeared-crack approach, and explicitly accounts for critical RC behaviors including tension stiffening, compression softening, shear transfer, bond-slip, reinforcement buckling, and confinement effects32,46.

Concrete material modeling in RCAHEST explicitly distinguishes between uncracked and cracked concrete states. For cracked concrete, directional models are employed to separately capture the normal, tangential, and shear responses across the crack plane using the nonorthogonal fixed-crack approach proposed by Kim et al.47. The constitutive model for reinforcing steel adopts a bilinear stress–strain relationship derived from the parametric studies by Kim et al.47, which specifically incorporates the effects of bond characteristics and rebar buckling. Additionally, the confinement of the concrete core provided by the transverse reinforcement is modeled using the confinement effectiveness approach proposed by Mander et al.48, ensuring accurate simulation of enhanced concrete ductility under cyclic loading conditions32,47.

A key feature of RCAHEST is its capability to simulate the progressive damage states of RC bridge piers using a modified DI for hollow RC bridge piers32. This DI numerically quantifies the structural damage on the basis of experimentally validated criteria, including initial flexural cracking, concrete cover spalling, longitudinal rebar buckling, and ultimate rebar fracture. The damage states are represented on a numerical scale ranging from 0.0 (undamaged) to 1.0 (collapsed), with distinct intermediate values clearly associated with specific physical damage conditions. For instance, a DI of 0.1 corresponds to minor yielding of reinforcing bars without buckling, whereas a value of 0.4 denotes significant damage characterized by concrete cover spalling due to severe flexural or shear cracking. These damage states are systematically linked to specific structural performance levels, as summarized in Table 1, facilitating practical explanations of seismic damage and decision-making regarding repair and serviceability. The threshold values of the DI were originally calibrated by comparing RCAHEST simulations with cyclic loading tests of RC bridge columns, where DI progressions were matched to observed damage states such as cracking, spalling, buckling, and fracture46,47. Its validity was further confirmed through quasi-static cyclic loading tests on hollow RC bridge piers, which demonstrated close agreement between DI values and observed damage progression (i.e., cracking, spalling, buckling, fracture), as well as global strength and ductility capacities32.

The modeling features enable RCAHEST to simulate the nonlinear behavior and progressive damage of RC bridges under seismic loading with improved accuracy. By quantitatively evaluating local damage mechanisms, such as cracking, spalling, and reinforcement buckling, the extended DI provides a consistent basis for defining damage states. In particular, the use of a DI facilitates the direct interpretation of structural performance levels, which can be systematically linked to repair strategies and serviceability considerations. In RCAHEST, these DIs are computed at the Gauss integration points of each element, capturing localized material behavior. For subsequent use in reliability analysis, the values are post-processed to derive representative element- and global-level indices.

Integration of FERUM and RCAHEST

To evaluate the seismic fragility of RC bridges by considering uncertainty factors, this paper proposes an integrated computational platform called FERUM–RCAHEST, which systematically combines the reliability analysis software, FERUM, with the nonlinear FE analysis software, RCAHEST. FERUM (Finite Element Reliability Using MATLAB) is an open-source reliability analysis program developed for structural and geotechnical engineering applications. It implements reliability methods such as FORM, SORM, and simulation-based approaches, and can be coupled with external FE solvers through a flexible interface35,49. As described in Sect. 2.1, FERUM can provide probabilistic evaluations using FORM, and RCAHEST can accurately simulate the nonlinear structural responses of RC bridges subjected to seismic loading and quantitatively evaluates structural performance on the basis of DI, as detailed in Sect. 2.2. However, RCAHEST alone lacks the inherent capability to conduct reliability analyses, such as FORM or MCS, thereby limiting its direct applicability to probabilistic seismic fragility assessments. By integrating FERUM’s reliability analysis with RCAHEST’s simulation strengths, the proposed platform overcomes this limitation. This enables a comprehensive and efficient seismic fragility assessment of RC bridge structures.

The proposed FERUM–RCAHEST platform automates the derivation of fragility curves by establishing an iterative loop between reliability analysis and structural simulation. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the process consists of the following steps:

Setup: Random variables (e.g., material strengths, load factors) and limit-state functions (defined in terms of DI thresholds) are specified within the FERUM environment.Input generation: For each iteration, FERUM generates a deterministic realization of the random variables and transfers these values to RCAHEST.FE analysis and damage evaluation: RCAHEST performs nonlinear FE analysis under the prescribed seismic loading conditions and computes the corresponding DI values.Feedback and update: The computed DI values are returned to FERUM, which evaluates the limit-state functions and updates the reliability index and failure probability accordingly.Convergence and fragility curve derivation: Steps (2)–(4) are repeated until the reliability analysis converges on a stable estimate of failure probability for a given seismic intensity level. By repeating this procedure across multiple intensity levels, the platform produces a set of exceedance probabilities (shown as white circles in Fig. 2), which are then fitted to obtain continuous fragility curves directly linked to physically meaningful damage states.

Application examples

To test the proposed FERUM–RCAHEST computational platform, it was applied to an RC structure, and for a comparison purpose, the hollow RC bridge pier, which had been investigated in a previous study32, was introduced as the target structure of this study. Nonlinear FE analyses were conducted using RCAHEST to simulate the structural responses under seismic loading conditions, explicitly incorporating the structural capacities, seismic demands, and associated uncertainties into the reliability analysis. The structural performance levels and corresponding damage states were quantitatively defined using DI-based limit states, enabling a systematic derivation of the seismic fragility curves. To validate the consistency and accuracy of the proposed platform, a comparative verification analysis using MCS was performed for a selected case. In addition, a parametric study was conducted to investigate the influence of the threshold value uncertainty on seismic fragility curves.

Target structure: Hollow RC Bridge pier

The target structure modeled in this study was a hollow RC bridge pier that was experimentally tested and validated by Kim et al.32. The pier has a circular cross-section and is designed to reduce material usage while maintaining adequate strength and seismic performance. It adopts a conventional reinforcement layout with longitudinal and transverse bars placed along both the outer and inner perimeters. The pier was designed in accordance with the AASHTO LRFD Bridge Design Specifications49 with an external diameter of 1400 mm, an internal diameter of 980 mm, and an effective height of 4900 mm, as shown in Fig. 3.

Based on this structural configuration and the material properties, the following modeling approach was adopted: To perform a nonlinear FE analysis, the original three-dimensional geometry of the bridge pier was converted into a two-dimensional equivalent plane-stress model compatible with the RCAHEST software. This conversion preserved key structural characteristics, accurately representing the original behavior (see Fig. 4).

Figure 5a shows the cyclic loading protocol employed in the nonlinear FE analysis conducted in this study. This protocol, adopted from the experimental validation, was applied at the top of the pier to simulate seismic loading conditions. The loading was displacement controlled and followed a sequence of increasing drift ratio amplitudes, ranging from 0.25% to 5.0%, with each amplitude repeated for two cycles. Additionally, a constant axial load equivalent to 10% of the axial capacity of the pier was applied to the top of the column throughout the analysis to simulate the loading effect of the bridge superstructures.

The structural damage induced by the cyclic loading was quantitatively assessed at the base of the pier, where it connected to the foundation. Figure 5(b) shows the resulting damage progression, which was evaluated using the DI based on the criteria defined in Eqs. (3) and (5). DI numerically represents the structural condition, ranging from no damage (DI = 0) to complete failure (DI = 1), and it increases progressively with each load step. As the drift ratio amplitude increased, the increments in DI became more pronounced, eventually reaching a value of 1.0 at approximately 8600 load steps. At this stage, significant damage characterized by extensive concrete crushing and reinforcement buckling or fracture was observed, closely aligning with the experimental damage pattern shown in Fig. 5b. The adopted modeling approach effectively captured the cyclic response and progressive damage patterns observed experimentally in the bridge pier. Detailed discussions of the experimental setup and observed structural performance are available in a previous study32.

Fragility analysis of the target structure

Deriving the seismic fragility curves required defining random variables for uncertainties in material properties, structural capacity, and loading, along with corresponding limit-state functions. Although various sources of uncertainty could be considered, three random variables were used in this study for the sake of simplicity: the compressive strength of concrete (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\it \:{\text{f}}_{\text{ck}}$$\end{document}), the yield strength of reinforcing steel (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\it \:{\text{f}}_{\text{yt}}$$\end{document}), and a structural capacity uncertainty factor (γ). The statistical characteristics of the concrete and steel strengths were defined on the basis of previous studies51–53, and the COV of γ was initially set to 0.3 as recommended by HAZUS-MH6. Additionally, a parametric study was conducted by varying the COV of γ from 0.1 to 0.3 to systematically investigate its effect on the resulting fragility curves. The load scale factor (ξ) was employed as the seismic IM and was incrementally varied across a predefined range to construct the fragility curves. Within each IM level, ξ was also considered as a random variable following a Gumbel distribution with a COV of 0.25 to account for the inherent variability in seismic demand54. The mean values, COVs, and probability distributions of the random variables, which were assumed to be statistically independent, are summarized in Table 3.

In this study, the seismic fragility curves were derived from three limit-state functions, reflecting the damage states corresponding to the DI thresholds listed in Table 1. Specifically, the limit states represent the Fully Operational, Delayed Operational, and Stability Only performance levels corresponding to DI thresholds of 0.1, 0.4, and 0.75, respectively. These functions are defined by the following equations:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {Fully\, Operational}: \mathit{g}(\mathbf{x}) = 0.1\,\gamma - \mathrm{DI}\,\big|{\xi}$$\end{document}7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {Delayed\, Operational}: \mathit{g}(\mathbf{x}) = 0.4\,\gamma - \mathrm{DI}\,\big|{\xi}$$\end{document}8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {Stability\, Only}: \mathit{g}(\mathbf{x}) = 0.75\,\gamma - \mathrm{DI}\,\big|{\xi}$$\end{document}

As indicated in Eqs. (6)–(8), the DI is treated as a conditional response to reflect its dependence on the externally applied load-scale factor. Each limit-state function was evaluated using the DI obtained from nonlinear FE analyses under the specified loading conditions. For a given IM level, the FE model computed the DI, which was then compared to the threshold scaled by γ. According to the limit-state definition, failure occurs when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathit{g}(\mathbf{x}) < 0$$\end{document}, indicating that the computed DI exceeds the scaled threshold. Based on this condition, a reliability analysis was performed at each IM level using FORM. The resulting exceedance probabilities were then used to construct the fragility curves.

Analysis results

Figure 6 shows the seismic fragility curves derived for the three limit states of the pier using the proposed FORM-based FERUM–RCAHEST computational platform. In each figure, the markers represent the exceedance probabilities of the specified damage states computed by FORM analysis at given values of the load scale factor. The curves shown in Fig. 6 were generated by assuming lognormal distributions and subsequently estimating the optimal distribution parameters using the MATLAB optimization tool fmincon on the basis of the calculated FORM results. To derive the fragility curves, the cyclic loading protocol depicted in Fig. 5a was modified by limiting the maximum drift ratio to 1.5%. This adjustment was necessary because applying the original cyclic loading protocol up to the final drift ratio would invariably result in DI values reaching 1.0 in all cases. The estimated parameters for the lognormal distributions corresponding to each damage state are listed in Table 4.

To verify the efficiency and accuracy of the FERUM–RCAHEST platform, the case of the fully operational limit state with structural capacity uncertainty (COV of γ = 0.3) was selected for comparison with MCS. Three distinct load-scale factors (ξ = 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7) were considered, and the results of both methods, including failure probabilities and the number of FE analyses required, are summarized in Table 5. For MCS, sufficient samples were generated to guarantee the 5% COV of probability results, ensuring reliable estimates of seismic fragility. This comparison indicates that the FORM-based analyses closely match the failure probabilities obtained from MCS, and this agreement is further visually confirmed by the additional markers shown in Fig. 6a. Importantly, in terms of computational efficiency, the FORM-based platform achieved comparable accuracy with only 80 FE analyses, a significant reduction from the 2,711 required by MCS. These results confirm that the proposed FERUM–RCAHEST platform offers an efficient and accurate approach, significantly reducing the computational effort required for cost-effective seismic fragility assessments.

The effect of structural capacity uncertainty (COV of γ = 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3) on the fragility curves derived for the three limit states was also investigated. The results indicate that increasing structural capacity uncertainty led to fragility curves with less-steep slopes and greater dispersion, resulting in higher exceedance probabilities at lower ξ values but comparatively lower probabilities at higher ξ values. This behavior can be attributed to the increased variability in the structural damage responses, where some specimens reach damage thresholds prematurely, whereas others withstand larger load intensities.

Additionally, all three limit states exhibited pronounced nonlinearity, characterized by a rapid increase in exceedance probabilities as ξ increased. The structural damage was quantified in a physically meaningful manner using a DI-based approach within the FERUM–RCAHEST computational platform. The structural capacity uncertainty was explicitly incorporated into the limit-state functions defining the damage thresholds, enabling the resulting fragility curves to reflect this uncertainty.

These analysis results show the potential of the proposed computational platform as an efficient and practical tool for seismic fragility assessment, and further research that systematically characterizes and quantifies the structural capacity uncertainty is recommended, because it would enhance the accuracy and reliability of seismic fragility assessments.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed the FERUM–RCAHEST computational platform for probabilistic seismic fragility assessment of RC bridges. The platform integrates an experimentally validated DI into a reliability-based framework, enabling fragility curves to be derived directly from physically meaningful damage states. Application to a hollow RC bridge pier demonstrated the feasibility of the method, with fragility curves efficiently obtained through FORM and consistent with MCS results. A parametric study further illustrated how structural capacity uncertainty influences fragility estimates. These findings confirm that the proposed platform provides an accurate and computationally efficient tool for fragility analysis.

While this study demonstrates the platform’s feasibility, its scope has several limitations that guide future research. Primarily, the methodology was applied to a single pier, thus broader applications to multi-column bents and full bridge systems are needed to confirm its generality. Furthermore, the seismic input was simplified to a cyclic loading protocol with a single load-scale factor, which does not capture record-to-record variability or the stochastic nature of actual ground motions. Nevertheless, RCAHEST has been applied to a two-span RC bridge under suites of earthquake ground motions34, demonstrating the scalability of the modeling approach and DI formulation. Future work will build on this by extending the platform to system-level bridge models. Using suites of ground motions will allow the derivation of fragility curves that explicitly capture seismic input uncertainty. Such advancements, when integrated with existing risk-assessment tools, will provide more reliable models that support critical decision-making for enhancing the seismic resilience of infrastructure.
