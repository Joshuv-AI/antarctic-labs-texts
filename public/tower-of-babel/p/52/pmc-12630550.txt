Pathologic Complete Response Predicts Long‐Term Survival Following Neoadjuvant Induction Chemotherapy and Chemo‐Radiotherapy in Stage‐III Non‐Small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Maja Guberina, Martin Metzenmacher, Christoph Pöttgen, Marcel Wiesweg, Nika Guberina, Anja Merkel‐Jens, Diana Lütke‐Brintrup, Servet Bölükbas, Wilfried E E Eberhardt, Georgios Stamatis, Fabian Doerr, Till Plönes, Christian Hoffmann, Gregor Zaun, Benedikt Höing, Cornelius Kürten, Emil Mladenov, George Iliakis, David Kersting, Wolfgang P Fendler, Thomas Gauler, Marcel Opitz, Aleksandar Milosevic, Michael Forsting, Felix Nensa, Lale Umutlu, Faustina Funke, Hubertus Hautzel, Ken Herrmann, Christian Taube, Dirk Theegarten, Clemens Aigner, Martin Schuler, Martin Stuschke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTo analyze the association of pathologic‐complete‐response (PCR) and survival after neoadjuvant concurrent chemo‐radiotherapy, we evaluated a large cohort of patients with potentially resectable stage IIIA–IIIC non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treated with a trimodality approach.MethodsConsecutive patients underwent neoadjuvant induction chemotherapy, followed by concurrent chemo‐radiotherapy and surgery. Patients received established imaging, and diagnostics. Leave‐one‐out cross‐validation was employed to identify the most effective prognostic classifier.ResultsAltogether, 403 patients treated between 06/2000 and 01/2020 were included. Median follow‐up was 111 months (IQR: 71–127 months). PCR was achieved in 34% (137 patients) after neoadjuvant therapy and major‐pathologic response without PCR in 30% (MPR> 0%–≤ 10% defined as viable cells in > 0% and ≤ 10% of the sample). PCR was significantly dependent on histology (p = 0.0005) and radiotherapy fractionation schedule (p = 0.027). PCR rates were higher for squamous than for non‐squamous carcinoma with 46.2% (95% CI: 37.8%–54.7%) versus 27.3% (95% CI: 22.0%–33.2%). PCR was the most significant prognostic factor for long‐term survival with an associated hazard ratio of 0.272 (0.192–0.386), while MPR was associated with a hazard ratio of 0.671 (0.498–0.905) in comparison to lesser response. Overall survival at 5/10 years with PCR was 72.9% (95% CI: 64.4%–79.6%)/ 62.8% (53.0%–71.1%)/ event‐free survival at 5 years 69.5% (60.9%–76.7%). Identified through cross‐validation, key prognostic features included PCR, MPR, and treatment period following 18F‐FDG‐PET/CT‐guided staging.ConclusionsInduction chemotherapy followed by chemo‐radiotherapy results in high PCR rates. In this investigation, PCR is followed by high event‐free and overall survival rates. These data warrant further investigation of chemo‐radiotherapy as a significant component of neoadjuvant treatment regimens in trials combined with immunotherapy. This strategy may increase the PCR rates, particularly for patients with more advanced, potentially resectable stage III NSCLC.Keywords: induction chemo‐radiotherapy, major pathological response, pathologic complete response, potentially resectable stage III NSCLC, predictor of survival

Introduction

Neoadjuvant chemo‐immunotherapy with adjuvant immunotherapy demonstrated in several randomized trials on patients with resectable NSCLC stage IB to III without EGFR or ALK alterations the efficacy to increase event‐free survival in comparison to neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. The CheckMate 816 trial found similar results after neoadjuvant chemo‐immunotherapy without adjuvant immunotherapy [6]. Both local and distant metastases were reduced by the addition of immunotherapy [5, 6, 7]. The crude incidences of loco‐regional recurrences as the first site of event ranged from 12% to 19.5%; the incidence of distant metastases from 11.4% to 18% after chemo‐immunotherapy and resection. Furthermore, with longer follow‐up, trials showed that the increase in event‐free survival led to better long‐term survival by chemo‐immunotherapy [6, 7]. In both trials, the PD‐L1 tumor proportion score was a predictive factor for the hazard ratio of death after chemo‐immunotherapy compared to neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone.

In this study, we want to analyze long‐term survival data from a large series of NSCLC stage‐III patients treated with neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy and to compare these data with emerging data from neoadjuvant chemo‐immunotherapy.

A survival benefit by sequential neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy in comparison to neoadjuvant chemotherapy could not have been shown by several randomized or retrospective studies [8, 9, 10, 11]. The trial by Pless et al. used non‐combined radiotherapy and chemotherapy [10]. Therefore, the neoadjuvant radiotherapy arm is not fully comparable to the present study using concurrent chemo‐radiotherapy. The trial by Thomas et al. used radiotherapy in both arms in different sequence [11]. So differences in local and loco‐regional effectiveness, between neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy and chemotherapy might be impacted by the use of postoperative (adjuvant) radiotherapy [11].

However, these trials also showed that a higher histopathologic response and the frequency of R0 resections could reduce the incidence of loco‐regional recurrences by neoadjuvant chemo‐ and radiotherapy protocols in comparison to neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone [10, 11].

Chemo‐radiotherapy is the definitive loco‐regional treatment modality in unresectable stage III NSCLC patients [12, 13, 14]. Immunotherapy consolidation leads to a major improvement in overall survival of patients without EGFR or ALK alterations [12]. Comparing neoadjuvant therapy with concurrent chemo‐radiotherapy followed by surgery with definitive chemo‐radiotherapy led to similar results in stage IIIA patients considered resectable before or after the neoadjuvant therapy phase [15, 16]. Between resectable and unresectable patients with stage III NSCLC, there is a heterogeneous group of patients with potential benefit of resection that might benefit from surgery after induction therapy in a multimodality concept [17, 18, 19]. In this group of patients, neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy can also play an important role within multimodal concepts including immunotherapy.

It has been shown that pathologic complete response (PCR) but also major histopathologic response (MPR) is a prognostic factor for event‐free or overall survival after both neoadjuvant chemo‐immunotherapy as well as neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy [20, 21, 22]. PCR served as a co‐primary endpoint in major randomized trials such as CheckMate 8166 and the AEGEAN Trial [1]. In addition, it was a key secondary endpoint in the other larger randomized trials on neoadjuvant chemo‐immunotherapy such as CheckMate 77T4, KEYNOTE‐671 [2], and the NADIM II trial [5].

In the present study, we characterize the frequency of PCR and MPR after induction chemotherapy and neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy in dependence on the histopathologic subtype from a large institutional database, their prognostic value and long‐term prognosis of patients according to histopathologic response. Comparing these data with emerging data from neoadjuvant chemo‐immunotherapy can help to identify and characterize patients with stage III NSCLC without targetable driver mutations who most likely can benefit from a neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy component within multimodality treatments including immunotherapy.

Methods

Primary Endpoints and Data Collection

The primary aim of this study was to define the PCR and MPR rates of patients with stage III NSCLC at our lung cancer center treated within a trimodality concept.

This analysis is based on a consecutive single‐center cohort between 06/2000 and 01/2020, incorporated to gather real‐world data over time including radiation in the neoadjuvant treatment protocol. A part of the patients with complete follow‐up was treated in randomized phase II and III trials. Thus, this analysis is based on collective data derived from prospective clinical studies and a large registry study, providing a comprehensive overview [16, 23, 24].

First, the treatment started with three cycles of induction chemotherapy with cisplatin and etoposide (until 2006) or paclitaxel (period thereafter). Then a combined CRT with cisplatin and etoposide (until 2002) or vinorelbine (period thereafter) followed. In the last step, surgical resection was performed in patients defined as operable.

This investigation followed good clinical practice guidelines. The local ethics committee of the Medical Faculty of Duisburg‐Essen approved the protocol.

Patients

Eligible patients had histopathological proof of invasive NSCLC of stage IIIA (N2 or ≥ T3 and N1)—IIIC (selected and limited cases) according to the respective applicable TNM classification within that time period and treated with neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy followed by surgery between 06/2000 and 01/2020 at this institution. Distant disease had to be ruled out until 12/2001 by computed tomography, and after that period routinely with 18F‐FDG Positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F‐FDG PET/CT), and cranial MR or cranial CT. Patients must have had adequate hematologic findings and adequate hepatic and renal function. All procedures except biochemistry and hematology (within 1–2 days) should have been performed within the 3 weeks before treatment start.

Patients were stratified according to clinical tumor stage, clinical lymph node status (positive or negative), stage, gender, histopathology, grade (grade 1–3), age, treatment time period (time period 1 after the introduction of 18F‐FDG PET/CT as staging (after 12/2001), time period 2 after the approval of immunotherapy in metastatic NSCLC (after 04/2016)) and the fractionation schedule of the radiotherapy part. These parameters were analyzed to determine if they were correlated with the PCR and MPR rates for the entire trial population and selective time period subgroups.

Diagnostics and Treatment

All patients received systematic mediastinal staging, either by EBUS‐TBNA or mediastinoscopy, and after December 2001 by an 18F‐FDG PET‐CT scan before starting therapy [25, 26, 27]. Neoadjuvant therapy consisted of 3 cycles of cisplatin‐based induction chemotherapy followed by combined CRT.

Following three cycles of induction chemotherapy (split dose cisplatin and paclitaxel doublets post 2006) (cisplatin/and etoposide prior to this period), concurrent neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy was given. The radiation schedule was applied either as accelerated hyperfractionated treatment at 1.5 Gy per fraction, twice daily up to 45 Gy (AHF) or conventionally fractionated (CF) at 2 Gy/fraction, one fraction per day up to a total dose of 46 Gy. Patients treated apart from the randomized trials were able to actively indicate their preference—once or twice daily radiotherapy—after receiving individual counseling. Thus, this allowed either a clear patient's preference and an active patient's choice. The concurrent chemotherapy component was cisplatin and etoposide until the end of 2002 and cisplatin plus vinorelbine thereafter given on days 1 and 8 during radiotherapy thereafter [16]. The treatment protocol included risk‐adapted anticoagulation alongside the administration of cisplatin in a split‐course regimen. Until the end of 2013, 3D‐conformal radiotherapy was used, and thereafter, intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) or volumetric Arc therapy (VMAT).

In addition, all patients were discussed in the interdisciplinary tumor board before each treatment block and underwent surgery in the third part.

Surgery was performed 3–7 weeks, median 33 days, after completed radiotherapy. Adequate local surgery was performed by the surgeons' expertise and lymph node clearance according to the institutions', current national and international guidelines. Pathologically clear margins were highly intended as goals for resection.

Pathologic Assessment

Grading of tumor regression after neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy was performed according to the criterion proposed by Junker et al. [28] A PCR was defined as the absence of invasive tumor in the final surgical lung sample (stage ypT0) as judged by the local lung cancer specialized pathologist. The major pathological response was defined as less than or equal to 10% residual viable tumor in the resection specimen also in accordance with the recommendations for pathologic assessment of the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) [29].

Statistics

The primary endpoint for this analysis was overall survival outcome from the time of start of neoadjuvant radiotherapy. In addition, event‐free survival was a secondary endpoint, where deaths and local as well as distant recurrences of the NSCLC after surgery were counted as events. Survival curves were calculated according to the Kaplan‐Meier method and prognostic groups were compared by the log‐rank test using the procedure life test from SAS (statistical software, version 9.4, SASTSTAT 15.1, Cary, NC). Median follow‐up was calculated according to the inverse Kaplan‐Meier method. For multivariate comparisons, proportional hazards analysis was performed using stepwise parameter selection at alpha = 0.025 (Procedure PHREG, SAS). The proportional hazards assumption was checked per prognostic parameter using a Kolmogorov‐type supremum test. Leave‐one‐out cross‐validation of the parameters found important using proportional hazards analysis of the whole data set using stepwise selection was performed with a SAS macro described by Rushing et al. [30] In addition, event‐free survival was an additional endpoint of this trial. The best classifier was according to the largest likelihood score statistic at each leave‐one‐out loop.

From the six prognostic parameters in Table 4, we built the best cross‐validated prognostic classifier. Leave‐one out cross validation was used to avoid overfitting and to develop a more robust and generalizable model. Figure 2 shows the chi [2] statistics comparing the cross‐validated high and low risks groups from the best 2–6 parameter classifiers by a log‐rank test. The four‐parameter classifier performed best, indicated by the highest chi [2]‐value (Figure 4). Inclusion of further variables did not improve the performance of the model on the test data sets indicating overfitting with more than four variables. The four parameters, which were selected in all of the leave out loops, were PCR, age, time period 1 prior of treatment before introduction of 18F‐FDG PET/CT staging as well as MPR. Discussion

The CheckMate 816 [6], AEGEAN Trial [1], CheckMate 77T4, and NADIM II5 detailed the PCR according to the histopathologic subtype, squamous versus non‐squamous NSCLC. The PCR rates in the intent to treat groups of patients with squamous cell carcinoma treated with chemo‐immunotherapy ranged from 21.3% to 33.3% over these trials.

The overall PCR rate of patients with squamous cell carcinomas weighted by the number of included patients per trial was 24.9%. Related to the number of resected patients, the overall PCR rate in these trials was 31.3% and was significantly smaller than the PCR rate for squamous cell carcinoma found in the present trial with 46.2% (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.0031, Supplement Figure 1).

The rate of pneumonectomies ranged from 9% to 16.2% in the neoadjuvant chemo‐immunotherapy arms of the randomized trials [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Enrollment of patients and necessity of pneumonectomy was consequently stopped during the course of the AEGEAN trial [1]. A further downstaging by chemo‐radiotherapy might reduce the extent of surgery in some of these patients.

The PCR rate in the present study using induction chemotherapy followed by chemo‐radiotherapy was 34% of the resected patients over all histopathologic subgroups. Compared to our previous report from 2013 with fewer patients and shorter follow‐up, we found an increase in PCR rates from 30% to 44% in the last 3.5 years of the inclusion period of the study [31].

The proportion of 34% was considerably larger than the overall PCR rate from trials on neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy or induction chemotherapy and neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy in the overview by Rosner et al. with 17.7% (p < 0.0001, Fisher's exact test) [22]. One reason might be the use of intense cisplatin‐based chemotherapy regimens; the other the use of hyperfractionated acceleration. Combining neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy with anti‐PD‐(L)1 and CTLA‐4 immunotherapy can increase the PCR rate even further to above 50%, as has been recently shown by the increase in a phase 2 trial by Bahce et al. [32] With respect to treatment‐dependent side effects, online monitored radiotherapy as breathing‐controlled IMRT within neoadjuvant protocols has become more conformal and normal tissue‐sparing than in former times with the use of 3D conformal radiotherapy [33, 34, 35]. Even in patients requiring pneumonectomies, postoperative mortality was not increased by this neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy regimen in comparison to induction chemotherapy alone [36].

PCR was found to be a prognostic factor for survival in the present trial. The Hazard ratio of events was 0.33 and 0.35 at the overall and event‐free survival endpoints for patients with or without PCR in the present study. These values are near the median of Hazard ratios comparing the survival of patients with and without PCR after neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy from the systematic review by Rosner et al. [22].

To be a valid surrogate marker, the full treatment effect on overall and event‐free survival should be captured by PCR. This criterion requires that survival of a patient with PCR should be independent of the modality of neoadjuvant treatment [37]. Data from trials on neoadjuvant chemo‐immunotherapy emerge, that allow the estimation of event‐free survival of patients with PCR after this modality at short follow‐up. The recent meta‐analysis by Marinelli et al. found an event‐free survival of 94% and 83% at 2 and 3 years among the 244 patients with PCR included from different prospective chemo‐immunotherapy trials [21]. These event‐free survival rates appear to be superior to those reported here for patients with PCR after chemo‐radiotherapy. The hazard ratio for event‐free survival of patients with or without PCR was as low as 0.13 (95% CI 0.08–0.21).

However, all the trials contributing to this result had median follow‐up times less than 30 months, short for these good prognosis patients. With a longer follow‐up of 36 months, the largest of the included trials, KEYNOTE‐671, showed similar event‐free survival for the 73 patients with PCR after chemo‐immunotherapy [2] as the present study after neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy (p = 0.29, log‐rank test, Supplement Figure 2), demonstrating a considerable overlap of prognosis of patients with PCR after chemo‐immunotherapy in comparison to neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy. Twelve percent of patients with PCR from this study had a loco‐regional recurrence as the first site of relapse and 10% distant metastases or combined distant and loco‐regional relapse. As consolidation immunotherapy could prevent about one third of these events, improvement of survival of these patients can be expected in further trials using also immunotherapy [12]. The long‐term event‐free survival synthesized from older studies treated with neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy or chemotherapy in the overview by Rosner et al. showed a large overlap with the data from the present study [22]. Hines et al. analyzed the surrogacy of PCR for event‐free and overall survival in randomized trials on neoadjuvant chemo‐immunotherapy versus chemotherapy alone in NSCLC [20]. On a patient level, a good correlation between the PCR rate and event‐free survival characterized at 2 years was found, quantified by a coefficient of determination of 0.82.

In addition, the odds ratios between the PCR rates in both arms showed a moderate close relation with the hazard ratios for event‐free survival across the different randomized trials. These data together also support PCR as a surrogate marker for event‐free survival after neoadjuvant therapies and surgery in NSCLC.

A convergence science approach offers a transformative framework for advancing this field. By bridging distinct domains, we could uncover this robust predictive biomarker, allowing us to optimize treatment sequencing, and to personalize therapeutic strategies, and thus, to improve patient selection for curative‐intent surgery. The urgent need to improve outcomes in stage III NSCLC, coupled with the increasing complexity of multimodal therapies, makes the study of optimized neoadjuvant treatment and pCR not only clinically relevant but also representative. Deepening our understanding of pCR through multidisciplinary collaboration is pivotal for driving precision oncology, accelerating therapeutic innovation, and ultimately, improving survival and quality of life for patients with lung cancer.

Limitations of the present study are the long‐time interval of recruitment and the retrospective nature. A potential constraint of direct comparability with other trials is that we also included higher TNM stages. Strengths are the large sample size and long‐term follow‐up at a high‐volume center with a close follow‐up. This study presents, for the first time, mature survival and clinical outcome data from a high‐volume thoracic oncology center using this comprehensive treatment approach. The findings aim to contribute to the optimization of treatment protocols in patients with locally advanced, marginally and potentially resectable stage III NSCLC.

Conclusion

Neoadjuvant chemo‐radiotherapy, which can be followed by definitive chemo‐radiotherapy or surgery if the tumor becomes resectable after treatment, is a viable option for patients with locally advanced potentially resectable NSCLC. This approach demonstrates high pathologic complete response (PCR) rates with an improved long‐term survival, particularly for squamous cell carcinomas. Neoadjuvant triple therapy with chemo‐immunotherapy and radiation is recommended for further investigation in clinical trials.
