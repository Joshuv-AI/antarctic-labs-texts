Retrograde Osteochondral Autograft Transplantation for Osteochondral Lesions of the Distal Tibial Plafond: Technique Tips and Early Clinical Experience
Authors: Jaeyoung Kim, Cordelia Burn, Jonathan Gamarra, Mark C Drakos
Published: 2026

Introduction

Osteochondral lesions of the distal tibial plafond (OLTPs) are far less commonly reported than lesions of the talar dome,1-3 and optimal treatment strategies remain poorly defined.4,5 Initial management is typically nonoperative; however, surgical intervention may be required in patients with persistent pain, mechanical symptoms, or functional limitation. In contrast to talar OCLs, there is no widely accepted treatment algorithm or consensus for distal tibial plafond defects, and most surgical strategies have been extrapolated from talar lesion management.4,6

Osteochondral autograft transfer (OAT) is an appealing option for relatively large or cystic lesions because it provides native hyaline cartilage with subchondral bone, potentially supporting durable restoration. However, tibial plafond lesions often require a retrograde approach through the tibia, with the trajectory optimized to minimize angular deviation from perpendicular to the articular surface. This makes the procedure technically demanding, and detailed technique descriptions remain limited in the literature since the procedure was first described by Carreira et al.7-10 The purpose of this Technique Tip is to describe the senior author’s technique for retrograde autologous OAT for distal tibial plafond lesions, including key technical pearls and descriptive early clinical experience.

Indications and Contraindications

Retrograde OAT was performed for patients with symptomatic OLTPs meeting 1 or more of the following criteria: (1) lesion diameter >10 mm, (2) associated subchondral cystic change, or (3) failure of prior arthroscopic treatment with orthobiologics or marrow stimulation procedures, including microfracture. Patients with corresponding talar kissing lesions were not treated with this technique. For smaller lesions with minimal or no cystic change, extracellular matrix scaffold combined with bone marrow aspirate concentrate (ECM-BMAC) was preferred instead of retrograde OAT.

Preoperative weightbearing radiographs and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were obtained to characterize lesion morphology and assist with surgical planning. The surgical approach was selected based on the ability to achieve a safe retrograde trajectory that is as orthogonal to the lesion surface as practically achievable. An anterior tibial approach was used for anteriorly located lesions, and a posterior tibial approach was used for posterior lesions. Centrally located lesions were not treated with this technique because a reliable perpendicular trajectory could not be achieved without excessive bone removal or risk of iatrogenic cartilage injury.

Technique

Step 1: Bone Marrow Aspirate Harvest and Preparation

With the patient supine, bone marrow aspirate is harvested early to allow adequate processing time. A Jamshidi needle is introduced into the anterior superior iliac crest, and approximately 60 mL of aspirate is obtained. The aspirate is processed (Angel System; Arthrex Inc., Naples, FL) to yield approximately 3 mL of BMAC, which is mixed with an extracellular matrix scaffold (BioCartilage; Arthrex Inc., Naples, FL) and reserved for later application. The harvest site is dressed in sterile fashion.

Step 2: Diagnostic Ankle Arthroscopy and Lesion Preparation

Standard anteromedial and anterolateral ankle arthroscopy portals are established, and diagnostic arthroscopy is performed with a 2.7-mm, 30°, 72-mm arthroscope. The tibial plafond lesion is identified and inspected (Figure 1). Synovitis, scar tissue, and unstable cartilage are debrided to clearly define lesion margins. A stable rim of surrounding healthy cartilage is preserved.

Arthroscopic view of the distal tibial plafond osteochondral lesion (A and B) after debridement of unstable cartilage and fibrous tissue (C).

Step 3: Osteochondral Autograft Harvest From the Knee

An approximately 3-cm longitudinal incision is made over the lateral knee. Dissection is carried to the lateral retinaculum, exposing the lateral femoral trochlea. An osteochondral plug is harvested from a non-axial weightbearing region of the lateral femoral trochlea (Figure 2). Care is taken to select the flattest possible donor site to better match the concave contour of the distal tibial plafond. The plug is soaked in BMAC until implantation.

(A-C) Harvest of an osteochondral autograft plug from a non-axial weightbearing region of the ipsilateral lateral femoral trochlea. A relatively flat donor plug is selected to better match the contour of the distal tibial plafond.

Step 4: Tibial Tunnel Creation and Trajectory Confirmation

A tibial incision is made anteriorly or posteriorly depending on lesion location. A joint distractor with K-wires is used for better visualization of the lesion. A targeting guide (meniscal root repair guide) is used to triangulate access, and a guide pin is advanced toward the center of the lesion (Figure 3). A meniscal root repair guide is preferred over a standard ACL guide because it allows greater angular freedom, including trajectories beyond 90°, which improves access to anterior and posterior plafond lesions. Fluoroscopy is used to confirm a safe retrograde trajectory that is as orthogonal to the articular surface as practically achievable (Figure 4). A bone tunnel is created using a 10-mm reamer, and necrotic bone and cystic tissue are thoroughly debrided.

Meniscal root repair guide (A) used intraoperatively with a guide pin to triangulate a retrograde trajectory to the center of the distal tibial plafond lesion (B and C).

(A) Anteroposterior and (B) lateral fluoroscopic views confirming a safe retrograde trajectory optimized to minimize angular deviation from perpendicular to the articular surface.

Step 5: Retrograde Graft Insertion and ECM-BMAC Augmentation

The osteochondral plug is inserted retrograde through the tibial tunnel and advanced until the cartilage surface is flush to slightly recessed relative to the surrounding tibial plafond cartilage (Figure 5). Fluoroscopy is used to confirm appropriate graft seating. Residual cystic cavities peripheral to the osteochondral plug are filled with bone graft obtained from tibial tunnel reaming. Under arthroscopic visualization, ECM-BMAC is applied circumferentially around the graft using an arthroscopic cannula to fill small residual peripheral gaps and address graft-host contour mismatch (Figure 6).

(A-C) Arthroscopic view during retrograde insertion of the osteochondral plug through the tibial tunnel, with the graft seated flush to slightly recessed relative to the surrounding plafond cartilage.

Arthroscopic application of an extracellular matrix–bone marrow aspirate concentrate mixture (A and B) around the graft to fill small residual peripheral gaps and improve graft-host contour congruity.

Step 6: Backfilling and Closure

The knee donor site and tibial tunnel are backfilled with a cannulated bone dowel and ECM-BMAC (Figure 7). Incisions are irrigated and closed in layered fashion.

Backfilling of the donor site and tibial tunnel with a cannulated bone dowel and extracellular matrix–bone marrow aspirate concentrate mixture after graft implantation.

Postoperative Care and Rehabilitation

Patients are placed in a splint and maintained nonweightbearing for 6 weeks. Range-of-motion exercises begin at 2 weeks. Partial weightbearing and formal physical therapy start at 6 weeks. Return to sports is generally permitted between 6 and 9 months depending on symptoms, strength, and functional recovery.

Descriptive Early Clinical Experience

This retrospective study was approved by the steering committee of an institutional review board–approved foot and ankle registry at the authors’ institution. Between 2016 and 2025, 9 patients underwent retrograde OAT for distal tibial plafond lesions (Table 1). Of these, 4 patients had more than 12 months of clinical follow-up and were included in the descriptive clinical summary. Mean clinical follow-up among the 4 patients with more than 12 months of follow-up was 47 months. No intraoperative complications occurred. One patient subsequently underwent matrix-induced autologous chondrocyte implantation (MACI) 2 years after the index procedure because of persistent symptoms. Among patients with available clinical follow-up, 2 returned to sports, 1 returned to activities of daily living with minimal pain, and 1 had residual symptoms managed nonoperatively with injection therapy. Preoperative and postoperative PROMIS scores were available for 3 patients and suggested improvement across measured domains. Postoperative MRI, obtained selectively when clinically indicated rather than per a standardized protocol, demonstrated graft incorporation at the recipient site without evidence of proximal graft migration in the cases imaged (Figure 8). Small residual cystic change was noted in 2 patients.

Discussion

Only limited prior reports have described retrograde techniques for OLTPs. The key technical challenge is establishing a safe trajectory that minimizes angular deviation from perpendicular to the lesion surface while avoiding iatrogenic violation of the articular surface. In our experience, a meniscal root repair guide is advantageous because it permits greater angular adjustment than a conventional ACL guide, which is particularly useful when a steeper retrograde trajectory is required. This can facilitate access to selected anterior and posterior plafond lesions and allow controlled preparation of the recipient bed under fluoroscopic and arthroscopic visualization.

A common concern with retrograde OAT is the potential for proximal graft migration through the tibial tunnel. Although some authors have described direct graft fixation or the use of a proximal “safety screw” at the tunnel opening,8-10 we have not routinely required additional proximal hardware when a stable press-fit is achieved at the recipient site. Although circumferential radial compressive force at the graft-tunnel interface is the principal stabilizing mechanism in OATS procedures, the cancellous bone dowel used to backfill the tibial tunnel provides an additional proximal boundary that may further resist graft migration in the retrograde configuration. In this series, postoperative MRI suggested graft integration without evidence of proximal migration, supporting that meticulous graft sizing and press-fit fixation may provide adequate stability in appropriately selected cases.

Another unique challenge of tibial plafond OAT is contour mismatch between the convex donor surface and the concave tibial plafond, which can result in peripheral gaps despite a flush graft surface. In larger lesions, the osteochondral plug is intended to reconstruct the principal structural portion of the defect rather than necessarily cover the entire chondral surface footprint. To address small residual peripheral gaps and graft-host contour mismatch, we routinely augmented the interface with ECM-BMAC applied circumferentially around the plug using an arthroscopic cannula; the paste consistency of the mixture provides inherent adhesion to the recipient bone surface. This adjunct may help optimize surface congruity, particularly in lesions with cystic change or irregular subchondral bone morphology. Accordingly, the graft should be seated flush to slightly recessed rather than left proud.

This series has several important limitations, including the small number of cases, absence of a prespecified primary outcome measure, incomplete follow-up in more than half of the treated patients, and nonstandardized postoperative imaging. These factors preclude any definitive conclusions regarding clinical efficacy and should be considered when interpreting the descriptive clinical experience reported here.

Several technical pearls emerged from our experience. First, careful patient selection and lesion location are critical; lesions that cannot be approached with a safe, optimized retrograde trajectory may not be suitable for this technique. In particular, centrally located plafond lesions were not treated in our practice because of the difficulty of achieving an orthogonal trajectory without excessive bone removal or risk of cartilage injury. Second, approach selection (anterior versus posterior tibial) should be based on lesion position and planned tunnel trajectory, and fluoroscopic confirmation is essential to avoid joint penetration. Third, thorough debridement of necrotic and cystic bone, combined with a well-prepared recipient bed, is intended to support stable graft seating and incorporation. Finally, stable press-fit fixation with a flush articular surface should be prioritized, as proud graft placement or excessive graft recess may compromise joint mechanics and clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

Retrograde OAT for osteochondral lesions of the distal tibial plafond is a technically demanding procedure that may be considered in carefully selected patients with larger or cystic lesions, or in those who have failed prior marrow stimulation or arthroscopic treatment, when a safe, optimized retrograde trajectory can be achieved. This Technique Tip describes the senior author’s approach and limited early clinical experience. Further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to determine the clinical effectiveness, durability, and appropriate indications for this procedure.
