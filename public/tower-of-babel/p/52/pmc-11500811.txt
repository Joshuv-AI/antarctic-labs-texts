Case report: intracoronary stent fracture complicated with coronary abscess and fistulization into the pericardium
Authors: Constantin Andrei Rusali, Lucia Cojocaru, Ioana Caterina Șerbanescu, Valentin Scupra, Daniela Iurco
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStent fractures are a rare complication of angioplasties and are an unusual substrate for coronary abscesses.Case summaryA 63-year-old patient came into the emergency department for ongoing chest pain. The patient had recently undergone coronary stent implantation, 3 months prior. Computed tomography coronary angiography revealed a coronary abscess and stent fracture, with blood effraction into the pericardium. The patient underwent emergency open-heart surgery to clear the infection and perform coronary artery bypass graft surgery. Post-operative outcome was tentatively favourable. The patient suffered acute kidney injury and required haemodialysis.DiscussionCoronary stent fracture is a rare complication which can be life-threatening and which can lead to severe sequelae.

Learning points

Coronary stent infections are a rare complication of angioplasties. Early recognition and diagnosis can improve patient outcomes. Echocardiography has low specificity for the diagnosis of coronary stent fracture and infection.

Introduction

Coronary stent infection (CSI) is a rare and life-threatening complication of percutaneous angioplasty. In the nearly 40 years of percutaneous angioplasties, there have been <30 case reports for stent infections.1 As such, the definition and classifications for this complication vary greatly. Some define early stent infection as sooner than 10 days post-procedure,2 while others classify infections occurring in the first 4 weeks as early.3

Symptoms are wide and varied. Usually, patients present with fever, chills, and chest pain, but also lethargy, weight loss, and malaise. The first presentation may very well be as dramatic as cardiac arrest, cardiogenic shock, septic shock, or cardiac tamponade due to aneurysm rupture into the pericardium.

Improving recognition among physicians of the symptom constellation associated with CSI can lead to early diagnosis and better patient outcomes.

Case presentation

The patient presented in the emergency department for ongoing chest pain. The chest pain was non-specific, and it was associated with acute dyspnoea and dry cough. Patient denied fever.

Physical exam revealed bilateral rhonchi on pulmonary auscultation. No other relevant findings were noted.

Initially, he was admitted as acute coronary syndrome. Echocardiography revealed the patient had a small pericardial effusion, measuring 10–12 mm; otherwise, there were no new lesions compared with the echocardiography from 3 months prior. There was no dynamic evolution of the cardiac necrosis markers, troponin being slightly elevated, in a plateau (117 pg/mL at admission, 125 pg/mL 12 h after admission; the troponin value at the previous discharge was 571 pg/mL) and CK-MB was normal (12 U/L at admission, 10 U/L 12 h after admission). He had positive inflammatory markers (at admission ESR 112 mm/h, CRP 32.55 mg/dL). Empiric antibiotic therapy was initiated for a possible lower respiratory infection.

Computed tomography angio-coronarography was performed the next day in order to visualize the lungs, the heart, and coronary arteries. It revealed a sacciform dilatation of the middle LAD, measuring 10–12 mm between the stents, and localized haemorrhagic pericardial effusion (40–65 mm) which determined left ventricular compression (Figure 1).

Computed tomography angiography shows contrast effraction (red arrow) and localized haemorrhagic pericardial effusion (yellow arrow) with a haematoma formed around the trunk of the pulmonary artery (blue arrows).

Emergent coronarography showed a pseudoaneurysm of the middle left anterior descending artery and stent thrombosis (Figure 2).

Stent fracture (green arrow) and a pseudoaneurysm of the middle left anterior descending artery and stent thrombosis (orange arrow).

Balloon angioplasty and stent implantation were attempted, but they failed.

Post coronarography, the patient went into cardiogenic shock which required vasopressor support with dobutamine and noradrenaline. Haemocultures came back positive for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Rifampicin was added to the antibiotic regimen.

The patient was transferred to cardiovascular surgery. A median sternotomy was performed to assess the lesions. The coronary abscess was evacuated (Figure 3), and a pericardial patch was placed.

Coronary abscess (blue arrow) was drained (green arrow).

To establish coronary flow, two coronary artery bypass grafts were performed (venous grafts on left anterior descending and right coronary arteries).

Post surgery, antibiotic therapy with vancomycin, gentamicin, and rifampicin was continued. Vasopressor support was also needed.

Recovery was long and arduous, complicated with acute kidney injury. Haemodialysis was instituted. Unfortunately, kidney function did not recover, and the patient remained on chronic haemodialysis.

The surgical site healed well, and the patient was discharged after more than a month spent in the hospital, in stable condition and requiring chronic haemodialysis.

At the 3-month post-operative follow-up, the patient still required chronic haemodialysis—two sessions a week (serum creatinine = 2.05 mg/dL)—had good functional status, and denied cardiovascular symptoms. Echocardiography showed no pericardial effusion.

Discussion

Stent infections can occur after the contamination of the stent before insertion, or from a haematogenous spread from another source of bacteremia.4 Drug-eluting stents have a higher risk of becoming infected, when compared with bare metal stents. It is believed that the drugs used lower local cellular immunity and favour the development of infections. Also, delayed endothelialization may provide with a possible site for bacterial adhesion because of the persistently exposed metal struts.3,4

Among the incriminated bacteria, S. aureus remains the most frequently isolated in cases of CSI. The more aggressive methicillin-resistant S. aureus was found in several cases. Other bacteria include Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Escherichia coli, Enterobacter cloacae, and Actinomyces oris.5

Echocardiography can be unassuming, or multiples anomalies can be identified. Some patients have concurrent valve endocarditis (both mitral valve and tricuspid valve endocarditis have been described in literature).2,6 In one case, transoesophageal echocardiography revealed a homogenous mass in the right atrioventricular groove. This mass was confirmed to be an abscess.3 Transoesophageal echocardiography can be useful to reveal abscess cavities or aneurysms located in the coronary arteries.6

Coronary stent infection can also present as acute coronary syndrome due to septic occlusion of the stent.6 These cases can be first interpreted as stent thrombosis.4

Stent fractures, on the other hand, have an incidence of 5.5%.7 Another study performed by Shaikh et al.8 found an incidence of 18.6% of stent fracture in patients presenting with stent restenosis.9

Diagnosing stent fractures and stent infections can prove to be difficult. Most studies used combined methods, like fluoroscopy and ultrasound or fluoroscopy and computed tomography.9

Conclusion

Coronary stent infections present a diagnostic challenge due to varied and non-specific symptoms. As in our patient’s case, treatment and recovery are long and arduous and at risk for multiple complications.
