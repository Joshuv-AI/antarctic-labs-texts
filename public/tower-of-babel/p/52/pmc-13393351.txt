Genetic and Environmental Influences on Tooth Loss: A Twin Study of Middle-Aged and Older Adults in Qingdao, China
Authors: Yixiao Wang, Xiaocao Tian, Weijing Wang, Tong Wang, Jingxian Li, Wanchun Wang, Fengchun Hou, Dongfeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to estimate the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors on acquired tooth loss among middle-aged and older twins in Qingdao, China.MethodsA total of 377 complete twin pairs (240 monozygotic, 137 dizygotic) were recruited from the Qingdao Twin Registry in China, and acquired tooth loss was recorded through structured interviews. Heritability of acquired tooth loss was estimated using correlation analysis and structural equation model, adjusting for age and sex.ResultsThe median age of the twins was 50 years (IQR: 46-57). The polychoric correlations for monozygotic twins (0.67; 95% CI: 0.55-0.78) were more than twice as high as that for dizygotic twins (0.25; 95% CI: 0.03-0.48). Therefore, we fitted an additive, dominant, and unique environmental factors model and its nested submodels. Based on the Akaike information criterion, the additive and unique environmental factors model was selected as the preferred model. After adjusting for age and sex, the model indicated that 56.51% (95% CI: 43.21%-69.81%) of the variance in acquired tooth loss was attributable to additive genetic factors, and 43.49% (95% CI: 30.20%-56.79%) to unique environmental influences.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that variation in acquired tooth loss is influenced by both additive genetic and unique environmental factors. These findings may assist in developing strategies for tooth loss prevention and oral health maintenance.Key words: Tooth loss, Heritability, Chinese twins, Geriatric dentistry, Epidemiology

Introduction

In middle-aged and older populations, acquired tooth loss is a prevalent oral health problem.1 Acquired tooth loss is associated with a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular disease, dementia, and diabetes and increased mortality.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Furthermore, the economic burden of dental restoration remains a challenge for older adults.7,8 Therefore, maintaining adequate oral function is of great significance for improving their quality of life and overall health.

As a multifactorial oral health problem, acquired tooth loss is influenced by both environment and genetics.9, 10, 11 The classical twin studies have assessed the contributions of environmental and genetic factors to acquired tooth loss. A Danish twin study on the number of remaining teeth reported a heritability estimate of 36%.12 At the molecular level, specific genes such as PHEX, IL6, COL1A1, and CALCR have been identified as associated with acquired tooth loss.13, 14, 15 Meanwhile, environmental factors such as age, smoking, and oral hygiene can compromise oral health and increase the risk of acquired tooth loss.16,17 Twin studies further quantify this relationship, attributing approximately 38% to 50% of the variance in tooth loss to environmental influences.12 Therefore, to inform prevention strategies for acquired tooth loss, understanding the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors is essential.

As China’s population is ageing at an unprecedented rate, the burden of oral health problems has intensified accordingly. According to the Fourth National Oral Health Survey, dentition defects affect 84.4% of Chinese adults, with only a small fraction (13.8%) retaining their complete natural dentition and a notable minority (1.8%) progressing to complete edentulism.18,19 While the role of genetic predisposition in acquired tooth loss is documented in Western populations, its heritability remains completely unexplored in China. Given that genetic architecture may vary across ethnic populations, obtaining heritability estimates for acquired tooth loss in the Chinese population is of critical importance.

This article presents the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors to the number of acquired tooth loss in middle-aged and elderly twin populations in Qingdao, China.

Methods

Study participants

This study utilized a sample from the Qingdao Twin Registry, the first population-based twins registry in China, established in 1998 at the Qingdao Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Qingdao CDC).20,21 The twin pairs analysed were recruited between 2012 and 2013 using residential registries, medical records, and community media outreach. We included all twins aged ≥40 years. Twins with missing acquired tooth loss data and those from incomplete pairs were excluded. The final analytical sample consisted of 377 complete twin pairs with a median age of 50 years, comprising 240 monozygotic (MZ) (114 male pairs and 126 female pairs) and 137 dizygotic (DZ) (40 male pairs, 38 female pairs, and 59 opposite-sex pairs) twin pairs. Zygosity was determined by using DNA marker technology at the central laboratory of Qingdao Blood Center, achieving a 99.9% accuracy in assignment.22,23

This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Boards of the Qingdao CDC (QDCDC-IRB) and was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. All participants have signed informed consent.

Measurements of acquired tooth loss

The number of acquired tooth loss

The number of acquired tooth loss was assessed by dentists through a comprehensive assessment that integrated oral examinations, participants’ oral reports, and clinical records. The total number of Teeth was defined based on a full set of 28 permanent teeth (excluding third molars) as the absence of teeth in the dental arch, excluding dental implants and any acquired tooth loss attributed to trauma or orthodontic treatment.

Categorization of acquired tooth loss

The World Health Organization defines a functional dentition as having 20 or more natural teeth that remain functional throughout life without requiring prosthodontic replacements.24 The relationship between dentition and oral function is well established. Multiple studies have confirmed that fewer than 20 teeth (loss of >8 teeth) yield impaired chewing efficiency and reduced masticatory ability.25, 26, 27, 28 Given that our continuously measured acquired tooth loss data could not be transformed to achieve normality, we converted the number of acquired tooth loss into a three-level ordinal variable (0, 1-8, and ≥9) to ensure robustness of the results.29

Statistical analysis

First, we calculated the polychoric correlations (PCC) within an ordinal threshold-based framework for the number of acquired tooth loss among MZ and DZ twins. For each twin pair, the twin who was born first was designated as ‘Twin 1’, and the twin born second was designated as ‘Twin 2’. According to the classical twin model, total phenotypic variance is partitioned into three components: additive genetic (A), shared environmental variables (C)/dominant genetic (D), and unique environmental (E) factors (the full ACE model or full ADE model). In this study, model selection (ACE or ADE) was determined by comparing the PCC between MZ and DZ twins. The ADE model was adopted only if the PCC for MZ pairs (rMZ) exceeded twice that of DZ pairs (rDZ), otherwise, the ACE model was applied. PCCs were calculated using the polychor package in R.

To quantify the influence of genetic and environmental factors on acquired tooth loss, we employed structural equation modelling (SEM), with age and sex included as covariates.12 Specifically, we used a liability threshold model for ordinal data to construct the SEM. Prior to model fitting, Blom transformation was applied to transform the age variable to approximate a normal distribution. The analysis employed the mets package in R for fitting the twin models.

To select the model with the best fit, we compared the full models (ACE/ADE) with its nested model (AE, CE/DE) in the analysis. To compare the full and nested models, likelihood ratio tests were performed to determine if their fit differed significantly, with the significance level set at α = 0.05. On the basis of genetic relatedness (100% for MZ twins and 50% for DZ twins), the maximum correlation values were fixed at 1.0 and 0.5 for MZ and DZ twins in the analysis. Model selection was determined by the Akaike information criterion (AIC) value, with a smaller value indicating a better model fit.30 Power analysis for the selected AE model was performed using the umx package (R) with an expected effect size of A = 0.5, alpha level = 0.05, target parameter ‘a’, and an ordinal analytic framework.

Results

Study sample and participant characteristics

Based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, this study ultimately enrolled a total of 377 twin pairs for genetic correlation analysis. All participants were between 40 and 80 years of age, with the overall cohort exhibiting a median age of 50 years. The median age was 51 years for MZ twins and 49 years for DZ twins. The distribution of acquired tooth loss was comparable between groups, with the majority in both having lost 1 to 8 teeth. The full ADE model showed that the variance for additive genetic, dominant genetic, and unique environmental factors was 29.34%, 28.45%, and 42.21%, respectively. After adjusting for age and sex, the AE model showed that additive genetic factors (heritability) contributed to 56.51% (95% CI: 43.21%-69.81%) of the variation in acquired tooth loss, with 43.49% (95% CI: 30.20%-56.79%) attributable to unique environmental factors. The DE model showed that dominant genetic factors (heritability) contributed to 58.43% (45.22%-71.64%) of the variation in acquired tooth loss, with the 41.57% (28.36%-54.78%) attributable to unique environmental factors. Although the AE model had the lowest AIC value, the small AIC difference between the AE and DE models suggests that the AE model may be marginally preferred based on the AIC criterion. In the AE model, the power of SEM A path was estimated at 0.84 (α = 0.05), based on a sample of 377 twin pairs.

Discussion

This study represents the first to estimate the heritability of acquired tooth loss among middle-aged and older Chinese populations from Qingdao, China. Based on the PCC for MZ and DZ twins, we selected the ADE model and nested model (AE and DE) to estimate the heritability of acquired tooth loss. After adjusting for age and sex, neither the AE nor the DE model differed significantly from the full ADE model. Based on the principle of parsimony, both nested models are more parsimonious than the full ADE model. Under the AE model, the heritability of acquired tooth loss in this Chinese cohort was estimated at 56.51%, with unique environmental factors accounting for 43.49%. Under the DE model, the heritability was 58.43%, with unique environmental factors accounting for 41.57%. The results collectively indicated a moderate influence of genetic factors on acquired tooth loss.

Previous studies have demonstrated the importance of genetic factors in acquired tooth loss. For example, a twin study involving Danish individuals aged 46 to 100 years attributed 36% of the variance in the number of remaining teeth to additive genetic influences.12 Consistent with this finding, our study also supported a significant influence of additive genetic factors on the number of acquired tooth loss. However, heritability in our cohort exceeded that documented in European studies. This discrepancy may be attributable to variations in allele frequencies and genetic effect sizes in different ethnicity. For example, Espinoza et al31 reported differences in lip print patterns among adolescents of different ancestries from different regions in Perú, which supports the existence of potential genetic heterogeneity for oral traits across populations. However, it should be noted that, although the AE model was selected as the preferred model according to the AIC in our study, the small AIC difference between the AE and DE models, and the ADE-AE and ADE-DE likelihood ratio tests were both very small and nonsignificant. Furthermore, the confidence intervals for the additive effect in the AE model (43.21%-69.81%) and the dominant effect in the DE model (45.22%-71.64%) showed substantial overlap. Therefore, our study indicates that genetic factors contribute to acquired tooth loss in the Chinese population, but the current sample size does not strongly discriminate between additive and dominant genetic variance structures. Thus, future studies with larger sample sizes or multiregional Chinese populations are warranted to further investigate the contributions of genetic components to tooth loss.

Unique environment explained 43.49% of the variance in acquired tooth loss, but the specific environmental factors remain unknown. Meanwhile, recent research has demonstrated that dental conditions can be effectively predicted using modelling approaches.32 Therefore, future studies may further investigate how specific environmental and genetic factors contribute to acquired tooth loss and develop a prediction model based on these factors.

First, our study has a notable advantage. Twins are highly matched in terms of age, intrauterine environment, and shared family environment. This matching enables a more precise estimation of the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors. In addition, our study also has several limitations. First, recall bias may affect the retrospective reporting of naturally occurring tooth loss, although tooth loss due to trauma or orthodontic treatment was excluded by dentists who verified the events using clinical records and questionnaire responses. Second, our moderate sample size may have limited the precision of variance estimates and the power to fully separate additive from dominant genetic effects. Larger future studies are warranted to further investigate the genetic mechanisms of tooth loss. Third, the Qingdao population-based study limits the generalizability of our findings. A study integrating data from more regions of the Chinese population is warranted to confirm our results on a national scale. Finally, as with any classical twin study, our heritability estimates may be influenced by the equal environments assumption, which assumes that MZ and DZ twins are equally influenced by their shared environment. Violation of this assumption could bias heritability estimates.33 Nevertheless, previous research suggests that the resulting bias is likely small.34

Conclusion

In summary, our study of twins from Qingdao, China, demonstrates that genetic factors significantly influence the number of acquired tooth loss, with an estimated heritability of 56.51%. Our findings address a gap in the literature concerning the genetic basis of acquired tooth loss in Chinese twin populations. These results enhance the understanding of genetic and environmental contributions to acquired tooth loss, which may assist in developing strategies to prevent acquired tooth loss and maintain oral health.
