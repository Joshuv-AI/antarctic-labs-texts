The trajectory of patient safety culture: a comparative analysis of Iranian healthcare workers’ perspectives during and post-COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Abolfazl Zakeri, Mohammad Ali Zakeri, Hassan Ahmadinia, Sayed Mortaza Hossini Rafsanjanipoor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic significantly impacted patient safety culture in hospitals. Patient safety culture is a critical factor in determining hospitals’ ability to manage and reduce patient risks. This study aimed to examine the perceived patient safety culture among healthcare workers (HCWs) in Iran during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsThis descriptive study included HCWs as the study population. A census sampling method was employed at a hospital in southeastern Iran. Data were collected using a demographic questionnaire and the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC), administered at two time points: initially in 2020 (during the Covid-19 pandemic) and again in 2023 (two different time periods). A total of 316 HCWs from a public hospital serving as a COVID-19 medical center participated in the initial phase (in 2020). In 2023 after the pandemic, 320 HCWs from the same hospital were included in the follow-up assessment. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 to calculate means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. Bivariate analysis using one-way ANOVA and t-tests examined the correlations between demographic/professional variables and HSOPSC dimensions.ResultsThe majority of participants in both 2020 and 2023 were female (77% vs. 73%), and most were nurses (72.46% vs. 75.93%). The overall safety culture scores were 44.06 in 2020 and 50.07 in 2023. The average positive response rate for patient safety culture was below 50% in 2020 (indicating a low safety culture) and exceeded 50% in 2023. In 2023, mean scores were significantly higher than in 2020 for Dimension 1 (Frequency of Event Reporting: 3.40 vs. 3.09), Dimension 5 (Teamwork Within Units: 3.69 vs. 3.44), and Dimension 10 (Hospital Management Support for Patient Safety: 3.29 vs. 3.00) (p < 0.001).ConclusionA strong patient safety culture can enhance patient safety and the quality of healthcare services. Therefore, hospital management should focus on strengthening all dimensions of patient safety culture by providing advocacy programs, soft skills (non-technical, cognitive and interpersonal), and support interventions based on sharing information and experiences, mentoring and peer-to-peer exchange to ensure safe patient care. Future research could investigate the factors that influence patient safety culture.Keywords: Patient safety culture, Patient safety, Healthcare workers, COVID-19, Pandemic

Introduction

Patient safety refers to the reduction of the risk of unnecessary harm associated with healthcare to an acceptable minimum. This minimum is defined by the collective implications of current knowledge, available resources, and the context in which care is delivered, compared to the risks of no treatment or alternative treatments [1, 2]. In fact, patient safety is one of the vital components and key quality indicators of healthcare systems [3].

Healthcare activities are among the most high-risk undertakings. This has made patient safety one of the most critical global health concerns [4]. However, the unprecedented challenges posed by the COVID-19 pandemic rendered patient safety culture more critical than ever, as health systems worldwide grappled with resource constraints, staff burnout, and rapidly evolving care protocols [5, 6]. For instance, emotional exhaustion and reduced job satisfaction documented among frontline workers during surges of the pandemic have been associated with increased medical errors and reduced adherence to safety protocols, thereby undermining patient safety culture [7]. Similarly, Bastani et al. found that implementing strategies to reduce physical and mental fatigue, balance work shifts, and manage task overload can significantly reduce medication error rates [8]. In Gilavand et al.‘s study, most nurses reported having made medication errors, which increased during shift work and the COVID-19 pandemic [9]. Patient safety is a cornerstone of high-quality healthcare, and the persistent risk of preventable harm has elevated safety culture to a global health priority.

Today, patient safety is a top priority for healthcare systems aiming to improve care quality [10]. According to the world health organization (WHO), patient safety is defined as “a framework of organized activities in healthcare that creates cultures, processes, procedures, behaviors, technologies, and environments that consistently and sustainably lower risks, reduce the occurrence of avoidable harm, make errors less likely, and lessen the impact of errors when they occur” [11]. The WHO emphasizes the need for global regions to prioritize patient safety and to develop science-based systems that improve safety standards and quality of care [12]. Patient safety plays a particularly crucial role in enhancing the quality of care in hospital settings [13], and evidence shows that it impacts all healthcare environments and systems [14, 15].

The primary responsibility of healthcare institutions is to deliver services in line with established standards while eliminating or minimizing risks that may compromise patient safety during care and treatment [16]. In hospital settings, reporting adverse events is a key strategy for improving patient safety and reducing negative outcomes. Non-punitive responses to incident reporting have proven effective in fostering a patient safety culture. Other essential elements include organizational learning, strong leadership commitment, efficient information flow, and communication based on mutual trust [17].

Globally, about 421 million people are hospitalized each year, and 42.7 million of them experience adverse events during their hospital stay. It is estimated that one in ten hospitalized patients is harmed, and at least half of these cases are preventable [12]. Second victim syndrome (SVS) is a phenomenon latent in healthcare organizations, coined in 2000 to describe the experience of healthcare workers (HCWs) who are directly or indirectly involved in an unexpected and unintentional error that has negative consequences for the patient and the HCWs [18]. After an adverse event (AE), healthcare workers may experience different outcomes: progression, survival, or resignation. Many studies have shown that the most common emotional reactions to an AE include anxiety, loss of self-esteem, guilt, worry, nervousness, anger, frustration, and lower professional self-confidence [19–22]. In addition, the main concerns are fear for the patient, the possibility of disciplinary action, and possible punishment [21]. Psychological consequences, such as dissatisfaction, burnout, emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and depressive symptoms [23], are often accompanied by physical symptoms such as sleep disturbances, frequent nausea, and increased blood pressure and respiratory rate [24, 25]. They are said to “suffer in silence” and their emotional state has negative consequences for patient safety and safety culture [26, 27]. Surveys show that up to 50% of all hospital workers become a second victim at least once in their career [26, 28]. Raising awareness about the “second victim” phenomenon, its causes and effects is crucial to breaking the culture of silence in healthcare and the isolation of healthcare workers, which are major obstacles to recovery strategies [29, 30].

Assessing safety culture allows healthcare organizations to better understand the perceptions and attitudes of their staff toward safety, and to identify factors that may contribute to errors [31]. A strong safety culture is essential for sustaining safety and guides healthcare providers in prioritizing patient safety at an organizational level [32]. Previous studies among medical staff have shown that higher awareness of patient safety culture is associated with greater patient satisfaction and a lower incidence of adverse events [33]. To foster a safety culture, healthcare leadership must adopt a collaborative approach and recognize that effective communication bridges the needs of healthcare professionals, support staff, and patients [34].

The COVID-19 pandemic has placed extraordinary pressure on frontline HCWs and hospitals. The influx of patients, high workloads, unpredictability and lethality of the virus, limited knowledge about the pathogen, uncertainty about treatment outcomes, unavailability of vaccines in many countries, and lack of definitive treatments created a unique emotional and psychological burden for HCWs [35, 36]. These unprecedented circumstances have worsened pre-existing health issues, increased chronic stress and burnout, and adversely affected the quality of care potentially leading to greater patient safety risks [37].

With the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, patient safety culture became increasingly important in hospital environments. However, a review of the literature indicates that comparative analyses of patient safety culture before and after the pandemic are limited. Most existing studies have employed cross-sectional designs to examine safety culture either during [38–40] or after [41, 42] the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the patient safety culture as perceived by HCWs during and after the COVID-19 pandemic in Iran. This will provide insight into how the pandemic influenced healthcare workers’ perceptions of patient safety culture after the COVID-19 pandemic.

Materials and methods

Hospital setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted at Ali Ibn Abi Taleb Hospital in Rafsanjan, which was the only designated facility for admitting COVID-19 patients in the city. The hospital employs approximately 1000 staff members and provides a wide range of healthcare services, as well as clinical training for medical students and professionals.

Patient Safety Units within the hospital are dedicated departments responsible for systematically mitigating care-related risks. Their functions include risk analysis, implementation of safety protocols, staff training, compliance monitoring, and patient safety research. These units have operated continuously since their establishment, both before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. In parallel, universities have incorporated ongoing patient safety education into their academic curricula. In collaboration with clinical specialists, these units work to prevent harm and improve care quality by fosteringa strong safety culture and developing preventive frameworks. During the COVID-19 pandemic, patient safety units implemented specific strategies—including theestablishment of rapid response safety teams, daily incident reporting meetings, and timely educational sessions focused on lessons learned from adverse events and near misses to reinforce a culture of safety throughout the crisis.

Sample size and sampling

The statistical population in this study included all staff, including nurses, midwives, anesthesia technicians, operating room, laboratory, pathology, etc (administrative staff, physiotherapists and pharmacy technicians). The sampling method was census. Inclusion criteria were work experience of at least six months and no history of mental disorders. Participants who were incomplete (response rate less than 50%) or withdrew, those who had to work for a certain period of time in a specific hospital, and those who worked in the hospital on overtime pay were excluded from the study.

Instruments

Demographic information

The demographic information collected from participants included age, years of work experience, weekly working hours, organizational position, type of work shifts, and hospital ward assignment.

Hospital survey on patient safety culture (HSOPSC)

The second one was HSOPSC designed by the US Agency for Health Care Research and Quality in 2004 [43]. Sinces this instrument has been implemented in this hospital regularly assessing the patient safety culture, we picked up the HSOPSC rather than the other instruments. This instrument is valid and reliable designed using various texts, cognitive factor analysis tests to assess patient safety culture in a hospital. This questionnaire was validated in Iran by Moghri et al. [44], confirming that the Persian version of the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPS) serves as a reliable tool for evaluating patient safety culture in Iranian hospitals. Across nearly all dimensions, the internal consistency of the translated questionnaire’s items ranged from 0.57 to 0.8 [44]. Cronbach’s alpha was re-evaluated to ensure the internal consistency of the instrument in this sample, which was equal to 0.85.

The 42-item HSOPSC measures 12 dimensions of patient safety culture. These dimensions includes dimension 1: Frequency of Reporting Events (items 1 to 3), dimension 2: General Understanding of Existing Safety (items 4 to 7), dimension 3: Actions and Preparations of the Supervisor of the Unit (items 8 to 11), dimension 4: Organizational Learning and Continuous Improvement (items 12 to 14), dimension 5: Team Work Within the Unit (items 15 to 18), dimension 6: Open Communication Channels (items 19 to 21), dimension 7: Communication and Providing Feedback on Errors (items 22 to 24), dimension 8: Non-Punitive Response when Errors Occur (items 25 to 27), dimension 9: Work Issues Related to Employees (items 28 to 31), dimension 10: Hospital Management Support for Safety (items 32 to 34), dimension 11: Teamwork Between Organizational Units (items 35 to 38) and dimension 12: Patient Transfer and Information Exchange (items 39 to 42).

The five-point likert scale was used: strongly agree, agree, niegther agree nor disagree, disagree, strongly disagree. Also, some other items were scored as follows: never, rarely, sometimes, most of the time, and always. Therefore, scoring will be different depending on the nature of the items. Some items were on the scale value of 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), from 5 (always) to 1 (never), and vice versa. The mean percentage of positive answers was used to analyze the data. First, the percentage of positive answers (the sum of items answered with strongly agree, strongly disagree, always, and most of the time as well as the sum of items answered with strongly disagree, disagree, never, and rarely) was determined at item-level, and then the mean percentage of positive answers was calculated at the composite-level by summing the percentages of positive answers at the item-level divided by the total number of items in one composite. The overall score of patient safety culture was also calculated by calculating the mean percentages of positive answers of the 12 dimensions of patient safety culture. According to the questionnaire guidelines, the 12-dimensional analysis defines the following safety culture levels based on mean positive response scores: ≥70 indicates high safety culture, 50–69 denotes medium safety culture, and < 50 reflects low safety culture. In addition, two items were added at the end of the questionnaire: the score respondents generally considered for the patient safety in their ward, and the frequency of errors reported in the past 12 months.

Data collection

The researcher visited the study sites and, upon securing necessary approvals, initiated data collection at the hospital. Data were collected through face-to-face distribution of the HSOPSC questionnaire and demographic forms during hospital shifts, ensuring privacy and confidentiality and obtaining informed consent. Both demographic data and HSOPSC questionnaires were completed and assessed in two phases: initially in 2020 and subsequently in 2023. The same Persian HSOPSC version was administered by the same research team, under identical instructions, and during equivalent phases of the hospital’s operational calendar. Of the total questionnaires distributed among HCWs in two different time periods (COVID-19 period: September to November 2020 and post-COVID-19 period: September to November 2023), 636 questionnaires were ultimately completed by them during the two periods (316 and 320 questionnaires, respectively) and used for the final analysis.

Analysis methods

The data were managed and analyzed using SPSS22. Descriptive statistics, including means, mean dimension scores, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were calculated. For comparison of mean HSOPSC dimension scores between the two study years (2020 and 2023), an independent sample t-test was used (between-year comparisons). Within each year, bivariate analyses were conducted separately to compare mean dimension scores across subgroups of demographic and professional variables such as gender, age group, work experience, and work shift. These within-year comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA or independent t-tests as appropriate.

Results

The characteristics of respondents

In both 2020 and 2023, a total of 636 questionnaires were completed by HCWs in the hospital setting. Of this number, 316 and 320 valid questionnaires were collected in 2020 and 2023, respectively. The majority of participants in both 2020 and 2023 were female (77 and 73%), and most of them were nurses (72.46% and 75.93%). Additionally, the majority of respondents were between the ages of 31 and 40, with 44% in 2020 and 46% in 2023 falling within this age range. Chi-square tests were conducted to compare the demographic characteristics between the two samples. The results indicated no statistically significant differences between the 2020 and 2023 respondents in terms of age (p = 0.881), gender (p = 0.404), profession (p = 0.269), shift type (p = 0.786), or weekly work hours (p = 0.787). However, a significant difference was observed in work experience (p = 0.036), with a higher proportion of respondents in 2023 had 11–15 years of experience compared to 2020. This variation in work experience may have influenced participants’ perceptions of patient safety culture, potentially confounding the observed differences. Regarding work experience distribution, the highest proportion of participants in 2020 had 6–10 years of experience (32.1%), while in 2023, the highest proportion was in the 11–15 years category (26%). A significant majority of participants (86% in 2020 and 89% in 2023) worked rotating shifts (morning, evening, or night). The weekly work time (hours) for most participants was between 41 to 50 hours (47.1% in 2020 and 46.6% in 2023) (Table 1). Ultimately, the safety culture score was 44.06 and 50.07 in 2020 and 2023, respectively. The overall patient safety culture score expressed as the average percentage of positive responses was below the 50% benchmark in 2020, indicating a weak safety culture, and rose above 50% in 2023, reflecting an acceptable level of safety culture.

Comparison of the HSOPSC dimension in 2020 and 2023

Initially, HSOPSC Validity was measured with the goodness-of-fit index (GFI), which was equal to 0.96. The statistical analysis revealed that dimension 3 (Actions and Preparations of the Supervisor of the Unit) had the highest mean score in both 2020 (3.91) and 2023 (4.07), while dimension 9 (Work Issues Related to Employees) had the lowest mean score in both years (2.31 in 2020 and 2.39 in 2023). As shown in Table 2, most dimensions showed higher mean scores in 2023 compared to 2020. However, dimensions 1, 2, 3, 5, and 10 showed statistically significant improvements (p < 0.05). These dimensions are key areas of patient safety culture, reflecting important shifts in perceptions over time. The remaining dimensions did not exhibit statistically significant changes (p > 0.05). Along with p-values, effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated to evaluate the practical significance of these changes. This index is used to assess and standardize the difference between two means. It allows for comparison of the effect of a treatment across studies that do not necessarily have the same units of measurement. This measure is similar to the Z-score. Hence, the interpretation of Cohen’s d is quite intuitive: a large Cohen d represents a large difference between two means. Cohen’s d magnitude is expressed in a number of standard deviations that separate the two groups [45].

Comparison of bivariable analysis of demographic in 2020 and 2023

To understand the factors affecting the respondents’ perceptions of patient safety culture in 2020 and 2023, a bivariate analysis between demographic variables and the HSOPSC dimensions was conducted separately for each year (Tables 3 and 4). In 2020, no significant differences were found in mean HSOPSC scores across age groups, except for dimension 4, where the mean score in the > 50 age group was significantly higher than in other age groups (p < 0.05). Similarly, in 2023, a significant difference was observed in dimension 10 across age groups, with the > 50 age group showing a higher mean score than other groups (Table 4). Regarding gender, no significant differences were observed in 2020. In 2023, female participants reported significantly higher scores than male participants on three dimensions of the safety culture survey: Dimension 2 (Overall Perceptions of Patient Safety), Dimension 5 (Teamwork Within Units), and Dimension 12 (Patient Transfer and Information Exchange) (p < 0.05). These findings may suggest gender-related differences in the perception or prioritization of key safety domains, particularly those involving interpersonal collaboration, situational awareness, and communication during care transitions. Perceptions of patient safety culture also varied with work experience. In 2020, only dimension 12 showed a significant difference, with HCWs having 6–10 years of experience reporting higher scores than other groups. In 2023, significant differences were found in dimension 10 (for those with more than 21 years of experience) and dimension 12 (for the 6–10 years group). Across both years, the vast majority of comparisons were not statistically significant, and the number of significant p-values was limited. Therefore, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied. Nonetheless, we acknowledge the risk of type I error and have added a cautionary note in the discussion section. All comparisons were conducted separately within each year, and no cross-year demographic analyses were performed in Tables 3 and 4.

Discussion

This study focused exclusively on healthcare workers (HCWs), a population whose perspectives provide valuable insight into the dynamics of patient safety culture within hospital settings. This targeted approach is particularly relevant given the unprecedented strain placed on healthcare systems during the COVID-19 pandemic. By examining differences in perceptions during (2020) and after (2023) the pandemic, this study offers a nuanced understanding of how patient safety culture evolved during this period.

Our findings revealed notable differences in patient safety culture between the two time points. Notably, the dimension “Actions and Preparations of the Unit Supervisor” consistently received the highest mean scores in both 2020 (mean = 3.91) and 2023 (mean = 4.07). This result aligns with the findings of Handayani et al. [46], who emphasized the critical role of frontline supervisors in fostering psychological safety and promoting a strong patient safety culture particularly under crisis conditions. It indicates that the supervisor of the unit was more often promoting safety to protect staff and during the pandemic. During the pandemic, the supervisor of the unit tended to be more caring and responsible for guiding their teams [47]. During the Covid-19 pandemic, the supervisor of the unit has a higher workload. In addition, the higher dependence of nursing staff on them makes them work hard to meet staff expectations. During the pandemic, supervisor of the unit must ensure nurses’ psychological and physical needs [48]. Supervisor of the unit in strategy implementation and goal-setting at each management level plays a vital role in promoting patient safety. Effective supervisors not only facilitate teamwork but also provide timely communication, feedback, and guidance elements essential for fostering a proactive safety environment [49, 50]. Recent focus group research with community pharmacists underscores how structured mentorship fosters resilience, reflective practice, and professional identity key pillars of psychological safety [51]. Mentors not only model constructive responses to errors but also create safe spaces for learning from adverse events. Given that our 2023 sample reported stronger perceptions of supervisor support and teamwork, it is plausible that informal or emerging formal mentorship dynamics contributed to these positive shifts.

In contrast, “Work Issues Related to Employees” which include staffing shortages, long working hours, and high workload had the lowest mean scores in both 2020 (Mean = 2.31) and 2023 (Mean = 2.39). These findings are consistent with global concerns about HCW shortages, especially in nursing. According to the WHO, by 2030 there will be a significant shortfall in HCWs worldwide, with the greatest impact expected in low- and lower-middle-income countries [52]. Furthermore, challenges in HCW education, retention, and performance remain prevalent across all income levels.

The persistent low scores in this dimension may also reflect the psychological toll associated with increased workload and extended working hours, particularly during the pandemic. Emotional exhaustion a state in which individuals lose the capacity to express positive emotions due to prolonged stress and psychological burden has been widely reported among HCWs [53]. For the hospital settings especially work overload and also low social support are associated with emotional exhaustion [54]. Ignoring of the role of the care setting in research on HCW’s emotional exhaustion is critical, as this contextual factor could be a risk factor in its own right or could shape the effect of other well-known risk factors [55]. Interventions at organizational and individual levels are needed to reduce emotional exhaustion in This condition can severely compromise staff well-being and, consequently, patient safety.

As a result, it is very important to find tools that can deal with such an issue, and one of these tools is peer support programs. This is a type of support that can be provided by someone who has suffered or is still suffering from the same illness, after training that makes them an experienced professional, that is, as a peer supporter. Sharing such life experiences with others can help to gain a deeper understanding of one’s condition [56]. The main goal of this type of intervention is to provide support based on sharing information and experiences, advice and mutual exchange between peers. The World Health Organization considers strengthening such social relationships as a strategy for promoting health and increasing sources of support, such as mutual aid [57].

Consistent with Chen et al. [58], our findings support the need for organizational and individual-level interventions to mitigate emotional exhaustion. Strategies may include access to psychological support services, structured emotional management training, and mechanisms for maintaining social connections with family and friends to provide emotional relief and resilience.

The mean scores for dimensions 1, 2, 3, 5, and 10 were significantly higher in 2023 compared to 2020, indicating key areas of improvement in patient safety culture during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, these findings must be interpreted with caution and in the context of broader literature and healthcare system dynamics. While the improvements in 2023 are encouraging, they should be interpreted with caution. The observed changes may be influenced by the easing of pandemic-related pressures, short-term interventions, or heightened awareness during data collection not necessarily by enduring shifts in organizational culture.

Dimension 1: Frequency of Reporting Events showed a statistically significant increase in 2023, suggesting that HCWs may have become more proactive in reporting patient safety incidents. Which, according to the results of the same study, could be related to the higher mean for dimension 2 (overall perception of patient safety) in 2023. So that staff responses indicated that despite increased pressures, patient safety remains a priority, likely due to increased awareness due to pandemic risks and the need to adhere to strict safety protocols. The increase in incident reporting in 2023 may reflect both improved non-punitive reporting systems and a gradual shift toward psychological safety, where staff felt more supported in disclosing errors consistent with hospital-wide efforts to decouple reporting from blame during the post-pandemic quality improvement initiatives.

Moreover, reporting systems are only effective when they not only collect data but also enable learning from errors and foster interprofessional dialogue [59]. This requires a fundamental cultural shift from assigning blame to fostering collective responsibility for improvement. The potential decline in reporting, as seen in other contexts, represents missed opportunities for learning, particularly during a global health crisis [60]. As such, hospital management must actively support event reporting, implement training in patient safety incident analysis, and empower supervisors to foster a psychologically safe environment for frontline staff [61, 62].

Despite the higher mean score for Dimension 2 (Overall Perceptions of Patient Safety) in 2023, it is important to note that the absolute value remained moderate, reflecting a persistently cautious perception of safety among HCWs. This could be attributed to high workloads and increased emotional strain during the pandemic. The findings emphasize the critical role of hospital management and supervisors in shaping these perceptions [63]. The staff responses suggested that, despite increased pressures, patient safety remained a priority likely driven by heightened awareness due to the pandemic’s risks and the need to adhere to strict safety protocols.

The improvement in Dimension 3 (Expectations and Actions of Supervisors/Managers) also suggests a shift toward stronger leadership engagement in promoting safety during the pandemic. Head nurses and unit managers became more proactive, protective, and involved in guiding their teams. This is consistent with findings from other studies, which indicate that managerial presence and responsiveness during crises are essential to maintaining trust and morale among staff [47, 64]. Effective crisis management includes proactive planning, multidisciplinary collaboration, adapting workflows, ensuring PPE availability, and addressing staff needs—both psychological and physical [65, 66].

Teamwork within units (Dimension 5) also improved in 2023, reinforcing the importance of collaboration, mutual respect, and coordinated efforts during health emergencies. Given the necessity of working closely across shifts and disciplines, strong intra-unit relationships are essential for resilience and efficiency. Nonetheless, while this dimension showed improvement, there is still room for growth, particularly given pandemic-related stressors such as staff turnover, role changes, and communication breakdowns. As noted in prior studies, poor leadership, unclear team structures, and limited communication contribute to weak team dynamics [12]. Evidence supports that targeted interventions such as team training, crew resource management, and simulation exercises can significantly enhance teamwork climate, boost staff performance, and improve patient outcomes [67]. Such strategies should be embedded into ongoing quality improvement and preparedness initiatives to ensure that healthcare teams are resilient in the face of future crises.

In 2021, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched the Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030, which for the first time recognized the importance of soft skills (non-technical, cognitive and interpersonal in healthcare and patient safety. Soft skills refer to a broad set of personal and interpersonal competencies such as communication, teamwork, adaptability, leadership and emotional intelligence that are transferable across professional contexts and are essential for effective collaboration and service delivery in healthcare. These skills complement technical competencies and significantly contribute to safe and person-centred care [68–70].

Finally, the significant increase in Dimension 10 (Hospital Management Support for Patient Safety) underlines the crucial role of institutional leadership during the COVID-19 pandemic. Hospitals activated crisis management protocols, developed disaster response plans, and adapted operations to ensure service continuity. Effective crisis response encompasses prevention, planning, response, and recovery. It requires clear communication, support for staff wellbeing, and managerial competence in handling complex, evolving situations [71]. The improvement in this dimension underscores the positive impact of visible, responsive management on shaping patient safety culture.

Limitation & recommendations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, staff perceptions of patient safety culture and communication are inherently dynamic and may fluctuate in response to day-to-day events and institutional changes. Second, the external validity of the results is limited by the single-site study design and the relatively small sample size. The study was conducted in a single regional hospital in Iran and included a limited group of HCWs, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to other institutions or countries. Although a census sampling approach was used, adopting a probability-based sampling technique, such as simple random sampling, in future studies may enhance the representativeness of the sample and improve result accuracy. Furthermore, a longitudinal study design would be more appropriate for capturing the long-term evolution of patient safety culture and the sustained impact of working with COVID-19 patients over time. Additionally, qualitative research methods such as interviews or focus groups are recommended to gain deeper insight into the lived experiences of HCWs during pandemics. Such an approach could uncover barriers and facilitators that quantitative surveys may overlook and provide richer context for understanding changes in safety culture. Lastly, although multiple bivariate comparisons were conducted, no formal statistical correction (e.g., Bonferroni or Holm adjustment) was applied to account for the risk of Type I error. As this study was exploratory and the number of significant results was limited, we opted not to apply such corrections. However, this increases the potential for false positives. Therefore, statistically significant results should be interpreted with caution, and future confirmatory studies should incorporate appropriate statistical corrections to improve the robustness and validity of findings.

Finally, Specific types of interventions that can be used to reduce emotional exhaustion include (1) learning to identify and control variables that increase emotional exhaustion; (2) developing employees’ coping skills to deal with frustration and work-related stress; (3) learning to give and receive positive feedback from colleagues and supervisors; and (4) receiving training in conflict and aggression management that helps employees develop strategies to effectively deal with daily problems, including workplace conflict [72]. Future qualitative studies such as in-depth interviews or focus groups could explore how HCWs experience safety culture on a daily basis, including their perceptions of trust, fairness, and support during high-stress periods. Such insights would reveal nuances that quantitative tools like HSOPSC may not capture.

Conclusion

The COVID-19 pandemic has introduced new challenges and intensified existing risks within healthcare settings, placing additional strain on patient safety and the well-being of HCWs. Our findings suggest that the pandemic prompted improvements in several key dimensions of patient safety culture particularly in areas such as communication, supervision, teamwork, and hospital management support. However, persistent issues related to staffing, workload, and emotional exhaustion continue to pose threats to patient safety. given the lack of management changes at the hospital and the lack of new policies adopted during the pandemic, the difference in some dimensions might actually result from the pandemic. The physical and mental health of HCWs is often overlooked during crisis conditions, which may compromise both their performance and patient outcomes. Demographic factors such as age, work experience, and professional role may influence how safety culture is perceived and should be considered when developing targeted interventions.

Given the above, in the post-crisis period it is essential to take care of how employees feel emotionally, because if the health organization manages burned staff who do not feel balance, trust and protection, good results cannot be achieved in everyday clinical practice. Also, that it is necessary within the health management to create an environment in which mutual trust will be developed and in which stakeholders will be able to communicate about problems regardless of whether they are individual problems or team problems. In other words, Ensuring the safety and well-being of healthcare workers is essential not only for their own health but also for safeguarding patient safety. A sustainable safety culture relies on both the physical and psychological support of HCWs. In addition, it is recommended that every health organization should aim to be an organization that effectively creates a person-centered culture of safety, a holistic, continuous improvement framework, and an effective model for sustainability. The key message of this study: “To keep patients safe, keep healthcare workers safe.” In such a way that healthcare systems must prioritize the long-term well-being of their workforce to ensure that both staff and patients are protected in future healthcare crises.
