Sex differences of synaptic plasticity and microglial remodeling in the dorsal hippocampus following trigeminal nerve injury in mice
Authors: Manindra Nath Tiwari, Lauren Paik, Fernando da Silva Fiorin, Man-Kyo Chung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Peripheral nerve injury patients have chronic pain as well as poor memory and cognitive functioning that is associated with hippocampal abnormalities. Despite sex-dependent changes in the hippocampus of patients with chronic pain, detailed sex differences in hippocampal plasticity after peripheral nerve injury are not well known. In mice with infraorbital nerve chronic constriction injury (ION-CCI), we found complex sex differences in the dorsal hippocampus (dHC). ION-CCI suppressed population spikes amplitude and long-term potentiation (LTP) in the dHC compared to sham group, which was consistent in females but not males. The field excitatory post-synaptic potential was comparable between sexes in the sham group, and ION-CCI suppressed it in both sexes. Short-term facilitation after paired-pulse protocol was also comparable between the sexes in the sham group but was only suppressed in males with ION-CCI. While microglial numbers in the dHC were comparable between the sexes, the proportion of hyper-ramified microglia was higher in males in the sham group. ION-CCI increased the total number of microglia in both sexes; however, the proportion of hyper-ramified microglia was increased by ION-CCI in females only. In novel object recognition tests, female mice exhibited better object recognition memory than males in the sham group, which was impaired by ION-CCI. Taken together, these results suggest that female mice exhibit stronger object recognition in the uninjured condition, which may be related to stronger hippocampal synaptic functions and potentiation. Furthermore, trigeminal nerve injury may produce a greater effect on synaptic functions and plasticity, along with cognitive deficits, in females.

Introduction

Trigeminal nerve injury can lead to debilitating chronic orofacial pain. In 20 % to 60 % of patients with chronic orofacial pain, clinically significant depression is also present (Karamat et al., 2022), which can severely affect patient quality of life (Madhavi et al., 2024). Patients with chronic neuropathic pain exhibit altered higher brain functions, including impaired memory, lack of attention, along with decision-making and specific cognitive deficits (Apkarian et al., 2004; Moriarty et al., 2017). The hippocampus is part of the limbic system and implicated in emotional responses. It also plays a critical role in the formation and maintenance of memory. The bidirectional relationship between the hippocampus and chronic pain has been intensively studied. In patients with temporal lobe epilepsy, the degree of damage in the left hippocampus correlates with lower mechanical pain sensitivity (Ayoub et al., 2024). Patients with acute lower back pain have larger hippocampi (Sacca et al., 2024); however, a reduced volume and altered shape of the anterior hippocampus are associated with persistent, chronic pain in patients with knee osteoarthritis or lower back pain (Apkarian et al., 2004; Barroso et al., 2023; Vachon-Presseau et al., 2016). Patients with chronic neuropathic pain also exhibit reduced hippocampi with shrunken gray matter (Geha et al., 2008; Mutso et al., 2012). Furthermore, patients with trigeminal neuralgia exhibit a reduced hippocampal volume, particularly in females (Vaculik et al., 2019). These hippocampal anomalies are proportionate to the longevity of pain in trigeminal neuralgia patients. Such reduced volume is normalized after treatment in treatment responders, but not in non-responders (Noorani et al., 2022).

Preclinical studies strongly support the role of the hippocampus in nerve-injury-induced chronic pain and emotional and cognitive dysfunctions. In the spared nerve injury model, the dorsal hippocampus (dHC) is associated with neuropathic pain (Wei et al., 2021). However, interestingly, the ventral hippocampus is not associated with allodynia after nerve injury; although, it is associated with anxiety- or depression-like behaviors in rodents (Fiore and Austin, 2019; Zhao et al., 2018). Spared nerve injury in mice induces hypoactivity of dHC CA1 neurons, and chemogenetic activation of CA1 neurons projecting to the medial prefrontal cortex alleviate allodynia and memory impairment (Han et al., 2024b). Trigeminal nerve injury induces anxio-depression-like behaviors and reduces spatial memory, as well as suppresses synaptic facilitation and increases the microglia in the dHC (Chen et al., 2023). Therefore, nerve injury drives the hypofunctioning of the dHC, which is likely to be associated with pain maintenance and emotional and cognitive dysfunctions.

In humans, hippocampal development and the structures of its subregions exhibit sex differences (Mu et al., 2020; van Eijk et al., 2020). Moreover, hippocampal volume, functional connectivity, and microstructural integrity depend on the estrous cycle (Barth et al., 2016; Lisofsky et al., 2015). Alterations of hippocampal structures in patients with chronic pain are also sex dependent. In brain imaging studies, the deformation of the anterior hippocampus is more evident in females with chronic back pain (Reckziegel et al., 2021). Furthermore, in patients with trigeminal neuralgia, hippocampal anomalies are more severe, and their restoration after treatment is more pronounced in females (Noorani et al., 2022; Vaculik et al., 2019). In rodents, sex differences in the hippocampus have been widely studied in terms of development, structure, and synaptic plasticity (Jain et al., 2019; van Eijk et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2018). Changes in anxio-depression-like behaviors and cognitive functions after sciatic nerve injury (Boullon et al., 2025; Cardenas et al., 2020; Won et al., 2020) or after trigeminal nerve injury (Chen et al., 2023) are sex dependent. In rodents, infraorbital nerve chronic constriction injury (ION-CCI) induces synaptic plasticity and microglial changes in dHP along with cognitive impairment and anxio-depression-like behaviors (Chen et al., 2023; Mu et al., 2023). However, sex-dependent mechanisms underlying trigeminal nerve injury-induced cognitive changes including memory, synaptic functions, and microglial changes in the dHC are not well known.

The objective of the study was to investigate the sex-dependent effects of trigeminal nerve injury on synaptic transmissions, synaptic facilitation, microglial changes in the hippocampus of mice with infraorbital nerve chronic constriction injuries (ION-CCI) and recognition memory in ION-CCI mice.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

All animal procedures strictly adhered to the guidelines outlined in the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Publication 85–23, Revised 1996). All of the animal procedures were performed following protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Maryland Baltimore. Animals were group-housed under standard conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. Total 38 C57bl/6 mice were randomly allocated to sham or nerve injury groups (19 sham and 19 CCI) and tested in behavioral assays. The mice were 8 to10 weeks-old at the time of surgery. Estrous cycles of female mice were not evaluated. After the completion of the behavioral assays, the 25 mice (13 sham and 12 CCI) were euthanized for electrophysiological assays. An independent cohort of 14 mice (7 sham and 7 CCI) were used for histological assays.

Trigeminal nerve injury

Trigeminal nerve injury was induced by ION-CCI, as described previously (Arora et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020). In brief, mice were anesthetized by injecting ketamine (80–150 mg/Kg) and xylazine (8–15 mg/Kg) peritoneally. In the ION-CCI group, the infra-orbital nerve was exposed and loosely ligated (two ligatures) using polystyrene thread, whereas in the sham group, the infra-orbital nerve was only exposed (without ligatures). Postoperative monitoring was performed up to 72 h after surgery. Two weeks post-surgery, behavioral assays, electrophysiology, and histopathological experiments were performed on the sham and ION-CCI mice.

Chemicals and solutions

The dissection solution comprised the following (in mM): 92 N-Methyl-D-Glucamine (NMDG), 3.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 20 2-[4-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazin-1-yl]ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), 25 glucose, 5 sodium ascorbate, 2 thiourea, 3 sodium pyruvate, 10 MgSO4, 0.5 CaCl2 (osmolarity 290–310 mOsm, pH 7.3–7.4). The solution for holding slices and recording (artificial cerebrospinal fluid; aCSF) contained (in mM) 124 NaCl, 3.5 KCl, 1NaH2PO4, 25NaHCO3, 10 glucose, 2 MgSO4, 2 CaCl2 (pH 7.3; osmolarity 290–310 mOsm). Reagents such as NMDG, KCl, NaH2PO4, NaHCO3, HEPES, glucose, sodium ascorbate, thiourea, sodium pyruvate, MgSO4, and CaCl2 were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Slice preparation

dHC CA1 brain slices were prepared using a vibratome brain slicer (Leica VT1000S; Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany) following the standard protocol (Tiwari et al., 2018). The mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and then decapitated. The brain was quickly dissected and placed in a chilled oxygenated dissection solution. It was then fixed on the vibratome platform using super glue, and 400 μm-thick coronal CA1 hippocampal slices were prepared from the sham and ION-CCI mice. Slices were warmed at 33 °C for 10–15 min, followed by incubation for recovery at room temperature for 30 min. The slices were then stored at room temperature in a storage chamber containing holding solution for further use.

Electrophysiology

Slices were placed in an interface chamber (BSC-HT, MA1 65–0075, Harvard Apparatus, Inc., Holliston, MA, USA) and perfused with prewarmed (34 ° C) aCSF at a constant flow rate (2–3 ml/min) via peristaltic pump (Gilson Minipuls 3 Peristaltic Pump; F117606, Middleton, WI, USA). The extracellular recordings were performed using a glass recording electrode filled with 3 M NaCl (1.2 outer diameter, 2–4 MΩ). A bridge amplifier (Axoclamp 2B, Molecular Devices, Foster City, CA, USA) was used to measure field potentials. The extracellular signals were acquired using a data acquisition system (Digi data 1322 A) and pCLAMP10 software (Molecular Devices).

Field potential (population spike)

The field potential was recorded by placing the recording electrode in the stratum pyramidale of the CA1 area. The bipolar stimulating electrode (FHC, ME, USA) was placed on the Schaffer collaterals under a stereoscope visual system. The input-output curves of the field responses were obtained by stimulating every slice at various intensities (from 0 to 24 V with increments of 2 V; duration 0.2 ms) via a stimulus isolator (ISO-Flex; A.M.P.I., Jerusalem, Israel). The field potential was quantified by measuring the peak amplitude (mV) of the population spike using pCLAMP10 software. The area under the curve (AUC) calculated from input-output field potential response was also compared.

Field-excitatory post-synaptic potential (f-EPSP)

The f-EPSP was recorded by placing a recording electrode in the stratum radiatum layer, while the stimulating electrode was placed on the Schaffer collaterals. The input-output curves of the f-EPSP responses were obtained by stimulating the slices with different intensities (from 0 to 24 V with increments of 2 V; duration 0.2 ms). f-EPSP was quantified by measuring its slope (mV/ms) using pCLAMP10 software. The AUC was also compared.

Measurement of the paired-pulse ratio

Synaptic facilitation was monitored in the CA1 hippocampus of sham and ION-CCI mice by paired-pulse stimulation using the standard protocol (Leung and Fu, 1994). In brief, two consecutive pulses (inter-pulse intervals of 60 ms) were delivered to the Schaffer collaterals, and the corresponding evoked f-EPSPs were designated as f-EPSP1 and f-EPSP2. The synaptic facilitations were quantified and presented as ratios of the f-EPSP2/f-EPSP1 slope.

Induction of long-term potentiation (LTP)

LTP was induced in CA1 hippocampal slices of sham and ION-CCI mice using the standard LTP induction protocol with slight modifications (Nguyen et al., 2000). The LTP protocol comprised three steps. In phase one, baseline f-EPSP was recorded by delivering low-intensity stimulation (with an adjusted stimulus intensity to evoke 30 %–40 % of the maximal f-EPSP response to avoid ceiling effects at low frequency (0.016 Hz). In phase two, high-frequency train stimulation (HFS) (paradigm: 4-trains of 1-s duration; frequency 100 Hz; spike width 0.2 ms; 5 min intervals) was delivered to the Schaffer collaterals area at the suprathreshold intensity (~1.5 times baseline stimulus intensity) to induce LTP. In phase three, f-EPSP was recorded by delivering the same stimulation paradigm used for baseline recording for a duration of 60 min after HFS delivery. The slopes of f-EPSP before and after HFS were plotted over time as mean ± SEM. For quantification of HFS-induced changes in f-EPSP slopes, we calculated the averaged f-EPSP slope as mean values of f-EPSP slope before or after HFS. Significantly increased f-EPSP slopes after HFS compared to the f-EPSP slops before HFS were interpreted as LTP. For group comparisons, the normalized f-EPSP slope were calculated as (averaged f-EPSP slope after HFS/averaged f-EPSP slope before HFS) X100 and expressed as % baseline.

Immunohistochemistry and morphological analysis of microglia

Immunohistochemical assays were conducted to analyze microglia in the CA1 of the dHC. Cryosectioning of brain in 20 μm thickness was performed for immunohistochemical assays. We used the primary antibody against ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba1; Cat: 019–19,741; Fujifilm Wako Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan). 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was also co-stained. Confocal microscopy was performed using a CSU-W1 spinning disk field scanning confocal system (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and z-stack images were acquired. We took images in the area of dorsal hippocampus corresponding to the location of −1.7 to −1.8 to Bregma. The images were analyzed by an examiner blinded to the experimental groups and sex of the mice.

Morphological profiling of the microglia

To estimate microglia density, we counted the entire microglia per image (850 μm × 650 μm). Eight different microglial shapes were identified and counted (Vidal-Itriago et al., 2022). Ramified microglia have three to four primary branches and a few secondary ones. Hyperramified microglia have five or more primary branches and multiple secondary/tertiary branches. Ameboid microglia have no processes. Ball-and-chain microglia have a ball-like structure on their process. Bulbous-tipped microglia have rounded ends to their processes. In addition, there are honeycomb microglia that have multiple nuclei interconnected by processes, jellyfish microglia that are elongated and have one process coming off of their soma, and trains of rod microglia, which are rod-shaped and include multiple nuclei in a linear path. The proportion of microglia in each category was calculated by dividing the number of microglia in each category by the total number of microglia.

Sholl analysis of the microglia

To compare the differences in microglial branching complexity between ION-CCI and sham mice, we performed a Sholl analysis (Morrison and Filosa, 2013). Concentric circles in five micrometer increments were overlayed on the microglia, with the innermost circle lining the edge of the soma and the outermost lining the edge of the longest process. The number of intersections between the circles and processes was then counted. We performed a Sholl analysis in microglia classified as ramified, hyper-ramified, ball-and-chain, and bulbous-tipped. To avoid selection bias, each brain slice was gridded into nine even squares where the microglia types were numbered, and a random number generator selected a cell per square. The microglia were traced with an SNT plug-in on ImageJ before the Sholl analysis was rendered.

Novel object recognition test (NORT)

NORT was performed to examine long-term object recognition memory (Lueptow, 2017). NORT is a well-established behavioral paradigm used to assess object recognition memory and cognitive function in rodents. This task relies on the tendency of rodents to explore novel objects more than familiar ones.

The NORT apparatus consisted of a rectangular arena (40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm) with two objects fixed on the floor of the arena. NORT was performed in three phases: In the habituation phase (days 1–2), mice were exposed to the new environment with full access to the arena without objects for 10 min for two consecutive days. In the training phase (day 3), the mice were placed in the arena with two identical objects (F1 and F2) for 10 min, allowing them to freely explore the objects and for memory consolidation. In the testing phase (day 4), one of the two objects was replaced by a novel object (N). Mice were placed in the arena to explore the familiar objects (F; randomly selected from F1 and F2) and the novel object (N) for five minutes. Training and test phases were recorded by a camera. Sniffing or touching the object was considered a positive response. Animals that did not explore the objects during the familiarization phase were considered to display negative responses, and those animals were excluded from the data analysis. The frequency and the time spent by each mouse exploring the familiar and novel objects were tracked and analyzed by video tracking software (Etho-vision XT-17; Noldus, Svendborg, Denmark).

Statistical analysis

The experimental design, the N numbers, and details of the statistical tests for each comparison are outlined in the figure legends. Results were summarized and expressed as means ± S.E.M. The statistical comparisons were performed in two groups by using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-tests. In instances when there were two variables in four groups, we used two-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc tests using GraphPad 10.1.2 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA). Two-way ANOVA results are reported in Table 1. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Trigeminal nerve injury-induced suppression of field potentials in the CA1 dHC was greater in females

We assessed field potentials in the CA1 hippocampus (Fig. 1A–C) by recording extracellular electrical signals from a population of pyramidal neurons in the CA1 area after electrically stimulating the axons arising from CA3. The amplitudes of population spikes with different stimulation intensities were assessed (Fig. 1D; Table 1). In the sex-aggregated analysis, the input-output responses obtained from evoked field potentials at different stimulus intensities were significantly suppressed in the CA1 hippocampus of ION-CCI mice compared to sham (Fig. 1D left). Furthermore, the population spike amplitude at half-maximal intensity (12 V) was significantly less in the CCI group than sham (Fig. 1E left; Table 1). The AUC obtained from input-output field responses was also significantly suppressed in the CA1 hippocampus of the ION-CCI group compared to sham (Fig. 1F left). In the sex-segregated analysis (Table 1), male mice did not exhibit significant differences in the input-output response (Fig. 1D; middle), population spike amplitude (Fig. 1E right), or AUC (Fig. 1F right) between sham and CCI groups. In contrast, female mice displayed significant suppression of the input-output response (Fig. 1D right), population spike amplitude (Fig. 1E right), and AUC (Fig. 1F right) in the CCI group compared to sham. These differences were primarily driven by the sex differences in population spikes in the sham groups. Comparing male and female input-output responses (Fig. 1D; middle filled circles vs right filled circles), sham/female mice showed significantly greater responses (Intensity X Sex interaction, F [12, 204] = 2.32, P = 0.009; Intensity, F [12, 611] = 38.0, p < 0.0001; Sex, F [1, 17] = 5.72, P = 0.029) than sham/male mice. Sham/female mice also showed significantly greater population spike amplitudes (P = 0.018; Fig. 1E right) and AUC (p = 0.018; Fig. 1F right; Table 1) than sham/male. However, in the comparisons of CCI/male and CCI/female mice, there was no significant difference in input-output responses (Intensity X Sex interaction, F [12,221] = 0.500, P = 0.913; Intensity, F [12, 221] = 12.56; P < 0.0001; Sex, F [1,221] = 16.34, P < 0.0001; Fig. 1D; middle open circles vs right open circles), population spike amplitudes (P = 0.1089; Fig. 1E right), and AUC (P = 0.1733; Fig. 1F right).

Trigeminal nerve-injury-induced suppression of f-EPSP in the CA1 dHC was not sex-dependent

We assessed the f-EPSP in the stratum radiatum of the CA1 hippocampus containing synapses with axons derived from CA3 pyramidal neurons (Fig. 2A). Stimulation of the Schaffer collaterals evoked f-EPSP, which was quantified by measuring its slope (Fig. 2B, C). In the sex-aggregated analysis (Table 1), the input-output response curve obtained from evoked f-EPSPs at variable stimulus intensities was significantly reduced in ION-CCI mice compared to sham (Fig. 2D left). The f-EPSP slope at half-maximal intensity was significantly smaller in ION-CCI mice compared to sham (Fig. 2E left). The AUC obtained from the input-output field response was also significantly suppressed in the CA1 hippocampus of the ION-CCI group compared to sham mice (Fig. 2F left). In the sex-segregated analysis (Table 1), male mice tended to suppress the input-output response and the AUC in the CCI mice compared to sham, but this was not statistically significant (Fig. 2D; Middle). The f-EPSP slope at half-maximal intensity and the AUC were significantly different between males in the sham and CCI groups (Fig. 2E, F; right). In contrast, female mice showed significant suppression of the input-output response, the f-EPSP slope at half-maximal intensity, and the AUC in the CCI group compared to sham mice (Fig. 2D, E, F right). When comparing male and female sham mice, there were no significant differences in the input-output response (Intensity X Sex interaction, F [12, 273] = 0.4010, P = 0.9626; Intensity, F [12, 273] = 20.29, P < 0.0001; Sex, F [1, 273] = 0.2183, P = 0.641; Fig. 1D middle filled circles vs right filled circles), f-EPSP slope at half-maximal intensity (P = 0.410; Fig. 1E right), or the AUC (P = 0.759; Fig. 1F right) Similarly, comparison between male and female CCI groups did not show any significant differences in the input-output response (Intensity X Sex interaction, F [12,312] = 0.118, P = 0.999; Intensity, F [12, 312] = 15.67, P < 0.0001; Sex, F [1,312] = 0.061, P = 0.806; Fig. 1D middle open circles vs right open circles), f-EPSP slope at half-maximal intensity (P = 0.5680; Fig. 1E right), or the AUC (P = 0.9124; Fig. 1F right). These results suggest that the CCI induces impairment of synaptic input equally in male and female and that sex differences in population spikes of CA1 pyramidal neurons are not due to sex differences in the synaptic inputs.

Trigeminal nerve injury-induced impaired short-term synaptic plasticity in the CA1 dHC and the impairment was more consistent in males

We investigated the short-term plasticity of synapses by paired-pulse facilitation in the CA1 hippocampus. Two consecutive, identical electrical stimulations of the Schaffer collaterals produced f-EPSPs in the CA1 and increased the slope of P2 compared to P1 in sham mice, supporting paired-pulse facilitation (Fig. 3A). However, the tendency of the change was modest in the CCI group, and the ratio of the P1 and P2 slope was significantly lower in the CCI group than sham in the sex-aggregated analysis (Fig. 3B, C left). Moreover, in sex-segregated analysis (Fig. 3C right), male mice displayed significant suppression of the P2/P1 ratio in the CCI group compared to sham, whereas female mice did not exhibit a significant difference. When comparing male and female mice, there was no significant difference in the ratio of the P1 and P2 slope in sham (P = 0.5574) or in CCI (P = 0.3342) (Table 1). These results suggest that short-term plasticity in dHC are not sex-dependent but CCI-induced suppression of short-term plasticity occurs more consistently in males than in females.

Long-term synaptic plasticity in the CA1 dHC was stronger in females and was impaired by trigeminal nerve injury

Long-term synaptic plasticity in the CA1 hippocampus was investigated by using the LTP protocol in sham and CCI mice (Fig. 4A). After HFS, there was a long-lasting increase in synaptic strength as assessed by the increased slope of f-EPSP from the CA1 hippocampus. In the sex-aggregated analysis (Table 1), f-EPSP slopes were significantly increased after HSF compared to baseline in sham mice (Fig. 4B–D left). In contrast, the f-EPSP slopes were not significantly different before and after HFS in CCI mice, indicating that trigeminal nerve injury impairs long-term synaptic plasticity in the CA1 dHC. In the sex-segregated analysis (Table 1), male mice did not exhibit a significant increase in f-EPSP slopes after HFS in the sham and CCI groups (Fig. 4C, D; Middle, and E). In contrast, female mice showed a more consistent and significant increase in f-EPSP slopes after HFS in sham, but not CCI, mice (Fig. 4C, D right, and E). When comparing male and female mice (Fig. 4E), there was no significant differences of normalized f-EPSP slopes in sham or CCI groups of male and female mice (F[1,44] = 0.228, P = 0.636). Posthoc comparisons between male and female groups were not significantly different in sham (P = 0.205) or in CCI (P = 0.566). These results suggest that the LTP of the CA1 dHC occurs more strongly in females than males and LTP in female is impaired by trigeminal nerve injury.

Sex differences in microglial changes in the dHC after trigeminal nerve injury

We determined the number of microglia in the CA1 dHC by counting the total Iba1-positive neurons per image (Fig. 5A). The total number of microglia in the CA1 dHC was greater in ION-CCI mice than sham mice in both the sex-aggregated and sex-segregated analyses (Fig. 5B; Table 1). The comparison of male and female mice did not show any significant change in the number of microglia in sham (P = 0.182) or in CCI groups (P = 0.154). The entire microglia population was profiled into one of eight categories based on their morphological characteristics (Fig. 5C, D). In the sex-aggregated analysis (Table 1), the most abundant type was ramified microglia in both sham and CCI mice (Fig. 5D, E). Approximately 50 % of the microglia in sham mice were ramified (Fig. 5E). In ION-CCI mice, the proportion of ramified microglia was slightly lower, but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.070; Fig. 5E left). This trend was also observed in males (p = 0.085) and females (p = 0.087; Fig. 5E right). When comparing the male and female sham groups, there was no significant change in the ratio of ramified microglia/total microglia in sham (P = 0.056) or in CCI (P = 0.055). The second most abundant microglial type was hyper-ramified, which was approximately 30 % in the sham group in the sex-aggregated analysis. The number of hyper-ramified microglia was significantly greater in the ION-CCI group than sham (Fig. 5D). The ratio of hyper-ramified microglia to all the microglia in each image was also significantly greater in CCI mice (Fig. 5F left). In the sex-segregated analysis (Fig. 5F right), ION-CCI significantly increased the hyper-ramified type by twofold in females compared to sham, whereas male mice did not show a significant difference (p = 0.166). When comparing male and female microglia in sham (Fig. 5F right), the ratio of the hyper-ramified type was significantly lower in sham/females than in sham/males. Comparing male and female CCI mice, the ratio of the hyper-ramified/total microglia was not significantly different (P = 0.405). These results suggest that hyper-ramified type of microglia are more abundant in male dHC in sham mice and CCI increases their number only in female.

Approximately 20 % of the total microglia in the dHC were classified as one of the six other types of microglia (ball and chain, bulbous tipped, honeycomb, jellyfish, and train of rod) (Fig. 5C, D). The jellyfish type showed a significant reduction in CCI mice compared to sham in the sex-aggregated analysis, but not between males and females (Supplemental Fig. 1). All of the other microglial types exhibited no differences between sham and CCI mice in either the sex-aggregated or sex-segregated analyses.

We further analyzed microglial morphology by quantifying processes and branching complexity through Sholl analyses (Fig. 6A). In the sex-aggregated analysis, microglia from ION-CCI mice showed a significantly greater number of intersections, and the processes reached farther from the soma compared to microglia from sham mice (Fig. 6B; Table 1). Furthermore, this significant difference was preserved in both males and females (Fig. 6C; Table 1). Comparing males and females (Table 1), male microglia displayed significantly greater numbers of intersections than female microglia in both sham and CCI mice (Fig. 6D). The size of the microglial soma was significantly greater in the ION-CCI group than sham in both the sex-aggregated and sex-segregated analyses (Fig. 6E). When comparing male and female mice (Fig. 6E right), there was no significant difference in microglia soma areas in sham mice (P = 0.9929) or in CCI mice (P = 0.6948).

Recognition memory was stronger in female mice and was impaired after trigeminal nerve injury

Recognition memory in sham and ION-CCI mice was evaluated by performing the NORT to assess long-term memory (Fig. 7A). We tracked the number of contacts and time spent exploring the two objects in the test arena (Fig. 7B). During the familiarization phase (Fig. 7C, E; Table 1), sham and CCI mice showed comparable preferences to the two identical objects (F1 and F2) in the sex-aggregated as well as sex-segregated analysis. During the novelty phase, in the sex-aggregated analysis (Fig. 7D, F left; Table 1), the number of contacts and the duration of exploration of the novel object (N) were significantly higher than those of the familiar object (F) in the sham group, which was interpreted to indicate that the mice preserved the memory of the familiar object explored on the previous day. In contrast, mice with ION-CCI did not show significant differences in the number of contacts or the duration of exploration among familiar (F) and novel objects (N). In the sex-segregated analysis, preference for the novel object was observed in female sham mice, which was not significantly different in female CCI mice (Fig. 7D, F; right). However, male mice did not show a significant difference in the number of contacts and duration of exploration between objects in either the sham or CCI group (Fig. 7D, F; middle). Comparing the male and female sham groups, the number of contacts were not significantly different (Object X Sex interaction, F [1, 34] = 1.936, P = 0.1732; Object, F [1, 34] = 7.069, P = 0.012; Sex, F [1, 34] = 1.626, P = 0.211), whereas the duration of exploration were significantly different in sham/male and sham/female mice (Object X Sex interaction, F [1, 34] = 3.123, P = 0.086; Object, F [1, 34] = 6.915, P = 0.013; Sex, F [1, 34] = 5.362, P = 0.027). In the posthoc analysis, the female/sham mice exhibited significantly higher exploration time than male/sham (P = 0.0134; Fig. 7F middle, right). In contrast, comparisons of male and female CCI groups showed significant difference neither in the number of contacts (Object X Sex interaction, F [1, 34] = 0.152, P = 0.699; Object, F [1, 34] = 1.867, P = 0.181; Sex, F [1, 34] = 0.718, P = 0.403) nor in the duration of exploration (Object X Sex interaction, F [1, 34] = 0.004, P = 0.952; Object, F [1, 34] = 1.494, P = 0.230; Sex, F [1, 34] = 0.505, P = 0.482). These data indicate that female mice show better recognition memory than male mice in sham groups and trigeminal neuropathy reduces recognition memory in females.

Discussion

Our data indicate that there are sex-dependent changes in synaptic functions, the microglia in the dHC, and recognition memory after trigeminal nerve injury. In this study, we focused our analysis on the dHC, as we were interested in the hippocampal structure as it relates to neuropathic pain and comorbid cognitive impairment in rodents (Han et al., 2024b; Wei et al., 2021). Our results suggest that trigeminal nerve injury leads to the suppression of dHC functioning and recognition memory. Furthermore, our findings regarding changes in the dHC after peripheral nerve injury are consistent with previous reports. Spared nerve injury in rats impairs synaptic transmission and reduces LTP in the dHP, which is associated with impaired object recognition memory or context extinction learning (Mutso et al., 2012; Xiong et al., 2020). ION-CCI in mice also suppresses LTP in the dHP and is associated with anxio-depression-like behaviors (Chen et al., 2023). However, it is unknown whether these changes exhibit sex differences.

Hippocampal LTP depends on estrogen signaling, and the underlying mechanisms are sex-dependent(Wang et al., 2018). However, sex differences in electrophysiological functioning in the hippocampus after peripheral nerve injury is unknown. Our study showed the presence of complex sex differences in hippocampal synaptic functioning and plasticity. In sham mice, females exhibited greater population spikes and stronger LTPs in the CA1 dHC than males. Furthermore, ION-CCI suppressed population spikes, post-synaptic potentials, and short- and long-term facilitation in the CA1 dHC. Trigeminal nerve-injury-induced suppression of population spikes and long-term facilitation was more evident in females, whereas the suppression of short-term facilitation was more distinct in males. We also showed that LTP induction in the CA1 dHC was more robust in females than males. This was not due to pre-LTP differences in synaptic inputs from CA3, as f-EPSPs in CA1 were not different between males and females. The generation of action potentials following EPSP was greater in females, indicated by the greater population spikes. These data suggest that trigeminal nerve injury alters dHC synaptic functions in a sex-dependent manner. However, the mechanisms of trigeminal nerve injury of LTP suppression and sex differences in hippocampal LTP are unknown and need to be determined. Since estrogen receptors or protein kinase A regulate hippocampal LTP in females (Jain et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018), these factors might be affected after trigeminal nerve injury and, in turn, modulate LTP in a sex-dependent manner. Brain regions involved in pain pathways might also drive sex dependent hippocampal plasticity after nerve injury. For example, the locus coeruleus to dentate gyrus connection reduces adult neurogenesis after nerve injury in males more than in females (Cardenas et al., 2020).

Microglia are the resident immune cells in the brain and exhibit diversity depending on brain region, age, and sex (Mouton et al., 2002). However, previous data on sex differences in dHC microglia are equivocal. The numbers of microglia in the female dHC have been reported to be either greater or lesser than in male C57bl/6 mice (Guneykaya et al., 2018; Mouton et al., 2002). Our data showed that the total number of microglia was comparable between the sexes in sham mice. However, the number of microglia in different brain regions increased under diverse pathological conditions, and changes in microglia in the dHC may depend on the type of peripheral nerve injury. Whereas common peroneal nerve ligation does not activate hippocampal microglia, spared nerve injury and partial sciatic nerve ligation do increase these numbers in the dHC (Hisaoka-Nakashima et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2008). We found that ION-CCI also increased the number of microglia in the dHC, which is consistent with a previous study (Chen et al., 2023). In our study, this increase occurred equally in both male and female mice. Microglial morphology also reflects the extent of their activation. Generally, ramified microglia are believed to be in a resting state with surveillant functions, whereas ameboid microglia are in an active state with phagocytic functions. However, recent studies have suggested that microglia morphology is diverse (with eight shape categories) and plays unique roles (Vidal-Itriago et al., 2022). We found that dHC microglia are sexually dimorphic. Among the eight different morphological categories, the proportion of hyper-ramified microglia was substantially higher in males. Consequently, male microglia showed more and longer branches than female microglia in sham mice. However, the functional significance of the sex differences in microglial morphology in dHC functioning is not clearly understood.

Interestingly, trigeminal nerve injury increased hyper-ramified microglia with longer and more processes. The proportion of this microglia was higher in males than females in the sham group, however, nerve-injury-induced increases in ramification were more evident in females. This is in contrast to a previous study reporting that de-ramified microglia were more enriched in the dHC after ION-CCI (Chen et al., 2023). In our study, de-ramified microglial types were in only minor proportions in both sham and ION-CCI mice. It is unclear why this discrepancy occurred, but we speculate that differences in the methods of assessment might contribute. An increase in hyper-ramified microglia in the dHC has been reported after chronic stress (Hellwig et al., 2016; Smith et al., 2019). While its functions are unclear, hyper-ramification of the microglia depends on CXC3R1-mediated signaling and is unlikely to be pro-inflammatory (Hellwig et al., 2016). Shock stress also increases hyper-ramified microglia and reduces the dendritic spines of pyramidal neurons in the dHC (Smith et al., 2019). In addition, shock stress increases complement C1q expression in pyramidal neurons (Smith et al., 2019), and C1q mediates the phagocytosis of synapses by microglia in the hippocampus after chronic stress (Han et al., 2022; Han et al., 2024a). Another model of trigeminal nerve injury via infraorbital nerve transection found reduced dendritic spines at the dHC (Jia et al., 2022); although there is no evidence for this in ION-CCI models. Therefore, we speculate that hyper-ramified microglia in the dHC after ION-CCI might be relevant to the decreased synaptic functions observed in our study, leading to the suppression of synaptic functions in the dHC.

This study showed that trigeminal nerve injury induced deficits in object recognition memory and that female mice exhibited better recognition memory than males. These behavioral phenotypes appear to be correlated with hippocampal electrophysiological properties. Since synaptic plasticity in the hippocampus is crucial for novelty acquisition (Kemp and Manahan-Vaughan, 2004; Ren et al., 2011; Xiong et al., 2020), our findings that females show greater population spikes and stronger LTP in the CA1 is consistent with better recognition of novel objects by females. Our behavioral data is consistent with a previous report that female mice are more sensitive to differences between familiar and novel objects than males (Bettis and Jacobs, 2012). Under our testing condition, male mice did not show learning of object recognition, and hence we could not determine if trigeminal nerve injury impairs object recognition memory in male. Further study is warranted to determine the impact of trigeminal nerve injury under new conditions in which male learning is facilitated. Indeed, the effect of nerve injury on cognitive functions and its sex differences in mice are more complicated, which may be attributable to different injury models and different tests for cognition domains. For example, object recognition is not consistently impaired after nerve injury (Boullon et al., 2025; Ren et al., 2011; Won et al., 2020; Xiong et al., 2020), and male mice are more vulnerable to spatial memory deficits after spinal nerve transaction injury or spared nerve injury than females (Boullon et al., 2025; Won et al., 2020). Thus, the effect of trigeminal nerve injury on different cognitive domains and sex differences needs to be determined more thoroughly in the future.

While our data suggest that trigeminal nerve injury induces sex-dependent changes in synaptic functions, plasticity, and microglia, their mechanistic connections with sex-dependent development of cognitive impairment or neuropathic pain need to be determined. In both humans and rodents, there is a side-specific association of hippocampal changes and chronic pain or anxio-depressive behaviors (Chen et al., 2023; Reckziegel et al., 2021). However, our assays were not confined to one side of the hippocampus, which could have contributed to the variations in some of our results. While our study shows the neurobiological consequences of nerve injury at the dHC, it is largely unknown how peripheral nerve injury leads to dHC alterations in a sex-specific manner.

In conclusion, we showed that trigeminal nerve injury induces sex-dependent changes in synaptic transmission, short- and long-term synaptic facilitation, and the microglia in the dHC. We also showed that female mice outperformed male in object recognition memory test, and trigeminal nerve injury impaired it in female. These changes in the dHC should be associated with cognitive and emotional changes in male and female patients with craniofacial nerve injury.
