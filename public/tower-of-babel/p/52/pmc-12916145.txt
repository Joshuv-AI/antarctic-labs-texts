The effects of a 13-year war on the reality of ophthalmology services and education in Syria
Authors: Oase Sbei, Waleed Kojan, Ibrahim Abboud, Lana Kuziez, Asad Loya, Ahmed Amer Zanabli, Fares Alahdab, Ahmad Al-Moujahed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To evaluate the impact of the Syrian conflict on ophthalmology services, residency training, and clinical infrastructure, and to identify areas requiring urgent reform in the post-conflict healthcare reconstruction period.Design:A mixed-methods study combining a comprehensive scoping review with two national cross-sectional surveys of ophthalmology residents and public ophthalmology hospitals across Syria.Methods:A scoping review of English-language ophthalmology-related literature concerning Syria (1979–2025) was conducted using five major databases and grey literature. Two structured surveys were administered: one targeting ophthalmology residents to assess training quality and educational structure, and another targeting public hospitals to evaluate equipment, service delivery, and patient volume. Descriptive statistics and thematic analysis were used to analyze survey data.Results:The scoping review included 49 studies categorized into five themes: war-related ocular trauma, disease prevalence, refugee eye health, ophthalmology education, and public awareness. The residency survey (n = 135) revealed that 63.5 % of respondents lacked structured curricula, 67.2 % reported no surgical case tracking, and 91.9 % reported absence of formal evaluation rubrics. The facility-based survey received responses from 4 of 13 hospitals, revealing critical equipment shortages, high patient loads, and unequal access to subspecialty and surgical services. The survey revealed significant shortages of functional ophthalmic equipment, including slit lamps, fundus cameras, perimeters, OCT machines, keratometers, tonometers, and YAG lasers. Many hospitals reported outdated or non-functional devices, with essential diagnostic and surgical tools either unavailable or in critical need of repair.Conclusion:Ophthalmology services and education in Syria have been severely affected by conflict-related damage, infrastructure collapse, and displacement. This study underscores the urgent need for national reconstruction efforts focused on standardizing residency curricula, upgrading hospital infrastructure, and ensuring equitable access to advanced ophthalmic care. International partnerships, targeted investments, and sustainable reform strategies will be critical in rebuilding Syria’s ophthalmic healthcare and education system.

Introduction

The onset of nationwide protests in 2011 marked the beginning of a prolonged conflict in Syria, severely disrupting healthcare, including ophthalmic services, through violence, infrastructural collapse, and displacement of medical personnel.1 Even before the war, Syria’s ophthalmology research and clinical infrastructure were limited; between 1979 and 2009, only six ophthalmology-related publications from Syria were indexed in Medline and Embase, reflecting chronic underinvestment in research capacity.2

Syria’s prewar healthcare system included public and private sectors. Public care, managed by the Ministries of Health and Higher Education, operated major teaching hospitals, while the private sector grew to one-third of hospital beds but remained largely unregulated and financially inaccessible.3,4 High-quality ophthalmic care was concentrated in cities, leaving rural populations reliant on underfunded public facilities.5 Fragmented administration, poor resource allocation, and shortages of ophthalmologists further limited care.5

Ophthalmology residency training was delivered primarily through structured programs at five major universities (Damascus, Aleppo, Al-Baath, Al-Furat, and Tishreen) and hospitals affiliated with the Ministries of Health and Defense.6 Although Syria’s ophthalmologist-to-population ratio exceeded WHO’s VISION 2020 targets, shortages of mid-level providers and lack of a national blindness prevention program persisted. Cataracts, refractive errors, glaucoma, and diabetic retinopathy remained inadequately addressed.7

With conflict escalation, half of Syria’s hospitals became nonfunctional due to personnel shortages, supply depletion, and infrastructure damage.3 Ophthalmology training, research, and patient care collapsed. Conflict-related ocular trauma due to explosives, gunfire, and chemical attacks have caused severe injuries including open-globe trauma, intraocular foreign bodies, retinal detachment, and endophthalmitis, often worsened by delayed treatment.8,9 Data on such trauma mostly derives from neighboring Turkey, while refugee displacement (over 5.7 million) strained healthcare in Jordan, Lebanon, and Turkey.10

The destruction of teaching hospitals weakened ophthalmology education and research capacity. Informal institutions such as Free Aleppo University and Idlib University emerged but lacked accreditation and resources.6 Research output briefly rose between 2010 and 2013, focusing on conflict-related health, but Syria’s overall research productivity and R&D investment remained among the lowest in the Eastern Mediterranean (0.88 publications per 100,000; 0.02053 % GDP), lagging behind regional and global averages.4,7,11,12

A major political shift on December 8, 2024, saw opposition forces overthrow Bashar Al-Assad and establish a caretaker government,1 creating a pivotal opportunity to rebuild Syria’s ophthalmic healthcare system and residency training. This paper presents a scoping review of ophthalmology-related literature in Syria together with two national surveys: one assessing residency training, including curriculum structure, faculty availability, and surgical exposure, and another evaluating hospital capacity in terms of equipment, patient volume, and specialized services. With the conflict’s conclusion, this study identifies persistent barriers and offers guidance for resource allocation, policy-making, and international collaboration to support reconstruction. The scoping review traces the evolution of ophthalmology services, training, and disease burden before, during, and immediately after the war, while the two cross-sectional surveys provide an updated snapshot (January 2025) of residency training and public hospital capacity.

Methods

Scoping review

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guideline13 to systematically map the available evidence on ophthalmology in Syria, with a focus on residency training, medical education, and clinical services before, during, and after the war.

Literature search and eligibility

A comprehensive search of peer-reviewed and grey literature was performed on September 22, 2025, across Ovidsearch databse, PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, and Research Square, using combinations of the terms “Syria,” “Ophthalmology,” “Ocular,” “Eye,” “Education,” and “Residency.” The full Ovid search strategy is provided in the Supplementary Material 1.

Screening process

Screening was performed in parallel by two independent reviewers using the Rayyan platform. Conflicts were resolved by discussion or, when necessary, by a third reviewer (AM). Titles and abstracts were assessed against predefined criteria, and full texts were reviewed when eligibility was uncertain.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria:

Studies were included if they were published in English between 1979 and 2025, addressed ophthalmology in Syria, and examined one or more of the following domains: clinical ophthalmic services, epidemiology or disease characteristics, residency training, or medical education. Studies were also included if they evaluated the impact of conflict in Syria or among Syrian refugees on ophthalmic care and education. Eligible study types were original research articles and reviews published in peer-reviewed journals or as grey literature.

Exclusion criteria:

Studies were excluded if they did not address ophthalmology, residency training, or education; if they lacked relevance to conflict in Syria or among Syrian refugees; or if they were case reports, conference abstracts, or editorials. Non-English publications, studies published before 1979, and animal or laboratory investigations such as those involving Syrian hamster models.

Data charting

Data extraction was conducted in parallel using Microsoft Excel with a standardized, pilot-tested template that captured the first author, year, study ID, title, country, design, sample size, methods, results, and limitations. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or, when necessary, by a third reviewer (AM). The charting process was designed not only to systematically capture study characteristics but also to support the primary objective of the review: to provide readers with a comprehensive picture of the status of ophthalmic health services, training, the impact of the war, and the current epidemiology of ophthalmic diseases as represented in the literature. Following data collection, studies were inductively grouped into thematic categories to facilitate synthesis and interpretation. Categorization was discussed among reviewers to ensure consistency and transparency and to minimize subjectivity in theme allocation. These categories included: War Injuries; Prevalence and Epidemiology of Ophthalmic Diseases; Syrian Refugee and Migrant Ophthalmic Health; Ophthalmology Training; Awareness and Knowledge of Medical Students on Ophthalmic Diseases; Awareness of Ophthalmic Diseases in Patients; Medical Ethics and Organ Donation; and WHO Reports.

Residency training survey

The resident survey was reported in accordance with the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) guideline.14 This cross-sectional survey targeted ophthalmology residents across all governmental training programs in Syria. A structured, anonymous Google Forms questionnaire was created in both Arabic and English and distributed to all ophthalmology chief residents in Syria, who then shared it with their fellow residents. Responses were collected until January 11, 2025.

The questionnaire focused on residency program structure, curriculum content, faculty involvement, and educational assessment. Items included closed-ended questions addressing the number of residents and their postgraduate year (PGY) distribution, faculty participation in teaching, availability of a structured curriculum, awareness of program objectives and expectations, and the presence and perceived effectiveness of structured teaching activities. Additional items assessed the use of formal assessment methods, such as systems for logging surgical cases and rubrics for evaluating clinical and surgical skills. To encourage participation, all survey questions were optional, accounting for variations in the number of responses across items.

Survey data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel. Only descriptive analyses were performed, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations; no inferential statistical testing was conducted.

Assessment needs survey

The second cross-sectional survey assessed the availability of ophthalmic equipment, patient volume, specialized services, surgical procedures, and residency training across public ophthalmology hospitals in Syria. A structured Google Forms survey was distributed to the ophthalmology department chairs of 13 out of 16 public ophthalmology hospitals located in Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, Hama, Latakia, Tartus, Idlib, and Daraa. Data were collected from January 5 to January 13, 2025. The survey included both closed-ended questions, which collected structured quantitative data, and open-ended questions, which elicited qualitative responses regarding perceived challenges, priorities, and resource needs.

Data collection

The survey collected quantitative and qualitative data on hospital infrastructure, equipment functionality, consultation and surgical service availability, staffing levels, and patient volumes. It covered key areas including the availability and condition of essential ophthalmic equipment; hospital capacity, patient volume and resource constraints; access to specialized services such as retina, glaucoma, and pediatric ophthalmology; and the frequency, cost, and accessibility of different ophthalmic surgeries.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and medians, were generated in Microsoft Excel to summarize equipment availability, service utilization, and surgical procedures across the surveyed hospitals. These analyses were used to highlight trends, disparities, and gaps in ophthalmic care delivery. Qualitative responses were examined through thematic analysis following the six phase approach of the Braun and Clarke analysis, focusing on challenges, resource needs, and critical areas for improvement.

Ethical considerations

The survey was conducted with the approval of hospital administrators, and participation was voluntary. No patient-specific data were collected to ensure compliance with ethical standards for medical research. Findings were anonymized and aggregated to maintain confidentiality. The specific names of residency programs and hospitals are not mentioned to maintain confidentiality.

This methodological approach allowed for a comprehensive assessment of the current state of ophthalmic care in Syria’s public hospitals, highlighting key deficiencies and areas requiring urgent intervention. This study was exempt from IRB approval from Wayne State University School of Medicine, as anonymous surveys were used, with no collection of any identifiable or sensitive personal information.

Results

Scoping review

Additionally, the first national cross sectional survey of ophthalmology residents yielded 135 responses from various programs across multiple cities in Syria. The second cross sectional institutional assessment needs survey was distributed to 13 hospitals, of which only 4 hospitals, each from a different city, provided responses. A summary of the study selection process is outlined in Fig. 1.

This scoping review identified and synthesized 49 studies addressing ophthalmic disease burden, education, training, and care delivery in Syria and among Syrian refugees. Studies were categorized into five thematic domains: historical contributions and infrastructure, disease prevalence and epidemiology, war-related trauma and chemical exposure, refugee ophthalmic health, and ophthalmology education and training (Table 1). Detailed characteristics of the included studies, including study design, sample size, and key findings, are summarized in (Supplemntary Material 2) and more briefly in (Supplementary Material 3).

Pre-War era (Before 2011)

Pre-War landscape

Before the war in 2011, Syria’s ophthalmic care system provided adequate specialist coverage but faced limitations in research, mid-level eye care staffing, and surgical capacity. A 2009 review identified limited contemporary ophthalmological research, citing infrastructural deficits and minimal research investment.2 Pre-war conditions reported by WHO indicated a satisfactory ophthalmologist-to-population ratio (1 per 26,354), although mid-level eye care personnel (MLEP) remained insufficient, and cataract surgery rates were moderate.9

War era (2011–2024)

The body of evidence in this category demonstrates substantial documentation of war-related ocular trauma and chemical exposure during the Syrian conflict, with multiple studies highlighting severe injuries from explosive fragments, gunfire, and chemical agents, underscoring the devastating impact of conflict on ocular health. Most of the included studies were published in this era, and the number of published studies increased dramatically in this era compared to the prior era.

War-Related ocular injuries and chemical exposure.

War-related ocular trauma and chemical exposure during the Syrian conflict have been documented across multiple retrospective and experimental studies (n = 7). 10,11,15–19Conflict beginning in 2011 markedly impacted ophthalmic health. Retrospective studies from Turkey highlighted severe ocular injuries primarily due to explosive fragments and gunfire, frequently resulting in open-globe injuries, traumatic cataracts, vitreous hemorrhage, and retinal detachment.10,15,16 A Damascus-based study indicated similarly devastating ocular outcomes, including substantial rates of bilateral injuries and permanent blindness, predominantly from improvised explosive devices (IEDs) .11 Chemical warfare exacerbated ocular morbidity, with -sulfur mustard, chlorine, and sarin- exposure causing significant ocular burns, corneal opacity, temporary blindness, itchy eyes, and nystagmus, and excessive lacrimation.17–19

Prevalence and epidemiology of ocular disease.

Thirteen studies (n = 13)20–30 assessed disease burden in various Syrian populations. Keratoconus prevalence varied significantly across studies, ranging from 1.34 % to 18.19 %.20,21 A high prevalence of dry eye disease correlated with lifestyle factors such as smoking and cosmetic use, consistent with regional patterns.22 Pediatric retinoblastoma presented commonly with leukocoria and strabismus, emphasizing the need for structured national programs.23,24 Assessments of corneal endothelial cell density found Syrians had lower densities than many Asian populations but higher than Iranians, suggesting a population-specific susceptibility to bullous keratopathy.25 Cataract studies revealed predominant nuclear cataracts affecting vision in patients aged 51–70 years.26

Ophthalmic health among Syrian refugees.

Thirteen studies (n = 13)31–43 examined Syrian refugee eye health. Cataract patients exhibited increased rates of astigmatism,31 and Syrian refugee children in Canada showed significantly higher visual impairment compared to Canadian peers.32 A Lebanese study identified ocular allergy and cataracts as prevalent among Syrian refugees, alongside notable rates of severe visual impairment and blindness.33 In Zaatari Refugee Camp, Jordan, refugee children exhibited a high failure rate during vision screening, primarily due to astigmatism and amblyopia.34 Limited ophthalmic care access, barriers related to awareness, and systemic healthcare issues were commonly identified in these populations.35–38

Ophthalmology training and education.

Eight studies (n = 8)8,44–49 evaluated the impact of conflict on ophthalmology training, awareness of ophthalmic care and diseases, and workforce development. Studies investigating ophthalmology training and professional engagement in Syria reflected wartime disruptions. Prior to the conflict, structured residency programs existed across five major public universities; war subsequently caused severe damage to infrastructure and mentorship resources, prompting decentralized and often unregulated training initiatives.8 Post-war trends indicated renewed interest in ophthalmology residency among medical students, yet significant proportions intended to pursue careers abroad, highlighting ongoing domestic challenges.44 Research participation among ophthalmology residents remained low due to limited resources and mentorship, despite positive attitudes towards academic engagement.45,46

Ophthalmic awareness among Syrian medical students.

Three studies (n = 3) assessed awareness of ophthalmological conditions among medical students and patients, revealing generally limited knowledge. At Syrian Private University, inadequate exposure to subspecialties and emergency conditions was reported, while social media emerged as an increasingly significant educational resource.50–51

Awareness of ophthalmic diseases in patients and medical ethics & organ donation in Syrian ophthalmology.

Two studies (n = 2) assessed patient awareness of ophthalmic diseases, showing poor knowledge of glaucoma and diabetic retinopathy, highlighting the need for improved education and screening.52,53 Two additional studies (n = 2) examined corneal donation awareness, which was relatively high among students but lower among the general population, reflecting cultural and systemic barriers to donation acceptance.54,55

Residency training survey results

A total of 135 responses out of approximately 475 ophthalmology residents in Syria were recorded by 1/11/2025, with participation from residents at various governmental ophthalmology programs.

The average number of total ophthalmology residents was 36.11 per program (SD=13.62; range: 3–55), with an average of 8.06 residents per postgraduate year (PGY) (SD=4.03, range: 1–24). Among respondents, 39 were in PGY-1, 32 in PGY-2, 39 in PGY-3, 28 in PGY-4 (Table 2). Resident numbers are presented by city rather than by individual institution in order to preserve confidentiality, as requested by the department chairs.

On average, each program was reported to have 6.56 faculty members involved in teaching and supervision (SD=3.78; range: 0–25). A structured residency curriculum was reported as not available in 63.5 % of 126 responses. Regarding awareness of residency objectives and expectations, only 28.9 % reported that objectives were clearly outlined, leaving 57.8 % reporting that they were only partially aware and 13.3 % stating that expectations were not clearly defined.

Regarding organized teaching activities offered by residency programs, 43.3 % of respondents reported them to be somewhat structured or well-structured. 16.4 % found them not very helpful, 35.1 % reported that no teaching activities were available. Only 5.2 % reported them as very helpful.

Assessment and tracking of surgical cases and skills were also limited. Of 122 respondents, 67.2 % reported that there is no system to track surgical case logs and procedural skills. Additionally, 91.9 % of 123 respondents reported that their programs lacked defined rubrics or formal evaluations for clinical and surgical skills.

Assessment needs survey results

Equipment and infrastructure deficiencies

The survey was completed by four out of the thirteen hospitals contacted. All four hospitals face critical shortages of functional ophthalmic equipment presented quantaittavely in Table 3 and qualitvately through a thematic analyses in Table 4. The functionality and availability of the machines were based off of the answers given in the questionaire. Slit lamps are mostly non-functional or outdated, with only one hospital having three fully operational units. Essential diagnostic tools such as OCT, tonometers, and perimeters are either unavailable or in severe disrepair, limiting the ability to diagnose and monitor conditions such as glaucoma and retinal diseases. Vitrectomy machines, crucial for retinal surgeries, are completely unavailable in three hospitals, and while Hospital 4 has two, both are non-functional. The availability of intraocular lenses (IOLs) is inconsistent, with two hospitals lacking any supply. Additionally, phacoemulsification machines, essential for cataract surgery, are present in all hospitals but none are fully operational, requiring urgent repairs or replacement.

Ophthalmic services and specializations

While all surveyed hospitals provide consultations in general, retina, glaucoma, cornea, and pediatric ophthalmology, patient volumes vary significantly across facilities. One hospital consistently reported the highest monthly case volumes, including 1000 retina, 900 pediatric, and 800 glaucoma patients. In contrast, another hospital, despite having the most extensive infrastructure, reported the lower patient numbers in most subspecialties. Refractive surgery services were not universally available, with one hospital not providing this service altogether.

Surgical services and procedures

All surveyed hospitals provide essential surgical interventions, including cataract and anterior segment surgeries, medical retina procedures, corneal surgeries, and glaucoma interventions. However, specialized surgeries varied across facilities. Certain facilities lack surgical retina and neuro-ophthalmology services, while refractive and ocular oncology surgeries were only limited to two hospitals.

Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive scoping review to consolidate evidence on ophthalmology in Syria, spanning clinical services, education, disease prevalence, war-related trauma, and refugee health disparities. The absence of prior reviews in this domain reflects the historically limited research output and documentation in Syrian ophthalmology.

War-related ocular trauma has had lasting effects on vision, with explosive and gunfire injuries frequently leading to irreversible blindness.56–58 Complications such as endophthalmitis, retinal detachment, and sulfur mustard–induced corneal burns underscore delays in specialized care, weak referral systems, and the absence of standardized chemical injury protocols.11,15,16,19,59 Reliance on cross-border healthcare institutions, particularly in Turkey prior to December 8th,2024, further reflects disruptions to national infrastructure and highlights the urgent need for Syrian-based trauma registries to improve clinical management and guide resource allocation.10,58

Epidemiological evidence suggests strong environmental and systemic factors influencing ocular disease patterns. Regional variations in keratoconus prevalence emphasize the importance of locally tailored screening programs,20,21,60,61 while the high burden of diabetic retinopathy compared to global benchmarks points to systemic failures in diabetes management and preventive ophthalmology.62–64 Cataract care remains fragmented, reflecting inequities in surgical distribution and underscoring the need for national strategies to expand surgical capacity, workforce training, and cataract outreach campaigns.26

Among displaced Syrians, ophthalmic health disparities are pronounced. Refugees experience higher rates of visual impairment and refractive error than host populations, with early-onset cataracts and astigmatism suggesting contributions from nutritional and environmental stressors.31–37,39,40 These findings reinforce the need for integrated refugee eye care strategies embedded within broader health systems.

Public awareness and health-seeking behavior represent another major barrier. Low recognition of glaucoma and diabetic retinopathy, limited screening uptake, and restricted access to advanced technologies such as minimally invasive glaucoma surgery point to systemic and educational deficiencies.38,53,65,66 Yet, comparatively higher willingness to donate corneas suggests opportunities to expand transplantation programs through targeted community education and addressing rural disparities.67,68–70

Finally, ophthalmology education and training remain critically underdeveloped. Structural weaknesses, including unaccredited residency programs, inadequate supervision, and limited resident research participation, threaten the sustainability of Syria’s ophthalmic workforce. Addressing these gaps requires formal accreditation processes, mentorship, and long-term international partnerships to build capacity.8,45,46,71

Taken together, this review demonstrates the profound impact of conflict on ophthalmic health and training in Syria. It underscores the urgent need for investment in infrastructure, education, and public health initiatives to mitigate preventable blindness and support long-term system recovery.

Residency training survey

The residency training survey provides valuable insights into ophthalmology education in Syria, though its generalizability is limited as only 130 responses were obtained from an estimated 475 residents. Major concerns included the absence of structured curricula (reported by 63 % of residents), limited faculty availability, inadequate assessment methods, and unclear residency objectives . Additionally, formal training activities were minimal, averaging slightly more than one hour weekly. This absence of standardized guidelines and surgical case tracking could significantly hinder competency development and preparedness for independent practice.7,45,46 Formal teaching was minimal, averaging just over one hour weekly. Targeted interventions through enhanced mentorship, clear training guidelines, and structured evaluations could significantly improve training quality. Online education initiatives, particularly those led by diaspora ophthalmologists, have also provided consistent, accessible learning opportunities despite infrastructure challenges.

Training quality is further constrained by the consequences of prolonged conflict, including limited advanced diagnostic and surgical equipment, unreliable internet, and limited consumables. Restricted opportunities for international fellowships reduce exposure to advanced techniques, widening disparities with regional peers. Moreover, the war-induced emigration of experienced ophthalmologists has depleted local teaching capacity, exacerbating mentorship gaps and leaving trainees dependent on junior faculty or even senior residents. Strategic collaborations with international institutions could help mitigate these deficits through resource support, capacity building, and knowledge exchange, thereby standardizing education and assessment while ultimately improving patient care.

Needs assessment survey

The assessment of ophthalmic hospitals highlights critical deficiencies but is limited by responses from only four public institutions, thus not fully representing conditions nationwide. However, these responding hospitals are major centers in Syria’s largest cities, offering valuable insights supported by direct communication with other facilities. Significant equipment shortages impact patient care and training, including largely non-functional slit lamps, absent or outdated optical coherence tomography and fundus cameras, and entirely unavailable vitrectomy machines, essential for treating retinal diseases.72,73 Furthermore, the lack of operational tonometers impedes glaucoma management, and insufficient modern phacoemulsification machines limit cataract treatment capabilities.74,75 All hospitals reported having recovery and waiting areas, but the stark differences in capacity highlight the need for resource redistribution and infrastructure expansion in hospitals with higher patient loads. Enhancing capacity through targeted equipment donations to overcrowded hospitals and optimizing underutilized centers could improve efficiency and patient outcomes, a proven concept in other post-conflict regions.76 For example, in Sierra Leone and South Sudan, redistribution of surgical equipment and international donations were instrumental in addressing uneven service delivery, while in Afghanistan, partnerships with non-governmental organizations helped re-establish ophthalmic services by equipping regional hospitals with basic diagnostic and surgical tools. These experiences demonstrate that strategic resource allocation, paired with collaborative support, can accelerate recovery and improve equity in post-conflict health systems.77

Surgical service disparities remain a major obstacle. While cataract surgery is relatively accessible, advanced procedures such as retinal and glaucoma surgeries show marked variability across hospitals. Financial barriers further complicate service access, though public facilities primarily face resource limitations rather than cost issues. Due to the limited faculty numbers, residents often have limited exposure to subspecialty procedures, contributing to varied surgical complication rates. Policy-driven partnerships with global institutions could enable resource sharing, innovative training models, and more consistent surgical outcomes. Developing tele-ophthalmology services could also enhance remote diagnostic capabilities, thus reducing barriers caused by geographic isolation or conflict-related disruptions. Such coordinated interventions would improve both the immediate quality of ophthalmic care and support the gap in ophthalmologic care due to regional disparities while the access ramps up with time.

Limitations and recommendations

This scoping review has several notable limitations. Primarily, the studies lacked national representativeness, as most were conducted within specific institutions in major urban centers such as Damascus, Aleppo, Latakia, and Homs. The predominance of small-scale, cross-sectional studies further restricts the generalizability of findings across the broader Syrian population, limiting the ability to evaluate long-term trends or measure the effectiveness of ophthalmic interventions over time. Additionally, many war-related ocular trauma studies depend heavily on data collected from neighboring countries due to the wide-spread destruction of healthcare infrastructure and the displacement of ophthalmic specialists, presenting challenges for accurately assessing local disease burdens.

Another limitation pertains to the residency training and hospital assessment surveys, which also exhibit restricted scope. The residency training survey had approximately 130 respondents out of an estimated 475 ophthalmology residents nationwide, and did not include participants from every residency program. This limitation, despite representation from major residency programs, could lead to incomplete reflections of residents’ experiences. In addition, the absence of demographic data such as age and gender limits the ability to explore how individual characteristics may influence perceptions of training quality, faculty support, or access to structured curricula. Similarly, the hospital assessment survey, although targeting major public ophthalmic institutions in Syria’s largest cities, received responses from only four public hospitals. Consequently, it may not fully capture the state of ophthalmic facilities throughout Syria, particularly in rural or non-government-controlled regions.

Although Arabic is the primary language of instruction in Syrian medical faculties, restricting the search to English-language publications is another limitation. However, this restriction was imposed because peer-reviewed ophthalmic literature in Arabic is scarce, not systematically indexed in major databases, and limited preprint servers publish in Arabic. Furthermore, most Syrian researchers publish in English-language, often U.S. based journals to establish international visibility and academic credibility, particularly if pursuing opportunities internationally.

Future research should prioritize the development of multi-center, longitudinal studies incorporating broader geographical representation, including regions beyond government-controlled areas. Establishing comprehensive ophthalmic disease registries could substantially enhance the monitoring of epidemiological trends and enable the evaluation of public health interventions over time. To address deficiencies in ophthalmology training, strategic efforts must include the accreditation of emerging medical institutions, implementation of standardized residency curricula, structured surgical mentorship programs, and rigorous assessments of resident competencies. Additionally, it is critical to secure sustained funding for research initiatives and to encourage international collaboration, facilitating innovation and the adoption of global best practices in ophthalmic care and training within Syria.

Conclusion

The consequences of prolonged conflict, displacement, and weakened infrastructure have severely impacted ophthalmology research, education, and patient care in Syria. This review underscores the critical need for substantial reforms across ophthalmic healthcare, emphasizing targeted investment in robust data collection, trauma management, enhanced residency training, and preventive public health initiatives. Additionally, the assessments conducted on residency training and hospital infrastructure provide valuable insights, revealing urgent gaps in educational resources, mentorship availability, and essential diagnostic and surgical equipment.

While this study may not fully represent all ophthalmology programs and facilities across Syria, it highlights significant areas requiring immediate intervention. Addressing these systemic deficiencies necessitates coordinated governmental efforts, active international collaboration, and strategic investments aimed at rebuilding both medical education frameworks and healthcare infrastructure. Through concerted actions and sustainable reforms, Syria can effectively reconstruct a resilient ophthalmic healthcare system, capable of providing equitable, high-quality care to its population and mitigating the long-term impacts of conflict-driven health disparities.
