Psychometrics evaluation of the Chinese version of the revised Rushton Moral Resilience Scale in registered nurses
Authors: Xu Tian, Ting Shuai, Maria F Jimenez-Herrera, Aisha Al Shdefat, Hongcai Shang, Xiaoling Liu, Yi Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRegistered nurses frequently face moral sufferings due to unavoidable ethical conflicts, which highlights the importance of moral resilience as a mitigating resource. A valid and reliable tool is essential to effectively measure moral resilience.AimThe study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the psychometrics of the revised Rushton Moral Resilience Scale (RMRS) for use with Chinese registered nurses.Study designA methodological study employing a cross-sectional survey design.Participants and contextA convenience sample of 432 Chinese registered nurses was recruited via an online survey platform from October to November 2023.Ethical considerationThe study received approval from the Ethics Committees of Hunan Traditional Chinese Medical College (Approval number: YXLL202401004).Clinical trial numberNot applicable.ResultsThe revised RMRS was successfully translated and culturally adapted into the Chinese version. Introduction

Healthcare professionals frequently encounter conditions that challenge or undermine their moral values and beliefs within their professional roles [1]. Ethical conflicts often arise from shifting protocols, additional responsibilities, resources shortages, and constraints in delivering patient care. Among healthcare professionals, registered nurses, who typically serve as the frontline providers of medical care [2], are particularly susceptible to these ethical conflicts [3–6]. These unresolved conflicts threaten the integrity of healthcare professionals [7–9], resulting in various manifestations of moral suffering, such as moral distress and moral injury [10]. Among these forms of moral suffering, moral distress has been most extensively investigated among healthcare professionals [11].

Moral distress, a well-documented phenomenon among healthcare professionals, generally manifests as a somatic, psychological, and cognitive response to real or perceived threats to one’s values, commitments, and integrity. If not addressed, moral distress can lead to numerous negative consequences, including increased stress, workplace fatigue, impaired professional relationships, job burnout, and lower professional identity, which can ultimately result in higher nurse resignation rates [12–14]. Moreover, moral distress can negatively impact patient care, as distressed nurses may provide inadequate support to patients due to insufficient attention to patient suffering and avoiding certain patient requests or needs, thereby compromising health outcomes [15]. Consequently, reducing moral distress is crucial for enhancing the quality of nursing care [16].

The cultivation of moral resilience emerges as an effective strategy for alleviating moral suffering, particularly moral distress, among healthcare professionals [17]. According to Rushton et al. [17], moral resilience is defined as “the capacity of individuals to maintain or restore their integrity in response to ethical conflicts”. Following Rushton’s definition, different interpretations of moral resilience have been conceptualized [18–20]. Rushton et al. [11] developed the Rushton Moral Resilience Scale (RMRS) to characterize the moral resilience of healthcare professionals and evaluate interventions designed to enhance it. However, cultural values significantly influence the ethical experiences of healthcare professionals [21], and differences between Western and Chinese cultures affect perceptions of ethical responsibilities. For example, Chinese nurses often base their ethical responsibilities on virtue, including a sense of obligation to patients, superiors, colleagues, and society, while American nurses focus on ethical principles, such as justice, beneficence, and autonomy [6]. Given these cultural differences, we previously translated and culturally adapted the original RMRS for Chinese registered nurses (Chi-RMRS), achieving satisfactory reliability and validity [22].

However, Rushton et al. [23] identified the need to refine some sub-scales to better reflect their content and improve reliability across interprofessional samples, resulting in a revised RMRS comprising 16 items (RMRS-16). Similarly, the Chinese adaptation of the original RMRS reported a high composite reliability (CR) coefficient of 0.920 for the overall scale, indicating potential item redundancy [22]. Therefore, we performed this study to translate and culturally adapt the revised RMRS for Chinese registered nurses and evaluate its psychometric properties. This effort seeks to provide a concise and effective tool for quickly measuring moral resilience among Chinese registered nurses, thereby contributing to better understanding and management of moral distress in this population.

Methods

Aim

This study focused on translating and culturally adapting the original revised RMRS into Chinese, and further evaluate the psychometric properties of the Chinese version of the RMRS-16 among Chinese registered nurses.

Design and participants

This study employed an online cross-sectional survey conducted from October to November 2023. A total of 475 participants were recruited using a convenience sampling strategy. Based on the rule of thumb for sample size estimation in quantitative research, each survey item requires 5 to 10 respondents [24]. To enhance the credibility and reliability of the findings, it was determined that a minimum of 10 respondents per item was necessary. With the questionnaire consisting of 16 items, the initial target sample size was set at 160 registered nurses. However, to account for potential non-response or invalid data, the recruitment goal was increased, ultimately involving 432 registered nurses [25]. Eligibility criteria included registered nurses with a minimum of one year’s nursing experience in hospital who willingly participated in the study. Nursing trainees and those without labor contracts were excluded.

Instruments

General information questionnaire

We designed a comprehensive questionnaire to collect various demographic and professional characteristics. The collected information encompassed participants’ demographic characteristics, including age, gender, place of birth, marital status, educational level, employment type and job title. Additionally, it included variables related to work conditions such as night-shift schedule, hospital level and department affiliation. Moreover, the data also covered monthly income and years of professional experience.

Rushton moral resilience scale-16 (RMRS-16)

The original RMRS [11] comprises 17 items designed to evaluate four essential components of moral resilience: response to moral adversity, personal integrity, relational integrity, and moral efficacy. Participants indicate their level of agreement with each item using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (disagree) to 4 (agree). The total score is calculated by averaging all 17 items, with higher scores reflecting greater moral resilience. The RMRS demonstrates strong overall reliability (α = 0.84) and shows convergent validity with the Connor Davidson Resilience Scale-10 (CD-RISC-10), as well as criterion validity with the Maslach Burnout Inventory-Human Services Survey [11, 26]. In a subsequent study, Rushton et al. refined the RMRS, shortening it from 21 to 16 items. The RMRS-16 achieved acceptable reliability for all subscales and the total scale (α ≥ 0.70), confirming its adequacy in terms of reliability and validity [23].

Connor-Davidson resilience scale-10 (CD-RISC-10)

The Connor-Davidson resilience scale-10 (CD-RISC-10) is a concise 10-item measure derived from the original Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), specifically designed to assess general resilience [27]. Each item on the CD-RISC-10 is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (not true at all) to 4 (true nearly all the time). The scale demonstrates strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.85. Additionally, the CD-RISC-10 has been translated into Chinese by Ye et al. [28], who reported both satisfactory reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) and validity for the translated version.

Moral distress thermometer (MDT)

The moral distress thermometer (MDT), developed by Wocial and Weaver in 2012, is a tool designed to accurately measure real-time moral distress and track its trajectory over time [29]. The MDT operates as a thermometer-like rating tool, with a scale of 0 to 10 indicating varying levels of moral distress 0–2 (none), 2–4 (mild), 4–6 (uncomfortable), 6–8 (distressing), and 8–10 (intense to worst possible). Wocial and Weaver also provided a guide to help users understand moral distress and use the MDT effectively: “Moral distress occurs when you believe you know the ethically correct thing to do, but something or someone restricts your ability to pursue the right course of action. Please circle the number (0–10) on the thermometer that best describes how much moral distress you have been experiencing related to work in the past 2 weeks, including today” [29]. To validate the MDT, Wocial and Weaver compared it with the Moral Distress Scale(MDS) 2009. They found a low correlation with the pediatric MDS and a moderate correlation with the adult MDS. Additionally, they assessed the mean MDT scores across different turnover groups and compared these with the results of MDS 2009, demonstrating satisfactory concurrent validity for the MDT. Our team has translated the MDT for use in the Chinese context, and evaluation of its psychometrics indicated that the Chinese version of the MDT is a reliable, valid, and user-friendly tool for rapidly assessing the real-time moral distress of registered nurses in China [30].

Procedures

Translation and cultural adaptation

After securing permission from Professor Rushton via email, two translators, X.T. and LJ. Y., translated the original RMRS-16 into Chinese using the modified Brislin’s translation process, which includes both translation and back-translation steps [31]. The two translators, both holding doctorates from the English doctoral program at the Universitat Rovira i Virgili (URV) and proficient in Chinese and English, undertook the translation with careful consideration of both the literal meaning and contextual relevance. Each translator translated the text into their respective native language, with one translating from English to Chinese and another from Chinese to English. The back translation was performed by an experienced nursing teacher with a focus on nursing ethics, who was unfamiliar with the RMRS-16, ensuring an unbiased translation. The research team then compared and discussed the translations to reach a consensus, aiming for operational equivalence in the Chinese context.

Data collection

We used Sojump (available at: https://www.wjx.cn/), the most popular online survey platform in mainland of China, to create our questionnaire. The principal investigator established collaborative relationships with nursing directors from various hospitals in Chongqing municipality. These directors were provided with the survey link, which included a general demographic questionnaire, the RMRS-16, and the MDT. After being briefed on the objective and procedures of this study, the nursing directors disseminated the link to their respective teams. This ensured that all registered nurses within their departments could participate in the data collection process.

Data analysis

In this study, statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 and AMOS version 22.0. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were described by the means and standard deviations (SD). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Items analysis

Item analysis was performed using critical ratio, correlation coefficient, and Cronbach’s α after removing one item. The critical ratio was defined as the absolute t-value from an independent sample t-test comparing the highest 27% (high group) and the lowest 27% (low group) [32]. An item is considered to have suitable discrimination if its critical ratio is ≥ 3 and P < 0.05 [33]. To evaluate item homogeneity, the Pearson correlation test was used to measure the correlation between each item score and the total score. An item demonstrates appropriate homogeneity if the correlation coefficient between its score and the total questionnaire score is ≥ 0.4 [33]. Furthermore, Cronbach’s α coefficient was analyzed after deleting each item. If the removal of an item results in an increased Cronbach’s α coefficient, it indicates that the item may be redundant and should be considered for elimination.

Reliability analysis

We conducted an assessment of the reliability of the Chinese version of the RMRS-16. To measure internal consistency, we employed Cronbach’s α and used the following cut-off values as indicators: excellent (α > 0.90), good (α > 0.75), and moderate (α between 0.50 and 0.75) [34]. The presence of floor and ceiling effects was determined by identifying whether more than 15% of participants scored the minimum or maximum possible on the Chinese version of RMRS-16 [35]. Furthermore, we evaluated reliability using composite reliability (CR) values, deeming CR values greater than 0.75 as acceptable indicators of reliability.

Validity analysis

The validity analysis of the Chinese version of the revised RMRS involved evaluations of content validity, construct validity, and criterion validity. The content validity was assessed using the Delphi method, involving six nursing experts who evaluated the translated questionnaire. A 4-point Likert scale was used, with responses ranging from 4 (highly relevant) to 1 (irrelevant). Item-level content validity (I-CVI) and scale-level content validity (S-CVI) were calculated from these ratings. I-CVI was defined as the proportion of experts rating an item as 3 or higher, ideally exceeding 0.78 [36]. The S-CVI was computed as the mean I-CVI across all 16 items, with a value of S-CVI ≥ 0.90 indicates satisfactory content validity [36].

To assess construct validity, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were conducted. A total of 432 registered nurses were divided into two groups: 205 for EFA and 227 for CFA. EFA aimed to identify the underlying factor structure of the translated questionnaire, while CFA aimed to confirm this structure. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett’s sphericity tests were used to determine the suitability of the data for factor analysis. A KMO value above 0.60 and a significant Bartlett’s test (P < 0.05) were considered indicators of appropriate factor analysis [37]. Eigenvalues ≥ 1.0, and factor loading values ≥ 0.5, were considered as the criteria to justify whether remove a certain item or not [38]. Furthermore, items with cross-loading over 0.4 on more than one factor were also removed [39]. CFA model fitting was performed using AMOS version 22.0, with a good fit indicated by CMIN/DF value > 3.0, good fit index (GFI) > 0.90, incremental fit index (IFI) > 0.9 [36, 40], and RMSEA < 0.08 [22].

Criterion validity was evaluated by examining the correlation of the Chinese version of RMRS-16 and established scales, namely CD-RISC-10 and MDT. Pearson correlation analysis was performed for this purpose. A correlation coefficient is greater than 0.70, between 0.4 and 0.70, and less than 0.40 indicates high, moderate and low validity, respectively [41].

Research ethics committee approval

All participating nurses volunteered for this study, providing electronically signed informed consent. Anonymity was ensured during the completion of all questionnaires. Additionally, the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Traditional Chinese Medical College (Approval number: YXLL202401004).

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

A total of 432 registered nurses were enrolled in the final analysis. In general, the sample size for EFA is set to 10 times the number of scale items, while the sample size for CFA should be greater than 200 cases. In addition, the sample size for CFA should be larger than that for EFA. Therefore, we divided 205 into the EFA group and 227 into the CFA group. Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic characteristics of all participants. The mean age of participants in the EFA group was 32.01 years (SD = 6.56), slightly higher than the CFA group at 31.87 years (SD = 5.67). The mean working years were comparable, with the EFA group at 9.36 years (SD = 7.07) and the CFA group at 9.15 years (SD = 6.07). Gender distribution showed a higher proportion of females in both groups, with 75.1% in the EFA and 81.5% in the CFA group. Most participants were from rural areas, 75.1% in the EFA and 67.0% in the CFA group. Marital status was nearly identical, with about 66% married in both groups. Educational levels were similar, with the majority holding a bachelor’s degree (82.0% in EFA, 79.3% in CFA). Employment types were also consistent, with most participants in contract positions (68.3% in EFA, 70.5% in CFA). Job titles varied slightly, with the EFA group having more primary level titles (54.6%) compared to CFA (49.8%), and the CFA group having more intermediate titles (44.1% vs. 39.0%). Night-shift work was common in both groups (66.3% in EFA, 68.3% in CFA). Most participants worked in tertiary hospitals (96.6% in EFA, 91.2% in CFA). Monthly income showed a balanced distribution across the categories. Lastly, the majority worked in departments other than emergency and critical care (89.8% in EFA, 88.1% in CFA).

The translation and revision of the RMRS-16

The original Chinese version of the RMRS-16 was systematically developed through a three-stage process: translation, back-translation, and cultural adaptation. Six experts were invited to critically evaluate the preliminary draft of the RMRS-16 in Chinese. This draft comprised 16 items, distributed across 4 dimensions: response to moral adversity, personal integrity, relational integrity, and moral efficacy. In the initial validation phase, 10 registered nurses were recruited to assess the comprehensibility and usability of the RMRS-16. Their unanimous feedback confirmed that the translation was easily understandable and questionnaire was straightforward to complete. Consequently, this feedback led to the formation of pre-test version of the translated RMRS-16, encompassing four dimensions and 16 items.

Content validity analysis

Six qualified experts participated in the content validity evaluation for the translated RMRS-16. The final results showed that the I-CVI values ranged from 0.833 to 1.000, while the S-CVI value was 0.906. As shown in Table 2, the results showed that the content validity of the translated RMRS-16 was sufficient.

Exploratory factor analysis

The first-round EFA obtained a KMO value of 0.756, and Bartlett’s spherical test got to statistical significance (χ2 = 1085.300; P < 0.001). After performing EFA, three factors with eigenvalues of more than 1 were extracted by principal component analysis, explaining 58.872% of the total variance. However, items 4 and 6 had the cross-loadings over 0.4 on two factors, we therefore firstly removed item 6 to perform the secondary-round EFA. The results revealed a KMO value of 0.752, and Bartlett’s spherical test was significant (χ2 = 969.375; P < 0.001). Three factors with eigenvalues of more than 1 were extracted from the EFA by principal component analysis, which explained 60.119% of the total variance. However, item 4 still had the cross-loading over 0.4 on two factor, we thus removed it to perform the third-round EFA. The results showed that KMO = 0.773 and Bartlett’s spherical test was statistically significant (χ2 = 870.797; P < 0.001). Three factors with eigenvalues of more than 1 were extracted by principal component analysis, which explained 61.684% of the total variance. The scree plot (as shown in Fig. 1) provides further support for the existence of the 3-factor model, as the downward trend of the curve diminishes after the third point. Each item exhibited a factor loading greater than 0.60, and there was no occurrence of double-factor loading (as shown in Table 4). A finalized 3-factor model comprising 12 items was obtained, as documented in supplementary file, with items 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 and 16 on the factor 1, items 13 and 14 on the factor 2, and items 1, 8, 12 and 15 on the factor 3.

Confirmatory factor analysis

CFA validated the 3-factor model, with results displayed in Fig. 2. Based on the correction index, four corrections were made to the initial model, namely e1 and e2, e1 and e3, e2 and e3, as well as e7 and e8. The final model fitting results were CMIN/DF = 2.697, GFI = 0.915, IFI = 0.906, RMSEA = 0.091 (90% CI: 0.072 to 0.111). These results suggested that the translated questionnaire exhibited an appropriate level of fit to the data.

Standardized three-factor model of the Chinese version of the revised RMRS (n = 432)

Criterion validity

We used the Chinese version of CD-RISC-10 and the MDT as the criterion scale to evaluate the criterion validity of the Chinese version of the RMRS-16. The results of correlation analysis showed that the Chinese version of RMRS-16 was significantly associated with CD-RISC-10 (P < 0.01), with a positive coefficient of 0.436. Meanwhile, the Chinese version of RMRS-16 was also significantly associated with MDT (P < 0.01), with a negative coefficient of -0.455.

Reliability analysis

Cronbach’s α value of the Chinese version of the RMRS-16 was 0.753. Cronbach’s α value of the three dimensions of the questionnaire ranged from 0.612 to 0.834. However, the composite reliability of this scale was 0.902, and the composite reliability of the three dimensions of the questionnaire were 0.811, 0.773, and 0.628, respectively.

Discussion

This study aims to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the psychometric properties of the revised RMRS using a convenient sample of 432 Chinese registered nurses. All findings indicate that the Chinese version of the revised RMRS, renamed Chi-RMRS-12, comprises three factors and demonstrates strong content and structural validity, as well as acceptable internal reliability. This version proves to be a reliable and effective tool for assessing moral resilience of registered nurses in Chinese cultural context.

The Chi-RMRS-12 was developed by translating the revised RMRS into Chinese and performing cross-cultural adaption using the modified Brislin’s translation process [42]. Two experts independently revised the initial translation based on their professional knowledge, clinical experience, and language preferences, resulting in the finalized translated version. To ensure equivalence, the back-translated version was sent to the original authors of the revised RMRS, who confirmed its full equivalence with the original version. The content validity of the translated questionnaire, assessed by six experts, showed I-CVI values ranging from 0.830 to 1.000 and an S-CVI of 0.906, indicating satisfactory content validity across all 16 items. Furthermore, item-scale correlation coefficients in the translated questionnaire ranged from 0.249 to 0.600, and Cronbach’s α coefficients if item deleted ranged from 0.719 to 0.753. After removing items 2 and 10, the remaining 14 items nearly met the recommended criteria, demonstrating that these items consistently measured moral resilience.

In EFA, structural validity was deemed satisfactory if the cumulative variance contribution of the extracted common factors exceeds 50% [43] and the item loadings on their respective factors are greater than 0.40 [44]. In this study, the results of the third-round EFA indicated that the three-factor structure derived from 12 items accounted for 61.684% of the total variance, with all item loadings exceeding 0.5. This suggests that the Chi-RMRS-12 gets robust construct validity, effectively measuring the moral resilience of Chinese registered nurses.

Factor 1: the ability to respond flexibly to moral adversity

Factor 1 comprises items 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 and 16. Items 3, 5, 7 and 9 belong to “response to moral adversity” in the original scale; however, items 11 and 16 are on the subscale namely “relational integrity”. Item 11 “In a situation of moral conflict, other people’s strong emotion easily distracts my concentration” and item 16 “I act in a way that undermines my value because of fear” indicate that one’s attitude to the decisions they made could be easily changed by external conditions. Actually, these two conditions consistently reflect whether one can response effectively to moral adversity. Therefore, we named factor 1 as “the ability to respond flexibly to moral adversity”. This factor reflects the nurses’ capacity to adapt their responses to moral challenges in a flexible manner, considering the dynamic nature of clinical settings and the need to balance various moral adversities.

Factor 2: moral efficacy

Factor 2 consists of items 1, 8, 12 and 15. Of which, items 1, 2 and 15 all reflect moral efficacy, while item 8 was assigned to subscale namely “personal integrity” in the original scale. Item 8 “I have the courage to take action even when others resist me” indicates a capacity of one successfully coping with moral adversity. In the Chinese cultural context, it more reflects one’s efficacy rather than maintaining a certain relationship. Therefore, factor 2 was named as “moral resilience”. This factor emphasizes the nurses’ sense of accomplishment and confidence in their ability to navigate moral challenges effectively, which is crucial for maintaining their professional integrity and well-being.

Factor 3: relational moral soundness

Factor 3 includes item 13 and 14, and these two items belong to the subscale namely “relational integrity” in the original scale. We therefore named this subscale as “relational moral soundness”. This factor highlights the importance of maintaining harmonious and ethical relationships within the healthcare team, which is deeply rooted in the collectivist values of Chinese culture. It reflects the nurses’ ability to uphold moral principals while fostering collaborative and supportive interpersonal dynamics.

Notably, in EFA, the “personal integrity” dimension in the original RMRS-16 was not retained in the Chi-RMRS-12, which may reflect culturally mediated differences in conceptualizing moral resilience. Despite the profound cultural transformations in China over the past forty years [45], traditional Chinese culture continues to significantly influence individual beliefs. The core elements of traditional Chinese culture, “Ren (benevolence)” and “Yi (righteousness)’, are deeply embedded in contemporary medical education [46]. In Confucian philosophy, “Ren” emphasizes compassionate interdependence, while “Yi” prescribes role-based obligations to maintain social order. Consequently, Chinese registered nurses may perceive moral resilience less as adherence to personal convictions and more as fulfilling collective responsibilities within hierarchical systems. For instance, qualitative studies report that Chinese registered nurses often describe moral resilience as “resolving conflicts without disrupting team cohesion” rather than “defending personal ethical boundaries” [22]. This cultural framework likely contributed to the poor psychometric performance of “personal integrity” items, as their individualistic framing conflicts with collectivist norms. The retained dimensions, the ability to respond flexibly to moral adversity, relational moral soundness, and moral efficacy, collectively emphasize adaptive navigation of systemic challenges rather than individual moral boundaries. This structural divergence underscores the necessity of culturally adapting scales: directly importing Western constructs risks construct bias, as evidenced by the Chi-RMRS-12’s exclusion of a dimension central to the original tool.

In addition, the results of CFA showed that except for item 1 which got a factor loading of 0.42, the remaining 11 items had factor loadings of higher than 0.50. After fitting the three-factor model to data, apart from RMSEA, other fit indices all met the recommended criteria. However, according to the study by Kenny et al. [47], it is more appropriate by referring 0.10 as the criteria rather 0.08 when the degree of freedom was small and sample size used for CFA. Generally, the results of model fitting showed that the current three-factor model is acceptable. However, the second-order factor structure reported in the original scale development study did not retain in this study [23], which was consistency with our previous validation study [22]. This result actually emphasizes the need to develop a new tool to assess moral resilience in Chinese context.

In this study, the CD-RISC-10 and MDT were selected to assess the criterion validity of the Chi-RMRS-12. The Chinese versions of CD-RISC-10 and MDT have demonstrated strong validity and are commonly used in China to measure general resilience and moral distress, respectively. Consequently, using the CD-RISC-10 and MDT as criterion validity indices is justified. Pearson correlation analysis revealed that all dimensions of the Chi-RMRS-12 as well as the overall scale, were significantly positively correlated with the Chinese version of CD-RISC-10 scale (r = 0.436; P < 0.01) and significantly negatively correlated with the Chinese MDT (r = -0.455; P < 0.01). These results indicate that the Chi-RMRS-12 effectively measures moral resilience of Chinese registered nurses. However, the total correlation coefficient between the Chi-RMRS-12 and the Chinese version of the CD-RISC-10 scale was 0.436, indicating a moderate correlation. This finding suggests that general resilience should not be equated with moral resilience, underscoring the significance of employing a specific scale to assess moral resilience rather than relying solely on a general measure of resilience.

The Cronbach’s α values for the overall Chi-RMRS-12 and its three subscales demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, suggesting that the scale is both coherent and homogeneous. Furthermore, the CR coefficient of the entire scale indicated excellent reliability, reinforcing its coherence and homogeneity. While it is important to acknowledge that Cronbach’s α values and CR coefficients are specific to the study’s participants sample and may not be universally applicable [48], the diverse departments of participants in this study likely enhances the reliability of the Chi-RMRS-12.

Limitations

This study had certain limitations. Firstly, while the sample size adhered to the minimum criteria specified in the COSMIN guideline and yielded satisfactory psychometric characteristics, a large sample size would enhance the representativeness of the findings. Secondly, data collection via an online platform may introduce response bias, potentially compromising the robustness of the results. Thirdly, although we thoroughly assessed the psychometric properties of the Chi-RMRS-12, we did not investigate the factors influencing the moral resilience of Chinese registered nurses. Addressing this gap will be a priority in our future research. Lastly, the interpretation of Chi-RMRS-12 scores remains incomplete. Specifically, categorical cut-off values have not been established. Future studies should focus on determining these categorical cut-off values and calculating a minimal clinically important difference score to optimize the scale’s utility in both research and clinical practice.

Conclusions

In conclusion, following translation and cultural adaption, the Chi-RMRS-12 has proven to be a valid and reliable instrument for assessing moral resilience of Chinese registered nurses. Consequently, the Chi-RMRS-12 is a valuable tool for measuring moral resilience within this demographic, offering nursing managers critical insights to develop targeted interventions aimed at enhancing moral resilience among registered nurses. Additionally, policymakers can use this tool to investigate the impact of moral resilience on professional burnout and turnover rates among clinical nurses, facilitating the creation of effective strategies to ensure workforce stability in the nursing sector.

Implications to clinical practice

The successful adaptation and validation of the revised RMRS for Chinese registered nurses have substantial implications for clinical practice. By providing a reliable and valid tool to measure moral resilience, healthcare institutions can better identify nurses who may be at risk of moral suffering due to ethical conflicts inherent in their roles. This scale enables nursing managers and educators to assess the moral resilience levels of their staff effectively and implement targeted interventions to bolster moral resilience. Such interventions could include tailored training programs, supportive policies, and resilience-building workshops designed to enhance coping strategies and ethical decision-making skills. Ultimately, by strengthening the moral resilience of nurses, healthcare providers can improve job satisfaction, reduce burnout, and ensure a higher quality of patient care. This tool also facilitates ongoing research into the factors contributing to moral resilience, helping to evolve practices that support a healthier work environment and promote the well-being of both nurses and their patients.

Implications to future research

The successful translation and cultural adaptation of the revised RMRS into Chinese, resulting in the Chi-RMRS-12, opens numerous avenues for future research on moral resilience among Chinese registered nurses. Future studies can build upon the established reliability and validity of this scale to explore deeper insights into the dynamics of moral resilience in varied nursing contexts across China. The relationship between moral resilience and other psychological constructs, such as job satisfaction, burnout, and professional commitment, deserves further exploration to understand how moral resilience influences broader occupational outcomes. Additionally, longitudinal studies could investigate how moral resilience evolves over a nurse’s career trajectory and in response to ongoing professional development and ethical training. Research can also extend to intervention studies, assessing the effectiveness of specific resilience-enhancing programs tailored to the unique cultural and organizational contexts of Chinese healthcare settings. Such studies will provide empirical evidence for targeted interventions, potentially enhancing both individual and organizational outcomes in healthcare.
