A Vanishing Lung Tumor in Congestive Heart Failure
Authors: Ruben David, Gidion Edwin, Baraka Alphonce, Happiness Kusima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhantom lung tumor is uncommon and reversible with diuretics, recognizing this phenomenon is crucial as it can avoid unwanted diagnostic procedures and costs.

Case Presentation

We present the case of a 56‐year‐old woman with a medical history of hypertension, obesity, and type 2 diabetes mellitus diagnosed 5 years ago. She was regularly attending outpatient follow‐ups, and both her blood pressure and blood glucose were generally well controlled, aside from complications such as peripheral neuropathy and retinopathy. She was admitted with progressively worsening shortness of breath, initially on minimal exertion and later at rest, accompanied by a nocturnal cough, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, palpitations, and bilateral lower limb swelling.

On examination, her blood pressure was 180/102 mmHg, pulse rate 101 beats per minute, respiratory rate 24 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation was 88% on room air. Clinical signs included elevated jugular venous pressure (13 cm H2O), bilateral pitting edema in the lower limbs, and basal crackles in both lungs. Echocardiography revealed diastolic dysfunction, left ventricular hypertrophy with a preserved ejection fraction of 67%. Chest X‐ray showed an enlarged cardiac silhouette with a prominent left heart border. There were bilateral peripheral‐based air‐space opacities, more on the left lung. There was a large, well‐defined opacity on the right lung (Figure 1). She was diagnosed with acute heart failure and started on appropriate treatment. After 6 days, her symptoms improved, and the chest X‐ray opacity resolved, confirming the diagnosis of a phantom tumor.

Discussion

Localized interlobar effusions in congestive heart failure, often referred to as “phantom” or “vanishing” lung tumors, are uncommon but well‐recognized clinical findings. These appear as well‐defined masses on imaging but are actually fluid collections within the interlobar fissures, typically associated with congestive heart failure [1]. The differential diagnosis for such findings includes a range of both transudative and exudative processes. Transudates may result from conditions like renal failure or cirrhosis, while exudates can be due to parapneumonic effusions, malignant pleural effusions, or benign asbestos‐related pleural disease. Other possibilities include fibrous tumors arising from the visceral pleura within the interlobar fissures [2].

The pathogenesis is thought to involve adhesions and obliteration of the pleural space near the fissure edges, often due to prior pleurisy. These structural changes hinder normal fluid drainage, allowing fluid to accumulate whenever transudation from the pulmonary vasculature exceeds the pleural resorption capacity. The tendency for phantom tumors to appear on the right side is best explained by the higher hydrostatic pressure found on that side in cases of congestive heart failure. This increased pressure leads to impaired venous and lymphatic drainage, resulting in fluid becoming trapped and localized [3]. Phantom tumors are fully reversible with diuretic therapy and management of the underlying cause. Recognizing this phenomenon is crucial, as it can help prevent unwanted diagnostic procedures and costs. Treatment focuses on diuretics and addressing the primary condition responsible for heart failure.
