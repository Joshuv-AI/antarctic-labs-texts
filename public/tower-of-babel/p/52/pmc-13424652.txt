Development and validation of an effectiveness evaluation scale for low-carbon construction technologies in building engineering based on multi-stage empirical evidence
Authors: Yikun Su, Zhongjie Sun, Junhao Liu, Zhizhe Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the lack of systematic evaluation instruments for assessing the “practical effectiveness” of low-carbon construction technologies (LCCTs) in the construction engineering sector, this study aims to develop a comprehensive evaluation scale. Based on the anomaly-seeking approach, a theoretical framework encompassing four dimensions—technology, organization, environment, and society—was developed through the extensive observation of research phenomena, the examination of underlying patterns within these phenomena, and their reinterpretation through alternative theoretical lenses. Subsequently, the scale was subjected to multi-stage empirical testing using content validity assessment, exploratory factor analysis, and confirmatory factor analysis. The results demonstrate that the proposed scale exhibits strong reliability, validity, and structural stability, and is capable of effectively characterizing the effectiveness of LCCTs in construction engineering. By approaching low-carbon construction from an “effectiveness” perspective, this study expands the scope of low-carbon construction research and provides quantitative tool support for project-level low-carbon performance diagnosis, optimization of management decision-making, and policy formulation.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of global carbon reduction targets, mitigating CO₂ emissions during the construction phase of building projects is of critical importance. The construction industry accounts for approximately 40% of global CO₂ emissions, with construction activities contributing about 13% of total societal emissions1,2. Owing to the high energy intensity and carbon sensitivity of material production, transportation, and on-site construction, the United Nations Environment Programme has emphasized the effective implementation of low-carbon construction practices through the efficient use of green materials and equipment3.

Existing research on low-carbon construction has primarily focused on life-cycle carbon accounting and policy-oriented analysis. Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) is widely applied to quantify emissions across material production, transportation, construction, equipment operation, maintenance, and demolition, and to support low-carbon management planning4,5. LCA-based methods perform well in projects with standardized structures and highly repetitive processes6, but exhibit clear limitations when evaluating low-carbon construction technologies in projects characterized by non-standard structures and highly discrete construction activities7.

Essentially, as an ex-post evaluation tool, LCA primarily focuses on accounting for carbon emission outcomes within predefined system boundaries, rather than explaining how these outcomes are generated during the construction process. As a result, the construction phase is often treated as a “black box” in the analysis, where the intermediate processes linking inputs—such as materials and energy—to carbon emissions are significantly simplified or even overlooked. However, during the construction stage of building projects, which is highly discrete and strongly context-dependent, critical process variables—such as construction organization methods, resource allocation efficiency, multi-stakeholder coordination, and on-site execution deviations—are difficult to capture effectively using LCA.

The construction phase is marked by fragmented work processes, diverse stakeholders, decentralized decision-making, and strong reliance on on-site execution8,9. Effective low-carbon construction therefore requires coordinated optimization of equipment operation, material selection, and construction schemes among all participants, thereby enhancing corporate sustainability and the performance of low-carbon construction technologies (LCCTs). To address construction-phase emissions, some studies have embedded sustainability indicators into project management tools, such as integrating waste management strategies or carbon metrics into Earned Value Management (EVM) frameworks to assess implementation performance10,11. Meanwhile, developed countries and regions have promoted LCCT diffusion through stringent emission targets, technological innovation, and demonstration projects12. In building engineering, LCCTs refer to technologies that reduce fossil energy consumption and CO₂ emissions during the materialization phase through technological innovation, management optimization, and material advancement, while supporting both project-level and organizational sustainability. Recent studies further suggest that LCCTs should address not only emission reduction and energy efficiency, but also long-term goals related to social expectations, environmental impacts, and resource circularity13–18.

Despite these advances, the effectiveness of LCCTs in real construction contexts has received limited systematic evaluation. In practice, project managers adopt low-carbon materials, improve material turnover, and optimize transportation and equipment allocation to reduce emissions19. However, material losses, low turnover efficiency, and execution uncertainties often lead to higher-than-expected emissions, raising concerns about LCCTs’ real-world effectiveness. Although standards and codes provide guidance for low-carbon construction at the subproject level20, implementation outcomes are strongly influenced by organizational structures, construction environments, and broader social contexts21, and existing studies offer few concrete measures for evaluating such effectiveness.

Based on the above considerations, it is essential to move beyond the existing perspectives of “carbon accounting” and “technology adoption” by introducing “effectiveness” as a new analytical lens. In this study, the effectiveness of LCCTs is preliminarily defined as the capability to consistently achieve expected emission reduction and overall performance goals within specific construction contexts, through the integrated functioning of multiple mechanisms, including technological implementation, organizational coordination, and resource allocation. This concept not only focuses on whether the final outcomes are achieved, but also emphasizes the realization process of these outcomes, as well as their stability and sustainability within complex project environments.

Although LCCTs are widely implemented under regulatory and sustainability pressures, their adoption does not necessarily guarantee effective implementation or the anticipated emission reduction outcomes. In the absence of a systematic evaluation of LCCT effectiveness, the assessment of construction project success may become inaccurate, potentially leading to resource misallocation in low-carbon construction practices and hindering the timely achievement of carbon peaking in the construction industry. Therefore, the “anomaly-seeking” approach should be regarded as a deliberate methodological choice to investigate the discrepancies between the expected outcomes and actual performance of LCCTs. Considering this, this study aims to develop a scientific and systematic evaluation scale for assessing the effectiveness of LCCTs in construction projects. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: the next section elaborates on the evaluation mechanism of LCCT effectiveness based on the anomaly-seeking approach; Chap. 3 introduces the methodology for scale development; Chap. 4 presents the empirical results; and Chaps. 5 and 6 provide discussion and conclusions, respectively.

Mechanism analysis of LCCT effectiveness evaluation based on the anomaly-seeking research approach

The research framework of this study is illustrated in Fig. 1. Anomaly-seeking research is a methodological approach that identifies systematic deviations between observed practices and established theories or dominant expectations and interprets these anomalies through alternative theoretical lenses. When real-world outcomes diverge from rational planning assumptions or idealized technological expectations, such anomalies expose explanatory gaps in existing frameworks and provide a basis for theoretical innovation9. This approach has been widely applied in project management, infrastructure governance, and sustainable development research to explain unexpected outcomes in complex engineering contexts10. Accordingly, this study adopts an anomaly-seeking perspective to re-examine the evaluation logic of LCCTs in building engineering based on construction-phase practices, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for developing an effectiveness evaluation framework for LCCTs.

Observing widespread phenomena in the effectiveness of low-carbon construction technologies in building engineering

In building engineering, LCCTs are widely viewed as key instruments for achieving construction-phase emission reduction targets. However, in practice, their emission reduction outcomes are often unstable and unpredictable. This mismatch—between anticipated technological effectiveness and uncertain practical results—deviates from dominant theoretical expectations. Existing research and engineering practice commonly assume that adopting low-carbon materials, optimizing construction methods, improving material turnover, and rationally allocating equipment can effectively reduce construction-phase emissions17, implying that LCCTs should yield measurable and verifiable benefits. Nevertheless, empirical evidence shows that LCCT implementation outcomes during construction are highly variable. Material overconsumption, high loss rates, and low turnover efficiency frequently weaken expected emission reductions, while organizational capacity, on-site environmental conditions, and multi-stakeholder coordination further constrain execution relative to design-stage assumptions22. Importantly, low-carbon construction does not fail uniformly: some projects achieve substantial reductions under complex conditions, whereas others do not. Such disparities cannot be explained solely by whether LCCTs are adopted, indicating that their effectiveness is context-dependent and shaped by implementation processes rather than technology alone. These observations point to a core anomaly—that the presence of LCCTs does not necessarily guarantee their effectiveness—highlighting the need to critically re-examine existing LCCT evaluation logics.

Examining the phenomena behind the effectiveness of low-carbon construction technologies in building engineering

The construction phase is the core of the building “materialization phase,” with carbon emissions mainly arising from the embodied carbon of construction materials and the direct energy consumption of machinery and temporary facilities23–25. Most existing studies rely on Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) for carbon accounting, focusing on emission outcomes rather than the complex, discrete, and site-dependent processes that characterize construction activities5. As a result, LCA has limited ability to reveal the actual contribution of LCCTs in projects involving non-standard structures or highly uncertain construction conditions.

To address this limitation, some studies have embedded low-carbon indicators into project management tools, such as incorporating carbon-related metrics into Earned Value Management (EVM), to evaluate construction-phase performance. For instance, construction waste management performance has been integrated into EVM to monitor environmental outcomes alongside cost and schedule11, and “green” indicators have been added to cost–schedule frameworks to enable real-time carbon monitoring in prefabricated buildings26. However, despite their emphasis on process control, these approaches remain anchored in traditional cost- and schedule-oriented objectives, treating low-carbon indicators as supplementary variables. Consequently, they are limited in their ability to systematically capture the comprehensive effects of LCCTs under complex construction conditions.

Moreover, evaluation approaches based on standards and policies primarily focus on compliance with low-carbon requirements, yet compliance does not necessarily imply effectiveness. Substantial differences in organizational capacity, construction environments, and social conditions across projects mean that even when identical regulations are met, LCCT implementation outcomes can vary significantly27. Accordingly, existing evaluation logics struggle to explain the variability and uncertainty observed in LCCT effectiveness during the construction phase.

Re-examining the effectiveness of low-carbon construction in building engineering through other theoretical lenses

When existing theories fail to account for practical outcomes, it is necessary to re-examine the core concepts of the research object9,10. In low-carbon construction research, this requires shifting the focus from emission outcomes alone to a systematic evaluation of LCCT effectiveness. This study defines LCCTs effectiveness not simply as statistically observed emission reductions, but as the capability of LCCTs to consistently achieve expected reduction targets within specific construction contexts through mechanisms such as resource allocation, organizational coordination, and process execution. This process-oriented and context-dependent conceptualization distinguishes effectiveness from traditional outcome-based evaluations. During the construction phase, LCCTs performance is jointly influenced by construction organization modes, material and equipment management, stakeholder coordination, and on-site environmental constraints8,9. Accordingly, LCCTs effectiveness must be evaluated from a systemic perspective rather than through single technologies or isolated indicators.

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) explains LCCT effectiveness through perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, reflecting stakeholders’ expectations of performance improvement and the associated learning and usage costs28. Owing to its parsimony, TAM has been widely applied in construction technology research, including studies on smart construction and blockchain technologies, which highlight the importance of integrating technological, organizational, and environmental dimensions when evaluating low-carbon construction technologies29–31.

In contrast, innovation resistance theory focuses on why technologies fail to realize expected effects, attributing resistance to functional, financial, and social risks, perceived complexity, and entrenched habits32. In construction projects characterized by multiple stakeholders, heterogeneous objectives, and risks such as greenwashing and carbon fraud9, trust becomes a critical determinant of LCCT effectiveness. Research based on trust transfer theory shows that sponsorship and early-stage technical feasibility assessments can enhance trust in innovative technologies, thereby reducing adoption barriers and improving implementation outcomes33,34.

From an organizational perspective, institutional theory emphasizes that LCCT effectiveness is shaped by interactions between organizations and their external environments. Empirical studies indicate that normative and coercive pressures play key roles in promoting effective LCCT implementation35,36. Through ongoing interaction, competition, and collaboration during construction, stakeholders form cooperative norms and reshape behavioral patterns, ultimately influencing how LCCTs are applied and how effective they become.

In summary, based on the anomaly-seeking approach, a discrepancy is observed in real engineering contexts between the expected emission reduction targets and the actual implementation outcomes of low-carbon construction technologies (LCCTs). Existing studies and engineering practices generally suggest that LCCTs can achieve emission reduction goals during the construction phase through material optimization, process improvements, and resource allocation. However, in practical applications, their emission reduction performance demonstrates significant instability and strong context dependency. On the one hand, factors such as material waste on construction sites, insufficient management capacity, and difficulties in multi-stakeholder coordination can undermine the expected effectiveness of these technologies. On the other hand, even when the same technologies are adopted across different projects, the resulting emission reductions may vary substantially. This indicates that the effectiveness of LCCTs is not determined solely by the technologies themselves but is jointly influenced by the implementation environment and underlying process mechanisms.

Theoretical framework for the effectiveness of low-carbon construction technologies in building engineering

Based on the anomaly-seeking approach, we identify how engineering practice and existing research respond to the effectiveness of LCCTs in construction projects by examining widespread empirical phenomena, probing into underlying patterns within these phenomena, and reinterpreting them through alternative theoretical lenses. Recognizing that no single theory or scale can sufficiently capture LCCTs effectiveness, the study integrates insights from multiple theories to provide a broader and more robust evaluative perspective9. Accordingly, key elements from technology acceptance theory, institutional theory, and innovation resistance theory are synthesized into four evaluation dimensions—technological, organizational, environmental, and social. The resulting theoretical framework is illustrated in Fig. 2, and the preliminary items of the LCCTs effectiveness evaluation scale are presented in Table 1. These dimensions and measurement items are not derived from any single existing scale; rather, they are synthesized from the key influencing mechanisms revealed through the identification and analysis of anomalous phenomena.

Technological Dimension: LCCTs effectiveness depends not only on energy-saving and emission-reduction performance and cost recovery potential, but also on technological complexity, risk controllability, and compatibility with existing construction systems. By improving construction efficiency and reducing energy consumption and environmental impacts, LCCTs generate both economic and sustainability value for projects37. Technologies such as automated equipment, intelligent monitoring, and green construction management systems can reduce material waste and emissions while improving quality, thereby strengthening competitive advantage under regulatory constraints36,38–40. Although LCCTs often require high initial investment, lifecycle benefits can be realized through reduced energy use, operating costs, and rework41–43. However, complexity, implementation risks, multi-device and multi-stakeholder coordination, and skill gaps may undermine effectiveness39,44. Thus, technological effectiveness also relies on process optimization, training, risk management, and system compatibility, with higher compatibility significantly reducing costs while safeguarding schedule and quality45,46.

Organizational Dimension: LCCTs effectiveness is strongly shaped by organizational culture, resource availability, managerial commitment, and technical personnel capability. Open, innovation-oriented cultures enhance acceptance of technological change and knowledge sharing, facilitating technology integration42,47. Adequate financial, human, and technical resources support equipment investment, system upgrades, and training, improving implementation stability37,38,43. Strong top-management support provides institutional assurance through clear decision-making and resource allocation, while increasing employee engagement41,48. As direct implementers, the competence and attitudes of technical staff critically influence LCCTs performance in practice39,48.

Environmental Dimension: Market competition and policy environments exert external incentives and constraints on LCCTs effectiveness. Early adoption by competitors creates efficiency and reputational pressures that accelerate technology diffusion42. Government interventions—such as subsidies, tax incentives, technical guidance, and regulatory standards—can lower adoption costs and risks49. Client demand and partner attitudes further shape implementation outcomes, with proactive collaboration enhancing technology integration and overall project performance50. Positive engagement from suppliers, contractors, and designers supports material provision, technical assistance, and knowledge sharing, while government agencies facilitate implementation through policy oversight and incentives49,51.

Social Dimension: A project’s social responsibility awareness and sustainability orientation determine whether LCCTs can deliver long-term comprehensive performance while meeting societal expectations. Social responsibility in building engineering requires balancing environmental protection, resource efficiency, and construction quality. Projects with stronger social responsibility commitments are more likely to adopt LCCTs systematically and enhance effectiveness through training and standardized management52. The broader benefits of LCCTs—such as pollution reduction, resource optimization, and improved safety—strengthen public trust, industry recognition, and project reputation49. From a sustainable development perspective, LCCTs function not only as emission-reduction tools but also as pathways for coordinated economic, social, and environmental development, and therefore require evaluation from a long-term, holistic performance perspective53.

It should be noted that although the “technology–organization–environment–society (TOES)” four-dimensional analytical framework proposed in this study is structurally similar to the existing technology–organization–environment (TOE) framework, its core extension lies in the independent introduction and theoretical elaboration of the “social dimension.” The “social dimension” is developed based on theories such as social license and corporate social responsibility. The theory of social license emphasizes that, beyond formal institutional requirements, enterprises or projects must obtain continuous recognition and support from the public, communities, and stakeholders. Corporate social responsibility theory further suggests that corporate behavior should respond to societal expectations and public values, to achieve a balance between economic objectives and social responsibilities. On this basis, the effectiveness of LCCT implementation is not only influenced by policy pressure or market conditions (the environmental dimension), but also by factors such as public perception, social oversight, industry norm recognition, and socially oriented value systems.

From the perspective of conceptual boundaries, the “social dimension” primarily captures non-institutionalized and non-coercive social cognition and value-based constraints, with its core focusing on the level of acceptance and support for LCCTs among social actors (such as the public, industry groups, and informal networks). In contrast, the “environmental dimension” emphasizes institutionalized pressures and external constraints, including formal institutional factors such as policies and regulations, market mechanisms, and industry standards. The “organizational dimension,” on the other hand, focuses on internal resources, capabilities, and management mechanisms within enterprises. Therefore, the three dimensions differ significantly in their mechanisms of action and levels of analysis.

Research methods and procedures

Pilot study and formal survey on the effectiveness of low-carbon construction technologies

To develop and systematically evaluate an LCCT effectiveness scale for building engineering, this study employed a pilot study and a formal survey grounded in a post-positivist worldview. Post-positivism acknowledges the existence of objective reality while recognizing that researcher bias cannot be fully eliminated and that scientific knowledge can only approximate, rather than fully capture, reality. Accordingly, subjectivity is retained in item development, dimension specification, and data interpretation. The pilot study comprised content validity assessment and exploratory factor analysis, followed by a formal survey using confirmatory factor analysis. Content validity assessment examined item relevance and clarity in the absence of strong a priori knowledge, while exploratory factor analysis identified the latent factor structure and tested theoretically proposed construct relationships. Confirmatory factor analysis was then used to evaluate model fit and establish the overall validity of the scale based on a larger sample. All participants were fully informed of the detailed research objectives and procedures, and each provided informed consent to take part in the study.

Pilot study on LCCT effectiveness and analysis of results

Content validity assessment and analysis of results

Content validity analysis is a fundamental step in scale development, assessing the consistency between measurement items and theoretical constructs through expert evaluation within a limited sample. In the absence of strong a priori knowledge on LCCTs effectiveness evaluation in building engineering, this approach enables a systematic examination of item relevance and wording accuracy, ensuring that the scale faithfully represents the theoretical structure of LCCTs effectiveness and enhancing the study’s rigor. The use of the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) further improves the transparency, objectivity, and replicability of the evaluation process54. In this study, purposive sampling was used to select six experts from academia and industry with extensive research or practical experience in low-carbon construction or engineering management to conduct the content validity assessment. This number satisfies general requirements for content validity testing54. All experts had relevant professional backgrounds, more than five years of experience, and participated in multiple rounds of feedback. The assessment combined questionnaire evaluation with semi-structured interviews. Experts first rated the relevance of each item to its corresponding dimension and then assessed item clarity and comprehensibility, using a four-point scale (1 = not relevant at all, 4 = highly relevant). Based on these evaluations, items were refined through interviews to ensure strong content validity and practical operability. The assessment followed standard content validity procedures, with calculation methods and detailed results reported in Appendix 155.

The combined relevance and clarity analysis indicates strong overall content validity. Most items achieved high agreement, with I-CVI values generally equal to 1, reflecting clear wording and well-defined constructs. The S-CVI/Ave and S-CVI/UA values were 0.9688 and 0.875, respectively, both exceeding recommended thresholds55, further confirming the scale’s content representativeness.

Among the measurement items, only EA and PE exhibited relatively lower levels of consistency, indicating some divergence in expert perceptions regarding their wording. Based on feedback from the semi-structured interviews, experts generally noted that the original wording of EA was somewhat broad and failed to clearly distinguish between “attitudinal agreement” and “actual behavioral intention.” They suggested that the item should be refined to focus more specifically on employees’ willingness to learn and use LCCTs, to better reflect their level of engagement in construction contexts. Regarding the PE item, some experts pointed out that the original description of the “policy environment” was overly general and difficult to directly relate to project-level perceptions. They recommended emphasizing the direct facilitating effect of policies on the adoption of LCCTs at the project level. Based on these suggestions, the EA item was revised to “Project employees are willing to learn and use LCCTs in their work,” and the PE item was revised to “Current policies have a clear facilitating effect on the adoption of LCCTs in this project.” These revisions enhance the specificity, clarity, and alignment of the items with their underlying constructs.

Exploratory factor analysis and analysis of results

Following confirmation of item relevance and clarity through content validity assessment, an Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted to identify item clustering and uncover the underlying factor structure. In the pilot study, 800 online questionnaires were distributed to managerial personnel from developers, supervision firms, construction contractors, material suppliers, equipment suppliers, and labor service providers. To avoid duplicate responses and interference from invalid samples, IP restrictions were implemented, and reasonable time thresholds for questionnaire completion were set to eliminate clearly abnormal responses.

The effectiveness of LCCTs is not merely a single technical outcome, but rather a comprehensive manifestation of the implementation process within complex construction contexts, shaped by multi-stakeholder collaboration, resource allocation, and organizational interactions. Relying on the perspective of a single organization or a single role would make it difficult to fully capture the actual performance of LCCTs in real-world projects. By integrating multiple stakeholder perspectives, it becomes possible to cover key decision-making, management, and execution stages across the entire lifecycle of construction projects. Accordingly, the respondent selection criteria are as follows: (1) at least five years of on-site construction experience; and (2) holding at least a middle-management position or serving as a project manager within their organization.

Item importance was assessed using a five-point Likert scale (1 = very unimportant, 5 = very important)56. Table 2 summarizes the characteristics of the pilot study respondents. All participants met the survey criteria and had experience across different project types, enabling informed judgments. A total of 628 valid questionnaires were collected, yielding an effective response rate of 73%, which is considered adequate for robust analysis in building engineering research9.

The EFA results indicate sufficient intercorrelations among variables. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value was 0.937, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p = 0), confirming the scale’s suitability for factor analysis. LCCTs effectiveness in building engineering was structured into four dimensions consistent with the theoretical framework, jointly explaining 71.75% of the total variance. All item factor loadings ranged from 0.699 to 0.744 and were evenly distributed across the four hypothesized dimensions. The scale demonstrated high internal consistency and reliability. The overall Cronbach’s α was 0.926, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.757. Corrected item–total correlation (CITC) values ranged from 0.601 to 0.680, while Cronbach’s α values after item deletion ranged from 0.920 to 0.922. Overall, the EFA results indicate strong reliability and validity of the scale, providing preliminary support for the theoretical framework.

Formal survey on LCCT effectiveness and analysis of results

The formal survey was conducted following the pilot study, as the results of the content validity assessment and exploratory factor analysis met theoretical expectations. Using the same survey standards, 1,200 questionnaires were distributed and 1,069 valid responses were collected, yielding an effective response rate of 89.08%, which is sufficient for rigorous analysis9. Respondent characteristics are summarized in Table 3. Data skewness was within ± 1 and kurtosis ranged from − 1.5 to 1, indicating minor bias but overall suitability for analysis. The overall Cronbach’s α was 0.924 and the KMO value was 0.935, demonstrating excellent reliability and validity. Cronbach’s α values for individual dimensions ranged from 0.863 to 0.873, all exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.7. Pearson correlation analysis showed that all items were positively correlated and statistically significant (p < 0.05**). Discriminant validity was confirmed by Heterotrait–Monotrait ratio (HTMT) values ranging from 0.597 to 0.656, below the threshold of 0.85 (Fig. 3). The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values ranged from 0.706 to 0.723, and Composite Reliability (CR) values ranged from 0.88 to 0.89, exceeding recommended thresholds. Overall and dimension-specific goodness-of-fit indices (Table 4) met accepted criteria, indicating that the scale exhibits strong structural validity.

Considering the post-positivist worldview, which acknowledges subjectivity in the research process, it is necessary to further examine the applicability and robustness of the scale to avoid potential influences of respondents’ characteristics on the different dimensions of the scale. This analysis aims to identify potential differences in cognitive evaluations across different groups. Accordingly, Partial Least Squares (PLS) analysis was employed to test the relationships between respondent characteristics and the scale dimensions. Collinearity values ranged from 1 to 2.2, indicating no serious multicollinearity concerns. The PLS results are shown in Fig. 4. The R² values for the four dimensions ranged from 0.025 to 0.054, all well below the thresholds of 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 that represent small, medium, and large effects, respectively9. These results indicate that respondent characteristics do not exert a substantive influence on the measurement outcomes. Under the post-positivist paradigm, the developed scale demonstrates sufficient applicability and robustness for measuring the effectiveness of LCCTs in construction projects.

To examine the stability and statistical significance of the path coefficients in the structural model, the Bootstrapping method with 5,000 resamples was employed to further investigate and validate the relationship between respondents’ characteristics and the effectiveness of LCCTs. This approach involves repeated sampling with replacement from the original dataset to construct the empirical distribution of the path coefficients, thereby enabling the estimation of standard errors and the assessment of their statistical significance. The results, presented in Table 5, show that respondent type and construction experience are significantly associated with all dimensions of LCCTs effectiveness, while job position is significantly related to the technological and social dimensions. Construction experience serves as a proxy for exposure and risk accumulation, differentiating emphasis on operability, risk–cost trade-offs, and reliance on managerial guidance. Variations by hierarchical position further demonstrate the scale’s ability to distinguish between macro-level institutional logic and micro-level on-site operational logic. The scale captures the core concerns of diverse project stakeholders regarding low-carbon construction technologies. Stable path significance under PLS and bootstrapping confirms the scale’s measurement accuracy and the soundness of the model specification. Overall, the scale exhibits stable and consistent measurement properties across respondent groups, with strong professional discriminative power and adaptability to engineering contexts.

Discussion

Aiming to provide a scientific evaluation of LCCTs effectiveness in building engineering, this study constructs a theoretical framework based on the anomaly-seeking research approach and validates it through a three-stage empirical process involving a pilot study and a formal survey, resulting in a robust and reliable evaluation scale. The key findings are summarized as follows:

Bridging a critical research gap. Existing studies on low-carbon construction have largely focused on carbon emission accounting or material development, while lacking operable tools to assess whether LCCTs are effective in real project contexts. Traditional life-cycle- or embodied-carbon-based approaches are well suited to explaining “when emissions occur” and “how they are calculated”, but are less capable of revealing “why technologies succeed or fail within project organizations.” By shifting the research focus toward effectiveness evaluation, this study highlights cross-scale interactions among technological, organizational, environmental, and social dimensions, thereby addressing the absence of effectiveness-oriented measurement approaches in prior research23.

Advancing a cross-scale effectiveness perspective. This study demonstrates that LCCTs effectiveness is jointly shaped by technological, organizational, environmental, and social factors, which constitutes its primary theoretical contribution. Integrating insights from multiple theories, the proposed scale extends the concept of LCCTs “acceptance” to effectiveness, defined as the stable realization of performance under combined schedule–cost–carbon constraints. The resulting framework aligns with the Technology–Organization–Environment (TOE) model and supports the inclusion of a social dimension in construction management research58. The “social dimension” primarily characterizes non-institutionalized and non-mandatory social cognition and value-based constraints. Its underlying mechanism is grounded in legitimacy perception and normative internalization, with the core emphasis placed on the acceptance and support of LCCTs by social actors, such as the public, industry groups, and informal networks. This refinement of the dimension helps explain why the practical effectiveness of LCCTs may still vary significantly across different projects or enterprises, even under similar policy environments and organizational conditions. Factors such as the degree of social license obtained, the level of internalization of social responsibility discourse, and the intensity of social supervision can generate independent moderating and driving effects beyond environmental and organizational factors, thereby influencing the ultimate realization of LCCT effectiveness.

Capturing multi-stakeholder differences. The developed scale reliably identifies differentiated focal concerns among multiple stakeholders, enhancing its external validity in multi-actor construction systems. Although respondent characteristics explain limited variance in dimension scores, PLS and 5,000-iteration bootstrapping analyses reveal stable and significant path relationships. These results indicate that the scale captures genuine differences in technological, economic, organizational, and social concerns arising from variations in responsibilities, authority, and information access. Further analysis shows that construction experience reflects differences in practical cognition of operability, risk, and cost, while job position corresponds to distinct cognitive scales for evaluating technological effectiveness. Accordingly, the scale distinguishes between macro-level institutional perspectives and micro-level on-site construction perspectives, making it more consistent with cross-organizational project governance and supporting future measurement invariance and cross-group comparison studies59. However, it should be noted that the scale primarily measures the favorable conditions and contextual readiness for LCCT effectiveness, rather than directly quantifying the final carbon reduction performance.

Integrating adoption and resistance logics. By synthesizing multiple theories, this study forms a coherent theoretical loop linking technology adoption and diffusion with resistance and risk. It distinguishes the acceptance logic of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) from the resistance logic of innovation resistance theory, incorporating perceived risk, complexity, habits, and trust into the explanation of LCCT effectiveness. Institutional theory is further employed to account for coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures that externally shape technological outcomes. This integrated perspective explains why low-carbon construction, often adopted under legitimacy and compliance pressures rather than efficiency considerations, may be implemented without achieving expected effectiveness, thereby resolving the paradox of “adopted but not necessarily effective” technologies60.

Providing a practical governance tool. The study delivers a quantitative instrument for project-level diagnosis, improvement, and benchmarking of low-carbon construction practices, addressing a key governance gap in both practice and policy. While standards and codes specify requirements, technological effectiveness also depends on ex ante planning, process adaptation, and cross-scale contextual factors. The absence of systematic evaluation risks resource misallocation and delays progress toward carbon peaking. The proposed scale enables the identification of weaknesses across technological, organizational, environmental, and social dimensions and supports dynamic improvement. Compared with case-based LCA or single-technology carbon reduction analyses, this tool is better suited for integration into enterprise low-carbon management systems and project governance processes, offering a common measurement language for industry benchmarking, policy incentives, and third-party evaluations23.

The “effectiveness” of LCCT defined in this study, although conceptualized as the “ability to consistently achieve emission reduction goals within specific construction contexts,” is not directly equivalent, at the empirical measurement level, to objective performance outcomes (e.g., actual emission reductions or resource savings). Instead, it focuses on the “realization potential and underlying mechanisms” through which technologies achieve expected outcomes in complex construction contexts. Existing studies indicate that in highly context-dependent domains such as construction, the actual performance of technologies is jointly influenced by multiple factors, including project conditions, construction organization, and external environments. As a result, objective indicators at the level of a single project are often insufficient to stably reflect the inherent effectiveness of a technology. On the other hand, whether a technology can be effectively implemented largely depends on its acceptance, level of support, and resource alignment within and across organizational contexts. In this study, cognitive indicators related to technology acceptance (e.g., “whether it is accepted” and “whether it is supported”) are incorporated into the measurement, and are regarded as key prerequisites and pathways for LCCTs to achieve their expected emission reduction outcomes. These subjective cognitive variables reflect the conditional capabilities underlying the realization of technology effectiveness.

Although this study develops and validates an effectiveness evaluation scale for low-carbon construction technologies (LCCTs) in building projects through a multi-stage empirical approach, several limitations remain that warrant further exploration in future research. First, the data used in this study are primarily derived from questionnaire surveys. Although the sample covers multiple types of stakeholders, it still relies on perception-based subjective evaluations, which may be influenced by respondents’ cognitive biases. Future studies could incorporate actual project operation data or process monitoring data to cross-validate the scale results. Second, this study focuses on the construction phase of building projects as the primary context. The external applicability of the proposed effectiveness evaluation framework to other types of infrastructure or different national institutional environments requires further examination. Variations in policy constraints, market mechanisms, and organizational modes across regions may influence the realization mechanisms of LCCT effectiveness. Finally, this study conceptualizes LCCT effectiveness across four dimensions—technology, organization, environment, and society. However, given the high complexity of construction practices, there may still be additional influencing factors that have not been fully captured (such as dynamic decision-making processes or unexpected risk events), which should be further explored in future research.

Conclusion

Addressing the lack of systematic evaluation of the real-world effectiveness of LCCTs in building engineering, this study focuses on their performance in actual construction contexts and develops a scientific and operable evaluation tool. While existing research has largely emphasized carbon accounting or the mitigation potential of individual technologies, it has struggled to explain effectiveness disparities in practice. Accordingly, this study establishes a four-dimensional evaluation framework encompassing technological, organizational, environmental, and social aspects. Using a multi-stage empirical approach—including content validity assessment, exploratory factor analysis, and confirmatory factor analysis—an evaluation scale is developed and validated. The results confirm that the scale demonstrates strong reliability and validity and can robustly capture the comprehensive effectiveness of LCCTs in multi-stakeholder settings. Theoretically, the study shifts the focus of low-carbon construction research from emission reduction outcomes to technological effectiveness, elucidating the joint influence of technical, organizational, environmental, and social factors. Practically, the scale supports project-level performance diagnosis and benchmarking, offering quantitative evidence for management decision-making, enterprise low-carbon strategies, and policy design, thereby contributing to high-quality, low-carbon development in the construction industry. Future research could incorporate actual project operation data, process monitoring data, as well as characteristics of different facility types or national institutional environments to cross-validate the scale results. In addition, more potential influencing factors could be further included in subsequent studies. The findings are primarily based on the Chinese construction context; future cross‑cultural validations are needed to generalize the scale to other institutional environments.
