Strain-enhanced stretchable molecular ferroelectric acoustic fibers
Authors: Lei Liu, Shengxin Xiang, Qiongfeng Shi, Shengshun Duan, Xiao Wei, Gang Yu, Zhishui Chen, Huiyun Zhang, Jianlong Hong, Tong Zheng, Yu-an Xiong, Yu-Meng You, Jun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMolecular ferroelectrics—hybrid organic-inorganic architectures that exhibit piezoelectric coefficients comparable to classic oxide ceramics while offering rich compositional and structural tunability—have emerged as promising materials for human-interfaced high-frequency acoustic sensing. Nevertheless, the inherent brittleness and relatively high modulus hinder their practical deployment on dynamically deformable interfaces. Here, we report stretchable molecular ferroelectric acoustic fibers fabricated by incorporating molecular ferroelectric trimethylchloromethyl ammonium trichlorocadmium (TMCM-CdCl3) crystals into flexible thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) via electrospinning. Intermolecular interactions between TMCM-CdCl3 and TPU, along with the interlocked layered architecture between electrodes and fibers, endow the fiber acoustic sensor with high stretchability (tensile strain >100%), high force sensing sensitivity (4.36 V/kPa), and wide-frequency acoustic sensing (30–5000 Hz). Crucially, a strain-induced sensitivity enhancement effect is proposed and verified, where the acoustic sensitivity of the sensor increases from 15.03 mV/dB to 30.16 mV/dB under strain. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the acoustic fiber can maintain a high speech recognition accuracy (97.05%) even for mixed speech data obtained using the acoustic fiber in original and 20% tensile strain states. This work establishes a route for acoustic sensing at dynamic deformable interface and advances human-interfaced acoustic applications such as robotic interaction, biomedical engineering, and virtual reality.Subject terms: Ferroelectrics and multiferroics, Sensors and biosensors, Polymer synthesis

Introduction

Sound is ubiquitous in both natural phenomena and human activities, serving as a fundamental medium for human perception and environmental interaction. Flexible acoustic sensors emulate this biological function by converting ambient acoustic information into quantifiable electrical signals1–3. Compared with conventional rigid devices, their intrinsic flexibility enables both conformal skin attachment and adaptability to dynamically curved surfaces4. These devices have emerged as a focal point of research due to their transformative potential in robotic interaction5,6, biomedicine7,8, and virtual reality applications9,10.

With advances in materials science and sensing technologies, various flexible acoustic sensors based on electromagnetic11, piezoresistive12, piezocapacitive13, optical14, triboelectric5, and piezoelectric15 mechanisms have been explored for acoustic perception and intelligent applications. For instance, Yan et al. utilized barium titanate (BTO)/poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-trifluoroethylene) piezoelectric fibers woven into a conformable acoustic fabric16. This smart fabric enables acoustic pulse direction detection and bidirectional acoustic communication. As applications evolve from requiring simple, flexible scenarios to those demanding integration with complex, dynamically deforming surfaces or environments (e.g., human body interfaces, typically exhibiting >10% strain), the development of stretchable acoustic sensors has therefore become imperative17. However, due to fundamental limitations in device architecture and working principles, existing acoustic sensors (such as piezoresistive and piezocapacitive types) often require complex structural designs to achieve real-time acoustic sensing under dynamic strain. In contrast, piezoelectric acoustic sensors rely on thin-film vibrations induced by acoustic waves for sound detection. This simple yet efficient working mechanism provides a feasible route toward stretchable acoustic devices 18.

Conventional piezoelectric materials often suffer from a trade-off between electrical performance and mechanical properties. Inorganic piezoelectric ceramics, represented by PZT and BTO, exhibit relatively high piezoelectric coefficients (d33) but are limited by excessive rigidity and complicated fabrication processes19. On the other hand, organic piezoelectric polymers such as PVDF offer good flexibility, yet their d33 remains relatively low20. Molecular ferroelectrics, as a novel class of piezoelectric materials, have garnered significant attention in recent years owing to their attractive merits, including structurally tunable frameworks, light weight, low acoustic impedance, and facile crystallization and film formation21–23. Taking hybrid organic-inorganic perovskite molecular ferroelectrics as an example, their piezoelectric behavior originates from the ordered arrangement and interactions of molecular components (e.g., organic cations and inorganic frameworks) within the crystal lattice24. These materials combine the advantageous characteristics of both organic molecules and inorganic frameworks. It is noteworthy that the d33 values of certain molecular ferroelectric materials have already surpassed those of traditional inorganic ceramics25. For example, our group previously reported the hybrid perovskite organic-inorganic perovskite molecular ferroelectric TMCM-CdCl3 (TMCM = trimethylchloromethyl ammonium [Me3NCH2Cl]+) which exhibits a remarkably high d33 value of 220 pC/N26. However, it also faces intrinsic brittleness, typically requiring doping strategies to enable its application in flexible sensors. Compared with inorganic piezoelectric ceramics, TMCM-CdCl3 can be mutually dissolved with polymer matrices in solvents. Furthermore, its abundant organic components allow for the formation of strong intermolecular interactions, such as hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, halogen interactions and other van der Waals forces, with the polymer matrix27,28. Consequently, uniformly dispersed TMCM-CdCl3 crystallites can form robust interfacial bonding with the polymer matrix, making them less prone to fatigue fracture under strain while enabling efficient stress transmission for electromechanical transduction. Based on this composite strategy, significant progress has been made in existing research. For example, the use of a porous TPU framework loaded with TMCM-CdCl3 crystals has yielded piezoelectric composite materials with high power output28. Additionally, TMCM-CdCl3/PVP composite fibers prepared via the electrospinning process have been successfully applied in low-frequency sound recognition15. These studies have confirmed the application potential of the TMCM-CdCl3 and polymer composite system. However, the electromechanical coupling behavior of molecular ferroelectric crystals and polymer matrices under dynamic tensile deformation remains unexplored, and its underlying mechanisms are still unclear. Furthermore, the interface contact issues between the composite material and electrodes during dynamic deformation have not yet been considered.

Herein, we designed stretchable TMCM-CdCl3/TPU (TCCT) composite fibers by uniformly dispersing high-d33 TMCM-CdCl3 crystals into a TPU matrix via electrospinning technology, which serves as the piezoelectric active layer. Utilizing the robust intermolecular interactions between the crystals and the matrix effectively improved the mechanical tensile stability of the fibers. In the sensor architecture, we constructed a dense intermediate layer (interlocked layered architecture) at the interface between the liquid metal (LM)/TPU electrode and the TCCT fibers through rational process design. This structure not only ensures a robust connection between the piezoelectric active layer and the electrodes but also effectively enhances the charge coupling efficiency of the electrodes. The resulting TCCT sensor demonstrated a pressure sensitivity (4.36 V/kPa) and strain performance (109%). For acoustic sensing, the sound sensitivity of the sensor under stretched conditions increased to 30.16 mV/dB, twice that in the unstretched state. This improvement primarily originates from the synergistic effect between the strain-induced intrinsic piezoelectric enhancement of the crystals and the increased stress transfer efficiency resulting from the densified fiber network. Notably, stretching enhances the sensor’s ability to recognize information features within the raw speech signal. Overall, the TCCT sensor with strain-enhanced perception capability provides a viable strategy for developing next-generation flexible acoustic sensing devices. These devices can adapt to dynamic and complex environments while maintaining high sensitivity and stability.

Results

To address the brittleness and inflexibility of molecular ferroelectrics and thereby render the resulting devices compatible with a broad range of complex working scenarios, we incorporated TMCM-CdCl3 crystals into a TPU matrix and fabricated TMCM-CdCl3/TPU (TCCT) composite fibers as the piezoelectric active layer via electrospinning (Fig. 1a). Multiple molecular interactions formed between the TMCM-CdCl3 crystals and TPU, including monopolar interactions and van der Waals forces between the -CH3 groups, -C = O groups in TPU chains and Cl atoms in [CdCl3]−27, and halogen bonds and abundant van der Waals forces between Cl atoms in [TMCM]+ ([Me3NCH2Cl]+) and Cl atoms in [CdCl3]−29. Moreover, the electrospinning process simultaneously induces a directional poling alignment between TMCM-CdCl3 and TPU via the applied high-voltage electric field. These molecular interactions promote strong interfacial adhesion between TMCM-CdCl3 and the TPU matrix, ensuring the overall tensile stability of the TCCT fibers.

The electrodes employed stretchable LM/TPU composite polymer as the material. The electrode solution was spread onto a flat-plate collector, enabling the simultaneous one-side electrode assembly during the fabrication of the piezoelectric active layer. The assembled bilayer structure was then placed onto the electrode solution, following slow curing, ultimately formed the complete sensor with top and bottom electrodes (Fig. 1b). Detailed fabrication procedures are provided in the Methods. The assembled TCCT sensor features a five-layer architecture, comprising symmetrically arranged upper and lower porous layers, dense intermediate layers and a central nanofiber layer. During electrode curing and electrospinning formation, a tightly interlocked structure develops between the piezoelectric nanofiber layer and the porous electrode layer. This architecture significantly enhances interfacial bonding between these layers, ensuring reliable electrical contact during device stretching 30.

The pressure-sensing capability of the TCCT sensor is enabled by its core filler, TMCM-CdCl3 crystals. Under external stress, deformation of the TPU matrix effectively couples to that of the TMCM-CdCl3 crystallites, and the compressive deformation of the inorganic [CdCl3]− anionic framework induces polarization rotation in the organic [TMCM]+ cations, generating a pronounced piezoelectric response (Fig. 1c)31. The authentic crystal structure of the paraelectric phase and configurations of different polarization orientations are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1b. Synergistically combining the high d33 coefficient molecular ferroelectric material with a stretchable polymer matrix confers exceptional electromechanical response capability and stretchability to the TCCT sensor. Remarkably, applied strain significantly enhances the sensor’s acoustic-vibrational sensitivity, demonstrating pronounced advantages for voice recognition applications (Fig. 1d).

Fabrication and characterization of TCCT fibers

To achieve stretchable piezoelectric active layer with high performance, we selected the hybrid perovskite organic-inorganic molecular ferroelectric TMCM-CdCl3 crystals as the functional filler, which exhibit a large d33 value of 220 pC/N (Supplementary Note 1). Its crystal structure is shown in Fig. 2a. The structure comprises organic cations [TMCM]+ and an inorganic anionic framework [CdCl3]− formed by CdCl6 octahedral units26. Alternating stacking of the [TMCM]+ cations and [CdCl3]− anions forms a typical hexagonal hybrid organic-inorganic ABX3-type perovskite structure. The synthesized TMCM-CdCl3 crystals and TPU were co-dissolved in N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) under magnetic stirring for 6 h to obtain a homogeneous electrospinning solution. During electrospinning, a 20 kV DC electric field was applied between the needle tip and the flat-plate collector. To characterize the elastic properties of the resulting composite fibers, atomic force microscopy (AFM) was employed to analyze fiber morphology and modulus variations before and after doping (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 2). Compared with pure TPU fibers, the TCCT nanofibers exhibited relatively rough surfaces, with Young’s modulus increasing from 235 MPa to 290 MPa. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations of fiber morphology revealed uniform surfaces with embedded white crystalline grains, indicating well-defined fibrous structures (Fig. 2d). Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the homogeneous distribution of elements C and Cl throughout the fiber matrix, which represents a characteristic signature of the TMCM-CdCl3 crystals. The TCCT fibers exhibited diameters of 406.3 ± 30 nm with fibers’ thickness as low as 70 μm (Fig. 2e, f and Supplementary Fig. 3). X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirmed matching diffraction peaks between the TMCM-CdCl3/TPU composite fibers and pure TMCM-CdCl3 crystals, indicating successful integration of the crystals into the TPU nanofibers (Fig. 2g). Furthermore, comparative analysis of the Raman spectra of TPU fibers, TCCT fibers, and TMCM-CdCl3 crystals reveals that Cl‑related vibrational features are present only in the pure crystal and the TCCT composite fibers, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4 and its expanded view of the 760–860 cm-1 region (Fig. 2h). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy demonstrated an enhanced characteristic peak in the 700–800 cm-1 range, which further confirmed the introduction of the C-Cl structure (Supplementary Fig. 5). Given the second-harmonic generation (SHG)-inactive nature of the TPU matrix and the noncentrosymmetric structure of TMCM-CdCl3, we performed SHG experiments to examine the polarization characteristics of the composite fibers. A femtosecond laser source at 800 nm was used to excite the TCCT fibers, the output SHG signals with a peak wavelength at 400 nm were collected (inset, Fig. 2i). As shown in Fig. 2i, a significant SHG response was observed in the TCCT fibers compared to the TPU fibers, attributable to the presence of TMCM-CdCl3’s noncentrosymmetric crystalline structure. The ferroelectric piezoelectric properties of the TCCT fibers were characterized by Piezoresponse Force Microscopy (PFM). The selected fiber morphology is shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. The typical in-plane and out-of-plane PFM amplitude and phase images showed distinct domain structure distributions in the two modes, confirming the presence of spontaneous polarization in the fibers. The differences between in-plane and out-of-plane components corresponded to the multipolar axis characteristics of TMCM-CdCl3 (Supplementary Fig. 7). Meanwhile, the difference in phase imaging before and after the application of voltage also confirmed the presence of ferroelectricity. Under the electric field, the dependence of amplitude and phase on voltage displayed typical butterfly and hysteresis loops, respectively, offering compelling quantitative evidence for polarization switching and hysteresis behaviors of ferroelectric polarization (Supplementary Fig. 8). Although bulk TMCM-CdCl3 crystals exhibit limited flexibility that restricts their practical applications in flexible, soft, and implantable devices, embedding these crystals within a soft TPU matrix significantly enhanced mechanical compliance while imparting stretchability. Figure 2j demonstrates the TCCT fibers undergoing intentional repeated bending, stretching, folding, and twisting, highlighting its exceptional flexibility and mechanical robustness. The electrospun TCCT fibers exhibited exceptional stretchability and maintain mechanical durability even after 1000 stretch-release cycles at 70% strain (Supplementary Fig. 9). Higher TMCM-CdCl3 crystals loading occupied space within the TPU elastic nanofibers and disrupted inter-fiber connections, decreasing the maximum strain from 141% at 1% content to 53% at 15% content (Fig. 2k).

Fabrication and characterization of TCCT sensor

To achieve full sensor stretchability, we engineered stretchable electrodes by blending LM conductive fillers with a polymer matrix. The LM/TPU electrodes exhibit negligible resistance change under 200% strain (Supplementary Fig. 10). The TCCT sensor features a sandwich structure (Fig. 3a, b), comprising two LM/TPU electrodes encapsulating a TCCT fibers. The porous LM/TPU layers function dually as both conductive electrodes and flexible supporting substrates. The cross-section EDS mapping (Supplementary Fig. 11) identified the middle layer as TCCT fibers, and the main element is Cl. Cross-sectional SEM and optical images revealed the device architecture (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 12), where an interlocked intermediate layer formed between the electrodes and piezoelectric active layer. This integrated structure ensures effective electrode-to-active-layer contact under tensile strain. The mechanical properties of the TCCT sensor were evidenced by cyclic stretch-release curves at varying strains and repeated stretch-release testing at 50% strain (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 13). Compared with the 1 wt% TCCT fibers, the TCCT fibers with LM/TPU electrodes exhibited higher tensile fracture strain. To further investigate the strain behavior of TCCT fibers, we fabricated a sensor with only a single-sided electrode. Under progressively increasing strain, the white TCCT fibers maintained conformal contact with the gray LM/TPU electrode. Upon reaching the fracture limit, the fibers ruptured while the remaining segments retained strong adhesion to the electrode surface (Supplementary Fig. 14). Simultaneously, real-time visualization under optical microscopy confirmed intact interfacial bonding in TCCT sensor during stretching (0–40% strain), with no observable interfacial delamination (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Movie 1). We then established a mechano-electrical measurement system and implemented multi-layer encapsulation to minimize interference from triboelectric on piezoelectric voltage during testing (Supplementary Fig. 15)32. The role of TMCM-CdCl3 crystals in the mechano-electrical response characteristics of the sensor was systematically studied. At a pressure of 1 kPa, the TCCT fibers showed obvious piezoelectric response, while the pure TPU fibers produced no voltage output (Supplementary Fig. 16). This confirms that the piezoelectricity is solely attributed to the molecular ferroelectrics. Upon altering the content of TMCM-CdCl3 crystals, it was observed that the trend of changes in the output voltage and current of the TCCT sensor exhibits an initial increase followed by a decrease. The output voltage of the TCCT sensor reaches its maximum at a 5% content (Fig. 3g, Supplementary Fig. 17). The d33 of the TCCT fibers was also measured by linear fitting of the charge versus force data. As calculated, 5% TCCT fibers exhibited the maximum d33 of approximately 32.00 pC/N (Supplementary Fig. 18a–e). To achieve a balance between satisfactory electrical output and mechanical strain capability, we determined the optimal content of TMCM-CdCl3 to be 5%. At this level, the TCCT fibers exhibited a maximum strain of approximately 109%. We hypothesize that the initial increase is caused by the gradual increase in TMCM-CdCl3 content. However, as the content continues to increase, the enhanced conductivity of the precursor solution exacerbates the instability of the jet33. Therefore, at concentrations of 10% and 15%, the beads formed due to jet instability create spot-like dense regions on the fibers surface (10% and 15%) (Supplementary Fig. 19). EDS analysis indicated a tendency for Cl elements to aggregate at these defect sites (Supplementary Fig. 20). Such microstructural inhomogeneities caused a non-uniform distribution of the polarization field, thereby compromising the macroscopic piezoelectric output. In contrast, the 5 wt% TCCT fibers not only maintained a homogeneous dispersion of Cl elements but also exhibited a significantly higher effective content than the 1wt% and 3wt% fibers, ultimately demonstrating the best piezoelectric performance. As shown in Fig. 3g, the TCCT fibers with LM/TPU electrodes exhibited significantly higher output voltages than those with Al electrodes across different TMCM-CdCl3 crystal doping levels. Supplementary Fig. 21 provided a comparative analysis of the voltage and current output curves for 5 wt% TCCT fibers under the two electrode configurations. This performance enhancement stems from the tightly interlocked intermediate layer, which effectively eliminates air gaps between the electrode layer and the piezoelectric active layer, while increasing the functionally effective contact area34. The resulting air gaps introduce additional parasitic capacitance, thereby reducing the induced charge at the electrodes. In contrast, the LM/TPU electrode design in the TCCT sensor effectively enhances charge collection efficiency by both eliminating interlayer air gaps and enlarging the contact area through its interlocked architecture—thereby significantly improving the sensor’s electromechanical conversion efficiency (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Fig. 22). Pressure sensitivity constitutes a critical performance metric for the TCCT sensor, defined as the ratio of output voltage variation (ΔU) to applied pressure change (ΔP) under stable operating conditions. The sensitivity formula is expressed as S = ΔU/ΔP. As shown in Fig. 3h, within the 0-6 kPa pressure range, the LM/TPU electrodes configuration achieved a voltage sensitivity of 4.36 V/kPa, demonstrating significant superiority over the conventional Al electrode configuration (see Supplementary information for calculation details and the data listed in Table 2). Under distinct pressures (0.5, 1 and 4 kPa), the TCCT sensor generated voltage waveforms with progressively increasing amplitude while maintaining signal stability (Supplementary Fig. 23). We evaluated the frequency stability of the TCCT sensor at 1–3 Hz, and the experimental results showed that its output voltage could maintain stability (Fig. 3i). The TCCT sensor exhibits a response time of 0.03 s, which corresponds to the duration required for the voltage to rise from 10% to 90% (Supplementary Fig. 24). Durability testing at 0.5 kPa demonstrates preserved waveform integrity through 7,500 cycles, confirming long-term operational reliability (Supplementary Fig. 25). When benchmarked against previously reported flexible ferroelectric sensors incorporating inorganic35, organic36–40, and hybrid molecular ferroelectric materials41,42 (Fig. 3j, Supplementary Table 1), the TCCT sensor exhibits exceptional performance in both sensitivity and tensile strain tolerance. This dual advantage enables precise detection of low-pressure vibrations (e.g., acoustic signals) and seamless adaptability to stretchable scenarios.

Responses to acoustic forces

Acoustic waves belong to low-pressure vibrational stimuli. To verify the low-pressure detection capability of the TCCT sensor, we constructed an acoustic testing system (Fig. 4a). In this system, a sound controller generated sound waves with a specific frequency and amplitude, a loudspeaker served as the sound source, and the data storage unit recorded the response voltage generated by the TCCT sensor. One end of the TCCT sensor and its lead wire were fixed to a support fixture, constituting a rigid constraint, while the other end was connected to PI tape secured to a fixture on the opposite side, forming an elastic constraint (Supplementary Fig. 26). The rigid constraint suppressed interference from lead vibrations during acoustic wave acquisition, whereas the elastic constraint permited micro-deformation of the sensor under sound pressure, preserving acoustic response sensitivity. The sound wave is a sine wave with a defined frequency and decibel level. Under constant 80 dB pure-tone excitation, the TCCT sensor demonstrated good acoustic response characteristics across the 30–5000 Hz frequency range (Fig. 4b). Subsequently, we investigated the influence of strain on the sensor’s acousto-electric conversion capability under an 80 dB and 100 Hz acoustic field (Supplementary Fig. 27). The results showed that the output voltage of the TCCT sensor continued to increase with strain (0–40% strain), but the rate of voltage rise slowed significantly once strain exceeded 20%. Therefore, to clearly demonstrate the improvement resulting from stretching, subsequent experiments focused on the 0–20% strain range. Across varying acoustic frequencies (30–5000 Hz) and decibel levels (70–100 dB), the TCCT sensor under 20% strain exhibits significantly superior acoustoelectric conversion capability and acoustic pressure sensitivity compared with those at 0% and 10% strain (Fig. 4c, d). Original voltage waveforms are documented in Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29. Stretching enhances the sensitivity of the TCCT sensor, which exhibited stable linearity with strain-dependent enhancement of acoustic sensitivity. Compared with its state before stretching, the acoustic wave sensitivity of the stretched TCCT sensor increases to 30.16 mV/dB. Figure 4e elucidates the mechanism of strain-enhanced piezoelectric response. Under the same acoustic excitation, lateral stretching induces a certain strain in the TMCM-CdCl3 crystals within the fibers (Supplementary Fig. 30, with material parameters provided in Table 3), which directly enhances the intrinsic piezoelectric response of the crystals43. Simultaneously, it promotes a denser internal structure of the TCCT fibers (Supplementary Fig. 31). The compact structure improves the mechanical coupling and stress transfer efficiency between the fibers44, ensuring that external loads are more effectively transmitted to the crystals embedded in the TPU matrix. The synergistic effect of strain-induced enhancement of the intrinsic piezoelectric response of the crystals and improved stress transfer efficiency resulting from the optimized fiber network structure enhances the electromechanical conversion efficiency of the TCCT sensor, resulting in a significant increase in surface charge density and output voltage. Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), a critical parameter for assessing output signal quality, is defined as the ratio of useful signal power to background noise power under specified operating conditions, expressed as SNR = 20 log(Voutput/Vnoise). Within the 0–1.3 Pa sound pressure range, the strained TCCT sensor demonstrated higher SNR values compared to its unstrained state (Fig. 4f). Benchmarked against state-of-the-art flexible piezoelectric acoustic sensors (Fig. 4g)2,15,16,45–49, the TCCT sensor achieves a broader dynamic detection range (30–5000 Hz) that fully encompasses the human voice frequency spectrum (300–3400 Hz). Although comparison results indicate that some flexible piezoelectric acoustic sensors do outperform the current TCCT sensor in terms of frequency response range, the TCCT sensor possesses a strain-enhanced acoustic sensing capability, which validates its significant advantages for dynamic and different voice detection applications.

Applications and demonstrations

Voice information represents a primary means of human interaction. We simulated the complete human auditory perception and recognition process by integrating TCCT sensor with deep learning algorithms (Fig. 5a). The TCCT sensor mimics the human auditory mechanism by converting external acoustic stimuli into electrical signals. Deep learning algorithms then process the sensor data, emulating the brain’s capacity to analyze, process, and recognize acoustic signals. Comparative analysis of the piezoelectric signals acquired by the commercial sensor (LDT0-028K) and the TCCT sensor during continuous audio playback demonstrated that the TCCT sensor achieves high fidelity in reconstructing the original waveform (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Movie 2). Further time-frequency analysis via short-time Fourier transform (STFT) revealed that the TCCT sensor completely preserved the complex time-varying frequency characteristics of the original speech within the 0–2000Hz range, whereas the commercial sensor exhibited severe information loss (Fig. 5c). Furthermore, a comparison of the piezoelectric signals collected by the TCCT sensor before and after stretching revealed a significant increase in output voltage, along with an improvement in its high-frequency response capability (Supplementary Fig. 32). Fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis revealed that the TCCT sensor outperformed commercial sensor in capturing and reconstructing key frequency components across the 0-1000 Hz spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 33). Under 20% strain, the sensor enabled partial reconstruction of frequency components within the 1000–2000 Hz band, albeit with variations in the amplitude ratios of certain subtle spectral peaks owing to the intrinsic damping of the device. Concurrently, a shift of the peak response toward higher frequencies was observed. This phenomenon originated from strain-induced changes in the elastic stiffness of the TCCT sensor, leading to an increase in high-frequency components (Supplementary Note 2). Although the change in stiffness caused by stretching introduces slight resonance peak shifts, the enhancement in sensitivity plays a decisive role in improving the spectral consistency with the original signal. Specifically, under 20% strain, the acoustic response characteristics of the TCCT sensor are enhanced. Compared with the unstretched state, the stretched sensor can more effectively capture the high-frequency details of the original sound signal. This strain-enabled rapid frequency response tuning allows customization of sensitivity ranges for targeted applications, demonstrating exceptional versatility in acoustic detection.

To evaluate the impact of strain on speech signal feature acquisition by TCCT sensor, we captured acoustic signals from sensors under before-stretching and after-stretching conditions (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 34). We tested words such as Science and Nature, and the TCCT sensor was able to accurately reconstruct the speech signals without degradation even under stretching deformation. The strained state exhibited significantly amplified voltage amplitudes while preserving waveform characteristics with only minor deviations. Simultaneously, we employed deep learning techniques to classify and recognize basic speech elements, utilizing a hybrid architecture of convolutional neural networks (CNN) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks to process the test data (Fig. 5e). Here, the CNN extracts local features of the speech signal, filters out noise, and captures key acoustic patterns effectively. The LSTM is designed to process sequential data and can model temporal dependencies in speech. The combination of CNN and LSTM enhances the model's robustness in recognizing speech under different strain conditions. The network employs three one-dimensional convolutional modules (channel configuration: 128-256-512, kernel size: 5) for local acoustic feature extraction. A bidirectional LSTM layer (128 → 64 units) captures temporal dependencies, with the initial 128-unit layer preserving sequence states and the subsequent 64-unit layer producing global contextual features. Final classification is performed through a fully connected layer. Using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE), we visualized TCCT sensor data under varying stretching conditions in a low-dimensional space. Distinct clustering was observed for signals acquired in the unstretched state and stretched state (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Fig. 35a). Clear clustering persists when combining before-stretching and after-stretching data (Fig. 5g). The confusion matrix synthesizes the classification accuracy, and the recognition accuracy of the mixed data can reach 97.05% (Fig. 5h). Although the prediction accuracy for the mixed data decreased compared to that in unstretched and stretched states alone (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 35b, c), it maintained distinct clustering and high recognition accuracy. This demonstrates the TCCT sensor’s capability to precisely reconstruct speech signal features under mechanical stretching.

Discussion

We have successfully fabricated uniformly distributed stretchable molecular ferroelectric composite fibers by incorporating TMCM-CdCl3 crystals into a TPU matrix. Through engineered interfacial processing, a tightly interlocked structure was constructed between the electrode interface and piezoelectric active layer, enabling monolithic stretchable device integration. The resulting TCCT fibers exhibited exceptional stretchability (109% maximum strain) and piezoelectric response (4.36 V/kPa). The hybrid organic-inorganic perovskite molecular ferroelectric TMCM-CdCl3 crystals demonstrate exceptional organic compatibility, forming multimodal bonding interactions with polymer matrices. While robust interfacial bonding ensures the composite fibers' fracture resistance under strain, the fibers' maximum strain decreased from 141% at 1% content to 53% at 15% content as the crystal content increased. This phenomenon is attributed to the high loading of TMCM-CdCl3 crystals, which act as stress concentration points within the TPU nanofibers and simultaneously restrict the motion of polymer chain segments. Concurrently, the interlocked architecture maintains stable electrical contact between the electrode and the piezoelectric layer during stretching deformation. The exceptional mechanical properties and piezoelectric responsiveness of the TCCT sensor enabled reliable sound detection under dynamic conditions. Notably, while mechanical strain enhances the intrinsic piezoelectric response of the TMCM-CdCl3 crystals embedded in the polymer matrix, it also induces significant densification of the fiber network. This compact network structure greatly optimizes mechanical coupling efficiency, enabling the minute vibrations generated by acoustic waves to be more effectively captured by the internal crystals, thereby enhancing overall piezoelectric output. Consequently, the TCCT sensor achieved a sound-perception sensitivity of 30.16 mV/dB at 20% strain. To demonstrate its capabilities, we employed machine learning to analyze the performance of speech signal acquisition. Results showed that the mixed dataset combining unstretched and 20% strained speech signals maintained 97.05% recognition accuracy. This work thus establishes strain-enhanced piezoelectric molecular ferroelectric sensors as a paradigm for flexible acoustic sensing on dynamically deformable surfaces, demonstrating significant potential for advancing wearable health-monitoring interfaces.

Methods

Synthesis of TMCM-CdCl3 crystals

All reagents were purchased from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. Equimolar amounts of trimethylamine (30 wt% in water) and dichloromethane were added to acetonitrile with stirring for 72 h at ambient temperature. Subsequently, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure to yield TMCM-Cl. 50 mmol TMCM-Cl and 50 mmol CdCl2 were dissolved in deionized water, followed by slow evaporation at ambient conditions to form white single crystals of TMCM-CdCl3.

Electrospinning TCCT fibers

A TMCM-CdCl3 solution was prepared by dissolving a measured quantity of TMCM-CdCl3 crystals in 10 mL of DMF (Aladdin, GC grade >99.9%) under continuous heating and stirring at 60 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 1.2 g of TPU was added to the TMCM-CdCl3 solution. After heating and continuous stirring at 60°C for 5h, a transparent spinning solution was obtained. The prepared spinning solution was loaded into a syringe with a metallic needle (Size: No. 19). A voltage of 20 kV was applied to the needle tip with a syringe feed rate of 0.65 mL h-1. The syringe underwent horizontal reciprocating motion with a travel distance of 120 mm at a traverse speed of 10 mm/s. A flat-plate collector was positioned approximately 120 mm from the needle to collect deposited TMCM-CdCl3/TPU composite nanofibers. Fibers’ thickness was tailored by varying the electrospinning duration.

Device fabrication of TCCT sensor

A TPU solution was prepared by dissolving 3 g TPU in 30 mL of DMF with continuous heating and stirring at 60 °C for 5 h. Subsequently, 3 g of liquid metal (LM, 75% Ga, 25% In) was added to the TPU solution. The mixture underwent probe sonication (KC-600W, 75%) in a room-temperature water bath for 0.5 h to yield a uniformly dispersed LM/TPU electrode composite solution. Prior to electrospinning the TCCT fibers, a certain amount of (1.0–1.5 mL) the electrode composite solution was transferred into a pre-fabricated rectangular Excoflex mold (length × width: 60 mm × 30 mm) positioned on the flat-plate collector. The mold was maintained at 40 °C and 50% relative humidity for 0.25 h to allow surface DMF to evaporate. Subsequently, environmental conditions were adjusted to 40 °C and 20% humidity to initiate the electrospinning process (parameters as previously described), enabling direct deposition of the TCCT nanofiber membrane onto the pre-cured electrode layer. Following electrospinning, TCCT fibers with unilateral LM/TPU electrodes were obtained. The resulting fibers underwent vacuum drying at 40 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, under identical environmental conditions (40 °C, 20% humidity), the electrode composite solution was maintained for 0.25 h. The TCCT fibers with a unilateral electrode was overlaid onto the LM/TPU electrode solution and immediately transferred to a fume hood for ambient drying (2 h). Subsequently, the assembly was sandwiched between parallel plates under uniform pressure and vacuum-dried at 40 °C for 12 h, yielding the final TCCT sensor with bilateral electrodes.

Measurements and characterization

The phase purities and identification of TMCM-CdCl3 crystals, TPU fibers, and TCCT fibers were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD; Bruker D2 Phaser). Internal microstructure of TCCT fibers was analyzed using second-harmonic generation (SHG; WITec alpha300R). The ferroelectricity of the TCCT fibers was characterized using an atomic force microscope (AFM, Asylum Research MFP‑3D) with an AC drive voltage applied to the conductive tip. Conductive Pt/Ir-coated silicon probes (EFM-50, Nanoworld) were used for domain imaging and polarization switching studies, with a nominal spring constant of ~2.8 nN/nm and a free-air resonance frequency of ~75 kHz. Fiber morphology, fibers thickness, and elemental composition of TCCT fibers were assessed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; ZEISS Sigma 360) coupled and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The topography of individual fibers and Young’s modulus were determined via atomic force microscopy (AFM; Oxford Asylum Research MFP-3D). Tensile properties of fibers were evaluated with a universal testing machine (Pubtester TST-01H). Piezoelectric response signals under varying pressure conditions were acquired using a digital oscilloscope (Keysight DSOX3024T) and electrometer (Keithley 6514). Sound pressure levels were measured using a sound level meter (AR814) coupled with a reference loudspeaker (R1080BT).
