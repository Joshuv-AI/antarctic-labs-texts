Outcomes of Posterior Oblique Medial Epicondylectomy for the Treatment of Cubital Tunnel Syndrome
Authors: Shinsuke Morisaki, Kengo Yoshii, Shinji Tsuchida, Ryo Oda, Kenji Takahashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the clinical outcomes and complication rates after posterior oblique medial epicondylectomy for the treatment of cubital tunnel syndrome (CuTS) and assess its efficacy in improving motor and sensory function with minimal postoperative morbidity.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 48 patients (51 extremities) with CuTS who underwent posterior oblique medial epicondylectomy between 2015 and 2023. All surgeries were performed by a single senior surgeon. Patients were assessed before surgery and at a mean follow-up of 22 months by using the McGowan grading system, the Wilson and Krout classification, grip and pinch strength, two-point discrimination, Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand, and visual analog scale scores. Statistical analysis included the Fisher exact test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank sum test.ResultsAfter surgery, 90% of extremities improved by at least one McGowan grade. Wilson and Krout grading revealed outcomes of “excellent” in 31 patients, “good” in 17 patients, and “fair” in 3 patients. Significant improvements occurred in two-point discrimination (ring/little finger: from 12 to 6 mm) (P < .001), side pinch (from 4.0 to 6.5 kg) (P < .001), tip pinch (from 3.0 to 4.5 kg) (P < .001), and grip strength (from 25 to 30 kg) (P < .001). Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand scores improved from 27 to 9.1, and visual analog scale scores decreased from 30 to 10 (P < .001). No patient had symptomatic ulnar nerve subluxation or elbow instability. Six patients reported mild medial elbow pain.ConclusionsPosterior oblique medial epicondylectomy for CuTS yielded favorable outcomes with significant improvement in motor and sensory function and minimal complications. This technique may offer advantages over traditional medial epicondylectomy by preserving elbow stability and enabling early active range of motion during the postoperative period.Clinical relevanceTherapeutic IV.

Cubital tunnel syndrome is the second most common compression neuropathy of the upper limb, after carpal tunnel syndrome.1 Surgical treatment is chosen for patients whose symptoms, such as sensory impairment and muscle weakness, have progressed and for whom nonsurgical treatment has been ineffective. The primary aim of surgery is to relieve compression of the ulnar nerve.2,3 There are various surgical techniques have been reported, including in situ decompression of the ulnar nerve,4, 5, 6 decompression with medial epicondylectomy,7, 8, 9 and subcutaneous,10,11 intermuscular12 or submuscular13 ulnar nerve transposition. Furthermore, there have been different procedures regarding the extent of osteotomy of the medial epicondyle of the humerus and whether to change the position of the ulnar nerve.14,15 Although prior analyses have suggested differences in neurologic outcomes and complication rates between techniques, the choice of surgical technique remains a matter of surgeon preference.16,17

Although osteotomy of the medial epicondyle improves symptoms of the ulnar nerve, the high incidence of medial epicondylalgia and medial elbow instability is problematic.18, 19, 20, 21 The high incidence is because of the detachment of the anterior bundle of the medial collateral ligament.18, 19, 20, 21, 22 Other reported complications include persistent tenderness at the osteotomy site, ulnar nerve subluxation over the residual epicondyle, elbow flexion contracture, and decreased grip strength related to the detachment of the origin of the flexor and pronator muscles.23,24 To reduce these complications, improvements in surgical technique have been reported, such as partial medial epicondylectomy21,25,26 and minimal epicondylectomy,27, 28, 29, 30 which reduce the amount of osteotomy. These techniques still involve some degree of invasion of the medial collateral ligament. However, posterior oblique medial epicondylectomy, which is a modified technique that has been reported since then, minimizes the complications of osteotomy by minimizing the osteotomy of the anterior part of the medial epicondyle.31

However, few reports of this technique exist, and it has not become a common practice.31 In this study, we examined the usefulness of this technique by analyzing the postoperative results in detail, including objective findings before and after surgery.

Materials and Methods

This study was a retrospective cohort series of patients who presented with cubital tunnel syndrome (CuTS) at a single center and underwent surgical treatment with posterior oblique medial epicondylectomy. The study was approved by the institutional review board (Review board of Saiseikai Shiga Hospital), and written informed consent was obtained from each patient. All patients were operated on by a senior surgeon (S.M). The inclusion criteria were patients aged ≥40 years who underwent posterior oblique medial epicondylectomy between 2015 and 2023. The exclusion criterion included a follow-up of <12 months. Three patients were lost to follow-up.

The cohort consisted of 48 patients, representing 51 extremities, and included 41 men and 7 women, aged 40– 89 years, with a mean age of 60 years. The average final follow-up was at 22 months (range, 12–99 months). The dominant extremity was affected in 36 (71%) of 51 patients. The mean duration of symptoms before surgery was 14 months.

Cubital tunnel syndrome was diagnosed via clinical assessment of symptoms, including history, assessment of distribution of numbness in the hand, Froment’s sign, and electrophysiological testing.

Preoperative assessment of severity by using the McGowan grading system revealed that 16 patients were classified with grade IIA; 16 patients with grade IIB; and 19 patients with grade III.32

The preoperative clinical examination included sensory assessment by using two-point discrimination (2PD) and muscle strength assessment by using side pinch, tip pinch, and grip strength. Pinch and grip strength were documented using a hand-held manual dynamometer and a hand-held pinch gauge, respectively. The 2PD values were recorded for the ulnar side of the ring and little fingers. A 2PD of ≤5 mm was recorded as “5 mm.” We also examined for the presence of intrinsic muscle atrophy and Tinel’s sign over the ulnar nerve, on the medial side of the elbow. We also examined the range of motion of the elbow joints. These clinical examinations were performed before surgery and at the final postoperative visit by the treating surgeon.

We also collected the data from the Quick Disabilities of the Shoulder, Arm, and Hand (QuickDASH) questionnaire and visual analog scale (VAS) before surgery and at the final follow-up after surgery. The QuickDASH is an 11-item measure of upper-extremity disability.

All patients underwent preoperative electrophysiological testing. Nerve conduction velocity of the motor of the ulnar nerve at the proximal and distal ends of the elbow was measured. All tests were performed using a Neuropack X-1 MEB 2306 neurodiagnostic system (Nihon–Koden).

We collected all follow-up data during the study-related follow-up examinations. At these encounters, we repeated all previously cited physical examination measures in addition to assessing medial elbow pain and medial elbow instability. To assess elbow instability, a valgus force was applied to the elbow while it was flexed to an angle of approximately 20–30 degrees with the wrist stabilized. A wide medial elbow joint gap indicated instability. With regard to medial elbow pain, the patients were asked if they experienced medial elbow pain; if pain persisted, it was considered “positive.” We assessed the overall postoperative clinical outcome by using the McGowan grading system and the Wilson and Krout classification.33

Surgical technique

All patients underwent posterior oblique medial epicondylectomy, as described by Osei et al.31 A diagram of the posterior oblique medial epicondylectomy is shown in Figure 1. The surgery was performed under local or general anesthesia. An air-pressure tourniquet was used at an average pressure of 250 mm Hg. A curved medial incision was made on the medial epicondyle on the ulnar side of the elbow. Dissection was performed down to the subcutaneous tissue. The medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm was preserved. The ulnar nerve was identified at the entrance to the cubital tunnel. Local decompression of the nerve was performed from the medial intermuscular septum to the elbow tunnel. The medial intermuscular septum was routinely excised. The tunnel was also opened by separating the Osborne ligament. In the distal tunnel, the fascia of the flexor carpi ulnaris was incised, and the ulnar nerve was completely relaxed. All branches of the ulnar nerve were preserved.Figure 1Diagram of the posterior oblique medial epicondylectomy. The bone cutting line was made at an angle of approximately 45° from the most medial tip of the medial epicondyle, cutting through the posterior bulge by using a 6-mm bone saw (Fig. 1). Therefore, the origin of the flexor and pronator muscles attached to the medial epicondyle was not damaged. Figure 2 shows a representative axial computed-tomography scan of the medial epicondyle; the bone cutting line is also shown. The proximal and distal corners of the medial epicondyle were prepared by using a rongeur and file to smooth the cut surface of the bone. After an osteotomy, the ulnar nerve slid slightly medially and anteriorly, but it did not ride over the medial epicondyle; thus, snapping was eliminated (Fig. 3). During surgery, the elbow was flexed to confirm the lack of impingement. If impingement was detected, we performed additional bone resection and confirmed that the ulnar nerve was not stretched. The remaining medial epicondyle was covered with the periosteal flap back down, and the skin was then closed. The patient was allowed to move freely beginning the day after surgery. Statistical analysis

Fisher exact test was used for the statistical analysis of the change in grade between the preoperative and postoperative clinical results. The Wilcoxon signed-rank sum test was used for the analysis of changes in the objective and subjective scores before and after surgery.

The mean range of motion of the elbow before surgery was 128° flexion and −6.7° extension, and after surgery it was 132° flexion and −5.5° extension. No patient reported pain or discomfort in the elbow because of dislocation of the ulnar nerve or elbow instability during valgus stress testing. No patient complained of ulnar nerve instability without symptoms. Six patients complained of slight medial elbow pain. No patient had paresthesia in the medial antebrachial nerve distribution.

Discussion

We reported the postoperative results of posterior oblique medial epicondylectomy. The postoperative results using this method were good, and the incidence of complications, such as medial elbow pain and instability, was low. We demonstrated that this technique is an effective surgical method for treating CuTS.

The number of cases in our study was greater than those in previous reports.31 In this article, we report a method in which the posterior surface of the medial epicondyle is osteotomized diagonally while taking care to ensure that the osteotomy line does not extend to the attachment of the flexor group and medial collateral ligament on the anterior side of the medial epicondyle. Furthermore, no recurrences occurred, even in long-term cases.

Various surgical techniques for treating CuTS have been reported.3,18,20,34 The basic principle of surgery is to decompress the ulnar nerve, secure the decompressed space for the nerve, and prevent recurrence. Excessive invasion of bone and soft tissue can cause additional complications and reduce patient satisfaction.

Medial epicondylar osteotomy is an excellent method for relieving nerve compression, but it has the disadvantages of causing muscle weakness and instability of the elbow because of the partial removal of muscles and ligaments.21,25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30 For this reason, efforts have been focused on minimizing the amount of tissue removal. Osteotomy in the anteroposterior direction inevitably involves removing muscles and ligaments; however, certain complications can occur.21,25,27,28,30 Posterior oblique osteotomy has the advantage of avoiding these complications because it does not involve osteotomy of the front of the medial epicondyle.

Two studies that evaluated the morphology of the retrocondylar ulnar groove, using multiplanar computed tomography, revealed that it has an inclination of approximately 100° and is a structure that contributes to stabilizing the ulnar nerve.35,36 However, this morphology may be a factor in the compression of the ulnar nerve. Therefore, an expectation is that oblique osteotomy of the posterior medial epicondyle would have the effect of preventing recompression by reducing this inclination, thereby causing the ulnar nerve to move slightly forward.31

In our postoperative results, the rate of improvement by ≥one levels in the McGowan classification31 was 90%, and the total of “excellent” and “good” scores in the Wilson and Krout classification was 94%. Our results were comparable to those of Osei et al, who performed the same surgical procedure as we did and reported that the rate of improvement was 74% in the McGowan classification and 93% in the Wilson and Krout classification. Kim et al30 reported “good” to “excellent” results in 84% patients, who had no evidence of ulnar nerve subluxation or elbow instability, using a similar coronal oblique osteotomy. Our findings are similar to the findings of Kim et al.

We also compared the objective findings before and after surgery and found significant improvements in perception and muscle strength after surgery. In addition, the QuickDASH and VAS showed improvements in patient-centered evaluation.

The specific complications of medial epicondylectomy include medial elbow pain (0%–52%), muscle weakness (0%–23%), medial elbow instability (0%–7%), and flexion contracture (0%–17%).25,29,37,38 The complication rate of this procedure may be relatively high, compared with that of other surgical treatments for ulnar nerve entrapment in the elbow. However, our method had a relatively low rate of complications. Furthermore, osteotomy eliminated snapping of the ulnar nerve by reducing the risk of additional transposition of the ulnar nerve after in situ decompression.

This study has several limitations. It was a retrospective study and had no control group. We did not compare the decompression-only procedure with the procedure that includes osteotomy. We consider this comparison an important task for future study. A strength of this study is that it reported a significant number of cases of oblique medial epicondylectomy, a procedure that is rarely reported, performed by a single surgeon. In addition, detailed assessments of sensation and muscle strength were conducted before and after surgery to examine the effects of improvement that do not appear in the McGowan grade or Wilson and Krout classification. The QuickDASH and VAS scores were also compared before and after surgery to evaluate the effects of improvement.

The postoperative results of the posterior oblique medial osteotomy for CuTS were good. The recovery of motor and sensory disturbance was equivalent to or better than that reported in previous studies using the same surgical procedure, and it had the advantages of few complications and early recovery.31
