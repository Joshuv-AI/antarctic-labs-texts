From Energy Deprivation Stress to Catalytic Adaptation of Reductive Dehalogenase TmrA for 1,1‐DCA Dechlorination: An Experimental and in Silico Study
Authors: Yi Ren, Evan Landers, Matthew Lee, Mike Manefield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOrganohalide‐respiring bacteria capable of metabolizing multiple organohalogens represent valuable tools for bioremediation and offer intriguing evolutionary potential for adaptation to non‐native substrates. In the present study, a chloroform respiring Dehalobacter restrictus strain was examined for its adaptability to a low affinity substrate (1,1‐dichloroethane) over five subcultures (~28 generations). We obtained an enhanced 11,1‐dichloroethane dechlorination rate by culturing with a 1:9 ratio of native (chloroform) to non‐native (1,1‐dichloroethane) substrates. We identified mutations corresponding to amino acids located in high‐entropy regions of the TmrA protein sequence, suggesting mutational plasticity at these sites. We revealed that the mutated TmrA structure showed increased binding affinity for 1,1‐dichloroethane by using molecular dynamics simulations and binding free energy computations. These findings provide insights into the adaptability of anaerobic organohalide‐respiring bacteria toward non‐native organohalogens and identify structural features of reductive dehalogenases that may be exploited for future enzyme engineering in bioremediation applications.

Introduction

Many enzymes exhibit catalytic promiscuity, enabling them to act on non‐native substrates in addition to their primary physiological targets (Khersonsky and Tawfik 2010). This versatility facilitates microbial adaptation by allowing the evolution of novel substrate utilization pathways and expansion of substrate range (Copley 2017). The short generation times of microorganisms increase the likelihood of genetic mutations which can enhance their ability to process novel substrates (Lynch et al. 2016; Elena and Lenski 2003).

Organochlorinated compounds represent a group of novel substrates arisen from anthropogenic activities. For example, the in situ trichloroethane (TCA) source can contaminate the groundwater for decades because of the slow dissolving rate and the high recalcitrance natural transformation rate (Scheutz et al. 2011). Moreover, the daughter 1,1‐dichloroethane (1,1‐DCA) has higher aqueous solubility and even higher recalcitrance than TCA (Scheutz et al. 2011). The recalcitrance of organochlorinated compounds for degradation can be attributed to high bond dissociation energy (Yu et al. 2025). Biodegradation represents a promising strategy for organochlorine remediation, and reductive dechlorination of TCA is faster than the abiotic hydrolysis and dehydrohalogenation (elimination) (Scheutz et al. 2011). Biodegradation of organochlorines is hardly degradable under oxic conditions because of induced oxidative stress for bacteria, which becomes a significant barrier for the evolution of aerobic pathways for chlorinated compounds (Nikel, Pérez‐Pantoja, and DE Lorenzo 2013). On the other hand, the anaerobic reductive dechlorination has more potential for adapting to novel organochlorinated compounds.

Organohalogen‐respiring bacteria are key players in the global halogen cycle and typically harbour multiple copies of reductive dehalogenase (RDase) genes (Jugder et al. 2015; Rupakula et al. 2013; Jugder, Ertan, Bohl, et al. 2016). Although RDases share highly conserved structural domains, even subtle amino acid changes can lead to significant shifts in substrate preference (Buttet et al. 2013). The resultant multiplicity in terminal respiratory enzymes broadens the range of electron acceptors that can be used for respiration and offers a palette for adaptive evolution to further expand substrate range for harvesting energy.

Dehalobacter restrictus strain UNSWDHB, isolated from chloroform (CF; trichloromethane)‐contaminated groundwater, encodes 17 full‐length and 3 truncated RDase genes (Lee et al. 2012; Deshpande et al. 2013). Among these, the tmrA gene encodes the chloroform reductase TmrA, which dechlorinates chloroform and 1,1,2‐trichloroethane, exhibiting high substrate affinity (K m = 30 μM and 62 μM, respectively) (Wong et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2012). TmrA also dechlorinates 1,1‐dichloroethane (1,1‐DCA) and 1,1,1‐trichloroethane (1,1,1‐TCA), but with lower substrate affinities (K m = 837 μM and 238 μM, respectively) (Wong et al. 2016). TmrA belongs to ortholog group (OG) 97 (Molenda et al. 2020; Picott et al. 2025), which is a classification method developed on the basis of a threshold of 90% pairwise amino acid identity in amino acid alignments between all members of a group (Hug et al. 2013). There are 7 characterized chloroalkane reductases (all have > 93% pairwise amino acid identity) with varying substrate specificities among a common group of chlorinated ethanes and methanes. A closely related OHRB, Dehalobacter sp. CF, isolated from 1,1,1‐TCA‐contaminated groundwater, encodes the chloroform reductase CfrA, sharing 95% amino acid identity with TmrA. CfrA exhibits high activity toward 1,1,1‐TCA but reduced activity toward CF compared to TmrA (Tang and Edwards 2013; Picott et al. 2022). Another RDase, DcrA, which shares 95% sequence identity with TmrA, is capable of dechlorinating 1,1‐DCA but shows no activity toward CF or 1,1,1‐TCA (Tang and Edwards 2013). CtrA (Desulfitobacterium sp. PR), sharing 93.85% sequence identity with TmrA, dechlorinates all three substrates, including CF, 1,1,1‐TCA, and 1,1‐DCA (Ding et al. 2014). ThmA (Dehalobacter sp. THM1) (Zhao et al. 2017), sharing 95.82% sequence identity with TmrA, has high activities for chloroform, bromoform, and 1,1,1‐TCA. AcdA (Dehalobacter sp. SAD) (Bulka et al. 2024; Bulka and Edwards 2024) can dechlorinate 1,1,1‐TCA and 1,1,2‐TCA. Given the similarities in sequence and differences in substrate affinities, these closely related dehalogenases represent an opportunity to explore genetic adaptation in the context of pollutant biotransformation, partly because their activities correspond to the contaminants at the sites they were discovered, potentially reflecting recent adaptation, but also because a small number of amino acids seem to underpin differences in substrate preference (Hug et al. 2013; Tang and Edwards 2013; Wong et al. 2016).

Previously, Guzman et al. (2019) demonstrated that Escherichia coli can acquire the ability to grow on non‐native electron donors (e.g., D‐arabinose) by being weaned off a native substrate (glycerol). Therefore, D. restrictus strain UNSWDHB could be adapted to an alternative electron acceptor (1,1‐DCA) by subculturing in the presence of different proportions of high‐affinity (CF) and low‐affinity (1,1‐DCA) substrates.

Mutations in genetic sequences occur randomly; however, amino acid residues located in less conserved regions, as identified through alignment of homologous sequences, tend to exhibit a higher likelihood of variation (Atchley et al. 2000; Ghanchi et al. 2021; Mullick et al. 2021). On the basis of this, it is hypothesized that variations in TmrA are more likely to occur in non‐conserved regions. Furthermore, adaptive mutations may alter the conformational dynamics of the enzyme, potentially enhancing catalytic efficiency. This improvement can be attributed to increased binding free energy between the enzyme and its substrate, which facilitates catalysis by stabilizing the substrate in an optimal orientation within the active site (Kua et al. 2002). Stronger ligand binding can reduce the activation energy barrier, thereby accelerating the reaction. Therefore, it is hypothesized that mutations acquired during adaptation to 1,1‐DCA as a non‐native substrate may enhance the binding affinity of TmrA for 1,1‐DCA, contributing to improved catalytic performance.

In the present study, we used an adaptive laboratory evolution experiment to improve the catalytic activity of D. restrictus strain UNSWDHB toward a non‐native substrate (1,1‐DCA). Mutations detected in tmrA were mapped onto a multiple‐sequence alignment of homologous RDases to assess residue conservation; positions with high conservation were considered less tolerant to amino‐acid change. How these mutations affect enzyme biophysical properties and 1,1‐DCA binding free energy was investigated by using molecular dynamics simulations. Collectively, these results can offer insights into the evolutionary adaptation of D. restrictus strain UNSWDHB toward a non‐native substrate and provide a framework for targeted bioremediation of xenobiotic contaminants.

Experimental Methods and Computation Details

Bacterial Strain

Dehalobacter restrictus strain UNSWDHB was inoculated from a previously described chloroform‐respiring pure culture, maintained in anaerobic glass bottles containing chloroform, H2, and acetate in a defined minimal medium (Lee et al. 2012). The cultures were incubated statically in the dark at 22°C under strictly anaerobic conditions. The original inoculum was derived from sediment samples collected from chloroform (trichloromethane) contaminated groundwater in Sydney, Australia. The initial culture has been maintained on chloroform as an electron acceptor for 15 years.

Culture Medium

The 100 mL minimal mineral medium used follows the same composition as described previously (Lee et al. 2012; Wong et al. 2016)—in brief (g l−1): NaHCO3 (2.5), NH4Cl (1.5), NaH2PO4 (0.6), CaCl2·2H2O (0.1), MgCl2·6H2O (0.1), 1 mL of trace element solution (1000×) (Löffler et al. 2005), 1 mL of vitamin solution (1000×) (Wolin et al. 1963), 1 mL of tungstate/selenate solution (1000×), and resazurin (0.25 mg l−1). H2 was used as the electron donor, which is supplied as 20% of the headspace volume. Sodium acetate was added as a carbon source with the final concentration at 10 mM. Yeast extract was added at a final concentration of 50 mg/L.

The culture medium was prepared by following previously described procedures (Lee et al. 2012; Wong et al. 2016). In brief: medium was distributed into culture flasks and sparged with N2/CO2 (4:1) gas for 20 min. The flasks were then sealed with Teflon‐faced rubber septa. Flask headspace was purged with N2/CO2 (4:1) gas for an additional 5 min. The medium was chemically reduced by the addition of an appropriate volume of a 20 mM stock sodium sulfide (Na2S.9H2O) solution to a final concentration of 0.2 mM. The cultures were sub‐cultured monthly, with 1 mL from the previous culture inoculated into the fresh culture (100× dilution).

Chlorinated solvents were added as electron acceptors, injected through septa with a glass syringe. Two types of chlorinated solvents were used, CF (trichloromethane, CAS: 67–66‐3) and 1,1‐DCA (1,1‐dichloroethane, CAS: 75–34‐3). CF was considered the native substrate because of the high specific activity (163 nmol.min−1.mg protein−1) and 1,1‐DCA as a non‐native substrate because of the low specific activity (13 nmol.min−1.mg protein−1) (Wong et al. 2016). To exert selective pressure, 3 ratios of chloroform and 1,1‐DCA were supplied. Group 1:99 had an approximately 1:99 ratio of CF to 1,1‐DCA (approx. 2 μmol and 150 μmol, respectively). Group 1:9 had an approximately 1:9 ratio of CF to 1,1‐DCA (approx. 18 μmol and 150 μmol, respectively), and Group 1:1 had an approximately 1:1 ratio of CF to 1,1‐DCA (approx. 80 μmol and 80 μmol, respectively). CF was resupplied weekly to maintain concentration. The cultures were sub‐cultured every 4 weeks, and there were 5 subcultures in total for the experiment. The doubling time of Dehalobacter was estimated as 5 days for chloroform‐respiring Dehalobacter sp. UNSWDHB under comparable conditions (Lee et al. 2012), which corresponds to 5.6 generations per transfer, or about 28 generations over the five subcultures.

Organochlorine Analysis

The quantity of organochlorines in anaerobic cultures was analysed by following the same method described previously (Lee et al. 2012, Wong et al. 2016). Briefly, headspace samples (100 μL) were withdrawn from culture flasks using a lockable gas‐tight syringe and manually injected into an Agilent 6890 N gas chromatograph (GC), equipped with a flame ionization detector (Garrison et al. 2015) and an Agilent J&W GS‐GasPro column. The GC inlet was set to 250°C with a split ratio of 1:10, total carrier gas (helium) flow of 36 mL/min, and a split flow of 30 mL/min. The back detector was maintained at 250°C with a hydrogen flow of 40 mL/min and an air flow of 450 mL/min. The oven temperature program began at 150°C and then increased at a rate of 30°C per min to 250°C, with no hold time.

DNA Extraction and Purification

Cells from liquid anaerobic cultures were collected by filtering through a 0.22 μm pore size PVDF 13 mm membrane filter from Millipore. Subsequently, the membrane filters were transferred to 2 mL Eppendorf tubes for DNA extraction. DNA was extracted using a phenol: chloroform DNA extraction protocol adapted from Mesapogu et al. (2012). The prepared DNA samples are subsequently further cleaned and concentrated by using the DNA Clean and Concentrator from ZYMO Research.

PCR

DNA extracts (2 μL) were added to 200 μL PCR tubes (from SSIbio UltraFlux) along with 10 μL Econo Taq PLUS Green (#F93481) Master Mix. The forward and reverse primers are designed using the NCBI primer designing tool. Forward primer (0.8 μL) for tmrA (5′‐TGG CTG CAA CTG ATT TTC GAC‐3′), 0.8 μL reverse primer for tmrA (5′‐TCC CAG GAC AAA AGT CAT CAG G‐3′) (produced by Integrated DNA technologies), and 0.4 μL Bovine Serum Albumin (Vanommeslaeghe et al. 2010). A BIO‐RAD T100 Thermal Cycler was used with cycling as follows: Step 1, 95°C 3 min, Step 2, 95°C 0.5 min, Step 3, 55°C 0.5 min, Step 4, 72°C 1.8 min, and goes back to Step 2 by 34 times, at last 72°C for 5 min. Sanger sequencing was carried out at the Ramaciotti Centre for Genomics at the University of New South Wales (UNSW). The primers were designed using the NCBI primer designing tool on the basis of the nucleotide sequence accession numbers: AUUR00000000 (Deshpande et al. 2013), and contig UNSW2932 for TmrA; the primers were designed to flank both ends of the sequence.

Shannon Entropy Calculation

The Shannon entropy was calculated by following the same method in a previous study (Mullick et al. 2021). Briefly: (1) Hi=−∑k∈LPki×log2Pki where L is a list of all possible amino acids in all the sequences, and P k (i) is the probability of finding the k th amino acid at the position. This equation was used to find the entropy for all the positions in the mutated TmrA amino acid sequence. Using the data set obtained from NCBI.

For the dataset, the TmrA sequence analogues were selected by using the wild‐type amino acid sequence of TmrA from D. restrictus strain UNSWDHB (Deshpande et al. 2013), a BLAST search was conducted using NCBI's tool to identify homologous reductive dehalogenases. Eleven sequences with high identity percentages (96.77%–98.47%) were selected, including two reductive dehalogenases from anaerobic chloroform and dichloromethane degrading consortia (sequencing id: CP148032.1, 98.47% identity, CP148031.1, 98.32% identity) (Bulka and Edwards 2024), CfrA (sequencing id: JX282329.1, 98.32% identity) (Tang and Edwards 2013), ThmA (Sequence ID: KX344907.1, 97.96% identity) (Zhao et al. 2017), CfrA (Sequence ID: CP003870.1, 97.96% identity) (Tang et al. 2012), DcrA (Sequence ID: JX282330.1, 97.67%) (Tang and Edwards 2013), DcrA (Sequence ID: CP003869.1, 97.67% identity) (Tang et al. 2012), CtrA (Sequence ID: KF155162.1, 97.16% identity) (Ding et al. 2014), CfrA‐like (Sequence ID: MT536772.1, 97.73% identity, Sequence ID: MT536773.1, 97.49% identity), and DcrA‐like (Sequence ID: MT536774.1, 97.73% identity). These 11 sequences are TmrA‐like reductive dehalogenases (96.77%–98.47% identity), including homologues such as CfrA and DcrA, and were used solely as a sequence set for the Shannon entropy analysis, without assuming identical substrate specificity (e.g., 1,1‐DCA reduction) for all members.

Quantitative PCR

The abundance of D. restrictus strain UNSWDHB in the cultures was determined using quantitative PCR (qPCR), following the procedures in Holland et al. (2021). The primers used for quantitative PCR were Eub1084 Forward Primer, 5′‐GTGSTGCAYGGYTGTCGTCA‐3′ and Eub1194 Reverse Primer, 5′‐ACGTCRTCCMCACCTTCCTC‐3′ (Holland et al. 2021). Standard curves were prepared in triplicate by making serial tenfold dilutions of plasmid DNA carrying cloned Dehalococcoides sp. 16S rRNA (for total bacterial quantification). Reactions were carried out on a CFX96 thermal cycler (Biorad) according to a previous method (Holland et al. 2021), and the data were analysed with CFX Maestro v1.0 software (Bio‐Rad). The cell quantity values, reported in Table S7, correspond to 16S rRNA gene copies per bottle (i.e., per culture), on the basis of the total DNA extracted from each bottle.

Comparison of Normalized Chloroethane Production Between the Wild‐Type Group and Adapted Groups

Cultures with fresh medium were prepared with the same composition in the present study, with bacteria inoculated 1 mL of the corresponding groups into separate cultures. The cultures were triplicated, including wild‐type group, Group 1:99, Group 1:9, and Group 1:1. The 100 mL fresh medium contained 1,1‐DCA as the sole chlorinated electron acceptor. No chloroform or mixed electron‐acceptor ratios were added at this stage. Thus, all specific rates correspond to 1,1‐DCA dechlorination under identical 1,1‐DCA‐only conditions for the wild‐type and each adapted culture. The cultures were cultivated for 2 weeks; the chloroethane (CA) production was calculated for the second week by subtracting the first week's CA production. CA productions were normalized to cell abundance, which was determined at the end of the two‐week incubation using a quantitative qPCR method.

Predicted Reductive Dehalogenase Enzymes Structures

To compare the reductive dehalogenase of Dehalobacter UNSWDHB TmrA and the 1,1‐DCA degrading enzymes CtrA and DcrA, these enzymes were predicted by using the AlphaFold2 algorithm (Jumper et al. 2021), with the amino acid sequence (wild‐type TmrA, CtrA, and DcrA) obtained from the ortholog group 97 of Reductive Dehalogenase Database (Molenda et al. 2020; Picott et al. 2025). All predicted enzyme structures were subsequently processed using the PDB Reader module in CHARMM‐GUI (Jo et al. 2008), with the pH set to 7 to assign appropriate protonation states. The AlphaFold2‐predicted structures of TmrA, CtrA, and DcrA were validated by structural comparison with the crystal structure of PceA (PDB ID: 4UQU). The validation results are presented in Figure S7 of the Supporting Information and show an agreement with the PceA crystal structure.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation

To calculate the binding free energy of TmrA and ligand with molecular dynamics simulation, the enzyme cofactor and ligands need to be parameterized. The cobalamin, as the cofactor of TmrA, and the ligand 1,1‐DCA were parameterized at B3LYP/6–311G(d,p) level of theory (B3LYP‐D3/6–311 + g(2d,2p) for ligand) (Becke 1993; Frisch et al. 1984; Grimme 2010; Krishnan et al. 1980; Stephens et al. 1994) using Gaussian 09 (Frisch et al. 2009). According to the previous study, at the reactant state Co in cobalamin is in the +2 oxidation state (Liao et al. 2016); therefore, the charge of cobalamin was set as 0 and spin as doublet.

By using the Sobtop package (Lu 2026), the generalized AMBER force field (GAFF) (Wang et al. 2004) was used for the atom types of 1,1‐DCA. The detailed forcefield parameters of the 1,1‐DCA, including atomic names, charges, bonds, angles and dihedrals, are shown in the Figure S1 and Table S1 and Table S2. For the atom types of the cobalamin cofactor, the universal force field (UFF) atom type (Rappé et al. 1992) was applied for the cobalt atom, and the GAFF atom type was applied for the rest of the cobalamin atoms. A similar approach of assigning UFF atom type for metal and GAFF for other atoms was also adopted by other studies (Keot and Sarma 2024; Li et al. 2024). The atomic charges were calculated with Restrained Electro Static Potential (RESP) by using the Multiwfn wave function program (Lu and Chen 2012). The detailed forcefield parameters of the cobalamin, including atomic names, charges, bonds, angles and dihedrals, are shown in the Tables S3 and S4 and Figure S2. The cobalamin cofactor parameters were validated by comparing the bond distance of Co and the connected N atoms, and the angles of connected N, Co and N of neighbouring. The validation results are shown in the Figures S3–S5 and Table S5.

The TmrA enzyme structure was simulated with Amber forcefield ff14SB (Maier et al. 2015) and the protonation state was assigned under 7.0 of pH value by using the CHARMM‐GUI platform (Jo et al. 2008). All the systems were solvated in a cubic box of TIP3P water molecules (Jorgensen et al. 1983), with a dimension of 89 Å in x, y and z dimensions (10 Å offset from the boundary of enzyme to the boundary of the water box). The charge of the systems was neutralized to zero by adding 2 sodium ions.

All the simulations and the analysis of the trajectories were performed with GROMACS software version 2024.3‐gpuvolta package (Abraham et al. 2015). All the systems were energy‐relaxed with 1000 steps of steepest‐descent energy minimization. The systems were then equilibrated by carrying out the process in two stages. Initially, an equilibration of the canonical ensemble (NVT) at 303.15 K using the V‐rescale thermostat (Bussi et al. 2007) is simulated for 4 ns (ns) using a time step of 1 femtoseconds (fs). This was followed by equilibration of the isobaric‐isothermal ensemble (NPT), where the pressure was maintained at 1 bar by using the Parrinello‐Rahman barostat (Parrinello and Rahman 1981; Ke et al. 2022), with an isotropic pressure coupling type. The NPT equilibrations are set as 4 stages with 2 ns for each, and the time step is set as 1 fs. The position restraints were applied to the backbone and side chains, starting with force constants of 400 and 40 kJ/mol/nm2, respectively, which were gradually reduced by 100 and 10, respectively. The position restraints for the ligands were also applied with a force constant of 500 kJ/mol/nm2, which was reduced by 125 throughout each stage of equilibration. At the last stage of equilibration, the system was fully relaxed. The Verlet cutoff scheme (Grubmüller et al. 1991) was employed. The cutoff radius for short‐range van der Waals interactions was set to 1.2 nm. The long‐range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle‐Mesh Ewald (PME) method (Petersen 1995). All hydrogen‐related atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm (Hess et al. 1997).

After equilibration, 100 ns production runs were launched using a 2 fs time step, which was sufficient for the purpose of binding free energy calculation (Zhang et al. 2022). The SHAKE algorithm (Ryckaert et al. 1977) was used to constrain all bonds involving hydrogen atoms. The molecular dynamics production stages are triplicated independently for reproducibility.

The binding free energy of the enzyme with ligand in solvent can be expressed as follows: in Equation (2) (Kollman et al. 2000; Gilson and Honig 1988) and Equation (3) (Kollman et al. 2000; Kuhn and Kollman 2000): (2) ∆Gbind=∆Gcomplex−∆Gprotein−∆Glig (3) ∆Gbind=∆H−T∆S where ∆H of the system is composed of the enthalpy changes in the gas phase upon complex formation (∆EMM) and the solvated free energy contribution ∆Gsol, whereas −T∆S refers to the entropy contribution to the binding free energy (Chaires 2008), which was not included for calculation in the present study. Equation (3) can then be approximated, as shown in Equation (4): (4) ∆Gbind=∆EMM+∆Gsol−T∆S where ∆EMM is composed of the bonded energy, the van der Waals (∆Evdw) energy and the electrostatic (∆Eele) energy, where the bonded energy is assumed to be zero for a single‐trajectory approach (Kumari et al. 2014; Homeyer and Gohlke 2012). Therefore, ∆EMM can be expressed as in Equation (5): (5) ∆EMM=∆Evdw+∆Eele The solvation free energy ∆Gsol can be computed by the contributions from polar solvation energy (∆Gpolar) and (∆Gnonpolar), which are the electrostatic and non‐electrostatic contributions to the solvation free energy, respectively, shown as in Equation (6) (Still et al. 1990; Gilson and Honig 1988): (6) ∆Gsol=∆Gpolar+∆Gnonpolar=∆GPB+∆Gnonpolar The ∆Gpolar is estimated by solving the Poisson−Boltzmann (PB) equation. ∆Gnonpolar is calculated by using the solvent‐accessible surface area (SASA) model (Sitkoff et al. 1994).

The enthalpic components of the binding free energies are calculated with the molecular mechanics Poisson−Boltzmann surface area (MM‐PBSA) approach (Homeyer and Gohlke 2012) by using the GMX_mmpbsa tool (Valdes‐Tresanco et al. 2021) with the default parameters implemented. A total number of 1000 snapshots were sampled evenly from the entire trajectory of each system. For each system, the simulation was conducted three independent times, and the computation results were averaged from the three independent simulations. We did not include an explicit entropic term (−TΔS) (Chaires 2008) in the MM/PBSA calculation. Normal‐mode or quasi‐harmonic entropy calculations for large protein systems are computationally expensive and with limited convergence, which can increase uncertainty and degrade the calculation results of the present methods (Wang et al. 2017). Therefore, we interpret our values as enthalpy‐dominated trends.

The trajectories are analysed by using gmx trjconv. The Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), Solvent Accessible Surface Area and radius of gyration are calculated by gmx rms, gmx sasa and gmx gyrate, respectively. The van der Waals contacts are counted by using gmx mindist, and contact was defined if the distance between the two atoms was ≤ 4 Å, as used previously (Nie et al. 2021).

Results

Comparison of Degradation Activities towards the Native and Non‐Native Substrate

To examine whether subcultivation of D. restrictus strain UNSWDHB with 1,1‐DCA as an electron acceptor can improve the consumption rate of 1,1‐DCA, three experimental conditions were established using varying ratios of chloroform (CF, native substrate) and 1,1‐DCA (low‐affinity substrate), namely, 1:99, 1:9, and 1:1 M ratios of CF to 1,1‐DCA. CF was consumed rapidly and replenished weekly to support the continued growth of D. restrictus strain UNSWDHB. Over five sequential rounds of subculturing, the consumption of electron acceptors and the production of dechlorination products were monitored (Figure 1).

Dehalogenation activity was monitored over five sequential subcultures, each lasting 4 weeks. Using an estimated doubling time of ~5 days for chloroform‐respiring Dehalobacter sp. UNSWDHB under comparable conditions (Lee et al. 2012), this corresponds to roughly 5.6 generations per transfer, or about 28 generations over the five subcultures. We therefore avoid over‐interpreting long‐term evolutionary change on the basis of generation count, but instead base inference on phenotype, qPCR‐normalized specific rates, and sequence/structure analyses.

To investigate how CA production changes over the subcultures, the CA production quantity was normalized to the initial 1,1‐DCA supplied in each bottle, and how this CA production proportion to 1,1‐DCA changed with each transfer was examined (Figure 2, last panel). The proportion of CA production to the added 1,1‐DCA is shown in the Table S6. Subculture 5 was removed from the figure because of the shorter incubation period of the subculture, which can artificially lower the CA production and affect the conclusion. In group 1:99, the CA proportion decreased markedly for the first 2 subcultures (from ~0.044 to ~0.015 mol CA per mol initial 1,1‐DCA, p < 0.05), and then remained low in the subsequent subcultures. Both group 1:9 and group 1:1 maintained a relatively high and stable proportion for the first 3 subcultures, followed by a slight decline in Subculture 4, with no significant differences between successive transfers. Taken together, these trends indicate that under the most stringent regimen (1:99), the culture progressively loses its capacity to convert 1,1‐DCA to CA over transfers, whereas the group 1:9 with intermediate suffocation stress sustains a relatively higher conversion fraction across subcultures.

To directly compare the 1,1‐DCA dechlorination capacity of wild‐type and adapted cultures, all lines were transferred into medium containing 1,1‐DCA as the sole electron acceptor, and specific rates were determined under these identical conditions. As shown in Figure 3B, Group 1:9 exhibited the highest CA production rate (0.0124 ± 0.00241 nmol/cell/day), which was significantly higher than that of the wild‐type group (0.0048 ± 0.00123 nmol/cell/day). These data indicate that Group 1:9 developed an enhanced capacity for 1,1‐DCA dechlorination. Group 1:99 also showed a significantly higher CA production rate compared to Group 1:1 (p‐value 0.02). However, neither Group 1:99 (0.00625 ± 0.000631 nmol/cell/day) nor Group 1:1 (0.0028 ± 0.000098 nmol/cell/day) showed a significant difference when compared to the wild‐type. Overall, these results suggest that cultivation under a 1:9 CF to 1,1‐DCA ratio led to the most pronounced improvement in normalized CA production relative to the wild‐type.

Reductive Dehalogenase Mutations Occur in High‐Entropy Regions

On the basis of the genetic sequencing results, six amino acid substitutions located near the active site of TmrA were identified in the adapted cultures compared to the original starting culture (Figure 6B). Three substitutions (Val84Ile, Glu255Asp, and Ala267Val) were present in all three adapted groups, Glu174Lys and Glu175Lys were detected only in the 1:1 and 1:9 groups, and Met183Arg was observed exclusively in the 1:9 group. To test the hypothesis that these mutations occur at flexible, adaptive regions of the enzyme, sites prone to variation, Shannon entropy analysis was performed. This analysis quantifies sequence variability by measuring the entropy at each amino acid position in a multiple sequence alignment of homologous RDases. Higher entropy values indicate greater sequence variability and mutational tolerance at a given position, whereas lower values reflect sequence conservation and structural or functional constraints. Thus, positions with high Shannon entropy are considered more adaptable and potentially more susceptible to functional diversification through mutation.

The blue line indicates the variation (entropy) at each position. Higher entropy values correspond to greater sequence variability and mutational tolerance, whereas lower values indicate conserved, stable regions. Red vertical lines mark the positions of mutations identified in this study that correspond to the entropy region (Val84Ile, Met183Arg, and Ala267Val). As shown in Figure 6A, both the Val84Ile, Met183Arg and Ala267Val mutations are located within high‐entropy regions (highlighted in red) on the basis of the alignment with closely related RDase sequences. The Val84Ile mutation is relatively common, occurring in 5 out of 11 homologous sequences identified through NCBI BLAST with high sequence identity (96.77% to 98.47%). At position 183, there are 8 mutations out of 11 homologous as Ile, whereas the Arg mutation at position 183 observed in the present study is unique. At position 267, a substitution to proline (Ala267Pro) was observed in 3 of the 11 aligned sequences, whereas the Ala267Val mutation identified in this study is unique. Although no mutation was directly observed at position 255 in the aligned RDase sequences, this site lies within a mutation‐dense region. Notably, position 254, which is adjacent to Glu255, exhibited the highest entropy value (1.199), with observed substitutions to threonine, serine, or valine among the aligned sequences. The positions at 174 and 175 are located in the consistently conversed region with 0 entropy values.

Discussion

Promiscuous activity is a common feature among enzymes (Khersonsky and Tawfik 2010; Copley 2021) and serves as a critical evolutionary foundation for the development of new catalytic functions (Copley 2021). In the context of organohalogen pollutant remediation, Dehalobacter restrictus strain UNSWDHB, isolated from chloroform‐contaminated groundwater (Lee et al. 2012), encodes the reductive dehalogenase TmrA, which catalyses the reductive dechlorination of both chloroform, with high substrate affinity (K m = 30 μM), and 1,1‐dichloroethane (1,1‐DCA), with considerably lower substrate affinity (K m = 837 μM) (Wong et al. 2016). This dual activity underscores the potential of TmrA as a versatile biocatalyst for environmental remediation and provides a valuable model for investigating the adaptability of D. restrictus strain UNSWDHB toward the non‐native substrate 1,1‐DCA.

To assess the adaptability of Dehalobacter restrictus strain UNSWDHB towards the non‐native substrate 1,1‐dichloroethane (1,1‐DCA), the present study cultivated the strain under varying ratios of native (chloroform, CF) to non‐native (1,1‐DCA) substrates: 1:99, 1:9 and 1:1. CF was provided to support cell replication, whereas 1,1‐DCA served as a potential alternative electron acceptor to allow the cells to harvest additional energy. Although we did not include dedicated abiotic controls in each transfer, the literature reports no dechlorination of 1,1‐DCA (or 1,1,1‐TCA/CF) in heat‐killed/no‐enzyme controls for Dehalobacter RDases, whereas live cultures dechlorinate 1,1‐DCA (Tang and Edwards 2013). Our carbon‐equivalent balances (Figure S8) were generally within ~80%–120%, indicating that the observed 1,1‐DCA and CF degradation was largely consistent with stoichiometric formation of DCM/CA. However, some bottle‐specific datasets showed a downward trend over time, most notably in the Subculture 4 experiments. Therefore, the mass‐balance results should be interpreted as generally constrained but not uniformly stable across all conditions. Because an abiotic control was not included in this study, the source of the observed decreases in some bottles cannot be resolved unambiguously. In particular, we cannot distinguish between physical non‐biological losses (e.g., leakage, volatilization, sorption or sampling‐related losses) and transformation to unmeasured products. Accordingly, we interpret the mass‐balance data cautiously and do not use them as the primary basis for mechanistic conclusions.

The doubling time of Dehalobacter was estimated as 5 days for chloroform‐respiring Dehalobacter sp. UNSWDHB under comparable conditions (Lee et al. 2012). Over five subcultures, corresponding to approximately 28 generations, CF degradation and dichloromethane (DCM) accumulation were observed across all groups and were consistent with previous findings, indicating sustained catalytic activity (Wong et al. 2016). The accumulation of chloroethane (CA), the dechlorination product of 1,1‐DCA, reached a plateau by week four, in agreement with the results reported by Wong et al. (2016). Notably, the 1:9 group exhibited a higher CA production rate compared to the wild‐type group, consistent with a stable, enhanced capacity of 1,1‐DCA dechlorination.

The adaptive evolution of Dehalobacter restrictus strain UNSWDHB in the present study resulted in a subtle but quantifiable improvement in its dechlorination activity toward 1,1‐DCA, indicating the metabolic plasticity of OHRB when electron acceptors are limiting. Previous studies have reported similar substrate promiscuity among other OHRB. For instance, Desulfitobacterium sp. strain Y51 in addition to its native substrates tetrachloroethene (PCE) and trichloroethene (TCE), demonstrated activity toward polychlorinated ethanes with a high degree of chlorination (Suyama et al. 2002). Similarly Dehalococcoides mccartyi (formally Dehalococcoides ethenogenes) has been shown to dechlorinate a range of haloalkanes and haloalkenes containing three to five carbon atoms, albeit at lower rates compared to its native substrates (Magnuson et al. 2000). These findings collectively suggest that OHRB possess broad substrate‐recognition potential, which can be unveiled or enhanced through adaptive evolution.

The results demonstrated that Group 1:9 exhibited the most effective adaptation of D. restrictus strain UNSWDHB to 1,1‐DCA as a non‐native substrate, despite not experiencing the highest level of energy deprivation stress compared to Group 1:99. In the 1:99 group, the more intense energy limitation led to an unsustainably low biomass, which likely impeded the adaptation process because of limited proliferation of beneficial mutations (Ueda et al. 2017). Previous studies have shown that, under energy deprivation stress, microbial cells initially adapt by modulating the concentrations of cellular components (such as enzyme expression levels) prior to the emergence of genomic mutations over successive generations (Furusawa and Kaneko 2015). Changes in cellular component concentrations during adaptation are often constrained along the components exhibiting the greatest sensitivity (those showing the largest relative changes) (Furusawa and Kaneko 2018; Kaneko and Furusawa 2018; Furusawa et al. 2005). This suggests that increased expression of specific enzymes necessary for adapting to non‐native substrates imposes a metabolic cost. Such costs may manifest as reduced growth rates because of the diversion of limited cellular resources toward adaptation rather than biomass production. In the present study, the high intensity of energy deprivation stress in Group 1:99 led to a significantly lower biomass, likely as a result of this metabolic trade‐off. The limited biomass in turn hindered sustained adaptation, as fewer cells reduced the likelihood of propagating beneficial mutations. Therefore, although high stress may provide strong selective pressure, it can also compromise long‐term adaptive potential by constraining growth and population size.

TmrA is a membrane‐bound enzyme capable of directly accessing its substrates (Jugder, Ertan, Wong, et al. 2016; Jugder et al. 2017; Bommer et al. 2014), thereby bypassing the need for dedicated transport mechanisms for substrate delivery. For this reason, the present study focused DNA sequencing efforts on the tmrA gene, under the hypothesis that mutations arising during adaptation could directly influence substrate binding and catalysis. Three mutations consistently appeared in three groups, two mutations both appeared in two groups (1:9 and 1:1), and unique for the group 1:9. To assess whether the observed mutations occurred at flexible nucleotide sites prone to variation or within structurally or functionally conserved regions, the mutated TmrA amino acid sequence was aligned with several closely related RDases sharing high sequence identity. Shannon entropy was calculated for each position in the alignment, which is a metric derived from information theory that quantifies sequence variability at individual sites (Bywater 2015). Higher entropy values indicate greater variability across sequences, whereas lower values reflect evolutionary conservation (Vinga 2014). The results of this study revealed that, besides the mutations at positions 174 and 175, all the rest of identified mutations were located within high‐entropy regions, suggesting they occurred at sites naturally prone to variation. This finding is consistent with previous studies. For example, an analysis of 242 protein sequences within the basic helix–loop–helix domain of a transcription factor family (Atchley et al. 2000) showed that residues involved in structural packing (and thus with conserved function) exhibited low entropy, whereas exposed, non‐contact sites displayed significantly higher entropy. Similarly, research on the SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein revealed that mutations associated with increased virulence were more likely to arise in high‐entropy regions (Ghanchi et al. 2021; Mullick et al. 2021), indicating a tendency for adaptive changes to occur at flexible, less conserved positions under selective pressure (Mullick et al. 2021). In the present study, the correlation between the identified TmrA mutations and high‐entropy regions suggests that adaptation occurred through modifications at inherently variable sites. This likely enabled functional fine‐tuning of enzymatic activity without compromising the core catalytic architecture of the protein.

To investigate the influence of the identified mutations on the biophysical properties of TmrA, molecular dynamics simulations were performed to calculate the binding free energy between wild‐type or mutated TmrA and 1,1‐DCA. The results revealed that the mutated TmrA from all three groups exhibited a significantly stronger binding free energy toward 1,1‐DCA, with the Group 1:9 has the highest improvement. These structural changes may contribute to the improved catalytic efficiency observed in the adapted enzyme (Lesk and Chothia 1980; Sprang et al. 1979; Bogatyreva and Ivankov 2008). The more favourable polar solvation energy [the energy cost of desolvating polar groups (Homeyer and Gohlke 2012)] was also found to improve the binding free energy. Simulation results further revealed that the Val84Ile, Ala267Val and Met183Arg substitutions can lead to an increase in intra‐protein van der Waals contacts between neighbouring secondary‐structure elements, consistent with a more compact local fold. In contrast, the Glu174Lys and Glu175Lys substitutions did not appreciably change van der Waals contacts intra‐protein van der Waals contacts between neighbouring secondary‐structure elements, likely because these residues are located on the protein surface and their side chains point outward from the TmrA. Although Glu174Lys, Glu175Lys and Met183Arg involve changes in side‐chain charge, these residues lie outside the immediate substrate‐binding pocket. Consistent with this, hydrogen‐bond analysis shows broadly similar occupancies and time evolution for wild‐type and mutant residues at these positions (Figure S9), suggesting that their primary effect is to fine‐tune local packing rather than to markedly alter polar interactions at the active site. Enhanced van der Waals interactions can promote tighter enzyme folding (Newberry and Raines 2019; Bogatyreva and Ivankov 2008), contributing to a more compact enzyme structure. In addition to global effects, these mutations also increased van der Waals contacts specifically with key active site residues in TmrA, potentially improving substrate positioning and binding stability. The improved overall packing of the TmrA can reduce the polar surface area that could move charged residues further from the deeply buried ligand. Previous study shows the mutation can rearrange polar and non‐polar interactions, which can induce binding free energy changes and therefore reduce the polar solvation penalty (Kar et al. 2011; Cavani et al. 2022). 1,1‐DCA is the native substrate of CtrA and DcrA, which share 97.16% and 97.67% amino acid identity with TmrA (Tang et al. 2012; Ding et al. 2014). The binding free energies of CtrA and DcrA with 1,1‐DCA are stronger than that of wild type TmrA because of reduced polar solvation penalty. The active site of CtrA and DcrA was aligned with the mutated TmrA from Group 1:9 to probe the amino acid difference (Figure S10) and found that 4 positions are not identical, among which 3 of them are all similar hydrophobic residues (Phe and Trp). Only the position 260 has different polar residues and hydrophobic residue (Tyr, Cys and Phe). Other different residues outside the binding pocket could contribute to the reduced polar solvation penalty.

It has been shown that during the degradation of unnaturally occurring organochlorine, toxic and/or highly reactive oxygen species can be accumulated because of the absence of enzymes for the rapid conversion of the resulting halogenated metabolites, which can cause damage to cells (Nikel, PEREZ‐Pantoja, and DE Lorenzo 2013). Similarly, the formation of reactive oxygen species was also observed in Burkholderia sp. DNT and Pseudomonas putida EM173 while adapting for 2,4‐dinitrotoluene biodegradation (Perez‐Pantoja et al. 2013; Akkaya et al. 2018). During evolution, the still‐evolving enzyme is not yet optimal for the novel substrate, resulting in faulty substrate‐enzyme recognition and incomplete reaction, which releases reactive oxygen species. Such endogenous stress limits the viability of bacteria while adapting to novel substrates. Although the present study did not explore the reactive oxygen species formation, the substrate ligand binding free energy calculation results of binding free energy with the native indicate that enhanced 1,1‐DCA binding does not come at the expense of CF affinity.

This finding aligns with previous studies showing that even a few amino acid substitutions in an RDase can markedly alter its substrate specificity. For instance, the chloroalkane RDases CfrA and DcrA share 95% amino acid sequence identity, yet differ significantly in substrate preference: wild‐type CfrA dechlorinates chloroform and 1,1,1‐trichloroethane (1,1,1‐TCA) but not 1,1‐dichloroethane (1,1‐DCA), whereas DcrA effectively dechlorinates 1,1‐DCA but not chloroform (Tang and Edwards 2013). Remarkably, the substrate specificity of CfrA can be shifted toward 1,1‐DCA through just two mutations (Tyr80Trp and Phe125Trp) (Picott et al. 2025), which increases vdW interactions with the ligand. The introduction of bulkier tryptophan residues enhances vdW stabilization of the smaller 1,1‐DCA molecule, illustrating how subtle structural changes can significantly influence RDase function.

Broadening the enzymatic repertoire of OHRB is particularly important given the complexity of many contaminated environments. Groundwater plumes often contain mixtures of halogenated compounds that require the cooperative activity of diverse microbial communities for complete detoxification (Liang et al. 2021). For example, the full dechlorination of trichloroethene (TCE) to ethene by a Dehalococcoides mixed culture was enhanced by the addition of Dehalococcoides strain BAV1, which expresses the BvcA enzyme capable of dechlorinating cis‐dichloroethene and vinyl chloride to ethene (Futagami et al. 2011). Dehalococcoides strain BAV1 was obtained from continued transfer over 4 year as a vinyl chloride growth‐supporting electron acceptor (He et al. 2003). In the present study, adaptive laboratory evolution was employed to select for D. restrictus strain UNSWDHB variants with an enhanced and stably maintained capacity to dechlorinate the anthropogenic halocarbon 1,1‐DCA under conditions where 1,1‐DCA is supplied as the sole chlorinated electron acceptor, consistent with its use as a growth‐supporting respiratory substrate. This phenotypic optimization was achieved without the use of genetic engineering—a notable advantage, given that strict anaerobes such as Dehalobacter spp. are not readily amenable to routine recombinant DNA manipulation. These findings highlight the potential of adaptive laboratory evolution as a practical strategy to enhance the biodegradation capabilities of OHRB for the remediation of recalcitrant pollutants. A limitation of this study is the absence of an abiotic control, which prevents definitive separation of biological transformation from non‐biological physical losses and/or unmeasured transformation products in the bottle‐level mass‐balance analysis. Future experiments should include abiotic controls (and, where feasible, additional product accounting) to resolve the source of the observed declines in selected bottles. Importantly, the central objective of the present work was to examine microbial adaptation to a novel chlorinated substrate and the associated RDase sequence/biophysical changes, and our main conclusions regarding adaptive trends are on the basis of the combined cultivation, genomic and in silico analyses rather than on a closed mass‐balance interpretation alone.

Conclusion

In conclusion, reductive dehalogenases (RDases) in OHRB possess highly conserved amino acid architectures that support catalytic activity toward structurally related organohalogens, though with varying substrate affinities. In this study, an adaptive laboratory evolution experiment spanning five subcultures (~28 generations) demonstrated that Dehalobacter restrictus strain UNSWDHB can adapt to utilize 1,1‐dichloroethane (1,1‐DCA) as a non‐native substrate under different levels of energy deprivation stress. Notably, the 1:9 ratio of native (chloroform) to non‐native (1,1‐DCA) substrate yielded the highest 1,1‐DCA degradation rate reported here for the first time. Sequencing of the tmrA gene revealed that 4 out of 6 mutations are located within high‐entropy regions, suggesting that these residues are naturally prone to variation. This study also provides the first evidence that these mutations enhance binding affinity for 1,1‐DCA. Molecular dynamics simulations showed that the mutated TmrA of the Group 1:9 has the greatest improvement in binding free energy for 1,1‐DCA, with the highest global flexibility of the TmrA structure and a more stable active site. The individual mutations Val84Ile, Met183Arg, and Ala267Val can increase the van der Waals contact with the enzyme structure. Together, these findings highlight the evolutionary plasticity of RDases and offer mechanistic insights into their potential for engineering and application in bioremediation of recalcitrant halogenated pollutants.
