Frugal innovation in adaptive seating for a child with cerebral palsy (Gross Motor Function Classification System Level V) in a resource-limited setting: a case report
Authors: Thavashnee Moodley, Silindile Brightness Khoza, Verusia Chetty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCerebral palsy (CP) classified as Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) Level V results in severe postural impairment and complete dependence on external support for upright sitting. In low- and middle-income countries such as South Africa, access to specialised adaptive seating devices within the public healthcare system is severely limited. Frugal innovation the adaptation of readily available, affordable resources to meet complex rehabilitation needs represents a promising strategy to address this gap.Case presentationWe report the case of a 12-year-old male (pseudonym: Jessie) with a confirmed diagnosis of CP GMFCS Level V, born at 28 weeks gestation following antepartum haemorrhage. He presented with profound generalised hypotonia, absent independent trunk stability, poor head control, postural asymmetry in sitting, complete dependence on caregivers for all activities of daily living, and bilateral visual impairment. He had outgrown his transporter buggy (a caregiver-propelled basic pram-style mobility device) and was unable to maintain a safe upright sitting position in a standard basic folding frame wheelchair due to postural collapse. Baseline assessment using the validated Posture and Postural Ability Scale (PPAS) yielded a total score of 3/12 (frontal: 1/6; sagittal: 2/6). A commercially available car seat was selected based on systematic individual anthropometric and postural assessment. Low-cost modifications were applied using repurposed materials: a trunk control harness (repurposed buggy fabric), a seat belt extension, an abduction wedge (sponge foam), a polystyrene footrest, and a wheelchair attachment system (Velcro straps). A built-in four-position tilt-in-space feature was used to optimise hip flexion angle. The total material cost was negligible. Following the intervention, PPAS scores improved to 12/12 (frontal: 6/6; sagittal: 6/6), representing improved postural alignment as measured by PPAS across both planes.ConclusionsA commercially available car seat, systematically modified using low-cost repurposed materials through a structured, physiotherapy-guided fitting process, achieved improved postural alignment as measured by PPAS in a child with GMFCS Level V CP. This case provides proof-of-concept that structured, physiotherapy-led modification of commercially available car seats can achieve significant postural correction in children with GMFCS Level V cerebral palsy in low-resource settings.

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is the most common childhood physical disability worldwide, characterised by permanent disorders of movement and posture resulting from non-progressive damage to the developing brain [1–4]. The global prevalence is substantially higher in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), ranging from 2 to 10 per 1,000 live births compared to approximately 1.6 per 1,000 in high-income countries (HICs) [1, 2]. In African contexts, the risk profile for CP is shaped by socio-economic and environmental factors including poverty, malnutrition, high infectious disease burden, and limited access to healthcare factors that collectively increase both prevalence and severity of disability [1, 4].

Children classified as GMFCS Level V have the most severe functional limitations of all CP subtypes. They are unable to maintain anti-gravity postures independently, require maximal external support for all activities of daily living, and depend entirely on wheeled mobility devices [5]. Without adequate postural support, these children are at significant risk of developing secondary complications including spinal deformities, hip dislocations, joint contractures, respiratory compromise, and aspiration [6, 7]. Appropriate adaptive seating is therefore a clinical necessity, associated with improved posture, safer feeding, better respiratory function, and reduced caregiver burden [8–10].

The World Health Organization and UNICEF report that fewer than 3% of people requiring assistive technology in LMICs have access to them, compared to up to 90% in some HICs. [11]. This refers to assistive technology broadly, not specific to assistive devices but highlights the unmet needs of assistive technology in resource limited settings. In the public healthcare system of KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, budget constraints and limited seating expertise mean that children with CP at GMFCS Level V who outgrow their transporter buggies are frequently provided with only a basic sling-back folding frame wheelchair that provide no postural support and are clinically inappropriate for this population. This observation is based on the authors clinical experience within the regional public service sector.

Frugal innovation the adaptation of readily available resources to meet complex needs at minimal cost represents a promising strategy for bridging this gap. Barton et al. demonstrated that locally produced paper-based seating devices improved posture in Kenyan children with severe CP [12]. Findings from an unpublished mixed methods study conducted at GJGMRH involving 20 children with CP classified as GMFCS IV and V suggest improvements in sitting posture scores following the use of unmodified commercially available car seats. (Moodley T, Khoza SB, Chetty V: Adaptive seating for children with cerebral palsy classified as GMFCS Levels IV and V at General Justice Gizenga Mpanza Regional Hospital). These finding should be interpreted cautiously pending peer review publishing. This case report describes the individualised assessment, systematic modification process, and postural outcomes for a child with GMFCS Level V CP at GJGMRH whose clinical complexity required targeted modifications beyond standard car seat fitting.

Patient information

Jessie (pseudonym) is a 12-year-old Indian male with a confirmed diagnosis of CP GMFCS Level V attending the multidisciplinary CP clinic at GJGMRH in the iLembe Health District of KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. He is currently not enrolled in formal schooling. Jessies caregivers main concern was that he had outgrown his transporter buggy and required a seating and mobility device.

Medical history

Jessie was born at 28 weeks gestation via Caesarean section due to antepartum haemorrhage, weighing 1,200 g (length 40 cm; head circumference 27 cm) (Table 1) He presented with APGAR scores of 6 and 7 at birth and required mechanical ventilation for four days. Two doses of antenatal corticosteroids and supplemental oxygen were administered until independent respiration was established. His neonatal course was complicated by pancytopenia requiring four blood transfusions and nosocomial sepsis. He was discharged from the Kangaroo Mother Care programme at 1.8 kg. Cranial computed tomography revealed white matter disease. He was born with right eye blindness and markedly reduced left eye vision. Feeding difficulties were present from birth. He was classified at GMFCS Level V, with spastic quadriplegia, and central and peripheral hypotonia. Chronic medications at the time of assessment were sodium valproate and probiotics.

Clinical timeline

Social and functional context

Jessie lives with a supportive family. His mother is his primary caregiver, is consistently engaged with the physiotherapy programme, and was fully compliant with the home exercise programme. Her primary concern at the time of assessment was that Jessie had outgrown his transporter buggy. His increasing body weight of approximately 14 kg made handling, positioning, and feeding significantly more challenging. Activities of daily living were substantially impaired by hypotonia-related postural instability.

Clinical assessment/ findings

Jessie presented with generalised hypotonia (Modified Ashworth Scale score: 0/4), poor head control, absent independent trunk stability, and marked postural asymmetry in sitting. Passive range of movement was full in all joints. Slight left hip adduction was noted. He demonstrated purposeful reaching and grasping but was unable to sustain grip for longer than two seconds. No independent motor milestones had been achieved (no independent rolling, sitting, crawling, or standing). He was alert, socially responsive, and non-verbal. He was fully dependent on caregivers for all positioning and activities of daily living.

When placed in the standard folding frame wheelchair, Jessie was unable to maintain an upright position, exhibiting posterior pelvic tilt, forward trunk collapse, and lateral trunk sway attributed to hypotonia-related postural weakness Table 1. These findings confirmed that a standard wheelchair was clinically inappropriate and that specialised postural support was required.

Diagnostic assessment

Baseline sitting posture assessment

Sitting posture was assessed using the Posture and Postural Ability Scale (PPAS), a validated tool for evaluating postural alignment in individuals with severe disabilities including CP, with demonstrated excellent inter-rater reliability [13]. The PPAS scores each postural item dichotomously: 1 indicates maintenance of midline symmetry; 0 indicates deviation or asymmetry, assessed in both the frontal and sagittal planes. Permission to use the PPAS was obtained from the instrument developer. Only the sitting position component was assessed. All PPAS assessments were conducted in a standardised clinical setting. The same trained assessor completed all assessments to minimise inter-rater variability. The participant was assessed in two sitting positions, sitting supported by a caregiver and sitting supported by a car seat. The participant was given sufficient time to settle into each sitting position before scoring. PPAS scores were recorded before and after the intervention.

Pre-intervention Assessment: The child was positioned in sitting on a plinth with the hips and knees maintained at approximately 90° flexion and the feet fully supported. The caregiver assisted the child to achieve and maintain an upright sitting posture with the trunk aligned in a neutral position during the assessment. The PPAS was used to analyse the sitting posture in the sagittal and frontal planes. Post-intervention Assessment: The child was seated in the modified car seat. The child was then positioned, adjusted, and strapped into the car seat using seatbelts, trunk harness, abduction wedge and modified footrest. The PPAS was used to assess the sitting posture in the sagittal and frontal planes. Table 4 presents the pre- and post-intervention PPAS scores.

Therapeutic intervention

Interim management pending car seat preparation

While the car seat was being prepared, an interim home management plan was implemented. Jessie’s mother received structured education on 24-hour postural management, passive joint range of motion exercises, and tone-normalising handling techniques. A practical interim sitting strategy was introduced: an oversized T-shirt placed over the child and looped over the back of a firm chair, then tied at the back, provided anterior trunk support for short supervised sitting intervals. This was intended to improve sitting tolerance and prepare the postural system for upright positioning.

Car seat selection

Car seats were sourced through a community donation drive, refurbished, and made available for clinical fitting at GJGMRH. Car seat selection was based on a systematic match between the child’s anthropometric measurements (Table 2), postural needs, and the structural features of available seats. The selected commercially available car seat was a birth-to-25 kg model providing adequate weight capacity (Jessie weighed 14 kg). Its built-in features included a multi-adjustable height headrest, an integrated three-point harness system, a cushioned moulded seat, a steel frame, four tilt-in-space recline positions, and a removable washable cover. Seat dimensions were: length 54.5 cm; height 76 cm; width 58 cm. Figure 1 Car Seat.

All modifications were designed to address the specific postural deficits identified at baseline and were guided by established principles of adaptive seating [8–10]. Results/Findings

At baseline, Jessie required maximum caregiver assistance to maintain any degree of upright sitting. He scored 1/6 in the frontal plane, 2/6 in the sagittal plane, and 3/12 overall (25%), reflecting severe limitations in postural symmetry and stability consistent with GMFCS Level V. Post-intervention, Jessie achieved a total PPAS score of 12/12 (100%): 6/6 in the frontal plane and 6/6 in the sagittal plane. This is an improvement from the baseline score of 3/12 (25%) to 12/12 (100%). Scores improved from 1/6 to 6/6 in the frontal plane and from 2/6 to 6/6 in the sagittal plane. All six postural items that had scored 0 pre-intervention were corrected to 1. The various components of the modified car seating system appear to contribute to the improvements. For example, the trunk harness appear to have contributed to improved trunk alignment; the extended seat belt and trunk harness together may have contributed to the neutralised pelvis; the abduction wedge appear to have corrected left hip adduction; the moulded car seat surface may have contributed to achieved symmetrical weight distribution; the footrest may have established neutral ankle positioning; and the tilt-in-space adjustment may have optimised the overall seating angle for the child’s hypotonia. The combined effect of these modifications was associated with improved seated postural alignment as measured by the PPAS.

Follow up and outcomes

Following fitting of the modified car seat, Jessie underwent post-intervention reassessment at follow up six weeks later using the PPAS. At baseline, his total PPAS score was consistent at 12/12 (100%). Post-intervention assessment demonstrated improved seated postural alignment, including symmetrical trunk positioning, improved pelvic alignment, more neutral lower-limb positioning, improved weight distribution, and adequate foot support while seated in the modified car seat. The combined seating modifications appeared to contribute to these improvements in seated posture as per the PPAS.

Beyond the objectively measured PPAS improvement, caregiver reported observations suggested visual engagement with the environment, safer feeding in an upright posture and reduced daily caregiving. These observations were not formally measured and therefore should be interpreted as subjective reports rather than objective outcomes.

No adverse events, discomfort, skin integrity concerns, or equipment-related complications were reported during the post-intervention period.

Discussion

A commercially available car seat, modified using low-cost and repurposed materials through a structured, physiotherapy-guided process, achieved substantial improvement in seating as assess by the PPAS (PPAS 12/12) in a child with severe CP (GMFCS Level V) and profound hypotonia in a resource-limited public healthcare setting in South Africa. To our knowledge and based on the literature reviewed by the authors this is among the first case reports to document postural correction across both planes using a modified car seat in a child with GMFCS Level V CP and profound hypotonia.

The postural improvements observed are consistent with established principles of adaptive seating biomechanics. Pelvic stabilisation is widely recognised as the foundational requirement for improving trunk control, head stability, and upper limb function in children with severe CP [8, 13–15]. The trunk harness and extended seat belt system directly addressed anterior and lateral trunk collapse the primary postural deficits identified in this case. The moulded seat surface provided distributed weight-bearing that reduced asymmetrical loading, addressing both the risk of pressure injury and scoliotic progression [6, 7, 14]. The abduction wedge corrected left hip adduction, a deformity risk factor associated with hip displacement in this population [16]. The four-position tilt-in-space feature typically found only in high-cost specialised seating systems was of particular clinical value in managing Jessie’s hypotonia by using gravity to assist trunk extension.

The use of a commercially manufactured product as the structural base for this intervention offers several advantages over locally fabricated seating devices. Commercial car seats are manufactured to established safety standards, are structurally tested, are stocked widely in the South African market in multiple sizes and offer a washable cover for infection control. These features compare favourably with locally produced alternatives such as paper-based seating [12], while requiring no specialised production skills or equipment.

The total material cost of all modifications was negligible, utilising repurposed components from discarded rehabilitation equipment, surplus materials, and locally available items. This is directly relevant given that cost and availability are the primary barriers to assistive device access in LMICs, where fewer than 3% of those in need have access to appropriate devices [11]. The dual-function design standalone home seating and wheelchair-mounted mobility system further enhanced the practical value of the intervention without additional cost.

This case also illustrates the importance of physiotherapy-led individual assessment in car seat selection and modification. The modifications were not generic but were directly driven by specific findings at baseline assessment trunk collapse, left hip adduction, absent foot support, and the need for anterior pelvic stabilisation. This individualised approach distinguishes therapeutic use of a car seat from incidental use and was essential to achieving the outcomes documented.

Several limitations of this case report should be acknowledged. Postural assessment was performed by the treating physiotherapist, introducing the potential for measurement bias. No assessor blinding was employed. Long-term follow-up data, including the durability of the modifications and the maintenance of postural gains over time, were not available. The PPAS measures static seated posture; dynamic functional outcomes such as feeding efficiency, communication capacity, and caregiver workload were not formally quantified in this report. The findings of this single case report cannot be generalised without replication across a broader population. Future case studies and controlled comparative studies evaluating modified car seats against standard care and against specialised adaptive seating devices in children with GMFCS Level V CP are needed to build the evidence base for this approach. It is also important to note that the modified car seat described in this report was used exclusively as a therapeutic seating device in home and clinical settings and must not be considered a substitute for a certified in-vehicle child restraint system during transport.

Conclusions

A commercially available car seat, modified using low-cost and repurposed materials through a structured, physiotherapy-guided process, achieved improved postural alignment as measured by PPAS (PPAS 12/12) in a child with severe CP (GMFCS Level V) and profound hypotonia in a resource-limited public healthcare setting in South Africa. The approach represents a potentially practical, replicable, and affordable model of frugal innovation in adaptive seating. However, its applicability across other LMIC settings require further evaluation in larger studies where specialised adaptive seating devices are inaccessible. Future research should explore the feasibility, scalability and acceptability of this approach within other resource constraint settings. Future research should evaluate this approach across larger samples and more diverse clinical presentations.
