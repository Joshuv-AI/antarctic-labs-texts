Sample processing methods affect salivary metabolomics in human exercise-stress studies
Authors: Staci Thornton, Michael R. Szymanski, Gabrielle J. Brewer, Douglas J. Casa, Timothy E. Moore, Shiying Xiao, Alexander A. Green, Keith Pardee, Elaine C. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSaliva biomarker research requires understanding of how saliva collection and processing techniques affect results and reproducibility. Direct comparison of the effect of processing and storage conditions on downstream assay outcomes is important to understanding the ways that delay in freezing, methods of freezing, filtration protocols, and other factors can affect results and thus, interpretations about stress response and adaptation. This type of study compliments the work that is required to understand how saliva compares to tissue and blood responses and what the implications of changes in saliva biomarkers mean to our understanding of biomarkers of stress during exercise and environmental stress exposures.ObjectiveIn this pilot study, we evaluated the effects of different processing methods on metabolomics results and discuss the implications of these findings for future experimental design.MethodsWe assessed the effects of centrifugation, time-of-day collection, filtration, and mucinase treatments and determined that there are quantifiable differences in metabolomics results with different treatments.ResultsAdding processing steps did not increase the number of metabolites detected or the sensitivity and specificity of results.ConclusionComprehensive descriptions of methods in this area will support better interpretation and reproducibility in this field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11306-026-02504-7.

Introduction

Saliva is collected for the assessment of immune markers, antimicrobial proteins, hormones, and other indicators of stress response and recovery during exercise (Papacosta and N. G 2011). Saliva collection is relatively noninvasive and easier to collect than blood, has potential for greater sample volume collection, and has a complex composition, including multifunctional and interacting components such as electrolytes, immunoglobulins, proteins, enzymes, mucins (Sue et al. 2001, Zhang and Wang 2012). There is a correlation between saliva and blood biomarkers when considering the use of saliva as a sample representative of systemic circulation or pool from multiple tissues and organs and general signaling. Examples of contexts in which metabolomic and proteomic changes in saliva are similar to changes in blood are published. Studies of blood and saliva proteomes has identified 597 of 19,474 proteins that align with similar gene ontology of core datasets, with ~ 40% of disease-related candidate blood biomarkers present saliva (Yan et al. 2009, Loo et al. 2010). Pyruvate and lactate metabolites increase similarly in blood and saliva samples after carbohydrate ingestion (Kelsay et al. 1972). Similarities in prominent biological mechanisms between saliva and blood samples is promising data that in at least these contexts, saliva is a feasible biosample type for interpreting physiological responses.

A major challenge in understanding and interpreting saliva biomarker changes is the variability in methods for sample processing and preparation and the potential effects on results and interpretation. Our understanding of the effect of different protocols for sample preparation is limited. Examples of key aspects of saliva sample processing that may affect results include protocols for reducing mucin content in samples, centrifuging samples to produce various clarified extracts or supernatants for analyses, and downstream data analysis pipelines. Mucin is a high-molecular weight glycoprotein secreted by epithelial mucosal cells, with long sugar side chains (Bar Dayan and Niv 2004). Mucins contain hundreds to thousands of amino acids, particularly threonine, serine, proline, alanine and glycine (Stuart Patton et al. 1995). Reduction or elimination of mucins in saliva with mucinase treatment may eliminate variation in the metabolomes across human subjects and conditions, reduce viscosity, and improve sample handling ease, but may limit detection of specific compounds or classes of compounds. In support of minimal sample processing, a recent study found over 3,413 proteins in whole saliva versus 2,340 in cell-free saliva. Additionally, variation in function of proteins is observed (e.g. neurodegenerative diseases, oxidative phosphorylation, were predominantly found in whole saliva) (Jiao et al. 2025). Furthermore, more low-abundance proteins were detected following nanoparticle enrichment, which may also be key for biomarker discovery (Jiao et al. 2025).

Additional limitations of metabolomics analysis include the absence of reference standards influencing the robustness of annotation of unknown metabolites, which limits reproducibility across instruments and labs. Moreover, metabolomics analysis is limited in detection of potential biomarkers due to unpredictable and informative fragmentation patterns. While the knowledge and applications gained from salivary metabolomics is promising, it is essential to address technical limitations and develop standardized practices to enable reproducible differentially abundant metabolomic discovery. In this study, we evaluated different sample preparation methods on the following criteria: number metabolites identified, number of annotated metabolites, and performance of methods in preliminary differential abundance and other statistical analyses that would be used in a typical workflow.

Methods

Exercise-environmental stress exposures

Data from healthy participants (n = 5, 3 male and 2 female) were used in this pilot study. These data are acquired from a larger study for these analyses. Participants were well-trained (VO2max (maximal oxygen consumption during prescribed testing session) ≥ 40 for females and ≥ 45 for males, mL⋅kg− 1⋅min− 1, age 22 ± 2 years old). Participants’ baseline measurements were recorded: height 170.82 ± 3.30 (cm), mass 72.09 ± 8.09 (kg), resting heart rate 72.25 ± 14.12 (bpm), VO2max 49.48 ± 5.00 (mL·kg− 1·min− 1), and vVO2max (calculated velocity at VO2max determined from prior maximal oxygen consumption testing) 14.29 ± 1.66 (km·h− 1).

All participants were medically screened and provided informed consent for this human ethics board-approved study. Participants were excluded if they were unable to tolerate exercise in the heat, habitually consumed a non-traditional diet, experienced sleep disturbances, or reported chronic or acute illnesses. Participants were asked to refrain from structured exercise training and using pain medication, caffeine (12 h prior to trial) and alcohol (24 h prior to trial) throughout the duration of their participation. Participants completed VO2max testing (treadmill at self-selected pace (2% grade) for five minutes, followed by a progressive increase in speed (0.5–1.0 m·h− 1) until fatigue/inability to continue).

Prior to each laboratory visit, hydration state of participants was checked by refractometer measurement of urine specific gravity (USG, Reichert, Lincolnshire, IL) (criteria for participation was USG < 1.020). Heat tolerance test 1 (HTT1), heat tolerance test 2 (HTT2), and heat acclimation trials were done in an environmental chamber (40 °C, 40% relative humidity (rH), wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT ≥ 32.2 °C) that was monitored every 30 min (Kestrel 5400 Heat Stress Tracker, Kestrel Instruments, Nielsen-Kellerman Co., Boothwyn, PA, USA). Participants were monitored throughout the session to ensure safety.

For HTT1, after a 10-minute equilibrium period in the chamber, participants walked at a 2% grade at 5.0 km·h− 1 for 2 h, during which oxygen consumption was measured between 30 and 40 min, and 90 and 100 min (G 1990, Micklewright et al. 2017). On the second day of heat tolerance testing, (HTT2), participants ran at 65% of the vV̇O2max until Trec = 39.5 °C). Both HTT1 and HTT2 occurred before and after 6 sessions of heat acclimation (jog at 70% vV̇O2max until Trec reaches 38.5 °C. Subjects exercised at speeds adjusted to maintain Trec between 38.5 and 39.5 °C for 60 min) with minimum 24 h between sessions completed over a maximum of 8 days. Time points for this pilot study only include the post-exercise HTT1 timepoint pre- and post-heat acclimation (HA) (preHA and postHA).

Saliva collection and processing

Saliva was collected at 2 time points (pre and post HA) using a passive drool collection kit (Salimetrics, State College, PA). Two (~ 2) mL of saliva was collected, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored in the − 80 °C freezer until processing. Samples were thawed and centrifuged for 20 min at 10,000 rcf, at 4 °C prior to assay to remove cellular debris. Resultant cell-free supernatant was transferred to a new tube. Saliva was further processed into 1 of 4 treatments: (1) supernatant only (Unfilt), (2) supernatant filtered (Filt), 400 µL was passed through a 0.45 μm filter (Costar® Spin-X, Product Number 8162), (3) supernatant from treatment (1) was incubated for 3 h with 1:10 mucinase at 37 °C (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. SAE0202) (UnfiltMuc); and (4) filtered supernatant from treatment (2) incubated for 3 h with 1:10 mucinase at 37 °C (FiltMuc) (Supplemental Table 1). For each condition, 200 µL of this supernatant was mixed and vortexed with 100% ice cold methanol in a 1:4 ratio. This mixture was incubated at −20 °C for 1 h. Samples were then centrifuged at 10,000 rcf at 4 °C for 15 min. Supernatant was collected into pre-weighed microcentrifuge tubes and dried using a speed-vac until the pellet was dry (approximately 2 h). The pellet was weighed and re-constituted in 0.1% formic acid to a concentration of 10 mg·mL− 1.

UPLC-MS methods

A 10 µL volume of each sample was loaded in Solvent A (0.1% formic acid in Optima grade water) onto a Waters Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.8 μm, #186003540) held at 40 °C. Analytes were separated with a gradient of 5–40% Solvent B (0.1% formic acid in Optima grade acetonitrile) over 6.2 min at a flow rate of 300 µL/min, followed by a ramp to 95% B over 0.2 min and a wash for 1 min, and re-equilibrating to starting conditions for 1.6 min, for a total of 9 min. Analytes were eluted directly into a Waters Synapt G2-Si mass spectrometer in positive polarity, resolution mode, and ionized by ESI with a sampling cone voltage of 40 V and a capillary voltage of 3 kV. Source temperature was set to 100 °C with cone gas flow of 50 L/hr and desolvation temperature was set to 250 °C with a desolvation gas flow of 600 L/hr. MS1 data were collected in continuum mode over a scan range of 80–2000 m/z, with a scan time of 0.1 s and a fixed collision energy of 4 eV. MSe data were collected from 50 to 2000 m/z with a fragmentation energy ramp of 5–40 eV. Real-time lockmass correction was enabled using infusion of leucine enkephalin with a cone voltage of 40 V and measurement of the singly charged peptide ion mass ([M + H] + = 556.2771) every 10 s, with a 0.2 s scan rate and applying a 3-scan average.

Saliva metabolomics analysis

Raw data collected on the Waters UPLC-MS were loaded into Progenesis IQ software, using “Profile” parameters, at high resolution, with positive adducts M + H, M + NA, M + K, 2 M + Na, 2 M + K. Automatic processing was performed using a reference chosen by the software. Following peak alignment, features were extracted, and the number of low- and high-energy MS/MS peaks (acquired at low or high collison energy, respectively) was quantified per sample. For the 40 samples, 27,732 ion features were detected. Alignment was reviewed in the ProgenesisIQ software. Detected features were matched to the Metlin database with standard fragmentation score cutoffs.

Treatment and timepoint comparisons using metabolites present in > 51% of samples (3/5): with data from Progenesis, one compound ID may correspond to several annotations, and the identified component was selected based on the top scoring spectrally matched annotation based on (1) fragmentation score (fragmentation pattern of compounds stored in a fragment database), (2) overall score (fragmentation score, retention time similarity, CCS (collision cross-section) similarity, mass similarity, and isotope similarity). Raw data were log2 transformed and normalized for effects of treatment (filtration and mucinase). Data were normalized via Quantile normalization and missing data was imputed with condition-specific minimum value imputation30.

A linear mixed-effects model was used to evaluate overall differerences in abundance between filter type, mucinase treatment and time point. The purpose of this analysis was not to perform differential abundance analysis, but to examine which components of our experimental design (e.g., mucinase/no mucinase, filter/no filter) affected metabolite abundance. This mixed-effects model included the fixed effects: filter type (unfiltered vs. filtered), mucinase treatment (mucinase vs. no mucinase), timepoint (preHA post-exercise (2, second timepoint of the 10 timepoints collected in the overall protocol) vs. postHA post-exercise (8, eigth timepoint of the 10 timepoins collected in the overall protocol)). In addition to the main effects, a three-way interaction (filter type x treatment x timepoint) was also included. The random effects (random intercepts) were replicate (different subjects) and compound; both of which were used to account for repeated observations on subjects and compounds. Fixed-effects significance was assessed using ANOVA, specifically, Wald Chi-Squared tests with Type III sums of squares, and Satterthwaite’s approximation for degrees of freedom. These analyses (ANOVA and linear mixed effects models) were performed using the car (Weisberg et al. 2019) and lme4 (Double Bates et al. 2015) packages in R. To assess complete absence or presence based on condition, infinity fold change can be used as a classifier in ProgenesisIQ for a within-subjects ANOVA.

Pairwise differential abundance analysis was conducted based on moderated t-tests, using an empirical Bayes procedure that squeezes compound-wise residual variances to a common value, as peformed in the limma (Ritchie et al. 2015) package in R. The degrees of freedom for individual variances are increased to reflect extra information gained from the empirical Bayes moderation, resulting in increased statistical power to detect differential expression31.

Comparisons were made for the following variables: Condition (a composite variable combining both mucinase, filtration, timepoint to test all combinations and detect any combination effects (reference: unfiltered, no mucinase, pre timepoint)), FilterType (average main effect of filtration, fixed mucinase and timepoint, reference is unfiltered), MucinPresence (average main effect of mucinase, timepoint and filtration are fixed, reference is no mucinase), TimePoint (average main effect of timepoint, mucinase and filtration are fixed, reference is preHA post-exercise).

Significance was determined by both p-value and fold change (p ≤ 0.05, fold changes ≥ 1 up or down). Comparisons were made using pairwise differential abundance (DA) analysis based on moderated t-tests using empirical bayes procedure, with main and fixed effects according to comparison of interest.

Effects of timepoint

Comparison of timepoint (with FilterType and MucinPresence as a constant), shows sum of squares of 62.53 (Supplemental Table 5), thus contributing less to variance than MucinPresence but more than FilterType. Of the 238 differentially abundant metabolites, 223 were non-identified (123 down, 100 up). 15 were identified, including 7 down and 8 up. (Supplemental Table 3). The main goal of this test was to ensure that what one would consider a differentially abundant metabolite (e.g. differential abundance in the post versus the preHA timepoint) is similar when comparing respective timepoints of each condition. The overlap is minimal, suggesting differential abundance (metabolomic comparisons) is greatly influenced by treatment. Of the top 10 most abundant compounds detected within each condition, ranked by average abundance of the pre timepoints, minimal overlap is observed. Arachidonoyl thio-PC, 1-Stearoyl-2-Arachidonoyl PC, 1,2-Dioleoyl PC, SM(d18:1/16:0) are the most common across the 4 conditions in the ranked pre timepoints. Discussion

Mucinase treatment was the highest contributing factor to variance in the metabolomics results. Twelve mucins have been identified (Perez-Vilar 1999) including two salivary mucins differentiated by molecular weight where MG1 and MG2 are the higher and lower molecular weight proteins, respectively (David et al. 1999). The expression of glycans by MG1 and MG2 vary to a high degree, with little overlap between the two. MG1 contains a much higher diversity of oligosaccharide structure as well (David et al. 1999, Kristina et al. 2002). Although mucins are a crucial component of saliva composition, they may negatively influence the ability to detect smaller molecules (metabolites) of interest. The mucinase treatment may eliminate that concern and also reduce viscosity of samples. Most DA metabolites following mucinase treatment (compared to no treatment) are amino acids. In total, 23 of the 73 metabolites detected as significant with mucinase treatment are peptides or amino acids. Filtration has a large effect on the DA metabolites. Certain peptides may be attributed to mucin, but no association to mucin function or signaling is apparent.

Our results are similar to those of previous studies assessing the influence of filtration on saliva metabolomics. A study in 2023 showed that these two methods cause significant changes in saliva composition measurements and should be considered when assessing results across studies and cohorts of subjects (Franco-Martínez et al. 2023). Filtration is a common method in pre-processing to eliminate the risk of contaminants that may damage hydraulics within the instruments and columns themselves. Filtration, however, may eliminate some metabolites of interest. Filtration affects detection of only 5 metabolites that are present in the filtered but not in the unfiltered. The metabolites most affected do not indicate a particular pathway or process (GO, enrichment) suggesting that this step can be omitted in untargeted metabolomics.

Our data show high variability in metabolomics results with saliva collection timing and processing. Whole saliva can contain endothelial cells or debris which may (1) mask low abundance proteins and/or (2) increase difficulty of salivary handling and variability in saliva viscosity and therefore composition. Comparisons of whole saliva versus cell-free saliva using proteomics have demonstrated differences in the raw number of proteins identified, altered global functional annotation results and interpretation, and hundreds of low-abundance proteins enriched after a low-abundance protein enrichment protocol (Jiao et al. 2025).

Our saliva was frozen immediately, and supernatant was collected after thawing. It may be beneficial to immediately collect supernatants prior to freezing to avoid any degradation from potential contaminants. Variation in processing methods affect results, but there were no clear biological relevance and implication for interpretation of the markers related to the testing intervention (exercise-heat stress and exercise-heat acclimation). This aids researchers conducting field studies during which on-site processing is limited or cost-related aspects of study limitations, such as the added cost of mucinase enzyme, per sample.

We conclude that treatment with mucinase or filtration produces similar metabolite detection across samples; however, when identifying differentially abundant metabolites across timepoints, the treatment effect is extensive. We anticipate key metabolites to be missing under certain treatments. This does not appear to be in a clear pathway or subset of metabolites. Based on these results, we interpret that unfiltered, no mucinase option of processing eliminates no key metabolites and limits extra cost and burden of processing. Moreover, we observed little improvements in saliva handling, or viscosity, with mucinase treatment. We suggest that saliva supernatant is the best option to use, in this case, due to the removal of cellular debris, improved ease of handling, and reproducibility among other literature that tends to utilize supernatant (Supplemental Table 8).

Limitations

While we did not test the storage effect on the salivary metabolome, it has been shown that total antioxidant capacity and total oxidative stress levels are significantly lower in saliva samples frozen at both − 20 °C and − 80 °C for 90 days as compared to fresh saliva samples. We did not test this in our hands, as assaying fresh samples is not possible in our study design. No significant difference was found between the freezing temperatures (Amadeu et al. XXX). Additionally, a common pre-processing step in saliva analysis is alpha amylase depletion. Alpha amylase makes up 50% of the salivary protein content and is commonly depleted prior to processing of saliva (Franco-Martínez et al. 2023). This study assessed analyte measurement with ELISA kits. Depletion of high-abundant compounds, like amylase, will allow for detection of other less abundant compounds. Depletion may co-deplete other compounds of interest.

We acknowledge the limitation of a small sample size, potential effect of filtration materials, and analysis methods using different databases. Such a small sample size raises concerns of high-dimensionality and our dataset represents one in which thousands of features relative to the sample number could lead to overfitting and reduced power. We have attempted to use strict normalization techniques and features selection methods (e.g., PLS-DA). In ongoing work, we can pursue rigorous cross-validation to distinguish true biological signals from noise. It is important to understand that our data are not attempting to identify biomarkers in the current study design, but rather to determine whether metabolomics distinguishes samples processed in different ways at the time of collection and before UPLC/MS assay. In future work exploring metabolomics profiling of adapted states (vs. naïve) and biomarker work, we acknowledge the extra steps that must be taken in analysis nad validation to frame our interpretations. Our results are in the context of our design and future comparative studies of different aspects of methods and analysis will improve our understanding of this field.
