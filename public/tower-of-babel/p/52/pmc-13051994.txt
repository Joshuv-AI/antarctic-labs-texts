An Integrated Genotyping Strategy for α/β‐Thalassemia: Based on the Analysis of the Coding Sequences and Expression Levels of HBA2 , HBA1 , and HBB in Peripheral Blood mRNA
Authors: Hongjian Chen, Qi Yao, Mianai Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo analyze the clinical diagnostic value for thalassemia genotyping using peripheral blood mRNA.MethodsTotal RNA was extracted from 103 pre‐genotyped peripheral blood samples, followed by cDNA synthesis. These samples comprised 21 wild‐type cases, 49 α‐globin gene mutant cases and 33 HBB mutant cases. An Integrated strategy was adopted, combining single‐reaction Sanger sequencing with the multiple quantitative fluorescence PCR (MQF‐PCR), to analyze the full‐length coding sequences and expression level of HBA2/HBA1/HBB respectively.Results(1) The substitution mutations located in the coding region (n = 14) were successfully detected, and the mutations located in the HBB non‐coding region displayed negative results (n = 13). The samples with β41–42M/βN (n = 10) and β71–72M/βN (n = 4) were not observed to have their small deletion or insertion mutation initially, but they were eventually identified after using allele‐specific MQF‐PCR. Moreover, MQF‐PCR results indicated that the level ranges of Peak area ratio 1 (PAR1, PAHBA2/PAHBA1) and Peak area ratio 2 (PAR2, PAHBB/PAHBA) can be used to identify the common α‐globin gross‐deletion types and to predict mutations in the HBB non‐coding region.ConclusionThis mRNA‐based strategy enabled detection of coding‐region mutations in the HBA2/HBA1/HBB and prediction of non‐coding‐region mutations as well as gross deletions with high coverage, low cost and simple operation.Keywords: coding sequence, expression level, genotyping, multiple quantitative fluorescence PCR, sanger sequencing, thalassemia

Introduction

Thalassemia is a prevalent autosomal recessive genetic disorder primarily attributed to mutations in the genes encoding the α‐globin or β‐globin chains [1, 2]. The genes encoding α‐globin were hemoglobin subunit α2 (HBA2) and hemoglobin subunit α1 (HBA1). In southern China, the most common types of α‐thalassemia with long‐length deletion were −α3.7, −α4.2 and —SEA [3, 4, 5, 6]. The first two types manifest as decreased expression (α+), whereas the latter represent absent expression (α0). Similar to α‐globin, defective forms of β‐globin are also classified as β0 and β+. More than 200 different mutations of hemoglobin subunit β (HBB) were implicated, and most of them were point mutations [7, 8]. The mutations of β0 classification were primarily frameshift mutations which were found in the coding region, such as 41–42 M (HBB: c.126_129del). The β+ classification was almost located within the promoter region of transcription, such as −28 M (HBB: c.−78A>G/C), or within the intron of several crucial sites, like IVS‐2‐654 M (HBB: c.316‐197C>T).

For genotyping of thalassemia, Gap‐PCR and PCR‐reverse dot blot hybridization (PCR‐RDB) were often used as classical methods to detect genome DNA [4, 9, 10]. In recent years, melting curve assay and other techniques have also been used for testing [11, 12, 13]. Furthermore, the result of routine blood tests implied that the patient had the indication of thalassemia with a negative result by conventional technology; it is necessary to use next‐generation sequencing (NGS) or third‐generation sequencing (TGS) technologies to comprehensively analyze the sequences of HBA2, HBA1, and HBB [14, 15, 16]. However, these techniques cannot replace the traditional PCR‐based screening techniques due to their complexity, time consumption, and high analytical difficulty [17]. Due to the characteristics of eukaryotic genomes, when the above techniques are used to detect mutations of genomic DNA, a large amount of non‐coding sequences is often additionally obtained. Some studies have begun to focus on using mRNA for thalassemia genotyping or assessing functional impacts. On one hand, a sample with an abnormal blood phenotype was detected for genotype using both DNA and cDNA as templates simultaneously [18]; on the other hand, methods for assessing the functional impact of mutations were also employed, such as detecting the mRNA relative expression levels of α/β‐globin using qPCR methods like TaqMan [19], SYBR Green I [20, 21].

In order to further evaluate the clinical diagnostic value of peripheral blood mRNA for thalassemia genotyping, we first obtained and analyzed the complete coding sequences of HBA2, HBA1, and HBB from the mRNA of peripheral blood. Subsequently, it was analyzed whether common α‐globin and β‐globin variants cause changes in the mRNA relative expression levels to form a new integrated genotyping strategy.

Materials and Methods

Sample Source and Pre‐Processing

One hundred and three samples were collected from patients who underwent α/β‐thalassemia genotyping at the Haikou Maternal and Child Health Hospital (Haikou, China) between 2, 2024 and 8, 2025. The genotypes of the above samples were pre‐confirmed using the α/β‐thalassemia gene detection kit (Daan Gene Co. LTD, China), as displayed in Table 1. This study had been approved by the Haikou Maternal and Child Health Hospital Ethics Committee, and all patients had been interviewed and signed the Chinese version of informed consent.

RNA Extraction and cDNA Synthesis

After removing plasma from whole blood by centrifugation at 6200 g, RT, 3 min, collected 400 μL of the remaining peripheral blood cell fraction for subsequent RNA extraction. 1200 μL of erythrocyte lysis solution (R1010, Solarbio Technology Co. Ltd., China) was added to the sample and incubated on ice for 15 min. During the incubation, the mixture was reversed and mixed twice. After centrifugation at 600 g, 4°C, the supernatant was carefully discarded and the precipitated cells were resuspended by adding 500 μL of fresh erythrocyte lysis solution again. After that, the precipitated cells were collected after centrifugation at 600 g, 4°C, and the supernatant was discarded.

RNA was extracted using RNAiso Easy kit (TCH021, TaKaRa, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted RNA was detected by ultraviolet spectrophotometer for RNA concentration and A260nm/280nm ratio. RT‐PCR and cDNA synthesis were performed using RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K16225, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) according to the following system and condition: 2 μL of 5 × Reaction Buffer, 1 μL of dNTP mixture (10 mmol/L), 0.5 μL of Oligo(dT)18 Primer (100 μmol/L), 0.5 μL of RiboLock RNase Inhibitor (20 U/μL), 0.5 μL of MMLV and 200 ng of total RNA solution. Double distilled water (nuclease‐free) was added to the reaction system to make a total volume of 10 μL. PCR conditions were 42°C, 60 min, followed by 85°C for 5 min.

Sequences Analysis of HBA2 , HBA1 and HBB

PCR Amplification and Product Purification

The cDNA from 103 samples was utilized to amplify HBA2, HBA1, and HBB. The primer pairs employed for amplification were HBA‐F1/HBA2‐R1, HBA‐F1/HBA1‐R1, and HBB‐F1/HBB‐R1, respectively (Table 2). It was worth mentioning that these amplicons encompassed the full‐length coding sequences of the above genes. TaKaRa Taq kit (R001A, TaKaRa) was chosen to carry out the PCR reaction according to the following conditions. The PCR system consisted of 2 μL of 10 × PCR Buffer (Mg2+ plus), 1 μL of dNTP mixture (2.5 mmol/L each), 0.5 μL each of primer, 0.2 μL of DNA polymerase, and 1 μL of cDNA (2.5 ng/μL), supplemented with ddH2O to 20 μL. The PCR procedure was initiated with 95°C for 10 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 45 s, annealing at 60°C for 60 s, and extension at 72°C for 45 s. The reaction was concluded with a final cycle at 72°C for 5 min. The PCR products were subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis followed by UV detection to analyze whether the target amplicon was produced. For samples exhibiting abnormal amplification as a result of variations in primer sites, additional primer sets (HBA‐F1/HBA2‐R2 and HBA‐F1/HBA1‐R2) were employed for amplification purposes. Subsequently, the products with target bands were purified using TIANquick Midi Purification kit (TIANGEN Biotech Co. Ltd, China).

Sanger Sequencing

The BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (4337455, ThermoFisher Scientific) was used to in accordance with the instructions for Sanger sequencing performance. Reverse primers for amplicons were mainly used for sequencing to ensure that complete coding sequences were obtained. The PCR product purification before sequencing was carried out by EDTA‐Na2 chelation combined with absolute ethanol precipitation. Finally, 10 μL of Hi‐Di Formamide (4440753, ThermoFisher Scientific) was added to the purified product and subjected to denaturation at 95°C for 4 min. And the ABI3500dx genetic analyzer (ThermoFisher Scientific) was used for sequencing.

Verification of Sequencing Graphics and Analysis of Rare Variants

The sequencing electropherograms were manually checked and corrected carefully. For the rare variants found in the sequence, the variant information was searched in ClinVar [22], HGMD (Human Gene Mutation Database) [23], and the pathogenicity was predicted by MutationTaster software [24].

Detection of mRNA Relative Expression Levels of HBA2 / HBA1 / HBB by Using Multiple Quantitative Fluorescence PCR (MQF‐PCR)

Sample Grouping and MQF‐PCR Testing

The 103 test samples were divided into groups according to their genotypes. The control group consisted of the 21 samples with “αα/αα, βN/βN” genotype. MQF‐PCR reaction was set for HBA2/HBA1/HBB expression levels. The 5′ ends of the forward primers HBA‐F2 and HBB‐F2 were respectively modified with fluorescences. The primer settings are shown in Table 2. All the cDNAs were performed MQF‐PCR reaction with Group‐1 primers. The MQF‐PCR system (R001A, TaKaRa) consisted of 2 μL of 10 × PCR Buffer (Mg2+ plus), 1 μL of dNTP mixture (2.5 mmol/L each), 0.2 μL each of primer, 0.2 μL of DNA polymerase and 2 μL of cDNA (2.5 ng/μL), supplemented with ddH2O to 20 μL. The PCR condition was as follows: 1 cycle of 95°C at 5 min, followed by 24 cycles of 95°C at 40s, 60°C at 50s, 72°C at 40 s, finally 1 cycle of 72°C at 5 min.

In addition, 14 samples with genotypes of β41–42M/βN and β71–72M/βN, were conducted allele specific MQF‐PCR (AS‐MQF‐PCR) reaction with Group‐2 primers (HBB‐MF was also modified with fluorescence). The PCR system (N8080241, ThermoFisher Scientific) consisted of 2 μL of 10 × PCR Buffer, 2.4 μL of 25 mM MgCl2, 1 μL of 10 mM dNTP Mix, 0.5 μL each of primer, 0.1 μL of AmpliTaq Gold DNA Polymerase, and 2 μL of cDNA (2.5 ng/μL), supplemented with ddH2O to 20 μL. The PCR condition was as follows: 1 cycle of 95°C at 10 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95°C at 40 s, 60°C at 50 s, 72°C at 60 s, finally 1 cycle of 60°C at 30 min.

Products Analysis

The MQF‐PCR products of Group‐1 were diluted 1:64 with ddH2O, while the products of Group‐2 did not need to be diluted. Mixed 1 μL of the above product with 9.5 μL of Hi‐Di Formamide and 0.5 μL of GeneScan 600 LIZ (4,408,399, ThermoFisher Scientific). Then, it was denatured at 94°C for 5 min and chilled on ice for 3 min. At last, the mixture was transferred to a 96‐well plate and subjected to fragment analysis using the ABI 3500dx genetic analyzer. The Genemapper v6.0 software was used to analyze the original data, which were mainly presented as values of peak area (PA) of every amplicon.

Data Processing and Statistical Analysis

Comparing the differences in the relative expression levels of HBA2 and HBA1 among the groups, the Peak area ratio (PAR) 1, calculated by “PAR1 = PAHBA2/PAHBA1”, were used to analyze the changes between HBA2 and HBA1, and was mainly employed to identify common α‐globin gross‐deletion types. Secondly, we compared the relative expression levels of HBB (with HBA as the reference), and attempted to predict mutations in the non‐coding regions. The calculation formula was used: “PAR2 = PAHBB/(PAHBA2 + PAHBA1)”.

The statistical differences between groups were analyzed by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All statistical tests conducted were two‐tailed tests.

In the aspect of sequence analysis of HBA2 and HBA1, 5 cases of ααWS/αα were found to have a heterozygous mutation of HBA2: c.369C>G; 3 cases of ααQS/αα were found to have a heterozygous mutation of HBA2: c.377 T>C; the remaining samples did not show any other rare mutations or SNPs. It was indicated that the sequencing results of cDNA were not inconsistent with the prior genotypes (Table 3).

Further Genotyping of c.126_129del and c.216_217insA Using AS‐MQF‐PCR

In order to analyze the reasons why these two mutations were not detected in cDNA sequencing, and to further improve the shortcomings of this method, an AS‐MQF‐PCR was adopted for implementation. The results showed that all 14 samples exhibited peaks at the specific fragment length positions. It was worth noting that a non‐specific fragment, which was 1 bp shorter than the target, was observed in non‐71‐72 M samples. However, this did not affect the result identification (Figure 2). This might be due to the difference in the initial copy numbers of the wild and mutant strands, as well as the limitations of the allele‐specific PCR. Further, the “allelic dropout” phenomenon occurred and affected the conventional PCR reaction as well as the subsequent sequencing reaction. Additionally, a SNP was found at position c.9 in the HBB with three types: C, C/T, and T. The above two mutant types did not found any heterozygous type at this position, which further confirmed that the sequencing results were only for the wild strand.

Identification of c.126_129del and c.216_217insA in cDNA templates using AS‐MQF‐PCR. At the 71–72 M locus, non‐specific amplification was observed in the wild‐type templates (71–72 N). However, the fragment size of the 71–72 N samples was 1 bp shorter than that of the 71–72 M, allowing for distinction.

MQF‐PCR for Predicting Common Gross Deletion in the α‐Globin Gene and Pathogenic Mutations in the Non‐Coding Region of β‐Globin Gene

The mRNA expression levels of HBA2, HBA1, and HBB were detected using the MQF‐PCR, and all samples were tested. The representative original peak graphs of MQF‐PCR were shown in Figure 3. PAR1 reflected the difference in the expression levels of HBA2 among groups when HBA1 was used as the reference, while PAR2 reflected the difference in the expression levels of HBB when HBA was used as the reference. The results showed that (1) −α4.2/αα and −α3.7/αα both lacked one HBA2, with PAR1 being 65.37% (1.85/2.83) and 66.08% (1.87/2.83) of the wild type, respectively; moreover, the PAR2 level of the latter was higher than that of the wild type, indicating that although both types have lost one HBA2 at the DNA level, due to differences in the splicing process after the breakage, the latter had a greater impact on the overall expression of α‐globin, and that can be used to distinguish between the two genotypes. (2) The —SEA/αα lacked one HBA2 and HBA1, with PAR1 being 116.96% of the wild type. There was no significant difference in the PAR2. The reason for the increase in PAR1 was the slight upregulation of HBA2 expression and the slight downregulation of HBA1. In addition, there may be feedback regulation that results in no significant difference in the PAR2 compared to the wild type. The compound heterozygous deletion, —SEA/−α3.7 and —SEA/−α4.2, only allowed one HBA1 to be expressed. PAR1 level was 0 (HBA2 was not expressed), and PAR2 of —SEA/−α3.7 significantly increased due to the downregulation of α‐globin gene. However, due to the small sample size, the two types could not be differentiated based on PAR2 and —SEA/−α4.2 showed no significant difference from the wild type. The above data were shown in Table 1. In summary, the above results implied that we can predict the common gross‐deletion types in the α‐globin gene based on PAR1 and PAR2 levels.

For the two substitution mutations occurring in the HBB non‐coding region, c.−78A>G/C and c.316‐197C>T, PAR2 was significantly down regulated, while there was no statistical difference in PAR1 compared to the wild type. This precisely indicated that the mutations in the regulatory region may reduce the relative expression level of HBB and can predict these two genotypes through PAR1 and PAR2 levels. Moreover, although the mutations occurring in the coding region of HBB also had a lower PAR2 value than the wild group, there was no statistical difference.

Discussion

Thalassemia is a severe genetic disease with abnormal hemoglobin production [25]. The mechanism of disease is the imbalance of α/β chain caused by decreased or absent synthesis of α‐globin or β‐globin [8, 26]. In the blood routine examination, the decrease of mean corpuscular volume (MCV) and changes of other values can imply patient may carry thalassemia. However, many studies have shown that the MCV values of α+/α type are still within the normal range [3, 27]. These cases can be prevented to a large extent by genotyping detection for genomic DNA [5, 6]. There were still false negative results being reported by conventional methods. In our current study, we developed a detection strategy involving peripheral blood‐derived mRNA, which was subjected to Sanger sequencing to analyze full‐length coding sequences of HBA2, HBA1 and HBB, and supplemented with MQF‐PCR to predict common α‐globin gene gross deletions and pathogenic mutations in the HBB non‐coding region.

Characteristics and Advantages of cDNA Sanger Sequencing in the Detection Strategy

In recent years, many new thalassemia‐genotyping methods had focused on the accuracy of genotyping, the expansion of the detection spectrum and the convenience of the operation. Xiong et al. established a four‐color real‐time PCR‐based melting curve analysis method for β‐thalassemia genotyping. Using this method to detect 15 common mutations in a validation cohort of 1022 samples yielded coincidence rate was 99.4% [28]. That was easy to operate but cannot identify mutations beyond hotspots. Shang et al. used NGS to reanalyze 2087 samples that previously genotyped by traditional molecular methods, the assay failed to detect only one large deletion in the a‐globin cluster. In addition, 10,111 couples were also screened by NGS, and a total of 4180 positive genotypes were reported, which was 35 cases more than the traditional method [15]. NGS has a broad detection spectrum, but the operation and analysis were still complex. Xu et al. used long‐molecule sequencing to analyze 74 samples and confirmed that the genotypes were consistent with those from traditional genotyping method. However, the error rate was approximately 1 in 200 nucleotides in the subreads [14]. In our study, the full‐length coding sequences of HBA2/HBA1/HBB were performed and analyzed by Sanger sequencing with high accuracy. Additionally, to overcome the drawback of short read length, we used cDNA instead of DNA as the template, and only three reactions were required to complete the above analysis. The results showed that cDNA sequencing can detect substitution mutations in the CDS with an accuracy of 100%. Its detection spectrum was similar to that of NGS, but it did not require complex bioinformatics analysis. The method was simple, intuitive and low‐cost. Unexpectedly, we found that the known small‐deletion and small‐insertion mutations were completely ineffective, that might be due to the attenuation of the transcriptional ability of these mutant strands or the existence of clearance mechanism, resulting in a significantly lower expression level compared to the wild strands, and an “allelic dropout” phenomenon occurred during PCR.

Our result was similar to that of Rawa et al.'s study. They had investigated two small‐deletion mutations located in exon 3 of the HBB and found that the mutated sequences could not be obtained, and believed that this could in principle result from rapid degradation of the aberrant transcript [29]. Subsequently, we considered setting an AS‐MQF‐PCR to address the methodological shortcomings. After the MQF‐PCR reaction, the mutation of c.126_129del and c.216_217insA were accurately identified in the samples of β41–42M/βN and β71–72M/βN, respectively. The results indicated the existence of the mutant strand transcription products with small deletions or insertions, but they were much lower than the products of the wild strand.

Characteristics and Advantages of MQ‐PCR in the Detection Strategy

Previous studies have suggested that the ratio of α/β‐globin could predict the impact of mutations on function. Zhang et al. detected and analyzed the ratio of α/β‐globin mRNA expression levels in two cases. The results indicated that the ratio of HBA2:c.53delC heterozygote was decreased, while for HBA2:c.95+11_95+34del CTCCCCTGCTCCGACCCGGGCTCC had no significant effect [20]. Ranjbaran et al. conducted tests on the β/α mRNA ratio of β‐thalassemia carriers and found that the mean of ratios in normal population, minor, intermediate, and major β‐thalassemia were 0.99, 0.81, 0.69, and 0.69 respectively [30]. In our studies, PAR1 and PAR2 levels can be used to identify the common gross‐deletion mutant‐types of the α‐globin gene and to predict pathogenic mutations in the HBB non‐coding region.

The expression levels of the three genes in the gross‐deletion α‐mutant types show that there may be a feedback mechanism for the balance of the α/β chains: Due to the downregulation of α‐globin gene expression, the expression level of HBB also decreased to maintain the balance of the chains. The results showed that in addition to PAR1 causing the expected changes in the expression levels of HBA2 and HBA1, −α3.7/αα also experienced an increase in PAR2, which was due to its different gene‐breakage pattern from −α4.2/αα. The breakpoints of −α4.2 type were located in the non‐coding sequence upstream and downstream of HBA2, that resulted in the loss of expression of HBA2, and only expressed the HBA1. In contrast, the breakpoints on both sides of the −α3.7 type were located in the coding regions of HBA2 and HBA1. After the breakage, the sequence was spliced into a new alpha‐globin gene. This type had the regulatory region of HBA2 combined with the new alpha‐globin gene, and its expression level was close to that of HBA2. Previous study has shown that the expression level of HBA2 was significantly higher than that of HBA1 [31]. Our study also confirmed this conclusion: the expression level of HBA2 was 2.83 times that of HBA1 in the wild type. Therefore, −α4.2 type has a greater impact on the α/β chain balance compared to the −α3.7 type. The reason for the increase in PAR1 level in the —SEA/αα may be related to the lower expression level of HBA2 compared to HBA1. In addition, the mutations located in the non‐coding region of HBB, −28 M and 654 M, resulted in a significant decrease in the PAR2. Although the PAR1 levels also changed, there was no statistical difference.

Limitations of the Detection Strategy

When employed as an analytical template, cDNA also had several limitations that are readily apparent. Due to the characteristics of mRNA, mutations in the HBB non‐coding regions and gross deletions of α‐globin gene can only be predicted but not directly determined for genotypes. The Gap‐PCR or PCR‐RDB methods are more effective for the above types. However, it may still be an effective detection strategy, especially as it has a mutation screening scope similar to that of NGS, while also featuring simple operation and low cost. What's more, the additional MQF‐PCR method can also further explain the reasons for the observed phenotypic differences.

In conclusion, our study presented a detection strategy for thalassemia genotyping based on peripheral blood mRNA. Although the strategy for mRNA had several limitations, it still represented a strategy worth considering for thalassemia genotyping: it can provide short‐length products, which can be analyzed using Sanger sequencing to achieve comprehensive screening of the entire coding sequence for three genes. And MQF‐PCR partially‌ explain the reasons for the differences in phenotype caused by mutations. Moreover, the analysis of mRNA also implied its advantages, limitations, and possible clinical application in genetic testing.
