The impact of alcohol and illicit substance use on the pharmacokinetics of first-line TB drugs
Authors: Marie Wijk, Kamunkhwala Gausi, Samantha Malatesta, Sarah E Weber, Richard Court, Bronwyn Myers, Tara Carney, Charles D H Parry, C Robert Horsburgh, Laura F White, Lubbe Wiesner, Robin M Warren, Caitlin Uren, Helen McIlleron, Frank Kloprogge, Paolo Denti, Karen R Jacobson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn South Africa, an estimated 11% of the population have high alcohol use, a major risk factor for TB. Alcohol and other substance use are also associated with poor treatment response, with a potential mechanism being altered TB drug pharmacokinetics.ObjectivesTo investigate the impact of alcohol and illicit substance use on the pharmacokinetics of first-line TB drugs in participants with pulmonary TB.MethodsWe prospectively enrolled participants ≥15 years old, without HIV, and initiating drug-susceptible TB treatment in Worcester, South Africa. Alcohol use was measured via self-report and blood biomarkers. Other illicit substances were captured through a urine drug test. Plasma samples were drawn 1 month into treatment pre-dose, and 1.5, 3, 5 and 8 h post-dose. Non-linear mixed-effects modelling was used to describe the pharmacokinetics of rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol. Alcohol and drug use were tested as covariates.ResultsThe study included 104 participants, of whom 70% were male, with a median age of 37 years (IQR 27–48). Alcohol use was high, with 42% and 28% of participants having moderate and high alcohol use, respectively. Rifampicin and isoniazid had slightly lower pharmacokinetics compared with previous reports, whereas pyrazinamide and ethambutol were consistent. No significant alcohol use effect was detected, other than 13% higher ethambutol clearance in participants with high alcohol use. Methaqualone use reduced rifampicin bioavailability by 19%.ConclusionNo clinically relevant effect of alcohol use was observed on the pharmacokinetics of first-line TB drugs, suggesting that poor treatment outcome is unlikely due to pharmacokinetic alterations. That methaqualone reduced rifampicin means dose adjustment may be beneficial.

Introduction

Alcohol consumption, particularly heavy consumption, is recognized as a significant risk factor for both development of TB disease and poor TB treatment outcomes.1 Globally, an estimated 700 000 new TB infections were associated with alcohol use disorders in 2022. Alcohol use has also been associated with a 35% higher risk of TB compared with no alcohol use [relative risk (RR) 1.35; 95% CI 1.09–1.68], and as many as 17% of incident cases and 15% of deaths due to TB could potentially be prevented by reducing alcohol use.2 The African region has some of the highest alcohol-attributable TB mortality, with six countries, including South Africa, having five times higher alcohol-attributable TB mortality than the global average. Furthermore, high alcohol use, defined as a volume or pattern of alcohol use resulting in adverse health outcomes,3 has been associated with delayed culture conversion and higher rates of relapse in patients treated for TB.4 Causal pathways that lead to these worse outcomes are unclear. Low TB drug exposure, mediated by pharmacokinetic changes, could explain poorer treatment responses, thereby identifying potential for a therapeutic intervention.5–7

Prior studies have suggested that alcohol use may impact the pharmacokinetics of the first-line TB drugs. Kimerling et al.8 explored factors affecting serum concentrations of rifampicin and isoniazid in patients with TB with poor treatment response. The investigators found approximately five times higher rifampicin serum concentrations at 2 h post-dosing in individuals who had reported >1 drink/day, possibly related to higher solubility of rifampicin in alcohol or an increase in rifampicin absorption in patients with alcohol use.8 The increased rifampicin concentration due to alcohol use should improve outcomes, but may also increase adverse events.9 Others have investigated the effect of alcohol on isoniazid pharmacokinetics.10–12 Koriakin et al.10 investigated isoniazid pharmacokinetics in 26 persons with TB and long-term moderate or high alcohol use. Maximal concentrations, calculated using non-compartmental analysis (NCA), were found to be 35% lower compared with non-drinking controls, possibly due to reduced absorption and increased metabolism. Lester et al.11 tested the hypothesis that heavy alcohol intake would result in slower inactivation of isoniazid in 28 participants with different drinking patterns. NCA was used to estimate isoniazid half-life in all participants and no correlation with alcohol use was found. However, a 30% shortening of the half-life of isoniazid when administering alcohol to two non-drinking subjects was reported. A more recent study by Wilcke et al.12 reported no effect of alcohol on the pharmacokinetics of isoniazid when using NCA to compare exposures in 10 healthy individuals receiving a single dose of isoniazid on two occasions, with and without prior administration of alcohol. Little can be found in literature on the impact of illicit drug use on the pharmacokinetics of first-line TB drugs. McCance-Katz et al.13 investigated the impact of buprenorphine on the pharmacokinetics of rifampicin in 12 individuals. Buprenorphine is an opioid partial agonist, and although rarely used illicitly, its pathways are similar to opioids.14 The study found no effect of buprenorphine on rifampicin pharmacokinetics. However, the study compared rifampicin pharmacokinetic estimates obtained through NCA with literature of rifampicin pharmacokinetics in the absence of buprenorphine co-administration, rather than using a crossover design.

The prior alcohol-related studies also have limitations. For some, alcohol use was a cofactor that the study was not specifically powered for, and others did not report standardized alcohol use measures or only used self-report, which can be vulnerable to underreporting. Recognition and capture of potential confounders between alcohol use and the pharmacokinetics of first-line TB drugs for model adjustment is also important. These include nutritional status, liver damage and adherence to treatment.15,16 Additionally, how far into treatment the pharmacokinetics are evaluated is important to consider, especially for rifampicin, which is subject to autoinduction of its own metabolism, further complicating interpretation of pharmacokinetic results.17 Another confounder could be use of other illicit substances. Illicit drug use is not uncommon in persons with TB.18 In South Africa, methaqualone (locally known as Mandrax) and methamphetamine (locally known as tik) are two of the most commonly used illicit drugs.19 Further complicating the picture is the previously reported association between low body weight and low TB drug exposures in patients on the standard first-line regimen,20–22 which is designed to administer the same mg/kg dose regardless of body size, disregarding allometry.23

In contrast to NCA—the methodology employed in the previous studies—non-linear mixed-effects modelling techniques can be used to interpret population pharmacokinetic data and have the ability to attribute the variability in the data to pharmacokinetic parameters related to specific processes of absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion, thus enhancing interpretability, and separate the overlapping effect of confounders.24 A detailed analysis specifically evaluating whether alcohol and other illicit drug use, captured via standardized instruments and blood and urine biomarkers, impacts the pharmacokinetics of TB drugs could lead to improved treatment options. Therefore, our aim was to investigate the impact of alcohol and illicit substance use on the pharmacokinetics of rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol, while adjusting for confounding factors using non-linear mixed-effects modelling.

Methods

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the South African Medical Research Council Human Research Ethics Committee (EC011–5/2016), University of Cape Town Human Research Ethics Committee (497/216), Stellenbosch University Human Research Ethics Committee (N17/04/039 and N18/02/024) and Boston University Medical Campus Institutional Review Board (H-34970). All participants provided written informed consent in either English or Afrikaans.

Study population

The Tuberculosis Treatment and Alcohol Use Study (TRUST, NIAID R01AI119037) in Worcester, South Africa is a prospective cohort study aiming to understand alcohol’s impact on TB treatment response.3 For the pharmacokinetic analysis, eligible participants were a minimum of 15 years of age, had rifampicin-susceptible TB, and were initiating TB treatment. Exclusion criteria included: rifampicin-resistant TB, extrapulmonary TB, having HIV, pregnant at time of enrollment, or treatment for TB in the 2 years prior to the current TB episode.

TB treatment

Participants were administered fixed-dose combination tablets once daily containing rifampicin (150 mg), isoniazid (75 mg), pyrazinamide (400 mg) and ethambutol (275 mg) for 2 months, followed by rifampicin and isoniazid for an additional 4 months. The dose was adjusted by body weight, as per the WHO guidelines.25

Data collection

Following enrollment, sociodemographic data were collected as well as blood and urine samples. Study nurses conducted behavioural assessments, including the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT), a score that assesses alcohol consumption and associated consequences over the past 12 months and categorizes people into low-risk (0–7 points), hazardous (8–14 points) or dependent (15–40 points) alcohol consumption.26 Timeline Follow-Back (TLFB),27 to assess amount of alcohol used in the 2 weeks prior to treatment initiation, and phosphatidylethanol (PEth), a biomarker reflecting alcohol use in the past 3 weeks, were also determined.28 We performed urine testing to assess recent use of methaqualone, methamphetamine, amphetamines, opiates, cocaine and cannabis.

At between 4 and 8 weeks of treatment, when all drugs are expected to have reached steady-state (including the autoinduction of rifampicin),22 we collected pharmacokinetic samples under fasting conditions pre-dose, and at 1.5, 3, 5 and 8 h post-dose. We captured time of previous doses using directly observed therapy, a method recommended by the WHO to record adherence.29

Drug quantification and genotyping

Plasma drug concentrations were determined using LC-MS/MS at the Division of Clinical Pharmacology, University of Cape Town. The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) was 0.117 mg/L for rifampicin, 0.105 mg/L for isoniazid, 0.2 mg/L for pyrazinamide and 0.0844 mg/L for ethambutol. The inter-day accuracies and precisions are available in Table S1 (available as Supplementary data at JAC Online). Genotyping was done at the Centre for Proteomic and Genomic Research using the Human, Health and Hereditary in Africa (H3Africa) array30 to obtain the genotypes for sites in N-acetyltransferase 2 (NAT2). NAT2 metabolizer status was determined for each participant based on combinations of rs1799930, rs1799931, rs1801279 and rs1801280 into fast, intermediate or slow isoniazid metabolizers, as described by Hein and Doll.31

Categorization of alcohol use groups

Participants with PEth <50 ng/mL,32 low risk/abstinence on AUDIT, and abstinence on TLFB, were classified as ‘low exposure’. We conducted a latent class analysis using the FlexMix package to classify all remaining participants into alcohol use groups. We modelled AUDIT, TLFB and PEth using a multinomial distribution and selected the number of classes using the Akaike information criterion. Participants were classified into exposure groups based on predicted class assignments, leading to low-, moderate- and high-use groups.

Pharmacokinetic modelling analysis

We analysed the pharmacokinetics of each drug using non-linear mixed-effects modelling in NONMEM version 7.5.0,33 and used Pearl-speaks-NONMEM (PsN),34 Pirana35 and R36 version 4.2.1 to aid the modelling process. One- and two-compartment disposition models were tested, with first-order elimination and first-order absorption (possibly preceded by a lag time or a series of transit compartments). For rifampicin, saturable elimination with a liver compartment was tested, as previously suggested.22 The NAT2 genotype was included in the model for isoniazid, considering it is a key determinant of exposure, and a mixture model was tested to assign metabolizer status to the participants with missing genotype data.37–39 Elimination via hepatic extraction was also tested for rifampicin and isoniazid.40 Between-subject variability (BSV) and between-occasion variability (BOV) separating the doses, were assumed to be log-normally distributed and tested on disposition parameters and absorption parameters, respectively. Each dose and its consecutive samples were treated as a separate occasion. A combined additive and proportional model was used to describe the residual variability, with the additive component constrained to be at least 20% of the LLOQ. Samples below LLOQ (BLQ) were handled with the M6 method according to Beal41 with an additive error increased by 50% of LLOQ for imputed data. The M3 method was also investigated but resulted in minimization issues including failure to obtain covariance matrix and complete convergence, as previously reported by Keizer et al.42

Allometric scaling was included on disposition parameters to adjust for the effect of body size, with the allometric exponents for clearances and volumes fixed to 0.75 and 1, respectively. Total body weight and fat-free mass43 were tested as body size descriptors. The dose of ethambutol was multiplied by 0.74 to adjust for expression as milligrams ethambutol dihydrochloride (molecular weight = 277.23 g/mol)44 rather than ethambutol (molecular weight = 204.31 g/mol).45 The model development and inclusion of covariates were guided by objective function value, inspection of goodness-of-fit plots and visual predictive checks (VPCs), and physiological plausibility.46 Covariates were tested using a stepwise approach with forward addition (P < 0.05) and backwards elimination (P < 0.001). Covariates tested included: alcohol use (PEth, AUDIT and as combined into alcohol use groups), illicit drug use as well as various biomarkers such as ALT, AST and creatinine clearance calculated using the Cockcroft–Gault formula.47 Covariate effects were also screened for among other variables collected. We also explored correlations between drug exposure and unexplained variabilities (see Supplementary material).

A posteriori parametric power estimation

We used stochastic simulation and estimation35 in PsN (n = 500) using the final models to determine what effect size (defined as an increase in clearance resulting from high alcohol use) we were powered to find.

Rifampicin pharmacokinetics

Rifampicin pharmacokinetics was best described with a one-compartment disposition model with transit compartment absorption and elimination using a well-stirred liver model. Allometric scaling by fat-free mass improved the model fit more than by body weight [difference in OFV (dOFV) = −63.0 versus dOFV = −12.0] and was therefore included on disposition parameters. Saturation of the hepatic extraction significantly improved the model fit [dOFV = −25.8, degrees of freedom (df) = 1, P < 0.001], but estimating the Michaelis-Menten rate constant (Km) rendered the model unstable and it was therefore fixed to the value the model estimated, which is similar to previous reports.48,49 A sensitivity analysis confirmed that fixing Km had no significant effect on the main findings. The typical values for maximum intrinsic clearance (CLint,max · fu) and volume were 42.5 L/h and 55 L, respectively. Participants with reported methaqualone use had 19% lower bioavailability (dOFV = −34.3, df = 1, P < 0.001). A VPC for the final rifampicin model stratified by reported methaqualone use can be found in Figure S1. A trend was observed for participants in the high alcohol use group having 13% higher bioavailability (dOFV = −6.11, df = 1, P = 0.01), but the effect decreased to 9.2% and was no longer significant after adjusting for methaqualone use (dOFV = −1.44, df = 1, P = 0.23), which was statistically much more significant, so alcohol use was consequently not included in the model (Figure S2).

Isoniazid pharmacokinetics

Isoniazid pharmacokinetics was best described with a two-compartment disposition model with transit compartment absorption, and first-order elimination with hepatic extraction to capture the first-pass effect following oral administration.50 Allometric scaling by fat-free mass improved the model fit more than by body weight (dOFV = −27.0 versus dOFV = −2.51) and was therefore included on disposition parameters. Inclusion of NAT2 genotype data along with using a mixture model assigning metabolizer status for participants with missing genotypic data further improved the model fit (dOFV = −49.8, df = 2, P < 0.001). The proportion of slow, intermediate and fast metabolizers was fixed to 36%, 40% and 24%, respectively, based on reported NAT2 genotype proportions in a South African population.51,52 The values for typical intrinsic clearance (CLint · fu) were estimated to 14.8 L/h, 36.3 L/h and 62.4 L/h for slow, intermediate and fast metabolizers, respectively. As the sampling schedule was sparse in the absorption phase of isoniazid, the absorption rate constant was difficult to estimate and was ultimately fixed to a value found by Gausi et al.53 in a study with dense sampling in the absorption phase. A VPC for the final isoniazid model stratified by metabolizer type can be found in Figure S1. We could not find any statistically significant effect of alcohol use or illicit drug use on the pharmacokinetics of isoniazid.

Pyrazinamide pharmacokinetics

Pyrazinamide pharmacokinetics was best described by a one-compartment disposition model with transit compartment absorption and first-order elimination. Allometric scaling by fat-free mass improved the model fit more than by body weight (dOFV = −107 versus dOFV = −35.6) and was therefore included on disposition parameters. The typical values for clearance and volume were 4.48 L/h and 41.4 L, respectively. A 2.49-fold larger BOV in absorption parameters (bioavailability, absorption rate constant and mean transit time) was estimated for the pre-dose concentrations (dOFV = −58.7, df = 1, P < 0.001), i.e. the concentrations resulting from doses whose intake was not observed by study staff. No statistically significant effect of illicit drug use or alcohol use was found on the pharmacokinetics of pyrazinamide.

Ethambutol pharmacokinetics

Ethambutol pharmacokinetics was best described with a two-compartment model with transit compartment absorption and first-order elimination. Allometric scaling by fat-free mass improved the model fit more than by body weight (dOFV = −30.0 versus dOFV = −22.0) and was therefore included on disposition parameters. The typical value for clearance was 48.8 L/h. Creatinine clearance calculated using the Cockcroft–Gault formula was included on clearance (dOFV = −25.7, df = 1, P < 0.001), with each 10 units increase in creatinine clearance from the median leading to a 2.6% increase in clearance. Alcohol use was found to have a statistically significant effect on clearance (dOFV = −9.84, df = 1, P = 0.002) with participants in the high alcohol use group having 13% higher clearance than participants with low or moderate alcohol use. No statistically significant effect of illicit drug use was found on the pharmacokinetics of ethambutol.

A posteriori parametric power estimation

The post hoc power analysis showed that our study had >85% power to detect (with P < 0.05) an increase of 45%, 40%, 30% and 30% in drug clearance caused by high alcohol use for rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol, respectively. This equates to a 31%, 29%, 23% and 23% reduction in AUC, respectively.

Discussion

We investigated the effect of alcohol and other illicit drug use on the pharmacokinetics of rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol used in first-line TB treatment. Alcohol use had no clinically significant effect. This suggests that poor TB treatment outcomes observed in individuals who consume alcohol is not due to pharmacokinetic differences and that providers do not need to dose adjust first-line TB drugs in persons with high levels of alcohol use.

Alcohol use was tested in multiple ways—using PEth and AUDIT alone, as well as the combined alcohol use groups—and none had any clinically significant effect on the pharmacokinetics of the drugs. Each of these measures captures different time frames of alcohol use, and given the likelihood of participants altering their alcohol use in proximity to their TB diagnosis, combining the measures into a single score provides a more comprehensive understanding of their alcohol use. Using the individual measures alone has additional drawbacks, such as individual variability, which could be mitigated through combining them into one variable.54

Although alcohol use was associated with a slight increase in ethambutol clearance, this finding is unlikely to be of clinical significance. We also found a trend suggesting higher rifampicin bioavailability in participants in the high alcohol use group; however, it was modest in size and not statistically significant after accounting for the use of methaqualone in the model. This can be explained by the negative correlation between methaqualone use and alcohol use in our cohort. Methaqualone decreases the bioavailability of rifampicin, and participants using methaqualone tend to have lower alcohol use, as shown in Figure S2. Kimerling et al.8 also reported an increase in rifampicin concentration with alcohol use, but much larger than the effect we observed. However, their study only observed concentrations at 2 h, which is suboptimal for a drug with erratic absorption such as rifampicin. Additionally, their study was not designed to specifically investigate the effect of alcohol use, and a number of confounders were present. For example, individuals were sampled at different times after treatment initiation, and the dose was not adjusted by weight.

The post hoc power analysis suggests that our study was powered to detect whether alcohol use decreases AUC by more than ∼30% for rifampicin and isoniazid, and by 23% for pyrazinamide and ethambutol. Therefore, the results of our study suggest that changes in exposure large enough to be of clinical relevance are unlikely.

Illicit drug use has previously been associated with worse TB treatment outcome; however, similar to alcohol use, the underlying pathways have been sparsely studied.55–57 Suggested mechanisms include impaired immune response, suboptimal adherence to treatment, and delays in seeking care.56,58 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the effect of illicit drugs methaqualone, methamphetamine, opiates and cannabis on the pharmacokinetics of first-line TB treatment. We found that methaqualone use decreased the bioavailability of rifampicin by around 20%, which is a modest reduction but might result in negative long-term effects if used throughout the duration of the treatment. Methaqualone has been reported to induce the activity of microsomal enzymes in rats.59 Because rifampicin is mainly metabolized in the liver through deacetylation,17 induction of these enzymes by methaqualone may explain the decreased rifampicin exposure we observed.

The structural model and parameter estimates found for all four drugs are in line with previous reports.20,22,40,53,60–63 Schematics of the structural models as well as model code can be found in Figures S5–S8 and additional Supplementary material. We found fat-free mass to be a better descriptor of body size compared with body weight for all four drugs, which is also consistent with previous findings. This further strengthens previous reports suggesting that individuals with lower body weight, whose total body weight is accounted for by fat-free mass to a higher extent, have lower drug exposures and thus need higher doses.64,65 The first-line TB treatment dosing guidelines, currently based on mg/kg, need to be optimized to be consistent with allometry and fat-free mass.

For isoniazid, the distribution of the NAT2 metabolizer status in this population is similar to that previously reported in a South African population.51 As genotypic data were only available for a subset of the participants, a mixture model was used to determine the isoniazid metabolizer status of the remaining participants, which has been done previously.40 For pyrazinamide, a 2.49-fold larger BOV in absorption parameters was observed in the pre-dose concentrations, likely accounting for the higher uncertainty in the pre-dosing information. Ethambutol clearance was significantly affected by creatinine clearance, which has been previously reported.66 Most participants within our study had normal renal function (see Table 1). For this reason, the effect of creatinine clearance on ethambutol clearance could not be fully characterized, and extrapolation with this model outside the observed creatinine clearance range should be interpreted with caution.

A limitation of our study was the sampling schedule. Ideally, more samples taken prior to Cmax would have enhanced the description of drug absorption and variability. The pharmacokinetic modelling of isoniazid was also affected by a lack of NAT2 genotype information, which was not available for most of the participants—we therefore used mixture modelling to attribute NAT2 metabolizer status for those with missing data. However, when testing the mixture imputation for participants with available NAT2 metabolizer status we observe good concordance (results not shown), thus supporting the accuracy of the mixture model.

In conclusion, we did not detect any difference in the drug exposure of rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol caused by alcohol use that could explain the poor TB treatment outcomes observed in individuals consuming alcohol. This suggests that poor outcomes are unlikely to be a result of altered pharmacokinetics of first-line anti-TB drugs and further studies should explore other potential mechanisms. Moreover, we found methaqualone use to decrease the bioavailability of rifampicin, warranting further studies into the underlying mechanism.
