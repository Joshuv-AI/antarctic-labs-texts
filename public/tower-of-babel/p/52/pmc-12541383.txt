Artificial sphincter with a new silicone band for treating faecal incontinence: IDEAL 2b prospective multicentre trial
Authors: Christopher Dawoud, José Manuel Devesa, Mathias Löhnert, Rosana Vicente, Sherif Akram Metwalli, Stefan Riss
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe management of faecal incontinence (FI) remains challenging and further treatment modalities are urgently needed. The aim of this study was to assess the efficacy of a novel artificial bowel sphincter (SimplyFI anal band), which is implanted around the anal sphincter complex to enhance continence.MethodsAn exploratory prospective multicentre study based on the IDEAL 2b framework was conducted in patients with FI unresponsive to conservative treatment. Participants underwent SimplyFI implantation, followed by assessments at 1 week and 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after implantation. Intraoperative and postoperative complications were recorded. Functional improvement and quality of life were measured using the St Mark's incontinence score (SMIS) and the Faecal Incontinence Quality of Life Scale (FIQLS).ResultsEighteen patients were included in the study (17 women, 1 man). The median operating time was 27 (range 13–60) min, with a median hospital stay of 2 (range 1–5) days. One intraoperative complication occurred without affecting the outcome. One patient (5.6%) underwent band removal due to anal discomfort without signs of infection. Relative to baseline, significant improvements were seen at 12 months in median scores for both the SMIS (from 16.5 to 12.5; P = 0.013) and the FIQLS (from 2.0 to 2.6; P = 0.006). Anorectal manometry showed a significant increase in the anal resting pressure from preoperative values to 3 months after implantation (median 15.5 versus 19.0 mmHg, respectively; P = 0.037); however, the increase in anal resting pressure was no longer seen at the 12-month follow-up.ConclusionIn this exploratory study of 18 patients, the SimplyFI anal band appears to be safe with short-term improvement in function. Future studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to better define the role of this new treatment modality. Registration number: NCT05708612 (http://www.clinicaltrials.gov).

Introduction

Faecal incontinence (FI) is a disorder characterized by the involuntary loss of bowel contents, including solid and liquid faeces and gas1. Epidemiological studies need to be interpreted with caution due to the risk of under-reporting for embarrassing symptoms, but recent prevalence rates showed a median of 8%, affecting both sexes equally2,3. Although FI can significantly impair quality of life, patients are frequently reluctant to seek medical help.

The aetiology of FI is complex, involving a spectrum of potential causes such as structural damage to the sphincter apparatus, neural impairment, or functional disorders3. When conservative treatments fail, surgery is considered, yet the variability in patient outcomes has been a persistent concern, with some patients experiencing little to no improvement4.

Sacral neuromodulation remains a key surgical therapy, with success rates ranging from 60 to 80%4. The SphinKeeper® (THD, Correggio, Italy) has been introduced recently as an alternative approach to treat FI by implantation of solid prostheses into the intersphincteric space5. Although the complication rate following SphinKeeper® implantation was low, functional improvement was achieved in only approximately 50% of patients6.

Artificial bowel sphincters were implanted with good functional success rates in recent few decades, but the infection rates were high and led to a high incidence of surgical device removal7,8.

SimplyFI, developed by the Agency for Medical Innovations (A.M.I.; Feldkirch, Austria) and one of the authors (JMD), has emerged as a novel minimally invasive method for the surgical management of FI9. This method involves the placement of a biocompatible silicone band around the external anal sphincter to exert controlled pressure and improve incontinence symptoms.

The objective of the present prospective approval study was to evaluate the safety profile of SimplyFI implantation in patients with FI. Additional aims were to assess the efficacy of this intervention and its effect on quality of life in the short term.

Methods

Study design and participants

A multicentre prospective single-arm study was conducted at five hospitals in three European countries conforming to the IDEAL (Idea, Development, Exploration, Assessment and Long-term follow-up) Stage 2b (exploratory) framework. The protocol was approved by the local ethics committees of the participating centres. All patients provided written informed consent before enrolment.

To be eligible for inclusion, patients had to be aged ≥ 18 years with a history of FI for at least 6 months and unresponsive to conservative therapy. Patients were included after preoperative assessment, with follow-up visits scheduled for 1 week and 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after implantation.

Patients were excluded if they a history of significant obstructed defaecation assessed by the Obstructed Defaecation Syndrome score10, current external full thickness rectal and vaginal prolapse, inflammatory bowel disease, colorectal cancer, previous resection of the rectum, pelvic radiation, and anal fistula.

Demographic data were recorded and standardized. Validated questionnaires were used to assess pelvic floor function and quality of life. The severity of FI was determined using the Browning and Parks classification11, ranging from grade I (normal continence) to grade IV (complete incontinence for solid/liquid stool and gas). All participants underwent endoanal ultrasound and endoanal manometry before and after surgery.

Study registration

This study conforms to the IDEAL framework and represents a Stage 2b (exploration) study as per the IDEAL recommendations12. The IDEAL Stage 2b checklist was completed and is provided in the supplementary methods. This study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05708612).

Surgical implantation technique

The operation is conducted under general or spinal anaesthesia. Patients are given an enema before surgery and perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis, consisting of a first-generation cephalosporin and metronidazole, which is continued orally for 5 days after the procedure.

Patients are positioned in the lithotomy position and the perianal region disinfected with povidone-iodine. Subsequently two perianal incisions are made at 3 o’clock and 9 o’clock positions, each approximately 2 cm in length, outside the external anal sphincter muscle. Dissection into the ischiorectal fossa is performed. A ventral tunnel is created by commencing at one incision to meet the other via blunt finger dissection and blunt clamp dissection through the rectovaginal septum. The band (Fig. 1) is guided through the ventral tunnel. A corresponding procedure is replicated at the dorsal aspect. Finally, the band is guided through the complete circumference of the newly established tunnel. The band is tightened through the closure slot into the adjustment grooves (levels 1–10), ensuring that no twisting of the band or perforation of organs has occurred (Fig. 2). The tightness of the band (levels 1–10) is adjusted at the discretion of the surgeon. The overhang of the band is cut off with a safety margin of 1 cm, and closure of both the subcutaneous tissue and the skin is performed.

Outcome measures

All patients underwent preoperative and postoperative endoanal ultrasound to investigate the configuration of the internal anal sphincter and external anal sphincter (Fig. 3). To analyse resting, squeezing, and straining pressure, anorectal manometry was performed in each patient before and after the surgery (normal pressure ranges are shown in Fig. S1).

Functional outcome was evaluated using standardized validated questionnaires before SimplyFI implantation and at every follow-up visit. St Mark's incontinence score (SMIS)13 was used to assess the severity of FI and the Faecal Incontinence Quality of Life Scale (FIQLS)14 was used to evaluate quality of life. Postoperative pain was defined using a visual analogue scale (0–10 points, where 0 indicates no pain)15.

Study endpoints

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the safety of the surgical technique and to record any associated adverse events, defined as intraoperative and postoperative complications during the 12-month follow-up period. A secondary aim was to assess functional efficacy in terms of improvements in FI symptoms and quality of life.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS® Statistics for Mac, Version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). For descriptive statistics, the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to evaluate normality of data distribution. For normally distributed data, the mean and standard deviation (s.d.) are reported. Otherwise, data are reported as the median with interquartile range (i.q.r.). Accordingly, Students t tests or Friedman signed-rank tests were used as appropriate. Non-parametric tests were used for the exploratory statistics due to the sample size of 18. Here, continuous variables are presented as either the median with i.q.r. or mean (s.d.), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as numbers with percentages. Quantitative variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or Wilcoxon test. To explore dichotomous variables, χ2 tests were used. P < 0.050 (two-tailed) was considered to denote statistical significance.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between April 2023 and December 2023, 18 patients (17 women, 1 man) with a median age of 66 (i.q.r. 50.0–74.3) years were treated using the SimplyFI silicon band. The grade of FI was defined using the classification of Browning and Park11; nine patients (50.0%) reported control of solid stool only (grade III) and the remaining nine patients (50.0%) were classified as grade IV, indicating complete incontinence.

Surgical outcome

The median operating time was 27 (range 13–60) min. Fifteen implants were adjusted to level 1 during the surgery. In 3 patients, the band was set to a higher level (level 2 in one patient, level 3 in one patient, and level 4 in one patient). The median length of hospital stay was 2 (range 1–5) days.

Safety outcome

One intraoperative complication occurred during blunt dissection. Specifically, when creating the tunnel for the anal band, the vagina was perforated. The perforation site was subsequently closed with absorbable sutures and the SimplyFI band was implanted without any further postoperative complications.

In one patient, the postoperative endoanal ultrasound demonstrated that the band was twisted 180°, without causing discomfort to the patient; no further action was required.

No other intraoperative or in-hospital complications were reported. None of the patients experienced any degree of local or systemic sepsis. There was no evidence of any acute or chronic inflammatory response around the implanted silicone band.

One patient reported perianal discomfort, after 2 months after the implantation (SimplyFI adjusted on level 3). Subsequently, the anal band was explanted based on the patient's wishes.

Four patients (22.2%) reported obstructed defaecation symptoms after implantation of the band. This occurred in three patients in the first weeks after implantation and resolved with a single administration of oral medication or suppositories. In the remaining patient, the symptoms persisted until the last follow-up visit. There was one death during the study period (cardiac arrest) that was unrelated to the product.

Postoperative pain was assessed using a visual analogue scale. One week after surgery, the median pain score was 2.5 (range 0.0–5.0), and this decreased to a median score of 0.0 (range 0.0–0.0) after 12 months (P = 0.005).

Functional outcome

The SMIS decreased significantly from baseline to the 12-month follow-up (median 16.5 (i.q.r. 12.5–18.0) versus 12.5 (i.q.r. 5.3–15.8), respectively; P = 0.013; Fig. 4a), demonstrating an amelioration of FI after SimplyFI implantation.

The FIQLS was completed by 15 patients, with significant improvements from baseline to the end of the 12-month follow-up period in the median scores for coping/behaviour (1.5 (i.q.r. 1.1–2.2) versus 2.0 (i.q.r. 1.5–3.4), respectively; P = 0.012), depression/self-perception (2.4 (i.q.r. 1.4–3.4) versus 3.29 (i.q.r. 1.8–4.0), respectively; P = 0.018), and embarrassment (1.7 (i.q.r. 1.0–2.7) versus 2.33 (i.q.r. 1.7–3.7), respectively; P = 0.041), but not in lifestyle scores (2.5 (i.q.r. 1.8–3.3) versus 2.7 (i.q.r. 1.9–4.0), respectively; P = 0.220).

In addition, the total FIQLS score improved significantly during the follow-up period from a median of 2.0 (i.q.r. 1.5–2.8) at baseline to 2.6 (i.q.r. 1.9–3.7) at the 12-month follow-up (P = 0.006; Fig. 4b).

Regarding anal manometry, there was a significant improvement in resting pressure from baseline to the 3-month follow-up (median 15.5 (i.q.r. 8.5–27.5) versus 19.0 (i.q.r. 14.0–24.5) mmHg, respectively; P = 0.037). There was also an improvement in squeezing pressure from baseline to the 3-month follow-up, although statistical significance was not reached (median 32 (i.q.r. 19.5–44.0) versus 34 (i.q.r. 27.0–47.0) mmHg, respectively; P = 0.054). However, evaluations at the 12-month follow-up revealed no statistically significant changes in either resting or squeezing pressure compared with preoperative values.

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to evaluate the safety profile of SimplyFI, a novel device for treating FI. This small exploratory study demonstrated that the implantation of this anal band is safe, with minimal intraoperative complications. In addition, in the first 12 months, the infection rate was low and only one band required removal due to anal discomfort.

Considering the numerous previous attempts to treat FI by perianal sphincter augmentations, the short-term results presented here are promising.

The idea of placing foreign material around the external sphincter muscle is not new; for example, Thiersch described surgical techniques in the 1950s to treat patients with full-thickness rectal prolapse16. In subsequent studies17–24, different materials, such as MERSILENE™ bands, dacron vascular prostheses, dacron-reinforced with silastic, polyester, silicone bands, or silastic mesh, were implanted. These techniques were abandoned, frequently due to high explantation rates caused by migration and material detorsion.

The use of various artificial bowel sphincters led to good functional outcomes but caused high complication rates during the postoperative period. Reoperation rates were high, reaching 95% in some series20,21, and led to a significant number of device removals8. Van der Wilt et al.25 reported their long-term experiences with an Acticon® artificial bowel sphincter, finding device removal rates of 49.2%, with each patient undergoing between one and six reoperations. The primary reasons for revision were device failure and infection25. At the 5-year follow-up, only 35% of patients had their continence restored25.

Magnetic anal sphincter (MAS) augmentation was another surgical option for treating faecal incontinence, although current clinical evidence remains conflicting. The Fenix™ device was designed to augment the native anal sphincter, with the implants being a series of titanium beads with magnetic cores linked together with independent titanium wires26. The findings of a recently published meta-analysis27 showed that the overall morbidity rate with MAS augmentation was 61.8%, with postoperative adverse events including MAS explanation, infection pain, and obstructed defaecation.

A randomized clinical trial28 compared the Fenix™ MAS to sacral nerve stimulation for the treatment of faecal incontinence. The primary endpoint was defined as successful treatment, indicated by the device being in use and achieving a ≥ 50% improvement in the Cleveland Clinic Incontinence Score at 18 months after randomization. The findings of that trial demonstrated no significant difference in success rates when excluding participants with unevaluable outcomes28. The overall success rate was 12.5%, with no statistically significant difference between the Fenix™ MAS and sacral nerve stimulation groups (successful in 6 of 41 (14.6%) versus 4 of 39 (10.3%) participants, respectively)28.

Devesa et al.29 described previous experiences with a Jackson-Pratt® flat drain used for anal encirclement; although initial results were good, the authors noted a substantial need for device explantation, primarily due to material degradation. Notably, an improvement of the Wexner incontinence score was achieved in 97% of patients, with follow-up ranging from 2 to 60 months29.

The SphinKeeper® procedure was introduced in 2016 and followed similar therapeutic concepts30. However, following insertion of up to ten implants into the intersphincteric groove, an improvement of incontinence symptoms was only achieved in up to 50% of treated patients6. In contrast, the complication rate was very low, with minimal surgical revision of the prostheses required6,31,32.

In a study conducted at three centres7, the Soft Anal Band, an artificial anal sphincter, led to complications in 21 patients (48.8%). Unlike the SimplyFI, the artificial anal sphincter is implanted under the skin and consists of four components connected by silicone tubes. When the patient applies pressure to a balloon, liquid is transferred to the band, enabling continence. For defaecation, the patient presses the valve, which moves the liquid from the band back to the balloon, thereby opening the sphincter. The system includes a subcutaneous safe puncture port (semi-automatic pump) located lateral to the iliac crest, which enables precise adjustment of liquid volume within the system. The reported adverse events included 10 surgical problems and 13 technical problems related to prosthetic component failures or handling errors7. The median time for complications was 3 months, and the device had to be explanted in 9 patients (21%)7. In contrast, the benefit of the SimplyFI is its simple use, because no handling is required to control stool.

This study has some limitations that need to be addressed. The number of patients included in the study is small and the follow-up duration is short (12 months). However, the results provide some good evidence about the short-term safety profile of the SimplyFI during and after surgery. Given the promising short-term results and safety profile, a subsequent IDEAL stage 3 study should be considered. A randomized clinical trial comparing the SimplyFI device with sacral nerve stimulation could provide robust evidence regarding long-term efficacy and patient selection.
