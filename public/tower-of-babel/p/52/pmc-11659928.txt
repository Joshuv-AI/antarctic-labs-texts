Protective role of antibodies in enteric virus infections: Lessons from primary and secondary immune deficiencies
Authors: Quentin Riller, Muriel Schmutz, Jacques Fourgeaud, Alain Fischer, Bénédicte Neven
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryEnteric viruses are the main cause of acute gastroenteritis worldwide with a significant morbidity and mortality, especially among children and aged adults. Some enteric viruses also cause disseminated infections and severe neurological manifestations such as poliomyelitis. Protective immunity against these viruses is not well understood in humans, with most knowledge coming from animal models, although the development of poliovirus and rotavirus vaccines has extended our knowledge. In a classical view, innate immunity involves the recognition of foreign DNA or RNA by pathogen recognition receptors leading to the production of interferons and other inflammatory cytokines. Antigen uptake and presentation to T cells and B cells then activate adaptive immunity and, in the case of the mucosal immunity, induce the secretion of dimeric IgA, the more potent immunoglobulins in viral neutralization. The study of Inborn errors of immunity (IEIs) offers a natural opportunity to study nonredundant immunity toward pathogens. In the case of enteric viruses, patients with a defective production of antibodies are at risk of developing neurological complications. Moreover, a recent description of patients with low or absent antibody production with protracted enteric viral infections associated with hepatitis reinforces the prominent role of B cells and immunoglobulins in the control of enteric virus.

INTRODUCTION

Enteric viruses are a diverse group of viruses that primarily infect the cells of the small gastrointestinal (GI) tract of humans and animals. 1 These viruses are mostly RNA viruses with positive‐sense single‐strand (ss) or double‐strand (ds) RNA transmitted via the fecal–oral route although other routes exist such as aerosolization or saliva when salivary glands excrete viruses. 2 Most enteric viruses are non‐enveloped allowing them to survive the acidic and enzymatic conditions of the gastrointestinal tract, and increasing their stability in the environment, their infectivity, and transmissibility. These viruses are a major cause of gastroenteritis (GE) that are usually self‐resolving in healthy subjects although they are still a major cause of death worldwide, in particular in young children, aged adults, and immunocompromised patients. Extraintestinal dissemination can also cause more complex conditions such as central nervous system (CNS) infections. 3 The common enteric viruses and most established causes of GE in humans include the rotavirus, a virus belonging to the family Reoviridae and the leading cause of diarrheal mortality in the world, and the norovirus belonging to the Caliciviridae family, also highly transmissible and a leading cause of acute GE across all age groups. 1 , 4 , 5 , 6 Sapoviruses, another member of Caliciviridae, are less well known but are still significant agents of GE. 7 Astroviruses of the Astroviridae family commonly cause GE across all ages. 8 As opposed for most enteric viruses, adenoviruses are double‐stranded DNA viruses, they belong to the Adenoviridae family, and some strains are well‐known causes of GE. Finally, Aichi virus, a Kobuvirus 9 , 10 that belongs to the Picornaviridae family, has been associated with GE. Enteroviruses are also part of the Picornaviridae family, including enteroviruses, polioviruses, coxsackieviruses, and echoviruses. 1 , 3 , 11 These viruses are transmitted via the fecal–oral route, they usually cause mild gastrointestinal manifestations (or alternatively infection can be symptom‐free) but they can also cause extraintestinal manifestations including severe neurological disorders. In a significant proportion of cases of acute infectious diarrhea, no agent can be identified suggesting that additional enteric viruses might contribute to acute GE in humans. Thanks to metagenomic next‐generation sequencing (NGS), allowing unsupervised detection of pathogens, one can speculate that new virus strains will be discovered in the future as recently shown for astroviruses. 8 Other viruses are considered enteric viruses but will not be the focus of our review, including hepatitis A and E viruses. Although studies of immune responses to poliovirus and rotavirus vaccinations in humans brought some understanding on immunity to these viruses, most of our knowledge on immunity to enteric viruses is derived from studies in mice, the relevance of which may be questionable given that enteric viruses may not induce the same disease in mice and human or infect different tissues. 12 The study of rare inborn errors of immunity (IEIs) provides a tremendous opportunity to study the role of the different arms of immunity involved in the control of pathogens and identify nonredundant molecules/pathways that are essential. Inborn errors of immunity (IEIs) represent a heterogeneous group of more than 500 genetically defined conditions characterized by a qualitative and/or quantitative defect of the immune responses. 13 Phenocopies of these inborn errors also exist in patients harboring neutralizing auto‐antibodies against various cytokines and interferons. 14 In this review, we will develop the diverse findings about immunity to enteric viruses with a specific focus on human immunology, the role of B‐cell mediated immunity in the control of enteric viruses, and the consequences of a defective production of antibodies. 15

WHAT DO WE KNOW ABOUT IMMUNE RESPONSES TO ENTERIC VIRUSES IN HUMANS?

Enteric viruses have an oro‐fecal transmission route, and the intestinal barrier is therefore the first line of defense against infection. Viruses have first to transit through the upper gastrointestinal tract where the low pH of the stomach and digestive enzymes exert an antiviral effect. In the small intestine, intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) secrete a mucosal barrier formed by the cross‐linking of highly glycosylated mucin proteins that impede the possibility for viruses to infect the cells in many ways. 1 , 12 This mucus is packed and forms a physical and chemical barrier with antimicrobial agents such as α‐defensins. In addition, mucus layer is important to concentrate secretory dimeric immunoglobulins A (SIgA) antibodies that are actively transferred into the lumen of the digestive tract through IECs and able to neutralize viruses before they reach the cells. This mucus is constantly excreted through digestive tract. In addition, villi and microvilli forming a brush border at the apical site and tight junctions of epithelial cells formed by multiprotein adhesion complexes between neighboring cells further impede viral entry in the cells or in other adjoining cells, respectively. Moreover, IECs have a high turnover that allows the elimination of infected cells further impeding viral dissemination.

When viral particles infect intestinal epithelial cells after recognition of its cognate receptor, pathogen recognition receptors (PRRs) trigger the innate immune response by detecting viral RNA or DNA. These pathways have been extensively reviewed elsewhere. 12 , 16 , 17 Cytosolic nucleic acids are recognized by cGAS (DNA) and RIG‐I/MDA5 (RNA), while toll‐like receptors (TLRs) detect nucleic acids in endosomes, with TLR3 targeting double‐stranded RNA, TLR7/8 single‐stranded RNA, and TLR9 recognizing CpG motifs in double‐stranded DNA. These pathways activate NF‐kB and the IRF3/IRF7 interferon pathway, leading to pro‐inflammatory cytokine production (IL‐6, TNFα, IL‐1β, and IL‐18) and interferons (types I, II, and III). These interferons induce interferon‐stimulated genes (ISGs), which typically inhibit viral replication, though some viruses evade them. 18 , 19 IECs mainly produce type III interferons due to high peroxisomal MAVS levels, though this response is limited by IFNLR1 receptor expression, which is rare outside of barrier cells like IECs and respiratory epithelium. 20 Despite this, studies of IEIs suggest redundancy in type III interferons, as patients with IL10RB deficiencies (the second co‐receptor of type III interferons with IFNLR1) do not exhibit high susceptibility to enteric viruses but rather suffer from inflammatory bowel disease due to impaired IL‐10 signaling as seen in IL‐10 or IL‐10RA deficiencies. 21 Likewise, patients with defects of type I interferon pathway mostly experience central nervous system or respiratory infections and abnormal response to live‐attenuated vaccines, not severe enteric viral infections, despite high environmental exposure. 22 , 23 Therefore, and up to now, no inborn errors of immunity (IEIs) in the innate immune system have been identified that result in overt susceptibility to enteric viral infections. This suggests that while the innate immune system is crucial, it is globally redundant in controlling these viruses. This redundancy is likely due to the involvement of multiple innate immune pathways, highlighting the robustness of the innate immune response. 24

Human beings face numerous infections with enteric viruses thorough their lifespan and studies suggest that the severity of infection declines with repeated exposure 1 and acquired memory. The highest severity of enteric viral infections is described in children less than 5 years old. This suggests an age‐dependent mucosal and systemic adaptive immunity to enteric viruses, although a study showed that age‐dependent TLR3 expression could explain the difference of severity in rotavirus infection. 25 Most of what we know about mucosal adaptive immunity against enteric viruses comes from animal studies that, for many of them, failed to reproduce the human infection with enteric viruses. It is therefore difficult to conclude on the precise physiology of human gut immunity to enteric viruses and on the role of adaptive immunity, especially on B‐cell immunity, against these viruses in humans. This is in part due to the difficulty to access mucosal tissues in the setting of acute gastroenteritis. Nevertheless, some models of viral infections and the vaccination trials against rotavirus and poliovirus have shed light on the prominent role of B‐cell immunity and especially on SIgA in the protection against enteric viruses, with multiple studies showing correlation between the presence of specific IgA in the lumen and clearance of enteric viruses, both in humans 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 and mice. 32 , 33 Although serum IgG and IgA against enteric viruses can be detected, it is unclear whether these antibodies exert a role in viral clearance in the gut in mice and humans. 34 Nevertheless, secretory dimeric IgA is much more potent to neutralize viruses than monomeric circulating IgA. 35 , 36 Schematically, the Microfold (M) cells of the epithelium process antigens from the lumen to the lymphoid follicles of the gut‐associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) through transcytosis. In the GALT, the prominent lymphoid structures are the Peyer's Patches (PPs) where B cells can undergo class switch recombination (CSR) and somatic hypermutation (SHM) by interacting with follicular dendritic cells and helper T cells (T‐cell dependent IgA). Following their activation, proliferation, and maturation, specific B cells migrate through the lymph to reach the blood and migrate again in the lamina propria of the gut as short‐ or long‐lived memory B cells or plasmablasts producing IgA. 33 This migration to the lamina propria is induced by the expression of the gut‐homing receptors CCR9 and α4β7 after their passage through PPs. 37 , 38

The T‐cell‐mediated immunity to enteric viruses has also been mostly studied in mice. After priming in the draining lymph node, activated T cells migrate to the lamina propria where they can become resident memory T cells and exert effector function upon viral infection. IgA+ B cells are still detected in the lamina propria of CD4+ T‐cell‐deficient mice or MHC‐II deficient mice, and they are still able to clear enteric viruses, suggesting that both T‐cell dependent and independent production of IgA occur. 33 , 39 , 40 Another study showed that mice lacking CD4+ T cells were still able to clear norovirus although the viral load was higher during acute infection. CD8+ T‐cell‐deficient mice had a delayed clearance of rotavirus and adenovirus 41 , 42 but are still protected from chronic infection. The cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells was also shown to be redundant to clear norovirus. 41 Gamma‐delta T cells were also found to be dispensable to clear rotavirus, in mice. 43 , 44 Collectively, there is compelling evidence, in the mouse, showing that T cells are largely redundant in the control of enteric viruses, despite the fact that the majority of immune cells in the gut tissues are T cells. In the following sections, we will discuss immunity to diverse enteric viruses with a specific focus on what we know in humans and especially what has been learned from the study of immunodeficiencies, both primary and secondary to treatments.

ENTERIC VIRUSES IN THE SETTINGS OF IMMUNODEFICIENCY AS A NATURAL EXAMPLE OF NONREDUNDANT IMMUNITY AGAINST ENTERIC VIRAL INFECTIONS

Picornaviridaes family

The Picornaviridae family is a highly diverse family of non‐enveloped icosahedral viruses with positive‐sense single‐stranded RNA genome 1 (Table 1). Virions are small, approximately 30 nm in diameter, and have a capsid generally composed of four structural proteins (VP1 to VP4) forming a protective shell for the RNA genome. Some members of the family (such as Parechovirus and Kobuvirus) have a capsid composed of three instead of four structural proteins. Several important genera belong to this family such as Enterovirus, Parechovirus, Kobuvirus, Hepatovirus, Cardiovirus, and Cosavirus among others. 45 Picornaviruses are primarily transmitted via the fecal–oral or the respiratory routes. Cell infection starts with the attachment of the virion to cell receptors, mostly integrins or immunoglobulins‐like proteins (identified for some viruses only), the virus is then taken up by endocytosis, the viral capsid is removed and the viral RNA, acting as mRNA is translated by host cell's ribosomes. The viral RNA is replicated in the cytoplasm. Newly synthesized viral RNA genomes and capsid proteins are assembled into new virions that are released from the host cell through cell lysis. 46 Innate immune responses that detect foreign RNA using sensing proteins (such as RIG‐I, MDA‐5, and TLR3) act in the early control of picornaviral infection. 12 Non‐lytic viral spread has been demonstrated for some picornaviruses such as hepatitis A virus, poliovirus, and coxsackie B3. 47 , 48 The release of virions cloaked in host‐derived membranes protects them from antibody‐mediated neutralization and facilitates the spread of viruses. Nevertheless, the adaptive response mediated by antibodies plays the definitive role in the resolution of the infection as particularly shown for Enteroviruses since patients with agammaglobulinemia develop chronic infections, as do mice lacking B cells 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 and antibody response mediate the protective effect of poliovirus vaccines. 53 , 54 Due to the low fidelity of viral RNA polymerase, picornaviruses display high mutation rates. This characteristic gives them a great potential for adaptation to environment modifications and for escaping the adaptive immune pressure. Recombination can also occur with a variable frequency if two or more RNA viruses infect the same host cell. 55

The Enteroviruses

Among picornaviridae, Enterovirus is the best‐studied genus. It is a large family with 15 species, of which seven are specific to humans: Enteroviruses (EV) A to D and Rhinovirus A to C (that fall outside the scope of this review). There are more than 100 human serotypes among A to D species of enterovirus including poliovirus (the etiological agent of poliomyelitis and prototype of EVs) the coxsackies and the echovirus. 56 Phylogenetic analysis has shown that circulating enteroviruses (EVs) exhibit high mutation rates (due to the errors made by RNA‐dependent viral RNA polymerase) and frequent recombination facilitated by co‐infection, resulting in significant genetic diversity that contributes to widespread epidemics and sporadic outbreaks. Recombination and mutation in the structural region of the capsid can alter the immunogenicity, increasing the ability to evade the adaptive immune system and bypass antibody defenses.

EV are primarily transmitted via the fecal–oral or the respiratory routes. Virions infect cells of the gut mucosa or upper respiratory tract. They replicate in epithelial cells and then spread to regional lymphoid organs before infecting other extraintestinal organs. Human EV are widespread across the globe and are among the most common viruses infecting humans. They often exhibit a seasonal pattern and are characteristically found in the summer and early autumn in temperate areas. Severity and organs involved depend on the viral genotype and the immune status of the recipient. Most enterovirus infections are asymptomatic or cause subclinical or mild symptoms such as mild respiratory symptoms and/or gastrointestinal manifestations. The course of the diseases is usually brief and self‐limited. Hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) is a common benign infection occurring in children <5 years old, characterized by fever and cutaneous vesicles in hands, feet, mouth, and buttocks caused by several EV, mainly coxsackies. Herpangina is another benign condition characterized by fever and ulcers in the oropharynx, also caused mainly by coxsackies. Some non‐polio EV such as coxsackies and echovirus have a neurotropism and can cause a range of central nervous system (CNS) infections such as aseptic meningitis, encephalitis, rhombencephalitis, and myelitis including acute flaccid paralysis. EV are the most frequent cause of viral meningitis when an agent is identified. Conjunctivitis, orchitis, and nephritis may also occur. Coxsackies are also a leading cause of viral myocarditis and pericarditis. EV infections can lead to severe acute diseases in infants and young children causing hepatitis, including fulminant disease with coagulopathy, neonatal sepsis, peri‐myocarditis, or CNS involvement. 3 , 57 Among the EV, two serotypes EV D68 and EV A71 have caused recent outbreaks of severe infections. EV A71 has caused multiple HFMD epidemics since 1969. Although HFMD is usually mild, the EV A71 was frequently associated with brainstem encephalitis and cardiogenic shock with a high rate of mortality. 58 , 59 Enterovirus D68 causes a respiratory infection causing from wheezing to respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation. Less frequently, EV D68‐associated respiratory failure was associated with acute flacid myelitis, encephalitis, or aseptic meningitis. 60 , 61 Whether the virus itself is the cause of the CNS manifestations or its immune‐mediated reactions remain to be determined.

Furthermore, enteroviruses are incriminated in occurrence of dilated cardiomyopathy and have been associated with initiation and/or aggravation of type 1 diabetes mellitus. 62 , 63 , 64 The burden of enterovirus (EV) infections varies globally. Most countries lack standardized EV surveillance systems, making it difficult to accurately assess the impact of EV infections and provide early outbreak warnings. Detailed clinical information and genotyping data should be recorded to understand the link between specific EV genotypes and associated diseases. A comprehensive EV surveillance approach would improve the understanding of EV infections globally. 11 , 59

Enterovirus infections represent a specific threat in patients with IEIs, especially in patients with defect of antibody production. 65 It has long been recognized in patients with X‐linked agammaglobulinemia (XLA), an IEI due to hemizygous mutation in BTK leading to early arrest in B‐cell differentiation, absence of B cells, and panhypogammaglobulinemia. 51 These patients are at risk of developing chronic enterovirus infection of the neurologic central system such as meningoencephalitis and myelitis also referred as chronic enterovirus myeloencephalitis (CEMA). This condition has a chronic course and a wide range of symptoms such as paralysis/paresis, convulsions, developmental regression, cognitive impairment, neurosensorial deafness, ataxia, and dysarthria. 50 , 66 , 67 Increased proteinorachia and pleocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid are inconstant. The definitive diagnosis is made by a positive specific EV PCR in the cerebrospinal fluid or, more cost‐effectively, in the brain biopsy. PCR in the stool, throat, and plasma can be negative when neurological symptoms appear. Dermatomyositis‐like disease syndrome, arthritis, and hepatitis have also been reported. 68 These are serious life‐threatening conditions. This susceptibility to enterovirus is described not only in children but across all ages. 65 Other B‐cell deficiencies are at risk such as patients with autosomal recessive agammaglobulinemia 69 , 70 and those with common variable immunodeficiency (CVID), a condition that develops mostly at teenage or adulthood, characterized by B‐cell dysfunction with impaired B‐cell differentiation into functional secreting plasma cells leading to hypogammaglobulinemia and decreased or absent specific antibody production. 15 , 71 Variable defects of cellular immunity can be associated in sub‐group of CVID patients. 72 Among IEIs, patients with severe combined immune deficiency (SCID) and those with combined T + B immune deficiency (CID) are also threatened by enteroviruses. Patients with X‐linked hyper‐IgM (XHIM), a disease characterized by defect in immunoglobulin class‐switching related to hemizygous mutation of CD40L, can be also affected by enteroviruses but possibly to a lesser extent thanks to the potent protective role of IgM. 73 In SCID, infection with attenuated poliovirus strains is the most prevalent. 74 , 75 Patients who received B‐cell‐depleting therapies such as rituximab are also potent candidates for such complications as highlighted in the literature. 76 There is a wide range of EV serotypes responsible for CEMA and chronic infections in immunodeficient patients. The viral serotype is rarely identified, but when it is accessible, echoviruses are the most frequent, particularly echovirus 11 (but also 6, 9, 13, 18, 25, 30, etc.), as well as coxsackieviruses (A16, B3, and others). There are limited pharmaceutical treatment options available once chronic EV infection develops, all with varying degrees of success, including pleconaril (a picornavirus capsid protein inhibitor), high‐dose IgIV, or intrathecal immunoglobulin. Other capsid protein inhibitors, including pocapavir, and other pharmacological inhibitors of the enterovirus life cycle are at various stages of development 45 . In the general population, enterovirus seroprevalence is typically high due to the widespread and often asymptomatic nature of infections. Seroprevalence tends to increase with age and can vary by region based on outbreaks and circulation. Overall, it is difficult to pinpoint seroprevalence for a single serotype due to the diversity within the enterovirus group, the lack of specific studies, and the variability of the sero‐neutralization assays. 77 These data are of importance for patients with antibody deficiencies treated with immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IgRT). These therapeutic preparations contain pooled antibodies from thousands of healthy plasma donors and should be representative of the donor population seroprevalence. 78 , 79

Poliovirus (PV), responsible for poliomyelitis, is one of the best‐studied enteroviruses. There are three serotypes (PV1‐PV3). After oral entry, PV multiplies in the intestines by infecting enterocytes expressing its receptor, CD155 a CAM‐like protein also expressed on motoneurons in the spinal cord. In rare cases, it spreads through the bloodstream to the CNS. PV can cross the blood–brain barrier by infecting immune cells mostly from myeloid lineage (acting as “Trojan horses”) or directly infecting endothelial cells. 3 Once in the CNS, PV destroys motor neurons, leading to symptoms like paralysis and death in severe cases with respiratory failure. 80

Poliomyelitis has led the world health agencies to provide a vaccine against this virus to eradicate wild poliovirus from the globe (Global Polio Eradication Initiative, GPEI). 11 , 81 Two types of vaccination have been developed, the oral polio vaccine (OPV) with live‐attenuated strain of the poliovirus and the inactivated proteinic polio vaccine given by intramuscular or intradermal injection (IPV). The development of these vaccines, beginning in the 1960s and clinical trials around different strategies of vaccination allowed the study of immunity against poliovirus in humans, while most of what we know about immunity against other enteroviruses came from mice studies. All these studies pointed toward the prominent role of neutralizing secretory IgA in limiting replication of poliovirus in the intestine. While OPV provides mucosal immunity with induction of a local secretory IgA response associated with a reduction in shedding of poliovirus from the intestine, the IPV induces strong systemic immunity and protection from paralytic disease, but does not induce local secretary IgA and has little effect on virus replication in the intestine. 82 , 83 This converged to show the necessary oral route for a proper induction of mucosal immunity with neutralizing secretory IgA (in contrast to the induction of peripheral monomeric IgA by the other proteinic vaccine). 36 Both OPV and IPV provide B‐cell‐mediated long‐term immunity against PV as assessed by seroprevalence and seroneutralization studies in different populations. 84 , 85

One caveat of these oral live‐attenuated vaccines lies in the appearance of polyomyelitis cases related to neurovirulent mutated vaccine strains of poliovirus (called vaccine‐associated paralytic poliomyelitis, VAPP) in patients chronically infected with a vaccine‐derived poliovirus (VDPV). Among these cases, immunodeficiency‐related chronic shedding of VDPV (iVDPV) occurs in patients with an IEI that fail to clear the VDPV and can excrete the virus for a very long period, giving the virus the possibility to mutate and become neurovirulent again. 86 , 87 Of note, iVDPV can remain asymptomatic and thus participate in chronic shedding. 88 This type of VDPV is a natural example of the importance of B‐cell‐mediated immunity to enteroviruses as 57% of patients with iVDPV reported in the literature between 1962 and 2012 had a predominant antibody deficiency (PAD) in a recent literature review, while 22% had a combined immunodeficiency and the others were not classified. 74 The risk of VAPP in the world is approximately four cases per million births while XLA patients have between 1.2% and 4% risk (3000 to 10,000 times more susceptible). 89 , 90 This is in line with the high risk of chronic enterovirus infection in XLA patients, as mentioned above. Although some cases of iVDPV arrived in patients under immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IgRT), we can hypothesize that these therapeutics probably lack the natural neutralizing capacity of luminal secretory IgA or lack neutralizing antibodies to mutated strains. 91 , 92 Of note, patients with autosomal recessive hyper‐IgM immunodeficiency or selective deficiencies of IgA were not reported to suffer from iVDPV, suggesting a role for secretory IgM in the protection against this condition.

Although IgA play a key role in controlling poliovirus, innate immunity is also involved but partly subverted by the virus. Poliovirus can cleave MDA5 in a caspase and proteasome‐dependent way, partially suppressing the type I interferon response. 93 It can also cleave p65, a key NF‐kB transcription factor, reducing inflammatory cytokine production while increasing apoptosis susceptibility. 94 Nevertheless, no reports link type I interferon deficiencies to chronic viral shedding of VDPV or wild poliovirus in IEI patients. This suggests that, despite their proven antiviral roles in vitro and in vivo in mice, interferons are largely redundant in humans for the control of polio viruses. 3 , 12 , 22 Only one study showed OPV elicited lower type I and III interferon responses in XLA patients' dendritic cells compared with controls, indicating an innate immune defect specific to poliovirus, but not influenza, although this did not affect viral replication. 95

The Parechovirus

As compared to enterovirus and poliovirus, understanding of physiopathology and epidemiology of human parechovirus (HPeV) infection is more limited. Among the Picornaviridae, parechoviruses are distinguished by their structure, with a capsid composed of three structural proteins instead of four. The PeV genus is composed of four species, A to D. Only PeV‐A can infect humans. Based on phylogenetic analysis of the VP1 encoding region, 19 serotypes have been described but PeV‐A 1 to 8 are the most prevalent. 96 As for other Picornaviridae, RNA polymerase of PeV‐A lacks proofreading activity resulting in high mutation rate that in association with recombination plays a role in PeV‐A evolution. PeV‐A circulate worldwide, but prevalent serotypes vary between continents. 96 Infection is presumed to occur via fecal–oral and respiratory routes based on similarities to infection routes of EV. For most PeV‐A genotypes, the receptors involved in viral binding and entry, as the cell tropism have not been identified. Lifecycles of PeV‐A are assumed to be similar to other picornaviridae although experimental evidence is lacking.

Infections with PeV‐A usually affect neonates and young children. 97 , 98 Clinical manifestations range from asymptomatic or mild symptoms to severe disease such as sepsis‐like illness and central nervous system involvement in neonates. PeV‐A are the second etiology of this condition after EV, 96 , 99 mostly related to PeV‐A3, followed by PeV‐A4 and 5. It is worth noting that diagnostic assays are not routinely available and therefore their involvement in human clinical illnesses is probably substantially underestimated.

Surprisingly, very little is known about the pathological role of PeV‐A in immunocompromised subjects. One child with CVID was reported with enteropathy associated with chronic shedding of PeV‐A and norovirus while an immunocompromised adult patient suffering from acute encephalitis displayed herpes simplex type I and PeV‐A in the CSF. 100 , 101 Role of PeV‐A is questionable in both cases because of co‐infections. A prevalence study performed in adults from the Netherlands showed a very low prevalence of PeV‐A among adults; however, large majority of positive samples came from patients with an humoral immunodeficiency including three with chronic shedding. 102 Pathogenicity of PeVs in immunocompromised patients is potentially largely underestimated and better diagnostic tools may help clarify their potent role.

The Aichi Virus subfamily

Human Aichi virus (AiV) is a poorly characterized picornavirus that belongs to the species Kobuvirus aichi and classified in the genus Kobuvirus together with five other animal species (Aichivirus B to F). 103 Like parechoviruses, Aichivirus capsid is composed of three structural proteins. 104 , 105 The first human AiV was isolated in 1989 in Japan during an outbreak of gastroenteritis that occurred after consuming oysters. Since then, three genotypes have been recognized among human‐infecting AiV: AiV‐A, B, and C with AiV‐A and B being the most prevalent around the world. Genotype C has been identified once and its real impact on human disease is unclear x. 10 , 103 Infection is believed to occur through fecal–oral and contaminated food. In vitro, AiV has shown high resistance capacity in acid milieu and with conventional methods of inactivation. The receptors that facilitate viral binding and entry, as well as the specific cell targets, remain unidentified. While direct experimental evidence is not available, it is assumed that the life cycles of AiV resemble those of other Picornaviridae.

In immunocompetent subjects, AiV infection is a possible cause of acute self‐limited gastroenteritis. Frequent co‐infections with other enteric pathogens have questioned its responsibility in digestive symptoms but others have reported mono‐infections supporting the hypothesis that it is a gastrointestinal pathogen virulent enough to cause medical attention. In any case, asymptomatic or subclinical infections are presumably the most frequent scenario. 103 , 106 , 107 , 108 , 109 Low prevalence of AiV in subjects with gastroenteritis contrasts with the high seroprevalence of AiV depicted in several studies in different populations. Anti‐AiV antibodies were found in approximately 60% in children under the age of 10 years and 90% individuals in adulthood suggesting a widespread exposure to AiV among human population. 110 , 111 , 112 , 113 , 114 AiV appears as an emerging pathogen in immunocompromised hosts. While higher prevalence of AiV in stool samples from patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) has been noticed, 115 recent reports highlight the immunopathological role of AiV in patients with IEIs. 49 , 116 , 117 , 118 , 119 The first observation came from Bucciol et al. who reported a young boy with X‐linked agammaglobulinemia and chronic condition that developed over several years with digestive symptoms, temporal epilepsy, progressive nephromegaly with impaired renal function, progressive pancytopenia, and increased liver enzymes. AiV RNA was identified by mNGS on kidney biopsy and specific PCR was found positive on several samples of liver, spleen CSF, urine, and sputum. Following this first observation, we reported six patients with IEIs, all with humoral deficiency associated in some cases with cellular defect (XLA = 2, transplanted SCID with B‐cell deficiency n = 3, ICF n = 1) and long‐lasting multi‐organ involvement of the liver in all cases and kidney in four individuals. 117 , 118 In all cases, chronic AiV infection was detected by mNGS on organ biopsies and confirmed by specific PCR on peripheral samples when possible. The similarity of the clinical presentation, the detection of viral genome in affected organs by in situ hybridization, and the reversibility of symptoms associated with AiV clearance after hematopoietic stem‐cell transplantation (HSCT) allowing restoration of normal B‐cell immune functions strongly argue for causality of AiV in these patients' clinical presentation. Three additional patients with AiV chronic infection and very similar immunopathological conditions occurring in a context of B‐cell deficiency (related to XLA n = 1 or following repeated course of anti‐CD20 treatment n = 2) were reported recently. Nephromegaly and inconstant impaired renal function are remarkable findings evocative of this condition. These studies support an essential role of antibodies in the control of systemic infection with AiV.

Astroviridae family

Human Astrovirus (HAstV) family can be divided into three divergent groups. The firstly recognized group contains eight closely related classic HAstV genotypes (1–8) with worldwide circulation. 1 , 8 Subsequently, two clades on non‐classic HAstV, called HAstV‐MLB and HAstV‐VA were described after the first strains were identified by mNGS respectively in Melbourne (MLB‐1) and Virginia (VA‐1). Both MLB and VA clades are quite distinct from the classic HAstV. These strains also have a large geographic distribution. 120

In immunocompetent hosts, HAstV typically causes acute and self‐limited gastroenteritis, although many infections are believed to be asymptomatic. 121 Extra‐digestive infections with neurotropism have been reported in fewer than 10 immunocompromised patients, mostly those with X‐linked agammaglobulinemia or recipients of HSCT. 122 , 123 , 124 , 125 , 126 HAstV‐VA1 was the most frequently identified strain, mostly on brain biopsies, responsible for subacute or chronic encephalitis, and HAstV‐MLB1 was reported once. A disseminated infection, including involvement of the CNS, was also reported in a patient with severe combined immunodeficiency who had received an HSCT and was infected with the classic HAstV genotype 4. 127 In immunocompetent hosts, three cases of meningitis or meningoencephalitis were reported, associated with HAstV‐MLB1, MLB2, and the classic serotype 1, respectively. 128

The seroprevalence of classic HAstV progressively increases during childhood, peaking between the ages of 5 and 9, when 90% of children have detectable serum antibodies for at least one classic human astrovirus genotype. 129 , 130 Studies on the seroprevalence of HAstV‐VA1, a serotype identified in encephalitis cases among immunocompromised patients, showed an increase between the ages of 2 and 9, followed by stabilization around 70%–80% in adults. Notably, the authors demonstrated the presence of neutralizing antibodies against HAstV‐VA1 in commercial batches of intravenous immunoglobulin preparations. 131 , 132

Calciviridae family

The Caliciviridae family includes small, non‐enveloped, positive‐stranded RNA viruses across five genera, among Norovirus and Sapovirus able to infect humans. These viruses are major causes of gastroenteritis outbreaks globally. Norovirus affects all age groups, while Sapovirus primarily targets children under 5 years and the elderly. Although acute gastroenteritis in immunocompetent individuals is usually self‐limiting, these viruses can lead to more severe, chronic infections in immunodeficient patients.

The Norovirus

Noroviruses are a leading cause of acute gastroenteritis worldwide (Table 1). These non‐enveloped, icosahedral viruses have a single‐stranded, positive‐sense RNA genome, categorized into 10 genogroups (G) based on the amino acid diversity of the VP1 gene encoding the viral capsid. Of these, genogroup I (GI), GII, and GIV infect humans. Genogroups are further subdivided into nearly 50 genotypes, with most human outbreaks caused by GII.4, which undergo rapid antigenic evolution. 133 , 134 Noroviruses bind to human histo‐blood group antigens (HBGAs) through the VP1 protruding 2 (P2) domain, which exhibits the highest sequence variability, involving motifs essential for cellular attachment and antibody recognition. 135 The expression of HBGAs on gut epithelial surfaces is regulated by the FUT2 gene. Numerous polymorphisms in the FUT2 gene confer resistance to infection, as seen in non‐secretors individuals whose intestinal epithelial cells do not express these carbohydrates, particularly in the case of the predominant GII.4 norovirus strain. 136

As other enteric viruses, noroviruses are primarily transmitted via the fecal–oral route, though recent findings suggest an additional transmission route through saliva, with viral replication occurring in the salivary glands. 2 Human norovirus pathophysiology research is limited due to the lack of in vitro infection models and of a relevant animal model that closely mimics human disease. Current understanding largely relies on studies involving murine norovirus in immunodeficient mice and human norovirus virus‐like particles (VLPs). Although a human B‐cell model that can be infected by human norovirus in the presence of HBGA‐expressing enteric bacteria has been developed, replicating these results has proven challenging. 137 , 138 Of note, new viral replication models are developing human intestinal enteroids (HIEs). 139 A current infection model proposes that, upon binding HBGAs on epithelial cells, noroviruses are transcytosed across the intestinal epithelium, partially via M cells, and subsequently infect target immune cells, particularly dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells. 140 , 141 Additionally, HBGA‐like molecules expressed by microbiota members, such as Enterobacter cloacae, can bind noroviruses and facilitate the infection of B cells. 137 Human studies demonstrate a rapid and extensive immune response to norovirus infection in immunocompetent individuals, both systemically and in stool samples. 142 This is followed by the development of antibodies and short‐term protection lasting 4–14 weeks after the initial challenge. 143 Research on antibody immunity in humans, including challenge studies and analyses of natural infections, highlights the importance of both serum and mucosal antibodies in the immune response. Norovirus‐specific salivary and fecal IgA are particularly effective in reducing viral load, shortening virus shedding duration, and mitigating disease severity. 30 Additionally, IgA antibodies are more effective than IgG in blocking norovirus binding to carbohydrates, showing stronger neutralizing titers. 144 The systemic B‐cell response also plays a crucial role in Norovirus immunity, involving antibody‐secreting cells and memory B cells post‐infection. 30

In healthy individuals, norovirus causes acute GE that resolves within 24–48 h, with virus shedding typically lasting between 2 and 8 weeks in the stool. 145 Seroprevalence in the general population varies around 15%. 146 In immunocompromised patients, noroviruses have an emerging role, as reflected by the growing number of chronic norovirus infection reported in patients with IEIs, 141 , 147 in patients who have undergone HSCT or solid organ transplantation 148 , 149 and in HIV‐infected patients 150 (Table 2). A prospective cohort of hospitalized children with IEIs estimated that Norovirus was the most frequently identified enteric pathogen. 147 Interestingly among IEI, chronic Norovirus infections are rarely documented in X‐linked agammaglobulinemia or pure B‐cell deficiency 151 but rather in CID and CVID. 141 , 147 All in all, patients at risk of chronic Norovirus infection suggest a role of T‐cell immunity in preventing chronic shedding. Immunocompromised patients exhibit longer viral shedding during months or even years. 147 , 152 The clinical consequences of chronic norovirus infection may be mild in some but can induce in others chronic diarrhea complicated by malabsorption, weight loss, organ failure, requirement for parenteral nutrition, and even death. 141 , 153 , 154 In patients with secondary immune deficiencies such as kidney transplant recipients, cases of worsened kidney function and allograft loss were reported. 155 In particular, in patients with CVID, norovirus infection may significantly contribute to severe complications such as enteropathy. This digestive inflammation, distinct of celiac disease regarding its evolution and treatment, is characterized by increased intraepithelial lymphocytes, villous atrophy, and follicular lymphoid hyperplasia but not responding to a gluten‐free diet. 156 The pathophysiology of this enteropathy remains not well understood, but it appears to be in part linked to chronic Norovirus infection, poorly controlled because of defective antibody production. A fraction of CVID patients with severe enteropathy showed persistent fecal shedding of norovirus, whereas none of the control patients exhibited evidence of norovirus carriage. 157 , 158 Of note, in three patients, viral clearance, either spontaneous or following treatment with Ribavirin, was associated with symptom resolution and restoration of duodenal architecture. 157 Importantly, humoral mucosal immunity is impaired in CVID patients, with an absence or near absence of intestinal plasma cells, especially IgA plasma cells. 156

The Sapovirus

Sapovirus is the second genus of the Calicivirus family known to infect humans, alongside Norovirus (Table 1). The burden of sapovirus disease went underestimated for several decades after its discovery in 1976 due to the lack of diagnostic tests other than electron microscopy. 159 Sapoviruses are morphologically distinguishable from other enteric viruses (e.g., norovirus, rotavirus, astrovirus, or adenovirus) by their typical “Star of David” surface morphology under the electron microscope. 159 Even today, it remains difficult to find and whole‐genome sequencing is sometimes required, as seen in a food‐borne outbreak in Sweden where reverse transcription‐PCR used for screening failed to detect the new variant GV.2. 160 Like Norovirus, Sapovirus is a non‐enveloped icosahedral virus with a positive‐sense, single‐stranded RNA genome, categorized into genogroups based on complete VP1 sequences. Four genogroups (GI, GII, GIV, and GV) are known to infect humans. 7 These genogroups are further subdivided into 19 genotypes, with GI.1 being the most frequently detected worldwide followed by GII.1 and GII.2. 7 , 161

The mechanisms of virus binding, entry into target cells, viral RNA replication, and translation are still undefined, due to the lack of infection model. Attempts to grow human sapoviruses in cell culture or mice have been reported, but all trials failed. 162 , 163 A significant advance was made with the expression of the recombinant capsid protein (rVP1) in insect or mammalian cells, resulting in the formation of virus‐like particles (VLPs) that are morphologically similar to the native viruses, although low expression levels make them difficult to use in large studies. 164 , 165 A recent model of Human Sapovirus Replication in Human Intestinal Enteroids suggests that the tested strains (GI.1 and GI.2) replicate in the small intestine with moderate levels of replication (but not in the colon). 166 Unlike Norovirus, whose susceptibility is related to HBGA phenotypes, no association was found with Sapovirus. 167 The immune response to sapovirus is primarily understood through seroprevalence studies, which have shown a gradual increase in seroprevalence with age, reaching over 90% by 3 years old and remaining high (80–100%) in adults. 7 , 168 Preexisting serum antibodies are linked to reduced sapovirus infection rates and illness for homologous infections but offer limited protection against different genogroups and possibly different genotypes. 168 , 169

In immunocompetent individuals, sapovirus infection can be asymptomatic or cause acute GE. Sapovirus is detected in both sporadic cases and outbreaks, particularly in children, and is responsible for approximately 3.4% of AGE globally. 170 Generally, sapovirus gastroenteritis is milder than that caused by rotavirus or norovirus. 7 , 171 Symptoms are typically self‐limiting, with recovery occurring within a few days. Age is a significant risk factor for diarrhea: children under five are most at risk, followed by older adults. 172 However, understanding the full burden of sapovirus disease is complicated by frequent co‐infections with other enteric pathogens, particularly other enteric viruses. 7 , 161

The significance of sapovirus infections in immunocompromised patients has often been underestimated due to detection challenges. The recent systematic screening using Gastrointestinal Pathogen Panels in stool has revealed a prevalence of 2%–30% of sapovirus infection in immunocompromised patients, with the highest rates among kidney transplant recipients. 173 , 174 , 175 Initially detected in kidney transplant patients with chronic post‐transplant diarrhea, sapovirus has since been identified in those with IEIs, HIV/AIDS, hematologic malignancies, or those undergoing allo‐HSCT or immunosuppressive treatment. 174 , 176 , 177 Sapovirus infections are still mostly reported in case studies or small cohorts. In a large screening of 5.333 immunocompromised patients tested for sapovirus RNA by real‐time RT‐PCR, 16 were positive, with 31% (three children and two adults) developing chronic diarrhea. 176 Similar to norovirus, sapovirus infections can persist for months, with viral shedding and diarrhea lasting up to 5 months. 155 , 176 The persistence of these infections and the reduction in viral load observed with decreased immunosuppression underscore the critical role of an intact immune system in controlling sapovirus, although the precise immune response remains unclear.

Sedoreoviridae family

The Sedoreoviridae family, characterized by its segmented double‐stranded RNA genome, consists of multiple genera, four of which—Orthoreovirus, Coltivirus, Orbivirus, and Rotavirus—are known to infect humans. While Rotavirus is a leading cause of epidemic gastroenteritis, the other three genera are less commonly reported in humans but are associated with gastroenteritis and, in more severe cases, neurological diseases. 178 , 179 , 180 We will focus on Rotavirus, the most significant genus within the Reoviridae family.

Rotavirus is a 70 nm icosahedral virus from the genus Rotavirus within the Reoviridae family 5 (Table 1). Its genome consists of double‐stranded RNA surrounded by six structural viral proteins (VP1‐VP7, no VP5). VP1 to VP3 form the core, VP6 the inner capsid, and VP4 and VP7 the outer capsid. There are seven rotavirus groups (A–G) based on B‐cell responses to VP6, with human infections mostly caused by rotavirus A. Groups are further divided by VP7 serotypes and VP4 genotypes. Rotavirus spreads via the fecal–oral route (and aerosols) and infects small intestine enterocytes through interactions between VP4 and host‐cell HBGAs and sialoglycans. 5 , 6 , 181

In the general population, rotavirus infection is one of the leading causes of epidemic gastroenteritis and dehydratation in children less than 5 years old. The range of severity correlates with age, more severe in young children than in adults, potentially severe also in aged people. Vaccination with live‐attenuated vaccine have mitigated the rotavirus‐associated death worldwide although 200,000 deaths are still linked to rotavirus per year, mostly in children from low‐income countries. Acute infection is associated (detected in affected organs but without causality proof) with other rare clinical manifestations including meningitis, encephalitis, and encephalopathy. 182 , 183 , 184 , 185 , 186 , 187 Interestingly, a reduction of around 20% of the risk of seizure requiring hospitalization was observed after the introduction of the vaccine, suggesting a link between rotavirus infection and CNS manifestations. 188

Rotavirus is not an exception, and knowledge about immunity to rotavirus mostly comes from animal studies (neonatal mice, gnotobiotic piglets), and the relevance to the human immunity to rotavirus is unknown. Innate immune response has no specificity except that non‐structural viral protein 1 (NSP1) has been shown to inhibit IFN production by interacting with several IRF including IRF3 and sending them for proteasomal degradation as well as β‐TrCP, an essential E3‐ubiquitin ligase for NF‐κB activation. The JAK–STAT response and the protein synthesis are two other pathways that rotavirus can also inhibit to favor viral replication. 5 , 12 , 189

Specific CD8+ T cells can be detected in children and adults at very low level in absence of acute infection although the role of these CD8+ T cells in viral clearance in human remain to be determined. 190 Chronic viral shedding with wild rotavirus has been described in immunocompromised patients including one XLA patient having a chronic symptomatic infection 191 (Table 2). After the implementation of oral live‐attenuated vaccine, several patients have been reported with vaccine‐associated rotavirus infection and chronic shedding in SCID patients. 192 , 193 These patients have both T‐cell and B‐cell immunodeficiency, it is therefore difficult to conclude on the prominent role of T‐cell or B‐cell mediated immunity in this presentation. Of note, no infection or chronic shedding with attenuated live vaccine strain have not been described in pure B‐cell deficiency to date, suggesting a role for T cells in this particular setting.

The prominent role of SIgA in the protection against rotavirus has been shown by studying vaccination response in the population. Studies in mice already showed that IgA−/− mice had a delayed clearance of rotavirus and failed to mount protective immunity after repeated exposures. 32 , 194 In humans, a correlation was historically shown between the level of stool rotavirus‐specific IgA and the level of detection of rotavirus in feces of children with acute gastroenteritis. 195 In most studies, as in polio vaccination, the oral vaccination induces an elevation of serum antiviral‐specific IgA and the level of these antibodies correlates with the level of protection, probably by correlating with SIgA in the gut. 27 , 189 , 196 These data emphasize the role of IgA in the protection against rotavirus in human.

Adenoviridae family

Human adenoviruses (HAdVs) belong to the genus Mastadenovirus and consist of seven species (A to G) in the Adenoviridae family. These non‐enveloped, double‐stranded linear DNA viruses have a diameter of 70–100 nm in an icosahedral shape, mainly composed of the hexon protein. Species classification is based on several factors, including phylogenetics, genome organization, GC content, and oncogenicity in rodents. Initially, 51 serotypes were classified based on hypervariable loops in the hexon protein (forming the serum neutralization epitope) and the fiber protein (responsible for the hemagluttination inhibition assay), which determined tissue tropism. However, advances in viral genomics have led to the discovery of new types, replacing the serotype terminology. 197 , 198

Epidemiological studies showed that most children are infected by HAdVs before 5 years old and can remain asymptomatic. 199 , 200 , 201 , 202 The transmission can occur through inhalation of droplets, direct conjunctival inoculation or fecal–oral route. The HAdVs can cause infection of multiple epithelial barriers including the eyes, the respiratory epithelium, and the digestive tract. 198 Therefore, the clinical manifestations vary from keratoconjunctivitis, upper or lower respiratory tract infection, and gastroenteritis. Certain serotypes, particularly adenovirus types EAd40 and 41, are associated with GE. 197 Other manifestations also include less frequently hepatitis, meningoencephalitis, myocarditis, or cystitis. Most often, infection in immunocompetent are mild although keratoconjunctivitis can lead to sequelae and some respiratory infections have been associated with death. HAdVs also have the possibility to chronically infect human lympho‐epithelial cells in a latent form.

In contrast, HAdVs are a particular threat for immunocompromised patients, in particular in the setting of HSCT and solid organ transplantation with both de novo infection and reactivation of latent forms. 197 , 203 Contrary to other enteric viruses, this particular at‐risk population emphasized a prominent role of T‐cell‐mediated immunity against HAdVs. Patients receiving T‐cell‐depleted allogenic HSCT and suffering from acute graft‐versus‐host disease (aGVHD) are more prone to have an adenoviral infection and disease, these patients being the more T‐cell immunocompromised. 197 , 204 , 205 Moreover, severe lymphopenia is also associated with more adverse outcomes.

Despite this apparent prominent role of T cells, patients with hypogammaglobulinemia (CVID) have been reported with chronic viral shedding of adenovirus, in particular in the lower respiratory tract, with or without symptoms, both retrospectively and prospectively. 206 , 207 XLA patients have also rarely been reported to suffer from an adenoviral disease, in two cases associated with hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) 208 and in one case to encephalitis leading to death. 67 These patients, and others with various diseases associated with HAdVs, were effectively treated with IgIV. 209 , 210 Whether these IgIV treated the infection by neutralizing circulating viruses or acted as immunomodulatory agents remains to be determined. 211

In summary, the nonredundant immunity appears to be virus‐dependent. While antibody response to picornaviruses seems to be crucial, cellular immunity is not dispensable in the control of other viruses (norovirus, rotavirus, and adenovirus). This suggests that for some viruses, antibody neutralization plays a more critical role in clearing the virus than the destruction of infected cells. The exact reasons for this are not yet understood.

ENTERIC VIRAL‐ASSOCIATED HEPATITIS (EVAH) AS A NOVEL IMMUNOPATHOLOGICAL CONDITION IN PATIENTS WITH A DEFECTIVE PRODUCTION OF ANTIBODIES

Deficiencies of immune responses against pathogens account for various infectious vulnerabilities, a major feature of IEIs, while loss of immune regulation accounts for a broad range of manifestations from well‐characterized autoimmunity, autoinflammation, and allergy up to less defined immunopathological conditions frequently referred to as dysimmunity. 212 These dysimmune manifestations could be chronic benign lymphoproliferation or organ‐specific inflammation affecting the lung, the gut, or the liver, among other organs. Most of these dysimmune processes remain unexplained. Although an infectious origin is frequently postulated, it is rarely confirmed. The recent development of new tools such as metagenomics NGS (mNGS), a pan‐microbial technique that allows the detection of rare and even novel pathogens, may bring new pathogenic explanation. For example, we and others have shown its relevance by detecting emerging causes of encephalitis in patients with IEIs, identifying Astrovirus VP1, Cache‐Valley virus, or Newcastle virus in patients with diverse causes of B‐cell immunodeficiencies. 124 , 126 , 213 , 214 , 215

Among the dysimmune manifestations of IEIs, chronic liver disease is notably prevalent among patients with B‐cell deficiency, affecting approximately 10–15% of individuals with CVID and up to 30% of those with XLA. 216 Due to the insidious progression of this liver disease, it may remain undetected until advanced stages, such as portal hypertension, resulting in significant morbidity and mortality. Histopathologically, this condition is characterized by CD8+ T‐cell infiltration and granuloma formation, indicating an ongoing immune response, whether infectious or autoimmune in nature. 217 Nodular regenerative hyperplasia (NRH), a diffuse transformation of normal hepatic parenchyma into small regenerative nodules with minimal fibrosis can occur. The recurrence of the disease after liver transplantation suggests an extrahepatic origin. 218 Interestingly, CVID patients suffering from liver disease frequently present with associated enteropathy, suggesting a pathogenic role of the gut‐liver axis and a probable continuum in their pathophysiology. 219 , 220 One hypothesis is that NRH arises from an immune response triggered by enteric protracted infection that an impaired antibody response patient cannot resolve.

We recently identified a liver disease in a series of patients with severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) recipients of hematopoietic HSCT or gene therapy (GT) that shares similarities with liver conditions described in other IEIs. 117 These 11 patients underwent transplantation without a myeloablative conditioning regimen, resulting in donor T‐cell engraftment but not myeloid or B‐cell replacement. Chronic hepatitis, characterized by NRH and lobular CD8+ T‐cell infiltration on liver biopsy (Figure 1), was associated with chronic infection by enteric viruses, including Norovirus (n = 4), Aichi virus (n = 3), or Sapovirus (n = 2). None of the patients from the control group (SCID patients with poor B‐cell reconstitution but without hepatitis) had such a chronic enteric viral infection. A multiomics analysis including Mass cytometry (CyTOF) and scRNA sequencing (scRNAseq) from PBMC of these patients, revealed an expansion of an activated effector memory CD8+ T‐cell (CD38high, HLA‐DRhigh) cluster, with low CD127 (IL‐7R) expression, a known marker of chronic viral infection. 221 , 222 Moreover, a strong type I and II IFN signature was observed in peripheral immune subsets, predominantly in CD8+ T cells among lymphocytes. Due to the severity of the liver disease and the lack of effective immunosuppressive or antiviral treatments, additional cell therapy was implemented to restore B‐cell function in some patients. In our study, three patients underwent HSCT, while another three patients received GT following myeloablative conditioning. The clinical and immunological outcomes were remarkable, with normalization of liver enzyme levels within a few months, coinciding with the clearance of enteric viruses. Unfortunately, three patients died of progressive liver disease before curative treatment could be proposed. This chronic liver disease, associated with an expansion of activated, oligoclonal CD8+ T cells concomitantly with chronic enteric viral infections, has been designated “EVAH” (enteric virus‐associated hepatitis) in transplanted SCID patients without B‐cell reconstitution. Fourgeaud et al. subsequently described six patients with chronic infection by Aichi virus (of which three were described in Riller et al.) and diverse clinical manifestation including hepatitis. 118 The implication of enteric viral infection in the development of EVAH in these three patients (2 XLA, 1 immunodeficiency type 1 with centromeric region instability and facial anomalies [ICF1]) was supported by: (i) the positive in situ hybridization signal for Aichivirus in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes, and (ii) the clinical recovery associated with viral clearance after restoration of effective humoral immunity. Two additional case reports of NRH recurrence after liver transplantation in CVID patients showed an association between the recurrence of liver disease and chronic Norovirus infection. 223 , 224 Other reports of chronic Aichi virus infection in patients with primary or secondary B‐cell defects are in line with our descriptions. 49 , 116 , 119 Of note, the expansion of peripheral CD8+ T cells is increasingly reported in CVID patients with lymphoproliferation and liver damage and is characterized by elevated activated effector memory CD8+ T cells phenotype with increased expression of cytotoxic markers. 225 , 226 In the liver of patients with NRH, CD8+ T cells also have an activated profile with overexpression of Granzyme B + . 117 , 227 We could therefore hypothesize that these patients fall within the spectrum of EVAH.

PERSPECTIVES

The study of rare IEIs sheds light on the prominent role of antibodies in the control of some enteric viruses. This is in particular the case for the Picornaviridae family, in particular EV and AiV (Table 2). For other viruses such as the Adenoviridae family, the role of T‐cell immunity seems to be prominent as most patients presenting with chronic adenoviral infection or acute disseminated adenoviral infection exhibit a severe T‐cell immunodeficiency. Nevertheless, rare cases of acute disseminated adenoviral infection have been described in the setting of XLA. Diseases associated with chronic enteric viral infections vary from one patient to another, and by viruses. We and others have described clinical association between these chronic viral infections and the onset of clinical manifestations ranging from hepatitis to life‐threatening HLH features. The exact pathophysiological mechanism of such diseases are still to fully determined. 49 , 117 , 118 , 209 The key pieces of evidence supporting the causality of enteric viral infection in the development of the clinical manifestations called “EVAH” include: (i) the chronic detection of enteric viruses in patients with defective production of antibodies harboring chronic liver disease (especially NRH), contrary to controls; (ii) the expansion of activated CD127low CD8+ T cells, suggesting a possible viral‐driven immune response; (iii) the strong type I and type II interferon signature as a possible consequence of chronic viral shedding and (iv) clinical recovery with viral clearance following the restoration of an effective humoral immunity. 117 The presence of CD8+ T cells in involved tissues led us to propose that chronic infection with enteric viruses, favored by the absence of B‐cell function, led to this activation of CD8+ T cells that migrate in affected tissues (Figure 3). The reason why this high percentage of activated CD8+ T cells does not lead to viral clearance is an open question. In fine, it may just reinforce the prominent role of antibodies to clear most enteric viral infections, especially SIgA. We do not know yet whether these CD8+ T cells are directed against viral‐derived peptides, an important step to provide in order to confirm the pathophysiology of the EVAH syndrome. We do not know whether characteristics of peripheral CD8+ T cells are shared with infiltrating CD8+ T cells in the liver. This is the subject of ongoing research. In situ hybridization studies showed that hepatocytes can be infected with Aichi virus. 118 Whether the liver disease is directly caused by viral infection or is indirect through immunopathology linked to this or to the CD8+ T‐cell infiltration, or both, is still not known. One possibility is that activated CD8+ T cells migrating to the liver target infected cells (e.g., hepatocytes) and lead to liver disease or (and not exclusively) that activated CD8+ T cells target non‐infected hepatocytes in a bystander effect, notably through the release of interferons.

Proposed physiopathology for enteric viral‐associated hepatitis (EVAH).

Another possible mechanism includes the direct role of infecting viruses in the downregulation of inhibitory checkpoint of the immune system. The detection of enterovirus and parechovirus in newborns has been associated with a decreased expression of FOXP3 in Tregs. 228 It is therefore possible that chronic infection with these viruses in the setting of B‐cell deficiencies leads to a T‐cell‐mediated immunopathology favored by dysfunctional Tregs.

From a therapeutic standpoint, patients with prolonged hypogammaglobulinemia are typically treated with immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IgRT). Studies have shown that these Ig preparations contain neutralizing antibodies against enteric viruses. 78 , 102 Therefore, it could be stated that the immunoglobulin deficiency alone does not account for the onset of chronic enteric viral infections. However, several points make this possibility unlikely. First, as described above, many enteric viruses have a high mutation rate, which has been shown to help them evade adaptive immunity. Second, these preparations do not contain dimeric IgA but monomeric IgA and these later have been shown to be less effective in the control of enteric viruses. 194 Moreover, even though these IgA antibodies could be effective, they may not reach the infected tissues in a sufficient concentration to be fully protective. Finally, we showed that restoring B‐cell immunity by GT or HSCT in patients chronically infected with enteric viruses despite IgRT led to viral clearance. This is an indirect proof that recapitulating a “natural” B‐cell immunity is necessary to treat these infections, although we did not measure appearance of neutralizing IgA in periphery and in feces. Whether the use of immunoglobulin preparations that are enriched in IgM and IgA, could lead to viral clearance in the setting of chronic enteric viral infections has not been studied. In acute viral infection with parvovirus B19 or adenovirus, the use of such preparations was shown to reduce inflammation and viral load (conference abstract). 229

The development of four available vaccines against rotavirus and poliovirus are strong argument of the importance of B‐cell‐mediated immunity against enteric viruses. These oral live‐attenuated vaccines are strong inducer of mucosal SIgA. The deployment of these vaccines led to the “quasi” disappearance of poliomyelitis worldwide while the severe rotavirus infections have drastically diminished.

Although vaccination is a major solution to some enteric virus in the general population, they are not candidate to treat patient with intrinsic B‐cell defects. Treatment options for patients with chronic enteric viral infections and IEIs are an unmet medical need. In this setting, the development of antiviral drugs or neutralizing monoclonal antibodies targeting enteric viruses are options that need further attention in the future.
