Introduction

Polyneuropathies are a diverse group of disorders affecting peripheral nerves, typically involving demyelination or axonal degeneration/loss [1, 2, 3], with the latter resulting in long‐term disability [4]. Demyelination may induce secondary axonal loss and conduction block, causing acute deficits. These impairments also denervate skeletal muscle, leading to intramuscular fat accumulation [5].

Nerve conduction studies (NCS) are a standard tool for diagnosing demyelinating and axonal neuropathies. The former typically shows slowed conduction velocities, conduction block, and/or temporal dispersion, while the latter is characterized by reduced amplitudes in compound muscle action potentials and sensory nerve action potentials [6, 7, 8, 9]. However, NCS is largely limited to assessing distal nerve segments and often becomes non‐responsive when most nerve fibers are damaged (i.e., in length‐dependent neuropathies) [5]. If demyelination occurs in the proximal nerves, NCS cannot access the proximal pathology, even though late responses may suggest proximal conduction block, but not specifically [10]. Accurately diagnosing demyelinating neuropathies is important for guiding treatment decisions. In inherited neuropathies, genetic mutations that cause demyelinating versus axonal polyneuropathies are distinct. With the emergence of gene therapies, the two groups of polyneuropathies require different outcome measurements and therapeutic strategies. For acquired polyneuropathies, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) responds well to immunomodulatory therapies such as intravenous immunoglobulin, glucocorticoids, or plasma exchange, but axonal polyneuropathies do not respond to these treatments [11, 12]. Thus, differentiating between the two types affects clinical decisions. There remains a major gap in non‐invasive, reliable biomarkers for differentiating demyelinating from axonal types for patients with lesions in proximal nerves that are not reliably accessible by distal NCS.

This gap can be addressed with quantitative MRI (qMRI) of peripheral nerves, a non‐invasive technique capable of assessing both proximal and distal nerves using various qMRI parameters sensitive to demyelination and axonal loss [13]. For instance, relaxation qMRI parameters such as T1, proton density (PD), and T2* are sensitive to myelin and water content changes [13]; magnetization transfer ratio (MTR) [14] and MT saturation index (MTsat) [15] provide myelin specific information; and fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD) derived from diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) are sensitive to tissue microstructural integrity [16, 17, 18]. However, given the challenges in imaging the small target of peripheral nerves, a single qMRI parameter may not reliably assess nerve pathologies [13]. To this end, multiparametric qMRI protocols have been developed to simultaneously quantify these measures and provide complementary information for a more reliable assessment [19]. We hypothesize that multiparametric qMRI of proximal and distal peripheral nerves can estimate probabilities of demyelination and axonal loss for individual patients with polyneuropathies, and can serve as a supplement to distal NCS in diagnosis and treatment management.

The objectives of this study are to test the hypothesis in patients with genetically confirmed demyelinating and axonal subtypes and to create a composite qMRI score as an imaging biomarker of disease severity. Data from healthy controls (HC) and three patient cohorts with Charcot–Marie–Tooth (CMT) diseases were included in this retrospective study: a demyelinating cohort of CMT Type 1 (CMT1), an axonal cohort of CMT Type 2 (CMT2), and a cohort with hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies (HNPP), which often shows intermediate features between these two classifications [1, 20].

Materials and Methods

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study was approved by the local institutional review board (IRB) at Wayne State University (WSU) and Houston Methodist Hospital (HMH) following NIH's single IRB requirement. Written consent was acquired for each participant at enrollment.

Participant Demographics and Clinical Data

Participants were enrolled from WSU and HMH between 2021 and 2025. The inclusion criteria were set as male or female participants from 18 to 90 years old who have been diagnosed with polyneuropathy, such as CMT, CIDP, HNPP, or idiopathic polyneuropathy. The exclusion criteria included: (1) inability to fit in the knee coil, (2) MRI contraindications, (3) pregnancy, or (4) orthopedic implants on both lower limbs. Volunteers without a history of neuropathy were also enrolled as HCs. All patients were seen by the physician (J.L.) for clinical assessment. The CMT Examination Score (CMTES) was obtained by neurologists, and NCS of the forearms and legs were performed by experienced technicians as described in publications [21, 22, 23]. Subsequently, the CMT Neuropathy Score v2 (CMTNSv2) [24] was calculated, reflecting overall neurological disabilities. CMTES was calculated using physical findings, excluding NCS readouts, whereas CNTNSv2 is a combination of CMTES plus NCS readouts. Finally, available qMRI data from HC and genetically confirmed CMT1, CMT2, and HNPP were selected for this retrospective study, as shown in the flowchart in Figure 1. Genetic analysis data were available for all these patients using next‐generation sequencing with a targeted gene panel for 72 CMT‐related genes. Detailed phenotypes are summarized in Table 1. There were a total of 82 participants (males/females = 33/49), with 80 participants (males/females = 33/47) for the sciatic nerve (CMT1 = 19, CMT2 = 12, HNPP = 24, and HC = 25), and 80 participants (males/females = 33/47) for the tibial nerve (CMT1 = 19, CMT2 = 12, HNPP = 25, and HC = 24). The 80 individuals for the sciatic nerve data were not the same 80 individuals for the tibial nerve data, as some of them only had data on the sciatic or tibial nerves. In addition, there were 3 idiopathic patients in the CMT2 cohort. One of them was confirmed as idiopathic CMT2, whereas the other two had inconclusive genetic testing and were clinically categorized as idiopathic axonal polyneuropathy. Given the limited number of CMT2 patients, and the goal of the study to differentiate axonal from demyelinating patients, we included these three patients in the CMT2 (axonal) cohort. Demographic characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and number of patients with clinical scores (CMTES and CMTNSv2), are summarized in Table 1.

MRI Data Acquisition and Processing

MRI scans were performed on each participant's right leg using 3.0‐T MRI scanners with a knee coil (Verio 3 T, Cima.X 3 T, and Vida 3 T, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). A standardized qMRI protocol was used to image the sciatic nerve (Sn) at the mid‐thigh and the tibial nerve (Tn) at the mid‐calf levels [19]. Briefly, the two‐session qMRI protocol employed a set of vendor‐supplied 3D gradient echo (GRE) sequences to compute nerve fascicular volume (fVol) in a resolution of 0.15 × 0.15 × 3.0 mm3, MTR, MTsat, T2*, T1, PD in a resolution of 0.6 × 0.6 × 3.0 mm3; and a DTI sequence in a resolution of 1.2 × 1.2 × 6.0 mm3 to compute FA, MD, AD, and RD metrics. There were 40 and 16 axial slices for the GRE and DTI sequences, respectively. The qMRI protocol was about 30 min per location. Detailed imaging parameters are shown in Table S1 of the Supporting Information.

Regions‐of‐interest (ROIs) for the sciatic nerve and tibial nerve fascicles were segmented using an established semi‐automated method on the fat‐suppressed GRE images for non‐DTI qMRI parameters and on the DTI B0 images for DTI parameters, as previously described [13, 19]. This is because the DTI scan has a slice thickness of 6 mm, whereas all other non‐DTI scans have a slice thickness of 3 mm. In addition, the locations of the axial slices were aligned during the scan for all sequences to ensure the same segment of the nerves was analyzed. Nerve fVol was computed by multiplying voxel count by voxel size. Mean values within each ROI were extracted for all qMRI parameters using the validated MATLAB pipeline, as previously described [13, 14, 15, 19, 25, 26, 27]. PD values were normalized to the adjacent normal‐appearing muscle signal.

Descriptive Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using custom Python (Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, OR, USA) scripts developed with Scikit‐learn [28], Numpy [29], and SciPy [30]. Data from all subjects are reported as mean ± standard deviation, along with the 5th to 95th percentile range. Two‐sided t‐tests were carried out for each qMRI parameter to assess the statistical significance of the differences between cohorts (CMT1, CMT2, HNPP, and HC). A one‐way ANOVA was conducted on the qMRI data across phenotypes to assess its separability. Statistical significance level was set as p < 0.05. Since the comparison of individual qMRI parameters between the patient and HC cohorts was intended to identify multiparametric qMRI patterns and select a subset of parameters for the separation model, multiple‐comparison corrections were not applied to the two‐sided t‐tests. In contrast, significance levels from the ANOVA tests were reported using Bonferroni corrections.

Polyneuropathy Classification Using qMRI

To test the hypothesis that multiparametric qMRI of peripheral nerves can differentiate demyelinating from axonal polyneuropathies, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with logistic regression (LR) models was performed for the qMRI data of the sciatic and tibial nerves from the four cohorts: CMT1 as the demyelinating group, CMT2 as the axonal group, HNPP as a group with mixed demyelination and axonal loss, and HC as the control group. All features were standardized using z‐score normalization to ensure comparability across variables before the LR model classification. A subset of parameters with F‐score higher than 10 was selected from Sn and Tn, including fVol, MTsat, T1, T2*, PD, FA, and RD. This threshold was selected to ensure stronger discriminatory power between groups, based on established practices in medical imaging literature, where F‐scores below 10 are usually excluded due to weak class separation [31, 32]. A stratified (80% train–20% validation) 5‐fold cross‐validation strategy was employed to ensure balanced representation of classification in each fold. For each fold, the model was trained on the training set and evaluated on the validation set. L2 regularization was applied to reduce the overfitting likelihood. To evaluate the model's ability to distinguish specific classes, ROC curves were plotted, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated. For robustness of results, ROC curves were averaged across folds, and the mean true positive rate was interpolated over a common false positive rate grid. Mean AUC values were reported for each of the three LR models—qMRI of Sn, qMRI of Tn, and qMRI of both nerves. Finally, the three LR models were verified on a temporal validation dataset unseen by the models to confirm their performance in individual patient classification. The testing dataset was from available one‐year follow‐up scans on 24 participants.

CMT Imaging Score to Reflect Disease Severity

To derive the CMT Imaging Score (CMTIS), a composite qMRI score reflecting CMTNS and CMTES, a linear regression model was trained using Python Scikit‐learn [28]. Stratified 5‐fold cross‐validation was employed to preserve the distribution of polyneuropathy subgroups (CMT1, CMT2, HNPP) across folds. For each training fold, a linear regression model was fit, and the average coefficient of all linear regressions was used as the final coefficient for each qMRI parameter. Details of the CMTIS calculation are shown in the Supporting Information.

Results

Multiparametric qMRI Patterns of Sciatic and Tibial Nerves in Different Polyneuropathies

Representative images of the sciatic and tibial nerves across cohorts are shown in Figure 2. As shown in Figure 3, qMRI of the sciatic nerve for CMT1 patients exhibited the most pronounced alterations, including significantly reduced MTR (37.2% ± 3.7% vs. 40.0% ± 2.5%), MTsat (2.24% ± 0.35% vs. 3.07% ± 0.39%), FA (0.32 ± 0.08 vs. 0.54 ± 0.06), and AD (1925.2 ± 216.6 μm2/s vs. 2183.6 ± 178.7 μm2/s), and elevated T1 (1689.1 ± 196.9 ms vs. 1305.4 ± 120.7 ms), PD (85.8 ± 8.5 vs. 75.0 ± 6.0), MD (1430.1 ± 176.6 μm2/s vs. 1292.5 ± 88.4 μm2/s), and RD (1177.2 ± 192.9 μm2/s vs. 843.3 ± 101.1 μm2/s), compared to HC. Nerve size measured by fVol (3733.7 ± 1426.9 mm3 vs. 1614.4 ± 286.3 mm3) was exclusively increased in CMT1, consistent with nerve hypertrophy. In contrast, CMT2 patients showed a reduced PD (67.6 ± 6.9 vs. 75.0 ± 6.0), but more moderate reductions in MTsat (2.66% ± 0.34% vs. 3.07% ± 0.39%), FA (0.39 ± 0.11 vs. 0.54 ± 0.06), and AD (1972.5 ± 197.4 μm2/s vs. 2183.6 ± 178.7 μm2/s), and elevated T1 (1435.1 ± 171.7 ms vs. 1305.4 ± 120.7 ms) and RD (1065.9 ± 196.6 μm2/s vs. 843.3 ± 101.1 μm2/s). The fVol (1588.6 ± 443.1 mm3 vs. 1614.4 ± 286.3 mm3) and MD (1369.3 ± 135.9 μm2/s vs. 1292.5 ± 88.4 μm2/s) in CMT2 were comparable with those in HC. T2* was significantly lower in CMT2 (25.24 ± 3.27 ms vs. 29.97 ± 4.28 ms). In contrast, the T2* was comparable with HC in CMT1 (26.91 ± 4.29 ms vs. 29.97 ± 4.28 ms) and HNPP (30.94 ± 3.71 ms vs. 29.97 ± 2.53 ms). HNPP patients demonstrated intermediate changes, with significant reductions in MTsat (2.57% ± 0.34% vs. 3.07% ± 0.39%), FA (0.46 ± 0.06 vs. 0.54 ± 0.06), and AD (2024.7 ± 203.9 μm2/s vs. 2183.6 ± 178.7 μm2/s), and elevations in T1 (1550.1 ± 183.9 ms vs. 1305.4 ± 120.7 ms), PD (85.77 ± 8.47 vs. 75.02 ± 6.03), and RD (924.3 ± 96.6 μm2/s vs. 843.3 ± 101.1 μm2/s), though less severe than in CMT1. The normalized CMTIS derived from linear regression of imaging parameters showed a significant correlation with clinical scores (Figure 5). Discussion

While NCS is widely used to diagnose and evaluate nerve pathophysiological changes, it remains limited in assessing proximal nerves and is often uninformative on severely degenerated distal nerves. We hypothesize that multiparametric qMRI of peripheral nerves could complement NCS by improving the differentiation between demyelination and axonal loss in individual patients. In this study, we analyzed qMRI data from the sciatic and tibial nerves of patients with genetically confirmed inherited polyneuropathies to test whether qMRI can distinguish demyelinating from axonal polyneuropathies. Our findings revealed distinct qMRI signatures for demyelinating (CMT1) and axonal (CMT2) patients. ROC analyses showed strong potential for differentiating demyelination and axonal loss at the individual patient level. Furthermore, the CMTIS had a strong correlation with disability scores measured by the CMTES and the CMTNSv2. These findings support the CMTIS as a potential objective monitoring biomarker in longitudinal studies to track disease progression. Long‐term, this approach may provide useful information for therapeutic decision‐making when extended to acquired polyneuropathies.

Polyneuropathy Classification by Multiparametric qMRI

The increased classification power of multiparametric qMRI compared to single qMRI parameters is demonstrated by the higher AUC observed when using a logistic regression model with an optimized combination of qMRI parameters, as compared to using single parameters (see Table S4). More data are needed to clarify whether the lower performance for identifying CMT2 compared to CMT1 relates to secondary axonal loss in CMT1 or the smaller sample size of the current CMT2 cohort. For the CMT1 and CMT2 groups, sciatic nerve qMRI alone provides better model performance, likely due to the larger size of the proximal nerve, which reduces partial‐volume effects and underscores the value of proximal nerve qMRI for more reliable assessment when pathologies are not accessible by NCS. It should be noted that the trends in the patient stratification models changed slightly when the cross‐validation seed changed; however, the AUC values remained relatively stable when running the model with 5 different seeds, yielding standard deviations of less than 5%. Outliers misclassified from the testing set (See Figure S4 and Table S5 of the Supporting Information) were mainly due to overlapping distributions. In addition, the HNPP cohort was included as a real‐world confounding group, as HNPP frequently demonstrates intermediate characteristics between demyelinating and axonal neuropathies. Therefore, a higher rate of misclassification was anticipated for HNPP cases compared with the demyelinating and axonal cases in the testing set.

CMTIS as a Potential Monitoring Biomarker for Disease Progression

The estimated CMTIS showed a strong and significant correlation with CMTES and CMTNSv2 scores, which suggests the qMRI has the potential to assess disease severity and may be used to monitor disease progression longitudinally. Importantly, the CMTIS is objective and avoids the subjectivity and inter‐rater variability inherent in clinical scales, making it a potentially more reliable and reproducible tool. The distal nerve CMTIS showed a stronger correlation with neuropathy scores than the CMTIS of the proximal nerve, consistent with more severe pathologies in distal nerves, a length‐dependent process in patients with inherited polyneuropathies [5, 13, 33, 34]. As shown in Table S3, these correlations were largely dominated by diffusion parameters (FA, MD, AD, and RD) and T2*. Although diffusion metrics and T2* contributed most strongly to CMTIS, this does not imply that myelin‐sensitive parameters are less important. Rather, diffusion metrics and T2* may better capture the cumulative microstructural alterations associated with inherited neuropathies that are reflected in disability scores. In contrast, MTsat provides complementary information related to myelin integrity and was retained in the final classification model, supporting its value in differentiating demyelinating from axonal neuropathies despite its relatively smaller contribution to the CMTIS. Combining the Sn and Tn qMRI data further increased the correlation with the CMTES and CMTNSv2 scores. The composite qMRI score, CMTIS, was to represent all patient cohorts. As a result, the fVol parameter was deliberately excluded from the CMTIS, due to the lack of correlation between this parameter and polyneuropathies other than CMT1 (see Figures S2 and S3), consistent with what has been previously reported in the literature [2, 3].

Microstructural Implications in Multiparametric qMRI

Multiparametric qMRI revealed distinct patterns of pathologies between CMT1 (demyelinating) and CMT2 (axonal), which is consistent with the established pathological differences between the two conditions. CMT1 patients exhibited significantly reduced MTsat and FA, alongside elevated T1, PD, RD, and fVol, which is consistent with widespread demyelination (or dysmyelination), increased water content, and nerve hypertrophy [14, 35]. The strong reduction in FA, driven primarily by increased RD rather than AD, suggests that transverse diffusivity is a key marker of myelin disruption in this group, consistent with previous studies [16, 17].

MTR and MTsat are both sensitive to myelin content, although they exhibit subtle but important differences in sensitivity to confounding features. More specifically, the enhanced performance of MTsat is likely due to its reduced dependence on T1 relaxation and B1 field inhomogeneities, which can confound MTR measurements [15]. By incorporating corrections for these confounding factors, MTsat provided a more specific and quantitative assessment of myelin integrity, making it a potentially more promising biomarker for detecting demyelination in polyneuropathies. The study also observed a reduced T2* in CMT1 patients, which could reflect microstructural changes rather than demyelination caused by water content increases in the nerves. T2* is sensitive to microscopic magnetic field variation within a voxel. Nerves of the CMT1 patients could undergo segmental demyelination and remyelination, onion bulb formation, Schwann cell proliferation, and secondary axonal loss in these patients [36]. These pathological changes create a more heterogeneous tissue environment, increasing microscopic susceptibility gradients and accelerating dephasing, resulting in a shorter T2*.

To address slab‐profile inhomogeneity effects (see Methods section), oversampling was increased along the partition dimension. This adjustment had minimal impact on qMRI normative values in healthy controls, aside from an approximately 10% increase in T1 compared with previous work [19]. This correction updates the normative T1 values for the Sn and Tn and provides a more accurate reference for interpreting pathological changes.

Although the MRI data were acquired on three different 3 T scanners (Siemens Verio, Vida, and Cima.X), standardized acquisition protocols and the offline post‐processing pipeline used to compute qMRI parameters helped to ensure cross‐scanner validity. Specifically, MTR, MTsat, T1, and PD were derived from multiple scans using a product GRE sequence, which inherently minimizes sequence‐specific signal variations arising from data reconstruction and scanner calibration. In addition, B1 corrections were applied within the post‐processing pipeline to further minimize hardware‐dependent signal variations [19, 25]. R2* was computed from multi‐echo acquisitions within a single scan, further reducing systematic variability across scanners. Finally, DTI parameters depend primarily on the processing algorithm, which was standardized in the offline pipeline [27] rather than relying on scanner‐generated diffusivity maps. Nevertheless, real‐world inter‐scanner validation remains an important next step to ensure broader applicability.

Limitations

First, the cohorts were not age‐matched; in particular, the health controls were younger than the CMT patients. Although nerve qMRI may exhibit physiological changes with age, these are generally much smaller than the pathological changes in polyneuropathies [19, 37, 38]. Second, the present study was limited to common inherited polyneuropathies and did not include patients with acquired polyneuropathies. Third, although the correlations between the CMTIS and clinical scores (CMTNSv2 and CMTES) were statistically robust, these results should be interpreted with caution due to the relatively small sample size. Future large‐cohort prospective studies involving both acquired and inherited polyneuropathies with longitudinal designs are needed to determine the reliability of multiparametric qMRI, its sensitivity to disease progression, its correlation with clinical outcomes, and its potential as an objective biomarker for disease progression and therapeutic response.

Conclusion

We demonstrated that multiparametric qMRI can differentiate demyelinating from axonal forms of inherited polyneuropathy at both the sciatic and tibial nerve levels. The qMRI metrics revealed distinct patterns across these inherited polyneuropathy types. These findings justify future efforts in differentiating acquired demyelinating from axonal polyneuropathies, with the goal of developing a deployable diagnostic tool alongside the current clinical standard. Additionally, the CMTIS derived from these qMRI parameters showed promise as an objective imaging biomarker of disease severity, exhibiting strong correlations with clinical disability scores and supporting its potential use for monitoring progression in future clinical trials in inherited polyneuropathies. These findings support the utility of standardized qMRI protocols in the non‐invasive assessment and monitoring of polyneuropathies.
