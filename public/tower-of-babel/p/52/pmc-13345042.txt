Autologous Fat Grafting to Prevent Erosion and Pain Over Neuromodulation Hardware: Technical Case Series
Authors: Noah J. Lubin, Jermaine Robertson, Melissa Janssen, Sima Mofakham, Christopher S. Bellber, Sami U. Khan, Charles B. Mikell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES:Implanted neuromodulatory devices can be threatened by thin overlying tissue and refractory device-associated pain, both of which present significant clinical challenges with few reliable management strategies. We describe the technical workflow and preliminary clinical outcomes of autologous fat graft (AFG) for the management of neuromodulation device-related pain and dermal erosion.METHODS:This retrospective single-center technical case series details the indications, operative technique, and early postoperative results in 2 patients treated with a novel application of a standard plastic surgery technique in the context of functional neurosurgery. Autologous fat was harvested from patient donor sites and applied around a spinal cord stimulator to alleviate pain in one case and around a deep brain stimulation electrode to prevent dermal erosion in the other. Patients were followed for a minimum of 1 month of postoperative care.RESULTS:In both cases, AFG successfully addressed the hardware-related complication without requiring complete device revision. The first patient experienced substantial and durable pain reduction for 2 years before requiring repeat grafting due to progressive graft resorption. In the second case, improved scalp thickness prevented progression of early erosion, although mild thinning was noted at 1 month. No perioperative complications occurred in either case.CONCLUSION:This early technical experience demonstrates that AFG is feasible, well-tolerated, and may provide meaningful symptomatic and reconstructive benefit as a minimally invasive adjunct in neuromodulation surgery. Longer-term prospective evaluation is required before definitive conclusions regarding durability and clinical efficacy can be drawn.

Neuromodulatory therapies, including deep brain stimulation (DBS), vagus nerve stimulation, and spinal cord stimulation (SCS), are widely used interventions for debilitating and refractory neurologic and psychiatric conditions.1-3 Over the past 2 decades, neuromodulation use has expanded substantially.3-5 However, this growth has been accompanied by a proportional rise in hardware-related wound complications. Dermal erosion occurs in 1% to 8% of cases, most commonly overlying extension cables, the pulse generator pocket, or the burr hole site.6-8 Erosion creates a nidus for hardware infection in approximately 2% to 25% of implants.9-11 In addition, up to 31% of patients report persistent focal pain localized to device components such as the implantable pulse generator (IPG) pocket or lead anchor sites.12-14 Together, these complications threaten device longevity and patient quality of life.

To address these challenges, we applied autologous fat grafts (AFGs) as an adjunct to prevent and treat dermal erosion and hardware-related pain. To the best of our knowledge, only one group has previously reported AFG for neuromodulation hardware protection, limited to SCSs.15 Beyond mechanical padding, lipoaspirate contains adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (AD-MSCs) with angiogenic, immunomodulatory, and proliferative properties that support tissue repair.16-18 AD-MSCs are harvested in quantities up to 500-fold greater per unit volume than bone marrow-derived sources,19-21 and AFG has become an established modality in wound management across many specialties.22,23 We report 2 cases in which AFG was applied around threatened or painful neuromodulation hardware to mitigate complications and extend device longevity. This is a novel technical innovation bridging reconstructive and functional neurosurgery, and we report the indications, instrumentation, operative workflow, and early clinical experience of AFG. This technical note is reported in accordance with the PROCESS 2025 guideline for surgical case series.

METHODS

AFG was performed as part of the authors' clinical practice. Written informed consent was obtained for the procedure, clinical photography, and publication. This retrospective case series was conducted in accordance with institutional policy, which does not require Institutional Review Board approval for deidentified retrospective surgical case reports. All procedures involved collaboration between the Departments of Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery and Neurological Surgery.

Indications

AFG is considered for patients with threatened skin integrity (dermal thinning, translucency, early erosion) or focal device-related pain at the IPG pocket, lead anchors, or extension cable junction. Patients are excluded if they have insufficient subcutaneous adipose tissue for graft harvest or active tissue infection. AFG has been applied during generator replacement or as a salvage revision procedure, although it could be incorporated during primary device placement to proactively mitigate the risk of adverse outcomes, particularly in patients with low body fat. All patients underwent standard preoperative evaluation, including medication review, infection screening, and surgical clearance.

Operative Technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with sterile technique, and standard perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis, performed collaboratively by board-certified neurosurgeons and plastic surgeons. Fat graft preparation followed a simplified Coleman technique21,24,25 and was divided into 3 steps: harvesting, refinement, and application.

Neurosurgical Re-entry

The affected incision and donor site are simultaneously prepped and draped. The neurosurgeon performs limited re-entry through stab incisions adjacent to the hardware or opens prior incisions if hardware adjustment is required. Proper device function is confirmed before grafting proceeds.

Harvesting

Donor site selection depends on patient positioning and available adipose tissue volume. The infraumbilical abdomen is preferred in supine patients; the inferior gluteal fold is used in prone positioning (Figure 1). The medial thigh is used when greater volume or tissue quantity is needed in thin patients.

Several extraction methods are described throughout the literature (i.e., dry, wet, super-wet, tumescent methods; manual extraction; vacuum extraction; power-vibration assistance; and radiofrequency assistance), and the surgeon's choice is largely dependent on the size and purpose of their graft.26 In this procedure, only a small volume of fat graft is required (50 mL). Therefore, we prefer to extract manually using a negative pressure syringe aided by tumescent solution infiltration. Small stab incisions are made at the donor site with a number 11 blade. Tumescent solution consisting of epinephrine 1 mg/mL (preservative-free) and lidocaine 2% (500 mg/25 mL) added to 1000 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride (total volume 1025 mL) is infiltrated through a Mentor infiltration handle with Luer lock fitting and a Mentor Lamis infiltration cannula (14 ga × 23 cm; catalog BN1423) (Mentor Worldwide LLC) (Figure 2). After 10 to 15 minutes to allow for adequate local anesthetic analgesia as well as maximal vasoconstrictive effect of the epinephrine in order to minimize blood loss, lipoaspirate is extracted manually: a 4-mm blunt-tipped barbed harvesting cannula (10-mm port × 30 cm long; catalog PAL-R404LL) (MicroAire Surgical Instruments) connected using Luer lock to a 10 or 20 mL syringe is advanced into the donor site, and negative pressure is applied with gentle back-and-forth strokes until the desired volume is obtained. Barbed cannulas are preferred, as they have been shown to maximize viable AD-MSC yield without compromising stromal viability.27

Refinement

A Telfa rolling technique was used for fat processing in both reported cases (Figure 3). Lipoaspirate was gently rolled back-and-forth against sterile Telfa pads (Medtronic) using a tongue depressor, removing blood, tumescent fluid, and stromal debris through capillary action while preserving intact adipocytes and AD-MSCs. This technique is efficient, affordable, and well-validated for small-volume grafts.28-31 Newer closed-circuit processing systems such as centrifugation-based platforms using warm lactated Ringer's irrigation are now available and may further optimize adipocyte viability, although they were not used in the case described here.28-31

Application

Processed fat was backloaded into a 10 cc Luer lock syringe and transferred in small aliquots into 1 cc syringes through a female-female Luer lock connector. Injection was performed manually using a blunt-tipped Tulip cannula (3 mm × 25 cm) (Tulip Medical Products) with a standard Luer lock hub (Figure 4), held by hand and connected to the 1 cc syringe. Fat was deposited in small, evenly distributed aliquots in a retrograde fanning pattern throughout the subcutaneous tissue circumferentially around the hardware to maximize distribution (Figure 5).

Closure

After grafting, any necessary hardware adjustments are completed with care taken to not disrupt graft distribution. The wound is closed in standard multilayer fashion without drains. A standardized harvesting, refinement, and injection protocol was used in both cases to minimize technical variation.

Postoperative Follow-up

Outpatient follow-up was scheduled at 2 and 4 weeks postoperatively and conducted by both the neurosurgical and plastics teams. No routine postoperative imaging was obtained. Both patients completed all scheduled follow-up visits through 1 month. This technical note and case series has been reported in line with the PROCESS Guideline.32

RESULTS

Two patients underwent AFG at the time of neuromodulation revision or generator replacement, one for pain and one for dermal thinning with early erosion. Outcomes were assessed by narrative clinical evaluation given the descriptive nature and small sample size.

The first case involved a 63-year-old female smoker with an SCS for chronic pain who developed severe discomfort at the IPG site, rated 10/10 on the numeric pain scale. Examination revealed visible skin deformity with bowing at the superior aspect of the battery, consistent with incisional pain secondary to scar contracture beneath the IPG. Repositioning of the IPG to an alternative pocket was considered but deferred given her active smoking status and the associated wound healing risk33,34; she also preferred to keep the device in the same location. The IPG was replaced and 50 mL of AFG placed circumferentially around the device. She reported substantial improvement at 2 weeks and described the site as “comfortable” at 1 month. Approximately 2 years later, she re-presented with gradual recurrence of tenderness at the lead anchor sites, consistent with progressive graft resorption, and underwent revision AFG (56 mL). At both 2-week and 1-month follow-up after revision, she reported minimal pain and satisfactory soft-tissue contour. At the time of manuscript preparation, the patient has not returned to clinic with recurrent SCS-related pain more than 2 years after her revision procedure.

The second case involved a 69-year-old man with Parkinson disease and mantle cell lymphoma who developed progressive scalp atrophy 2 months after DBS implantation. He presented with a palpable right DBS lead protruding through thinned dermis and early epidermal erosion (1-2 cm) over the right frontal burr hole. Given his immunocompromised state and ongoing chemotherapy, the risk of complete erosion with resulting infection requiring explantation was significant. Formal flap-based reconstruction was considered high-risk in this context. A total of 2.5 mL of autologous adipose tissue was injected circumferentially around the exposed lead. AFG immediately improved scalp contour and eliminated visible and palpable hardware. At 2 weeks, the scalp demonstrated intact healing with uniform contour. By 1 month, mild thinning was noted compared with the previous examination, although overall thickness remained improved with no recurrence of erosion. The patient relocated out of state 4 months postoperatively and was subsequently lost to follow-up. No complications or recurrence of erosion were identified at any point during his available follow-up.

No perioperative or postoperative complications related to AFG occurred in either case, including infection, hematoma, seroma, fat necrosis, wound dehiscence, or hardware malfunction (Clavien–Dindo Grade I-V). DISCUSSION

AFG offers a biologically active and mechanically protective solution that can be integrated into neuromodulation workflows. The graft is autologous, minimizing immunogenic risk and promoting natural tissue integration. Harvesting and processing require no specialized equipment beyond standard liposuction cannulas and syringes, add minimal operative time, and can be performed concurrently with the neurosurgical portion of the procedure. These properties make AFG accessible to any center with plastic surgery collaboration and distinguish it from more resource-intense reconstructive alternatives.23,35,36

Device-related pain is among the most prevalent and difficult-to-manage hardware complications in neuromodulation, affecting up to 31% of patients.12-14 It arises from a combination of scar contracture, neuroma formation, foreign body response, and direct mechanical pressure from prominent hardware against thin overlying tissue.12,37-39 Management options are limited: systemic analgesics carry dependency risk, local corticosteroid injections provide only transient relief, and surgical revision carries its own morbidity. Body habitus is a clinically important modulating factor. Patients with low body mass index and minimal subcutaneous tissue have less natural cushioning between hardware and dermis, increasing mechanical pressure on overlying skin and the likelihood of focal pain. This relationship also affects donor site availability in thin patients and should be discussed during preoperative planning.

Long-term durability and graft volume retention are the central unanswered questions raised by this early experience. Published data suggest AFG retention of 20% to 80% at 1 year, varying with recipient site vascularity, tissue mobility, processing technique, and individual metabolic factors.40-42 In our first case, meaningful symptom relief was maintained for approximately 2 years before repeat augmentation was required, and she has not returned with recurrent complaints more than 2 years since the revision procedure. Together these observations suggest that AFG can provide clinically durable benefits even in the setting of progressive resorption. The mild scalp thinning observed at 1 month in our second case, in a patient with likely compromised vascularity from chemotherapy, underscores that resorption may be faster in immunocompromised patients. Surgeons should counsel patients that repeat grafting may be a component of the long-term management strategy rather than an indication of primary failure. Importantly, repeat AFG carries the same low procedural burden as the initial procedure and does not preclude definitive reconstruction if ultimately required.

A practical consideration is the effect of future device revisions on established fat grafts. Generator replacement or lead revision will necessarily disrupt graft architecture to a variable degree. Therefore, in our practice, we anticipate incorporating repeat AFG into any planned revision in previously grafted patients to restore a protective soft-tissue layer. This reframes repeat grafting as a manageable maintenance component of longitudinal neuromodulation care.

AFG should be understood in the context of the full reconstructive spectrum. For patients with established full-thickness erosion, active infection, or prior failed conservative measures, definitive reconstruction is required. Scalp rotation flaps provide robust, well-vascularized coverage but entail donor-site morbidity, alopecia risk, extended recovery, and significant operative complexity.43-45 Device relocation, including subfascial pocket revision or alternative subcutaneous placement, can be effective when device position is the primary driver of complications but requires a more extensive procedure and is not always anatomically feasible. AFG is not intended to replace these approaches, but for some patients particularly those with early-stage soft-tissue compromise or focal device pain, it may represent a definitive intervention rather than a bridge to invasive reconstruction. It occupies a meaningful niche between standard wound closure and formal reconstruction, addressing complications at a stage when a minimally invasive solution is sufficient.

Beyond its potentially curative role, AFG holds theoretical appeal as a prophylactic measure at primary implantation, particularly in high-risk patients: those with low body mass index, prior radiation, active smoking, immunosuppression, or history of poor wound healing. Prophylactic grafting could be incorporated at no additional surgical exposure, adding only modest operative time. However, no validated predictive model currently identifies which patients will develop hardware-related complications, making routine prophylactic use difficult to justify. We therefore regard this application as a hypothesis warranting prospective evaluation in high-risk patients rather than a currently endorsable practice.

Together, these early experiences suggest that AFG can fill a meaningful gap, one that currently lacks reliable minimally invasive solutions, in the management of hardware-related soft tissue complications. By offering a biologically active method of soft-tissue reinforcement that can be seamlessly incorporated into existing revision workflows, AFG has the potential to reduce hardware-related morbidity, preserve device longevity, and spare patients from cumbersome reconstructive procedures. No specialized training beyond standard plastic surgery skills is required and no AFG-related complications occurred in either case. Although these cases used manual syringe aspiration and Telfa rolling for fat processing, newer technologies such as power-assisted liposuction and closed-circuit centrifugation platforms may further optimize adipocyte viability as adoption grows. Continued prospective evaluation with longer follow-up, objective soft tissue measurements, and patient-reported outcomes will be necessary to establish formal indications, define optimal regrafting intervals, and realize the full preventative and reconstructive potential of AFG in neuromodulation surgery.

Limitations

This report is best understood as a technical feasibility note rather than an outcomes study, and formal conclusions regarding durability, optimal regrafting intervals, or long-term complication rates cannot be drawn from 2 cases. Follow-up for the first case extends beyond 2 years from her revision procedure based on absence of recurrent complications, although this reflects passive clinical surveillance rather than prospective assessment. For the second case, follow-up beyond 4 months was unavailable as the patient relocated out of state and was lost to follow-up. No complications or recurrence of erosion had been documented before that point. Additional limitations include graft volume retention depending on recipient site vascularity, tissue mobility, and patient metabolic factors, potentially necessitating repeated augmentation. Limited donor volume availability in very thin patients and inability to treat established deep infections represent additional procedural constraints. Prospective evaluation with standardized follow-up intervals, objective soft tissue measurement, and patient-reported outcome measures will be necessary to establish formal indications and optimize technical parameters for wider adoption.

CONCLUSION

As neuromodulation use expands, managing hardware-related soft-tissue complications is increasingly important. AFG provides a minimally invasive, biologically active adjunct that can be integrated into existing neuromodulation workflows without meaningful additional morbidity. This early experience demonstrates technical feasibility and short-term clinical benefit in patients with focal device pain and early dermal erosion. Longer-term prospective study is needed to define optimal patient selection, regrafting intervals, and the full scope of clinical benefit.
