Nonlinear association between blood eosinophil counts and clinical outcomes in ICU-Admitted patients with acute exacerbation of COPD: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Rundi Gao, Suqun Zheng, Xiao Shen, Liying Xu, Huihua Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis retrospective cohort study aimed to examine the association between blood eosinophil counts and in-hospital mortality among patients with acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU).MethodsData were retrieved from the MIMIC-IV 2.2 database. AECOPD patients with blood eosinophil counts measured within the first 24 h following ICU admission were included. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis compared in-hospital mortality across eosinophil thresholds (0.10, 0.15, 0.20, and 0.30 × 10⁹/L). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis based on a Cox proportional hazards model was used to assess the non-linear relationship between eosinophil counts and in-hospital mortality.ResultsA total of 1,855 patients were analyzed. KM survival curves showed significant mortality differences at eosinophil thresholds of 0.10, 0.15, and 0.20 × 10⁹/L. Compared with Group I, Group II consistently exhibited a significantly lower risk of in-hospital mortality across all Cox models. Group III showed a mortality reduction only in the unadjusted model, which lost statistical significance after adjustment. RCS analysis revealed an “L”-shaped association, with an inflection point around 0.042 × 10⁹/L.ConclusionLower eosinophil counts were associated with increased in-hospital mortality among critically ill AECOPD patients. Absolute eosinophil counts may serve as a prognostic biomarker to support personalized ICU management. Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation and chronic symptoms such as cough, dyspnea, and recurrent exacerbation according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) [1]. These manifestations result from structural and inflammatory changes in the airways (e.g., bronchitis, bronchiolitis) and alveoli (emphysema), contributing to significant morbidity and mortality worldwide [2]. COPD currently ranks as the third leading cause of death globally, with acute exacerbation being a major driver of hospital admissions and intensive care unit (ICU) utilization [3, 4].

Increasingly recognized as a heterogeneous disease, COPD comprises multiple phenotypes with varying pathophysiological mechanisms, inflammatory profiles, and responses to treatment [5]. Although neutrophilic inflammation is the predominant pattern in most cases, a subset of patients exhibits eosinophilic airway inflammation, which is gaining attention due to its therapeutic relevance [6]. Blood eosinophil count has emerged as a practical biomarker for identifying eosinophilic phenotypes to guide treatment decisions, particularly for the use of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), systemic corticosteroids, and biologic agents [7–9].

Evidence suggests that patients with higher eosinophil counts may experience more frequent exacerbations but tend to respond better to corticosteroid therapy than those with lower counts [6, 8]. However, the prognostic value of blood eosinophil levels, especially in critically ill patients with acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD), remains unclear. Conflicting results in the literature may stem from differences in eosinophil quantification methods (percentages vs. absolute counts), patient populations, and chosen thresholds [10–12].

To address these gaps, we conducted a retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC-IV database to investigate the association between admission eosinophil counts and in-hospital mortality in ICU-admitted AECOPD patients. We further explored potential non-linear relationships and sought to identify clinically meaningful cutoff points that could improve risk stratification and inform treatment strategies in critical care settings.

Methods

Data source

All patient data for this retrospective study were extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) version 2.2 database. MIMIC-IV is a publicly available database containing comprehensive clinical data of patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) in Boston, Massachusetts, between 2008 and 2019 [11]. One of the authors (Dr. Huihua Hong) was granted access to the MIMIC-IV database after completing the required “Data or Specimens Only Research” training and certification via the CITI Program (ID: 11356022), in compliance with the data use agreement and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) requirements of BIDMC. All study procedures complied with applicable regulations and ensured the protection of patient privacy. Due to the use of de-identified data, the informed consent was waived.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Consistent with prior research, AECOPD was defined when any of these International Classification of Diseases (ICD)−9 (491, 492, 496) or ICD-10 (J41-J44) codes appeared within the first three diagnosis fields [13]. For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only data from their first ICU stay were used. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 18 years; (2) patients without survival outcome data. (3) patients without eosinophil measurements within the first 24 h of ICU admission.

Data extraction

Structured Query Language (SQL) was used in PostgreSQL (version 15.1) and pgAdmin4 (version 6.21) to extract patient information. Variables were grouped into four categories: (1) demographics, including age, gender, weight, race; and so on; (2) comorbidities, including sepsis, asthma, myocardial infarct, cerebrovascular disease, and so on; (3) laboratory tests such as eosinophils, lymphocyte, hemoglobin, creatinine, and so on; (4) illness severity scores at admission, including the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and the Acute Physiology Score III (APS III). The follow-up process initiated on the admission date and concluded on either the date of death or the discharge date. All laboratory values and severity scores were obtained within the first 24 h after ICU admission. Follow-up extended from the time of admission to either hospital discharge or in-hospital death.

Missing data management

To reduce bias from incomplete data, variables with > 30% missing values (e.g., serum lactate) were excluded. For continuous variables with < 5% missingness, mean imputation was applied. Variables with 5–30% missingness were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations, with results pooled according to Rubin’s rules; categorical variables were imputed using logistic or polytomous regression as appropriate.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. The secondary outcome was hospital length of stay (LOS).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on the distribution assessed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Between-group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the independent t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test.

Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves were constructed to compare in-hospital mortality across different eosinophil count thresholds. We selected eosinophil thresholds of 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, and 0.30 × 10⁹/L based on the current GOLD guidelines and thresholds commonly used in biologic therapy trials. We additionally performed a sensitivity analysis using tertile-based categorization derived from the distribution of eosinophil counts in our study population. Statistical differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test.

We applied restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis to evaluate the potential non-linear association between blood eosinophil counts and in-hospital mortality. The RCS analysis was conducted within a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model framework, adjusting for clinically relevant covariates. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated across the range of eosinophil counts. Four models were built: Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for age, sex, vital signs, and comorbidities; Model 3: adjusted for Model 2 plus laboratory parameters; Model 4: adjusted for Model 3 plus illness severity scores (SAPS II, APS III, SOFA) and treatment variables (e.g., mechanical ventilation and corticosteroid use). For all Cox models, patients were categorized into three groups based on admission eosinophil counts: eosinophil counts < 0.1 × 10⁹/L (Group I), 0.1 × 10⁹/L ≤ eosinophil counts < 0.3 × 10⁹/L (Group II), and eosinophil counts ≥ 0.3 × 10⁹/L (Group III). Group I was used as the reference group for HR estimation. The proportional hazards assumption was tested for all models using Schoenfeld residuals and visual inspection.

To assess potential non-linear relationships between eosinophil counts and in-hospital mortality, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were incorporated into the Cox framework. Knots were placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of eosinophil values to balance model flexibility and overfitting. Adjusted HRs with 95% CIs were plotted across the range of eosinophil counts, and non-linearity was tested using the Wald test. Furthermore, stratified RCS analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modification by key clinical variables, including sepsis, mechanical ventilation (MV), asthma, gender, and cortisone use. Subgroup-specific RCS curves were generated to assess whether the shape and magnitude of the eosinophil-mortality association differed across clinical subgroups. Visual comparison of curve trends and confidence intervals was used to interpret potential heterogeneity in effect.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.0).

Results

A total of 2,470 patients were initially screened, of whom 1,855 (75.1%) met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. Among them, 252 (13.6%) patients died during hospitalization (non-survivors), while 1,603 (86.4%) survived. The overall in-hospital and ICU mortality rates were 13.6% and 9.8%, respectively. The mean age of survivors was 72.0 ± 11.1, and 53.9% of patients were male.

Baseline characteristics between survivors and Non-Survivors

Baseline characteristics

Compared to survivors, non-survivors were older and exhibited higher severity of illness scores on admission, including SAPS II, APS III, and SOFA scores. They also had a higher prevalence of comorbidities such as congestive heart failure, renal disease, malignancy, and sepsis. Laboratory findings showed elevated neutrophil counts, urea, and creatinine levels among non-survivors. Additionally, non-survivors had longer ICU stays (median 3.6 vs. 2.1 days, p < 0.001) but shorter total hospital stays (median 6.2 vs. 7.9 days, p < 0.001). Notably, eosinophil counts were significantly lower in non-survivors compared to survivors (mean 0.09 ± 0.2 × 10⁹/L vs. 0.1 ± 0.3 × 10⁹/L, p = 0.026). Detailed comparisons of the demographic, clinical, and laboratory data are shown in Table 1.

Outcomes

Eosinophil counts and in-hospital mortality

To evaluate the association between eosinophil counts and in-hospital mortality across various clinically relevant thresholds, we conducted four separate Kaplan-Meier survival analyses using cutoffs of 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, and 0.3 × 10⁹/L. These thresholds were selected based on current GOLD guidelines and thresholds commonly used in biologic therapy trials [8, 12, 14]. These cutoffs reflect clinically meaningful categories and were supplemented with a data-driven sensitivity analysis using tertiles.

The result showed that a decrease in eosinophil counts was correlated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality (Fig. 2). When stratifying by the 0.1 × 10⁹/L cutoff, patients with eosinophil counts below this threshold exhibited markedly higher mortality rates compared to those with counts ≥ 0.1 × 10⁹/L (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2 A). Similarly, a 0.15 × 10⁹/L threshold showed a significantly higher in-hospital mortality risk among patients with eosinophil counts below this level (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2B). At the 0.20 × 10⁹/L cutoff, a statistically significant association was observed, with counts ≥ 0.2 × 10⁹/L, conferring reduced mortality risk (p = 0.015) (Fig. 2C). However, the 0.30 × 10⁹/L cutoff did not yield a statistically significant difference (p = 0.15) (Fig. 2D).

The distribution of blood eosinophil counts in the study population is shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Additional sensitivity analyses using tertile-based categorization yielded results consistent with our main findings (Supplementary Figures S2 and Figure S3).

Multivariate Cox regression analysis

To adjust for potential confounding factors, four Cox regression models were constructed. In all models, patients were categorized into three groups based on blood eosinophil counts measured at ICU admission: Group I (< 0.1 × 10⁹/L, reference), Group II (0.1–0.3 × 10⁹/L), and Group III (≥ 0.3 × 10⁹/L).

In the unadjusted model (Model 1), both Group II (HR 0.41, 95% CI 0.28–0.60; p < 0.001) and Group III (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.35–0.96; p = 0.035) were associated with reduced in-hospital mortality compared with Group I. After adjusting for demographic characteristics, vital signs, and comorbidities (Model 2), the protective association remained statistically significant for Group II (HR 0.53; 95% CI, 0.36–0.78; p = 0.001), whereas the association for Group III was attenuated and no longer significant (HR 0.67; 95% CI, 0.40–1.12; p = 0.13).

Further adjustment for laboratory parameters (Model 3) yielded similar results, with Group II showing a significant reduction in mortality risk (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.45–0.98; p = 0.038), while Group III remained non-significant (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.41–1.31; p = 0.30). In the fully adjusted model including illness severity scores and treatment variables (Model 4), Group II maintained a significantly lower risk of in-hospital mortality (HR 0.63; 95% CI, 0.43–0.93; p = 0.019), whereas Group III showed a non-significant trend toward decreased risk (HR 0.71; 95% CI, 0.40–1.27; p = 0.20) (Table 2).

These findings indicate that AECOPD patients admitted to the ICU with eosinophil counts between 0.1 and 0.3 × 10⁹/L experience the most consistent survival benefit, underscoring the prognostic value of eosinophil stratification in critical care.

Restricted cubic spline analysis

RCS were integrated into the Cox proportional hazards model to explore potential non-linear associations between eosinophil counts and in-hospital mortality. In our cohort, 42.85% of patients had blood eosinophil counts greater than 0.05 × 10⁹/L, whereas only 3.35% had counts exceeding 0.50 × 10⁹/L, indicating a sparse distribution at the higher end of the range. The RCS curve revealed a distinct L-shaped relationship (p for non-linearity < 0.001), indicating that the mortality risk sharply increased when eosinophil counts fell below approximately 0.042 × 10⁹/L, after which the risk plateaued (Fig. 3A). This suggests that very low eosinophil levels are associated with disproportionately elevated mortality risk, while higher levels do not confer additional benefit beyond the threshold. To further examine the relationship around the inflection point (~ 0.042 × 10⁹/L), we generated a zoom-in RCS plot restricted to the range of 0–0.1 × 10⁹/L, where the majority of patient data were concentrated (Supplementary Figure S4). Within this range, the hazard ratio demonstrated a stable L-shaped association, and the distribution plot confirmed adequate data support.

Subgroup analyses

Stratified RCS analyses were performed to examine whether this non-linear association differed across key clinical subgroups, including sepsis, mechanical ventilation (MV), asthma, gender, and corticosteroid use (Fig. 3B). This L-shaped pattern was consistently observed across all subgroups, although the strength of the association varied.

In patients with sepsis and those requiring MV, the inverse association was particularly pronounced. At eosinophil counts below 0.042 × 10⁹/L, the HR rose steeply, peaking above 8 and 4, respectively, before leveling off. This indicates that critically ill patients with both sepsis or MV and eosinopenia face a markedly elevated risk of death.

In contrast, among patients stratified by asthma, gender, or corticosteroid use, the L-shaped relationship remained consistent across all subgroups, but the RCS curves largely overlapped. The curves in these subgroups remained consistently below a hazard ratio of 1 at higher eosinophil levels, though confidence intervals crossed 1.

Discussion

Utilizing data from the MIMIC-IV database, our study presents a comprehensive analysis of the relationship between blood eosinophil counts and mortality in critically ill AECOPD patients. The primary findings confirm the inverse association between eosinophil levels and in-hospital mortality, with a distinct “L”-shaped relationship and an inflection point around 0.042 × 10⁹/L. These results suggest eosinophil counts at admission might serve as a clinically relevant prognostic marker in this population.

COPD is a heterogeneous disease encompassing various phenotypes [5, 15]. Approximately 20–40% of patients exhibit an eosinophilic phenotype, which has distinct clinical features and better corticosteroid responsiveness [16]. Patients with higher eosinophil counts often present with more severe symptoms and an increased risk of recurrent exacerbations [17, 18]. However, paradoxically, they may exhibit lower in-hospital mortality, as indicated by our findings, likely due to lower systemic inflammatory burden and better treatment response [8, 16].

Current guidelines such as GOLD recommend blood eosinophil counts to predict ICS responsiveness [1]. Our findings further highlight the prognostic value of eosinophils in critically ill COPD patients, supporting routine eosinophil measurement to aid risk stratification and treatment decisions.

Previous studies have reported inconsistent associations between eosinophil counts and mortality, influenced by differences in patient populations, measurement methods, and eosinophil metrics (percentage vs. absolute counts). Holland et al. observed that eosinopenia (≤ 0.04 × 10⁹/L) was associated with increased mortality and longer hospital stays [19]. Similarly, Zhang et al. reported that higher eosinophil counts (≥ 150 cells/µL) were linked to reduced all-cause mortality [20]. Conversely, Zysman et al. reported no significant association between blood eosinophil levels and three-year survival rates in COPD patients [21]. Other studies, such as the Copenhagen General Population Study, suggested that elevated eosinophil counts (≥ 345 cells/µL) might be associated with increased mortality, although this association was predominantly observed in stable COPD populations rather than during acute exacerbations [22].

The controversy extends to patients requiring ICU admission. The percentage levels of blood eosinophils prior to patients’ ER visits and their peak levels within the first 7 days of treatment had no association with hospital mortality in AECOPD patients needed ICU admission [12]. Jiaqi Pu et al. discovered that lower blood eosinophils (with EOS < 2%) were linked to an elevated risk of in-hospital mortality in total AECOPD patients, yet this was not the case for patients admitted to the ICU [12], but over 95% of critically ill patients exhibit eosinophil levels below this cutoff, thereby limiting its discriminative capacity in this high-risk cohort [23]. In contrast, Dr. Jia Yang and colleagues found that elevated blood eosinophils percentages were linked to reduced in-hospital mortality and shorter hospital stays among AECOPD patients admitted to ICU with eosinophil threshold of 0.35% [11]. Notably, eosinophil percentages appear less reliable than absolute counts for mortality prediction [24]. By using absolute counts, our study strengthens the evidence for a protective association of higher eosinophil levels in severe COPD cases.

There is no universal consensus on the appropriate cutoff values for eosinophilic COPD [25]. GOLD suggests 100–300 cells/µL for ICS indication [1]. However, these thresholds are not universally applicable to other clinical outcomes or settings. For example, some studies have used absolute eosinophil counts such as ≥ 150 or ≥ 300 cells/µL to stratify corticosteroid response or mortality risk, while others relied on eosinophil percentages (e.g., ≥ 2% or < 1%) as prognostic markers [8, 10, 12, 26], leading to inconsistencies in findings. In our study, we evaluated multiple clinically relevant cutoffs (100, 150, 200, 300 cells/µL) and found that lower eosinophil counts consistently predicted higher mortality, underscoring their prognostic value in critical care.

Eosinophil levels can be affected by age, sex, BMI, smoking, comorbidities, infections [27–30]. Furthermore, therapeutic measures like corticosteroids can cause eosinopenia, complicating interpretation. Additionally, non-eosinophilic AECOPD have been associated with worse clinical outcomes, including higher rates of invasive mechanical ventilation and elevated severity scores [12]. To minimize confounding, we adjusted for key factors such as age, asthma, sepsis and mechanical ventilation. Even after adjustment, lower eosinophil counts remained associated with higher mortality, although statistical significance diminished when the threshold was set at 0.3 × 10⁹/L, possibly due to a smaller sample size in this subgroup.

Importantly, our study is the first to reveal a distinct non-linear, L-shaped relationship with an inflection point around 0.042 × 10⁹/L. In subgroup analyses, the inverse association between eosinophil counts and mortality was most pronounced in patients with sepsis or requiring mechanical ventilation. In contrast, among patients stratified by asthma, gender, or corticosteroid use, the L-shaped relationship remained consistent across all subgroups, but the RCS curves largely overlapped, indicating a weaker or non-significant association. These findings highlight eosinopenia (< 0.042 × 10⁹/L) as a potential threshold for identifying high-risk patients, especially those with severe clinical conditions such as sepsis or mechanical ventilation. Below this level, the risk of in-hospital death increased sharply, highlighting the potential of this value as a clinically significant cutoff for identifying high-risk patients and guided intervention. This non-linear analysis adds important nuance, demonstrating that the prognostic value of eosinophil counts is not uniform across the full range of values. Rather, a critical threshold exists, below which mortality risk rises disproportionately. These findings suggest that reliance on fixed cutoffs-as done in many previous studies-may obscure meaningful clinical signals in high-risk ICU populations.

Limitations include the retrospective, single-center design, lack of pre-hospitalization data such as prior ICS and systemic corticosteroid use, and absence of differentiation according to the GOLD ABE grouping, which is important as previous severe exacerbations and prior treatments can influence both mortality risk and blood eosinophil measurements. Additionally, the use of ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes in the first three diagnosis fields to identify patients likely with acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) may not perfectly distinguish between a history of COPD and true acute exacerbations. Furthermore, as the majority of patients in our cohort were White, the findings may have limited generalizability to more diverse populations. Future prospective, multicenter studies with more precise phenotyping and detailed clinical data are needed to validate these findings and explore underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

This study identifies a critical non-linear, L-shaped association between admission blood eosinophil counts and in-hospital mortality in critically ill AECOPD patients, with an inflection point at 0.042 × 10⁹/L. Below this threshold, mortality risk rises exponentially, particularly in patients with sepsis or requiring mechanical ventilation. These findings establish absolute eosinophil counts as a robust prognostic biomarker for risk stratification and therapeutic guidance in intensive care settings. Incorporating eosinophil counts into ICU triage or early management protocols may help clinicians identify high-risk patients more accurately and optimize resource allocation. Further studies are needed to confirm these findings and refine the application of eosinophil-based risk stratification.
