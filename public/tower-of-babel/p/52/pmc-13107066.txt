3D imaging with enhanced transparency, signal-to-background ratios, and antigen detection using HyPer-3D
Authors: Tansol Choi, Lauretta A. Lacko, Neranjan de Silva, Nang Kham Hsi Liang, Ying Liu, Edgar A. Jamies, Todd Evans, Romulo Hurtado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe developed HyPer-3D (hydrogen peroxide-NaN3-DMSO), a chemical treatment that addresses persistent challenges in 3D imaging of cleared tissues. Imaging across stem cell, zebrafish, and murine model systems demonstrated that HyPer-3D quenches tissue autofluorescence, increases signal-to-background ratios (SBRs) by 30×, potentiates optical clearing capacity by 6×, increases detection of poorly recognized antigens by 4.5×, and enables a 5× increase in fluorescent reporter detection, which are conversely often diminished in 3D imaging. These improvements enhanced multiplex interrogation of highly autofluorescent and fibrous organs, including previously undocumented nephron segments, cardiac conduction system elements, and diffuse spermatogonial stem cells. Optimization for human tissues, which remain among the most difficult to 3D imaging due to autofluorescence and structural density far exceeding those of commonly used animal tissues, yielded a 5× increase in SBRs and enabled multiplex imaging with cellular resolution. Collectively, HyPer-3D provides a robust approach for high-contrast 3D imaging of basic and clinical specimens.

Introduction

Understanding the global tissue architecture of organs provides key insights into normal and pathological organ physiology. However, visualizing the three-dimensional (3D) organization of a tissue is limited by its natural opacity that causes light scattering and prevents deep penetration of light. Traditionally, these challenges have been addressed by assaying serial thin histological sections and then attempting to derive 3D information. This approach, however, is laborious and time consuming and can result in unsatisfactory reconstruction in the case of damaged or lost sections. To this end, our understanding of system-level organ structure has been greatly advanced by optical clearing methods that enable direct 3D imaging through intact organs.1,2

In general, optical clearing methodologies consist of a lipid removal step that makes the whole tissue accessible, and a further step of immersing the tissue in refractive index-matching solutions that allow light to travel through the specimen without light scattering. These protocols can be broadly categorized into either solvent-based or aqueous-based protocols.1,2 Aqueous methods such as CLARITY,3 Scale,4,5 ClearT2.6 SeeDB,7 FRUIT,8 CUBIC,9,10,11 and EZ Clear12 are advantageous because they are simple, maintain the natural hydration state of tissues, are non-toxic, and can preserve antigens and fluorescent reporters. Conversely, solvent-based methods such as TRISCO,13 Adipo-Clear,14 and those of the DISCO series (iDISCO+,15 sDISCO,16 uDISCO,17 and FDISCO18) can also retain fluorescent reporters and have been broadly implemented because they are robust and generally more amenable to widely varying tissue types. The incorporation of solvents and dehydration steps in this latter category may benefit from thoughtful optimization to preserve antigenicity and ensure user safety.2

Tissue types vary on how amenable they are to optical clearing and 3D imaging. For instance, fibrous tissues, such as muscles, are very difficult to optically clear. Moreover, the autofluorescence (AF) naturally exhibited by tissues remains a major, widely recognized challenge in 3D imaging, as it limits the ability to detect fluorescently labeled structures.1,2 This presents difficulties for multiplex 3D imaging of organs such as the kidneys that exhibit notoriously high background noise or the heart that is both fibrous and has significant AF. Notably, interrogating human biopsies of these organs is even more challenging, as human tissues are more fibrous and exhibit significantly higher AF than analogous tissues of commonly used small mammalian animal models.

Here, we developed HyPer-3D (hydrogen peroxide-NaN3-DMSO), a tissue treatment technique that can be incorporated into 3D clearing methods and specifically addresses the difficulties associated with imaging fibrous tissues and those that naturally exhibit a high background noise. We demonstrate that HyPer-3D potentiates the clearing capacity of all tested optical clearing chemicals, increases fluorescent reporter protein signal detection, and facilitates the imaging of poorly recognized antigens and multiplex interrogation of highly autofluorescent and fibrous tissues. Finally, HyPer-3D was further optimized for use with human specimens for complex 3D imaging in challenging research and clinical samples with advanced transparency and antigen visibility.

AF, autofluorescence.

Decolorization of tissues facilitates 3D imaging, for instance, with the removal of blood that is a known source of AF and deterrent of optical clearing. This can be readily seen in perfused murine hearts where regions of the organ flush with blood exhibit the highest AF (Figure S1A) and are also the most resistant to optical clearing (Figures S1B and S1C). Thus, we theorized that a decolorization method would be beneficial for mitigating background noise and augmenting optical clearing. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) has been broadly used for blood removal, as well as for quenching AF. However, H2O2 damages tissue as catalase converts H2O2 into water and oxygen, and the accumulation of oxygenation causes tissue tearing and increases in the AF of damaged tissue regions.19 Tissue tearing was readily observed when the heart or kidney sections were treated with standard H2O2 (Figures S1D and S1E). Tissue regions damaged by H2O2 oxygenation exhibited increased AF (Figures S1F and S1G).

To address this challenge, we aimed to develop an alternative AF-quenching technology. Because oxygenation corresponded with tissue damage and increased AF, we screened ten putative reversible inhibitors of catalase to moderate oxygen production while maintaining blood removal (Figure S1H). Sodium azide (NaN3) used with H2O2 in a basic, buffered solution containing DMSO, termed HyPer-3D, inhibited oxygenation while preserving tissue decolorization (Figure S1H, showing effervescing oxygenation, as observed in all other conditions, but not with NaN3 (see also Video S1, standard H2O2 treatment; and Video S2, HyPer-3D treatment of kidney tissues). In contrast to standard protocols, treatment of whole kidneys or 1 mm heart sections showed that HyPer-3D fully decolorized the tissues without causing tissue degradation (Figures S1I–S1N). HyPer-3D significantly reduced tissue AF, as illustrated in kidneys divided into two equal halves, with one-half treated with HyPer-3D and the second-half (control) left untreated (Figure 1B). HyPer-3D progressively diminished AF in a time-dependent manner, resulting in a compelling difference in green (500–550 nm) and red (578–633 nm) AF background noise between the treated and control kidney tissue lacking treatment (Figures 1B, 1C, and S1O–S1Q). Time-dependent quenching of green and red AF was validated in 1 mm thick heart sections (Figures 1D and S1R–S1T). Treatment for up to 72 h with HyPer-3D facilitated an average of 52% decrease in green AF and >85% decrease in red AF of the kidney and heart tissues (Figure 1E).

Consistent with our underlying hypothesis, HyPer-3D also significantly augmented optical clearing (Figures 1F–1S). To this end, adjacent 0.5 mm sections from unperfused kidneys were left untreated, serving as controls, or treated with HyPer-3D, and then both sections were immersed in refractive index-matching, optical clearing solutions from published 3D clearing techniques, and then imaged side-by-side (Figures 1F–1I). Optical clearing was assessed for maximal transparency (as determined by underlying grid visibility), as well as for the rate of optical clearing by real-time analyses (see Videos S3, S4, S5, and S6). Incubation with EZ view (EZ Clear12 protocol), for instance, showed a striking difference in optical clearing with or without HyPer-3D treatment. Live imaging revealed almost instantaneous clearing of HyPer-3D-treated tissues, whereas control tissues took longer to begin clearing and never reached the same level of transparency (Figure 1F, EZ view; Video S3). Similar findings were noted by live imaging of control and HyPer-3D-treated tissues with CUBIC-2 (CUBIC9 protocol; Figure 1G and Video S4), MACS-R2 (MACS protocol20; Figure 1H and Video S5), and FOCM (FOCM protocol21; Figure 1I and Video S6), with HyPer-3D tissues exhibiting faster and more thorough optical clearing (Figure 1J). Figure S2 shows representative images from movies of whole sections cleared over time. As shown in Figures 1K–1O, significant increases in optical clearing were similarly seen with ScaleA2 (ScaleS protocol5), SeeDB,7 ClearT2.6 sRIMS (PACT protocol22), and glycerol (CLARITY protocol3). These data were further corroborated by performing complete 3D imaging assays on unperfused adult murine kidneys, hearts, and brains (Figures 1P–1R). For instance, whole adult murine kidneys were bisected; one-half was processed as per the published CUBIC L/R + (N) protocol11,23 (Cont), and the second-half was treated first with HyPer-3D (HyP). HyPer-3D significantly increased the optical transparency of the kidney (Figure 1P). Similar results were seen with the EZ Clear protocol, with kidney and brain tissues exhibiting greater transparency with HyPer-3D treatment (Figures 1Q and 1R). Heart tissues treated with HyPer-3D and subsequently by EZ Clear were rendered transparent in just 3 h (Figure 1Q; inset, heart prior to optical clearing). Indeed, treatment with HyPer-3D increased the optical clearing capacity of all tested refractive index-matching solutions, giving a striking increase in the transparency of over 600% for CUBIC-2, 280% for EZ View, 470% for MACS-R2, 200% for FOCM, 320% for ScaleA2, 330% for ClearT2, 260% for glycerol, 210% for SeeDB, and 90% for sRIMS (Figure 1S; see also Table S1 in supplemental information for corresponding quantitative values and statistics).

Analyses of tissues before and after treatment with these clearing methods revealed varying levels of increases in AF, except with formamide that produced a moderate reduction in AF (Figures S1U–S1E′). A mixture of formamide with glycerol gave higher optical clearing than the formamide-based clearing method ClearT2, and when tissues were first treated with HyPer-3D, formamide + glycerol-based clearing was sufficient to render the heart and kidney tissues clear with a high degree of preservation of morphology and size (Figures S3A–S3E). However, formamide + glycerol clearing of whole hearts and kidneys was slow and took up to 2 weeks. Conversely, as aforementioned, use of HyPer-3D followed by EZ View cleared whole adult murine hearts in 3 h.

IF, immunofluorescence.

To further corroborate these findings, we assayed whole adult murine hearts that are both fibrous and highly autofluorescent. Whole unperfused hearts were bisected, with control and HyPer-3D-treated halves assayed for the coronary arterial bed (SMA, red), using the CUBIC L/R + (N) protocol (Figures 2F–2J). Light sheet microscopy showed a robust difference in the immunofluorescence (IF) signal (Figure 2F). Coronary arteries were readily distinguishable from background tissue in HyPer-3D-treated tissue, in contrast to control (Figure 2G). High-magnification views showed that both large and smaller caliber vessels were readily differentiable from background in the HyPer-3D-treated tissue, whereas in control tissue, large vessels were apparent, but smaller vascular branches were difficult to distinguish from background (Figures 2H and 2I). The HyPer-3D-treated heart tissue exhibited a 32× greater SBR than the control (Figure 2J; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).

The brain is well recognized to exhibit high AF (Figures S4F–S4J). Whole unperfused neonatal (P10) mouse brains were bisected into left and right hemispheres, with one serving as an untreated control and the second treated with HyPer-3D, and both subsequently processed for the arterial bed (SMA) by using the EZ Clear method. Light sheet microscopy showed robustly enhanced signal clarity in the HyPer-3D-treated tissue compared with the control. Again, corresponding control tissues lacked discernible smaller vascular branches (Figure S3G, mid optical slice; H and I, high magnification views). HyPer-3D-treated tissues exhibited an 18× greater SBR than the control (Figure S4J; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001). These findings were corroborated with additional 3D imaging methods, using the tough fibrous heart as a representative tissue (Figure S5). HyPer-3D-treated tissue exhibited robust IF staining for coronary arteries (SMA), providing 57% greater SBRs using CUBIC (Figures S5A–S5C, M), 94% greater SBRs using iDISCO+ (Figures S5D–S5F, M), 133% greater SBRs using ScaleS (Figures S5G–S5I, M), and 70% greater SBRs using PACT (Figures S5J–S5L, M; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001; see also Table S2 in supplemental information for corresponding quantitative values and statistics).

To date, 3D imaging protocols have, at best, retained varying levels of endogenous fluorescent reporter protein signals. We discovered that HyPer-3D increases the detection of transgenic fluorescent reporters (Figure 3). HyPer-3D was used to treat heart tissues expressing RFP (578–633 nm emission) in the coronary vasculature, as well as kidney and testis tissues expressing the green Venus reporter protein (500–550 nm emission) in interstitial and spermatogonial stem cells, respectively.24 In all cases, treatment with HyPer-3D increased reporter protein detection. In the heart tissue, prior to treatment, both RFP+ large vessels and microvasculature could be detected, but the demarcation of these structures from the background was difficult, especially for the smaller vessels (Figures 3A, 3C, and 3E are high-magnification images). Imaging of the exact same tissues after HyPer-3D treatment revealed markedly enhanced signal clarity, corresponding to a 64% increase in the SBR, which was evident by the now well-defined boundaries of both large vessels and microvasculature, and an abrogated background noise (Figure 3B, low magnification; 3D and 3F, high-magnification views; 3G, RFP SBR levels, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001). Analyses of kidney tissues expressing Venus corroborated our findings in the heart tissue. Here, the Venus signal exhibited by the circular interstitial cells could be directly compared with the distinct AF exhibited by the renal tubules that these cells surround. Imaging of the exact same tissue, in the control state prior to treatment (Figures 3H and 3J, pretreatment) and after HyPer-3D treatment (Figures 3I and 3K), showed that HyPer-3D nearly abolished renal tubular AF (see asterisks in 3J vs. 3K), while the Venus reporter signal remained unchanged. HyPer-3D was next used to facilitate detection from Spry4H2B−Venus mice of Venus+ (492–550 nm emission) interstitial and spermatogonial stem cells in whole testis, an organ that also exhibits marked tissue AF25 (Figures 3L–3Q). Testes were bisected, and the halves were either treated with HyPer-3D or left untreated (Figure 3L). Stereo fluorescence microscopy showed that Venus+ cells were more visible in HyPer-3D-treated tissues than in control tissues (Figures 3M and 3N). These findings were corroborated by high-magnification imaging of Venus+ cells (Figures 3O and 3P). In control tissues, bright Venus+ cells were distinguishable, but cells with low Venus+ signal were difficult to distinguish from the background tissue AF (arrowheads in Figure 3O). Conversely, background tissue AF was negligible in HyPer-3D-treated tissue, which readily facilitated the distinct visualization of cells exhibiting lower Venus+ signal (arrowheads in Figure 3P). HyPer-3D-treated testis tissues exhibited Venus+ SBRs that were 5.8× higher than those of control (Figure 3Q, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).

Implementation of HyPer-3D for complex imaging studies

To evaluate multiplex interrogation of challenging organ types, we first assayed the kidney tissues. Specifically, we chose to image the renal nephrons, the most autofluorescent segment of the kidney exhibiting the highest AF among other tissues. To perform direct comparisons, individual unperfused adult murine kidneys were cut at 0.5 mm thickness, with adjacent sections left untreated or treated with HyPer-3D. IF was performed on both sections in the same receptacle for E-cadherin (ECAD) to mark the nephrogenic renal tubules, and both were cleared with formamide/glycerol (Figures 4A–4I). Renal tubules were robustly detected in HyPer-3D-treated kidney sections in contrast to control adjacent sections (Figures 4A and 4C). HyPer-3D-treated tissues exhibited SBRs that were 97% greater than those of control (Figure 4I, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001). These data were corroborated using artificial intelligence (AI)-based autosegmentation algorithms, which readily detected and segmented the renal tubules in HyPer-3D-treated tissues (Figure 4D) but not in control (Figure 4B). High-magnification analyses validated the increase in signal discernibility, with HyPer-3D enabling visualization of separate, distinct points in adjacent renal tubules, as well as the luminal area of individual tubules (Figures 4G and 4H). We also discovered that enhanced SBRs facilitated by HyPer-3D enabled the use of up to 10-fold lower antibody concentrations for IF. Adjacent 0.5 mm thick kidney sections were treated with or without HyPer-3D and then immunolabeled for ECAD, using standard, 4- or 10--fold lower antibody concentrations (Figures 4J–4O). HyPer-3D-treated tissues continued to exhibit high SBRs at all concentrations, whereas IF signal was diminished at 1/4 antibody concentrations and below in control tissues (Figures 4L and 4M). HyPer-3D-treated tissues exhibited robust tubule signals, even at 10-fold lower concentrations, whereas control tissues lacked detectable signals at this concentration (Figures 4N and 4O). These data were corroborated in the heart and testis tissues, where up to 10-fold lower antibody concentration could be used to visualize the coronary arteries and spermatogonial tubules, respectively (Figure S6).

We also tested if HyPer-3D could facilitate multiplex imaging of glomerular tissue, which is challenging not only due to its high AF but also due to the high tissue preservation required to maintain its delicate architecture.6,26,27 As shown in Figures 4P–4S and Video S7 (3D view), the endothelium (Figure 4Q, endomucin, green), mesangium (Figure 4R, platelet-derived growth factor receptor β [PDGFR-β], blue), and podocytes (Figure 4S, podocalyxin, red) of the murine glomerulus could be readily distinguished in HyPer-3D-treated and formamide-glycerol-cleared tissues.

To further substantiate these findings, we performed multiplex 3D imaging of HyPer-3D-treated and formamide-glycerol-cleared hearts (Figures 6F–6H). 3D confocal microscopy imaging of whole atria treated with HyPer-3D and immunolabeled with HCN4 and troponin-T to mark the myocardium facilitated the localization of a robust HCN4+ SAN tissue at the terminal crest (Figure 6F; inset HCN4+ SAN alone). Thus, we demonstrate that HyPer-3D facilitates high-fidelity imaging of the heart, including specialized elements of the conduction system.

HyPer-3D was found to be beneficial for imaging studies across experimental model systems, including those using transgenic reporters (Figure S8). Embryoid bodies (EBs) derived from murine stem cells with a doxycycline-inducible GFP reporter (492–555 nm emission) were treated with HyPer-3D and formamide-glycerol cleared after immunostaining for ECAD (red) to label all cells. GFP was readily detected in the doxycycline-induced EBs (Figure S8A) but was lacking in control EBs without induction (Figure S8A, inset). GFP was also robustly imaged in the ventricles of adult zebrafish hearts expressing GFP from a cardiomyocyte-specific transgenic promoter (Figures S8B–S8D).39 Treatment with HyPer-3D mitigated the AF in the zebrafish trunk, while preserving GFP expression in the heart (Figures S8B and S8C). GFP expression was visualized with high SBRs (SBR before treatment = 14.84; after HyPer-3D treatment = 28.71), with GFP robustly detected in the ventricular myocardium but not in the non-contracting bulbus arteriosus of formamide-glycerol-cleared hearts (Figure S8D, inset, DAPI).39 We used HyPer-3D to perform multiplex imaging of Venus reporter protein (492–555 nm emission), in combination with IF for the renal vasculature (SMA, orange) of adult Spry4H2B−Venus transgenic mice. As shown in Figures S8E and S8F, Venus signal could be readily detected despite the high AF of the kidney. Interestingly, we discovered by IF that the renal artery (RA) contains a previously undocumented population of Venus+ interstitial cells (Figure S8F, arrows)—the cells known to play an important role in controlling vascular tone and compliance.40 Consistent with these findings, HyPer-3D facilitated robust imaging of coronary arteries expressing RFP (577–632 nm emission) in the heart (Figure S8G, tdTomato). Not only were major branches observable in Hyper-3D-treated and formamide-glycerol-cleared hearts, but smaller vessels sprouting from main branches were also noted (Figure S8H). Thus, we show the versatile use of HyPer-3D in organoid, zebrafish, and murine models.

The preservation of in vivo dyes was next assayed by performing intravital dye labeling of blood conducting vessels in neonatal murine pups40,41 (Figure S9), when complex vascular remodeling was occurring, notably in the eye. Half-mounted heads were isolated, treated with HyPer-3D, formamide-glycerol cleared, and imaged by confocal microscopy. The results showed a robust preservation of fluorescently labeled cranial and facial vessels throughout the head (Figure S9A), and high-magnification analyses provided a detailed 3D view of the intricate vascular bed perfusing the neonatal eye cup and retina (Figure S9B and Video S9). HyPer-3D was also proven to provide a sophisticated modality to characterize an organ’s cellular structures when IF was combined with intravital dye labeling. This is shown in Figures S9C and S9D, where neonatal torsos were treated with HyPer-3D and formamide-glycerol cleared after immunolabeling for the vasculature post intravital dye labeling. The intravital dye labeling (red) was readily distinguishable from the immunolabeled vasculature (green, endomucin) and, at high magnification, enabled the visualization of mature vessels that conduct blood (yellow) versus immature vessels (green only) that are not yet perfused (Figure S9D and Video S10).

Optimization of HyPer-3D for use in human samples

Thus, we sought to implement the use of HyPer-3D to address this long-standing and well-recognized technical challenge. As shown in Figures 7E–7H, green AF (415–455 nm) as compared with red AF (577–632 nm) proved much more difficult to quench. This limited the efficacy of HyPer-3D for multiplex imaging using the green field in human tissues. Thus, we continued methodology development to address this shortcoming. We hypothesized that we could combine HyPer-3D with light-based AF quenching. A single murine kidney was bisected and placed in HyPer-3D solution, with one-half additionally placed under blue light, and the second kept in the same plate, but in the dark and out of view of the blue light (Figure 7I). Green AF of the mouse kidney was abolished in a single hour with HyPer-3D + blue light (Figure 7J, HyP + BL), which was not observed in control kidneys not exposed to blue light (Figure 7J, HyP) or those treated with blue light alone (Figure S10). Based on these findings, we tested HyPer-3D + BL on human kidney tissues. Strikingly, green AF was abolished in just 4 h (Figures 7K and 7L).

Thus, SHANEL was performed using the green light field. A single 1 mm thick human kidney section was bisected, with one-half left untreated as a control and the second-half treated with HyPer-3D + BL. Both samples were then placed in the same receptacle and processed using SHANEL for the renal arterial bed (Figures 7M–7Q, SMA, green). 3D imaging showed a marked improvement in contrast-driven signal discernability in the HyPer-3D + BL samples (Figure 7M). Smaller vessels surrounding the tubular networks were hard to differentiate from the nephrogenic tissue noise without BL treatment (Figure 7M). In contrast, HyPer-3D + BL-treated tissues exhibited almost no tissue noise, and smaller vessels within the nephrogenic zone were readily visible. We performed high-magnification imaging of the vasculature in the renal cortex of HyPer-3D + BL and control tissues. HyPer-3D + BL tissue exhibited close to no AF in nephrogenic tissue (Figures 7N–7O). In contrast, control tissue exhibited the robust, characteristic nephrogenic tissue noise known of the kidney, which limited the differentiation between noise and small caliber vessels (Figures 7P and 7Q). Indeed, HyPer-3D + BL-treated tissue exhibited SBRs that were 5× greater than those of control tissues (Figure 7R). Finally, we used HyPer-3D for multiplex imaging of human tissues. We aimed to determine if the different tissue layers of the human glomerulus could be visualized (Figures 7S–7W). Human samples were stained for podocalycin, PDGFR-β, and CD31 to mark the podocyte, mesangium, and endothelial layers of the glomerulus, respectively. All three tissue layers were distinctly imaged, with single-cell resolution (inset in Figures 7S–7W shows high magnification of the bracketed region). Thus, we demonstrate the use of HyPer-3D for complex imaging studies of both basic and clinical samples (see Figure S11 for the schematic of HyPer-3D workflow in basic and clinical samples).

Discussion

HyPer-3D provides numerous advantageous properties including enhanced imaging clarity, with an up to 30× increase in SBRs obtained with leading 3D imaging methods; up to 5× increase in fluorescent reporter protein signal detection; compatibility with up to 10× lower antibody concentrations; and a significant augmentation in the speed and efficacy of refractive index-matching solutions to render tissues transparent. These properties made the implementation of HyPer-3D ideal for performing imaging studies of challenging tissue types such as the kidney, testis, and heart that exhibit robust AF. Moreover, HyPer-3D was further developed for use in human tissues with higher AF and complex matrix structure. HyPer-3D + BL significantly enhanced the efficacy of quenching and facilitated multiplex imaging of human tissues. Cumulatively, our findings establish HyPer-3D as a powerful tool to perform complex 3D imaging studies of preclinical and clinical samples.

Numerous 3D imaging methods include alcohol dehydration and H2O2 treatment for bleaching and quenching AF.15,35,43,44,45 However, AF reduction is modest and the robust oxygenation observed when treating with H2O2 results in tissue damage and increased AF at damage sites.19,46,47 This led to our search for an alternative AF quenching methodology. Our initial protocol development involved screening for putative inhibitors of oxygenation observed when treating with H2O2 in PBS. NaN3 was shown to moderate oxygenation without completely blocking it, enabling tissue treatment to continue over time without causing damage. Moreover, the permeabilizing agent DMSO was found to facilitate the treatment of progressively thicker tissue sections and whole organs. We found that these core components of HyPer-3D, in a basic buffered solution with a low level of detergent, serve as a powerful tissue treatment technology that provides numerous benefits for 3D imaging studies. We performed numerous validations showing the capability of performing complex imaging studies with HyPer-3D, including in combination with a novel formamide-glycerol optical clearing formulation.

HyPer-3D was shown to significantly decrease AF in a time-dependent manner, allowing investigators to vary the treatment time to fit the AF of specific samples. This is particularly useful for studies interrogating wide-ranging samples with varied levels of AF. Importantly, HyPer-3D also significantly increased the SBRs obtained with leading 3D imaging methods including CUBIC L/R + (N), EZ Clear, iDISCO+, ScaleS, PACT, and SHANEL.

Live imaging showed near instantaneous optical clearing of 0.5 mm heart and kidney sections, using HyPer-3D in combination with EZ View, CUBIC-2, MACS-R2, and FOCM. By contrast, adjacent control tissue sections that lacked HyPer-3D treatment took much longer to begin clearing and never reached the same level of transparency. We corroborated a significant increase in clearing potency with all the published refractive index-matching solutions we tested (nine in total). HyPer-3D can, therefore, be integrated into the already established working protocols to both mitigate AF and enhance tissue transparency.

The highest increases in SBRs, up to 32×, were observed with whole adult murine organs, using light sheet microscopy that maximizes contrast through planar illumination. Moreover, HyPer-3D was found to be beneficial when using standard stereo microscopy, where we observed more modest SBR increases (e.g., from 2–4×) but demonstrated that it enabled high-throughput imaging and AI-based segmentation of challenging structures such as renal nephrons. Indeed, the increased SBRs with HyPer-3D allowed visualization of the architecture of adjacent connecting and collecting tubule nephron segments that exhibit the highest tissue AF; up to 4.5× increase in the detection of poorly recognized spermatogonial stem cell antigens; the discrete tissue layers of the renal glomerulus that exhibits an intricate architecture; and the anatomic location of the HCN4+ SAN pacemaker that was not readily discernible in control tissues.

HyPer-3D also facilitated increased transgenic fluorescent reporter protein detection. HyPer-3D significantly resolved the signal from RFP+ arteries in transgenic heart samples, with SBRs increasing by up to 64%. These findings were corroborated in transgenic kidneys showing that Venus+ interstitial cells exhibit 50% higher SBRs. In the testis, Venus+ interstitial cells that were not discernible from background tissues due to their low Venus expression became readily detectable using HyPer-3D, and SBRs were found to be 5.8× higher. These finding were consistent across model systems, as shown in 3D imaging of GFP-expressing stem cell-derived EBs and GFP-expressing zebrafish hearts. Moreover, HyPer-3D was shown to be compatible with fluorescent tracers. Using intravital dye labeling, we were able to visualize the complex 3D architecture of the ocular vasculature, as well as the nascent and patent vessels of the murine trunk.

In summary, we developed HyPer-3D coupled with tissue clearing for advanced 3D imaging. HyPer-3D provides increased imaging clarity with significantly greater SBRs, greater detection of poorly recognized antigens, augmented optical clearing, and enhanced detection of fluorescent reporters. These properties facilitate 3D interrogation of challenging tissue types, including human specimens, and thus make HyPer-3D a valuable new tool for performing complex multiplex imaging studies.

Limitations of the study

While HyPer-3D provides significant benefits, several practical considerations and limitations merit discussion. A main challenge is the persistence of green field AF that greatly limits the ability to perform multiplex imaging. Green AF is naturally higher and more difficult to quench than red AF. To address this challenge, we developed HyPer-3D + BL, which was shown to abolish green AF in human kidney samples in as little as 4 h and increased the SBRs obtained when imaging in the green field with SHANEL by 5×. Samples can first be treated with HyPer-3D to quench red AF, which occurs concurrently with the priming of tissue for optical clearing. Once red AF is abrogated, persistent green AF can be quenched with HyPer-3D + BL, thus making tissue amenable to multiplex imaging, as shown in our visualization of the discrete human glomerular tissue layers. However, how green and red AF differ in nature remains an outstanding question beyond the scope of this work. Additional research into this area would provide much needed insight that could lead to further protocol development.

As with all emerging technologies, user familiarity is a practical consideration. In the initial stage, it is critical to observe tissue to ensure that oxygenation is limited. To guide users, we provide representative, real-time examples of tissue treatment showing adequate vs. excessive oxygenation. Notably, if excessive oxygenation is observed, it can simply be stopped by washing samples directly with cold PBS. Once oxygenation has ceased, H2O2 levels can be lowered and the treatment be started again. This leads directly to a related practical consideration; user familiarity treating tissue types with which they specialize. The onset of oxygenation varies among different tissue types, as well as the organ source (different animal models or humans). To ameliorate this technical challenge, our study employed a wide range of sample types from various sources. We found that an initial concentration of 0.05% H2O2 in HyPer-3D was generally effective as a starting point and avoided damaging tissue types across the spectrum. From here, users can increase H2O2 concentration as the AF quenches and the tissue is adequately primed for optical clearing.

Stem cell-derived embryoid bodies

Mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) with a doxycycline inducible GFP transgene49 were maintained on gelatin coated tissue culture treated plates in serum-free conditions. Medium (2i plus LIF) was composed of 50% Neurobasal media (Gibco) and 50% DMEM/F12 (Corning cellgro) supplemented with 0.5X N2 and 0.5X B27 (Gibco), 100U/mL Penicillin, 0.1 mg/mL Streptomycin (Corning), 0.05% BSA (Gibco), 2 mM Glutamine (Corning), 0.15 mM monothioglycerol (Sigma), 1000U/ml mouse LIF (ESGRO, Sigma), 3 μM CHIR99021 (Stem Cell Technologies), and 1uM PD0325901 (Stem Cell Technologies). To produce embryoid bodies (EBs) mESCs were differentiated in serum free differentiation media (SFD). SFD consisted of 75% IMDM (Gibco), 25% Ham’s F12 (Corning cellgro), 0.5X N2 (Gibco), 0.5X B27 (Gibco), 0.05% BSA (Gibco), 0.5mM Ascorbic Acid (Sigma), 2mM Glutamine (Corning), 0.45 mM monothioglycerol (Sigma), 100U/mL Penicillin and 0.1 mg/mL Streptomycin (Corning). On the first day of differentiation, mESCs were dissociated with Accutase (Sigma) and plated at 40,000 cells/ml in 12.5mL of SFD in 100mm petri dishes. After 48 h, EBs were dissociated with Accutase and plated again at 80,000 cells/ml in SFD supplemented with 75ng/ml Activin A (R&D Systems). After 24 h, expression of GFP was induced by adding Doxycycline (Sigma) for 24 h before EB collection.

Method details

Experimental design

To limit confounding factors in comparisons of control tissues processed by standard published protocols and those first pre-treated with hyper-3D, we used adjacent sections cut from individual organs. Furthermore, control and Hyper-3D treated tissues were stained in the same receptacle, and then imaged side-by-side in the same field of view to eliminate imaging discrepancies. This experimental design allowed for the direct comparison of analogous immunostained structures in control and Hyper-3D treated tissues, such as extended vascular networks.

Organ fixation

Organs were immersion fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4OC using a rotisserie-style shaker. Fixation time was modulated according to tissue thickness, and whether organs were of soft tissue or muscular tissue type. For example, three week-old whole adult mouse hearts and kidneys were fixed for 72 h, mouse neonatal tissues and adult zebrafish hearts were fixed for 24 h, and stem cell-derived EBs were fixed for 3 h. After fixation, tissues were rinsed with copious amounts of PBS, washed at 4 OC for at least 48 h with PBS, and then stored in PBS until use. All steps of fixing and optical clearing protocol are done using rotisserie-style shaker to ensure proper penetration of solutions.

HyPer-3D

The base solution of HyPer-3D consists of the following final concentrations: 150mM KCl (w/v), 0.5% Triton X-100 (v/v), 20% DMSO (v/v), 0.5% NaN3 (w/v), 10 mM Tris-HCl (w/v), pH 9.5 made in basic double distilled H20 (pH 9 with NaOH; final pH of base solution is 9.5). Base solution is mixed well, and then allowed to settle for 10 min. Just before use, H2O2 is directly added to HyPer-3D base solution. To start, a final concentration of 0.05% H2O2 is used (diluted from 30% stock solution v/v, Sigma, H1009). HyPer-3D containing 0.05% H2O2 is mixed well, and allowed to acclimate at RT for 10 min. Organs are then completely submerged in HyPer-3D within a suitable receptacle, such as an Eppendorf tube or cryogenic vial, and gently rocked on a rotisserie shaker at RT. Tissues are observed to ensure that air pockets are not accumulating within the tissue; minor bubbling at the edges of tissues is permissible. In instances where robust oxygenation was observed, the reaction was stopped by rinsing tissue in cold PBS, and the reaction mixture was adjusted to proceed more slowly by lowering the concentration of H2O2 to 0.01%. Once oxygenation was confirmed to be lacking, samples were incubated in HyPer-3D at room temperature for up to 4 h. Tissues were then rinsed 2x and incubated with wash buffer comprised of 150 mM KCl, 10% DMSO, 0.5% Triton X-100, and 20 mM Tris HCl, pH 9.5 dissolved in basic DDH20 for 1 h at RT. Tissue decolorization and AF was then assessed. If further processing is needed, a new round of fresh HyPer-3D treatment was done by increasing the concentration of H2O2 by 0.05%. In this manner, HyPer-3D treatment can continue until desired results by increasing the concentration of H2O2. The maximum concentration of H2O2 required for our studies was 0.5% H2O2. In between HyPer-3D treatments, samples are left rocking in wash buffer at RT (for example overnight, so as to continue HyPer-3D treatment at higher H2O2 concentration on day 2). Finally, to address stubborn AF, we developed HyPer-3D + BL. HyPer-3D + BL treatment was performed on a SMZ1500 Nikon microscope at 0.75x magnification using a solid-state LED white light source at 100% intensity (3W Sola Light Engine, Lumencor) in conjunction with a filter cube containing a 470/40 nm excitation filter (450–490 nm), a 495 nm long pass dichroic beamsplitter, and a 525/50 nm emission filter (500–550 nm). In this case, tissues are placed in a plate containing HyPer-3D solution with 0.05% H2O2. The plate is then placed under blue light source, to quench green AF. For efficient quenching, tissue should be fully exposed to the blue light. Tissues are checked periodically at every half to one hour when treating with HyPer-3D + BL. In this case, for cumulative treatment, tissues are treated with HyPer-3D for quenching of red AF first, which coincided with sufficient treatment time for priming samples for augmented optical clearing. Once red AF is nearly abolished, treatment with HyPer-3D + BL is performed to further quench green AF.

Tissue clearing

After HyPer-3D treatment, samples were amenable to previously established optical clearing protocols, as referenced in the text. Alternatively, based on our empirical observations noting a mitigation of AF with formamide, we coupled Hyper-3D with a formamide-glycerol formulation for clearing. Optical clearing consisted of impregnating samples with sequential solutions of varying formamide/glycerol concentrations, with each solution rendering tissue increasingly more transparent (deionized formamide, Sigma, F7503; ultrapure glycerol, ThermoFisher, AC410985000). Specifically, tissues were immersed with the following sequence of solutions made in basic DDH20 at RT: 20% formamide +0.5% Triton X-100, 20% formamide, 20% formamide/5% glycerol, 20% formamide/10% glycerol, 20% formamide/25% glycerol, 20% formamide/50% glycerol, 30% formamide/70% glycerol, and then a final solution comprised of 17% formamide +5% Triethalomine +70% glycerol +3% Tris base (w/v, Tris, Promega, H5135) made in basic DDH20. At the onset, tissues are incubated with 20% formamide +0.5% Triton X-100 overnight at RT to prime the tissue for optical clearing. Traces of Triton X-100 are then removed by washing 3 times with 20% formamide 3x. The time of incubation in each subsequent solution varied depending on the tissue being cleared, because the viscosity of clearing solutions increases with rises in glycerol concentration. Thus, care is needed to ensure that the entire tissue has been impregnated with the current solution before moving forward to subsequent solutions. Penetration of clearing mixtures into samples could be ascertained by ensuring samples sank to the bottom of containers. For large organs, dabbing tissue clear of solutions before adding the subsequent mixture further facilitated optical clearing. For tissue sections, the number of solutions was reduced to enable faster clearing, as follows: treatment was started with 20% formamide +0.5% Triton X-100, then 20% formamide alone, followed by quarter percent increases of glycerol, starting with 20% formamide/25% glycerol. We observed that for 0.5 mm kidney sections, tissue was rendered transparent with 20% formamide/25% glycerol. After clearing, tissues were imaged directly in final clearing solution, using glass bottom plates with spacers, and then placing a coverslip over the sample to ensure stability. Samples can be stored in final clearing solution for months.

Confocal microscopy was performed on a Zeiss LSM 800 and stereo fluorescence microscopy on a Nikon SMZ1500 mounted with a Nikon DS-FI3 camera. Light Sheet microscopy was performed on an UltraMicroscope II (Miltenyi Biotec). 3D and AI analyses were performed using Imaris Imaging Software (Bitplane). For quantification of transparency, tissue clarity was quantified using the gradient magnitude of the background grid as a measure of how well the grid showed through the tissue. Gradient magnitude of the image was calculated in MATLAB using a Prewitt operator. Grid visibility ratios were computed by assaying horizontal edges in each image and dividing the gradient magnitude of the grid edge behind the tissue section by the gradient magnitude of the grid edge adjacent to the tissue. A ratio of one indicates perfect transparency and a ratio of zero indicates complete opacity.

Immunofluorescence

Fixed tissues were rinsed 3 × 5 min with PBS +0.5% Triton X-100 (PBST), and then permeabilized in blocking solution consisting of PBST +1% NDS +1% DMSO overnight at 37OC. Primary antibodies were then incubated in PBST +1% DMSO. Antibodies used in this study were: anti-α-smooth muscle actin (F3777 clone 1A4, Sigma, for titration studies standard concentration of 1:200 of 1mg/ml stock concentration; for comparative studies of 3D imaging protocols, at 1:1000), anti-ENAC (Ergonul et al., at 1:200),48 anti-PARVALBUMIN (R&D AF5058, at 1:200), anti-ENDOMUCIN (ab106100, Abcam, at 1:200), anti-PODOCALYXIN (3169, R&D Systems, at 1:200), anti-HCN4 (APC-052, Alomone, at 1:500), anti-PDGFR-β (3169, Cell Signaling Technology, at 1:100), anti-CD31 (BD Biosciences, 550389 at 1/500), anti-E-CADHERIN (AF748, R&D, for titration studies standard concentration of 1:200 of .2mg/ml stock), anti-MCAM (134702, BioLegend, for titration studies standard concentration of 1:500 of .5mg/ml stock), and anti-cardiac TROPONIN-T (NB110–2546, R&D Systems, at 1:500), anti-GFRα1 (AF560, R&D Systems at 1:200), anti-STRA8 (Ab49405, Abcam at 1:200), and anti-cKIT (AF1356, R&D Systems at 1:200). Minimally cross-absorbed Alexa Flour secondary antibodies were used to detect primary antibodies.

Image analyses

SBRs were calculated as has been reported,50,51,52,53,54,55 by detecting the mean signal intensity in an ROI within the stained structure, and dividing this by the averaged mean signal intensity obtained from three adjacent background regions using the same ROI (ImageJ ROI function). To disentangle the effect of HyPer-3D on detection of poorly recognized antigens, we also separated raw signal intensity and noise levels by segmenting stained structures from background using the Imaris Surface Creation Module (v. 10.2, Oxford Instruments), with thresholding for size and intensity (via background subtraction), as we and others have previously described.25,41,56,57 AI machine learning segmentation of renal nephrogenic tubules was also performed in Imaris using the AI Trainable Segmentation XTension in the Surfaces Rendering Module as has been described.58,59,60 Images of adjacent ECAD-stained HyPer-3D treated and control untreated kidney sections, cut from the same kidney, were used for segmentation. Foreground and background regions were manually annotated to train the AI classifier, after which the model was applied to the full dataset to generate surface objects representative of nephrons. For assessment of tissue transparency, optical clearing capacity was quantified as per previous reports,13,61,62,63 by assessing the grid visibility through cleared organs. Tissue clarity was quantified using the gradient magnitude of the background grid as a measure of how well the grid showed through the tissue. Gradient magnitude of the image was calculated in MATLAB using a Prewitt operator. Grid visibility ratios were computed by assaying horizontal edges in each image and dividing the gradient magnitude of the grid edge behind the tissue section by the gradient magnitude of the grid edge adjacent to the tissue. A ratio of one indicates perfect transparency and a ratio of zero indicates complete opacity.

Intravital dye labeling

Intravital dye labeling was performed as previously described.41 Briefly, 100 μL of fluorescently tagged tomato lectin (0.2mg/ml in PBS, Vector laboratories) was injected into the circulation of anesthetized newborn pups (postnatal day zero) by intracardiac injection. Pups were euthanized after 5 min to let the lectin circulate, and tissues harvested and fixed.

Microscopy

Stereo fluorescence microscopy was performed on a Nikon SMZ1500 microscope equipped with a 1x HR Plan Apo objective (Nikon, NA, 0.029; Working Distance (WD), 53 mm). For imaging of green light autofluorescence and fluorophores, samples were illuminated using a Sola white-light LED (Sola) in conjunction with filter cube containing a 470/40 nm excitation filter (450–490 nm), a 495 nm long pass dichroic beamsplitter, and a 525/50 nm emission filter (500–550 nm). Images were acquired using a Nikon DS-FI3 camera at a resolution of 2880 × 2048 pixels using the NIS Elements software (Nikon). For imaging of red light AF and fluorophores, a filter cube containing a 560/40 nm excitation filter (540–580 nm), a 585 nm long pass dichroic beam splitter, and a 605/55 nm emission filter (578–633 nm) was used. Confocal imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 800 laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a gallium arsenide phosphide (GaAsP) detector, four laser excitation lines (405 nm, 488 nm, 561 nm, and 640 nm), and a 5× EC Plan-Neofluar objective (Zeiss, NA 0.16; WD 18.5 mm), a 10×/0.3 EC Plan-Neofluar objective (Zeiss, NA 0.3; WD 5.2 mm), and a 20×/0.75 Plan-Apochromat objective (Zeiss, NA 0.75; WD 0.6 mm). Fluorescence detection windows were configured as follows: blue fluorescence was excited using the 405 nm laser and detected from 400 to 490 nm; green fluorescence (e.g., green AF) was excited using the 488 nm laser and detected from 492 to 555 nm; red fluorescence (e.g., red AF) was excited using the 561 nm laser and detected from 578 to 620 nm; and far-red fluorescence was excited using the 640 nm laser and detected from 630 to 700 nm. For all channels, images were acquired at a 1 Airy unit pinhole diameter with z-step intervals matched to the optical section thickness for each objective, with 4× averaging, using a 1024 × 1024-pixel frame size using the ZEN acquisition software (Zeiss). Light-sheet imaging was performed using a Miltenyi Ultramicroscope II light sheet microscope equipped with four laser excitation lines (405, 488, 561, and 640 nm) and a 4.2Mpx low-noise sCMOS camera (Miltenyi). Samples were imaged using the M1 Plan 1.1× DC57 objective (NA 0.03; WD, 17 mm). For imaging of red fluorescence, samples were excited using the 561 nm laser and emission was collected from 575 to 615 nm using a 595/40 nm bandpass filter. Dual-side illumination was used to ensure uniform excitation across the sample volume, with z-steps intervals of 0.5x the axial light-sheet thickness, at a 2048 × 2048-pixel frame size using the built-in ImSpector acquisition software (Miltenyi).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.6.1 (GraphPad Software). As described above, to minimize potential confounding factors, comparisons between control and HyPer-3D–treated tissues were conducted using adjacent sections derived from the same individual organ, which were processed in parallel for IF and imaged side-by-side. Comparisons between these groups were evaluated using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test, with Prism used to calculate p values, differences between group means, and 95% confidence intervals. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with n values and p values reported for each comparison. For analyses comparing the same exact tissue before and after HyPer-3D treatment—such as assessments of increased fluorescent reporter signal—statistical significance was determined similarly but with a paired, two-tailed Student’s t test.
