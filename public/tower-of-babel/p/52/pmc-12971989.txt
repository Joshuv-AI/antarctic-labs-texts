A machine vision dataset for automated quality inspection and grading of sweetpotatoes
Authors: Jiaming Zhang, Yuzhen Lu, Jiajun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSweetpotatoes grading, particularly for defects, is a labor-intensive operation and often constrained by inconsistencies in manual inspection. To facilitate the development of automated quality grading systems, this article presents a unique, unified machine vision dataset specifically for multi-view quality inspection and grading of sweetpotatoes. The dataset consolidates data derived from two independent experimental campaigns to enhance diversity in sample characteristics and imaging conditions. It comprises the data of 390 samples divided into two subsets; Subset A consists of 123 fresh-market sweetpotatoes sourced from grocery stores and imaged under ambient indoor lighting at a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, and Subset B includes 267 sweetpotatoes of two varieties harvested from a research station and imaged in an enclosed chamber under controlled illumination at a resolution of 1280 × 720 pixels. In both subsets, sweetpotato samples were rotated on a custom-designed roller conveyor for multi-view imaging, and RGB (red-green-blue) frames were extracted from recorded video streams and labeled for individual sweetpotato instances. The curated dataset contains a total of 1400 images (Subset A: 232, Subset B: 1168) with 3700 annotated instances, along with 39 raw video recordings as well as physical measurements. Each instance is labeled with a polygon segmentation mask and assigned a quality grade (Grade 1, Grade 2, or Grade 3) based on surface defect severity. This dataset represents the first publicly available machine vision dataset dedicated to automated sweetpotato grading. It provides a diverse and valuable resource for training and evaluating computer vision algorithms and models for instance segmentation and surface-quality assessment of sweetpotatoes and beyond.Keywords: Computer vision, Grading, Online inspection, Postharvest automation, Sweetpotato

Background

Sweetpotato grading is a critical postharvest operation that affects market value, packing decisions, and downstream supply-chain efficiency [3]. The quality of marketable sweetpotato storage roots is evaluated based on size, shape, and defect characteristics [4]. Defect inspection is still performed manually, with workers visually inspecting roots and removing defective products by hand [5]. This process is labor-intensive and prone to inconsistency and variability arising from human subjectivity and other factors (e.g., vision acuity, fatigue, stress, and operational conditions). To reduce labor dependence and costs while improving quality consistency and packing efficiency, there is a strong need for automated inspection solutions. However, developing an automated system that meets practical expectations remains challenging, particularly because sweetpotatoes exhibit substantial shape irregularities and variability in localized surface defects [[6], [7]], necessitating effective full-surface inspection.

Machine-vision systems that rely on single-view imagery are limited or ineffective for comprehensive defect detection of agricultural products [8]. In contrast, roller-conveyor systems that rotate samples during imaging provide an effective approach for increasing surface coverage by presenting multiple views of samples to the camera. Recently, a roller conveyor-based machine vision system was developed for automated sweetpotato grading and sorting based on multi-view quality inspection [1,9,10]. Multi-view imagery was collected and used for developing and evaluating deep learning-based algorithm pipelines for sweetpotato instance segmentation, tracking, and grading.

This dataset described here complements these prior studies [1,9,10] by consolidating curated image frames and instance-level annotations from two independent experiments and making the compiled dataset publicly accessible. The primary objective for releasing this dataset is to facilitate algorithm development, benchmarking, and reproducibility for automated, multi-view-based grading of sweetpotatoes and related agricultural products. In addition, the dataset that comprises two distinct subsets acquired under different conditions also enables investigation of cross-dataset generalization and domain shift, as well as the exploration of methods that leverage unlabeled video data for enhanced grading performance.

Data Description

Dataset overview

The dataset described here consists primarily of 1400 RGB images with corresponding annotation files, comprising a total of 3700 annotated sweetpotato instances, along with 39 raw video sequences. The data are organized into two distinct subsets derived from two independent studies conducted on sweetpotato samples sourced from different locations and captured under different imaging conditions [1,10]. Label definition

Sweetpotato instances were labeled into three grades based on the visual inspection of defect severity across the entire dataset, which are Grade 1 [Normal (defect-free or negligible surface defects)], Grade 2 (Moderate defects, such as minor skinning, shallow cuts, or localized discoloration), and Grade 3 (Severe defects, including deep cuts, severe skinning, and decay). Fig. 3 shows example labelled instances from the two subsets A and B. It should be noted that these labels reflect the localized surface quality captured in specific views rather than the overall grade of the entire root. Although the USDA quality standards [4] were used as a reference for defect assessment, other grading criteria, such as size (length and width) and shape, were not incorporated into the instance-level labeling scheme.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Sample collection

This dataset comprises a total of 390 sweetpotatoes storage roots collected from two distinct sources to ensure diversity in sample origin and quality characteristics. Subset A includes 123 fresh-market sweetpotatoes purchased from local grocery stores, originally produced and packed in Mississippi. These samples had undergone commercial packing operations and therefore reflect variability in shape and surface appearance typical of retail products. To mitigate this limitation and capture the natural biological variability of sweetpotatoes prior to any commercial grading/sorting, Subset B consists of 267 samples of ‘Bayou Belle’ and ‘Orleans’ varieties harvested and shipped directly from the Louisiana State University (LSU) AgCenter Sweet Potato Research Station (Chase, LA, USA). These samples represent quality variations of freshly harvested roots prior to commercial postharvest packing. The two subsets of samples serve complementary purposes, and their combination captures a broad spectrum of sweetpotato quality conditions.

Before imaging, all the sweetpotato samples were cleaned and individually labeled. Trained personnel manually evaluated each sample for surface defects and recorded size measurements (length and width) [1,10].

Machine vision system and image acquisition

A prototype machine vision system designed for online inspection and grading of sweetpotatoes was used for image acquisition. The system primarily consists of a custom-designed roller conveyor driven by a gear motor with a variable speed controller, an overhead camera mounted above the conveyor, and an onboard computer with associated peripherals. The roller conveyor employs a chain–rack–gear-driven mechanism that simultaneously translates and rotates the sweetpotatoes as they are transported. This synchronized motion is essential for multi-view inspection, as it progressively exposes the entire surface of each root over time, thereby minimizing the likelihood of undetected defects. A detailed description of the mechanical design and construction of the conveyor is given in [9]. A consumer-grade RGB-D (depth) camera (RealSense™ L515, Intel Corp., Santa Clara, CA, USA) was mounted approximately 210 mm above the conveyor. Although the camera supports depth imaging, only the RGB stream was utilized for this dataset to focus on surface defect detection.

As shown in Fig. 4, the two configurations of the system operated without and with an enclosed chamber were employed for sweetpotato imaging [1,10], reflecting both ambient and controlled lighting environments, corresponding to the resultant Subsets A and B, respectively. For Subset A, the vision system operated without the chamber enclosure under ambient indoor lighting (Fig. 4 left). Images were captured at a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, resulting in natural variability in background, shadows, and overall brightness. For Subset B, as shown in Fig. 4 (right), imaging was conducted within an enclosed chamber measuring 457 mm × 660 mm × 762 mm (length × width × height). Uniform illumination over the scene was provided by two symmetrically mounted 8-watt white LED (light-emitting diode) strips, providing a stable background with minimal lighting variations. Images in Subset B were captured at a resolution of 1280 × 720 pixels.

Photographs of the roller-conveyor-based machine vision system without (left) and with (right) an enclosed chamber for sweetpotato image acquisition.

The camera control and automated image acquisition were implemented via a custom software interface developed using the Intel RealSense™ SDK 2.0,3 ensuring consistent frame rates and synchronized data storage. Video streams of sweetpotato traveling on the conveyor were recorded in AVI format at 30 frames per second (fps) with a fixed exposure time of 3 ms to minimize motion blur. The conveyor speed was maintained at approximately 5 cm s−1, providing an effective balance between stable transport, sufficient sample rotation, and image clarity. During experimentation, the imaging process followed a standardized protocol. Prior to recording, sweetpotato samples were manually loaded in batches onto the roller conveyor at a location outside the camera’s field of view (FOV). Each sample was placed individually between adjacent rollers with its longitudinal axes oriented perpendicular to the conveyor’s direction of motion. Video recording was initiated manually before the samples entered the FOV. As the conveyor advanced, each batch of sweetpotatoes traversed the entire imaging region, where multiple surface views of each sample were captured by the overhead camera as a result of synchronized sample translation and rotation. The recording was terminated once all samples in the batch had completely exited the FOV. A total of 39 video sequences were acquired, with each video corresponding to one batch, comprising 19 videos in Subset A and 20 videos in Subset B.

Dataset curation

To construct a dataset suitable for algorithm development and evaluation, discrete RGB frames were extracted from recorded video streams using a fixed temporal sampling strategy. For Subset A, a batch-level sampling approach was initially applied, extracting approximately 15 representative frames per video at evenly spaced time points. For Subset B, an instance-oriented sampling strategy was utilized; given the conveyor speed of 5 cm s⁻¹ and the camera’s FOV, each individual sweetpotato typically appeared in approximately 15 frames as it moved and rotated through the imaging area. Following the initial extraction, a rigorous manual screening process was conducted to exclude frames that did not contain sweetpotato instances or exhibited severe motion blur and redundancy. This manual curation resulted in a final set of 232 images for Subset A and 1168 images for Subset B, ensuring that every image included in the dataset provides high-quality, relevant views for machine learning tasks.

Ground truth annotations were generated at the instance level for extracted frames. Images in Subset A were manually annotated using LabelMe, whereas Subset B was annotated using AnyLabeling with assisted labeling functionality. To improve annotation efficiency while maintaining high precision, initial polygon proposals were generated using a Segment Anything Model [11] and subsequently refined through rigorous manual correction to ensure boundary delineation. A consistent annotation protocol was applied across both subsets: polygon masks were drawn tightly around the samples, and any occluded regions were excluded to avoid semantic ambiguity. Each sweetpotato instance was assigned a categorical quality label (Grade 1, Grade 2, or Grade 3) based on the visual assessment of the defect severity of the instance in a given frame. Although different annotation tools were used, the output annotations were standardized into a unified JSON format containing consistent polygon coordinates and class metadata. Technical validation

The reliability of the dataset was ensured through rigorous quality control implemented at both the acquisition and annotation stages. During image acquisition, consistency was maintained by fixing the camera mounting height and conveyor speed within each subset. For Subset B, the use of an enclosed chamber in Subset A effectively eliminated external light interference, providing uniform and stable illumination conditions. After acquisition, all raw video streams underwent manual review, and frames exhibiting motion blur, severe occlusion, or redundancy were excluded. This screening process ensures that the final set of 1400 annotated images provides clear, representative, and non-redundant views suitable for algorithm development and evaluation for sweetpotato grading.

Recommended usage

This dataset is primarily recommended for, but not limited to, a diversity of supervised computer vision and machine learning tasks: instance segmentation, frame-level quality grading, multi-view-based sample-level quality grading, multi-object tracking (MoT), and cross-dataset (Subset A <=> Subset B) generalization. It is also useful to develop and evaluate semi-supervised and unsupervised learning-based methods for enhanced sweetpotato grading models by leveraging abundant unlabeled frames in original video recordings.

To facilitate reproducible research and benchmarking, adherence to specific dataset splitting protocols is critical. The dataset contains sequential multi-view images grouped by batch identifiers (IDs), where each batch corresponds to a single video capturing multiple sweetpotato samples. The image annotation metadata does not support direct linkage between individual instances to specific physical sample IDs; such linkage is only available at the batch (video) level. Hence, random splitting of the dataset at the individual image or frame level is strongly discouraged, as it would lead to severe data leakage—different views of the same physical samples (or samples from the same batch) could appear simultaneously in training and testing partitions. Instead, users partition the dataset at the batch level by following predefined file naming prefixes, ensuring that all images derived from the same batch of physical samples remain within the same data split. In our experimental practice, Batches 1–13 from Subset A were used for training, with Batches 14–19 for testing [10]; similarly, Batches 1–16 from Subset B were used for training, while Batches 17–20 were reserved for testing [1]. To map between instance annotations and individual physical Sample IDs, a video-assisted tracking approach can be employed by applying MoT algorithms as done in our previous work [[1], [10]] to the continuous frame streams. In this way, users can generate stable tracking IDs for each sweetpotato within a batch, and subsequently cross-reference the visual traits of these tracked instances (e.g., estimated size and specific surface defect patterns) with the detailed manual descriptions provided in the supplementary physical measurement datasheet, thereby achieving physical sample ID mapping.

Furthermore, to help users contextualize the difficulty of the dataset and provide a concrete starting point for algorithm development, it is important to point out that, in our two related studies [1,10], YOLOv8-based pipelines with a tracking algorithm were developed and evaluated for sweetpotato instance segmentation and multi-view-based quality grading. These studies provide detailed quantitative benchmarks that effectively serve as baseline references for future researchers.

Limitations

Although the dataset provides meaningful diversity across two separate imaging experiments [1,10], its overall scale (1400 labeled images and 39 videos) remains moderate compared with large-scale general-purpose computer vision corpora. Consequently, training large-capacity models may require strong regularization or data augmentation. To maximize the dataset's utility and prevent overfitting, users are highly recommended to employ specific augmentation strategies proven effective in our baseline evaluations [10], such as random geometric transformations (rotation, horizontal/vertical flipping), mosaic augmentation, and hue-saturation-value color space shifting. These techniques would effectively expand the training sample distribution and enhance model robustness. Samples in Subset A were sourced from grocery stores and had undergone packinghouse sorting, which can reduce the prevalence of certain defects that may occur prior to postharvest packing. The dataset is structured primarily to support frame-level vision tasks (segmentation and detection). Although the roller conveyor enables full-surface exposure of samples, the current metadata organization does not provide deterministic instance-to-sample ID mapping for tracking specific sweetpotato roots across frames. It should be noted that the dataset was deliberately collected at a relatively low conveyor speed (5 cm/s). While prior experiments on sweetpotato grading and sorting performance evaluation have demonstrated capabilities at higher speeds (e.g., 12 cm/s) [[1], [12]], the speed of 5 cm/s was intentionally selected for the dataset curation to ensure high-fidelity ground truth annotations and minimize image artifacts due to increased motion, mechanical vibration, and rotation variability. Further validations at higher conveyor speeds typical of representative commercial packing lines may be needed to fully assess grading performance under industrial throughput conditions. Users should take these considerations into account when developing models and evaluation protocols, particularly for deployment-oriented research.
