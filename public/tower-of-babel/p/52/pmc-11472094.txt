Adult-onset Williams-Campbell syndrome: Clinical presentations, diagnostic challenges, and treatment approaches
Authors: Leonardo Cano-Cevallos, Arturo Cortes-Telles, Juan C Calderon, Karla Robles-Velasco, Nelson Rivadeneyra, Ivan Cherrez-Ojeda
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWilliams-Campbell syndrome (WCS), traditionally recognized in childhood, is a rare congenital disorder characterized by subsegmental bronchial cartilage deficiency, leading to bronchiectasis. However, its occurrence in adults presents unique diagnostic complexities. We present two cases: Case 1, a 53-year-old male with recurrent rhinosinusitis and COVID-19-associated lung cysts; Case 2, a 59-year-old cyclist with pulmonary bullae. Diagnostic evaluations included pulmonary function tests and imaging studies. Both cases underwent extensive diagnostic evaluations before WCS diagnosis. Management was focused on symptom alleviation and pneumococcal vaccination. Adult-onset WCS poses diagnostic challenges, often mimicking other respiratory conditions. Pathology confirmation is a gold standard for definitive diagnosis; however, in the case of WCS, the literature supports a diagnostic approach primarily based on clinical and radiological findings. Early recognition and tailored management strategies are essential to enhance patient outcomes.Keywords: Williams-Campbell syndrome, Bronchiectasis, Lung disease

Introduction

Williams-Campbell syndrome (WCS) is a rare disorder characterized by a complete or partial cartilage deficiency in the subsegmental bronchi. The exact pathophysiology is still unknown. However, this disorder is characterized by a lack of cartilage in the smaller airways, causing them to collapse and resulting in bronchiectasis, characterized by permanent dilation of bronchi and bronchioles, commonly affecting bronchial divisions ranging from the fourth to the sixth order, leading to the development of bronchiectasis in the affected bronchi [1]. Due to its congenital nature, this syndrome is generally identified during childhood, nevertheless, some authors suggest the possibility of it being acquired later in life [2]. Symptoms derived from airway collapse, such as cough, sputum production, wheezing, and frequent lung infections are typically observed in children. They are associated with specific bronchiectasis affecting the mid-level bronchioles [3]. Diagnosing this syndrome often necessitates bronchoscopy, but it is an invasive procedure that can have many complications, even more so if the segmental bronchi are very fragile, as in WCS. Another diagnostic tool is the combination of expiratory/inspiratory computed tomography (CT) scans. Considering the non-invasive nature, ease of execution, and good patient tolerance associated with CT scans and acknowledging the potential complications of lung biopsy, it is highly advised to include expiratory/inspiratory chest CT as a diagnostic procedure for patients presenting with central diffuse cylindrical or saccular bronchiectasis [1]. Currently, the disease remains poorly understood, with no established description, comorbidities, or associations with any habits or substance consumption reported. While WCS has been predominantly reported in pediatric cases, it is noteworthy that the literature describing the diagnosis of Williams–Campbell Syndrome in the adult population remains limited and without pathologic confirmation [4]. This underscores condition's rarity in adults and highlights the importance of recognizing and differentiating it from other bronchiectasis-related disorders, especially in cases where it manifest later in life. We aim to convey the findings of two individuals, aged 53 and 59, who were newly diagnosed with Williams-Campbell syndrome during their adult years and experienced barely detectable symptoms.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 53-year-old man presented to the office complaining of sore throat, headache, and dry cough for the previous two weeks. He has never been hospitalized and denies a significant history of respiratory disease during childhood. His family's past medical history does not include relevant data. He is married with two sons and one daughter who have never experienced significant respiratory symptoms. He has a history of five episodes of rhinosinusitis, presenting every four to five years, ranging from mild to severe. Chronic rhinosinusitis has been ruled out. Additionally, he takes antacids tablets on demand for gastroesophageal reflux disease symptoms diagnosed five years ago. He smoked three to four cigarettes on weekends between ages 20–24 and never used other drugs. The patient has had COVID-19 three times, all of which were asymptomatic, but a follow-up CT scan revealed cysts in all lung lobes. On physical examination, vital signs were normal. Thoracic examination revealed fine crackles in both lung bases and SpO2 was 99 % on room air. The pulmonary function tests were between normal ranges (FEV1/FVC 89.3 %, FVC 75%p (Z-Score −1.34), FEV1 82%p and TLC 99%p); lung diffusion was also between normal ranges (DLCO 115%p). Oscillometry did not reveal any abnormality. A chest CT scan was performed again, revealing the presence of bronchiectasis with varicose or saccular characteristics, predominantly central, with a reduction in their diameters during the expiratory phase of the study. There were faint ground glass opacities in the posterior basal areas, with a left-sided predominance indicative of pneumonitis. Thin-walled cystic airspaces measuring 20 mm were observed in the lateral basal segment of the right lower lung lobe and subcentimetric (smaller than 1 cm) ones in the posterior basal region of the left lung. Focal ground glass opacities associated with septal thickening were noted in the lateral segment of the middle lobe (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Additionally, a subcentimeter calcified lung nodule was found in the posterior segment of the upper lobe of the right lung. These findings were suggestive of Williams-Campbell syndrome. A bronchoscopy was not performed. After history and physical examination, fractional exhaled nitric oxide testing was performed, reporting low levels (<25 ppb) for which he has been prescribed inhaled corticosteroids plus olopatadine and levodropropizine for ten days for symptom management. Additionally, he received vaccination against pneumococcus. At the follow up after six months the patient did not report any symptoms suggestive of upper or lower respiratory tract disease. Respiratory function remained stable and vaccination scheme was up to date.

Case 2

A 59-year-old patient presented to the office complaining of moderate exertional dyspnea and palpitations, odynophagia, fever, general discomfort, nasal congestion, fatigue, and myalgia, for which he has been taking antihistamines and acetaminophen. The patient has a history of H1N1 infection in 2019. A previous CT scan of the chest revealed abnormal findings compatible with pulmonary bullae. His vaccination status consisted of Ad26.COV2.S COVID-19 vaccine doses and a BNT162b2_ booster shot. The patient also underwent a nephrectomy for idiopathic hydronephrosis. He has been a cyclist for the past 12 years, does not report alcohol consumption, but smoked half a pack of cigarettes daily (10 cigarettes a day) which stopped 23 years ago. Current medications include candesartan, ezetimibe, and simvastatin due to hypertension and dyslipidemia. Physical examination was normal. A pulmonary function test showed a mild restrictive pattern with no response to the bronchodilator. DLCO was normal (78%p), and oscillometry as well. The chest x-ray revealed cysts throughout both lungs, leading to a presumptive diagnosis of mild COPD. However, IgG and alpha-1 antitrypsin tests came back negative. A second CT scan of the chest reported cystic bronchiectasis, centrally predominant and bilateral, along with thin-walled cystic airspaces in the basal regions (Fig. 3). There was also cystic bronchiectasis in the peripheral areas and thickening of the walls of the right apical bronchiectasis (Fig. 4). These findings were suggestive of Williams-Campbell syndrome. He was prescribed a combination of oral and nasal corticosteroids plus antihistamines and isotonic solution (sodium hyaluronate) for nasal irrigation for ten days for symptom management. He was vaccinated against pneumococcus. At the six-month follow-up, the patient did not report any symptoms suggestive of upper or lower respiratory tract disease, as did case number 1. Respiratory function remained stable, and the vaccination scheme was up to date. Yearly follow-up was recommended for both cases.

Discussion

Williams–Campbell syndrome has been described as an unconventional form of bronchiectasis. It was first reported in 1959 by Williams and Campbell in pediatric patients who presented with cough, difficulty breathing, and recurrent respiratory infections [5]. The presentation of WCS in adults can be diverse and requires an appropriate clinical history and is highly advised to have a CT scan of the chest revealing expiratory collapse of the airways specifically in the bronchiectasis. It may mimic other respiratory conditions, posing diagnostic challenges—case 1 presented with recurrent rhinosinusitis episodes, a history of GERD, and COVID-19 with lung cysts. Case 2 had a history of pulmonary bullae, H1N1 infection, and a cyclist lifestyle. Both patients underwent extensive diagnostic evaluations, including pulmonary function tests and imaging studies, before WCS was considered. These cases emphasize the importance of considering WCS as a differential diagnosis in adults presenting with chronic respiratory symptoms, even in the absence of typical childhood manifestations. The rarity of adult-onset WCS and its potential overlap with other respiratory conditions make its diagnosis challenging [6].

While biopsy or pathology confirmation is indeed a gold standard for definitive diagnosis in many medical conditions, in the case of WCS, the literature supports a diagnostic approach primarily based on clinical presentation and radiological findings. When analyzing a CT scan that shows several thin-walled cystic formations, it is important to consider the patient's age at the time of presentation, symptoms, past medical history, and lung function to make an accurate differential diagnosis. McAdams suggests that a chest x-ray or plain chest tomography may effectively exclude congenital causes other than WCS, such as cystic adenomatoid malformation, pulmonary sequestration, pneumatocele, or necrotizing pneumonia [7]. In addition, tomography enables to exclude the existence of acquired abnormalities such as allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, pulmonary fibrosis, recurrent pneumonia, and even tracheobronchomegaly, which may be quickly ruled out if the diameter of the trachea is normal. Tracheomegaly, although primarily associated with Mounier-Kuhn syndrome [8], can ocassionally present in conditions linked to chronic respiratory stress and structural alterations in the airway system caused by recurrent infections and airway collapse [9] but its relationship to Williams-Campbell syndrome (WCS) is not well-documented. WCS typically affects the bronchi, with imaging studies such as high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) revealing bronchiectasis and airway collapse rather than tracheal enlargment [10]. In the cases discussed, there was no evidence of tracheomegaly or recurrent infections. However, it is crucial to rule out this condition because the specific prevalence of tracheomegaly in WCS is not well-established, highlighting the need for additional research to better understand this aspect of the syndrome.

Previous reports of WCS diagnosis in adults have consistently underscored the significance of considering various clinical factors and radiological features in the absence of histopathological confirmation. McAdams et al., Konoglou et al., and Amano et al., have collectively demonstrated that WCS diagnosis in adults has been routinely made without the necessity of biopsy [4,7,11]. These studies have emphasized the importance of comprehensive imaging modalities such as high-resolution CT scans, which provide crucial insights into characteristic radiological features indicative of WCS. Furthermore, recommendations from McAdams et al. and Watanabe et al. regarding the utility of specific diagnostic techniques such as high-resolution CT scan with inspiration and expiration phases, which have proven to be more informative than bronchoscopy in identifying WCS-related abnormalities [7,12].

The pathogenesis of WCS is not fully understood, and the exact mechanism leading to cartilage deficiency in subsegmental bronchi remains elusive. While the deficiency of cartilage is a hallmark of WCS, there is no evidence to suggest that cartilage deficiency occurs outside of the bronchi or secondary to additional factors or pathophysiology [11,13]. Furthermore, the variable clinical course observed in WCS patients highlights the need for further research into the underlying mechanisms that determine the extent of cartilage maldevelopment and the subsequent clinical outcomes. This underscores the importance of genetic studies and familial screening, as evidenced by reports of familial heredity in some cases [14]. No specific management exists for adult-onset WCS, instead, primarily focuses on symptom management and preventing recurrent respiratory infections. Treatment typically includes inhaled corticosteroids, antihistamines, pneumococcal vaccination, and other supportive measures. The goal is to alleviate symptoms, improve the quality of life, and reduce the risk of pulmonary exacerbations. Some studies have suggested lung transplantation in severe cases; its practice is still controversial due to evidence of compromise in proximal airways and the development of complications found after autopsy evaluation [15]. Follow-up is essential to monitor disease progression and treatment response and detect any potential complications. The long-term prognosis is variable, with rapid clinical deterioration and death in some children and prolonged survival in others, especially in adults with a different natural history evenly asymptomatic [16]. Both cases received follow-up recommendations within six months to assess their clinical status and adjust treatment strategies as needed.

Conclusions

Due to its rarity and variety of clinical symptoms, Williams-Campbell syndrome in adults can be challenging to diagnose and treat. In adults with chronic respiratory problems, even without a typical childhood history, WCS should be considered. Healthcare practitioners must be more vigilant in detecting and treating adult-onset WCS.
