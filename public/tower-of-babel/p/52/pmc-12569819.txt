Effect of metal types on the controllable supramolecular self-assembly of protein-polyphenol conjugates and their carrier capabilities☆
Authors: Qian-Da Xu, Zhi-Long Yu, Qiang He, Wei-Cai Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegulation of different metal ions on the properties, structure, self-assembly and encapsulating capabilities of soybean globulin (SG)-tannic acid (TA) conjugates were investigated. Through coordination interactions, metal ions promoted the supramolecular self-assembly of SG, resulting in the efficient encapsulation of β-carotene in its hydrophobic domains. Fe3+ and Cu2+ enhanced particle integrity by forming stable metal–polyphenol networks, while Al3+ and Zn2+ achieved the highest β-carotene encapsulation efficiency due to their appropriate capability to interact with SG/TA and lower destructive effect on β-carotene. In the presence of TA, Fe3+/Cu2+ imparted strong pH stability to the carriers, Al3+/Mg2+/Zn2+ preserved β-carotene under light/heat/oxygen conditions, and Al3+/Zn2+ promoted superior antioxidant activity and β-carotene bioaccessibility during digestion. These findings demonstrate that metal ions with distinct coordination characteristics significantly influence the functionality of SG–TA conjugates, offering insights for the rational design of metal-induced protein–polyphenol delivery systems.Keywords: Soybean globulin, Metal ion, Tannic acid, Encapsulation, Functionalities

Introduction

Biomacromolecule-based carrier matrices, including proteins, polysaccharides, and lipids, demonstrate excellent compatibility with hydrophobic small molecules due to the presence of numerous hydrophobic groups and cavities (Zhang et al., 2024). Among these, the amphiphilic nature of proteins contributes to the encapsulation of hydrophobic compounds and their subsequent industrial applications (Jaganathan et al., 2014; Webber & Pashuck, 2021). Recently, encapsulation systems using soybean protein, zein, and whey protein as carriers have been developed (Akhtar et al., 2023; Kianfar, 2021). These protein carriers have been widely applied in nutritional supplementation, food preservation, and biodegradable packaging. However, protein carriers are often unstable in extreme pH, high salt concentrations, elevated temperatures, and enzymatic environments, which can lead to the premature release and deactivation of the encapsulated contents (Kianfar, 2021). Therefore, improving the stability of protein-based carrier particles, especially under acidic/alkaline and ionic stress, is essential for their application in in food processing and nutrition delivery.

Carotenoids, as typical hydrophobic small molecules, are widely employed in the chemical industry due to their good chemical reactivity and physiological benefits (Boon et al., 2010; Rehman et al., 2020). However, their sensitivity to acidic/alkaline, light, heat, and oxygen, along with their low solubility, limits their industrial applications (Boon et al., 2010). To overcome these challenges, researchers have developed encapsulation systems such as nanoparticles, liposomes, hydrogels, and emulsions to improve carotenoid stability (Rehman et al., 2020). While emulsion systems for carotenoid encapsulation have been extensively studied, the challenges of storage and transportation have hindered their successful commercialization (Albert et al., 2019; Sheth et al., 2020).

In previous study (Xu et al., 2025), we introduced a ferric ion-induced, tannic acid-enhanced protein self-assembly system for encapsulating carotenoids. Soybean globulin, an ideal carrier matrix, offers excellent functional properties, is environmentally friendly and cost-effective (Tang, 2021). Tannic acid, a natural extract with multiple hydroxyl groups and strong reactivity, not only imparts new biological activities to the protein-polyphenol conjugates but also strengthens protein-protein interactions (Chen et al., 2021; Yang & Song, 2019). Metal ions can induce protein aggregation and self-assembly by coordinating with side-chain groups (Huo et al., 2019), facilitating the encapsulation of hydrophobic small molecules. In the presence of tannic acid, the metal-phenolic network formed by ferric ions and tannic acid (Chen et al., 2023) further stabilizes the carrier particles, enhancing the stability of carotenoid-encapsulated carriers. However, excessive ferric ions may promote carotenoid oxidation and degradation (Boon et al., 2010). The use of other metal ions that can coordinate with proteins/tannic acid, while causing minimal degradation of carotenoids, to form self-assembled particles may be advantageous for improving carotenoid encapsulation efficiency and supporting their industrial application.

In this study, we employed several metal ions (Fe3+, Cu2+, Al3+, Mg2+, Ca2+, and Zn2+) to trigger the self-assembly of tannic acid–soybean globulin conjugates for the encapsulation of β-carotene, with the aim of improving its stability during storage and processing, as well as enhancing its bioaccessibility. We hypothesize that, the self-assembly of carrier particles can be modulated by the coordination mode and strength of different metal ions, thereby supporting antioxidant activity, color enhancement, and nutrient-delivery in food systems under varying pH and ionic conditions. The capability of these metal ions to induce protein self-assembly was evaluated using the fluorescence spectrum and Stern-Volmer equation. Infrared spectroscopy was employed to analyze the structural changes upon self-assembly and β-carotene encapsulation. Scanning electron microscopy and laser confocal microscopy were used to determine the microscopic morphology of the particles and the distribution of the contents inside the particles. The pH stability, light/heat/oxygen stability, and the fate of the particles during in vitro digestion were also evaluated and compared.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagent

Dry soy protein isolated powder (>90 %) was obtained from Yuanye (Shanghai, China). Trypsin (1000–2000 units/mg), pepsin (∼2500 units/mg), and bile salt were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Ferric chloride (FeCl3), zinc chloride (ZnCl2), magnesium chloride (MgCl2), calcium chloride (CaCl2), copper chloride (CuCl2), aluminum chloride (AlCl3), tannic acid (>98 %), β-carotene (>96 %) and other analytical grade reagents were obtained from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Purified water was obtained by using UPR-II-10 T (ULUPURE, Chengdu, China).

Preparation of protein microparticles

Isolation of soybean globulin

Soybean globulin (SG) was isolated from soy protein isolated according to protein isoelectric point settlement method described by Nagano et al., 2002. Detailed methods were according to the previous study (Xu et al., 2025).

Preparation of protein-polyphenol conjugates

The 3 mmol/L of tannic acid (TA) solution (pH 9.0) was stirred at 500 r/min and 45 °C for 1 h to prepare oxidized TA solution (Wang & Xiong, 2021). Meanwhile, SG solution (12.5 g/L, pH 9.0) was prepared by heating at 100 °C for 15 min. Then, the 100 mL of oxidized TA solution, 800 mL of SG solutions and 100 mL of water were mixed and incubated at 45 °C for 2 h to prepare SG-TA conjugates solution (30 μmol TA/g SG, pH 9.0) (Balange & Benjakul, 2010; Guo, Bao, Sun, Chang and Liu, 2021). Following pH adjustment to 7.5, the free TA was removed via dialysis using a membrane (10,000 Da). The solutions were then freeze-dried to obtain SG-TA conjugates and named as SG-30TA sample. Moreover, 100 mL of water was used in place of the oxidized TA solution to prepare SG-0TA sample (SG without TA) with the same procedures.

Preparation of protein-polyphenol carriers

Twenty milliliters of SG-30TA solutions (10 g/L, pH 7.5) were vortexed. Meanwhile, 20 mmol/L of different metal chloride solution was added dropwise. The SG–30TA solutions required the addition of 300 μL of FeCl3, 440 μL of ZnCl2, 3.2 mL of MgCl2, 1.1 mL of CaCl2, 440 μL of CuCl2, and 300 μL of AlCl3, respectively, to induce complete protein precipitation. The precipitation was freeze-dried to obtain S30-metals microparticles, named S30—Fe, S30—Cu, S30—Al, S30—Mg, S30—Ca, S30—Zn. The SG–0TA solutions (20 mL, 10 g/L, pH 7.5) required the addition of 240 μL of FeCl3, 340 μL of ZnCl2, 2 mL of MgCl2, 0.8 mL of CaCl2, 350 μL of CuCl2, and 260 μL of AlCl3, respectively. The precipitation was freeze-dried to obtain S0-metals microparticles, named S0—Fe, S0—Cu, S0—Al, S0—Mg, S0—Ca, S0—Zn.

Encapsulation of β-carotene

Twenty milliliters of SG-0TA and SG-30TA solutions (10 g/L, pH 7.5) were vortexed, respectively. Meanwhile, 2 mL of β-carotene solution (4 g/L, 55 °C, dissolved in ethyl acetate) was added dropwise. After incubating and stirring at 45 °C for 30 min with appropriate sonication, the mixture was cooled to 25 °C (Chen et al., 2024). Then, 20 mmol/L of different metal chloride solutions were added dropwise until the complete precipitation of protein. The consumption of metal ion solution in SG-30TA-β-c and SG-0TA-β-c solutions was exactly the same as in section 2.2.3. The precipitation was freeze-dried to obtain microparticles and named as S30-βc-metals (S30-βc-Fe, S30-βc-Cu, S30-βc-Al, S30-βc-Mg, S30-βc-Ca, S30-βc-Zn) and S0-βc-metals (S0-βc-Fe, S0-βc-Cu, S0-βc-Al, S0-βc-Mg, S0-βc-Ca, S0-βc-Zn).

Determination of encapsulation amount and encapsulation efficiency

The encapsulation amount of β-carotene was determined according to Guo et al., 2022 with some modifications. Briefly, 10 mg of particle was immersed and vortexed with 5 mL of ethanol: n-hexane (2:3), thereby centrifuging at 5000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C to collect supernatant. The precipitate was extracted five more times, and a total of 30 mL supernatant was combined for further testing. Then, 0.5 mL of supernatant was vortexed with 4.5 mL of ethanol: n-hexane (2:3) and 1 mL of water. Meanwhile, the residual precipitate was fully dissolved in 5 mL of water (pH 12) as quickly as possible, and 0.5 mL of solution was vortexed with 5 mL of ethanol: n-hexane (2:3) and 0.5 mL of water. The upper hexane phase extracted from both the supernatant and residual precipitate was collected and measured using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (UV-2000, UNICO CO., LTD, Shanghai, China) at 450 nm, respectively. The β-carotene content in hexane phase was calculated using the β-carotene standard curve. The encapsulation amount (mg β-carotene /g) of β-carotene was the total amount extracted from the supernatant and residual precipitate. Encapsulation efficiency (EE) was calculated as: encapsulation amount/total added amount × 100 %.

Fluorescence spectral analysis

The binding capabilities between metal ions and SG were determined according to the Stern–Volmer analysis described by Sun et al., 2017 and Sharma et al., 2013. A fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-7000, Hitachi Inc., Japan) was used to determine the fluorescence intensity of SG after binding with different amounts and types of metal ions. SG solution (5 g/L) was vortexed and mixed with metal chlorides, subsequently, incubating at 293 K, 303 K, and 313 K for 15 min, respectively. The final concentration of metal chlorides in the mixed solution was 0–5 × 10−5 mol/L of Fe3+, 0–15 × 10−5 mol/L of Cu2+, 0–20 × 10−5 mol/L of Al3+, 0–120 × 10−5 mol/L of Mg2+, 0–80 × 10−5 mol/L of Ca2+ and 0–30 × 10−5 mol/L of Zn2+, respectively. The excitation wavelength was 290 nm, and the emission wavelength was 310 to 500 nm.

Fourier infrared spectral analysis

The interactions among TA, SG, β-carotene and metal ions in microparticles were determined using a type Nicolet Is50 Fourier infrared spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA). S0-metals, S30-metals, S0-βc-metals and S30-βc-metals were grinded and pelleted with KBr and scanned 12 times at 2 cm−1 resolution. The transmittance (%) was recorded within the wavenumber range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 (Xu et al., 2025).

Scanning electron microscopy and particle size analysis

Based on our previous method (Xu et al., 2025), the morphology of microparticles was determined using a type Apreo 2C scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA). The particle size was measured using a laser particle size analyzer (LA-960, HORIBA Inc., Kyoto, Japan).

Confocal laser scanning microscopy analysis

The spatial distribution of β-carotene within the microparticles was measured using a type STELLARIS 8 laser scanning confocal microscopy (CLSM, Leica, Weztlar, Germany). The particles were dyed by Nile Blue A and placed on a slide. The excitation wavelengths were 405 nm, 450 nm, 633 nm, and the emission wavelengths were received to generate CLSM images (Phan et al., 2017; Pravinata & Murray, 2019).

Determination of particles stability

pH stability

Twenty milligrams of microparticles were immersed in 5 mL of phosphate buffer solutions (0.01 mol/L, pH 1–12, adjusting by HCl or NaOH) and shaken for 1 h at 25 °C. After that, the mixtures were filtered by 0.45 μm polyether sulfone membrane. The undissolved particles were collected and dried until a constant weight was achieved. The pH stability = mu / mo × 100 %. Where mu and mo are the weight of undissolved particles and original particles (mg), respectively.

Stabilities under extreme conditions

Twenty milligrams of microparticles were uniformly spread across an open petri dish with a 5 mm diameter and incubated under convective air conditions at 60 °C, with exposure to natural light for 48 h. At designated sampling times, the microparticles were extracted, and the β-carotene content was quantified using the method described in section 2.3. Stabilities = Cs / Co × 100 %. Where Cs and Co are the content of β-carotene at sampling time and initial time, respectively.

In vitro digestion characteristics of particles

Preparation of the digestion procedures

The preparation of the digestion procedures was based on the ‘INFOGEST 2.0’ method (Brodkorb et al., 2019) with some modifications. The detailed methods of digestion procedures and the preparation of simulated digestive fluid were given in our previous study (Xu et al., 2025). Undigested sample (S0), digesta after oral digestion (2 min for S1), gastric digestion (40 min for G1, 80 min for G2, 120 min for G3), and intestinal digestion (40 min for I1, 80 min for I2, 120 min for I3) were stored in cold water bath (0 °C) and determined immediately.

Protein digestibility

Protein digestibility in all digesta samples (S1 ∼ I3) was determined according to the procedures established by Cao et al., 2016 with some modifications. Briefly, the 8 mL of digesta sample was mixed with 1 mL of trichloroacetic acid (10 g/L) to precipitate undigested protein, and the mixture was centrifuged at 8000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C. After that, precipitate was washed in 5 mL of water twice and dried to a constant weight. Protein digestibility was calculated by following equation: protein digestibility = (m1 - m2) / m1 × 100 %. Where m2 and m1 are the weight of in digesta samples (S1 ∼ I3) and undigested sample (S0), respectively.

Bioaccessibility of β-carotene

Bioaccessibility of β-carotene in all digesta samples (S1 ∼ I3) was determined according to the procedures established by Feng et al., 2024. Briefly, the β-carotene in micelles was collected and extracted by ethanol: n-hexane (2:3) solution. The absorbance of the upper hexane phase was measured at 450 nm, and the β-carotene content was calculated using a standard curve. Bioaccessibility of β-carotene was calculated by following equation: bioaccessibility = mt / mo × 100 %. Where mt and mo are the β-carotene amount in protein micelles of digesta samples (S1 ∼ I3) and encapsulation amount in original microparticles, respectively.

ABTS+ free radical scavenging capacity

ABTS+ free radical scavenging capacity were determined according to our previous method (Xu et al., 2024). The ABTS+ free radical scavenging capacity of the digesta was calculated based on the scavenging capacity of ascorbic acid, expressed as mg ascorbic acid equivalents /mL.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as means ± standard deviations from at least three replicates. The analysis of non-parametric test variance was performed with the SPSS software (Version 22.0 for Windows, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and the Friedman test was used to determine significant differences between means (P < 0.05).

Results and discussions

Metal ion-induced self-assembly of SG

Metal ions typically induce protein self-assembly by interacting with side-chain groups of proteins (Huo et al., 2019). The mode and strength of protein self-assembly are correlated with the outer electronic activity and electron transfer capacity of the metal ions (Davies et al., 2009). The quenching ability of metal ions on protein fluorescence was shown in Fig. 1 and Fig. S1. Within the experimental concentration range, the fluorescence intensity of the protein gradually decreased with increasing metal ion concentration. The ratio of fluorescence intensity in the presence of the quencher (F) to the protein's initial fluorescence intensity (F0) progressively increased, which could be expressed by the Stern-Volmer equation. The results indicated that dynamic and static quenching generally occurred simultaneously during the quenching of protein fluorescence by metal ions (Sharma et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2017). For example, with Fe3+ groups (Fig. 1A), the Stern-Volmer plot showed a gradually increasing slope. At the highest experimental concentration (5 × 10−5 mol/L Fe3+), a small amount of protein-metal complex precipitate was observed. This suggested that within this concentration range (0–5 × 10−5 mol/L Fe3+), static quenching, dominated by the formation of non-fluorescent complexes, was influenced by dynamic quenching (the collision between the quencher and the fluorophore) (Sharma et al., 2013). In this case, Kq was observed much higher than 2 × 1010 L/(mol × s) (the maximum diffusion collision quenching constant), static quenching was the dominant mechanism (Sharma et al., 2013).

Stern-Volmer plot of soybean globulin in the presence of various metal ions (A)Fe3+, (B)Cu2+, (C)Al3+, (D)Mg2+, (E)Ca2+, (F)Zn2+.

As the outer electronic activity of the metal ions decreases, their ability to collide with and quench protein fluorescence weakens, and they tend to form stable complexes via electron transfer (Nam, 2023). Therefore, in the case of Cu2+ group (Fig. 1B), Al3+ group (Fig. 1C), and Zn2+ group (Fig. 1F), protein fluorescence quenching was mainly observed at lower concentrations (Cu2+ < 7.5 × 10−5 mol/L, Al3+ < 14 × 10−5 mol/L, Zn2+ < 24 × 10−5 mol/L), where relatively clear solutions and dynamic quenching were observed concurrently. At higher concentrations, static quenching became more pronounced, accompanied by a larger amount of protein-metal complex precipitate. As the outer electronic activity of the metal ions further weakens, static quenching became the only mechanism. Mg2+ group (Fig. 1D) and Ca2+ group (Fig. 1E) required higher concentrations (Mg2+ > 70 × 10−5 mol/L, Ca2+ > 30 × 10−5 mol/L) to observe fluorescence quenching, accompanied by substantial complex precipitation.

Table S1 summarized the quenching capabilities of different metal ions on protein fluorescence and the maximum absorption wavelengths of the complexes. As the concentration of metal ions increased, the maximum absorption wavelength of protein fluorescence undergone a significant red shift, indicating an enhancement in the polarity of the microenvironment around tryptophan and tyrosine residues (Cao et al., 2018). The KSV value decreased with the increasing of incubation temperature, suggesting that the fluorescence quenching mechanism of metal ions on proteins was predominantly static quenching (Rudra et al., 2016). At the same temperature, the order of KSV values was as follows: Fe3+, Al3+ > Zn2+, Cu2+ > Ca2+, Mg2+. Therefore, Fe3+ and Al3+ exhibited the strongest quenching ability on protein fluorescence, followed by Zn2+ and Cu2+, while Ca2+ and Mg2+ showed the weakest fluorescence quenching abilities. The binding sites and modes of interaction between metal ions and proteins were further analyzed using infrared spectroscopy.

Binding sites and interaction modes of metal ions with proteins

The infrared spectra of SG self-assembled particles induced by metal ions were shown in Fig. 2. The changes in the protein amide bands, as shown in Table S2 and Fig. 2A, reflected the structural effect of metal ions on SG (S0-metals). The amide A band (3200 to 3500 cm−1) of SG exhibited broader peak widths and higher peak frequencies after binding with metal ions, indicating that the -NH stretching vibration and the strength of the hydrogen bonds were affected (Wang et al., 2017). Metal ions induced protein aggregation, leading to a more compact and stable folding, which enhanced the hydrogen bond interactions within the system (Lee et al., 2018). The effect of metal ions on the SG amide A band might correlate positively with the degree of protein aggregation induced by the metal ions, with Mg2+/Ca2+ showing the greatest changes and Zn2+ the least. After tannic acid (TA) modification, nearly all S30-metals samples exhibited further broadening and a shift to higher frequencies of the amide A band (Table S2 and Fig. 2B). These changes might due to the introduction of additional hydrogen bonds by TA and the tighter aggregation of the protein, which increased the vibrational energy of the system (Wang & Xiong, 2018). The cooperation of metal ions and TA significantly reduced the peak intensity of the amide II band, which might due to the covalent interaction between TA and the protein (C—N bond transforming into C-N-C bond) and/or the formation of new coordination bonds between the metal ions and SG (Guo et al., 2021; Wang & Xiong, 2018).

FTIR spectrum of S0-metals (A), S30-metals (B), S0-βc-metals (C), and S30-βc-metals (D) particles.

The most significant infrared spectral changes in this study were observed in the 1350 to 1500 cm−1 region and the amide III band (1200 to 1300 cm−1). Binding of metal ions caused a decrease in peak intensity and a high-frequency shift of the -COO− (1400 cm−1) and -COOH (1440 cm−1) groups, indicating that -COO− was one of the primary binding sites for metal ions with the protein (Wang & Xiong, 2018). Fe3+, Cu2+, and Al3+ significantly reduced the peak intensity of -COO− group and -COOH group. Meanwhile, Mg2+ and Ca2+ induced strong high-frequency shifts in -COO− group (enhanced vibrational energy), shifting the peak towards -COOH group. Some relative studies have shown that metal ions, as electron-withdrawing groups, coordinate with the electron-rich sites of -COO− to form -COO-metals complexes, resulting in more stable complexes (Wang et al., 2017; Wang & Xiong, 2018). The modification of TA did not significantly affect the absorption peak in the 1350 to 1500 cm−1 of SG, but it caused a significant decrease of intensity and a high-frequency shift of the peak in the amide III band (primarily a combination of C—N stretching and N—H bending vibrations). This change suggests that TA is able to interact with the C—N and/or N—H groups of SG (Cai & Singh, 2004; Rohn, 2014). Interestingly, SG without TA modification (S0) exhibited a noticeable high-frequency shift in the amide III band after binding with metal ions (S0-metals). However, among the TA modification (S30) samples, no significant changes of the peak at this band were observed after binding with metal ions (S30-metals). These results indicate that metal ions interact with the amino groups of SG, but when TA preferentially modifies the protein and occupies the -NH sites, metal ions may not directly react with the SG's -NH groups, but instead interact with TA that occupying those sites. When β-carotene was encapsulated (S0-βc-metals and S30-βc-metals), the changes in the infrared spectra of the protein amide bands (Fig. 2C, D, Table S2) were similar to those observed in the non-encapsulation state (S0-metals and S30-metals), with only slight broadening and shifts due to the inherent infrared absorption of β-carotene. These results indicate that β-carotene does not significantly interfere with the metal-induced protein self-assembly process. Additionally, a new peak appeared at 965 cm−1 (polyene chain), suggesting successful encapsulation of β-carotene (Schlücker et al., 2003).

Metal ions coordinate with proteins primarily through carboxyl oxygen and amino nitrogen atoms in different modes, aligning with some previous studies. These distinct coordination modes may result in various complexation models, thereby modulating the structural integrity of the protein-ligand network. Metal-induced modifications in the secondary and tertiary structures of proteins are closely associated with the strength and stability of the assembled particles. In particular, changes in β-sheet content (a sheet-like structure formed by hydrogen bonding between parallel or antiparallel peptide chains) are often associated with variations in protein rigidity and aggregation. Fig. S2 illustrated the changes in protein secondary structures in the presence of different metal ions. Ca2+ and Mg2+, having fewer valence electrons, primarily coordinate in a monodentate mode with individual residues (Yang & Song, 2019), resulting in only slight secondary structure changes even at high concentrations. In contrast, Fe3+, Cu2+, Al3+ and Zn2+, which have more valence electrons, tend to form multidentate coordination with proteins (Yang & Song, 2019). These ions can simultaneously bind to multiple sites across or within peptide chains, thereby significantly enhancing β-sheet formation and inducing efficient protein self-assembly. Moreover, Fig. S2 also demonstrated that the presence of β-carotene did not interfere with these metal-induced assembly behaviors.

Encapsulation capability of carrier matrices

The encapsulation efficiency of β-carotene by the carrier matrix was shown in Fig. 3. Al3+ and Zn2+ achieved the highest encapsulation efficiency, followed by Mg2+ and Ca2+. Cu2+ and Fe3+ exhibited the lowest encapsulation efficiencies. The encapsulation efficiency of β-carotene by metal ions was related to their ability to induce protein self-assembly and their reactivity with β-carotene. In the S0-βc-metals groups, the required metal ion amounts for completing SG self-assembly precipitation were: 24 μmol Fe3+/g SG, 35 μmol Cu2+/g SG, 26 μmol Al3+/g SG, 200 μmol Mg2+/g SG, 80 μmol Ca2+/g SG, and 34 μmol Zn2+/g SG. Transition metal elements usually have strong oxidation catalytic capability (Choe & Min, 2009). While Fe3+ and Cu2+ can induce protein self-assembly at lower doses, their strong reactivity with β-carotene may lead to destruction of β-carotene during the encapsulation process (Boon et al., 2010). Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions have the weakest reactivity with carotene, but at high doses, they may still have a negative impact on the native structure of β-carotene. Al3+ and Zn2+ enabled to encapsulate β-carotene at lower doses, and their reactivity with β-carotene is significantly weaker than that of Fe3+ and Cu2+. As a result, the highest amounts of original β-carotene were encapsulated within the Al3+ and Zn2+ carrier particles.

Encapsulation efficiency of β-carotene in S0-βc-metals and S30-βc-metals particles.

After modification with tannic acid (S30-βc-metals), the required metal ion for completing SG self-assembly precipitation increased to: 30 μmol Fe3+/g SG, 44 μmol Cu2+/g SG, 30 μmol Al3+/g SG, 320 μmol Mg2+/g SG, 110 μmol Ca2+/g SG, and 44 μmol Zn2+/g SG. This increase may attribute to the consumption of more metal ions due to the formation of metal-phenolic networks (MPNs) by TA with a partial of metal ions (Guo et al., 2021). Under the antioxidant protection of TA (Xu et al., 2024), the additional metal ions did not cause further destruction of the original structure of β-carotene. Therefore, no significant difference in β-carotene encapsulation efficiency was observed between S0-βc-metals samples and S30-βc-metals groups.

Fig. 4 illustrated the principle and process of β-carotene encapsulation induced by SG self-assembly in the presence of metal ions. SG unfolded in an alkaline/heat environment (Lynch et al., 2006), exposing more hydrophobic sites. β-carotene interacted with some of the hydrophobic sites on SG through non-covalent interactions and was retained within the hydrophobic cavities of the protein (Chen et al., 2024). Metal ions induced SG to perform supramolecular self-assembly and aggregation via metal coordination bonds (Yang & Song, 2019), thus encapsulating the β-carotene retained in the hydrophobic cavities of SG. After covalent bonding with the SG's side-chain groups, TA enhanced the intermolecular interactions between protein molecules (Huo et al., 2019; Xue et al., 2024). Additionally, tannic acid, by forming dense MPNs with metal ions (Guo et al., 2021), further strengthened the structure and stability of the particles (Ejima et al., 2013).

Formation mechanisms of carrier matrix and encapsulated particles.

Morphology of the carrier particles

The morphology of SG self-assembled particles encapsulating β-carotene was shown in Figs. 5, S3, and S4. Following alkali-heat treatment and freeze-drying, the SG particles (SG-original) unfolded into a planar structure (SG-0TA). TA-modified SG particles (SG-30TA) exhibited a smoother and denser appearance, indicating that TA enhanced the molecular interactions between SG molecules (Xue et al., 2024). Layered interwoven structures were observed on the surfaces of both S0-βc-metals and S30-βc-metals self-assembled particles. In comparison to the 0TA groups, the particles in the 30TA group displayed a more compact and smoother layered interwoven structures, which might due to the increased amount of metal ions and the formation of MPN structures (Ejima et al., 2013; Guo et al., 2021). The particles induced by Mg2+ and Ca2+ showed the densest structure, which might be related to the widely coordination of protein carboxyl and amino groups observed in the FTIR analysis. However, excessive addition of Mg2+ and Ca2+ might result in some of them failing to fully bind to the protein side chains, leading to the crystallization of salt ions on the particle surfaces. The SG self-assembled particles induced by the other four kinds of metal ions exhibited irregular large layered interwoven structures, but the addition of TA facilitated the transformation from large to more uniformly dense small scales.

Scanning electron microscopy images of the morphology of particles.

As indicated in Fig. 4, the freeze-drying process may induce additional protein aggregation and irregular particle formation due to water crystallization and solute concentration. Particle size distributions were provided in Fig. S5 and Table S3. The largest average particle sizes were observed in the Fe3+ (47.46 μm) and Cu2+ (47.58 μm) induced carrier particles, followed by those formed with Al3+ (45.79 μm) and Zn2+ (41.30 μm). In contrast, the Mg2+ (35.53 μm) and Ca2+ (33.24 μm) groups exhibited the smallest average particle sizes. This size variation closely corresponds to the degree of protein folding observed in FTIR analysis. Metal ions with a higher number of valence electrons tend to facilitate protein folding and self-assembly through multidentate coordination, ultimately resulting in larger particle sizes. In addition, TA modification further increased particle size, likely due to the formation of cross-linked networks between TA and protein peptide chains.

Spatial distribution of β-carotene within the particles

After Nile Blue A staining, SG particles emitted red fluorescence under 633 nm excitation (Fig. 6), while β-carotene exhibited its intrinsic green fluorescence when excited at 450 nm (Pravinata & Murray, 2019). Carrier particles that were not loaded with β-carotene did not show significant fluorescence at 450 nm (Fig. S6). After modification with TA, a weak blue fluorescence from tannic acid was observed at 405 nm (Fig. S7) (Phan et al., 2017). Figs. 6 and S8 show the planar and three-dimensional spatial distributions of β-carotene within the carrier particles, respectively. A large portion of the β-carotene was evenly distributed within the carrier matrix, with some concentrated in larger cavities inside the particles. Additionally, a small amount of β-carotene was also found on the surface of particles. Under the same detection conditions, SG self-assembled particles induced by Al3+ and Zn2+ exhibited higher green fluorescence intensity, indicating that more β-carotene was encapsulated within these particles.

Stability of the carrier particles

Carrier particles composed primarily of proteins are generally unstable under acidic or alkaline conditions due to the protonation (or deprotonation) of acidic (or basic) subunits (Lynch et al., 2006), which weakens intermolecular aggregation and promotes protein solubility. Unmodified SG particles (SG-0TA/SG-30TA) were susceptible to disruption in almost all pH environments. After metal ion induction and self-assembly, the SG carrier particles (S0-βc-metals) showed good stability across a wide pH range (3–8) (Figs. 7A, S9, and S10). However, under strongly acidic (pH < 2) or strongly alkaline (pH > 9) conditions, all unmodified particles remain unstable. The Ca2+ and Mg2+ groups exhibited greater stability under acidic and alkaline conditions, which may attribute to the occupation of more acidic (carboxyl) and basic (amino) residues. The occupation of residues restricted the unfolding of protein chains in extreme pH environments. Although the other four metal ions promoted self-assembly by enhancing protein folding structures, these hydrogen bond–driven conformations remained unstable under acidic or alkaline conditions, ultimately resulting in particle disintegration.

Stabilities of β-carotene-encapsulated particles in varying pH solutions (A)S0-βc-metals, (B)S30-βc-metals and extreme conditions (C)S0-βc-metals, (D)S30-βc-metals.

SG self-assembled particles induced by Ca2+ and Mg2+, which possessed more and stronger SG-metal coordination interactions, exhibited a dense structure and better pH stability. The particles induced by the other four kinds of metal ions, with fewer SG-metal interactions and rougher surfaces, were less stable than those induced by Ca2+ and Mg2+.

TA modification significantly enhanced the pH stability of the carrier particles, particularly under alkaline conditions (Figs. 7B, S11, and S12). TA strengthened the interactions between SG molecules and reinforced the particle structure through the formation of numerous MPNs with metal ions (Fig. 5). The capability of metal ions to form MPNs with TA played a crucial role in improving the stability of the particles (Ejima et al., 2013; Guo et al., 2021). Fe3+ and Cu2+ formed complex and high-strength MPNs with TA, greatly enhancing the stability of the particles under both acidic and alkaline conditions. MPNs formed with Al3+ and Zn2+ also improved the stability of the particles. However, Ca2+ and Mg2+, with lower coordination numbers, generated simpler MPNs and have a weaker effect on particle stability. Compared to β-carotene-loaded systems based on Pickering emulsions (Wei et al., 2021; Zhan et al., 2025), solid carrier particles exhibited significantly greater stability in acidic and alkaline environments. Most emulsion systems remain stable only within a narrow pH range (pH 4–8), whereas the metal–induced particles preserved structural integrity across a wider pH spectrum (2–9). This broader stability range enhances their applicability in diverse food matrices with acidic or alkaline conditions.

β-Carotene, with its numerous unsaturated double bonds, undergoes oxidation when exposed to light, oxygen, and/or high temperatures, leading to the formation of degradation products and a loss of bioactivity (Boon et al., 2010). The stability of β-carotene-encapsulated particles under extreme conditions was shown in Fig. 7C and D. Encapsulation of β-carotene in the carrier matrix helped improve its stability under extreme conditions. Unprotected β-carotene was almost completely inactivated after 36 h under high temperature (60 °C), natural light, and airflow. After encapsulation in metal ion-induced SG particles, the carrier matrix delayed the oxidation and degradation of β-carotene due to its shielding effect against light and oxygen. However, since Fe3+ and Cu2+ catalyzed oxidation reactions, β-carotene in these particles decreased to less than 20 % after 24 h and was almost completely degraded by 48 h. In contrast, β-carotene in particles induced by the other four kinds of metal ions retained over 40 % of original structure after 24 h and still showed some retention rate (10 %) after 48 h. Some studies have used Pickering emulsions to encapsulate β-carotene (Wei et al., 2021; Zhan et al., 2025). However, after heat treatment at high temperature for 1 h, not only was the stability of the emulsions severely compromised, but the retention rate of β-carotene in most systems also dropped to approximately 60 %. Therefore, the use of solid particles for encapsulation provides benefits in improving the stability of β-carotene against heat, light, and oxidative degradation.

TA modification further enhanced the stability of β-carotene in all particles under extreme conditions (Fig. 7D). Tannic acid, rich in phenolic hydroxyl groups, protected β-carotene from oxidation through its strong antioxidant activity (Quideau et al., 2011). Moreover, the particles modified with TA (S30-βc-metals) showed stronger structural integrity compared to the S0-βc-metals group. After 48 h of incubation, SG self-assembled particles induced by Al3+, Mg2+, and Zn2+ retained more than 30 % of their β-carotene, while particles induced by Ca2+ and Cu2+ retained more than 20 %. In the Fe3+ group, β-carotene was almost completely depleted after 48 h. Therefore, in the presence of TA, metal ions that could form stable carrier structures and did not easily catalyze oxidation reactions provided the best protection for β-carotene.

Fate of carrier particles in simulated digestion

Protein digestibility

As shown in Fig. 8A and B, the SG groups without metal ion induction (S0-control, S30-control) reached approximately 50 % of protein digestion rate after 40 min of gastric digestion (G1), with the highest value (> 90 %) observed at the end of intestinal digestion. The results indicated that SG particles without metal ions were unstable in the gastrointestinal tract. The addition of metal ions inhibited the digestion of the particles in the stomach, with all groups (S0-βc-metals) showing less than 35 % of digestion rates after gastric digestion. TA further strengthened the structure of particle, reducing the protein digestion rate to less than 30 % in all groups (S30-βc-metals) after gastric digestion. Metal ions primarily induce protein self-assembly by interacting with the carboxyl groups of acidic amino acids and amino groups of proteins (Huo et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2024). This may result in the occupation of some digestion sites of pepsin (acting on peptide bonds of acidic/aromatic amino acids) (Cao et al., 2016). Furthermore, the enhanced structural integrity of the particle made it more resistant to disintegration under the acidic gastric environment. Among the groups without TA (S0-βc-metals), the Ca2+ and Mg2+ groups exhibited the lowest gastric digestion rates, followed by the Cu2+/Zn2+ and Al3+/Fe3+ groups, which might correlate with the stabilities of the carrier particles. By extensively coordinating with protein carboxyl groups, Mg2+ and Ca2+ occupied key acidic residues, thereby hindering the recognition and cleavage efficiency of pepsin. After the addition of TA, the Ca2+ and Mg2+ groups still displayed the lowest gastric digestion rates. Notably, SG-30TA-βc-Fe demonstrated a significant decrease in gastric digestion rate due to the formation of high-strength MPNs between Fe3+ and TA.

Protein digestion rate, β-carotene bioaccessibility, antioxidant capacity of S0-βc-metals (A, C, E) and S30-βc-metals (B, D, F).

During intestinal digestion, trypsin acts on the peptide bonds near the aromatic/basic amino acids in SG (Ren et al., 2009), further breaking them down into smaller peptides. In both S0-βc-metals and S30-βc-metals groups, the protein digestion rates gradually increased with extended digestion times. At the end of intestinal digestion (I3), the digestion rates of the S0-βc-metals groups were ranked as follows: Al3+, Zn2+ > Cu2+, Mg2+, Ca2+ > Fe3+. The Al3+ and Zn2+ groups reached relatively high digestion rates of 90.18 % and 91.12 %, respectively, while the Mg2+ and Fe3+ groups achieved relatively low digestion rates of 86.98 % and 83.57 %. Tannic acid, by further strengthening the carrier structure, slightly reduced the digestion rates of the particles. The digestion rates of the S30-βc-metals groups were ranked as follows: Al3+, Zn2+ > Cu2+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+, Fe3+. At the end of intestinal digestion (I3), the Al3+ and Zn2+ groups reached relatively high digestion rates of 88.79 % and 87.23 %, respectively, while the Mg2+ and Fe3+ groups achieved relatively low digestion rates of 77.81 % and 77.59 %, respectively. Protein digestibility indirectly reflects the stability of carrier particles in the gastrointestinal tract. A lower protein digestion rate indicates higher gastrointestinal stability, which is closely related to the disintegration of the particles and the release of β-carotene.

Bioaccessibility of β-carotene

Fig. 8C and D illustrated the variation in the bioaccessibility of β-carotene within the complex particles during the digestion process. β-carotene was progressively released after the initiation of gastric digestion. In the S0-βc-metals groups, the β-carotene content increased rapidly during the early stages of gastric digestion (G1) but slowed towards the end of gastric digestion (G3). During gastric digestion, the highest amount of β-carotene was detected in the digesta at the G3 stage for most S0-βc-metals groups, except for the Fe3+ group. In the S30-βc-metals groups, a common trend was observed in gastric digestion, where the β-carotene content increased initially and then decreased. This result might be attributed to the deactivation of β-carotene caused by the strong acidic and high ionic environment in the gastric fluid. The addition of TA significantly reduced the digestion rate of the carrier particles in the gastric digestion. Therefore, the β-carotene content in the digesta at all stages of gastric digestion (G1 ∼ G3) was lower in the S30-βc-metals groups compared to the S0-βc-metals groups.

During intestinal digestion, most groups in the S0-βc-metals reached the highest bioaccessibility of β-carotene at the end of intestinal digestion (I3), except for the Fe3+ and Cu2+ groups. Studies have shown that carotenoids are sensitive to the pH and ionic environment of the stomach and duodenum, and they are primarily absorbed in the jejunum and ileum through micelle formation with proteins and lipids (Li et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2018). The environment of the intestine and the metal ions released into the intestinal fluid may cause a loss of β-carotene before the end of intestinal digestion. The self-assembled particles induced by Al3+ and Zn2+ exhibited the highest bioaccessibility at the end of intestinal digestion, with values of 19.90 % and 18.61 %, respectively. Because of the lowest protein digestion rate, Mg2+ and Ca2+ groups showed lower β-carotene content after 120 min of intestinal digestion (I3), resulting in reduced bioaccessibility (16.95 % for Mg2+ and 15.89 % for Ca2+). Cu2+ and Fe3+ groups reached only 15.10 % and 8.07 % bioaccessibility at the end of intestinal digestion.

The addition of TA helped to improve the bioaccessibility of β-carotene in the S30-βc-metals groups at the end of intestinal digestion, which was related to the carrier structure and the protective effect of TA on β-carotene. TA enhanced the stability of the carrier particles in the gastric fluid, reducing the release and loss of β-carotene during gastric digestion. Furthermore, the excellent antioxidant capacity of tannic acid also contributed to a better stability of β-carotene. The highest β-carotene content was detected in the micelles of the digesta from the Al3+ and Zn2+ induced self-assembled particles, with the highest bioaccessibility observed at 24.63 % and 22.20 % after intestinal digestion, respectively. Compared to carrier particles formed by physical mixing of β-carotene with proteins (Chen et al., 2020), the particles prepared via protein self-assembly encapsulated more of β-carotene, thereby achieving higher delivery amount. In summary, the oxidative protection from TA and the appropriate structural strength of the carrier particles favored the digestion and absorption of β-carotene.

Typically, the bioaccessibility of carotenoids directly ingested from plants is under 10 %. Encapsulation of β-carotene using metal-induced protein carriers has been shown to effectively enhance their bioaccessibility. It was noteworthy that a large amount of β-carotene residue was detected in the precipitate obtained by centrifuging the digesta, which might account for its still relatively low bioaccessibility. Chen et al., 2020 observed that in a protein-based carrier system, β-carotene released into the intestine adhere to bile salts and other hydrophobic components in the simulated intestinal fluid before recrystallizing, forming microparticles that were subsequently absorbed. The addition of lipophilic excipients to the protein carrier can enhance the bioaccessibility of β-carotene by promoting the formation of protein/lipid micelles. Even the addition of lipids to the in vitro digestion system can significantly enhance the bioaccessibility of β-carotene encapsulated in protein-based particles. (Chen et al., 2020) Other studies also suggest that the triglycerides from regular diet are ideal carriers for β-carotene. When a dietary supplement containing β-carotene is consumed alongside a regular diet, whether in the form of dry tablets or microparticles, it will exhibit high bioaccessibility (Faulks et al., 1997; Faulks & Southon, 2005). Therefore, we recommend taking these carrier particles during meals to achieve greater bioaccessibility.

Antioxidant capacity of carrier particles

As shown in Fig. 8E and F, the antioxidant capacity of the carrier particles increased progressively during digestion, reaching its maximum at the final stage of intestinal digestion (I3). The antioxidant capacity of the carrier particles during digestion might be attributed to three main factors: (I) small peptides with antioxidant properties generated from protein hydrolysis during digestion; (II) β-carotene released upon the disintegration of the carrier particles; (III) the exposure of phenolic hydroxyl groups from TA, which exhibited strong antioxidant properties, upon the breakdown of the carrier particles. In the presence of TA, the carrier particles induced by Al3+ and Zn2+ exhibited the highest antioxidant capacity throughout the digestion process. Therefore, the addition of TA and the appropriate structural strength of the carrier particles play a key role in enhancing the antioxidant capacity of the digesta. Studies have shown that the good antioxidant abilities of phenolic compounds and carotenoids help reduce oxidative damage and inflammation caused by free radicals, promoting the health of various systems and organs, including the intestinal system (Boon et al., 2010; Phan et al., 2017; Quideau et al., 2011). Additionally, the addition of metal ions helps supplement the essential trace elements (iron, copper, zinc) needed by the human body.

Conclusion

This study compared the structural strength of soybean globulin (SG) self-assembled particles induced by six metal ions and their capability to encapsulate and protect β-carotene. Additionally, the structural strength of the carrier matrix was further enhanced with the modification by tannic acid (TA). All six metal ions could induce SG self-assembly through coordination bonds and encapsulate β-carotene, which was evenly distributed in the hydrophobic cavities inside the particles. TA introduced additional covalent and non-covalent interactions, further strengthening the particle structure through the formation of MPNs with metal ions. The interactions between metal ions and SG, the structural strength of MPNs, and the metal ion's degradation effect on β-carotene collectively affected the stability of the carrier particles and β-carotene in various environments. The Mg2+/Ca2+ groups, under high concentrations, formed abundant SG-metal coordination bonds, enhancing particle stability. The Fe3+/Cu2+ groups mainly strengthened the carrier particle structure by forming complex and stable MPNs with TA. The Al3+/Zn2+ groups, due to its appropriate capability to interact with SG/TA and its lower destructive effect on β-carotene, achieved the highest encapsulation efficiency for β-carotene. In the presence of TA, the Fe3+/Cu2+ groups demonstrated the strongest pH stability of carrier matrixes; the Al3+/Mg2+/Zn2+ groups were observed the best stability of β-carotene under extreme conditions; and the Al3+/Zn2+ groups exhibited the highest antioxidant capability and β-carotene bioaccessibility during simulated digestion. By adjusting the modification degree of TA on SG and the choice of metal ions, the self-assembly strength of the carrier particles can be effectively controlled. Thus, the carrier particles can be utilized for different application scenarios, including antioxidant in both acidic and alkaline food matrices, delivery of nutritional supplements, and intelligent food packaging systems.
