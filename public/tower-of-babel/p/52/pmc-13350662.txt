Early Degeneration of a Next-Generation Mitral Bioprosthesis in the Tricuspid Position Treated With Valve-in-Valve
Authors: Silvia Corona, Sebastián Mrad, Reda Ibrahim, Blandine Mondésert, Marco Julien, Louis-Philippe David, Patrick Garceau, Denis Bouchard, Stephanie Tan, Ralph Stephan von Bardeleben, Walid Ben Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBioprosthetic tricuspid valve replacement is increasingly performed, but long-term durability data are limited. The Edwards Mitris Resilia valve, designed for the mitral position, has been used off-label in the tricuspid position, with scarce evidence of outcomes.Case SummaryWe report 2 patients who developed early (<2-year) structural valve degeneration of the Mitris Resilia bioprosthesis in the tricuspid position. Both presented with symptomatic severe intraprosthetic tricuspid regurgitation. Given the prohibitive surgical risk of redo sternotomy, we opted for valve-in-valve (ViV) transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) using the Edwards Sapien 3 Ultra Resilia transcatheter heart valve. Both procedures were successful, with no residual regurgitation or paravalvular leak.DiscussionTo our knowledge, this is the first reported series of early Mitris Resilia structural valve degeneration in the tricuspid position. The cases highlight potential concerns about the durability of bioprostheses in the tricuspid position, especially in congenital heart disease or redo settings. Valve-in-valve tricuspid valve replacement with balloon-expandable transcatheter valves proved to be feasible, safe, and effective, preventing high-risk surgical reoperation. Early intervention is crucial to avoid irreversible right ventricular dysfunction.Key words: reoperation, SVD, tricuspid valve, TTVR, tricuspid regurgitation, ViV

Early Structural Valve Degeneration of the Mitris Resilia Bioprosthesis Implanted in the Tricuspid Position Occurred Within 2 Years of Surgery and Was Associated With Severe Intraprosthetic Tricuspid Regurgitation

Valve-in-valve transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement guided by multimodality imaging was performed, with successful procedural and early clinical outcomes. CT = computed tomography; PVL = paravalvular leak; RV = right ventricle; SVD = structural valve degeneration; TEE = transesophageal echocardiography; TR = tricuspid regurgitation; TTVR = transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement; ViV = valve-in-valve.

We present a series of 2 patients referred for early structural degeneration of the Mitris Resilia (Edwards Lifesciences) tricuspid bioprosthesis. To our knowledge, this is the first such series reported in the literature. As transcatheter tricuspid therapies expand, understanding mechanisms and timing of surgical bioprosthetic failure is critical to optimize lifetime management strategies.

History of Presentation

Patient 1

A 72-year-old woman with chronic atrial fibrillation receiving edoxaban 60 mg daily, prior robotic mitral valve repair (7 years ago), and tricuspid valve replacement (2 years ago) was referred for progressive exertional dyspnea, NYHA functional class II status, and intermittent leg edema.

Patient 2

A 59-year-old woman with a history of congenitally corrected partial atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD) at the age of 6 years and a first surgical redo for mitral and tricuspid valve repair 3 years ago, followed by a second redo for tricuspid valve replacement 1 year after, was referred to our center for a progressive dyspnea on exertion and orthopnea (NYHA functional class III), with right heart failure symptoms and several hospitalizations for cardiac decompensation during the past few months. She was unable to perform daily activities and used to increase diuretics dosage by herself because of a progressively compromised quality of life. At the time of admission, she was on 160 mg/daily of oral furosemide + 100 mg of spironolactone and 2.5 mg of oral metolazone, and her N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide level was 4,100 ng/L.

Past Medical History

Patient 1

Her medical history included robotic mitral valve repair, followed by tricuspid valve replacement with a 33-mm Mitris Resilia bioprosthesis via minimally invasive videothoracoscopy for severe functional tricuspid regurgitation. She required a leadless pacemaker for postoperative third-degree atrioventricular block.

Patient 2

Her medical history included repair of partial AVSD at age 6, first surgical redo at age 57 for mitral and tricuspid repair, and a second redo for tricuspid valve replacement with a 31-mm Mitris Resilia bioprosthesis via resternotomy. The postoperative course was complicated by Dressler pericarditis, persistent atrial fibrillation, dual-chamber leadless pacemaker for third-degree atrioventricular block, chronic kidney disease (clearance: 40 mL/min/m2), recurrent kidney stones, chronic anemia (hemoglobin: 80-90 g/L), and prior maxillofacial surgery. Oral anticoagulation with apixaban 5 mg twice daily was maintained throughout the clinical course preceding the diagnosis of bioprosthetic valve dysfunction.

Differential Diagnosis

In patients with progressive dyspnea, recurrent mitral regurgitation after repair, annular dilatation progression, or endocarditis should be considered. Pulmonary hypertension is another frequent cause in patients with congenital heart disease.

Left atrioventricular valve regurgitation is also common at follow-up in patients with repaired AVSD. A history of tricuspid valve replacement raises suspicion for structural valve degeneration (SVD), though early degeneration is rare. Mechanisms include leaflet thickening, calcification, pannus formation, and fibrosis. Other causes of failure include thrombosis and endocarditis.

Investigations

Patient 1

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed a de novo severe central intraprosthetic tricuspid regurgitation (effective regurgitant orifice area: 0.7 cm2, regurgitant volume: 56 mL) with an effective area at the 3-dimensional planimetry of 3.5 cm2, true internal diameters of 21 × 20 mm, without significant stenosis (transvalvular mean gradient: 5 mm Hg). The mitral valve showed only mild residual regurgitation, with a mean gradient of 6 mm Hg, as expected postrepair (Video 1). The aortic valve was normal.

The assessment indicates qualitative mild systolic dysfunction of the right ventricle, whereas left ventricular systolic function remained normal.

Right heart catheterization showed a right ventricular (RV) pressure of 27/1/11 mm Hg and a right atrial (RA) pressure of 12/14/10 mm Hg (heart rate: 81 beats/min).

Computed tomography (CT) showed the absence of thrombosis or pannus of the tricuspid bioprosthesis, with surprisingly very thin leaflets (Video 2A). Preprocedural CT evaluation with the aid of 3Mensio is shown in Figure 1 (upper panels).

Preprocedural Evaluation on Computed Tomography With 3Mensio

(A) Perpendicular plane with true internal diameter measurements, (B) hockey puck view, and (C) double oblique multiplanar reconstruction with Sapien 3 phantom valve are shown for patient 1 (upper panels) and patient 2 (lower panels).

Patient 2

TEE revealed the presence of a severe degeneration of the tricuspid bioprosthesis with a thickening of the leaflets and a severe central intraprosthetic regurgitation due to a coaptation deficit, with a transvalvular mean gradient of 2 mm Hg and the presence of the Aveir leadless pacemaker at the level of the right atrial appendage slightly protruding a few millimeters above the bioprosthesis, without contributing in any way to its regurgitation (Video 3). No shunt was visualized at the level of the repaired atrioventricular canal, while the repaired mitral valve showed residual moderate regurgitation with a calculated valve area of 2.2 cm2 at the 3-dimensional planimetry. Systolic left and right ventricular function were normal.

Right heart catheterization showed RV pressures of 20/4/8 mm Hg and RA pressures of 16/15/12 mm Hg (heart rate: 80 beats/min).

CT showed the absence of thrombosis of the tricuspid bioprosthesis, with mild thickening of the leaflets (Video 2B). The anatomical proximity of the atrial leadless pacemaker with the tricuspid bioprosthesis and other relevant measurements are shown in Figure 1 (lower panels).

Management

Patient 1

Given the patient's age and 2 previous sternotomies, she was deemed at high risk for a second surgical reoperation, with a Society of Thoracic Surgeons score of 4.3% and a TRI-SCORE of 4 out of 12, so the heart team decided on valve-in-valve (ViV) transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR). The procedure was performed under general anesthesia and TEE guidance. An overfilled (+3 mL) 29-mm Edwards Sapien 3 Resilia Ultra transcatheter heart valve (THV) was implanted under rapid pacing at 180 beats/min via a Lunderquist extra-stiff 35-mm wire guide in the RV, via the right femoral vein. Access was obtained with a 26-F DrySeal introducer sheath (Gore Medical) in the right femoral vein, and the tricuspid bioprosthesis was crossed with the aid of an 8.5-F Agilis medium-curl sheath (St Jude Medical) (Video 4).

Postimplantation right heart catheterization measurements showed RV pressures of 36/5/10 mm Hg, RA pressures of 10/10/8 mm Hg, and pulmonary artery pressures of 39/18/27 mm Hg (heart rate: 70 beats/min). Hemodynamically calculated tricuspid transvalvular mean gradient was 2 mm Hg, and final angiography showed no paravalvular leak, confirmed on the intraprocedural TEE (Videos 5A and 5B). The patient was discharged on postprocedural day 2 on continued oral anticoagulation.

Patient 2

Taking into account the previous surgeries in the context of the congenital background, and the patient's fragility despite her young age, the heart team deemed this patient unsuitable for a third surgical redo and decided to proceed with a transcatheter approach with a ViV TTVR. The patient's TRI-SCORE was 7 out of 12.

The procedure was conducted similarly to that of patient 1. A 29-mm Edwards Sapien 3 Resilia Ultra THV with 4 mL overfilling was implanted. Postimplantation right heart catheterization measurements showed RV pressures of 46/10/18 mm Hg and RA pressures of 25/22/17 mm Hg (heart rate: 84 beats/min). Despite successful valve implantation and the absence of residual regurgitation, right atrial and ventricular pressures increased. This finding likely reflects acute RV afterload mismatch in the context of chronic right-sided remodeling and longstanding venous congestion rather than residual prosthetic valve dysfunction. Another explanation is that an increased left ventricular preload after successful tricuspid regurgitation may have led to an acute elevation of left ventricular end-diastolic pressures, worsening preexisting mitral regurgitation and resulting in pulmonary congestion and elevated right-sided pressures. Hemodynamically calculated tricuspid transvalvular gradient was 1 mm Hg, and final angiogram showed no paravalvular leak (Figure 2). Intraprocedural TEE revealed a postimplantation moderate systolic RV dysfunction and no interference between the ViV THV and the leadless pacemaker (Video 6, A).

Final Right Ventricular Angiogram (Patient 2)

Absence of paravalvular leak after ViV TTVR with a 29-mm Sapien 3 Ultra Resilia (+4 mL) in the failed 31-mm Mitris Resilia bioprosthesis for patient 2. Arrows point to the double leadless pacemaker (atrial and ventricular). ViV TTVR = valve-in-valve transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement.

The postprocedural course was uncomplicated, and the patient was discharged on postprocedural day 7 after optimal medical therapy optimization. Anticoagulation regimen was continued.

Follow-Up

Patient 1

At the 3-month follow-up, the patient was at NYHA functional class I status, and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed good functioning of the tricuspid ViV THV with a mean gradient of 2 mm Hg across the ViV TTVR and a trace of central regurgitation (Videos 5C and 5D). The left ventricular ejection fraction was 55%, and RV function was normal.

Patient 2

At the 3-month follow-up, the patient was at NYHA functional class I-II status. She reported a significant improvement in her functional capacity during exertion and started engaging in some small activities again. She did not have leg edema anymore; however, she reported chronic fatigue. N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide levels went down to 716 ng/L (being >2,000 ng/L before the ViV TTVR). TTE showed good functioning of the tricuspid ViV THV with a mean transvalvular gradient of 3 mm Hg and no regurgitation (Videos 6B and 6C). The left ventricular ejection fraction was 60%, and the RV had mild systolic dysfunction.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported series of early (within 2 years) structural degeneration of the bioprosthesis Mitris Resilia in the tricuspid position.

Conflicting long-term results have been reported after tricuspid valve replacement with a bioprosthetic valve. Previous studies have reported 10-year survival rates ranging from 33% to 68.7%, indicating unsatisfactory long-term results of tricuspid valve replacement using bioprosthesis,1,2 unlike those of aortic and mitral valve replacement. Preoperative liver dysfunction, residual pulmonary hypertension, and decreased RV function are associated with poor long-term outcomes.

Nakano et al2 reported their experience among 84 patients who survived the initial tricuspid valve replacement, 12 of whom required redo replacement for bioprosthetic dysfunction at a mean interval from the initial tricuspid valve replacement of 85.9 months. Only 2 cases showed structural valve deterioration, such as significant calcification or degeneration, after evaluation of the explanted valves. Nonstructural dysfunction, such as pannus formation and native valve attachment, was a more common cause of the dysfunction and was seen in 7 and 5 cases, respectively. The investigators also reported that subclinical valve dysfunction became increasingly common after 5 years, with careful evaluation using echocardiography, and redo tricuspid valve replacement rates increased significantly after 8 years.2 More recently, increasing attention has been given to the concept that bioprosthetic valve dysfunction may represent a continuum ranging from subclinical leaflet thrombosis to SVD, encompassing reduced leaflet motion, hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT), overt prosthetic valve thrombosis, and ultimately structural valve deterioration.3

Emerging data from TTVR cohorts have shown that HALT and reduced leaflet motion are not uncommon phenomena after tricuspid valve interventions and may be associated with increased transvalvular gradients and less symptomatic improvement. Importantly, TTE appears to have limited sensitivity for detecting HALT, whereas cardiac CT provides a more reliable modality for early identification of leaflet thrombosis and restricted leaflet motion.3

A recent systematic review of TTVR studies reported a pooled incidence of valve thrombosis of approximately 15.8 events per 100 patient-years, including both clinical and subclinical forms, despite routine anticoagulation therapy.4

In our 2-patient series, SVD was seen within 2 years after the surgical tricuspid replacement with the Mitris bioprosthesis (Edwards Lifesciences). This valve incorporates the Resilia tissue, a bovine pericardial tissue treated to enhance durability and resist calcification. Surgical advantages include a soft ring and fixed struts, while long-term durability and a potential for ViV procedures are ongoing areas of study. The Mitris Resilia experience for mitral valve replacement highlights successful early clinical outcomes, demonstrating the valve's safety and effectiveness, particularly for younger patients. Its low-profile design facilitates easier implantation. Data from the COMMENCE mitral clinical trial5 (N = 82) showed a 98.7% actuarial freedom from SVD through 4 years.

There are no data or intended experience for the Mitris Resilia valve in the tricuspid position. It was learned through the implant patient registry that a patient with a 33-mm 11400M mitral valve, implanted in the tricuspid position, underwent explantation after an implant duration of 6 months given severe tricuspid regurgitation, thrombus, and stenosis. The explanted valve was replaced with another 33-mm 11400M mitral valve.

However, it is a common practice to use the same available bioprosthesis in the mitral and tricuspid position, irrespectively, and it has been done for decades with the Carpentier-Edwards or the St Jude Medical Epic bioprosthesis, as there is no tricuspid-dedicated bioprosthesis. Furthermore, it has been shown that porcine bioprostheses (such as the Hancock valve and the Carpentier-Edwards bioprostheses) in the tricuspid valve position yield significantly better long-term results than those in the mitral position when the same bioprostheses were implanted in both mitral and tricuspid valve positions in the same patient.6

Recently, a successful case of the world's first use of the Edwards Mitris Resilia valve in the tricuspid position and the world's first use of Edwards Resilia valves in all 4 positions for a carcinoid heart was published by Mujtaba and Clark.7

The real cause of SVD can be difficult to assess in the absence of analysis of the explanted valve, with native valve attachment and pannus formation being common causes of nonstructural dysfunction that are not precisely identifiable using routine imaging. Although both patients in our series were maintained on therapeutic anticoagulation because of persistent atrial fibrillation, thrombosis-related mechanisms cannot be completely excluded, particularly given the known susceptibility of right-sided prostheses to thrombotic events under lower-flow conditions. Moreover, atrial fibrillation itself has been identified as an independent predictor of prosthetic valve dysfunction, potentially related to the loss of atrial contraction and altered right-sided flow dynamics. Whether more aggressive rhythm-control strategies could affect valve durability remains speculative.

The 2 patients in our series present very different backgrounds and risk factors. AVSD and other congenital pathologies significantly contribute to the etiology of tricuspid regurgitation at the index TTVR. Although the most common indication for reoperation after initial repair of partial AVSD is left atrioventricular valve pathology, followed by recurrent left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, tricuspid valve regurgitation surgery is not rare, given leaflet dysplasia, residual clefts, and anatomically related hemodynamic changes. Up to now, only limited data exist regarding the durability of bioprostheses in the tricuspid position in patients with adult congenital heart disease. In these patients, biological tricuspid valve prosthesis dysfunction increases in a linear way, beginning soon after implantation. In the Burri et al cohort,8 34% of patients developed prosthesis dysfunction within 5 years.

Given that, different risk factors could account for the SVD in patient 2, while it is difficult to explain such an early dysfunction in patient 1.

In patient 2, the leadless pacemaker was located in close proximity to the tricuspid bioprosthesis, raising the question of whether device-valve interaction could have contributed to the early dysfunction observed. At present, no evidence supports a direct effect of the pacemaker's electromagnetic field on bioprosthetic tissue durability, as Micra devices do not emit continuous magnetic fields capable of accelerating leaflet calcification or degeneration. More plausible mechanisms include direct mechanical impingement of the device on the valve leaflets or sewing ring, local flow disturbance leading to thrombus or pannus formation, or device-related endocarditis. None of these can be definitively excluded without explant analysis. In contrast, patient 1 developed early degeneration in the absence of any device-valve proximity, suggesting that additional risk factors beyond device interaction likely play a role.

Taken together, these observations highlight the need for systematic surveillance of tricuspid bioprostheses in patients with leadless pacemakers and warrant further investigation into potential device-valve interactions.

In patients with bioprosthetic valve degeneration, transcatheter treatment provides a valuable minimally invasive alternative for those at high operative risk, while also reducing the lifetime need for multiple redo sternotomies. There is an increasing body of evidence supporting transcatheter tricuspid (TT)-ViV as a treatment option in patients with SVD.

Experience in patients with adult congenital heart disease is relatively limited, but various different approaches have been undertaken. In the Valve-in-Valve International Database (VIVID) Registry,9 in which approximately one-half of the cohort had a history of congenital heart disease, ViV TTVR was associated with excellent rates of technical success and outstanding clinical and hemodynamic outcomes at the 3-year follow-up. Additionally, a single-center series study demonstrated favorable evidence of positive cardiac remodeling after ViV TTVR in patients with adult congenital heart disease at up to 5 years.10

The Melody stented bovine jugular vein conduit (Medtronic) and the Edwards Sapien XT and Sapien 3 balloon expandable valve have been used in the right atrioventricular position.

Both our patients had mild or no RV dysfunctions, and this did not change over the follow-up period. The early timing of the intervention is crucial to prevent irreversible RV damage; this is pivotal not only to the TT-ViV function but also to the clinical outcomes.

Finally, although the ViV procedures were associated with excellent early hemodynamic and clinical outcomes, the 3-month follow-up duration remains insufficient to draw conclusions about long-term durability. Given the limited sensitivity of echocardiography for detecting HALT, systematic CT surveillance may be considered in selected cases of early tricuspid bioprosthetic dysfunction, although the long-term clinical significance of low-grade HALT remains uncertain.

Conclusions

Early structural valve degeneration of the Mitris Resilia valve in the tricuspid position may occur within 2 years of implantation. In such high-risk patients, ViV TTVR is a safe and effective alternative to redo surgery, restoring valve competence, improving functional status, and preserving right ventricular function. These cases underscore the need for systematic surveillance of bioprosthetic valves in the tricuspid position and for prospective data on the durability of Mitris Resilia in this setting.
