Local Circadian Clocks Are Desynchronized From the Central Circadian Pacemaker in a Mouse Model of Inflammatory Arthritis
Authors: Lance A. Riley, Luke E. Springer, Sandra Paola Cárdenas‐García, Tatiana Simon, Farshid Guilak, Christine T. N. Pham, Erik D. Herzog
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease affecting joints marked by daily and intermittent surges in inflammatory cytokine levels that result in “flares” of disease activity. Because circadian proteins regulate inflammatory responses in arthritis, continuous behavioral‐ and tissue‐level monitoring may clarify therapeutic dynamics. Using the K/BxN serum transfer arthritis (STA) mouse model, we tested whether locomotor activity and circadian clock gene expression track RA progression from onset through symptom resolution.MethodsC57BL/6NJ and PER2::luciferase mice (8–10 weeks) received 180 μL K/BxN serum via retroorbital injection. Uninjected littermates served as controls. We measured daily ankle thickness and continuously monitored open‐field locomotion from singly housed mice, a subset of which received dexamethasone (DEX) at dusk daily for 6 days. PER2::luciferase bioluminescence from paw, suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCNs), and femoral heads explants from mice treated with or without DEX were compared during STA.ResultsTotal daily locomotion decreased alongside ankle swelling approximately 2 days following K/BxN STA. STA altered the timing of daily PER2 peak expression in arthritic paws but not in the SCN or nonarthritic joints. By day 22, total daily locomotion, ankle thickness, and paw circadian phase had resolved. In vivo DEX reduced circadian phase disruption in arthritic paws, accelerated the resolution of ankle swelling, and increased total daily locomotion without altering locomotor timing.ConclusionLocomotor activity is a real‐time, noninvasive supplement for measuring RA onset and resolution. Altered PER2 expression indicates that local circadian synchrony in arthritic joints is impaired. DEX may aid recovery by resynchronizing circadian rhythms in arthritic tissues.

INTRODUCTION

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic and debilitating inflammatory joint disease affecting approximately 1.5 million adults in the United States with an annual cost of $24 billion. 1 Although the mechanisms leading to RA are not fully understood, interactions between genetic predispositions and environmental triggers are implicated in immune dysregulation that subsequently lead to RA. 2 Because the daily and intermittent cytokine flares that often accompany arthritis progression typically peak in the morning, RA has been described as a circadian disease. 2

Circadian rhythms synchronize physiology and behavior to daily environmental cues to anticipate challenges including food availability and a variety of other stressors. 3 Interacting activator and repressor loops control cell type–specific, circadian gene expression. The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus acts as a central pacemaker, directly and indirectly synchronizing daily rhythms in peripheral tissues to the external environment. The SCN orchestrates glucocorticoid secretion (cortisol in humans and corticosterone in rodents) by the adrenal glands, 4 which in turn coordinates and amplifies the expression of core clock genes across organ systems. Disruption of daily rhythms in the SCN or glucocorticoid secretion increases risk for many diseases in humans, including inflammatory arthritis. 2 , 5

The K/BxN serum transfer arthritis (STA) mouse model recapitulates many aspects of the initiation, effector, and resolution stages of RA.6 K/BxN F1 mice spontaneously develop arthritis because of the generation of autoantibodies against glucose‐6‐phosphate isomerase. Transferring serum from F1 mice to naive mice produces a transient inflammatory arthritis that primarily affects the paws. This arthritis typically lasts approximately 21 days as assessed by serial daily measurements of ankle thickness to score disease activity. 6

Previous work has shown that locomotion declines during arthritis in mouse models, including K/BxN STA. 7 , 8 , 9 For example, Downton et al noted that collagen‐induced arthritis reduced total locomotion without disrupting diurnal rhythmicity 1 week after disease onset using data collected via telemetry. 10 Although important, this study and others use methodologic approaches that are invasive and performed at discrete time points, limiting their ability to fully capture the diurnal nature of this disorder throughout both disease onset and resolution. In addition, exercise modifies RA progression in the K/BxN mouse model. 11 Here, we tested if continuous actigraphy can provide a noninvasive reporter of RA progression with and without therapeutic intervention. We monitored locomotion in the presence and absence of a running wheel using passive infrared sensors to evaluate the entire course of K/BxN STA, with and without dexamethasone (DEX) treatment. We found that total daily activity provides a sensitive and accurate measure of disease state. We further found that inflammatory arthritis leaves behavioral and central diurnal timing largely intact while disrupting the local circadian clock in arthritic paws. Together, these findings support locomotor activity as a practical indicator of arthritis progression and suggest that inflammation reduces daily movement while locally disturbing diurnal timing in affected joints.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

K/BxN serum transfer model

Male K/BxN mice were crossed with female B6.I‐Ag7 mice to generate F1 mice that spontaneously develop arthritis at approximately age 4 weeks. 6 Serum from approximately 50 arthritic F1 mice was collected, pooled, and frozen. To induce STA, 8‐ to 10‐week‐old male C57BL/6NJ mice or homozygous PER2::luciferase (Luc) mice received 180 μL of K/BxN serum by retroorbital injection. Uninjected littermates served as controls. All mice were on a C57Bl6/NJ (Jax: 005304) genetic background and aged 8 to 10 weeks at the time of experimentation. PER2::Luc homozygous mice were backcrossed onto the B6NJ background at least 10 times. Mice were singly housed for 7 days before and up to 22 days after serum transfer under a 12‐hour light/12‐hour dark cycle, with lights on at 7:00 am and off at 7:00 pm, termed Zeitgeber Times (ZTs) 0 and 12. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Washington University in St. Louis.

DEX treatment

A subset of mice received 10 mg/kg/day DEX (Sigma‐Aldrich; D2915) via intraperitoneal injection daily at 4 pm (ZT9) from days 3 to 9 post‐STA as previously described. 12 Sterile saline was used for vehicle control injections. For bioluminescence studies, DEX was given from days 3 to 6. Tissues were then collected for culturing and bioluminescence measurement on day 7.

Ankle thickness

Ankle thickness was measured immediately before serum transfer and every other day through day 22 using digital calipers across the malleolus of each hind paw. Measurements were taken at 4 pm (ZT9) to minimize time‐of‐day variability, and the average of both hind paws was used for analysis. Change in ankle thickness was calculated relative to each mouse's day 0 baseline. Disease onset was defined as the first day at which ankle thickness exceeded 2 SDs above baseline, peak disease as the day of maximum thickness, and remission as return to within 2 SDs of baseline for at least 2 days.

Locomotor activity monitoring and analysis

All mice were monitored continuously with passive infrared sensors (Pallidus MR1, St. Louis, MO) mounted on the side of the cage. Sensors sampled movement five times per second and summarized activity as the percentage of each minute spent active. Some mice were housed with a running wheel to test whether wheel access altered disease measures.

Activity data were aligned to lights‐on on the first day of individual housing. Total activity, diurnal period, and amplitude were analyzed using a chi‐squared periodogram (Rethomics R framework). Daily activity onset, offset, and acrophase (ie, the time of peak daily activity based on a best‐fit sine function with a 24‐hour period length) were determined with ClockLab software (version 6.1.02, ActiMetrics). Using a threshold of 2 SDs below each animal's baseline activity, RA onset and recovery were defined as the first day daily locomotion dropped below and later returned to baseline, respectively. Peak disease by activity was defined as the day of minimum locomotion.

PER2::Luc bioluminescence

To determine whether STA altered tissue‐specific circadian clocks, SCN, paw, and femoral head explants were collected from PER2::Luc mice 7 days after K/BxN serum transfer. Coronal brain slices (300 μm) containing the SCN were prepared on a vibratome (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and placed in chilled in Hanks’ balanced solution, supplemented with 0.01 M HEPES, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 4 mM NaHCO3. We dissected the SCNs with scalpels and cultured them on 0.4‐μm membrane inserts (Millipore). Paws were isolated by removing the skin, tendons, and muscles from the paw pad then removing the paw from the tibia just above the malleolus. Femoral heads were collected after hip dislocation. Tissues were cultured in serum‐free air‐buffered Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 25 U/mL penicillin, 25 mg/mL streptomycin, 10mM HEPES, 2% B27, 0.35 g/L NaHCO3, and 0.1 mM D‐Luc (GoldBio); sealed; and recorded under photomultiplier tubes in a light‐tight 36°C incubator for up to 6 days. Bioluminescence was measured as photons per 180 seconds.

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise noted. Repeated‐measures one‐way analysis of variance with multiple‐comparison testing was used for most analyses (emmeans R package). F tests compared variance, and Welch's t‐tests compared means when evaluating ankle thickness and locomotion as disease measures. Statistical significance was reported as * = P less than 0.05, ** = P less than 0.01, and *** = P less than 0.001. Data for this study are available at https://doi.org/10.7936/6RXS-108365.

RESULTS

Daily locomotion and ankle thickness track RA duration in K/BxN STA mice

To test if continuous monitoring of locomotor activity provides a low‐burden, real‐time assessment of inflammatory arthritis, we measured movement using passive, infrared sensors from individually housed K/BxN STA mice that were treated with either DEX or a saline control (Figure 1A; Supplementary Figure S1). To account for differences in exercise responsiveness, all mice were given access to a running wheel. Following K/BxN serum transfer, saline‐injected control mice exhibited decreased daily locomotor activity for 17.5 ± 1.6 days compared with 9.5 ± 0.5 days for mice given a daily, evening dose of DEX (Figure 1B). Saline‐injected, control K/BxN STA mice displayed an increase in ankle thickness that lasted for 16.0 ± 1.6 days compared with 10.0 ± 0.3 days in DEX‐treated mice (Figure 1C). Unmanipulated, nonarthritic controls did not show decreased locomotion or increased ankle thickness. These results suggest that total daily locomotion closely correlates with RA duration and therapeutic response to glucocorticoid treatment and therefore may serve as a sensitive noninvasive measure of disease activity.

Note the decrease in locomotion a few days after K/BxN serum transfer (vertical dashed line at time 0). (B) Total locomotor activity significantly decreased relative to baseline beginning 2 days after K/BxN serum transfer but recovered sooner in K/BxN STA mice treated with DEX. (C) Ankle thickness tracked disease activity throughout the time course of K/BxN STA including in mice treated with DEX. $ indicates a significant difference (P < 0.05) between K/BxN − DEX and the unmanipulated, nonarthritic control. # indicates a significant difference (P < 0.05) between K/BxN + DEX and the unmanipulated, nonarthritic control (n = 5–6 per group). DEX, dexamethasone; STA, serum transfer arthritis.

Passive locomotion monitoring reveals that DEX, but not running wheel access, accelerates RA resolution

To determine if running influences the dynamics of RA, we treated K/BxN STA mice with DEX, a glucocorticoid used to treat inflammatory arthritis and monitored response in mice with or without access to a running wheel. We found that DEX reduced disease duration following K/BxN serum transfer as measured by ankle thickness (10.9 ± 0.1 days with DEX compared with 18.3 ± 0.1 days without DEX) or total daily locomotion (11.1 ± 0.2 days with DEX compared with 15.6 ± 0.4 days without DEX) regardless of access to running wheel (Figure 2A).

Real‐time locomotor monitoring provides a temporally sensitive measure of disease activity and treatment response that correlates with periodic ankle thickness measurements. (A) Total daily activity (mean ± SEM) decreased when ankle thickness (mean ± SEM) increased, marking the onset of disease (days 0–2, numbers indicate days after K/BxN injection). As ankle thickness increased to its maximum, locomotion declined to its minimum, marking the progression to peak disease (days 3–8). The reversal of ankle swelling coincided with increased locomotion (days after peak disease), marking the resolution of inflammation. Further, these changes in locomotion were also detected in K/BxN STA mice treated with DEX from days 3 to 9 after serum transfer (n = 5–6 per group). (B) Total daily locomotion was as sensitive as ankle thickness monitoring for detecting disease onset in the absence of therapeutic intervention. (C and D) Total daily locomotion was more sensitive than ankle thickness measurements for detecting peak disease symptoms and disease resolution in the absence of DEX treatment and equally as sensitive as ankle thickness measurements in mice treated with DEX. Statistical significance was reported as *P < 0.05; ***P < 0.001. DEX, dexamethasone.

To directly compare the sensitivity of locomotion versus ankle thickness as a measurement of inflammatory arthritis activity, we calculated the days to disease onset, days to peak disease, and days to disease resolution in each mouse. Because we found no significant differences in the timing of disease onset or resolution between mice with or without access to a running wheel (10.4 ± 1.0 days with wheel access compared with 11.8 ± 0.2 days without wheel access at disease resolution; P = 0.20), we combined these data. We found that measurements of locomotion and ankle thickness were similarly sensitive for detecting disease state except that saline‐injected mice reached peak disease and disease resolution earlier based on daily locomotion compared with ankle thickness (Figure 2B–D). These results indicate that monitoring locomotion is easier and more sensitive than monitoring ankle thickness during spontaneous arthritis progression and is as sensitive in assessing therapeutic efficacy.

Diurnal measures of locomotion do not change throughout the K/BxN STA time course

Because RA in humans has been associated with increased symptom severity in the morning, we measured diurnal changes in locomotion of K/BxN STA mice. We found that access to a running wheel but not K/BxN serum transfer increased circadian amplitude of locomotion (Supplementary Figure S2). Period, daily time of locomotor onset, acrophase, or offset did not change throughout the K/BxN STA time course (Supplementary Figure S3). These results combined with our total daily locomotor activity measurements indicate that during K/BxN STA passive infrared sensors placed conveniently on the animal's home cage can detect inflammatory arthritis onset within 1 day, peak of disease at 7 days, and recovery within 21 days. The reduction in total daily activity without disruption of the time of daily activity onset and offset is consistent with a prior report on days 7 to 10 of collagen‐induced arthritis. 10 Home‐cage monitoring also revealed that evening DEX treatment reduced disease magnitude and accelerated recovery. These results indicate that central control of daily behaviors remained largely intact during RA progression and resolution in these mice.

K/BxN serum transfer disrupts the joint circadian clock but not the central circadian clock

We next asked if RA induction impacts circadian coordination of the SCN or joint tissues. We used explants from PER2::Luc mice obtained on day 7 or 21 following K/BxN serum injection. All tissues from K/BxN STA mice showed circadian rhythms, but the time of daily peak PER2 expression in arthritic paws at 7 days after serum transfer showed a wider phase distribution (ZT6.1–ZT14.9 in saline‐injected control paws compared with ZT14.6–ZT15.9 in DEX‐treated paws, P < 0.01) and lower circadian amplitude (0.6 ± 0.1 in saline‐injected control paws compared with 0.8 ± 0.01 in DEX‐treated paws; P < 0.05) compared with paws from saline‐injected control mice (Figure 3A and B). Strikingly, 3 days of DEX treatment decreased the phase variability and increased circadian amplitude of PER2 expression in arthritic paws compared with those of saline‐injected controls. Neither K/BxN serum nor K/BxN serum + DEX altered the amplitudes or phase distributions of daily PER2::Luc rhythms in SCN or hip explants. Disease resolution was associated with a restoration of synchronized circadian rhythms across all tissues measured 21 days after serum transfer (Figure 3C and D). These data indicate that arthritic tissues became desynchronized from other tissues in the body and the central circadian pacemaker during inflammatory arthritis and DEX rescued this desynchrony.

Circadian rhythms were locally disrupted in arthritic paws of K/BxN STA mice and resynchronized by in vivo DEX treatment. (A) PER2::luciferase bioluminescence recordings beginning 7 days after K/BxN serum transfer show disrupted circadian rhythms in arthritic paws (blue, n = 5 saline, 5 DEX) but not in the SCN (n = 5 saline, 5 DEX) or femoral heads (n = 5 saline, 5 DEX) of the same mice. Treatment with 10 uM DEX (yellow) from days 3 to 7 after K/BxN serum transfer resynchronized these daily rhythms without disrupting circadian rhythms in other tissues. (B) On the first day in vitro, arthritic paws from K/BxN STA mice displayed higher‐amplitude rhythms with a smaller phase distribution after 4 days of in vivo DEX treatment (each yellow circle shows the time of peak PER2 from one explant) compared with paws cultured from vehicle‐treated K/BxN STA mice (blue circles). By 21 days after serum transfer, all tissues explanted from K/BxN mice showed high‐amplitude circadian rhythms (C) with tissue‐specific times of peak PER2::luciferase expression (D) regardless of whether the mice received DEX or saline. DISCUSSION

This study examined disease severity, behavior, and diurnal organization in K/BxN STA mice using three complementary measures: total locomotor activity, ankle thickness, and PER2 circadian clock protein rhythms. Consistent with earlier studies showing reduced locomotion in arthritic animals, 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 we found that declines in total daily locomotion provide a sensitive, noninvasive index of disease severity and treatment response, independent of running wheel access. Our results with a passive infrared sensor on the side of each mouse cage support prior reports using implantable telemeters or video monitoring systems. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 The commercially available wireless sensor used here (MR1 from MCCI) provides a noninvasive and inexpensive monitor of in‐cage activity and environmental conditions (eg, light levels and temperature). Importantly, continuous locomotor monitoring effectively measures disease onset, peak, and resolution both with and without disease‐modifying treatments in mice. Although only male mice were used to limit the effect of the estrous cycle on K/BxN STA progression because of the fast time course of this model, these findings align with recent human studies showing that wearable accelerometers can detect disease severity and symptomatic flares in people with RA, particularly by measuring total daily locomotion. 13 , 14 , 15 Future work should be performed to extend these studies in female mice. In both mice and humans, reductions in daily movement appear to track worsening disease, suggesting translational value for passive activity monitoring in inflammatory arthritis.

Our use of continuous locomotor monitoring captures the dynamics of daily and intermittent arthritis symptoms. Using activity monitoring supplements other measures of disease progression such as joint swelling, weight loss, and lowered pain thresholds. Motion sensors provide a robust, high‐throughput, and sensitive approach to real‐time monitoring of RA in both mice and humans. Notably, RA‐induced desynchrony (increased phase dispersion and lower peak‐to‐trough amplitude of daily PER2 expression) between the arthritic paw and the SCN and resolution of circadian rhythms in arthritic paws did not impact diurnal timing of locomotion. Because RA‐induced changes in total locomotion, but not diurnal metrics like time of daily locomotor onset, coincided with ankle swelling and restoration, we conclude that actigraphy in mice can track RA progression but not desynchrony between circadian tissues.

Glucocorticoids such as DEX are commonly used in inflammatory arthritis for their immunosuppressive effects, but they also act as strong synchronizers of peripheral circadian clocks. 4 Combined with our finding that circadian clocks of arthritic joints have a wide phase distribution that is restored in K/BxN STA mice treated with DEX, we posit that the beneficial effects of DEX in inflammatory arthritis may be because of its effect as a circadian synchronizer in addition to its anti‐inflammatory function. This interpretation is consistent with broader evidence linking circadian disruption to inflammation. Future experiments should evaluate whether daily DEX administration that aligns with the circadian profile of cytokine release will show improved therapeutic efficacy in the treatment of inflammatory arthritis. Prior studies have shown that misalignment between central and peripheral clocks can alter tissue‐specific Per2 amplitude 2 and increase inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‐1. 16 Likewise, interleukin‐1 receptor antagonism can protect chondrocytes from inflammation‐induced loss of circadian amplitude and extracellular matrix. 16 Further, mice that lack the core clock gene Bmal1 in fibroblast‐like synoviocytes display worse symptoms following induction of inflammatory arthritis. 17 , 18 It will be interesting to determine if evening DEX and other anti‐inflammatory treatments work via immunosuppression, resynchronizing disrupted tissue‐specific circadian clocks with the SCN, or a combination of these pathways, as has been previously suggested in inflammatory arthritis. 18 Our findings of therapeutic responses to evening DEX should motivate future studies comparing the effects of glucocorticoids on different mouse genetic backgrounds at different times of day. 19 Together, these observations support the idea that local circadian dysfunction may contribute to inflammatory joint pathology.

Research into local circadian clock disruption has largely been conducted via tissue‐specific circadian clock gene knockout models. We found that K/BxN serum transfer disrupted circadian rhythms in arthritic joints without affecting clock gene expression in other tissues including the SCN. Consistent with this, we found that RA induction did not interfere with the timing of daily rest‐activity patterns. Thus, we conclude that, in a light cycle, RA locally desynchronizes arthritic tissues from the rest of the body with little impact on the light‐mediated entrainment or masking of daily rest‐wake patterns. Further, our data suggest resynchronizing peripheral tissue clocks with the central circadian pacemaker in a light cycle may be therapeutic. We thus introduce a novel model system for circadian biologists to interrogate circadian synchrony between tissues without altering the genetic landscape or environmental cues. These findings may also have relevance beyond this specific model. Many human diseases involve organ‐specific circadian changes without mutations in canonical clock. Shift work, for example, is associated with increased risk of RA and metabolic disease, yet the extent to which different tissues become desynchronized in those settings remains unclear. Identifying how communication is disrupted between tissue‐specific clocks in disease states may provide insight into how daily changes in disease symptoms occur and thus how they may be prevented.

In conclusion, continuous locomotor monitoring is a practical and sensitive way to assess disease status and treatment efficacy in inflammatory arthritis. At the same time, our tissue‐level data show that arthritis disrupts circadian synchrony specifically in affected joints while leaving central behavioral timing largely intact. These results highlight the importance of considering tissue‐specific circadian organization in RA and suggest that therapies may benefit not only by suppressing inflammation but also by resynchronizing disrupted peripheral clocks.
