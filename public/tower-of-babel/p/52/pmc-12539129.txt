Breed-specific variations in canine spinal cord anatomy: conus medullaris and dural sac termination and filum terminale internum length
Authors: Amanda Valentino, Rodrigo Gutierrez-Quintana, Natasha Olby
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited research exists on breed differences in conus medullaris termination (CMT), dural sac termination (DST), and filum terminale internum length (FTIL). Dramatic vertebral column variations exist among breeds, suggesting possible differences in caudal spinal cord structures. This retrospective magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study aimed to investigate breed-related differences with three cohorts of dogs. Cohort 1 consisted of 117 dogs without spinal cord compression (SCC) to identify demographic influences. Cohort 2 included 37 Corgis with and without SCC to assess if SCC affects the CMT, DST, or FTIL. Cohort 3 comprised 163 dogs to assess the effects of tail morphology and breed.ResultsIn Cohort 1, heavier dogs had a more cranial CMT and DST (p=<0.001) and longer FTIL (p<0.001, R=0.47). Controlling with L2 mitigated this effect for FTIL (p=0.09, R=-0.16). Age was not associated with CMT or DST, but correlated with FTIL/L2 (p<0.001, R=0.31). Sex had no impact. Most breeds had their CMT at L6. Only Cavalier King Charles Spaniels (CKCS) and Corgis reached the sacrum, while Boston Terriers ended more cranially at L5 or L6. Cohort 2 showed no significant difference in CMT, DST or FTIL/L2 between Corgis with and without SCC (p=0.5, 0.18, and 0.46, respectively). In Cohort 3, weight and tail type affected CMT and DST (p<0.001). Ninety-six percent of normal tailed dogs had a CMT at L6 or L7 while screw tailed breeds ranged from L4-L7 and CKCS terminated at L7 or in the sacrum. These trends were similar for the DST. FTIL/L2 differed between groups with CKCS having a higher ratio and widest range and screw tails the lowest ratio (p<0.001) compared to normal tails. Among breed investigation, CMT differed for CKCS, Corgis, Boston Terriers, English Bulldogs and French Bulldogs versus normal tailed dogs (p<0.001). Similarly, DST varied among breed and by weight (p=<0.001). CKCS and German Shepherds had higher FTIL/L2 ratios, while Boston Terriers, French Bulldogs, and Labradors were lower than other breeds.ConclusionsThis study highlights the importance of considering breed when evaluating the caudal spinal cord anatomy. Variation in the CMT, DST, and FTIL were influenced by weight, tail-type, and breed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05068-1.Keywords: Spinal cord, Conus medullaris, Dural sac, Filum terminale internum

Background

In mammals, the spinal cord terminates in the conus medullaris (CM) and becomes the filum terminale, anchoring the spinal cord to the vertebral column [1]. The canine spinal cord is reported to terminate at the level of the sixth or seventh lumbar vertebra [1]. However, this level is affected by body size, with a more cranial termination in larger dogs, due to the continued growth of the vertebral column after the extension of the neural tube has ceased [2–4].

Three processes are involved in forming the caudal neural tube: caudal neuropore closure, secondary neurulation, and degeneration and differentiation of the secondary neural tube [5]. Secondary neurulation plays a vital role in forming the caudal spinal cord in mammals and develops within the tail bud [6, 7]. During this process, neuroectodermal cells proliferate and condense at the caudal end of the neural tube while mesenchymal cells accumulate beneath the surface ectoderm [5, 6, 8, 9]. This transition leads to the development of the CM, cauda equina, and filum terminale [10]. The ventriculus terminalis forms at the caudal end of the neural tube, providing the site where the CM develops [10, 11]. A fibrocellular structure, the filum terminale internum, arises from the most caudal point of the CM and is surrounded by the dural sac (DS), creating the lumbar cistern. The DS ultimately fuses with the filum terminale internum to form a tough fibrous tether that anchors to the periosteum [1, 10, 12–15]. The spinal cord initially extends the entire length of the vertebral column, but as development progresses, the vertebral column continues to elongate while the spinal cord growth slows [1, 4]. As a result, in adult animals, the spinal cord does not extend along the full length of the vertebral canal [2, 4].

There is little research on differences in the location of terminal spinal cord structures across dog breeds. The vertebrae that make up the tail consists of approximately 20 caudal vertebrae, although this number can vary across breeds, with some dogs having more and others having less [1]. Different breeds show dramatic variations in the structure of their sacrocaudal vertebral columns. Short-tailed (brachyuria) and tail-less (anuria) dogs are reported in many breeds due to mutations in the T-box gene [16, 17]. Other breeds with short, kinked tails known as screw tails (Boston Terriers, French Bulldogs, English Bulldogs) or curly tails (Pugs) have a different genetic background [18]. In a separate study, we found that the Cavalier King Charles Spaniel has a CM and DS that terminate further caudally than expected for their body size, with variability in the length of the filum terminale internum [19, 20]. Another study noted that while the conus medullaris termination (CMT) level varied with size, the location of the dural sac termination (DST) did not [2]. It can be postulated that interbreed differences in the location of the CM and DS might exist. Understanding the location of the CM and DS is important when correlating neurological signs to imaging findings, when attempting to diagnose conditions such as a tethered cauda equina, and when performing procedures such as an epidural or intrathecal injection [2, 21, 22].

We hypothesized that the level of the CMT and DST, and the filum terminale internum length (FTIL) differ between breeds of dogs, independent of body size. This study aimed to describe the vertebral level of the CM and DS as well as the length of the FTIL in dogs of different breeds and to examine the effect of sex, age, weight, and tail classification.

Methods

Subjects

Dogs with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies of the thoracolumbar spine were identified by searches of the imaging database from North Carolina State Veterinary Hospital. Inclusion criteria were pure-bred dogs of any sex or weight, aged over 1 year to be skeletally mature, with an MRI of the thoracolumbar spine including sagittal HASTE and T2-weighted images of the thoracolumbar and lumbosacral spine. The images had to be of suitable quality to view the terminal structures of the spinal cord and DS. The study was excluded if the end of the HASTE signal continued off the end of the image because this prevented measurement of the FTIL.

All dogs were scanned in dorsal recumbency, except for CKCS, which were scanned in sternal recumbency as part of a previous study protocol. In both positions, the pelvic limbs are positioned caudally in a relaxed, slightly frog-legged posture, allowing the lumbosacral junction to rest in a neutral state.

The age, weight, and sex of the dogs were recorded at the time of imaging. Dogs with incomplete signalment at the time of imaging and mixed-breed dogs were excluded.

Cohort 1 - The imaging database was searched for radiology reports that indicated a structurally normal scan of the thoracolumbar spine. Each study identified was visually inspected, and studies with no structural pathology of the spinal cord or congenital vertebral anomalies caudal to T13 were included.

Cohort 2 - Cohort 1 highlighted certain breeds as outliers that needed further evaluation in larger cohorts. These included screw-tailed breeds, but the majority of MRIs performed in these breeds revealed extradural compressive lesions. To evaluate these breeds, we first needed to determine whether compressive lesions at the level of the thoracolumbar junction or lumbar vertebral column altered the termination level of the CM, DS, or the FTIL. Corgis were selected because there was a balanced number of MRI studies that were structurally normal as well as those with extradural compressive lesions. Sixteen Corgis included in Cohort 1 were used as normal dogs with no evidence of TL spinal cord compression. In order for Corgis with compressive lesions to be included, the CM had to be clearly visible on the T2 sagittal images and the HASTE signal had to be present from at least one vertebra cranial to the CM to the caudal limit of the DS.

Cohort 3 - This final cohort was selected to examine dogs with different tail morphologies and specific breed differences. In addition to the dogs in Cohort 1, studies from screw tail breeds (Boston Terriers, English Bulldogs, and French Bulldogs) and the Pug (curly-tailed breed) were identified. No additional dogs from Cohort 2 that had compressive lesions were included to avoid overrepresentation by one breed. Three dogs that had unknown tail status and were not Corgis included an Australian Cattle Dog and two Australian Shepherds which may also be born with a natural short tail phenotype. These were excluded from this cohort leaving the Corgi as the only group that could have a natural short tail phenotype. Based on the analysis of Cohort 2, dogs with extradural compressive spinal cord lesions cranial to the CM were included. Due to the high frequency of dysplastic vertebrae in Pugs and screw-tailed breeds, the second lumbar vertebra (L2) was assessed for congenital malformations. Dogs with congenital malformations of this vertebra were excluded, as it was used to control for body size when measuring FTIL. (Fig. 1)

MRI analysis

All images were reviewed by one author (AV) using eUnity Viewer (V6.10 Client Outlook Inc. 103 Bauer Pl, Waterloo, ON N2L 6B5 Canada). Images were reviewed for sufficient image and sequence quality to be able to identify the location of the CMT, DST, and measure the FTIL and L2. Image identities were masked before performing morphometric analyses in Cohort 1. Once morphometric evaluations were complete, masking was removed, and details on signalment (breed, age, sex, weight) were added.

The CMT was determined by viewing T2-weighted mid-sagittal images of the lumbar spinal cord and recording the vertebral level at which the spinal cord was no longer tapering (Fig. 2) [19]. The location of the DST was evaluated on the HASTE sequences while viewing sagittal T2-weighted images simultaneously [19]. The point where the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) signal on the HASTE sequence terminated was identified as the end of the DS, and the determination of the vertebral level was located using the triangulation tool on T2-weighted mid-sagittal images [19].

If the location of CMT or DST occurred over a disc space, the more cranial vertebra was recorded.

The filum terminale internum length (FTIL) was measured as the distance from the end of the CM to the end of the DS [20]. When needed to accommodate curvature of the spine, the distance between the DST and CMT was measured by connecting straight lines. To account for body size, the second lumbar vertebrae (L2) were measured longitudinally on T2-weighted mid-sagittal images, and the FTIL length was then divided by L2[20, 23].

Statistical analysis

For each cohort of dogs, summary statistics were generated for age, sex, breed, weight, CMT, DST, and FTIL. Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to assess the normality of continuous data (age, weight, FTIL, FTIL/L2). Data that were normally distributed were recorded with the mean and standard deviation. Non-normal data were represented by the median and range. Categorical data were reported as proportions. Vertebral levels were assigned ordered numerical values for ordinal analyses (L4 = 4, L5 = 5, L6 = 6, L7 = 7, sacrum = 8, coccygeal = 9).

In Cohort 1, the relationships between age and weight with CMT and DST were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, and Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was used for FTIL and FTIL/L2. Contingency tables were created to assess the association between sex and CMT and DST, and comparisons were made using chi-square tests. The relationship between FTIL and sex was evaluated using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Due to the small sample size and the large proportion of Cavalier King Charles Spaniels (CKCS) in the intact dogs, only spayed or neutered dogs were included in this statistical analysis.

In Cohort 2, the location of CMT and DST in dogs with and without extradural lumbar compressive lesions was compared using ordinal logistic regression, controlling for weight. FTIL/L2 was assessed using ANOVA, while controlling for age. The same analyses were completed with dogs grouped as either Cardigan or Pembroke Welsh breeds.

A similar approach was applied in Cohort 3 to investigate whether CMT, DST, and FTIL/L2 differed across dogs with different tail morphologies and breeds of dogs. When investigating breed differences, only breeds with at least 10 dogs were included in the analysis, and the rest were classified as other and used as a reference group for analysis.

All analyses were performed using JMP Pro18 (SAS Institute Inc; Cary, North Carolina). Findings were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 level.

Results

Cohort 1–139 MRIs were included. There were 42 different breeds represented (supplementary data 1) and of these, the Cavalier King Charles Spaniel was over-represented due to the high frequency of screening MRIs within this breed. To correct for this, 22 studies were randomly excluded, leaving 16 for analysis (comparable to the other highly represented breed groups). This left 117 dogs for analysis. The median age of dogs was 7.9 years, with a range of 1–15 years. Weights ranged from 4.1 to 62 kg, with a median of 20 kg. There were 8 intact females, 53 spayed females, 3 intact males, and 53 neutered males. (Details in supplementary data 2)

Body weight was significantly related to the vertebral level of the CMT and DST and to the FTIL (p = < 0.001), with heavier dogs having more cranial termination sites and a longer FTIL (Fig. 2). There were 4 outliers in terms of CMT with 2 German Shepherd dogs, 1 Newfoundland, and 1 Boxer having a CMT at L7. When FTIL and L2 were expressed as a ratio, the relationship with weight was controlled for (Fig. 3).

Age was not associated with the level of the CMT (p = 0.25) or DST (p = 0.21). However, there was a small but significant relationship with FTIL/L2 (p = < 0.001, R = -0.31) (Fig. 4). This demonstrated a negative linear correlation, with younger dogs having a larger FTIL/L2 ratio. Since in CKCS the FTIL/L2 ratio was already known to differ from other breeds, and many in this cohort were less than 3 years of age, the analysis was repeated with them removed and the relationship between age and FTIL/L2 remained (p = 0.02, R = -0.24) (Fig. 4) [20].

There was no difference in the vertebral level of CMT (p = 0.20), DST (p = 0.20), or FTIL/L2 (p = 0.31) between neutered males and spayed females. (Table 1). The CMT was at the level of L6 in the majority of breeds. The only two breeds in which the CMT reached the level of the sacrum were the CKCS and Corgi, with the majority of dogs in these two breeds terminating at the level of L7. There were only 4 dogs whose spinal cords terminated within L5. These included one Boston Terrier, one Labrador Retriever, and two Golden Retrievers. Two Boston Terriers were included in Cohort 1, one whose CMT was at L5 and the other at L6; both of which were more cranial than expected for their weight. These findings led us to examine these outlier breeds such as the Boston Terriers, CKCS, and Corgis in more depth.

In the normal tail group 96% of dogs had a CMT at the vertebral level of either L6 or L7. In the Corgi and curly tail groups, each had 1 dog whose CMT was in the sacrum, but otherwise terminated at L6 or L7, similar to the majority of the normal tail group. The screw tail group had a CMT ranging from L4 to L7 with 87% terminating at L5 or L6. Among CKCS, 87.5% terminated at L7, with the remainder terminating within the sacrum. Similar trends were seen with the DST site. In normal tail breeds, 59.7% had a DST at L7 and 25.5% at the sacrum. Curly-tailed dogs (Pugs) had dural sacs that terminated at either L7 or in the sacrum, which could be expected for their weight. Corgi’s DS terminated in either L7 or the sacrum with one dog terminating in the coccygeal vertebrae. CKCS showed a more caudal termination of the DS with 37.5% terminating in the coccygeal region. As with the CMT, screw tailed breeds showed a larger range in DST with 3 terminating at L5 and 1 dog terminating in the sacrum. The remaining 89.7% terminated at L6 or L7 (Fig. 5).

The FTIL/L2 model was significantly different between the tail types although age did not have a significant effect on this model. Corgis were the only group who did not have a significantly different FTIL/L2 compared to the reference group, while CKCS, curly tails, and screw tails were significantly different (Table 5).

Finally, all major breed groups with 10 or more dogs were compared. This included CKCS, Corgi, Boston Terrier, English Bulldog, French Bulldog, Pug, Labrador Retriever, and German Shepherd Dog. All breeds that had fewer than 10 dogs were categorized as other. Our model of CMT was statistically significant, with weight and breed group having a significant effect on the model. CKCS, Corgi, Boston Terrier, English Bulldog, and French Bulldog groups all were significantly different from the reference group, although Pug, Labrador Retriever, and German Shepherd Dog groups were not (Table 6). English Bulldogs had a more cranial termination than the rest of the breed groups with 2 dogs terminating at L4. Discussion

This retrospective study investigated differences in the vertebral level of the CMT and DST, and the FTIL between dog breeds. Our initial focus was on dogs with normal MRI studies of the thoracolumbar and lumbosacral spine to examine the influence of weight, age, and sex, and to identify breeds of interest. Our results demonstrate the importance of body size when considering the location of the termination of the spinal cord and DS and the FTIL but also show differences between breeds with different tail morphologies even after controlling for weight. Finally, age does have a weak negative relationship with FTIL again when controlling for body size.

Our results replicated previous findings of the relationship between body weight and the CM. In contrast to a previous study, there was a relationship between weight and the vertebral level of the DST on MRI. This could reflect a difference in methods between the two studies. The first study determined the DST from mid-sagittal T1-weighted images and on this sequence, CSF is hypointense [2]. By contrast, in this study, the HASTE sequence was used to identify the DST site. CSF is hyperintense on this sequence, providing clear delineation of the DS; it is possible that use of different sequences produces slightly different results.

A study conducted in humans looked at the effect of age and sex on the position of the CM or DS and found no differences between sexes or age-related changes to the position of the DS[21]. The CM did become higher in children as they aged due to the continued elongation of the vertebral canal compared to the spinal cord [21]. Our study population included only dogs that had reached skeletal maturity, and as a result, age was not associated with the location of the CMT and DST. The negative correlation between age and the FTIL/L2 ratio suggests that the FTIL does become shorter with age in dogs. Although this has not been specifically investigated in people, one study showed that CSF pressure significantly decreases at around age 50 and continues to progressively decline [24]. In this study, we utilized the HASTE sequence to measure the FTIL, which appears hyperintense due to the water content in CSF. A reduction in CSF pressure might lead to a shorter FTIL in older dogs due to changes in the hyperintense signal seen.

In previous work we have shown that CKCS have a more caudal termination of their CM and DS than other age and size matched breeds of dog [19]. The study population used here overlapped with those dogs, and our findings were the same. CKCS have a high incidence of Chiari-like malformation and it has been proposed that these anomalies reflect a mismatch between not only the skull and brain, but also the spinal cord and vertebral column [19]. We have also previously demonstrated that the FTIL is shorter in CKCS with CM pain (no syringomyelia) than in unaffected dogs, again demonstrating the potential importance of these structures in clinical syndromes and the need to describe their normal limits in different dog breeds [20].

The formation of the caudal vertebrae and the spinal cord structures is closely linked embryologically. During secondary neurulation, both structures originate from the tail bud [6]. This structure forms at the caudal end of the embryo, resulting in various cell fates that lead to the development of neural structures, such as the CM[6]. In contrast, other cell fates in the tail bud contribute to the formation of the mesodermal cells that give rise to the vertebral structures of the tail [6]. As the tail bud differentiates, the neural and mesodermal components develop in a parallel manner to result in proper differentiation [6]. Fate specification is crucial for the appropriate development of these structures within the tail bud. Due to this embryological link between the caudal vertebrae and the spinal cord caudal structures, both structures develop within close association, and proper regulation of this process results in anatomical accuracy at the caudal portion of the embryo and it is perhaps unsurprising that dog breeds with different tail morphologies exhibit differences in caudal spinal cord structures [6].

Corgis were outliers in our studied population with a CM and DS that terminated more caudally when compared to other breeds of the same size, with the exception of CKCS. Two different Corgi breed groups exist, the Pembroke Welsh Corgi and the Cardigan Welsh Corgi. Cardigan Welsh Corgis are naturally born with a normal, long tail, while Pembroke Welsh Corgis can have either a short or no tail, and those born with a longer tail are often docked to meet breed standard [25]. Given the genetic differences governing tail length in these two breeds, and the difficulty in confirming which breed group the dogs belonged to because of the frequency of tail docking in the Corgi, we compared CM, DST and FTIL/L2 between the two groups and found no difference, so we combined them for analysis. The T gene encodes a T-box transcription factor essential for normal posterior mesoderm development [16, 17]. A mutation (C189G) in exon 1 of the T-box transcription factor affects the DNA-binding properties of the T protein, resulting in the short tail phenotype [16, 17, 25]. This T factor gene is a member of the T box family of transcription factors comprising over 20 genes [25]. In heterozygous dogs, this causes the short tail phenotype that seems to be due to the haploinsufficiency of T protein [17]. The short tail phenotype is not associated with congenital spinal defects if they are heterozygous for the mutation. Still, homozygotes for this mutation result in severe anatomical defects and embryonic lethality [16, 17, 25]. Given secondary neurulation occurs within the developing tail bud, it is possible that alterations in the tail development are associated with differences in the final position of the terminal spinal cord structures in Corgis [16].

Boston Terriers, French Bulldogs, English Bulldogs, and Pugs were examined more closely due to their differing tail morphologies. The majority of these dogs had extradural compressive lesions when imaged, with very few normal MRI studies available for analysis. We therefore investigated whether extradural compressive lesions affected CMT, DST, or FTIL. Corgis were used to examine this question because they had a mix of structurally normal MRIs as well as extradural compressive lesions due to the prevalence of both degenerative myelopathy and intervertebral disc disease in this breed [26]. No differences in caudal spinal cord morphometry measures were noted between Corgis with and without compressive lesions, thus we made the decision to include dogs with thoracolumbar junctional compressive lesions into our analysis, thus allowing the examination of breeds of dogs that are usually undergoing spinal MRI due to compressive spinal lesions. We did, however, exclude any dog with caudal spinal cord or cauda equina pathology that would directly affect the areas being investigated.

Pugs often have congenitally abnormal vertebrae, although their tails are longer than the screw tail dogs with a long, curly phenotype. As such, they are not classed as a screw tail breed [27]. This breed did not differ from reference breeds in terms of their caudal spinal morphometry. By contrast, Boston Terriers, French Bulldogs, and English Bulldogs are genetically related breeds and have abnormal caudal vertebrae resulting in a short screw tail [18, 27]. When controlling for weight, the CMT and DST were more cranial in screw-tailed dogs than other breeds, and their FTIL/L2 was shorter. The screw tail appearance is due to malformed and fused vertebrae with a reduction in the overall number of caudal vertebrae [28]. The DISHEVELLED 2 (DVL 2) gene variant has a high frequency in Boston Terriers, and is ubiquitous in English Bulldogs and French Bulldogs [28]. Wnt signaling pathways play essential roles during embryo development, and the DISHEVELLED genes encode proteins necessary for these pathways [29]. Compromised Wnt signaling can result in abnormal cell fate determination, abnormal proliferation, and abnormal cell movement during embryonic stages, and the effect on the tail bud might also be affecting the caudal spinal cord structures [28, 29]. Differences during secondary neurulation may result in a mismatch between the vertebrae and the caudal spinal cord structures resulting in the more cranial termination we can see among dogs with screw tails.

Labrador Retrievers and German Shepherd dogs were assessed because of the availability of more than 10 studies that had no evidence of compressive pathology in each breed. These dog breeds are similar in size and have long tails. Although German Shepherd dogs do have a higher incidence of transitional lumbosacral vertebrae, these were not noted in any of the dogs included in this study [30].

Our analysis demonstrated that different breeds have slightly different morphometry even in the absence of altered tail phenotype. Labrador Retrievers had a DS that terminated more cranially, and German Shepherds terminated more caudally. They also had significantly different FTIL/L2, with Labrador Retrievers having a shorter FTIL/L2 and German Shepherd dogs having a longer FTIL/L2. German Shepherd dogs are predisposed to degenerative lumbosacral stenosis, which can lead to compression of the cauda equina, leading to lower back pain and neurologic dysfunction [31]. The mismatch between imaging findings of compression of the cauda equina and clinical signs is well recognized, and it is interesting to consider whether the more caudal CMT and DST in German Shepherd dogs predispose them to develop clinical signs associated with compressive lumbosacral lesions.

Clinically, accurate knowledge of the caudal lumbar anatomy is essential when performing lumbar puncture, when making diagnoses, performing surgery and for therapeutic interventions. For example, tethered cord syndrome is associated with caudal traction and immobilization of the CM[11]. When establishing this diagnosis it is clear that using a single cut off for CMT in all dogs would result in inaccurate diagnoses, and instead, dogs’ body size and breed should be considered and establishment of normal parameters in terms of location and movement of the CM for specific breed groups is indicated. Epidural injections are used routinely to control pain in certain types of surgery, and therapeutically for degenerative lumbosacral stenosis [32]. Knowledge of the CMT and DST location will reduce the risk of iatrogenic damage, inadvertent intrathecal injection and treatment failure.

For this study, the measurement methods were based on previously published protocols [19]. [20]. Although we selected the image that best matched the mid-sagittal plane, we did not review transverse images simultaneously to confirm this. This may have introduced minor variability to our measurements. It is important to note that although there are no apparent spinal cord changes on MRI in Cohort 1 or within the normal Corgi group, these dogs all presented due to abnormal neurological examinations. Another limitation was that most breeds had only one or two dogs in their group, preventing a thorough assessment of all breed-specific differences. Given the retrospective nature of this study, we could not assess pedigrees or personally examine the dogs, so we relied on hospital records to confirm breed identification. Similarly, we could not visually confirm tail conformation, relying instead on breed records. Another constraint is that only Corgis and CKCS had HASTE signal that extended beyond the limit of the image because of the more caudal location of their DST. These studies were excluded from analysis, potentially resulting in an overrepresentation of dogs with a more cranial dural sac and shorter FTIL in these breeds. This did not occur in any other dogs included in this study. In the French bulldogs, many MRIs did not fit our criteria due to the frequency with which their compressive lesions affected the HASTE signal around the terminal spinal cord structures. Such studies had to be excluded because the pathology prevented accurate measurements of the DST and FTIL in those dogs. This could have skewed our population to mainly include dogs with more caudal CM and DS unaffected by their compressive lesions.

Conclusions

Weight was associated with the vertebral level of the CMT, DST, and with FTIL. Age in these skeletally mature dogs, was associated only with the FTIL/L2, while sex had no significant effect on any of the structures. Cavalier King Charles Spaniels, screw-tailed breeds, and Corgis showed differences in CMT, while all breeds exhibited variations in DST. Screw-tailed breeds and Labrador Retrievers had a shorter FTIL/L2 ratio, whereas CKCS and German Shepherds had a larger FTIL/L2 ratio. These findings highlight the importance of considering breed-specific differences when evaluating and treating conditions affecting the caudal spinal cord and cauda equina.
