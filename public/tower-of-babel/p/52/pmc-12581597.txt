Supporting healthy weight in times of crisis: results of a one-year community health worker-led intervention among food (in)secure low-income Latinas
Authors: Maud Joachim-Célestin, Brian Distelberg, Samuel Habimana, Eufracina Lizbeth Rivas, Susanne B Montgomery
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic resulted in a major health crisis, one that left the US healthcare system overburdened and with persisting health disparities between certain groups. A group that was and remains disproportionately affected is low-income Latino women (Latinas), especially those with food insecurity. Community health worker (CHW)-led interventions have been successful at improving the results of interventions among disadvantaged populations but their efficacy during times of crisis is not clear. Our purpose was to assess the one-year effects of an intervention for low-income overweight/obese Latinas delivered by CHWs during the first year of the pandemic. Results between food insecure and food secure individuals are compared.MethodsFor this non-equivalent multi-method/mixed methods study, overweight/obese low-income Latinas (N = 62) living in Southern California attended a weight-management lifestyle intervention led by trained CHWs. The intervention began immediately prior to the pandemic and lasted for a year. Hemoglobin A1C and biometric measurements were collected in three months intervals from baseline. Baseline to one-year post results are the focus of this paper. Qualitative feedback from participants is also analyzed and compared.ResultsParticipants’ weight and waist circumference improved (p = .004 and p = .005, respectively) with younger participants experiencing larger improvements. However, those experiencing food insecurity benefited as much as food secure participants at one year follow-up. Weight loss was attributed mainly to social/emotional support and increased knowledge.ConclusionWhile most of their fellow community-members gained weight, obese and overweight low-income, food insecure Latinas enrolled in a CHW-led intervention were able to lose weight and reduce their risk of obesity-related chronic diseases during the peak of the pandemic.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24825-3.Keywords: Latino women, Health crisis, Food insecurity, Weight management intervention, Community health workers, Health disparities, Economic crises, COVID-19 pandemic, Obesity

The peak of the pandemic appears to be behind us, yet some groups, such as Blacks and Latinx populations groups, especially those that are low income, continue to be disproportionately affected by COVID-19 related health conditions such as obesity, a condition which dramatically increases the risk of diabetes, cardiovascular disease and COVID-19 complications. Several years after the pandemic began, women from socially disadvantaged socio-economic setting are particularly disproportionately at a health disadvantage [1, 2].

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, Hispanic/Latinx females (Latinas) living in the US had the highest rate of obesity (for purposes of this article, “Latinx” refers to a person with an ethnic origin from a predominantly Spanish-speaking country in the Americas). During the pandemic, Latinx experienced a higher percentage of job loss, were disproportionately affected by the disease and its complications [3] and were one of the groups most affected by weight gain: when compared with other ethnic groups including Blacks, Asians and Whites, this group had the highest proportion of individuals gaining weight (71% Hispanic vs. 64% Black, 58% White and 54% Asian) [4, 5].

Among women, and especially so for Latinas, obesity has been associated with food insecurity [6, 7]. As one of the predictors of morbidity and a risk factor for all-cause mortality, food insecurity is responsible for 2.5 to 4.5 years shorter lives when compared to those without food insecurity and for more than 50% mortality risk of premature death by age 80 [8]. The association between food insecurity and premature death is stronger in women than men. At age 50, women with high food insecurity have 5.8 years less years of life expectancy than those without, a metric that declines to a 3-year difference for men with and without food insecurity [8].

The pandemic worsened already existing food insecurity related health disparities among Latinas - in particular obesity - and brought to light the need to address related factors more effectively.

After a reduction in the trend in 2021, food insecurity has been on the rise, increasing from 10.2% to 13% of the US population by the end of the year 2023 [9]. As of 2024, Latinx and non-Hispanic Blacks suffer the most from food insecurity. Thus, although the food insecurity distribution has never been even before the pandemic, this gap expanded for certain groups [10, 11].

Factors associated with food insecurity include the state of the economy, policies, household circumstances, and other social determinants of health such as a lower income, lack of health insurance and belonging to certain racial and ethnic groups. Considering how many with food insecurity struggle with obesity, identifying factors or programs that reduce the strength of that relationship including social determinants of health (SDoH) would seem to be an important step toward helping address the obesity crisis.

While issues such as lack of access to healthcare, affordable food or safe places to engage in physical activity contribute to obesity and should be taken into consideration [12] providing viable and sustainable interventions, especially of preventive nature, among those at risk and supporting those most affected is also highly recommended [13, 14].

The gold standard for weight loss programs is delivery in groups or on an individual basis with effects that persist for at least one year [15]. Most weight management programs are implemented in a clinical setting or - at a cost - by private companies. Often, while those with higher income are able to fare well, fewer participants with low-income, low-literacy and food insecurity enroll and those who do so struggle to succeed in these programs due to factors related with SDoH [16]. Indeed, in the few programs available to and accessible to low-income individuals with food insecurity, closing the disparity gap among those with food insecurity remains elusive [17]. As a result, even successful evidence-based lifestyle programs may actually increase the health disparity gap because access and results among food insecure, low-income people – those who need it most - lag behind.

While Latinas present with the highest rates of overweight and obesity in the USA, they are least likely to benefit from available lifestyle/weight loss programs. This is even more pronounced among those with lower income.

Community health workers (CHWs) are a group of lay providers and professionals who have been leading programs with some success among low-income communities. Because they share similar backgrounds and are trusted, they often help participants address challenges and problem solve food insecurity related challenges. Their ability to identify and relate with others of similar background has proven critical. Some of the CHW-led weight-management interventions include interventions for children and adults – both men and women [18–20]. Having CHWs lead a program that promotes a healthy weight for the most vulnerable subset of the Latino population – food insecure women - seems promising in the context of the current rise in obesity.

The Full Plate Living (FPL) intervention adapted for Latinas is a comprehensive approach to lifestyle changes which is relevant and culturally engaging to obese Latinas and was successfully delivered by CHWs prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. The intervention is guided by the popular education framework developed by Paul Freire that “empowers” individuals to have an active part in determining what is best for them [21], and by the Social Determinants of Health (SDoH) framework, a framework developed by Solar and Irwin [22] which takes into account the “conditions in which people are born, live, learn, work, play and age” that have an impact on their health. Thus, participants’ economic status, education, healthcare access, neighborhoods and communities are all considered throughout the intervention.

Results have previously shown that this intervention benefited participants independently of their food security status but were limited in follow-up length to three months, instead of the recommended one-year follow up for clinical impact [16]. The purpose of this pilot study was to explore outcomes of the CHW-led Full Plate Living lifestyle intervention for Latinas for the recommended one-year follow-up time period, which in our case was complicated by the COVID-19 pandemic and the associated lock down. Weight and waist circumference were the primary variables examined. A secondary goal was to explore whether improvements remained among participants during the peak of the pandemic, and if so, whether these results remained after controlling for food security.

Methods

Study participants, recruitment and setting

Recruitment was done via flyers posted at stores, community centers and schools, and through word-of-mouth and personal invitation from community health workers.

Participants (N = 62) were residents of the Inland Empire of Southern California – a region with a high percentage of Latinx - who self-identify as “Latina” and were mono and bi-lingual Spanish-speakers [23, 24]. Inclusion criteria included being at least 18 years old, overweight or obese (BMI≥25 kg/m2), willingness to participate in the study evaluation procedures and attend at least 80% of the Full Plate Living intervention (see description below). Participants were excluded if they were pregnant, breastfeeding, had a BMI below 25 kg/m2, were unable to perform physical activity due to a medical condition, unable to write or read Spanish or English, or unable/unwilling to answer the surveys questions. Individuals who reported using obesogenic medication during the pandemic were also excluded from the analyses.

Procedures

For this non-equivalent multi-method/mixed methods study two CHWs delivered the didactic phase of the intervention (hereinafter referred to as “intervention”) to groups of less than 20 participants at a time, at either a church or a local elementary school, for eight consecutive weekly sessions. They then followed up and provided support to participants for one year. Respondents gave active written informed consent in Spanish or English, depending on their preferred language, before completing data collection. As an incentive, each participant received a $10 gift certificate to a local grocery store at each data collection. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Loma Linda University Institutional Review Board (IRB #518068) As an additional incentive during the last data collections, we offered participants and their families COVID-19 antibody testing since – at that time – no reliable screening tests were yet available. Following quantitative data collection qualitative input from each group was collected at each data point.

Data collection and measures

Pre-post measurements were collected at baseline (just before the intervention) and at three months intervals up to approximately one year post the intervention by trained CHWs and 1 research staff (of note, due to the pandemic, a number of participants were not reached until 14 months or so after the original intervention but for clarity’s sake we refer to all as one year post results). This paper focuses on baseline and one-year results. Biometric measurements included weight, height, waist and hip measurements. Hemoglobin A1C was also measured. Once the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC] defined specific pandemic precautions, all measurements were collected following a detailed protocol developed with CDC guidelines [25]. Due to lingering COVID-19 fears, we performed all labs outdoors and used extra caution, especially when having to provide participants with any type of written material.

Basic demographic information (age, relationship status, children, household size, education, employment, annual household income and health insurance) was collected at baseline via self-reported surveys. Questions pertaining to food security, acculturation, depression and anxiety were also assessed at baseline. The entire survey can be found in a previously published article [16].

An additional survey question (described as “qualitative question” for purposes of this paper) requesting participants’ feedback about the intervention and follow-up period was added and completed by participants every three months once the pandemic began, to better evaluate the needs and progress of participants. Given the pilot feasibility nature of our study, we wanted to explore implementation and delivery issues further and conducted 2 focus group discussions among participants at the one-year mark: one for those who lost weight and one for those who gained weight. In addition, CHWs kept detailed field notes throughout the project which we also included in the qualitative analyses. To reduce the risk of infection from the COVID-19 virus, paper surveys were replaced with online surveys (all key variables remained the same) using the Qualtrics software (Qualtrics, Provo, UT, 2020).

Biometric measures

The biometric measures used in this study include glycated hemoglobin, height, weight, body mass index and waist and hip circumferences as described below:

Glycated hemoglobin (Hemoglobin A1C)

Blood glucose was measured by collecting a drop of blood from a finger prick using Alere Afinion AS100 Analyzer and Alere Afinion HbA1c (Axis-Shield, Oslo, Norway) assay tests. Results were reported in percentage.

Height

Participant’s height was measured to the closest ½ inch with participant standing without shoes nor headwear against and in front of Seca portable stadiometer (Seca North America, Chino, CA) with eyes looking in front and with arms to the sides.

Weight

Using the Inbody 270 (InBody USA, Cerritos, CA) Body Composition Analyzer, participants were instructed to empty bladder, remove metal accessories and heavy clothing and to stand straight and tall without wearing shoes. Weight was recorded in lbs.

Body mass index (BMI)

Body mass index was read from the InBody 270 (InBody USA, Cerritos, CA) Body Composition Analyzer and confirmed by calculations based on the formula "weight (lbs) x 703/(height (inches)2" and was reported in kg/m2.

Body mass index (BMI) category

Categories of BMI were determined based on the following cut-off: participants with a BMI between 25 kg/m2 and 29.9 kg/m2 were classified as overweight, those between 30 kg/m2 and 34.9 kg/m2 were classified as having Obesity Class I, those between 35 kg/m2 and 39.9 kg/m2 were classified as having Obesity Class II, those with a BMI of 40 kg/m2 or above were classified as having Obesity Class III [26].

Waist circumference

With participant standing and having an empty bladder, waist was measured by wrapping a measuring tape snugly around the narrowest part of the abdomen, approximately 2 inches above the navel and reading the measurement immediately after asking participant to exhale. The circumference was reported in inches (to the nearest ¼ inch).

Hip circumference

This measurement was assessed by wrapping a measuring tape snugly around the widest part of the hips while participant is standing with hands to the sides, empty pockets and no thick clothing on hips. Hip circumference was reported in inches (to the nearest ¼ inch).

Waist-hip ratio

This measure was calculated by dividing waist circumference by hip circumference and reported with up to two decimals.

Demographic measures

Demographic data included age, relationship status, number of children, household size, maximum education, employment status, health insurance as well as information pertaining to acculturation and mental health.

Age

Age was determined based on the response to the following question: “What is your age?” and was reported with no decimals.

Relationship status

Participants responded to the question “What is your relationship status?” and selected one of 4 choices: single, married or living with partner, divorced or separated and widow(er). The last 2 categories were merged during analyses.

Number of children

The number of children was determined based on the response to the following question: “How many children – sons or daughters – do you have?”. The six choices of answers included 0 (no son or daughter), 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or more children.

Household size

The household size was determined based on the answer to “How many people live in your house, including yourself?” Five options were available: only 1 (myself), 2, 3, 4, 5 or more living at home.

Maximum education

Maximum formal education whether in their country of origin or in the USA was determined by the response to the question “What was the last grade that you completed at school?” Response categories included: “No schooling”, “Elementary School”, “Some high school”, “High school diploma”, “Vocational”, “Some college”, “College or University degree” and “other”. During analyses, the latter category was merged to one of the 7 previous options that was the best fit.

Employment status

Employment categories were determined by asking “Which of the following best describes you or your job situation?” The 7 answer choices included “Employed for wages”, “Out of work”, “Retired”, “Unable to work due to disability”, “Volunteer”, “Self-employed (working for yourself)” and “Other (please specify)”. For analyses, all categories were merged into either “employed or “unemployed”.

Family income

Family income reported household annual income as determined based on the response to the following question: “What is your current family level of income?” Five options were given: “I don’t know”, “less than $21,000”, “between $21,000 and $50,000”, “between $51,000 and $75,000”, and “more than $75,000 annually”. The first option (“I don’t know”) was reported as missing data. We determined the categories and ranges based on other studies of populations similar to ours, where exact income can sometimes be difficult to determine [27–30].

Food security status

Food security status was determined using the responses to the following two statements from a validated scale: “Within the past 12 months we worried whether our food would run out before we got money to get more” and “Within the past 12 months we didn’t have money to get more food.” Three choices of answers are given: “never true, “sometimes true” and “often true”. Either one of the last 2 choices qualified participant as food insecure [31]. Although a range is the recommended screen test for food (in)security and is available in Spanish [32] we chose the 2-item screener option to keep questions for our low literacy participants to a minimum.

Health insurance

Health insurance category was determined based on the response to the following question: “What type of insurance do you have?” Options included “I currently do not have any health insurance”, “Medical (IEHP or Molina)”, “Medicare (65 years or older, SSD or disability)”, Private Insurance”, “Other (please specify)”. Responses were merged into 3 categories: No insurance, Federal or government insurance, Private insurance.

Acculturation Score

Acculturation level was based on the response to the validated Brief Acculturation scale for Hispanics (BASH) by Mills, et al. [33], which was translated and adapted from the validated original English scale by Norris, Ford, & Bova [34]. Acculturation scores were divided into 2 categories with scores equal or less than 3 indicating a low level of acculturation.

Birth country

Birth country was determined based on the response to the following question: “In what country were you born?” For analyses, countries in Central and South America were merged.

Years living in the USA

This measurement was determined based on the response to the question: “How long have you lived in the US?”. For analyses, responses were separated into 5 categories: “1–5 years”, “6 to 10 years”, “11 to 15 years”, “16 to 20 years” and “21 or more years”.

Depression

Depression level was determined based on the responses to the validated Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) which is a 4-point Likert-type scale which has also been validated in Spanish [35, 36]. Response options for each of the nine questions ranged from “0” (not at all) to “3” (nearly every day) points for a maximum of 27 points. The scores are interpretated as follows: 0 to 4 is categorized as minimal depression, between 5 and 9 is considered mild depression, between 10 and 14 is considered moderate depression, a score between 15 and 19 is categorized as moderately severe depression and any score above 19 is classified as severe depression. It is generally accepted that a score above 14 warrants treatment for depression.

Anxiety

Level of anxiety was determined by using responses from the validated 4-point Likert-type generalized anxiety disorder questionnaire (GAD-7) which has also been validated in Spanish [37–39]. Response options for each of the seven questions ranges from “0” (not at all) to “3” (nearly every day) points for a maximum of 21 points. A score of up to 5 is considered minimal anxiety, a score between 5 and 10 is categorized as moderate anxiety and 15 or above is considered severe anxiety. The cut-off of 10 usually warrants further evaluation.

Qualitative data collection

Qualitative data collection was obtained from two focus groups and from one survey question as well as field notes completed by the CHWs throughout the year. Focus group participants were selected purposively: program participants who gained weight and those who lost weight were contacted and asked whether or not they would be willing to participate in a focus group discussion. Questions for the focus group guides were partially created based on field notes and written participant feedback and allowed us to explore the perceived impact of the program, barriers and facilitators of weight loss as well as specific aspects of the program. Probes were used to expand exploration and to help identify new issues. According to concurrent analyses, data gathered from the FGDs reached saturation.

One open-ended question was asked as part of the post-survey to determine what aspects of the program stood out most to participants by asking each participant “What did you like most about the program?” (see Supplementary Table 1 for complete post-survey). The same question was repeatedly asked at five data points: immediately after the intervention, then at 3-months, 6 months (summer 2020), 9 months (fall 2020) and finally at 1-year post-intervention (March 2021). See Table 1 for a complete list of qualitative questions.

Quantitative/Statistical analyses

For the test-retest evaluations, we assumed a moderate effect size (D = 0.5) and 1-β = 0.80, and it was determined that a sample of 27 in total would be sufficient. Before analyses, all data were inspected for inconsistencies and outliers and checked for accuracy and possible errors in the transfer of handwritten notes.

All quantitative data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27. Data were assessed for univariate assumptions and frequency and pattern of missing data. All data met the assumptions with the exception of the hemoglobin A1C measure which exhibited significant kurtosis. All resulting univariate analyses employed a log transformation of the A1C variable which measured an acceptable kurtosis (< 2.00). Missing data ranged from 5 to 8% but was considered missing at random (Little’s MCAR Test = 43.15, df = 47, p =.63). Case-wise deletion was used and follow-up pairwise comparison showed no significant difference between outcome measures across missing data from attrition.

The analysis began with a simple univariate repeated measures t-test, followed up with a generalized linear modeling approach to adjust the pre and post means (e.g. A1C, weight, BMI, waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio) for the covariates of age, marital status (married versus not married), number of children in the home, education level, and whether the participant began the study as food insecure, or not food insecure.

Analysis of qualitative data

Two focus group discussions were held at one year (March 2021) in Spanish by two trained bilingual interviewers via Zoom. One focus group (N = 10) was composed of participants who lost weight and lasted 1h 30 min. The other group (N = 8) was composed of participants who gained weight and it lasted 1h 32 min. Data were coded via line-by-line emerging coding by two independent CHW coders and final codes, themes and subthemes decided in coding meetings with conflict coders (lead authors). We used the 2020 version of computer software program MaxQDA® [40] to code and analyze transcripts. In addition, for each group (food insecure, food secure) written responses to the question were compiled, translated (if originally written in Spanish), tallied manually and organized by themes and subthemes and by time of collection: immediately after the initial intensive eight weeks (post 1), a month after completion of the intensive program (post 2), in summer 2020, in October-November 2020 and in March 2021. Field notes from the CHWs were also taken into consideration to determine context. All themes were verified by researchers to check for any misinterpretations or errors using constant comparison.

The intervention

The Full Plate Living intervention for Latinas is an adaptation of the Full Plate Diet [41], a program that consists of encouraging participants to progressively replace calorie-dense low fiber foods with fiber-rich low-caloric options (“super-foods”), drinking only water instead of liquids with calories, and engaging in moderate physical activity. No food is prohibited; no calories are counted. Instead of portion sizes, the emphasis is on food proportion (3:1) of recommended foods vs. non-recommended foods.. Furthermore, participants do not have to go through the effort of determining which foods are recommended as these are color-coded (name and pictures) for easy identification in a booklet. The program also provides tips for food preparation on a small budget, shopping for healthy foods, eating out and addressing weight management challenges. The intervention is a cultural adaptation created by a team of community health workers, medical students, community members and researchers for delivery by CHWs as an eight-week lifestyle-based weight management program. The adaptation also includes evidence-based stress management techniques based on the Community Resiliency Model [42]. Some of the interactive components of the program include participants preparing easy-to-make healthy meals using their cultural dishes and foods and helping one another in support group settings. Besides facilitating the sessions, CHWs (trained for 60 hours) gave each participant a tape measure for self-monitoring, called participants, encouraged them and facilitated support groups. Telephone coaching was implemented with CHWs calling or communicating with participants via text weekly or bi-weekly, encouraging participants to engage in social media posts or social networking among themselves. More details on this intervention have been published elsewhere [16].

Adaptations to the Full Plate Living follow-up period during the pandemic

Once the COVID-19 pandemic began, more adaptations became necessary to facilitate the planned one-year participants follow-up period. This forced our team to become even more creative and to spend a large amount of time in activities that were critically relevant to social determinants of health but were not originally intended or planned or directly related to the original program.

For instance, for self-monitoring of weight, a scale was placed at a location easily accessible to most participants for them to weigh themselves, as needed. Although not as accurate as In-body measurements, it allowed some degree of self-monitoring besides the tape measure.

Monthly meetings were now held via zoom and WhatsApp to allow us to answer participants’ questions (including specific questions about COVID-19) and provide them with a booster to the intervention. Furthermore, the CHWs became a resource for several of the participants who were struggling to find food for their family members, and we networked with local organizations to help them with access to food boxes, information and updates about food banks and similar resources. Lastly, to facilitate social networking and reduce isolation during the holiday season a Holiday “Secret Sisters” exchange was held and facilitated by one of the CHWs - between December 11 and March 25. This exchange consisted of sending one’s “secret sister” encouraging notes, or dropping off a special small gift for her at a pre-defined location based on the information provided about that “sister” (birthday, challenges she faces, favorite color, flower, hobby and meals, family composition). At the end of the 3-month period, “secret sisters” would reveal themselves at a culminating party. A subset of 13 participants volunteered to participate in this holiday exchange.

Results

Participant baseline characteristics

While we originally recruited 89 participants, we were able to retain 74 over the course of the year (83% retention). However, due to logistical challenges of the pandemic, complete demographic data and pre-post was only available for 62 participants. Table 2 describes participant baseline characteristics for this paper’s participants. Of note, there were no statistically significant differences between those included in the study and baseline participants. All participants were female and 66.1% were obese, with all but one having a waist circumference of more than 31.5 inches (a cut-off used to determine risk of chronic diseases). Most (83.8%) were married and had at least 3 children (72.7%). Formal education was relatively low with 8.1% having never attended school and 45.2% never earning a high school diploma. A little over three quarters (77.4%) were unemployed, 56.1% had no health insurance and 40% had food insecurity at baseline. Lastly 90.3% were born in Mexico and almost all (96.8%) had a low acculturation rate, despite most reporting living in the USA more than 11 years. While not the focus of this paper many participants struggled with mental health, with average depression scores of 6.12 and anxiety scores of 4.48. Not included in Table 2, baseline comparison between food security groups revealed that those with food insecurity tended to be younger (45.92±9.01 vs. 51.70±11.96, p =.04) and have more anxiety (7.15 ±6.02 vs. 2.91 ±3.15, p =.007) than those without food security.

Quantitative results - intervention effects

Based on the univariate t-test comparisons biometric measures for weight (mean difference = −3.33lbs, t = 3.00, p =.004), waist circumference (mean difference = 1.54, t = 2.92, p =.005) and waist-hip ratio (mean difference = 0.02, t = 2.31, p =.03) showed significant improvement (see Table 4). There was also a non-statistically significant trend towards lowered (improved) hemoglobin A1C (mean Difference = 0.01, t = 1.78, p =.08). After controlling for age, marital status, number of children, level of education and food security, the same changes were seen in weight, waist circumference and waist-to-hip Ratio (see Table 3). Furthermore, a within-subject contrast comparing pre and post weight revealed a significant effect of age: F (1, 50) = 4.43, p =.04. Food insecurity status impacted the effects on weight - F (1, 50) = 6.97, p =.01, partial ŋ2 = 0.12 - and waist circumference – F (1, 40) = 4.04, p =.05 - with partial ŋ2 being 0.09 for the latter. To this end, participants with greater reductions in weight and waist circumference were those who were reportedly food insecure at time of enrollment (see Fig. 1; Table 3).

Qualitative results

Focus group discussions were held in Spanish with participants who lost weight and participants who gained weight and were audio-taped and transcribed verbatim and translated by bilingual CHWs and research assistants after ensuring participants’ permission. Probes were added to the questions and – upon analyses - it was determined that the discussions reached saturation The transcripts were then analyzed inductively for emergent themes using the grounded theory (Charmaz, 2006) supported by critical quotes using the 2017 version of computer software program (v.12, Berlin, GDR) to code them. Aside from applying a grounded theory approach to analyses, no theoretical framework was used for the development of the themes as it was a follow-up of the written responses given by all participants.

For the written survey question, participants were all asked to answer as best as possible the qualitative question in writing and were given ample time to respond to the survey, including the qualitative question. The written answers were analyzed through thematic analysis.

Lastly, field notes were organized by CHWs as part of their routine observations and as a way to keep track of issues and have reliable references to report from.

Upon analyzing the focus group discussions, responses to the written qualitative question, five main themes stood out as can be seen in Fig. 3 (and Supplementary Table 2). These were contextualized over time and by food security status: (1) Emotional/social support and interaction; (2) Knowledge acquisition (theoretical or practical); (3) Tangible resources, including food and gift cards; (4) Perceived personal responses to the intervention (motivation to engage in healthy behavior, and behavior modification); and (5) Access to health assessments (lab tests, BMI, etc...).

Food secure and insecure participants' changing perceived benefits of the intervention during COVID-19

Emotional support was by far the most mentioned aspect of the intervention to be appreciated. Those with food insecurity showed appreciation for emotional and psychological support from the beginning and in greater proportion in summer - compared to those without food security, a group that only began expressing their appreciation for this factor at that time. They also progressively acknowledged help with practical resources and appreciated social interaction throughout the entire year, as opposed to those who were food secure. By the end of the year, it was evident that both groups were very appreciative of the emotional support received during the intervention and follow-up period. For context, refer to Fig. 4, a visual representation of local mortality trends during the first year of the pandemic in San Bernardino and Riverside counties, the two counties where most participants resided.

COVID-19 cases and deaths in San Bernardino and Riverside counties during year 1

Participants mentioned social support received mainly from CHWs via telephone calls and texts, but also from each other (including informal support groups) and other staff members involved in the project:

“…the calls from CHWs asking how we are and encouraging us.”(FS, 43 years old).

“…that the CHWs kept in touch with us. We were not just another number.” (FS, 65 years old).

Repeatedly, the concept of not having been forgotten was brought up:

“…I did not feel alone”(FI, 42 years old).

“…the unconditional support of the CHWs and of the fellow classmates” (FS, 50 years old).

“…they did not abandon us” (FS, 51 years old).

There was also the sense that the CHWs were accessible and an appreciation for them intentionally reaching out to them on a continuous basis:

“…I felt supported and that I could call and ask for advice at any time” (FS, 48 years old).

“…they are interested in our welfare”(FI, 29 years old).

“they kept supporting us emotionally” (FI, 54 years old).

Throughout the entire year, participants appreciated the new acquisition of knowledge, both theoretical and practical. Although both groups seem to have expressed as much appreciation for newly acquired information throughout the year, as the pandemic intensified, they expressed more appreciation for the information specifically about the pandemic and how to “protect” themselves from the ill-effects of the virus we provided.

Thus, statements went from acknowledging “…instructions about eating healthy”(FS, 82 years old) and “how to create plates” (FI, 46 years old) at post 1 and post 2, to “stress management” (FI, 42 years old) in Summer 2020 and being “…informed about COVID and how to protect [our] bodies by eating healthy and exercising” (FS, 45 years old) and “…you clarified any doubts that we had…”(FI, 54 years old) in Fall 2020 and “…receiving information that was relevant to what was happening” (FS, 53 years old) by Winter 2021.

Because of the need for social distancing, one of the ways that participants were able to connect with each other and with the CHWs became the internet. Participants appreciated that the team was willing to use technology (we trained some to do so) to keep them socially connected with each other and to inform them of new developments in the healthcare system that could be useful to them:

“…they found a way to communicate with us via zoom” (FS, 47 years old).

“the zoom meetings with critical information to help us overcome the pandemic” (FI, 61 years old).

In regards to personal responses to the intervention, several participants felt that the program had led to positive changes within themselves (increased self-motivation, feeling of empowerment, a desire to spend more time with family and preparing healthy meals): “…motivation to eat more fiber”(FS, 51 years old) and their new “…ability to practice… healthy recipes,”(FS, 45 years old) “…I was able to identify my emotions”(FI, 47 years old). Participants with food insecurity expressed gratitude for behavior modification patterns they noticed early on whereas these changes were not mentioned by those without food insecurity until several months later.

As part of the program, several tangible resources were made available to participants: information about food banks and locations providing vaccination especially as information became available about the pandemic. Educational lectures were also provided as more information became confirmed and available. Some of the more tangible resources included gift cards donated by local organizers and food baskets given to participants needing it the most. Appreciation for these resources was especially expressed by those with food insecurity beginning in the summer of 2020, increasing during the fall of 2020 and peaking at 1-year. Whereas mention of food by the food secure group was minimal, those who were food insecure repeatedly referred to the food that they received:

“…they brought us food and sent us resources” (FI, 56 years old).

“the help with food, the gift cards and resources” (FI, 75 years old).

Lastly, having access to health assessments was mentioned more as the pandemic advanced, especially by those without food insecurity. Letting them know about their body seemed to have been perceived as evidence of care:

“that they kept checking our blood levels” (FS, 37 years old).

“they were always paying attention to us with labs…” (FS, 41 years old).

By the end of the year, four of the emerging themes remained with emotional support surpassing all others. However, access to tangible resources remained, more important for the food insecure than for those without food insecurity. Focus groups transcripts analysis consisted of developing an emergent code book and intentionally coding to allow new codes to emerge.

When asked what they liked most about the program in focus group settings at the end of the year, participants mentioned receiving relevant and timely information about healthy behaviors and about COVID-19, learning about stress management, receiving reports of their blood and biometric measurements as well as the emotional support from participants and mostly from the CHWs. They were grateful for the consistent genuine interest in their well-being, something they perceived due to the time and attention they received, as well as the practical resources. Participants who lost weight attributed their success to behavior modification (dietary choices, exercise), application of stress (CRM) management techniques and social support from the program. Participants who gained weight attributed their weight change to obesogenic medications they were receiving and health issues (pain, thyroid, mental health). Lack of family support was also mentioned by this group. See Supplementary Table 3.

Discussion

Chronic diseases such as obesity and related conditions impose a significant burden on the healthcare system. Prioritizing prevention by promoting lifestyle behavioral changes can significantly reduce the financial burden brought about on the healthcare system by these chronic diseases, especially during times of crises. In this long-term evaluation of our Full Plate Living pilot study for Latinas, we assessed the results of a lifestyle-based weight management intervention which began just before the start of the pandemic with follow-up during the first year of the pandemic. Despite the pandemic and its many related challenges, participants benefited from the program by losing weight and reducing their waist circumference and waist-hip ratio by the end of the one-year follow-up year. These results remained statistically significant even after controlling for age, marital status, number of children, education and food security.

The proportion of individuals who lost weight (70%) in our program is important, considering that only 15.6% of Latinos attending the National Diabetes Prevention program lose weight (NDPP). Furthermore 14% of our participants reached the 5% weight loss (a threshold used by the NDPP at the one year follow up mark). Of those who lost weight, 85% lost more than 1 kg, a weight loss known to lower risk of diabetes by 16% [43]. Although the average weight loss was 3.127 (SE = 1.42) lbs or 1.82 (SE = 0.65) % of the average initial weight, maximum weight loss was 28.50 lbs with 11% losing more than 10 lbs. Weight loss of more than 10 lbs has been shown to significantly improve cardiovascular health. The final waist measurements were also important with statistically significantly lower circumference at 1 year compared to baseline and 67% of participants reducing their waist circumference (i.e., visceral fat). Eliminating visceral fat (vs. reducing BMI) is increasingly considered a better indicator of reduction in cardiovascular disease and/or diabetes risk, especially in this population [44–47]. Thus, it seems that our intervention had a protective effect on obesity-related health conditions for at least 2/3 of the participants for as long as 14 to 15 months after the intervention was delivered.

Based on field notes from the CHWs (confirmed by the New York Times), the last assessment occurred during the worst months of the COVID-19 pandemic (several of the families experienced the loss of loved ones, in addition to job loss), with timing of the last labs coinciding with the highest peak of deaths in the two counties where our population resided. Still, participants continued to attend program activities on-line and - much to our surprise and delight - were willing to have us conduct the labs (though with many precautions such as the use of protective gear, social distancing participants waiting in cars until it was “their” time).

These positive results should be taken in the context of the USA where obese Latinx showed trends towards weight gain during the first year of the pandemic. Indeed, weight gain for Latinx’ living in this country was reportedly more than for other groups during that time period [4, 5]. In our study, the program seems to have mitigated potential pandemic weight gain for both the food secure and the food insecure groups. Indeed, those who were food insecure had relatively better results. Furthermore, those who had a stronger, more consistent support system (participants in the Holiday “Secret Sisters” exchange) during the last months of the study seem to have fared better than their counterparts.

Several studies report improvements among individuals enrolled in interventions led by CHWs [48]. However, it is difficult to interpret the results, partially because those interventions faced challenges during the COVID-19 pandemic [49]. A few studies followed individuals during the first year of the pandemic and assessed associations between weight gain and other factors.

One study in the USA among 55 individuals (mostly non-Hispanic Whites) enrolled in a weight management program during the pandemic reported that the majority of participants (58%) gained weight (16% gained more than 10 lbs) and 27% kept their weight, while only 15% lost weight during the first 3 months of the pandemic. The authors found an association between participants’ weight gain and loneliness, sedentary behavior and working remotely, while planning/tracking food consumption, and staying active helped avoid weight gain [50]. Another study, in Italy showed that weight gain among obese participants (N = 1232) was associated with emotional status and lack of contact with physician during the lockdown period [51].

Still, other studies tracking weight changes among overweight and obese individuals during the first year of the pandemic have attributed weight gain to more sedentary behaviors/less physical activity, increased snacking frequency (particularly after dinner), increased alcohol intake, decreased water intake, emotional eating, decreased sleep quality, and being overweight or obese [50, 52] at the beginning of the pandemic. Interventions implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic or that began before and continued during the pandemic have been held in various countries - France, Italy, Canada and the USA - and have shown minimal success in some cases (in terms of weight management) while others report equal or more success than our study.

All studies reporting poorer weight outcomes, associated the results mainly to emotional or mental health. Recent publications emphasize the impact of providing food and addressing SDoHs on weight and related conditions [53–56]. One study reported positive results after 39 weeks of the National Diabetes Prevention Program (NDPP). Some reasons given for the success included people cooking more at home and therefore eating more healthy food options. Another study mentioned that isolation only impacted weight changes among women. However, only one of the studies mentioned above placed as much emphasis on social connection as ours. And only one recent study followed participants for a year. Even then, the population consisted of higher socioeconomic status white women, unlike our participants (Latino, low income, mainly monolingual Spanish speaking women) [57–60].

We suspect that the needs of the participants with food insecurity in our study, which could have put them at a disadvantage, were addressed and reduced as CHWs provided them with relevant support that influenced matters related to food security and information relevant to the COVID-19 pandemic. It is evident from our qualitative data that emotional support and reduced social isolation was considered important for the participants. We had several social exchanges and several participants attributed their success to CHW support. In general, but especially among Latinos, this type of support is an important factor [61, 62].

Explanations for the success given by participants who lost weight included social support and external factors (social support and increased knowledge) as well as internal factors, such as changes in habits and in self-esteem/motivation. Interestingly, among written feedback from participants at the end of the year, emotional/social support and knowledge stand out, followed by tangible resources (among food insecure participants only).

We think the reason why some of the studies showed more weight gain than weight loss may be that they did not emphasize as much social support from a person – such as a community health worker (CHW)– which could have had a positive effect on their mental health (mostly anxiety) and therefore may not have provided practical and stress management resources for participants to survive through the worse period of the pandemic. In our study, our CHWs had a relationship with the participants and adjusted the intervention (without losing track of the basic intervention content) to focus on meeting the basic needs of participants and listen to their concerns besides their weight rather than focusing on reaching goals or on their weight. We paid attention to the patients’ perceived needs first, then helped them as they went along to refocus on their weight. We believe that helped solidify trust and keep participants engaged, even trying to lose weight to please the CHWs in some instances.

The pandemic was the source of distress for many causing disruptions in their routine, family dynamics and their ability to meet and to purchase healthy foods, as several participants have mentioned. Research has also shown that loneliness and isolation during that time period was associated with weight gain [62]. Throughout the follow-up period in our study, CHWs not only encouraged a healthy lifestyle (more water intake and reinforcing the need for healthier food and physical activity), but took into account the participants’ circumstances and needs and addressed their emotional and physical needs while providing them with a variety of resources that were often unrelated to lifestyle but critical to their functioning. Providing participants with timely relevant information about the pandemic (a major source of stress) while doing so, seems to have reduced their fears and concerns. The feedback provided in the qualitative responses is ample evidence that this mattered to participants. The ongoing contact with our CHWs and provision of practical resources seem to have also reduced the participants’ sense of loneliness and positively affected results.

Our study is an example of yet another CHW-led intervention with the potential to impact and slow the rate of diabetes and other obesity-related conditions, especially among the subset of low-income, low-literacy individuals, many with food insecurity. The results indicate that the program reduced the risk of diabetes and cardiovascular disease despite the challenges of the pandemic. Moreover, program outcomes did not exacerbate health disparities between those with food security and those with food insecurity, unlike several other interventions. Indeed, the intervention seems to have reduced the health disparity gap, as it benefited those with food insecurity (i.e., at higher risk) as much, despite being implemented during a time of public health crisis.

Our intervention also seems to have resulted in better outcomes among the younger participants. We suspect that older participants – a group that happens to have had less food insecurity at baseline - may have been more reluctant to go out and expose themselves to the virus (as they were identified as a higher risk group) or may have been suffering from health issues which impeded the implementation of the intervention, as stated by some participants [63].

Even so, implementing some of the strategies used in this program could lead to positive biometric changes that show promise of closing the gap among the FS and the FI groups, in the aftermath of the pandemic (even 4 years after the lockdown), at a time when those most affected and with the least resources, are still facing major uncertainties.

Challenges and liabilities of implementing the intervention during the pandemic

One aspect of the pandemic that was challenging is that the women who struggled to get food were less able to provide healthy meals for themselves or their families. They were forced to take whatever they were given to eat and therefore could not control much of what they would eat. Not surprisingly, we learned that health and weight concerns change once individuals face food insecurity. Participants were no longer interested in talking about their weight because most of what occupied their minds was COVID-19 and surviving the lack of employment for themselves or their family members, putting their livelihood in significant peril. This shift in priority has been well described in the literature [64, 65]. While we are excited to report that our program nevertheless worked for our participants despite the pandemic, The “take home” message is that it’s critical to ensure that we reasonably do all we can to help address our target population's SDoH context, to help them get their basic needs met. This is a challenge we decided to meet, especially during the worst of the lock-down period. It extends to programs delivered to complex low-income populations that should be more regularly involved in lifestyle programs if the obesity epidemic in the US is expected to be improved. Simply teaching without community context does not work.

As can be seen in Table 2, some of the participants had a very low literacy level, and a couple never had any formal education. These individuals required one-on-one intervention during sessions, especially to fill out surveys. We were able to resolve this challenge by having a CHW dedicated to helping these individuals stay connected with the group during the sessions, but this meant having someone fully dedicated to this purpose. As indicated above, we also had to use extra caution when providing participants with any type of material and while performing laboratory tests. It was costly in that it took longer and required additional resources and the use of protective equipment. While we offered antibody testing for COVID-19 to all participants during one of the final labs, the results only allowed us to say that they had been exposed, but not if they were currently infectious (at this time there was no definite screening test available). As part of our protocol, we referred all who had a positive antigen test for further testing to a list of available sites nearby as these became available approximately six months into the pandemic.

Both qualitative and quantitative data were collected several times throughout the year. Performing lab tests at 3-months was justified since hemoglobin A1C changes take several weeks to reflect lifestyle changes. A report of the first 3 months- data assessment has already been published. Thereafter, data collection was repeated due to the changes in the pandemic trends (first no COVID-19 vaccine available, then after the vaccine testing was available). After the first 2 data collections, labs were repeated at approximately 9 months to preserve consistency and as a way to gauge the status of participants. However we felt that the study and this article should focus on the year-end quantitative data as this is the evidence-based time frame used in most weight related studies.

A major barrier to recording biometric information (quantitative data) during this most intense period of the first year of the pandemic were that several participants were either reluctant to have others nearby who might have COVID-19 and infect them or were reluctant to present at the sites because they felt they might have COVID symptoms and be contagious. Therefore, biometric measurements were incomplete until the very final assessment at which point participants knew it would be their last opportunity to be assessed as part of this program.

Lastly, the use of technology was challenging for several of the participants. Several had to have a CHW translate questions to them while others needed someone to help them connect to zoom meetings which became so critical for connectivity. Sometimes participants would also interfere with meetings because they did not know how to adjust their settings. Others had challenges answering the online questions or answered them incorrectly. As a precaution, we offered them the opportunity to be interviewed by the CHWs (approximately one third elected to do this). Others did not complete the more challenging questions in the on-line survey, stopped the survey all together or did not save their surveys by mistake. For the latter group, we decided to follow up with personal interviews either by phone or during the final laboratory tests. However, due to lack of sufficient data, we were unable to include those participants in the data. This was sometimes frustrating to both participants and CHWs as they all took great pride in their continued participation and truly wanted to be part of the study.

Strengths and liabilities

One of the strengths of this study is the qualitative data which is corroborated in more than one way and adds to the quantitative data. We were able to gather information several times throughout the year and to confirm context with CHWs field notes and – at the end of the year – with focus group discussions.

A liability of this study relates to the impact of psychotropic and obesogenic medication on weight. Several of the participants had a change in the use of these medications and therefore were eliminated (and are therefore counted as dropouts). This may have introduced a bias. Furthermore, some participants were unable to participate in the final labs due to fear of being contagious or due to symptoms, even when they were available and willing to attend the labs. Work schedules changed for other participants so they could not attend laboratories tests as a result. We used survey questions to determine how they felt.

Towards the end of the year, several participants also had to travel (mostly to Mexico) due to deaths among family members. All these factors reduced the number of individuals that we were able to gather information from and may have led to some unanticipated bias. Still, we were able to retain 83% of enrolled participants with only 15 participants being lost to follow-up after 1 year. While this was a pilot feasibility study and was never designed to have a control group, this lack of a control group also represents a limitation as it makes it difficult to attribute the observed changes directly to the intervention. Therefore, our conclusions need to be considered with caution. Lastly, given participant burden concerns, we used a 2-item screener to determine food insecurity instead of the full range of food insecurity. This may not have provided the full picture of the food security status of participants.

Conclusion and recommendations for future

During the first and most intense year of the pandemic, low-income Latinas at high risk of cardiovascular diseases and diabetes were able to lose weight and reduce their waist circumference, thus reducing their risk of diabetes and other chronic diseases. More importantly, we were able to bridge the food insecurity disparity gap and thus provide beneficial changes to those most vulnerable to failure in similar circumstances.

With health and economic crises often closely associated with one another, our findings suggest that it is possible to provide healthcare to the poor and underserved without increasing the health disparity gap nor the health burden even in times of economic and public health crises. This is especially important as several government-funded programs in the USA currently face a reduced budget, and access to certain healthcare services may be drastically reduced if not eliminated for many.

In our study, community health workers were very resourceful and their role throughout the project, including their insightful work on the program adaptation, development of culturally attractive materials, and their ability to connect with the community was invaluable. In particular, the presence of constant support, of information and problem-solving was critical to participants.

Personal contact is important no matter what food security status a person has. It is critical to ensure that individuals have their basic needs met before we can get their attention and consistent interest in maintaining or improving their health. This is not only the “right” thing to do but also serves to help build trust with all participants.

With the COVID-19 pandemic continuing to some degree and some healthcare system challenges remaining, CHW-led interventions that rely less on the complex healthcare system could be an effective way of reducing the risk of chronic disease among food insecure women, especially Latinas.

Based on our results we heavily encourage a healthcare “team” approach involving CHWs, healthcare providers or institutions and fellow participants to facilitate social support and lifestyle changes among underserved populations who need to lose weight and improve their quality of life. Engaging CHWs in leading health promotion programs for high-risk individuals such as low-income Latinas could ultimately help reduce the current health disparity gaps and the nationwide health and economic burdens associated with them even during times of crisis.
