Building phenotypic character matrices for phylogenetic inference: exploration of 35 years of practice
Authors: Melanie J. Hopkins, Mark C. Nikolic, James D. Holmes, Daniela S. Monti, Ernesto E. Vargas‐Parra, Russell D.C. Bicknell, Gregory D. Edgecombe, Katherine Jordan‐Burmeister, John R. Paterson, Shravya Srivastava‐Losey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecent methodological development in phylogenetic inference has focused predominantly on molecular data. However, renewed interest in other data types, particularly morphological data, has followed from the increased recognition of the power of total evidence and tip‐dating approaches, including fossil data, for inference of time‐scaled trees and rates of evolution. However, attention has largely focused on the improvement of models of morphological evolution and other analytical tools with much less discussion about data acquisition itself. Here we review past and current practice for describing and collecting morphological data for phylogenetic inference. We present a systematic review of 164 phylogenetic analyses conducted over the last 35 years and focused on a diverse group of extinct arthropods: trilobites. Trends in increasing matrix size, data type, and coding strategy are evident. Where present, polymorphic characters have been predominantly derived from discretized continuous characters, although increasingly practitioners are utilizing alternative approaches for the treatment of quantitative characters. Not surprisingly, traditional indices that describe character consistency are highly correlated with matrix size but show surprising variation at different taxonomic scales. More recent attempts to describe data quality using information theory imply that characters can have high information content even if data are missing for many tips, providing support against the exclusion of characters because of missing data. In consideration of this, as well as advances in the study of developmental biology and variational complexity, we identify several avenues for increasing the quality and quantity of morphological data going forward.

INTRODUCTION

Phylogenetic inference – the construction of hypotheses examining how organisms are related to one another – provides a fundamental framework for evolutionary and palaeobiological studies. The majority of recent phylogenies are based on molecular data and are limited to extant organisms. However, there is mounting evidence that including even a small number of fossils can induce exploration of novel tree space (Mongiardino Koch & Parry, 2020), decrease discrepancies in phylogenetic signal between molecular and morphological analyses (Legg, Sutton & Edgecombe, 2013; Davesne et al., 2016; Keating, Garwood & Samsom, 2023), improve the quality of inference (Mongiardino Koch, Garwood & Parry, 2021), and increase the robustness of downstream analyses (Gearty et al., 2024). In these cases, the use of morphological data is often necessary for including fossils, especially if taxonomic constraints for those fossils are uncertain (Nikolic, Warnock & Hopkins, 2025). As a result, there has recently been a resurgence in focus on the performance and improvement of models of morphological evolution (Tarasov, 2023; Mulvey et al., 2025), modification and expansion in applying transition matrices (Wright, Lloyd & Hillis, 2016), and the treatment of different types of morphological data (Monti & Confalonieri, 2017).

In general, most methodological focus has been on what to do with an existing matrix once you have one. Nonetheless, coding morphological data for phylogenetic inference raises challenges not seen for molecular data, particularly for fossil data. Numerous models of molecular evolution have been developed based on our understanding of the biochemical properties and frequencies of nucleic acids (Arenas, 2015). However, morphological characters are not directly equivalent to molecular characters; character states across characters do not share the same properties and are thus not directly comparable (Keating et al., 2023). This has two implications: (i) existing models of morphological evolution are simpler than those for molecular data (e.g. Lewis, 2001) and (ii) the practice of character construction can vary considerably between, and even within, studies (Stevens, 1991; Poe & Wiens, 2000). Although discussion of best practice for coding morphological characters for use in phylogenetic inference is ongoing (see Brazeau, 2011; Simões et al., 2016, for two recent examples), there has been no broad examination of whether such recommendations have been put into practice. In this paper, we briefly review the multiple sources of morphological data and considerations that researchers have made when creating character matrices. We then explore how practice has changed over the last few decades, focusing on an entirely extinct group with a long history of phylogenetic analysis: trilobite arthropods. Finally, we put this in the context of general trends in the field of phylogenetic inference, while reflecting on how these might better inform character construction.

CURRENT CONSIDERATIONS AND CHALLENGES FOR MORPHOLOGICAL CHARACTERS

Ideally, morphological characters represent heritable features subject to evolutionary change independent from other characters; character states describe variable conditions of characters and are mutually exclusive of one another (Sereno, 2007). Most generally, characters are viewed as either qualitative, that is, they represent features that can be described efficiently with concepts (e.g. shape, colour, or composition); or as a quantified variable such as an absolute or relative measurement. Considerable discussion has been devoted to whether all characters are, in fact, quantitative or could be expressed in quantitative terms (Baum, 1988; Stuessy, 1990; Wiley, 1981); in practice, ‘qualitative’ characters persist because they are efficient descriptors of variation.

Qualitative characters need to be semi‐quantified for use in phylogenetic inference. This is accomplished by assigning descriptors to integer values. This allows them to be considered ‘discrete’ characters, distinguishing them from ‘continuous’ characters, which are represented by an unbroken continuum of real numbers. Meristic characters, which are count variables that can take on any positive integer value, may be considered discrete when the subset of values is small, or when they are expressed as ranges. An example of the latter might be the number of segments in the thorax of an arthropod, where one state is used for all taxa with 1–4 segments and another state is used for taxa with 8–10 segments. Meristic characters can also be expressed with a variable descriptor, such as mean or median, in which case they would be considered continuous. The basic distinction, then, between ‘discrete’ and ‘continuous’ characters is best understood as a reference to the mathematical properties of the set of numbers used to describe the trait or attribute.

Discrete characters frequently describe the absence or presence of a trait. The absence of the character is conventionally represented by zero; historically, the perceived primitive or outgroup state might be assigned to zero instead, so that in cases where the absence was considered a loss within the clade of interest, ‘absence’ would be coded as ‘1’. In practice, unless explicitly specified through transition matrices (Hoyal Cuthill & Lloyd, 2025), search and optimality algorithms treat character states as equivalent with no inherent directionality, so any ‘order’ implied by lists of character states is for the convenience of the coder. Discrete characters are not limited to two states, however. Theoretically there need not be any limit, as long as a unique symbol for a character state can be found and is available. Thus, the number of states possible is typically limited by the software being used. For example, in the early 1990s, the software program PAUP (Swofford, 2003) was limited to 32 states and Hennig86 to 10 (Thiele, 1993). In practice, however, the number of states used for any particular character is generally much smaller (see Section IV.1).

Different coding strategies for discrete characters

At its simplest, a character matrix may be comprised of some number of binary characters that describe the absence and presence of traits. This is almost always unsatisfying because traits show variation among individuals when present, and it may be informative for inference to include the variable expression of traits in the analysis. There are several ways in which this can be done, but they fall largely into three categories.

Exclusive use of binary characters to describe all variation

For each character, one of the states is ‘absent’ and the other state combines the presence and expression of the trait. Different, non‐overlapping expressions are represented in individual characters, for example, ‘Yellow feathers: absent (0), present (1)’ and ‘Brown feathers: absent (0), present (1)’. Occasionally a character describing the absence/presence of the character, regardless of its expression, will also be included [‘Feathers: absent (0), present (1)’]. This coding strategy has been referred to as ‘nominal variable coding’ (Pimentel & Riggins, 1987), ‘method D’ of Pleijel (1995), ‘reductive coding’ sensu Wilkinson (1995), and ‘nonadditive binary coding’ (Strong & Lipscomb, 1999). An example of this coding strategy for complex traits with multiple attributes is the following set of characters: ‘Yellow, long, striped feathers: absent (0), present (1)’, ‘Red, short, non‐striped feathers: absent (0), present (1)’, ‘Yellow, short, striped feathers: absent (0), present (1)’, etc., and has been referred to as ‘compound coding’ (Brazeau, 2011). This category of coding strategy overweights absences and creates logical interdependencies because a presence coding for one character necessarily requires an absence coding for all other characters describing alternative expressions of the same trait.

Multi‐state characters where all attributes of a trait are described within different states of a single character

One of the states is absence of the trait, for example, Feathers: absent (0); yellow (1), red (2). This coding strategy has been referred to generally ‘multistate coding’, and some workers have made a distinction between multistate characters describing variation in one attribute and ‘multiple character‐state variables coding’ such as ‘Feathers: absent (0), present: yellow, long, striped (1), present: red, short, non‐striped (2), present: yellow, short, striped (3), etc.’ (Simões et al., 2016). One or both variants have also been referred to as ‘method A’ of Pleijel (1995), ‘composite coding’ (Maddison, 1993; Wilkinson, 1995), and ‘fused coding’ (Lee & Bryant, 1999). This coding strategy avoids the problems of nonadditive binary coding but conflates homology statements, the cost of transformation is the same for a shift from red to yellow feathers as it is from absence to presence of feathers (assuming equal weights, see Section II.5), and for complex characters, may neglect independence among different character attributes. Furthermore, workers using this strategy have lumped different structures together into single characters, conflating possibly independent transformation series (‘unjustified composite locator coding’, Simões et al. 2016).

‘Method B’ of Pleijel (1995) is a variant that could be considered a combination of nonadditive binary coding and composite coding. Under this strategy, a complex trait with multiple attributes is described as a set of characters for each attribute (colour, length, etc.), each of which also includes an absent state. This variant suffers from the problems of both nonadditive binary coding and composite coding.

Hierarchically structured characters

Under this category of coding strategy, one binary character is used to describe the absence or presence of the trait and additional characters are used to describe attributes of the trait when present. When the character does not apply, an inapplicable token (−) is frequently used for the coding. This coding strategy has been referred to as ‘Method C’ of Pleijel (1995), ‘conventional coding’ (Hawkins, Hughes & Scotland, 1997), ‘contingent coding’ (Forey & Kitching, 2000), and ‘reductive coding’ sensu Strong and Lipscomb (1999). This coding strategy makes explicit homology statements and does not overweight absences but shifts the analytical problem of having inapplicable tokens to the treatment of data during inference. Common ways to accomplish this include treating the inapplicable tokens as missing data, treating the inapplicable token as another state (‘absence coding’, Strong & Lipscomb, 1999; Simões et al., 2023) (or ‘repeated absences’, Brazeau, 2011), or applying algorithmic approaches that make explicit use of contingent coding. Several problems arise when inapplicable tokens are treated as missing data in parsimony analyses (Maddison, 1993), and various alternative approaches have been proposed that eliminate some or all of these problems (Hopkins & St. John, 2021; Brazeau, Guillerme & Smith, 2019; Goloboff et al., 2021). Recently Bayesian analysis of contingently coded characters where inapplicable tokens are treated as missing was shown to outperform parsimony‐based approaches (Simões et al., 2023), but this may change with ongoing development of coding strategies (see Section V). Bayesian approaches that model inapplicable characters as hidden states (Tarasov, 2019) or using amalgamated continuous‐time Markov chains (Tarasov, 2023) are also in development.

Continuous characters

The vast majority of continuous characters used in phylogenetic analysis are length measurements and ratios of length measurements to capture general aspects of shape. In earlier practice, continuous characters were discretized to include them in analysis with discrete characters. Numerous methods for discretizing characters have been proposed, including various types of gap‐coding, statistical methods, and model fitting.

Gap‐coding methods employ simple algorithms to generate gaps of a critical size, producing discrete codes for quantitative values. Taxon samples are classified into states only when the gap size between sample distributions exceeds a specified reference value. The simplest method, ‘simple gap coding’ (Mickevich & Johnson, 1976; Simon, 1983; Almeida & Bisby, 1984), considers a gap equivalent to one standard deviation. Thorpe (1984) proposed a modification of the gap‐coding procedure to retain a minimum amount of information about character divergence among populations when no gap larger than one within‐group standard deviation is found. In Thorpe's ‘gap coding B’, or ‘divergence coding’, the divergence level between terminals corresponds to the least significant difference within a 95% confidence interval.

Other approaches include (i) ‘Segment coding’ (Simon, 1983; Thorpe, 1984; Colless, 1980; Chappill, 1989), which divides the range of character states into equal‐sized segments, with taxa coded according to their segment membership; (ii) ‘Homogeneous subset coding’ (Simon, 1983; Archie, 1985) which employs an a posteriori multiple comparisons test to create groups (e.g. the sums of squares simultaneous test procedure, or the T‐method and proposed extensions, as discussed in Sokal & Rohlf, 1981), and (iii) ‘Generalized gap coding’ (Archie, 1985; Goldman, 1988), which incorporates within‐taxon character variability into phenetic analyses by utilizing generalized or Mahalanobis distances (D or D 2) to measure similarity, followed by standard clustering techniques (e.g. Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean [UPGMA]).

Finally, ‘gap weighting’ (Thiele, 1993) relies on the differential weighting of gaps between coded states within a single character. This method divides the observed range R (the difference between the maximum and minimum observed values) into as many states N as allowed by the program to be used. A taxon with an observed value V is assigned a discrete state S (with 0 ≤ S ≤ N) if: V ≥ SR/NV and V < (S + 1) R/N. Wiens (2001) proposed the ‘step‐matrix gap‐weighting’ method to address the limitation on the number of states in Thiele's method. ‘Step‐matrix gap‐weighting’ employs step matrices to weight the gaps between mean species values, allowing the discretization into a greater number of states, equivalent to the maximum cost between states defined in the step matrix.

Statistical methods involve variance analysis followed by a multiple range test (MRT). The objective is to identify homogeneous groups of means for each character by analysing variance among taxon samples (e.g. analysis of variance and multiple range tests [ANOVA‐MRT], Guerrero, de Luna & Sanchez‐Hernandez, 2003).

Model‐fitting approaches involve fitting different numbers of normal distributions to a dataset containing specimens from all species. The quality of the fit is evaluated using the bias‐corrected Akaike's Information Criterion (AICc), and specimens are assigned to specific groups based on the probabilities of belonging to each distribution (e.g. finite mixture coding, Strait, Moniz & Strait, 1996).

The goals of the above methods are to identify non‐arbitrary, biologically meaningful breaks in the distribution of variation across taxa, as well as provide replicable means of describing variation between different studies. However, for many methods, the identification of gaps or groupings depends highly on the sample, and can result in situations where two taxa, despite having notably different means, are assigned the same state if the distribution of other taxa between them lack significant gap; or where two taxa with non‐significantly different values are assigned to different states (Farris, 1990). Gap‐coding methods that avoid these situations, such as gap‐weighting (Thiele, 1993), do so by range‐standardizing the data.

Continuous characters can be coded directly as a value representing a state, and the cost of transformation between the two values is the numerical difference between states (or directly proportional to that difference). In this sense, continuous characters behave as linear additive (i.e. ordered) and may also be analysed using the Farris (1970) algorithms for optimization in a parsimony framework (Goloboff, Mattoni & Quinteros, 2006). As implemented in the software package TNT (Goloboff, Farris & Nixon, 2008), continuous characters may take values between 0 and 65 with up to three decimal values. In effect, this method is comparable to discretizing continuous characters into a very large number (65,001) of ordered character states but does so in a computationally tractable way for datasets with larger numbers of taxa (cf. step‐matrix method of Wiens, 2001). Goloboff et al. (2006) refer to this method as treating continuous characters ‘as such’ (see also Goloboff [2022, chapter 9] for an updated discussion of the method). One benefit of treating continuous characters ‘as such’ might be that the amount of variation ‘contained’ within each character state is dependent on measurement precision. In contrast, with gap‐coding, the amount of variation ‘contained’ within each character state is largely dependent on (and therefore subject to) the range and distribution of variation captured in the overall dataset and the number of states allowable by the software being used. An additional advantage that this method has over approaches for discretization more generally is the option to include a range of values for each operational taxonomic unit (OTU) (for example, observed ranges or confidence intervals around means).

Because the change in state between two OTUs should be proportional to the magnitude of difference they exhibit, scaling is a fundamental consideration for continuous characters. Differences in unit scale across characters impact on the relative character weight. Continuous characters are commonly rescaled, for example by standardizing ranges to a common magnitude, to reduce this effect. The scaling effect can be enhanced through the use of ratios as continuous characters because the choice of numerator/denominator changes the magnitude over which characters vary (Mongiardino Koch, Soto & Ramírez, 2015). While rescaling does not remove the problem with ratios, log‐transformation does, as this achieves linearity between a quotient and its inverse form (Mongiardino Koch et al., 2015). Note also that treating a continuous character as ordered has almost always been realized during analysis by applying differential weights or rates to different character‐state transformations. This is distinct from ‘state accretion’ coding, where states are defined, for example, using inequalities to force ordering (Simões et al., 2016). Among the matrices analysed herein (Section III), state accretion coding is rare. We also rarely observed characters for which one state was defined using an inequality for taxa with an extreme trait number or value (e.g. ‘more than X’).

Geometric morphometric data

Phylogenetic characters have seldomly been derived from geometric morphometric data and their use remains controversial. There are three primary ways that researchers have attempted to incorporate geometric morphometric data into phylogenetic inference. First, derived shape variables, such as principal component scores, have been used as continuous characters in, for example, maximum likelihood analyses (Caumul & Polly, 2005; Parins‐Fukuchi, 2018). Principal component scores have also been used for analyses to jointly infer tree topologies and rates of cultural evolution of Upper Palaeolithic stone tool shape (Matzig et al., 2024).

Second, derived shape variables have been used to discretize shape variation. Two examples include Fink and Zelditch (1995), who used partial warps to describe shape change during ontogeny for species of piranha and then created binary absence/presence characters for change in each dimension, and Gonzalez‐Jose et al. (2008), who used principal component scores to generate sets of ordered multistate characters. Objections to the use of derived shape variables for discretized or continuous characters include (i) components derived from ordinations are not individually meaningful and several are needed to summarize morphological variation and difference; (ii) components derived from ordinations are not necessarily expected to represent directions of biological interest; (iii) components derived from ordinations are geometrically orthogonal but may be statistically or biologically correlated; (iv) components derived from ordinations are sensitive to the reference form (Rohlf, 1998; Adams & Rosenberg, 1998) and/or the addition of OTUs (Adams et al., 2011).

Third, an estimate of shape difference or distance has been used as a continuous character and treated as a separate partition in parsimony analyses (Catalano & Goloboff, 2012; Goloboff, 2022) or through clustering techniques based on distance matrices, often in comparison to other approaches or analytical results (e.g. Polly, 2001; Zelditch et al., 2017). Only very recently have superimposed landmark coordinates been used as continuous characters in a Bayesian framework (Zhang, Drummond & Mendes, 2024).

Use of ontogenetic information

Despite the influence of increased understanding of developmental processes on character conceptions and homology principles (DiFrisco, 2021, 2023; Wagner, 2014), ontogenetic information has largely been used to identify additional characters that might be included in the matrix. These characters may describe trait expression in an earlier developmental stage or change in trait expression during ontogeny. The addition of characters describing variation at different developmental stages also makes it possible to include OTUs where only juveniles are known (Cau, 2024).

There has been minor experimentation in treating individuals of the same species but at different life stages as different OTUs in the phylogenetic analysis. Following from Hennig's (1966) definition of a semaphoront as an individual during a certain period of time, this has come to be referred to as ‘semaphoront coding’ even though the latter regards the selection of OTUs rather than the coding of semaphoronts through character construction. Although the practice was introduced in an effort to understand fossil taxa of unknown developmental stage (Wolfe & Hegna, 2014; Lamsdell & Selden, 2013), semaphoront coding has been challenged on theoretical and empirical grounds (Sharma, Clouse & Wheeler, 2017).

Analytical framework

The majority of phylogenetic analyses using morphological data, particularly where focused on fossil taxa, have used parsimony as the optimality criterion. Use of morphological data in Bayesian analyses has increased more recently as some studies have shown better results compared to parsimony (e.g. Wright & Hillis, 2014; O'Reilly et al., 2016; Simões et al., 2023; but see Keating et al., 2020 for situations in which both perform poorly). In addition, there has been a shift from ‘node dating’ molecular phylogenies to including fossil data through combined morphological and molecular (‘total’) analysis. Increased interest from both molecular phylogeneticists and palaeobiologists has motivated the development of tools for expanded use of morphological data in Bayesian phylogenetics, including the tripartite model approach for divergence time estimation (Warnock & Wright, 2021), expansions on the Lewis (2001) Markov k (Mk) character model for discrete data (Wright et al., 2016; Pyron, 2017), and the fossilized birth–death tree model (Heath, Huelsenbeck & Stadler, 2014).

So far, this shift has influenced character matrix construction only in specific ways, namely that practitioners are more likely to include autapomorphic characters and characters of lesser potential taxonomic importance because their inclusion impacts on branch lengths and estimated rates of evolution. However, two recent studies (Khakurel et al., 2024; Mulvey et al., 2025) explored the impact of model misspecification on the inference of topology and branch lengths, specifically where the character state space has been incorrectly characterized. The focus of these studies was on the Q‐matrix, which describes the transition rates between character states. The size of the Q‐matrix is determined by the number of states and in practice characters are partitioned by the number of states. It is possible to apply one Q‐matrix of maximal observed size and run an unpartitioned analysis, but this is clearly an incorrect specification that overestimates the state space for a subset of the characters (Mulvey et al., 2025). More insidious is the possibility of underestimating the state space, for example because there happens to be a state that has not been sampled or because some variation has been incorrectly interpreted as a reversal instead of a unique state (Khakurel et al., 2024). It must be understood, then, that just as choices are made about character coding in consideration of the analytical approach (e.g. Brazeau, 2011; Tarasov, 2019, 2023; Hopkins & St. John, 2021), how characters are coded determines the models that may be considered for the data (Khakurel et al., 2024).

Continuous characters may also be included in Bayesian analyses and modeled using Brownian motion or an Ornstein‐Uhlenbeck (OU) process. Currently, of the three most widely used programs that have implementations for continuous characters, one (BEAST2) cannot handle missing data and the other two have only recently accommodated them (RevBayes, see Wynd et al., 2025; MrBayes, see Wang & Zhang, in press). Despite the potential benefit (Parins‐Fukuchi, 2018), few studies have included continuous characters as a separate partition in Bayesian analysis (Wright & Hopkins, 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Wynd et al., 2025).

TRENDS IN CHARACTER CONSTRUCTION: CASE STUDY USING TRILOBITES

Trilobites are an extinct group of marine arthropods; they are generally considered crown‐group arthropods along with other extinct artiopods, but it is not clear whether they are more closely allied with mandibulates or chelicerates (Daley et al., 2018). Trilobites were amongst the first macroscopic animals to appear in abundance in the fossil record and were a major component of Palaeozoic marine communities, with more than 22,000 named species (Paterson, 2020). Because of this, they have been important for understanding the early evolution of animals and marine ecosystems. Trilobite researchers were among the first palaeobiologists to adopt new approaches for inferring evolutionary relationships, including numerical taxonomy (e.g. Temple & Tripp, 1979; Temple, 1980) and cladistics (summarized by Fortey, 2001 and Lieberman & Karim, 2010). One of the first published cladograms to feature fossils was given by Eldredge (1972) to depict the hypothesized relationships between species of the trilobite genus Eldredgeops. Hence, there is a long history of describing variation within and amongst trilobite groups using quantitative and semi‐quantitative characters. Further, their morphologically complex exoskeleton was heavily biomineralized from an early ontogenetic stage, which means that post‐hatching ontogenetic information is potentially available for many species (Hopkins, 2017).

We compiled phylogenetic analyses of trilobites using Webster (2007, Table S1) for literature published before 2007 and supplemented our knowledge of the literature as trilobite systematists with online search tools such as Google Scholar for publications since 2007 to November 2023 (see online Supporting Information [SI], Table S1). We included some matrices for burlingiid and agnostid arthropods because even though their relationship with trilobites is unclear (Paterson, 2020), there is overlap between the researchers working on them. For each study we compiled descriptive information (Table S2). Because previous assessment of character selection indicates that researchers are inconsistent with how explicitly they describe their approach, if they do at all (Poe & Wiens, 2000), we focused on aspects of character construction that could be inferred from inspecting the matrices themselves, using the manuscript text as a secondary source. Some of this information, such as taxonomic scope and publication year, is self‐explanatory. Other types of information required us to define explicit categories, particularly for coding scheme and treatment of continuous characters (Appendix S1). We also compiled all the character matrices in a standard format (Data S1) from which we extracted matrix size, and counts of character states, missing data, polymorphisms, median and maximum number of character states, and number of inapplicable and autapomorphic characters (R script S1) as well as information metrics (R script S2). Data collection and analysis was done in the R Programming language (R Core Team, 2024) using functions from the ‘stringr’ (Wickham, 2023), ‘tidyr’ (Wickham, Vaughan & Girlich, 2024), ‘TreeTools’ (Smith, 2019), ‘ggplot2’ (Wickham, 2016), ‘ggridges’ (Wilke, 2024), and ‘gridExtra’ (Anguie, 2017) R packages.

Our dataset of character matrices for trilobites consisted of 164 analyses drawn from 108 publications (Table S1). Information was incomplete (for example, the character matrix or character list was excluded) for eight publications. For sets of analyses drawn from single studies, matrices that varied in numbers of OTUs, number of characters, or specific state codings, were treated as independent records for comparison. In studies where the difference was strictly analytical (for example, different framework, weighting schemes, or ordering schemes), duplicate matrices were removed where appropriate. This reduced the number of analyses to 140.

Matrix size

Across these analyses, the number of OTUs ranged from 6 to 107, with a median of 19, and the number of characters ranged from 10 to 115, with a median of 30 (Fig. 1). This is comparable to that tallied in a recent compilation across 81 studies of Paleozoic invertebrate groups, most (67%) of which were of trilobites (Barido‐Sottani et al., 2020). The median ratio of characters to OTUs is 1.6, but 16 of 140 matrices included more OTUs than characters.

Taxonomic scope

Taxonomic scope varied from intrageneric to supraordinal, although the vast majority (88%) of analyses focused on the family level or lower (Fig. 3). Most (92%) of the analyses used species or species exemplars as OTUs even at the broadest taxonomic scope (Table S2). We noticed that some studies used species as OTUs but the outgroup was coded at a more inclusive taxonomic level or was coded as a hypothetical ancestor, but this appears to have been more common in earlier studies and is not recent practice.

Hierarchical coding scheme

Half (51%) of the matrices were coded consistently using composite coding, and 11% coded consistently using contingent coding (Fig. 4). Not quite one fifth (16%) of the matrices had a mix of contingent and composite coding (Fig. 4). A similar percentage (16%) were unclear, although these are largely early studies (Fig. 4), implying that the informativeness of character descriptions has increased. Only 4% of studies used absence coding (Fig. 4), one of which was to compare with treating inapplicable tokens as missing (Westrop, Poole & Adrain, 2010). Only a few studies (2%) used exclusively binary coding, but for 74% of the matrices, the median number of states per character was two (Table S2). The maximum number of states was 31, achieved for gap‐weighted characters (Hopkins, 2011).

Missing data

Changes in the amount of missing data may be related to changes in methodological choices, including coding scheme, framework, and taxon‐specific considerations. For example, because no study used a recent algorithmic approach for treating inapplicable characters, we assumed that all inapplicable tokens were treated as missing data. Indeed, some studies explicitly coded inapplicable characters as ‘?’ (Ebach & Ahyong, 2001; Wernette et al., 2020; Du, Liu & Zong, 2024; Swisher, Westrop & Amati, 2016; Ebbestad & Budd, 2002). There were no analyses that included inapplicable character tokens before 2001 (Fig. 5A), although this does not mean that there were no inapplicable characters included in those matrices (they may have been coded as missing instead). There is no increase in the proportion of missing data, whether truly missing or inapplicable tokens treated as missing (Fig. 5B, R = −0.02, P = 0.814). Thus, there does not appear to be any change in how conservative researchers are about including characters with abundant missing data across OTUs.

(D) Proportion of characters sampled from different parts of the exoskeleton. Blue = cephalic characters; grey = thoracic characters; pink = pygidial characters.

For trilobites, some parts of the exoskeleton, such as the thorax, historically include more missing data because they are not as readily available for all species, especially those known from disarticulated specimens. The proportion of characters belonging to different parts of the holaspid (‘adult’) exoskeleton (cephalon, thorax, and pygidium) has remained steady, with researchers continuing to rely most heavily on characters describing the cephalon (Fig. 5D, blue points). Inclusion of characters coded from juvenile specimens remains infrequent, with only a few analyses using exclusively juvenile characters (Fig. 5C). We did not recover any analyses that coded juveniles as separate semaphoronts from adult specimens.

Autapomorphies

There have been only a modest number of Bayesian analyses performed for trilobites, and all of those since 2005 (Fig. 6A). In contrast to studies using parsimony, studies using Bayesian inference have explicitly included autapomorphies (e.g. Paterson, Edgecombe & Lee, 2019) and matrices used for Bayesian analysis have more of them (Fig. 6B). However, it is common for autapomorphies to be included in parsimony‐based analyses through coding practices, such as via multistate characters (e.g. Hopkins, 2011, p. 1133). This may be the reason that there is no trend through time in the proportion of characters that are autapomorphic (Fig. 6C). Note that this represents an effective decoupling between the number of autapomorphic states and the number of parsimony‐uninformative characters. We noticed also that occasionally autapomorphies exist because the outgroup is the only taxon coded for a particular state. Usually only one outgroup is included but it is not uncommon to include more; there does not appear to be a notable change in the number of outgroups through time (Fig. 6D).

Polymorphisms

For many biologists, the term ‘polymorphism’ refers specifically to the presence of two or more genetically determined morphs in a single interbreeding population, and excludes seasonal, geographic (also environmentally induced), or continuous variation (Ford, 1945). Morphs may differ in morphology or behaviour and may occupy different ecological niches (Jamie & Meier, 2020). Examples of polymorphisms are dominated by colour morphs (Jamie & Meier, 2020).

In the context of character construction for phylogenetic inference, the definition of polymorphism has been relaxed to refer more generally to any variation within species (Wiens, 2000), as long as that variation is expressed among individuals, not within individuals (Simões et al., 2016). Early discussions of character creation focused on whether relatively highly variable characters should be included in character matrices and how they should be coded if they were included (described in Wiens, 2000). Common practice is to indicate the presence of intraspecific variation by coding any observed states in a software‐specific codified way, typically within brackets and separated by a comma. Polymorphic coding of this type is not uncommon amongst trilobite researchers, and indeed Webster (2007) was able to use polymorphic coding to document a decrease in intraspecific variation in trilobites through time.

We found that 34% of the matrices coded at least one character as polymorphic. However, the proportion of polymorphisms within each matrix is low, e.g. under 5% for all but three analyses (Fig. 7A). We also found that most polymorphic codings were unique, i.e. a character was usually coded as polymorphic only once in any given matrix (Fig. 7B, Table S3).

Proportion of matrices (N = 140) with polymorphic codings (A) and the number of taxa for which the same character is coded polymorphically in matrices with at least one character coded polymorphically (B). Most polymorphic characters are coded only once in any given matrix.

To determine whether some characters are more likely to be coded as polymorphic than others, we categorized characters into types and applied likelihood ratio tests (G tests) to assess whether counts of characters coded polymorphically were independent of those categories. The categories are as follows: (i) binary absence/presence characters (bAP); (ii) composite coding of absence/presence characters (cAP); (iii) meristic counts (MI); (iv) shape characters coded by visual inspection (MD); and (v) shape or morphometric characters coded using relative measurements (M). Examples of shape characters coded by visual inspection (category 4) included the expression (e.g. depth) of furrows and the degree of curvature in a margin or suture. Category 4 characters could conceivably be described quantitatively but were instead evaluated as a qualitative matter of degree (e.g. ‘shallow’ or ‘deep’). Meristic characters (category 3) were sometimes coded on an integral scale and sometimes states were defined as ranges of integers. Note that these categories are not necessarily mutually exclusive of each other: for example, a compositely coded absence/presence character could include states that describe shape determined by visual inspection (cAPmd) or states that describe additional presence/absence characters (cAPap).

Ideally, we would apply this categorization to all of the matrices; however, this would have required individual evaluation of almost 6700 characters and was beyond the scope of this project. Instead, we selected three matrices with the most polymorphic codings (Cotton, 2001; Paterson et al., 2019; Sundberg, 2004; Tables S4–S6). These matrices also had larger numbers of discrete and/or discretized characters overall (97, 107, and 70, respectively), of all types, and for which a relatively large proportion were coded polymorphically (Tables 1 and S2). Additionally, characters rarely eluded the established categories. Characters that we could not categorize included those describing: (i) discrete positional information (for example, the type of hypostomal attachment [character 70 in Cotton, 2001, p. 205; character 62 of Paterson et al., 2019, SI p. 11], or the segmental association of a macropleural spine [character 72 of Paterson et al., 2019, SI p. 13]); (ii) conceptually defined categories (for example, the type of eye [character 40 in Paterson et al., 2019, SI p. 10] or segmentation schedule [character 107 in Paterson et al., 2019, SI p. 16]); (iii) changes during life history (for example, characters 60, 102, 105, 106 in Paterson et al., 2019, SI p. 11); (iv) conflation of conceptual categorization and shape (for example, character 71 in Cotton, 2001, p, 205); (v) or a mix of meristic data and shape data (for example, character 88 of Paterson et al., 2019, SI p. 15–16).

It is worth noting that as we started examining these matrices in more detail, we noticed that ‘absent’ and ‘present’ might be used as character states for both novel traits as well as the binary expression of traits, including shape characters. For example, character 20 in Cotton (2001, p. 204) is ‘Sinuous facial sutures. 0: absent, 1: present’. This character describes the shape of the sutures, not their presence (for which there is another character), and could more adequately be described as ‘Curvature of facial suture. 0: straight, 1: sinuous’. and assigned to category 4. But because of the original phrasing, our initial protocol (Appendix S1) would have counted this character among binary absence/presence characters (category 1). This ultimately weakened any inferences across all matrices where the point of comparison was the proportion of binary absence/presence characters (see below). More generally, this further revealed how much variation in character construction there is and how this complicates any attempt at categorization and generalization.

For discretized continuous characters, almost all coded polymorphically for at least some taxa (Table 1). Treatment of quantitative characters

Over two thirds (69%) of analyses included quantitative characters that had been discretized using a sample specific division, i.e. states defined based on observed values or ranges across OTUs, and this method has continued to be popular over time (Fig. 9). In contrast, only 11% of analyses used regularly spaced intervals independent of variation in the OTUs (‘universal’, Fig. 9) and this approach was more popular amongst earlier workers. The use of an explicit approach (e.g. gap coding and other methods, see Section II.2) was intermittently popular, until Goloboff et al. (2006) introduced their method for treating continuous characters ‘as such’ in TNT (Fig. 9). Although all methods have been used in the half dozen years, there has been a long‐term shift towards methods that use an explicit method or avoid discretizing at all (Fig. 9).

ARE WE GETTING ANY BETTER AT BUILDING CHARACTER MATRICES?

The number of OTUs and the number of characters in trilobite matrices has increased over time, leading to an overall increase in matrix size (Fig. 2). The matrix sizes analysed here do not represent the maximum seen in the literature, especially compared to focal groups that include modern organisms (e.g. Legg et al., 2013). Nonetheless, the observed trend is general across the field, as evidenced by current discussion of quantity of characters versus quality of characters. For example, although it is suspected that increasing matrix size results in a higher frequency of poorly formulated characters (Simões et al., 2016), the pervasiveness of such a problem and its impact on tree inference remains unclear (Laing et al., 2017).

Consistency and retention indices

Recent assessments of character quality have deep roots in the literature, going back to methods for assessing individual characters for similarity and conjunction (Patterson, 1982) as well as the degree to which resulting trees show congruence across all characters or different data sets (Rieppel & Kearney, 2007). In addition, various measures have been developed that purport to assess the quality of the trees resulting from phylogenetic analysis. Many of these focus on support for individual nodes, but it is pervasive practice to also report consistency and retention indices (CI and RI, respectively; see Fig. 10 legend for definitions), at least for parsimony analyses. CI and RI assess the frequency of homoplasy, defined generally as a recurrence of phenotypic similarity due to independent evolution (Santis, 2024). Because the parsimony optimality criterion seeks to minimize homoplasy when identifying candidate trees, these indices have often been used as proxies for tree quality. However, since CI and RI strictly quantify homoplasy, they measure how character changes are distributed within a dataset for a given tree, primarily reflecting properties of the dataset rather than tree reliability. Variations in dataset composition have a direct impact on these indices. Empirical studies and simulations have shown that CI tends to decrease with the inclusion of more taxa and characters in a dataset (Hoyal Cuthill, 2015; Sanderson, 1991; Hauser & Boyajian, 1997; Murphy et al., 2021). In contrast, RI has been found not to vary significantly with the number of taxa (Hauser & Boyajian, 1997; Murphy et al., 2021). It reflects the homoplasy observed in a tree relative to the maximum possible homoplasy, making it a more useful measure of the phylogenetic signal of the characters. However, RI consistently underestimates the proportional extent of homoplasy in a given dataset and cannot be reliably compared across alternative datasets because it is not scaled between zero and one (Archie, 1996).

Consistency (A) and retention (B) indices over time (N = 164). The consistency index (CI) is the minimum number of changes possible (the total number of states) divided by the number of changes across the tree (the tree length) (Kluge & Farris, 1969). The retention index (RI) is the difference between the maximum number of changes possible and the tree length divided by the difference between the minimum and maximum number of changes possible (Farris, 1989). Box plots summarize data in five‐year bins (inclusive) as follows: 1989–1993; 1994–1998; 1999–2003; 2004–2008; 2009–2013; 2014–2018; 2019–2023. Dashed line = linear regression line.

Given these caveats, we cautiously assess reported values of CI and RI over time. Across trilobite analyses, 95% have been parsimony‐based (Fig. 6A), and of these 86% reported CI and 77% also reported RI (Table S2). As expected, CI and RI values are strongly negatively correlated with the number of characters and character states, but differ in their association with number of taxa (Table 3), with less variance in RI being explained by the number of taxa, characters, or character states. It is because matrix size has increased over time (Fig. 2B, C) that we see any decrease over time in CI (Fig. 10A, R = −0.15, P = 0.083) and RI (Fig. 10B, R = −0.09, P = 0.317). As such, changes in consistency indices appear to largely reflect changes in data quantity rather than data quality. These results are consistent with a recent survey of tetrapod morphological datasets (Murphy et al., 2021), which also interpreted the different behaviour of CI and RI with matrix size to indicate that while homoplasy might increase with matrix size, this is not necessarily indicative of decreasing phylogenetic signal.

What is perhaps more interesting are the patterns of CI, RI, and matrix size at different taxonomic levels. Across trilobite analyses, we see an increased number of characters at higher taxonomic scope (Fig. 11A). However, there is only a significant difference between CI values at the genus level compared to all other taxonomic levels (Fig. 11C). One possible explanation is that the impact of increasing characters is dampened by the low median number of states at all taxonomic scales (Fig. 11B) and/or the greater proportion of binary absence/presence characters utilized at broader taxonomic scopes (Fig. 8). This is supported by the shallower slope for regressions of CI on matrix size (Fig. 11D). There is also a surprising increase in RI values at order and supraordinal scales (Fig. 11E), which cannot be explained by an association with matrix size at higher taxonomic levels (Fig. 11F). Instead, the higher RI values might be explained by a potential bias in how matrices are constructed for higher taxonomic levels. These matrices seem to be more structured, perhaps due to the selection of terminal taxa. In many cases, one or two species are chosen to represent a given group, often those considered the most representative or plesiomorphic within the group, which could lead to fewer homoplastic characters. In contrast, matrices at the genus level often include most species, capturing greater within‐group diversity and potentially resulting in more homoplasy. It is also possible that characters uniting smaller (e.g. family‐level) clades within larger, ordinal and supraordinal analyses, which might be omitted from studies at lower taxonomic scales, exhibit less homoplasy. If so, including these may increase the ratio of consistency relative to maximum possible homoplasy. Going forward, it would be useful for workers to adopt newer consistency indices, such as the relative homoplasy index which is scaled from 0 to 1 (Steell, Hsiang & Field, 2025), for reporting on the extent of homoplasy.

Character information content

Unlike consistency indices, measures of character information content do not reference specific topologies, except indirectly, and are a measure of the potential utility and quality of a dataset. They generally have a basis in information theory, for example, the cladistic information content (CIC), whereby the amount of information conveyed by a character is measured as the reduction in uncertainty about the tree, where a tree is permitted only if the character can be mapped on it without homoplasy (Cotton & Wilkinson, 2008). The CIC is the log of the ratio between the number of permitted binary trees for that character and the total number of possible binary trees; it is impacted by the number of character states, and the evenness of the distribution of the character states across taxa, which means it is also impacted by the number of taxa (Table 1 in Cotton & Wilkinson, 2008). The ‘total CIC’ of the matrix is the sum of the individual CICs of the characters. For a set of characters that fit the same tree without homoplasy, one may also calculate the CIC of the matrix; however, this remains undefined for any other set of characters, which includes all the matrices analysed in this study, and likely all empirical matrices that have ever been created other than toy examples.

Because the only application of this index to existing matrices is by summing across characters, the total CIC is highly correlated with different aspects of the matrix size (Fig. 12A–C). When standardized by matrix size, the total CIC of the trilobite matrices has not changed through time (Fig. 12E). Further, any apparent difference in total CIC across taxonomic scales (Fig. 12D) disappears when matrix size is taken into account (Fig. 12F).

What is more revealing from methods for assessing character information content is what they demonstrate about the impact of missing data. The presence of missing data will decrease the CIC for any individual character (Table 2 in Cotton & Wilkinson, 2008). Unknown and inapplicable tokens could represent all possible states (‘?’) or zero possible states (‘‐’) and therefore contribute no information, whereas any given state will provide a non‐zero amount of information (a polymorphic state could also give a non‐zero amount of information, albeit much lower than a given single state). However, simulating matrices with different levels of missing data and state evenness demonstrates that matrices with more even state distributions can handle much higher amounts of missing data than those with less even state distributions (Fig. S1; R scripts S5). Increasing the proportion of underrepresented states with incomplete taxa or characters has an outsized effect on increasing the total CIC of the matrix compared to having a complete matrix with fewer differences in states among taxa. Indeed, among the empirical matrices analysed here, we observed some of similar size with very different proportions of missing data that nonetheless have similar total CIC values. This suggests that those matrices with relatively higher proportions of missing data have more even state distributions or representation of rarer states. Moreover, for a given matrix size, the information content of the matrix is not correlated with the proportion of missing data (R = −0.079, P = 0.361, N = 138). This implies that characters or taxa with a lot of missing data may still convey useful information for inference and downstream comparisons.

More recently, concepts from information theory were adapted to partition the information in character matrices into the component sufficient for diagnostic purposes (‘source coding’) and the additional component that decreases the uncertainty in the inferred phylogenetic relationships (‘channel coding’) (Yu et al., 2021). In analysing six vertebrate morphological matrices, Yu et al. found that a relatively small number of characters were needed to uniquely distinguish taxa and that additional characters added robustness to analyses where there was otherwise ‘noise’ (presumably from missing data, but possibly also due to homoplasy). Channel capacity is the ability of a dataset to effectively and reliably communicate information in the presence of this noise. They saw a ‘saturation’ point in the channel capacity of all six matrices, after which adding more characters no longer reduces the impact of noise, and the increased noise from adding characters outweighs the information they carry. For the datasets they analysed, this point was reached at minimally 100 characters and frequently many more. Moreover, these datasets range from 24.1–49.3% missing data. Thus, it is no surprise that given the sizes (Fig. 1) and the proportions of missing data (Fig. 5) for the trilobite matrices analysed herein, ‘saturation’ is never reached (Fig. S2). Like the results from considering CIC, this implies that workers should strive to increase sample size even where there might be considerable missing data unless they already have a very large matrix with a high proportion of missing data.

That there does not seem to be any saturation for trilobite matrices might seem to contradict previous studies of character exhaustion, or the likelihood that character changes yield homoplasy rather than novelty, where trilobites were shown to have higher levels of character exhaustion than other taxa (Wagner, 2000). However, Hoyal Cuthill (2015) determined that for one trilobite analysis of relatively large size (Cotton, 2001), trends in homoplasy were indicative of phylogenetic inertia rather than a finite character space (see also Murphy et al. [2021] for tetrapods). The biological implication is that trilobites added characters, but not character states, as they evolved. It is also possible, however, that there is a tendency for systematists to develop separate characters for early groups that then appear to become obsolete and are replaced by new characters. This practice would bias the matrix towards having more characters with fewer states than fewer characters with more states. More generally, it is likely that character descriptions also remain simplistic relative to reality. For example, the analysis focusing on polymorphisms in three matrices revealed a large proportion of characters based on visual assessment or discretized characters, i.e. use of only a few states to describe variation in complex characters. The increased inclusion of continuous characters via partitions may provide increased granularity and additional frameworks for assessing character exhaustion. There may also be opportunities for character refinement through the use of geometric morphometrics and more explicit incorporation of developmental information (see Section V.2).

Character type and description

Polymorphic characters, characters with high levels of missing data, and continuous characters were previously found more likely to be excluded from matrices (Poe & Wiens, 2000). Despite modest advancements in the treatment of such characters (see Section II.2 for examples for inference, and also Revell & Harmon, 2022 for tree‐based comparative analyses), the case study on trilobites presented here indicates that this has not changed over time.

Although the inclusion of continuous traits in analyses has not increased proportionally, the methods for defining states have become more refined. In recent years, researchers have shown a greater tendency to adopt explicit methods for discretizing continuous traits or to treat these traits as continuous. However, some studies still rely on sample‐specific or universal discretization approaches, which can hinder the integration of data across different matrices. Moving forward, reconsidering such practices would be advantageous. For explicitly continuous characters, a practical approach is to record raw measurements and then decide whether to treat them as continuous or to apply a clearly defined discretization method. Additionally, including measured variables (either as raw values or with a specific descriptor) for each OTU can ensure that these characters remain accessible for inclusion in future studies, regardless of the coding method chosen.

Although there is some indirect evidence that the information content (and therefore the quality) of character descriptions has increased over time (see Section III.3), lack of explicitness continues to plague the interpretation and repeatability of analyses. One outcome of this can be the perception of redundancy in character lists. For example, Webster (2007) interpreted character 21 to be a duplicate of character 24 in Yuan, Fortey and Turvey's (2006) matrix (see Table S1 in Webster, 2007). The characters do appear to be different wordings of the same length: length ratio, but the coding is inconsistent with this interpretation. Either there is an outright error in the description (e.g. ‘anterior border’ should be ‘frontal area’) or the authors were not explicit enough about the length measurements being taken (e.g. sagittal versus transverse, showing a diagram, etc.) for the reader to interpret this correctly. Better practice includes using illustrations to clarify character descriptions, a recommendation currently much more commonly met for continuous or morphometric characters than for discrete characters. The illustrations in Beech and Lamsdell (2021) provide a good example of how this can be accomplished, even for larger datasets of discrete characters.

FRONTIERS AND CHALLENGES

In the course of surveying practice of character construction over the last 35 years, several topics emerged as having high potential for informing future best practice and improved character conception and design. At the same time, we identified significant challenges facing their realization. Although we discuss topics below in the context of trilobites, the more general questions that arise are relevant to any organismal group with complex morphology and life histories.

Increasing taxonomic scope

Most of the phylogenetic work on trilobites has focused on lower taxonomic levels (Fig. 4). Attempts at broader phylogenetic frameworks has been hampered by the perceived ‘cryptogenesis’ problem (Stubblefield, 1959; Whittington, 1981), which refers to a poor understanding of the phylogenetic relationship between major post‐Cambrian trilobite groups and their Cambrian sister taxa (Paterson, 2020, p. 38). Major challenges to resolving relationships between older and younger clades include: (i) the frequency with which groups are defined based on a small number of symplesiomorphies observed in older members but not retained in younger members; (ii) homology statements based on superficial similarity between traits in different groups; and (iii) vague or inconsistent character definition. In trilobites, these issues have been exacerbated by the fact that workers tend to specialize on certain time intervals. Such disjuncts (e.g. between Cambrian and Ordovician workers) are likely to be at least partly responsible for the challenges listed above (e.g. 2 and 3), as well as the generally restricted scope of previous analyses and the cryptogenesis problem more generally. Broader analysis across such ‘boundaries’ will assist in resolving these issues and would benefit greatly from collaboration between workers specializing on groups from different time intervals.

These challenges are not unique to trilobites but instead apply to any group for which morphological data are being used (Lee & Palci, 2015). In general, strategies for quickly increasing taxonomic scope, like combining different morphological matrices, can be undermined by character redundancy, conflicting character dependencies, and missing data (although if the latter is due to inapplicable sets of characters, this can be addressed during analysis, see Section II.5). Furthermore, there is currently no clear and transparent framework for versioning morphological matrices, even when deposited digitally in online repositories. Changes to character coding, where made explicit, are reported in text or supplementary files with tenuous or obscure connection to the previous matrices. Current practices for transcription and remobilization of data can result in the introduction and/or propagation of errors. Solutions to this problem will become increasingly important as matrix sizes continue to increase.

Taxonomic scope is also increased through new species discovery, whether extant or fossil. New finds are exciting because they have the potential to impact phylogenetic hypotheses and shed light on evolutionary processes and pathways. From the perspective of character construction, they may also necessitate the creation of novel characters or states to accommodate new variation. Generally, we expect that this will increase matrix size and information content, making for better resolved trees.

Use of ontogenetic information

The trilobite fossil record includes an abundance of post‐hatching ontogenetic data that can be used to increase the number of characters and inform homology statements in characters at the holaspid (‘adult’) stage (Wright, 2015). However, there are a number of challenges. First is the identification of semaphoronts at the same stage of development. It is largely assumed that a character is being coded from holaspids unless otherwise specified. However, morphological change in the exoskeleton was gradual even though trilobites grew iteratively through moulting. Thus, it can be difficult to assign disarticulated specimens (the vast majority of the trilobite fossil record) to exact stages for comparison across species. In practice, palaeobiologists typically use coarse binning to include any information about differential trait expression during ontogeny. There are also different ways of dividing up ontogenies into bins. For example, the vast majority of trilobite ontogenies can be divided into anamorphic (post‐hatching segment generation) and epimorphic (no post‐hatching segment generation) phases, as well as meraspid (post‐hatching articulation generation) and holaspid (no post‐hatching articulation generation) stages. These two transition points occur simultaneously in some trilobites but not in others (Hughes, Minelli & Fusco, 2006; Holmes, Paterson & García‐Bellido, 2021b), and both transitions are suggested to be associated with abrupt changes in growth trajectories (Holmes, Paterson & García‐Bellido, 2021a; Hopkins & Pearson, 2016; Hopkins, 2021). However, determining transition points between such phases/stages in non‐articulated material for inclusion in phylogenetic analysis is difficult. Second, trilobites had indeterminate growth, continuing to moult, grow and change shape even after reaching epimorphosis and holaspis (Hopkins & Pearson, 2016; Holmes et al., 2021a). In addition, some species may have continued to add segments throughout life history (Paterson & Edgecombe, 2006) or stopped moulting and growing altogether (Dai et al., 2021). It is not possible to know from this review, for example, if and what proportion of polymorphisms in the analyses represent variation across semaphoronts. In broad analyses, one way of (partly) addressing these issues is to focus on species for which amount and rates of ontogenetic change have been assessed and utilize this information to select specimens for coding. For example, even without trunk information, ‘adult’ specimens (e.g. cranidia) can potentially be determined by looking at inflection points or decreasing rates of ontogenetic change across specimens of different sizes; however, such information is generally restricted to well studied species.

Beyond trilobites, developmental approaches may help elucidate challenges around serial homology. A recent example comes from work on the phylogeny and early evolution of panarthropods. Some early arthropods have large, spinose anteriormost appendages, but it is a matter of debate whether, for example, the frontal appendages of stem‐group arthropods like Kerygmachela (Budd, 1993; Park et al., 2018) and those of possible crown‐group arthropods like Leanchoilia (Haug, Briggs & Haug, 2012) are segmentally aligned (and homologous) because they are potentially associated with different segments of the arthropod head. Recent experiments in gene expression and regulation of the pre‐gnathal segments suggest that the second and third head segments may have originated from splitting a single ancestral head segment (Lev, Edgecombe & Chipman, 2022) rather than having been recruited into the head from the trunk (Ortega‐Hernández, Janssen & Budd, 2017), providing an argument for homology. Competing homology statements that arise from the consideration of developmental information (or any other information) can also be explicitly compared during analysis (e.g. King & Rucklin, 2020)

While promising, there are a number of more general challenges still facing the use of developmental information in character construction. For example, should we only code static characters at particular stages or code the process of change through ontogeny? The latter is likely to be important if we think that the process itself is homologous, and doing so may also mitigate problems associated with having to identify corresponding semaphoronts. For example, if a homologous process occurs at different stages of juvenile ontogeny, this could be captured by coding the process of change, but not necessarily by coding individual stages. Another example is the presence and timing of a metamorphosis, both process‐based characters rather than stage‐specific characters. Similarly, growth rates can be treated as a continuous character and may have phylogenetic signal but also change over the life of the individual. The examples show clearly that we need to be aware of how change during ontogeny may impact the homology statements we are making and adjust our character coding accordingly.

Geometric morphometrics, modularity, and phylogenetic characters

Aside from discretized or direct use (Section II.3), another avenue for geometric morphometric data may be in the identification of modular patterns of variation within elements (shells, sclerites, bones) which then may be used as the basis for homology statements. An example from trilobites is the cryptic association of expanded lateral regions on the cephalon (head shield) with the glabella. Across, presumably, distantly related groups, this swelling has been variably termed baccula, ala, lateral muscle impression, lunette, and paraglabellar area (Whittington, 1997). Debates of whether this structure should be considered an alternative expression (i.e. character states) of the glabella or genal regions of the cephalon (or both) can be resolved by examining patterns of modularity/integration as one would expect structures with a common correspondence to be relatively more integrated with each other such that they may constitute a module (Wagner, 1996). However, if patterns of modularity/integration with respect to this feature differ by taxon, then this implies these swellings may not be homologous across trilobite groups, or that modularity patterns have evolved through time. Regardless, quantifying patterns of modularity associated with these structures will provide useful information that can be used to code this trait appropriately. The structure of modularity, including the number and organization of modules, may also be informative in partitioning characters in phylogenetic analyses as one would expect characters of a common module to have similar rates of change. The assumed, obvious partitioning of characters based on the cephalon, thorax, and pygidium tagma of the adult trilobite body plan has not yet been tested in a modularity/integration context, and plausible alternative partitioning of characters could be based on axis/pleurae or cephalon/trunk divisions of the trilobite body plan.

As noted in Section II.3, the use of geometric morphometrics to define characters and character states for phylogenetic analysis is problematic. This is particularly true when using broad configurations that may comprise multiple characters (unless this is the aim, for example if a ‘module’ is trying to be captured). However, in some instances it may be appropriate to capture shape data for the coding of characters. A possible example from trilobites is that of the facial sutures—lines of weakness in the cephalon (head) that facilitated in moulting the exoskeleton. These have been classified into several ‘types’ based on their shape and position (i.e. opisthoparian, proparian, gonatoparian). Both suture type and sutures more generally may have evolved more than once (e.g. Paterson et al., 2019). In addition, sutural shape is complicated and requires complex description of multiple states to discretize; for example, character 3 of Paterson et al. (2019; SI p. 3–4) ‘Opisthoparian suture pattern’ lists 16 character states just for Cambrian trilobites. A geometric morphometric analysis focusing specifically on sutural shape might draw out major patterns of variation in a more efficient way and potentially distinguish between variation associated with the convergent adoptions of similar sutural patterns in unrelated taxa. Such an analysis would take considerable time and effort to conduct and would provide data for only one or several characters. Nevertheless, for characters conducive to such digitization and deemed of high importance this approach might be worthwhile.

More generally, it is possible to avoid the objections to the use of geometric morphometrics in constructing characters (outlined in Section II.3), by vetting major elements of variation (e.g. principal components) prior to use. This would allow the use of previously conducted analyses to develop characters. For example, the results of a recent geometric morphometric analysis of Cambrian trilobite cephala showed that the first principal component of variation largely reflected genal spine morphology (Holmes, 2023). This component represents variation that is biologically meaningful, summarized in a single component, and largely independent of other morphological changes, suggesting it could be used to define a character while largely avoiding the issues raised above.

Character independence

Logical character dependence, where the state of one character predicts the state of another by virtue of how the characters are designed, can be mitigated by choice of coding strategy (see Section II.1). However, characters may also be correlated for biological reasons, such as pleiotropy, allometry, biomechanics, or adaptation (Emerson & Hastings, 1998). Highly covarying characters, such as those corresponding to a common module, violate the assumption of character independence in phylogenetic analyses (Emerson & Hastings, 1998). Character non‐independence has been shown to impact phylogenetic inference by influencing character state changes, branch lengths, and estimation of divergence times (Goswami et al., 2014; Guillerme & Brazeau, 2018), although recent work shows that Bayesian methods can still recover accurate topologies even though branch lengths and rates may be underestimated (Liu & Zhang, 2026). Further, issues arising from character non‐independence may be compounded by the use of large character matrices with high character saturation and character exhaustion (Dávalos et al., 2014; Guillerme & Brazeau, 2018), as documented in trilobite phylogenetic studies (Wagner, 2000). Also, characters of serially homologous structures, of interest in segmented arthropods such as trilobites, may show high levels of integration and, thus, non‐independence (Billet & Bardin, 2019; Grams et al., 2025).

Currently there has been some development of methods for identifying character correlation (Naylor & Adams, 2001; O'Keefe & Wagner, 2001; Goswami & Polly, 2010; Goswami et al., 2014; Guillerme & Brazeau, 2018) but these methods do not distinguish between correlations due to shared ancestry or due to a relation of genuine dependence (Billet & Bardin, 2019). Mitigating the negative effects of character non‐independence may lie in the selection and construction of morphological characters, informed by patterns of modularity and integration, prior to phylogenetic analyses (Billet & Bardin, 2019; Brocklehurst & Benevento, 2020; Dávalos et al., 2014; Goswami et al., 2014; de Bivort, Clouse & Giribet, 2010). For instance, composite coding, as opposed to reductive coding, has been favoured in addressing the non‐independence of characters of serially homologous traits (Billet & Bardin, 2019). One question that has not been broached is how to treat character non‐independence if it changes through geological time as alluded to by macroevolutionary shifts in modularity/integration (Goswami, 2006; Goswami et al., 2014, 2015).

Such methods concern the detection and mitigation of biological correlation. However, it has also been argued that coding for character dependence can be justified on biomechanical or functional grounds (Goloboff & De Laet, 2024). Links between characters formulated in this manner have been recently conceptualized and incorporated into new commands for parsimony analysis in the software package TNT. This approach eliminates spurious character transformations that arise when independence is taken as a given. Furthermore, a syntax has been developed for character inapplicability by Grams and Richter (2023) based on the hierarchical nature and ontological dependencies of such morphological data, and this syntax has been translated into TNT's xlinks command (Grams et al., 2025).

Another recent approach to conceptualizing characters considers phenotypes through the context of the expression of gene regulatory networks (GRNs) (Tarasov, 2019, 2020). In this conception, a GRN determining a character may form a character identity network, triggering downstream GRNs to determine character states (Wagner, 2007). Importantly, characters in this context are hierarchical and scale‐dependent, such that, for example, a character at a fine‐level may be a character state on the level of the whole organismal GRN. Structuring characters in such a way as to reflect a GRN with many states based on its variable expression offers a useful and biologically realistic approach to character in/dependencies. The genotype–phenotype map is unlikely to be 1:1 for most morphological characters, and therefore the evolution and expression of GRNs represents a hidden process in the evolution of the trait. Hidden Markov models based on amalgamated characters (representing a GRN and all its phenotypic expressions) provide an appropriate and efficient approach to modelling these characters and likelihood methods can be used to compare and assess their quality (Tarasov, 2019, 2023). This approach has only seen very recent development and is yet to be completely implemented in any complete phylogenetic analysis. Further, amalgamation of multiple dependent characters into a single multistate character essentially substitutes correlation between characters with penalties between character states (by appropriately structuring the transition rate matrix) (Tarasov, 2020). This amalgamation may apply to obviously dependent characters identified from anatomical ontology yet is not as straightforward in cases where correlation between characters is unknown a priori, which would require detection by further statistical analysis (Tarasov, 2019).

Ontologies and computable phenotypic data

Ontologies are structured controlled vocabularies that describe knowledge in a given domain, including information about relationships (Vogt, 2009; Porto et al., 2024). Specialized ontologies, such as those within medical fields (https://obofoundry.org/) and for model organisms (mouse [Mus musculus], zebrafish [Danio rerio], Caenorhabditis elegans, Drosophila melanogaster), have seen the most development in the last couple of decades. However, because they explicitly describe the hierarchical and interactive nature of traits, ontologies have been used to manage inapplicable characters during coding and analysis. In such cases, archiving of ontologies can be as simple as supplementary text files (e.g. Hopkins & St. John, 2021). Ontologies are also incorporated as features in databases for managing phenotypic data (e.g. see https://www.morphobank.org/project/773/matrices in support of O'Leary et al., 2013), and are gaining momentum at broader taxonomic sampling (e.g. Ramírez & Michalik, 2014; Hoehndorf et al., 2016; https://kb.phenoscape.org/). In recent years, there has been increasing development of tools for building ontologies (e.g. Jupp et al., 2012) and graphically mapping them (e.g. Tarasov, Mikó & Yoder, 2022), as well as assessing information content (e.g. Porto et al., 2022), reconstructing ancestral morphologies (e.g. Tarasov, 2019), and estimating evolutionary rate (e.g. Porto et al., 2024) using ontologies.

In the context of building character matrices, one of the most promising potential uses for ontologies is providing the necessary annotation for the generation of computable data (Deans et al., 2015). For example, Dececchi et al. (2015) used ontologies to extract presence/absence characters from published matrices of sarcopterygian fish. The resulting supermatrix had less missing data and revealed areas of character space that were undersampled for important transitions in the evolutionary history of the clade. However, they cautioned against using the resulting matrix for phylogenetic inference because of the large fraction of characters belonging to isomorphic clusters, i.e. sets of characters that are perfectly correlated, in many cases because they contained redundant information or were logically equivalent. It is conceivable that post‐hoc analysis of isomorphic clusters could be automated through the ontology itself, but we are not aware of any study that has done this.

Finally, ontologies can potentially be used to underpin data extraction from text (e.g. Farrell et al., 2022, 2024) or images (e.g. Balk et al., 2024; East et al., 2025). Although such tools are being used to build trait databases (e.g. Cornelius et al., 2025), phenotypic traits extracted from text and images using machine learning methods are only beginning to be used for phylogenetic inference (Hunt et al., 2025). One open question for such approaches is how to best report the resulting characters. Although image‐based extraction might lend itself naturally to our recommendation above to use illustration to clarify character description, for traits sampled from pre‐existing matrices or extracted from textual taxonomic descriptions, it may be most effective to annotate the ontology itself.

CONCLUSIONS

(1)Use of morphological characters for phylogenetic inference has seen a resurgence as the importance of including fossil taxa for inference and downstream analyses becomes increasingly evident. Building morphological character matrices that include fossil data is nevertheless a challenge. From the analysis presented herein, it is also clearly a labour‐intensive, slow‐changing process. Despite this, there are clear shifts in how quantitative data are being treated. Overall, there has been a long‐term trend of increased matrix size, especially as analysis expands in taxonomic scope. Strategies for character coding are also influenced by taxonomic scope.(2)No matter how careful our data collection, most fossil datasets are based overwhelmingly (often exclusively) on biomineralized (skeletal) material and often have high proportions of missing data. Thus, there are some limitations due to preservational processes that may never be overcome. Nonetheless, methodological advances continue to be developed and have been increasingly adopted over the last 35 years. Current computational and analytical limitations, especially those involving the treatment of missing data, are likely to be only temporary. Our analysis of character information content indicates that most studies have not, in fact, exhausted the information available from increasing matrix size, even if that means including characters with considerable missing data.(3)With this in mind, logical and consistent character construction is essential. We know the data are not always perfectly known or complete, and we will never know the true relationships, but we can continue to leverage increased understanding of complexity, development, and emergent properties of organisms to better describe the morphological diversity we observe and improve how we model the evolution of that diversity over deep timescales.
