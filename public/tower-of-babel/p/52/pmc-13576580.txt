The V6R/V4R Index: A Novel ECG Parameter for Identifying Idiopathic Ventricular Arrhythmias Originating From the Aortic Cusp
Authors: Yoshihisa Naruse, Kohki Nakamura, Kanae Hasegawa, Toshihiko Tsuji, Machiko Miyoshi, Tatsuhiro Kataoka, Hideyuki Aoki, Yoshinori Okazaki, Shigeto Naito, Hiroshi Tada, and other members of the V6R/V4R Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRadiofrequency catheter ablation within the aortic cusp can eliminate left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) ventricular arrhythmias (VAs). However, in a significant proportion of cases, successful ablation requires targeting the aorto‐mitral continuity, the endocardium just below the left ventricular summit, and/or the mitral annulus. This study aimed to identify electrocardiographic (ECG) indices useful for differentiating VAs originating from the aortic cusp from those arising outside the cusp among LVOT‐VAs.MethodsThis retrospective study included 159 idiopathic VAs (79 in the derivation cohort and 80 in the validation cohort) successfully ablated from the LVOT. Based on the site of VA origin, VAs were classified into cusp (n = 125) and non‐cusp (n = 34) groups. Detailed PVC morphologies were compared between the two groups.ResultsIn the derivation cohort (mean age 60 ± 16 years, 43 men), the ratio of R‐wave amplitude in lead V6 to that in lead V4 was significantly higher in the cusp group than in the non‐cusp group (0.64 [0.56–1.00] vs. 0.32 [0.24–0.40], p < 0.001). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis showed that a V6R/V4R amplitude index > 0.55 predicted cusp‐origin VAs with 0.80 sensitivity and 0.88 specificity (area under the curve, 0.865; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.762–0.967). In the validation cohort, the sensitivity and specificity of a V6R/V4R amplitude index > 0.55 for predicting cusp origin were 84% and 82%, respectively.ConclusionThe V6R/V4R index is a simple and practical ECG criterion for distinguishing VAs originating from the aortic cusp from those arising outside the cusp among LVOT‐VAs, with high accuracy.Keywords: aortic cusp, electrocardiogram, left ventricular outflow tract, premature ventricular contraction

Introduction

Radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) is one of the therapeutic options for idiopathic ventricular arrhythmias (VAs), including premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) and ventricular tachycardia (VT) [1, 2]. Several algorithms have been proposed to predict the origin of VAs from 12‐lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) [3, 4, 5], as PVC morphology, which reflects electrical propagation from the site of origin, serves as a preprocedural indicator of VA origin [6, 7, 8]. Although extensive research has focused on distinguishing VAs arising from the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) from those originating in the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) in idiopathic VAs with an inferior axis [9, 10, 11, 12], ECG indices that classify the detailed origin of LVOT‐VAs have not been fully investigated.

The aortic cusp is one of the most frequent sites of origin in patients with LVOT‐VAs. Although RFCA within the aortic cusp can eliminate LVOT‐VAs, a significant proportion of cases require RFCA at the aorto‐mitral continuity (AMC) and/or the mitral annulus for successful ablation. No ECG parameters have been fully established to differentiate between VAs that can be ablated from the aortic cusp and those that require ablation from other sites. Therefore, this study aimed to identify ECG indices useful for distinguishing VAs originating from the aortic cusp from those arising outside the cusp among LVOT‐VAs with an inferior axis.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This retrospective study enrolled 514 consecutive VAs in 511 patients who underwent catheter ablation for idiopathic VAs with an inferior axis at the University of Fukui, the Gunma Prefectural Cardiovascular Center, and Hamamatsu University School of Medicine between 2011 and 2021. To ensure the highest diagnostic accuracy and avoid including potentially “pseudo‐successful” sites, 93 VAs in 91 patients were excluded from this study because acute and chronic procedural success was not achieved. We excluded 262 VAs successfully ablated from the RVOT or a combination of both the RVOT and LVOT. Consequently, 159 VAs in 158 patients were included in the present study. The VAs at the University of Fukui and a subset from the Gunma Prefectural Cardiovascular Center were used as a derivation cohort (n = 79). A subset of cases from the Gunma center was assigned to the derivation cohort to align the proportions of cusp and non‐cusp origins across the two cohorts. A validation cohort (n = 80) consisted of VAs at the remaining cases from Gunma Prefectural Cardiovascular Center and all cases from Hamamatsu University School of Medicine. All patients had a normal ECG during sinus rhythm, and no structural abnormalities were apparent by physical examination or echocardiography. Successful ablation sites were classified into two groups: the cusp and the non‐cusp group (Figure 1). The cusp group was defined as VAs successfully eliminated by RFCA within or just beneath the aortic cusp, or a combination of both supra‐valvular and infra‐valvular areas of the aortic cusp, whereas the non‐cusp group was defined as VAs successfully ablated from other LVOT sites, including the AMC and the mitral annulus, or by sequential unipolar ablation involving the cusp, AMC, and mitral annulus. Baseline and ECG characteristics were compared between the cusp and non‐cusp groups. Optimal ECG indices for distinguishing the cusp group from the non‐cusp group were derived from the derivation cohort and validated in the validation cohort.

Study flowchart.

This study was approved by the Central Review Committee of Hamamatsu University School of Medicine (Approval No. 23‐094). Following this central approval, administrative permission for the implementation of the study was obtained from the hospital directors or local ethics committees at the other two participating centers (the University of Fukui and the Gunma Prefectural Cardiovascular Center). Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective observational nature of the study; however, participants retained the right to refuse enrollment through an opt‐out option.

Electrocardiographic Analysis

The 12‐lead ECG obtained before RFCA at a sweep speed of 25 mm/s was used for analysis. In this study, an experienced observer (M.M.) blinded to patients' clinical status and catheter ablation results assessed ECG parameters. In addition, to confirm that the measurements were free from any issues, a second experienced evaluator (H.T.)—also blinded to the patients' clinical status and ablation outcomes—independently verified the data. Established parameters for predicting the origin of VAs were calculated, including the R‐wave amplitude in lead I, the aVL/aVR Q‐wave amplitude index, the transition zone score, the V2 R/S amplitude index, the V2 R‐wave duration index, the V2 transition ratio, and the V2S/V3R amplitude index. The transition zone was defined as the precordial lead in which the amplitudes of the R‐ and S‐waves were equal. The transition zone score was graded in 0.5‐point increments according to the site of R‐wave transition; for example, when the transition occurred in V3 or between V3 and V4, the scores were 3 and 3.5, respectively. Figure 2 illustrates the calculation of each parameter. An S‐wave in V6 was defined as an S‐wave > 1 mV in amplitude in V6. The maximum deflection index was calculated using the original method [13]. Briefly, the time to maximum deflection was measured from the onset of the QRS complex to the point of maximum deflection in the precordial lead with the largest amplitude. This time was divided by the QRS duration to obtain the maximum deflection index. Based on our hypothesis, the R‐waves in V4 and V6, the V6R/V4R amplitude index, and the corrected V6R/V4R amplitude index were also analyzed (Figure 2). The V6R/V4R amplitude index was calculated by dividing the R‐wave amplitude in lead V6 by that in lead V4. The corrected V6R/V4R amplitude index was calculated by dividing the V6R/V4R amplitude index during VAs by that obtained during sinus rhythm.

Catheter Ablation

Catheter ablation was performed under either conscious or deep sedation. Diagnostic electrode catheters were positioned at the right ventricular apex, coronary sinus, great cardiac vein, and anterior interventricular vein at the operator's discretion. Intravenous heparin was administered to maintain an activated clotting time of 300–400 s throughout the procedure. The CARTO (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA) and EnSite (Abbott, St. Paul, MN, USA) systems were used for three‐dimensional electroanatomical mapping. Activation mapping of VAs was performed to identify their sites of origin, and pace mapping was additionally used to confirm the presumed origin. RFCA was applied at the identified origin site. Either non‐irrigated or irrigated catheters were used during RFCA. Coronary angiography was conducted before ablation within the coronary cusp. Sequential unipolar ablation from anatomically adjacent sites was performed if RFCA at the presumed origin failed. No patients underwent an epicardial approach, bipolar ablation, or RFCA from the distal coronary sinus. The VA origin was identified as the successful ablation site according to the three‐dimensional mapping system and intracardiac echocardiography (ICE). ICE was performed in nearly all cases at Hamamatsu University School of Medicine and the University of Fukui, and in approximately 75% of cases at the Gunma Prefectural Cardiovascular Center. The use of ICE enabled real‐time visualization of catheter tip contact with specific anatomical structures, such as individual aortic cusps, aortomitral continuity, and the mitral annulus. Successful catheter ablation met three criteria [5, 14] (1) absence of spontaneous or induced clinical VAs, both in the absence and presence of isoproterenol, at the end of the procedure; (2) absence of clinical VAs over 48 h of ECG monitoring in the absence of anti‐arrhythmic drugs; and (3) no recurrence of symptomatic VAs in the absence of anti‐arrhythmic drug therapy during at least 3 months of follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range [IQR]) and were compared using the unpaired t‐test, Mann–Whitney U test, or Kruskal–Wallis test, depending on data distribution and normality. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages and were analyzed using Fisher's exact test. To assess the predictive performance of each ECG parameter, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed, and the area under the curve (AUC) and optimal cutoff points were determined. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐tailed p‐value < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Representative ECGs are shown in Figures S1 and S2. There were no significant differences in age, sex, PVC burden, prevalence of VT, or left ventricular ejection fraction between the two groups. Receiver Operating Characteristic Analyses

ROC analyses were performed using electrocardiographic parameters that showed statistically significant differences between the cusp and non‐cusp groups. The ROC analysis demonstrated that the V6R/V4R amplitude index had the highest AUC value of 0.865 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.762–0.967). A V6R/V4R amplitude index > 0.55 predicted VAs originating from the aortic cusp with a sensitivity of 80%, specificity of 88%, positive predictive value of 94%, and negative predictive value of 54% (Table 3 and Figure 3A). Although a combination of conventional ECG parameters, such as QRS duration and positive concordance, might be hypothesized to differentiate cusp from non‐cusp origins, their diagnostic performance was insufficient in our cohort. Specifically, QRS duration alone yielded a low AUC of 0.593 (95% CI, 0.444–0.742). Even when combining positive concordance with a wide QRS duration (> 156 ms), the predictive performance remained limited with an AUC of 0.630 (95% CI, 0.498–0.763), which was significantly inferior to that of the V6R/V4R amplitude index.

This intra‐patient comparison eliminates inter‐individual anatomical variations, providing a pure representation of how different origins affect the electrical vector. Although the two PVCs displayed distinct morphological differences (including QRS duration), they shared the common features of an inferior axis and positive concordance, which are typical of VAs originating from the LVOT. PVC1 had a V6R/V4R amplitude index of 0.67 (> 0.55) and was successfully ablated from the left coronary cusp (LCC), whereas PVC2 had a V6R/V4R amplitude index of 0.38 and was eliminated by RFCA from the mitral annulus.

Subgroup Analysis Within the Aortic Cusp Group

The successful ablation sites within the cusp group were further categorized into three subgroups: LCC in 30 VAs, right coronary cusp (RCC) in 9, and the LCC‐RCC junction in the remaining 17. There was no significant difference in the V6R/V4R amplitude index among the three subgroups (LCC: 0.60 [0.56–0.72]; RCC: 0.70 [0.59–0.92]; LCC‐RCC junction: 0.77 [0.64–1.06]; p = 0.126 by Kruskal–Wallis test).

Validation Cohort

The validation cohort comprised 80 VAs in 80 patients (mean age, 61 ± 16 years; 44 men). The aortic cusp was the origin of VAs in 63 patients, whereas the remaining 17 patients had VAs originating from non‐cusp sites. Although there was no significant difference in R‐wave amplitude in V4 (2.4 [IQR, 1.6–3.0] mV vs. 2.3 [IQR, 1.8–3.1] mV; p = 0.685), the cusp group exhibited a higher R‐wave amplitude in V6 than the non‐cusp group (1.7 [IQR, 1.4–2.0] mV vs. 1.0 [IQR, 0.6–1.6] mV; p = 0.002). Furthermore, the V6R/V4R amplitude index was significantly higher in the cusp group than in the non‐cusp group (0.75 [IQR, 0.63–0.92] vs. 0.46 [IQR, 0.38–0.52]; p < 0.001). The distribution of the V6R/V4R amplitude index and the individual data points in the validation cohort are illustrated in Figure 3C. When the cutoff value of 0.55 for the V6R/V4R amplitude index was applied to the validation cohort, the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value for predicting cusp‐origin VAs were 84%, 82%, 95%, and 58%, respectively. These results were comparable between the derivation and validation cohorts (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

Major Findings

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate the usefulness of a novel electrocardiographic parameter—the V6R/V4R amplitude index—for distinguishing cusp‐origin VAs from non‐cusp‐origin VAs in patients with LVOT‐VAs. Importantly, this index is intended for use only after cases with right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) origins have been excluded. A V6R/V4R amplitude index > 0.55 predicted VAs originating from the aortic cusp with a sensitivity of 80% and a specificity of 88%.

Electrocardiographic Predictors for the Origin of VAs

Several electrocardiographic parameters have been reported to predict the origin of idiopathic VAs. The aVL/aVR Q‐wave amplitude index and maximum deflection index were found to be higher in LV summit VAs than in endocardial LVOT VAs [13, 15, 16]. However, these parameters did not differ between the cusp and non‐cusp groups in this study, possibly because the non‐cusp group included both LV summit and endocardial LVOT VAs. An S‐wave in V5 or V6 has been recognized as a predictor of VAs originating from the LV endocardium. However, in this study, the presence of an S‐wave in V5 or V6 was not useful in distinguishing cusp‐origin from non‐cusp‐origin VAs, likely because both groups were left‐sided. A review article reported that a QS pattern in aVR and an rS pattern in aVL are useful for identifying the origin of VAs in patients with right bundle branch block morphology [3]. However, these parameters did not differ significantly between the cusp and non‐cusp groups in this study. An R/S amplitude index in V2–4 of > 3 has been reported to indicate VAs originating from the mitral annulus [5]. In this study, the V2 R/S amplitude index was < 3 in most of the cusp group; however, the median value of the V2 R/S amplitude index was 2.95 (IQR, 1.82–3.91) in the non‐cusp group. This may be because the non‐cusp group included not only VAs from the mitral annulus but also from the AMC and LV summit.

The positive concordance, transition zone score, V2 R/S amplitude index, V2 R‐wave duration index, V2 transition ratio, and V2S/V3R amplitude index have been recognized as useful electrocardiographic parameters for differentiating between RVOT and LVOT origins. A transition zone score > 4 indicates VAs originating from the RVOT, whereas a score < 2 suggests an LVOT origin [4]. Cutoff values of 0.5 for the R‐wave duration index and 0.3 for the R/S amplitude index have been reported as useful parameters for distinguishing RVOT VAs from LVOT VAs [12]. An R‐wave deflection interval in lead V3 > 80 ms, combined with an R‐wave amplitude index in lead V1 > 0.30, can reliably differentiate LVOT from RVOT origins in idiopathic outflow tract VAs in patients with a transition zone at V3 [17]. A V2 transition ratio < 0.6 has also been reported as a useful parameter for identifying VAs originating from the RVOT in patients with a V3 transition [11]. Yoshida et al. reported that the V2S/V3R index, with a cutoff value of 1.5, outperformed other electrocardiographic criteria in differentiating LVOT from RVOT VAs [9]. In this study, these parameters showed statistically significant differences between the cusp and non‐cusp groups. The cusp and non‐cusp groups could be considered the right‐ and left‐sided origins of LVOT VAs, respectively. However, the sensitivity and specificity of these parameters were inferior to those of the V6R/V4R amplitude index in this study.

Although practicing clinicians routinely assess combined conventional parameters, such as the transition zone and QRS duration, our analyses demonstrated that QRS duration alone—or in combination with positive concordance—cannot reliably differentiate cusp from non‐cusp origins within the LVOT. The major incremental value of the V6R/V4R amplitude index lies in its ability to capture subtle differences in the lateral‐to‐mid‐precordial electrical vector break, providing superior diagnostic accuracy over conventional parameter combinations.

Clinical Impact of the V6R/V4R Amplitude Index on Differentiating Cusp From Non‐Cusp VAs

When specifically applied to LVOT‐VAs after the strict exclusion of RVOT origins, our findings demonstrated that the V6R/V4R amplitude index had the highest AUC value of 0.925 (95% CI, 0.856–0.994), with a sensitivity of 0.84 and a specificity of 0.94. The internal validation cohort confirmed the reliability and usefulness of the V6R/V4R amplitude index. The VAs originating from the aortic cusp are expected to have a stronger electrical vector directed toward lead V6 than those arising from non‐cusp LVOT sites (Figure 5). This may explain why the V6R/V4R amplitude index was higher in the cusp group than in the non‐cusp group. According to the subgroup analysis within the aortic cusp group, although the V6R/V4R amplitude index does not differentiate between specific aortic cusps (e.g., LCC vs. RCC), it serves as an excellent screening tool for distinguishing the entire cusp group from other adjacent basal origins in the LVOT.

Clinical Implications

The V6R/V4R amplitude index offers clear incremental value when applied in a stepwise diagnostic approach. In real‐world clinical practice, differentiating between RVOT and LVOT origins is the critical first step. Once a VA is estimated to originate from the LVOT based on established conventional criteria (e.g., transition zone score or V2 transition ratio), the V6R/V4R index can be subsequently utilized to specifically differentiate between aortic‐cusp and non‐cusp origins.

Predicting the detailed origin within the LVOT using this stepwise approach provides specific clinical advantages. First, it facilitates a “targeted mapping” strategy. By predicting whether the origin lies within the aortic root or the more basal non‐cusp areas, the operator can determine the most logical starting point for detailed electroanatomical mapping. Although mapping both regions is anatomically straightforward once access is achieved, starting at the most probable site streamlines the procedure and improves overall mapping efficiency. Second, predicting the specific origin aids in comprehensive procedural planning and patient counseling. Anticipating the target site allows operators to optimize the procedural workflow and equipment selection, while providing patients with tailored informed consent regarding the procedural complexity.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective observational study with a relatively small sample size, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the ability to establish causal relationships between electrocardiographic indices and VA origin. Second, although data were collected from three institutions in Japan, the findings may not be generalizable to broader populations due to potential regional and institutional differences. Third, this study excluded 262 patients with RVOT VAs, which narrows the scope of applicability. The proposed ECG indices were validated only for LVOT‐VAs with an inferior axis, thereby limiting their generalizability to broader idiopathic VA populations. Fourth, since cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and high‐density electroanatomical substrate mapping were not performed in all patients, concealed forms of cardiomyopathy or early‐stage arrhythmogenic substrates could not be completely excluded. Fifth, compared to the cusp group, the non‐cusp group had a relatively small sample size and consisted of a heterogeneous group of anatomical origins, including the AMC and various peri‐mitral regions. This imbalance and heterogeneity may influence the generalizability of our findings, and further validation in larger, more balanced cohorts is required. Therefore, future large‐scale, prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to confirm and expand upon these findings.

Conclusion

The V6R/V4R index is a simple and practical electrocardiographic criterion for distinguishing PVCs originating from the aortic cusp from those arising outside the cusp among LVOT‐VAs, with high accuracy.
