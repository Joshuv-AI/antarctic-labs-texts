Nonradiative Energy Transfer Enables a hv < Eg Response of Perovskite Quantum Dots in Si-Based Inorganic–Organic Hybrid Solar Cells
Authors: Yunqing Cao, Qinshuai Guan, Ruijin Hu, Zongguang Liu, Yuxue Zhou, Feng Xu, Xiangdong Meng, Xiulin Jiang, Shaobo Zhang, Jun Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
High performance is a crucial factor in seeking a more competitive levelized cost of electricity for the extensive popularization of c-Si solar cells. Here, CsPbBr3 quantum dots (QDs) have been first applied as the light-converting layer to enhance the full-spectrum light response, resulting in an ∼71% enhancement of power conversion efficiency within silicon-based solar cells. Remarkably, even if the photon energy is smaller than the bandgap of CsPbBr3 QDs, the long-wavelength external quantum efficiency shows a significant increase. Such surprising results can be attributed to the nonradiative energy transfer (NRET) mechanism of CsPbBr3 QDs, which can transfer long-wavelength-generated dipoles into the Si base with the assistance of a Coulomb force. Furthermore, a dipole-transferring model, which considers that the Al2O3 passivation layer would play a negative role in the NRET process, is creatively but supportively proposed. These results highlight a simple, low-cost but promising strategy to improve the performance of c-Si solar cells.

Nowadays, crystalline silicon (c-Si) based solar cells dominate ∼95% of the photovoltaic (PV) market.1 So, even an insignificant performance improvement of these commercial c-Si solar cells, to seek a more competitive levelized cost of electricity (LCOE), is highly valuable.2−4 Until now, a bunch of strategies have been demonstrated, such as constructing different stacked tandem even multijunction cell architecture (perovskite/c-Si tandem,5−7 polymer/perovskite tandem,8−10 or amorphous Si/c-Si tandem,11−13 and so on), to overcome the Shockley-Queisser limit of ∼33% for single-junction c-Si solar cells.14 For example, the perovskite thin film materials, attractive in recent years for their high spectral response, and low cost as well as a high initial performance of a single junction,15−17 can be used as the top cell, to form perovskite/Si tandem solar cells.18,19 However, there are lots of challenges in designing/fabricating tandem/multijunction, such as photocurrent matching, tunnel junction, and so on.20,21 Besides, the high-energy photons, particularly in the ultraviolet (UV) wavelength region, which have energy much higher than the bandgap, are mostly absorbed nearly at the surface of Si devices but provide few effective carriers for device performance because of more defect states at the interface.22 Therefore, exploring an easier/simpler way to efficiently utilize these high-energy photons of short wavelength even the UV region for higher performance is needed, such as using light-converting layers in front of the c-Si solar cells may be a promising approach.23−25

Therefore, employing energy transfer properties of nanostructures, such as energy-downshift quantum dots (QDs), for better utilizing the solar spectrum to improve conversion efficiency, has attracted much attention. Appropriate luminescent downshifting (LDS) materials can absorb UV light (high energy) in the low response spectral of the c-Si solar cells and emit visible light (low energy) in the region where the response of the cell is high.26,27 For example, Wang and his co-workers have applied CsPbCl3:Mn2+ QDs onto the front of the multicrystalline Si solar cells as the luminescent downconverter to improve light harvesting in the short wavelength region and accomplished a photocurrent enhancement of 5.1 mA/cm2 and power conversion efficiency (PCE) enhancement of 6.2%, which can not only directly improve the output parameters but also simplify the fabrication process.28 As to enhancing the long-wavelength response, the nonradiative energy transfer (NRET) has been found in the hybrid micro-/nano-heterostructures by marrying light-absorbing components (e.g., an adjacent layer of organic molecules or colloidal QDs, such as CdS, CdSe, and PbS QDs) to a single high-mobility/conductive semiconducting channels under optical excitation.29−35 The NRET can transfer energy to an adjacent layer through a long-range dipole–dipole interaction and can exceed both short-range charge transfer and radiative energy transfer (RET). Getting the benefit from the NRET effect of Si QDs in SiNW homojunction solar cells, an increased short-circuit current density (Jsc) from 32.7 to 38.7 mA/cm2 can be easily observed, accomplished with an improved PCE from 10.9% to 12.9%.36 However, although it is an interesting/important issue, there is little research on NRET.

As a novel and burgeoning category of QDs, inorganic cesium lead halide CsPbX3 (X = Cl, Br, I) QDs have drawn widespread interest, due to their exceptional and controllable optical/electric properties as well as great application potentials in different optoelectronic devices, such as solar cells, photodetectors, and light-emitting diodes.37−42 Due to their excellent quality in adjustable dimension, UV radiation sensitivity, as well as high transparency in the visible light wavelength region, inorganic perovskite QDs are thought promising to be used as efficient spectral converters,43−45 while their NRET effect has never been investigated when combined with a conjugate organic hole transporting layer, such as poly(3,4-ethylene dioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS), which is a rather promising, simple, and production-line-compatible method to improve their energy conversion efficiency.

In this work, we have demonstrated an undoped CsPbBr3 QDs modified SiNW inorganic–organic hybrid solar cell by using the RET and NRET effect to enhance the full-spectrum response, which can largely improve the photocurrent density (Jsc) from 21.2 mA/cm2 to 33.2 mA/cm2, accomplished with an ∼71% PCE enhancement. Even during the long-wavelength region (>550 nm), the external quantum efficiency (EQE) response still shows a significant increase, which can be attributed to the NRET effect of the CsPbBr3 QDs. Furthermore, a dipole-transferring model, which considers that the Al2O3 passivation layer would play a negative role in the NRET process, is creatively but supportively proposed. These results highlight a simple, low-cost but promising strategy to improve the performance of c-Si solar cells.

Figure 1e shows a TEM image of CsPbBr3 QDs with a counted/average size of ∼11 nm, as well as the photo of CsPbBr3 QDs shown in Figure S1a. Traditionally, such a QDs layer was spin-coated outside the insulated antireflection layer of Si-based solar cells to solely act as the light converting layer, which can convert the short-wavelength incident light into a long-wavelength one to match the sunlight spectrum. As shown in Figure 2a, the CsPbBr3 QDs have a bandgap of ∼2.34 eV, with an obvious PL peak at the 513 nm, and thus can transfer the high-energy light (hv > 2.34 eV) into the low-energy one, which is normally called down-shift. It is interesting to find that the PL intensity of CsPbBr3 QDs on Si substrates is much lower than that on quartz substrates, as shown in Figure 2a, marked and filled with a green and orange color. Meanwhile, a unique phenomenon can be observed by the PL lifetime when the CsPbBr3 QDs were spin-coated on the conductive substrates, which is quite different from that of CsPbBr3 QDs on the nonconductive/insulated substrates, as shown in Figure 2b, where the PL lifetime of CsPbBr3 QDs on Si (τSi = 4.24 ns) is nearly 6 times faster than that (τQuartz = 25.91 ns) of CsPbBr3 QDs on quartz. Similarly, such an interesting phenomenon can also be observed for CsPbCl3 (Eg∼ 3.02 eV) and CsPbCl2Br (Eg∼ 2.56 eV), as shown in Figures S4 and S5. As presented by the PL spectra shown in Figure S5a,b, the lifetime of CsPbCl3 QDs on Si is 3.56 ns, while on the quartz is 12.63 ns (faster about 3.5 times); meanwhile for CsPbCl2Br QDs it is 4.03 ns on Si, and 16.52 ns on quartz (faster about 4.1 times), which has been measured and compared in Table S1. How and what causes such a lower intensity but faster lifetime of CsPbBr3 QDs? As reported, when the light-absorbing components, such as organic molecules or inorganic QDs are married to the high-mobility semiconductor to form hybrid nanostructures, a different but functional energy transfer mechanism named NRET exists in such a composited system, as shown in Figure 2d.46 Therefore, the light absorption of CsPbBr3 QDs on the Si substrate would be followed by the longer-range energy transfer, and then the photogenerated carriers will be in the form of dipoles to go across the interface between the CsPbBr3 QDs and the Si substrate, as shown in Figure 2e. Then, with the assistance of the built-in electric fields, the separation and transport of photogenerated carriers are accomplished entirely within the Si substrate, avoiding interface charge separation issues inherent to traditional organic devices. For a clearer understanding, Al2O3 dielectric/passivated layers with different thicknesses have been deposited on the surface of the Si substrate as a space layer to verify and investigate the NRET effect of CsPbBr3 QDs. As known, the electromagnetic interaction is strongly dependent on the distance between the light-absorbing QDs and the high-mobility substrate, which can be explained within the recognized macroscopic electrodynamic theory.47 Thus, as the thickness of the Al2O3 layer increases from 0 to 20 nm, which can actually decrease the Coulomb force, the τSi increases from 4.24 to 12.18 ns, as well as the τNRET increases from 4.88 to 19.53 ns, as shown in Figure 2c. Besides, for more direct and reliable verification of the NRET effect, a simple but classic calculation of electromagnetic interaction theory has been performed, where the for CsPbBr3 QDs on the quartz substrate and for CsPbBr3 QDs on the Si@Al2O3 substrate have been calculated (more details in Supporting Information Part II). As a result, the decay rate ratio has been calculated and plotted in Figure 2f, along with the experimental measured data, as a function of the Al2O3 layer thickness (ranging from 0 to 20 nm). Expectedly, the calculated values are very consistent with those extracted from the experimental measured data. Similarly, the measured and normalized PL intensity values for spin-coated CsPbBr3 QDs on Si and quartz substrates, as well as different-thickness Al2O3 coated Si substrates, have been plotted in Figure 2f and Figure S6, which shows a monotonically increasing trend with increasing Al2O3 thickness. All of these results indicate that the NRET process of the CsPbBr3 QDs exists in such energy transfer.

Furthermore, the consequence of the NRET effect in the energy converting layer was examined in terms of the photovoltaic performances of the inorganic–organic hybrid SiNW solar cells, as shown in Figure 1g. Figure 3a shows the J–V curves of these hybrid solar cells fabricated with/without the CsPbBr3 QD layer or with/without the Al2O3 passivation layer, measured under standard AM1.5G illumination, while the corresponding Voc, Jsc, FF, and PCE are extracted and displayed in Figure 3e. Note that, among these hybrid SiNW solar cell units, without the CsPbBr3 QD layer and Al2O3 passivation layer, a very poor but normal photovoltaic performance can be obtained with a PCE value of 6.84%, along with a Voc of 0.51 V, Jsc of 21.2 mA/cm2, and FF of 63.4%. When the Al2O3 passivation layer is added, the PCE increases from 6.84% to 9.88%, accomplished with an increased Voc to 0.53 V, Jsc to 25.9 mA/cm2, and FF to 72.5%. This obvious improvement of performance parameters, especially a largely enhanced FF, as shown in Figure 3c, can be assigned to a well-passivated interface between the SiNW surface and polymer layers, which can be supported by the electron spin resonance (ESR) curves of SiNWs coated with/without an Al2O3 passivation layer, as shown in Figure 3d. Meanwhile, if just a CsPbBr3 QD layer is spin-coated around the bare SiNW sidewall surface, a worse FF (58.1%) but a better Jsc (29.2 mA/cm2) could be obtained, in sympathy with the bare SiNW sample. The increasing Jsc shows a good consistency with the EQE curves as presented in Figure 3b, where a prominent EQE enhancement dominated by a down-shift performance of the CsPbBr3 QD layer can be clearly distinguished in the short-wavelength region < 380 nm. As to the final-optimized construction of Al/SiNW/Al2O3/CsPbBr3 QD/Polymer/Ag, the PCE increases to 11.70% (champion efficiency among 15 samples in 3 batches), along with a Voc of 0.53 V, Jsc of 33.2 mA/cm2, and FF of 68.0%, showing an ∼71% enhancement, in comparison with that of the bare SiNW solar cells. Meanwhile, the average values of PCE, along with standard deviations, measured and extracted from 15 samples in 3 batches, are plotted in Figure S7d, as well as the average values of Voc, Jsc, and FF in Figure S7a–c, respectively. It can be found that the varied trends of the counted average values of these photovoltaic parameters show a good consistency with the varied trend of those parameters extracted from the J–V curves for champion efficiency of samples under the same fabrication conditions, indicating a uniform efficiency distribution as well as a reasonable reproducibility. Furthermore, the obvious large improvement of Jsc, from 21.2 mA/cm2 to 33.2 mA/cm2, can be clearly seen with an ∼57% enhancement from the bare SiNW sample to SiNW@Al2O3+QDs, which can be supported by the corresponding EQE enhancement ratio as shown in Figure 4a, where the EQE enhancement ratio is calculated using the EQE value of SiNW@Al2O3+QDs solar cells divided by that of bare SiNW samples. During the short-wavelength region (<380 nm), the EQE enhancement ratio is dominated by the down-shift performance of the CsPbBr3 QDs, with a significant value of about 300%. However, when the incident light switches to the long wavelength region (>550 nm), the EQE enhancement ratio drops rapidly down to about 150% but much larger than 100%. To verify this obviously enhanced light response arising from the NRET effect, the light reflection curves of bare SiNWs and SiNW@Al2O3+QDs samples have been measured and are plotted in Figure S8. It is clear that the light reflection of SiNW@Al2O3+QDs does not have an obvious difference in comparison with that of the bare SiNW sample, indicating that the obviously enhanced light response cannot mainly be attributed to the light harvesting effect. Thus, all these results can provide a conclusion that the NRET effect of CsPbBr3 QDs on conductive Si substrates actually works, and helps enhance the light response during the long wavelength region (>550 nm).

To gain deeper insights into the effect of the energy transferring process of the CsPbBr3 QD in hybrid solar cells, especially on the long-wavelength response, the comparisons of EQE enhancement ratios are calculated and presented in Figure 4b,c, respectively. As shown in Figure 4b, when the incident light is <380 nm, the EQE enhancement ratio of SiNW@Al2O3+QDs vs SiNW@Al2O3 is much larger than that of SiNW+QDs vs bare SiNW, which suggests that during the down-shift-determined working region, the Al2O3 passivation layer positively works. However, when the light is >550 nm (the NRET effect plays a decided role in the response enhancement), it is interesting to note that the EQE enhancement ratio of SiNW+QDs vs bare SiNW is larger than that of SiNW@Al2O3+QDs vs SiNW@Al2O3. Similarly, as shown in Figure 4c, when the wavelength is <380 nm, the EQE enhancement ratio of SiNW@Al2O3+QDs vs SiNW+QDs is larger than that of SiNW@Al2O3 vs bare SiNW, and if >550 nm, an inversion happens like above. These results suggest that the Al2O3 passivation layer may play a negative role in boosting the NRET effect. In other words, the defect states on the surface of the SiNW may help enhance the NRET effect, which is worthy of investigation.

As is well-known, when the CsPbBr3 QDs absorb the long-wavelength incident light that with the energy smaller than the bandgap (hv < Eg), the dipoles would be generated in the forbidden band, and then transferred into the Si base due to the Coulomb force, enhancing the long-wavelength response. There is a fundamentally interesting, as well practically important, issue that should be explained: whether the lifetime of dipoles can support the transferring process. When the dipoles are generated, the Coulomb force applied on the dipoles can be expressed as,3where V is the built-in potential and d is the thickness of Al2O3 layer, and thus the time of transferring process can be expressed as,456where m* is about 10–30 to 10–31 kg, e is 1.6 × 10–19 C, V is about 0.5 V, and d is 2 nm, and thus the transferring time t∼ 10–14 s can be obtained, which is much smaller than the nonradiative lifetime (magnitude of 10–9 s). Thus, the dipoles generated in the CsPbBr3 QDs can be transferred to the Si base/substrate before recombining.

Furthermore, based on the photovoltaic performance, the band profile of the CsPbBr3 QD-decorated inorganic–organic solar cell has been schematically illustrated in Figure 4d, to reveal the transferring process of NRET dipoles. According to Figure 3b, the Al2O3 layer can passivate the surface of wet-etching SiNWs, to decrease the defect states largely. Thus, during the down-shift-determined working wavelength region, the converted-light-generated carriers in Si base can be well separated and collected, because there are few defect states as recombination sites. However, switching to a long-wavelength region (>550 nm), the NRET effect cannot bring a larger EQE enhancement of the Al2O3 passivated device than that of no-Al2O3-passivated devices, which exceeds our expectations. Under the same exciton generation process in CsPbBr3 QDs and separating process in the Si base, and excluding the negligible decay effect of the Al2O3 layer, what would weaken the NRET effect on enhancing the long-wavelength response may be during the transferring process of dipoles. Thus, based on these real test data, a theoretical model for the transferring process has been reasonably proposed, where the defect states on the surface of wet-etching SiNWs can help the dipoles generated in CsPbBr3 QDs transferred into a Si base, as shown in Figure 4d.

In summary, inorganic CsPbBr3 QDs were used as a light converting layer in a Si-based inorganic–organic solar cell to improve the light response. So, an ∼71% PCE enhancement from 6.84% to 11.70% can be obviously observed after spin-coating the CsPbBr3 QDs layer and depositing the Al2O3 passivation layer. Surprisingly, EQE curves have an obvious long-wavelength enhancement when hv < Eg, which is dominated by the NRET working mechanism of QDs. Thus, a long-wavelength-generated dipoles transferring model was proposed based on the photovoltaic parameters, which reasonably thinks that the defect states on the surface of wetting-etching SiNW can help dipoles transfer. It is also important to note this first experimental demonstration of undoped CsPbBr3 QDs incorporation within inorganic–organic SiNW solar cells, which can provide a unique perspective and practical guide in seeking an easier/simpler process to fabricate high-performance c-Si solar cells compatible with industrial-mature technology.

Experimental Methods

Device Fabrication. The hybrid solar cells were fabricated with a coaxial multilayer structure of an Al back electrode/SiNW/Al2O3 layer/CsPbBr3 QDs/spiro-OMeTAD/PEDOT:PSS/Ag grid electrode, as depicted schematically in Figure 1a. First, the n-type c-Si wafer (1.7–3.2 Ω·cm, 500 μm thickness) was immersed into the solution mixed with hydrofluoric acid (HF, 5 M) and silver nitrate (AgNO3, 0.02 M) to get SiNW by a wet-chemical etching process at room temperature. Then, a homogeneous Al2O3 layer was deposited on the bare SiNW arrays by a plasma-assisted atomic layer deposition (ALD) system (Oxford OpAL), using trimethylaluminum (Al(CH3)3) and oxygen (O2) as precursor gases at 250 °C. Third, 5 mg/mL CsPbBr3 QDs in the n-hexane solution were spin-coated on the Si NWs at 2000 rpm for 1 min. Subsequently, the spiro-OMeTAD layer (72.3 mg/mL spiro-OMeTAD, 28.8 μL 4-tert-butylpyridine (tBP) and 17.5 μL lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonvl)imide (Li-TFSI) solution (520 mg Li-TFSI in 1 mL acetonitrile) all dissolved in 1 mL chlorobenzene) was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 30 s. Next, a conducting polymer of PEDOT:PSS with 5 wt % dimethyl sulfonate (DMSO) was spin-coated to improve the carrier collection properties of the interfaces, at 3000 rpm for 1 min. The SEM images of the typical wet-etched SiNWs, CsPbBr3-coated SiNWs, and spiro-OMeTAD+PEDOT:PSS-coated SiNWs are shown in Figure 1b–d, respectively. Finally, the front Ag grid electrode was prepared by DC sputtering through a shadow mask, and the Al back electrode was prepared by thermal evaporation. The final-fabricated SiNW inorganic−organic hybrid solar cell units with a radially stacked structure is schematically illustrated in Figure 1g, as well as the photo presented in Figure S1e.

Device Characterizations. The morphology was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Sigma). The microstructures are characterized by using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, a Technai G2 operated at 200 kV). The PL spectra were measured by a HORIBA Jobin Yvon system. Time-resolved PL measurements were performed on a time-correlated single-photon counting system with a 375 nm laser as the excitation light source. The light absorption curves were measured by using a UV–vis-NIR spectrophotometer (UV-3600 Plus, Shimadzu). These detailed characterizations for CsPbCl3/CsPbCl2Br/CsPbBr3 QDs can be found in Part I of Supporting Information. The photocurrent density–voltage (J–V) curves of the samples were measured under standard AM 1.5 G conditions. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) curves were measured by the QEX-10 system within a wavelength range from 300 to 1100 nm. The electron spin resonance (ESR) measurements were carried out by using a Bruker EMX 10/12 spectrometer to characterize the defect states on the surfaces of SiNWs.
