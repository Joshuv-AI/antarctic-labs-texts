Development and temporal validation of a machine learning model to estimate total MoCA scores from telephone-based items in patients with mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Chan-Young Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodic cognitive monitoring for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is crucial. However, standard assessments like the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) require in-person administration due to visual-based items, creating significant accessibility barriers.ObjectiveThis study aims to develop and rigorously validate a machine learning model to accurately estimate total MoCA scores using only telephone-based (remote) items and demographic information.MethodsData from three independent MCI cohorts (2022–2024) were used. Data from 2022 to 2023 (N=425) formed the training set; 2024 data (N=356) were the temporal test set. Models were trained to predict the 8-point visual-based score using 37 telephonic/demographic features. Fivefold cross-validation (80% training/20% validation per fold) was performed exclusively on the training set to compare three heterogeneous machine learning models. Random Forest, Linear Regression and XGBoost models were compared. The final score was reconstructed by summing actual telephonic scores with the predicted visual score.FindingsIn the fivefold cross-validation on the training set, the Random Forest model achieved the lowest mean Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE=1.2034) of 1.2034 points (SD=0.0407; 95% CI 1.1528 to 1.2540) for Visual Score prediction, though pairwise differences between models did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting the limited power of fivefold comparisons. When applied to the 2024 temporal test set, the reconstructed total score showed high concordance with the actual total MoCA score (RMSE=1.29 points; mean absolute error=1.01 points; R2=0.9248). The most important features were education (28.7% importance) and age (13.8%), followed by sustained attention (4.1%).ConclusionsOur machine learning model accurately and reliably predicts total 30-point MoCA scores using only telephone-based information. The model demonstrated satisfactory generalisation performance on a temporally separated cohort, supporting its potential clinical applicability pending external validation.

Background

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is widely recognised as a critical transitional stage between the normal ageing process and dementia, particularly Alzheimer’s disease.1 Individuals with MCI face a substantially higher risk of progressing to dementia compared with the general population. A recent systematic review found that approximately 41.5% of individuals with MCI transitioned to dementia over an average follow-up period of 5.2 years.2 Given this elevated risk of progression, periodic monitoring of cognitive function in individuals with MCI constitutes a core component of clinical management. Such monitoring is essential for tracking disease progression, assessing the efficacy of potential therapeutic interventions and ensuring that patients receive timely and appropriate preventive care.

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) is a widely adopted and validated screening tool that is highly sensitive for detecting MCI.3 However, the standard MoCA is fundamentally a face-to-face assessment. A significant portion of the score (8 out of 30) comes from visuospatial and motor tasks that require visual stimuli and motor (drawing) responses. These include tasks such as alternating numbers and letters (Trail Making B), reproducing a three-dimensional cube (Cube Copying), drawing a clock (drawing a clock face and setting the time to ‘11:10’), and naming objects (recognising three animals in a picture: a lion, a rhinoceros and a camel).2 This fundamental reliance on sensory inputs is a significant methodological challenge, as most standardised cognitive tests, including the MoCA, presume sufficient hearing and vision for valid administration.4 A recent scoping review highlighted that older adults with sensory impairments may consequently underperform on these tests, making it difficult to differentiate true cognitive decline from sensory limitations.4

The recent COVID-19 pandemic has accelerated the adoption of telehealth, exposing significant gaps in remote cognitive assessment.5 For patients with MCI who are older or have mobility or transportation difficulties, telephone-based (audio-only) follow-up is practically essential.6 While variations such as the Telephone MoCA (T-MoCA) exist,7 these differ in scoring criteria and domains, limiting direct comparison with the standard 30-point MoCA score, which underlies extensive clinical research and longitudinal data. This discrepancy poses significant challenges for clinicians seeking to integrate remote assessments into existing patient records.

Objective

To address these challenges and bridge the gap between remote and in-person assessments, we hypothesised that scores on omitted visual-based items are not random noise but are intrinsically correlated with other cognitive domains (eg, executive function, attention) that can be measured via telephone. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to develop a machine learning model that accurately estimates the 30-point MoCA score in patients with MCI using only remotely available data (ie, the telephone-based MoCA items and basic demographics including age, education and sex).

Methods

Study design and data source

This study represents a secondary analysis of baseline assessment data originally collected as part of routine Busan Korean Medicine Dementia Prevention Project (BKMDP), organised by Busan Metropolitan City and the Busan Association of Korean Medicine, employing a rigorous time-series validation design. BKMDP is a 6-month community-based programme targeting individuals at high risk of dementia, providing acupuncture and herbal medicine twice weekly to patients with MCI. This programme is implemented across approximately 100 Korean medical clinics in the Busan region.8 For the current study, baseline assessment data of newly recruited MCI patients, consecutively enrolled over 3 years (2022, 2023 and 2024) through BKMDP, were selected as the primary analytical dataset. Each cohort represented an independent, cross-sectional group newly participating in its respective year. All participants received a clinical diagnosis of MCI based on clinical assessments performed by public health centre staff or the participating Korean medicine doctors. This analysis used only the pre-intervention baseline assessment. Data from the 2022 (N=211) and 2023 (N=214) cohorts were combined to form a training set (N=425). Data from the 2024 cohort (N=356) were retained as a completely unseen test set to evaluate the model’s performance on future data, thereby assessing the model’s generalisability and temporal stability.

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research. This study was reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD)+Artificial Intelligence reporting guideline (online supplemental file 1).9

Montreal cognitive assessment

The Korean version of the MoCA was administered by trained assessors at baseline for all participants.10 The MoCA is a 30-point test assessing eight cognitive domains: visuospatial/executive function (5 points), naming (3 points), attention (6 points), language (3 points), abstraction (2 points), delayed recall (5 points) and orientation (6 points). One additional point is added for individuals with ≤12 years of education. For the purposes of this study, MoCA items were categorised into two groups based on their feasibility for telephone administration.

Non-telephonic (Visual-based) items (8 points total)

These items require visual stimuli or motor responses and cannot be administered over the phone. They include (1) Trail Making B test (connecting alternating numbers and letters, 1 point); (2) Cube copying (reproducing a 3D cube drawing, 1 point); (3) Clock drawing: drawing the clock contour (1 point), correctly placing the numbers (1 point) and setting the hands to ‘ten past eleven’ (1 point) and (4) Naming: identifying three animals from pictures—lion, rhinoceros and camel (3 points total).

Telephonic (audio-based) items (22 points total)

These items can be administered and scored via telephone as they rely solely on verbal responses. They include items from attention (digit span, vigilance, serial 7s), language (sentence repetition), abstraction, delayed recall and orientation domains.

Variable definition and feature engineering

The input features (X, 37 total) were strictly limited to data that could be collected via telephone-only interaction.

Demographics (3 features)

Age (in years), education (in years) and sex. These were included as critical predictors known to influence cognitive reserve and test performance.

Telephonic MoCA items (34 features)

All subitem scores that are verbally administered and scored: (1) Attention items: Letter A tapping task (sustained attention), forward digit span, backward digit span (working memory), serial 7 subtraction (five items: 100–7, −7, −7, −7 and −7); (2) Language items: Sentence repetition (2 sentences testing working memory and language); (3) Abstraction items: Similarity judgement between two words (2 pairs); (4) Delayed Recall items: Free recall of 5 words after a delay; (5) Cued Recall items: Category cue recall (5 words) and multiple-choice recognition (5 words) and (6) Orientation items: Temporal orientation (date, month, year, day of week) and spatial orientation (place, city).

To avoid the methodological flaw of including parts of the target in the input (ie, predicting a total score that includes the input score), we defined a more robust prediction task. The model’s target variable (Y) was defined as the sum of the 8 non-telephonic items (Visual Score, range 0–8 points), which is the sum of the 8 non-telephonic items: Trail Making B (1 pt), Cube copying (1 pt), Clock contour (1 pt), Clock numbers (1 pt), Clock hands (1 pt), Naming lion (1 pt), Naming rhinoceros (1 pt) and Naming camel (1 pt). This isolates the missing information as the sole prediction target. After model training and prediction, the final estimated total MoCA score was reconstructed by summing the actual telephonic item scores with the model-predicted Visual Score.

Statistical analysis and model development

All data processing and modelling were performed using Python (V.3.12). The primary libraries used for analysis were pandas (V.2.3.0), scikit-learn (V.1.7.2) and xgboost (V.3.1.1). The analysis process consisted of following main steps (figure 1).

Model training and selection (cross-validation)

Each fold used 80% of the training data for model fitting and the remaining 20% for validation. Fivefold cross-validation (CV) was performed exclusively on the training set (2022–2023 cohorts, N=425) to compare the performance of three heterogeneous machine learning models.

Linear Regression (LR): A baseline linear model to assess the degree of linear relationships between predictors and the target.

Random Forest Regressor (RF)11: An ensemble learning method based on decision trees, known for its robustness to overfitting and ability to capture complex non-linear interactions. The model was configured with 100 trees (n_estimators=100) and a fixed random state for reproducibility.

XGBoost Regressor (XGBoost)12: A gradient boosting framework optimised for speed and performance on structured data. The model was configured with 100 estimators, squared error objective function and a fixed random state.

All models were constructed within scikit-learn Pipeline objects to ensure proper preprocessing and avoid data leakage.13 The pipeline incorporated a SimpleImputer (median strategy) as a methodological safeguard, although no missing values were present in the dataset (missing rate=0.00% across all cohorts). StandardScaler was additionally applied for LR only. Model performance was evaluated using Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) as the primary metric; mean RMSE, SD and 95% CIs (t-distribution, df=4) were computed across folds. Pairwise differences in fold-level RMSE distributions were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The model achieving the lowest mean RMSE was selected as the optimal model.

Final training and temporal validation

The optimal model was retrained on the entire training set (N=425) from 2022 to 2023. To assess whether retraining on the full dataset introduced overfitting relative to the cross-validated estimates, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by averaging the predictions of the five individual fold-trained models and comparing the resulting performance metrics against those of the retrained model. This finalised model was then applied to the unseen 2024 test set (N=356) to generate Visual Score predictions.

Total score reconstruction and evaluation

For each participant in the 2024 test set, the final estimated total MoCA score was reconstructed by combining the actual observed telephonic item scores with the model’s predicted Visual Score. The final performance was comprehensively evaluated by comparing this reconstructed score to the actual total MoCA score (the sum of all 30 items) in the 2024 data. The key evaluation metrics were: (1) RMSE, which measures the average prediction error; (2) mean absolute error (MAE), which measures the average absolute prediction error and (3) Coefficient of Determination (R2), which measures the proportion of the variance in the actual scores explained by the predicted scores.

Feature importance analysis

For the final model, feature importance was examined using two complementary approaches: (1) Gini impurity-based importance derived from the RF model structure and (2) SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values,14 which provide a theoretically grounded, model-agnostic quantification of each feature’s marginal contribution to individual predictions. SHAP analysis was performed on the 2024 test set using the TreeExplainer method.

Classification performance analysis

To evaluate whether the reconstructed total score could reliably classify patients relative to commonly used MoCA clinical thresholds, a binary classification analysis was performed on the 2024 test set. The predicted total scores were evaluated against actual scores at three cut-off points: ≥26 (standard MoCA threshold for normal cognition), ≥23 and ≥22. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value were computed for each threshold.

Cohort comparability assessment

To formally assess the comparability of the three annual cohorts, continuous variables (age, education, total MoCA score and Visual Score) were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with post hoc pairwise comparisons conducted using Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test. Sex distribution was compared using the χ2 test.

Sample size

The sample size for this study was predetermined based on the annual enrolment numbers in the BKMDP programme, with N=425 for the training set (summarised for 2022–2023) and N=356 for the test set (summarised for 2024). With 37 predictor variables, the predictor-to-sample size ratio was 11.49:1, exceeding the minimum sample size of 10–15 per predictor variable typically recommended for regression models.15 16 To avoid the circularity inherent in post hoc power analyses that use the observed effect size,17 an a priori power analysis was conducted using a conservatively hypothesised large effect size (Cohen’s f2=0.35), consistent with estimates from prior machine learning studies in cognitive assessment.18 At α=0.05 with N=425 and p=37 predictors, the estimated statistical power exceeded 0.999, indicating that this study was adequately powered to detect clinically meaningful predictive relationships. For reference, the post-hoc analysis based on the observed R2=0.9248 (f2=12.29) yielded a power exceeding 0.99; however, this estimate should be interpreted with caution given its circularity.

Findings

Participant characteristics

The mean age across all cohorts was approximately 72–73 years (range: 71.9–72.8 years), with a relatively consistent education level of approximately 8 years (range: 8.2–8.7 years). The participants were predominantly female, comprising 82.9% of the 2022 cohort, 79.0% of the 2023 cohort and 78.1% of the 2024 test cohort. The mean total MoCA scores ranged from 24.2 to 25.3 points, with Visual Scores ranging from 5.9 to 6.3 points and Telephonic Scores ranging from 18.3 to 19.3 points. These values are consistent with the MCI population targeted by the BKMDP programme. Formal statistical comparison confirmed that the three cohorts were comparable in age (F=1.93, p=0.146), education (F=1.40, p=0.248), and sex distribution (χ2=2.00, p=0.368). One-way ANOVA revealed statistically significant differences in total MoCA scores (F=4.00, p=0.019) and Visual Scores (F=4.76, p=0.009) across cohorts. Post hoc Tukey HSD testing identified that these differences were localised to specific pairings: total MoCA scores differed significantly between the 2022 and 2024 cohorts (mean difference=−1.12 points, p-adj=0.015), and Visual Scores differed between the 2023 and 2024 cohorts (mean difference=−0.36 points, p-adj=0.006). Training cohorts (2022 and 2023) were not significantly different from each other on any variable, supporting the validity of their combination into a single training set. The magnitude of the observed differences was small (≤1.12 points on a 30-point scale), inconsistent in direction and confined to cognitive scores rather than demographic characteristics (table 1).

Model selection and CV performance

In the fivefold CV on the training set (2022–2023 combined, N=425), the prediction task was to estimate the Visual Score (0–8 point scale) from the 37 input features. The RF model achieved the lowest mean RMSE of 1.2034 points (SD=0.0407; 95% CI 1.1528 to 1.2540), outperforming LR (RMSE=1.2290, SD=0.0759; 95% CI 1.1348 to 1.3232) and substantially outperforming XGBoost (RMSE=1.3223, SD=0.0360; 95% CI 1.2776 to 1.3671) (online supplemental file 2). Pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests revealed no statistically significant differences between models (RF vs LR: W=4.0, p=0.438; RF vs XGBoost: W=0.0, p=0.063; LR vs XGBoost: W=1.0, p=0.125). These non-significant results should be interpreted in the context of the inherent power limitation of five-fold comparisons (minimum achievable p=0.0625); notably, the RF vs XGBoost comparison approached this boundary (p=0.063) (online supplemental file 3). RF outperformed LR but at a modest scale, suggesting that the relationship between verbal cognitive domains and spatiotemporal performance is not purely linear and that ensemble methods benefit from the ability to capture complex interactions. The RF model also exhibited lower fold-to-fold variability, demonstrating more stable performance. Based on these results, RF was selected as the optimal model for the final temporal validation (table 2).

Temporal validation performance (2024 test set)

The RF model was retrained using the entire training dataset from 2022 to 2023 (N=425) and then applied to a separately reserved 2024 test dataset (N=356). The model calculated the final estimated total MoCA score by summing the predicted Visual Score and the actual telephone-based score. This calculated score was then compared with the actual total MoCA scores of the 2024 cohort. The results showed a high concordance between the model’s predictions and the actual scores. An R2 value of 0.9248 demonstrates that the model, utilising only remote telephonic data and demographic information, explains over 92.4% of the variance in patients’ actual face-to-face MoCA scores. The magnitude of the mean prediction error (RMSE) was 1.29 points on a 30-point scale (rounding 1.2851), and MAE was 1.01 points (rounding 1.0062). Detailed performance metrics are presented in table 2, and figure 2 demonstrates a strong linear relationship between the predicted and actual scores.

Feature importance analysis

Analysis of the RF model’s feature importances provided valuable insights into which variables were most predictive of the missing Visual Score. The demographic variables were the most critical predictors. The two most dominant predictors were years of education (28.7%) and age (13.8%). Notably, participant sex emerged as the third most important demographic and the fifth most important predictor overall (2.8%). Following the demographic variables, the cognitive item in the attention domain, the Letter A tapping task for sustained attention (4.1%) and a cued recall item (2.9%) were the most influential (online supplemental file 4; figure 3).

SHAP analysis

SHAP analysis of the 2024 test set corroborated and extended the Gini importance findings. Education (years) was by far the dominant predictor (mean |SHAP|=0.523), approximately six times greater than the second-ranked predictor, age (mean |SHAP|=0.086). Notably, sex emerged as the third most influential predictor by SHAP value (mean |SHAP|=0.081), ranking higher than its Gini-based position (fifth, 2.8%), suggesting that Gini importance may underestimate sex’s true marginal contribution to individual predictions. Among cognitive items, memory cued recall (multiple choice, item 1; mean |SHAP|=0.059) and sustained attention (letter A tapping; mean |SHAP|=0.053) were the most influential. The convergence between SHAP and Gini importance rankings for the top predictors (education, age) supports the robustness of these findings (figure 4; online supplemental file 5).

Classification performance at MoCA cut-off thresholds

When the reconstructed total scores were used to classify patients relative to standard MoCA clinical thresholds, the model demonstrated excellent discriminative performance across all cut-off points evaluated. At the standard threshold of ≥26 (N_above=149, N_below=207), the model achieved an AUC of 0.9841, sensitivity of 0.8926, and specificity of 0.9614. At ≥23 (N_above=231, N_below=125), AUC was 0.9746, sensitivity 0.9091, and specificity 0.8960. At ≥22 (N_above=262, N_below=94), AUC was 0.9854, sensitivity 0.9198, and specificity 0.9468. These results suggest that the model’s continuous score estimates can reliably support clinically meaningful binary classifications (figure 5; table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate whether retraining the final model on the full training set introduced overfitting relative to cross-validated estimates, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by averaging predictions from the five individual fold-trained models. The ensemble approach yielded an RMSE of 1.2729, MAE of 1.0019, and R2=0.9262, compared with the primary retrained model’s RMSE of 1.2851, MAE of 1.0062, and R2=0.9248. The negligible differences (ΔRMSE=0.0122, ΔR²=0.0014) confirm that retraining on the full training set did not meaningfully inflate performance estimates (online supplemental file 6).

Statistical power

An a priori power analysis using a conservatively hypothesised large effect size (f2=0.35) indicated that the study was adequately powered (power >0.999 at α=0.05). Even at a medium effect size (f2=0.15), power remained high at 0.998. Post hoc power analysis based on the observed R2=0.9248 (f2=12.29) also exceeded 0.99, although this post hoc estimate is subject to circularity bias and should be interpreted accordingly. The favourable predictor-to-sample size ratio (11.49:1), combined with the use of RF, an ensemble method inherently resistant to overfitting, minimised the risk of model overfitting and supported the generalisability of the study results.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully developed and temporally validated an RF machine learning model that can accurately estimate the total MoCA score of MCI patients using only telephone-based assessment items and basic demographic information. When applied to a completely unpredictable future cohort (2024 data, N=356), the model performed exceptionally well, with an R2 of 0.9248, RMSE of 1.29, and MAE of 1.01. This means that, on average, the model’s predicted values differed from the actual total MoCA score by approximately 1 point out of 30.

It is important to contextualise the high R2 value (0.9248) observed in this study. The reconstruction approach sums the actual telephonic score (22 points, directly observed) with only the predicted Visual Score (0–8 points). Consequently, a substantial portion of the variance in the final total score is attributable to the directly measured telephonic component rather than to the model’s prediction per se. This structural feature of the reconstruction paradigm should be considered when interpreting the R2 value; it does not invalidate the approach but reflects the intentional design of predicting only the missing information. The clinically meaningful performance metric is therefore the Visual Score prediction accuracy (CV RMSE=1.2034 on a 0–8 point scale) and the final total score error (RMSE=1.29 on a 30-point scale).

The primary clinical significance of this study is that our model can reliably substitute for total MoCA scores even in telephone-based environments. This offers several substantial practical advantages. First, it provides an option for cognitive monitoring without requiring clinic visits for elderly patients with MCI who have limited mobility or transportation access, or for residents in rural areas.6 Furthermore, telephone-based assessment is advantageous in terms of cost-effectiveness and accessibility, allowing for more frequent detection of cognitive changes compared with traditional face-to-face examinations.19 This enables clinicians to track patients’ cognitive trajectory at shorter intervals, thereby increasing the likelihood of early detection of critical declines or conversion to dementia. Additionally, with the widespread adoption of telemedicine as a common healthcare delivery method following the COVID-19 pandemic,5 this model can serve as a validated tool to effectively integrate cognitive assessment into telehealth platforms while maintaining compatibility with the widely used MoCA scoring system.

Beyond its support for remote monitoring, the most important clinical significance of this model lies in its ability to improve both accessibility and accuracy of assessment for patients with sensory impairments. In the conventional MoCA assessment, the 8-point visual assessment items are inherently limited for individuals with severe visual impairment, as a low score may reflect sensory limitations rather than genuine cognitive decline.4 Our model calculates a 'Visual Score’ by using telephone-based auditory items and demographic information. This approach effectively separates the influence of patients’ visual-motor abilities during the assessment process. Consequently, it mitigates sensory-related measurement errors that can occur in face-to-face examinations, approaching the MoCA 30-point system, even for patients with MCI and visual impairment.

Although the performance difference between RF and LR models was not substantial, the selection of RF as the final model suggests that the relationship between telephone-based cognitive performance and visuospatial performance exhibits complex, nonlinear interactions rather than a simple linear correlation. Such characteristics can be more effectively captured by ensemble tree-based models.11 The robustness of the RF model against overfitting was further supported by the negligible difference between the retrained model and the ensemble sensitivity analysis (ΔRMSE=0.0122), and by the inherent out-of-bag error estimation mechanism of the RF algorithm, which provides continuous internal validation during training.

The results of the feature importance analysis also showed clinically consistent patterns. Specifically, education (28.7%) and age (13.8%) emerged as the most significant influencing factors, reaffirming their critical roles in determining cognitive reserve and the trajectory of cognitive ageing.20 SHAP analysis further underscored the dominance of education (mean |SHAP|=0.523), revealing a magnitude approximately six times greater than that of age, a finding that was less apparent in the Gini-based rankings. Notably, sex emerged as the third most influential predictor by SHAP value (mean |SHAP|=0.081), suggesting that Gini importance may underestimate its true marginal contribution to individual predictions. This finding warrants future subgroup analyses to determine whether model accuracy differs systematically by sex. It is worth noting that the importance of attention-related tasks (particularly sustained attention, 4.1%) as a secondary outcome is consistent with modern neurocognitive models. Visuospatial-executive tasks do not merely measure visual processing; instead, they demand a complex interplay of several cognitive domains, including executive function, working memory, processing speed and sustained attention.21 These functions are predominantly associated with the fronto-parietal network and are similarly activated in language-based attention tasks, such as serial 7 subtraction and digit span.22 The developed model in current study effectively learnt the common neurocognitive structures underlying these tasks by predicting the outcomes of visuospatial motor execution tasks using the performance outcomes of a telephone-accessible attention assessment task.

The primary strengths of this study include its rigorous methodology and novel score estimation paradigm, which distinctly set it apart from conventional alternative assessment methods. From a methodological perspective, we defined the target variable (Y) based on missing Visual Score to prevent data leakage. Subsequently, the robustness of the model was verified through time-series validation, simulating real-world conditions using independent cohort data from 2024. Clinically, this score estimation approach effectively avoids the equivalence issues raised in recent research,19 which demonstrated that alternative scales (T-MoCA and K-MoCA-22) are not statistically equivalent in the elderly population. Instead of creating a new assessment scale, our model is designed to be fully compatible with the existing 30-point MoCA standard. This ensures continuity of longitudinal data essential for MCI tracking, allowing clinicians to seamlessly integrate remote and in-person data. Furthermore, this model, based on real-world BKMDP data, is optimised for the Korean elderly population, with an average education level of 8.2 to 8.7 years. Empirical analysis revealed that education level served as a key predictor (28.7%), highlighting the need for population-specific customised models and demonstrating high ecological validity in the Korean clinical setting.

However, several limitations must be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study population consisted solely of individuals with MCI. Because the developed tool was designed for monitoring rather than diagnosis, it inherently falls short of providing diagnostic criteria to distinguish between MCI patients and cognitively normal individuals. Second, all study participants underwent cross-sectional assessments at a single time point. While the model was adept at accurately predicting total scores at a specific time point, validation of within-individual changes over time (ie, how well the ΔPredicted Score reflects the ΔActual Score) has not yet been conducted. Such validation is a crucial next step to establish the tool’s usefulness as a long-term monitoring tool. Third, while our analysis included participant sex as a predictor, we did not perform subgroup analyses to confirm if the model’s accuracy is equivalent across sexes; this remains an area for future validation. Fourth, data collection was limited to the Busan region. Therefore, the model’s performance may vary in populations with different educational levels, language backgrounds or cultural contexts. Additionally, the clinical diagnoses recorded in the BKMDP dataset did not include MCI subtype classification (amnestic vs non-amnestic MCI). Given the well-established heterogeneity between aMCI and non-aMCI in terms of cognitive profiles and MoCA domain contributions,23 the inclusion of mixed MCI subtypes may represent a source of uncharacterised clinical heterogeneity. Future studies with subtype-stratified data would help to clarify whether model performance varies across MCI subtypes. Furthermore, all participants were Korean-speaking older adults residing in the Busan metropolitan area, with a mean education level of approximately 8 years. Language-specific characteristics of the Korean MoCA items (eg, sentence repetition difficulty, culturally specific orientation items), educational norms and cultural attitudes towards cognitive assessment may all influence item-level performance and feature importance rankings. The finding that education level was by far the most dominant predictor (Gini importance=28.7%; mean |SHAP|=0.523) likely reflects population-specific characteristics of the BKMDP cohort and may not generalise to populations with markedly different educational profiles. Therefore, extensive external validation, including a more diverse demographic group, is essential for widespread applicability.

Clinical implications

In this study, we developed and rigorously validated an RF machine learning model that predicts MoCA scores (out of 30) using only telephone-based assessment items and demographic information. The model explained over 92.4% of the total variance (R2=0.9248) and achieved a MAE of approximately 1 point (1.01). Furthermore, when applied to additional temporally separated cohorts, it demonstrated satisfactory generalisation performance, supporting its potential clinical applicability pending external validation. This model is particularly valuable because it offers a practical alternative for individuals with MCI who face challenges with cognitive assessment due to factors such as remote monitoring constraints or visual impairment. It can be seamlessly integrated into existing clinical workflows and continuous assessment systems while maintaining compatibility with the standard MoCA scoring system.
