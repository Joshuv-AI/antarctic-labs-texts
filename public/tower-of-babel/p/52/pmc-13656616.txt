Safety and feasibility of bronchoscopic indocyanine green–methylene blue versus CT-guided autologous blood–methylene blue localization for pulmonary nodules: a propensity score–matched analysis
Authors: Jingjing Pan, Jianyang Wu, Jianxin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreoperative localization may facilitate intraoperative identification of selected small, subsolid, or poorly palpable pulmonary nodules during video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). This study compared the safety and feasibility of bronchoscopic indocyanine green plus methylene blue (ICG + MB) localization with CT-guided autologous blood plus methylene blue (blood + MB) localization.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included patients who underwent preoperative localization for selected pulmonary nodules between October 2019 and October 2025. Propensity score matching with replacement was performed using age, sex, body mass index, nodule size, and shortest distance to the pleura as covariates. Outcomes included localization success, post-localization pneumothorax, intraoperative visibility score (0–5), and localization time. Firth logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with post-localization pneumothorax.ResultsAfter matching, 17 patients in the bronchoscopic ICG + MB group and 41 unique patients in the CT-guided blood + MB group were analyzed. Localization success rates were comparable (94.1% vs. 90.2%, p = 1.000), and visibility scores did not differ significantly (p = 0.210). Pneumothorax occurred in 22.0% of the CT-guided group and in none of the bronchoscopic group (p = 0.047). CT-guided localization (OR = 9.85, 95% CI: 1.09-1307.06; p = 0.039) and WBC count (OR = 1.46, 95% CI:1.03–2.24; p = 0.036) were associated with pneumothorax. The model yielded an AUC of 0.810.ConclusionsIn this matched cohort, bronchoscopic ICG + MB localization showed localization success and intraoperative visibility comparable to CT-guided blood + MB localization. Pneumothorax occurred less frequently in the bronchoscopic group. Bronchoscopic localization may represent a feasible airway-based option for selected nodules when preoperative localization is considered necessary and avoidance of pleural puncture is preferred.

Background

With the widespread implementation of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) for lung cancer screening, the detection rate of small pulmonary nodules, particularly ground-glass and part-solid nodules, has increased substantially.Large-scale screening trials have demonstrated that more than half of early-stage lung cancers are detected when the nodule diameter is ≤ 2 cm [1, 2]. Many of these lesions are small, subsolid, or located away from the pleural surface, making intraoperative identification challenging during minimally invasive surgery.

The optimal surgical management of small peripheral lung cancers has evolved in recent years. Recent randomized trials have shown that sublobar resection can be an appropriate option for selected patients with small peripheral non-small cell lung cancer [3, 4]. In the JCOG0802/WJOG4607L trial, segmentectomy showed favorable survival outcomes compared with lobectomy in patients with small peripheral NSCLC, although local recurrence was more frequent after segmentectomy. The CALGB 140,503 trial also demonstrated that sublobar resection was noninferior to lobectomy with respect to disease-free survival in patients with peripheral NSCLC measuring 2 cm or less and pathologically confirmed node-negative disease. These findings have increased the clinical importance of achieving accurate intraoperative lesion identification and adequate surgical margins during sublobar resection [3–5].

However, not all small peripheral nodules require preoperative localization. Lesions that are solid, superficial, and easily palpable may be managed by direct thoracoscopic inspection, palpation, or biopsy-first strategies when appropriate [6]. Preoperative localization is mainly considered for lesions that are difficult to identify intraoperatively, including pure or part-solid ground-glass nodules, small nodules with poor tactile feedback, lesions located relatively deep from the pleural surface, or nodules for which limited resection requires precise margin planning [5, 7]. Therefore, the value of localization lies not in replacing diagnostic biopsy or standard oncologic principles, but in facilitating accurate intraoperative identification and margin-secure thoracoscopic resection in selected patients.

CT-guided percutaneous localization is widely used because of its technical simplicity and direct access to the target lesion [8–10]. However, this approach requires transpleural puncture and may be associated with complications such as pneumothorax and pulmonary hemorrhage. Bronchoscopic navigation localization has emerged as an airway-based alternative [7, 11]. By integrating virtual bronchoscopy with thin-slice CT reconstruction, this technique allows bronchial pathway planning and dye delivery through the airway without direct pleural puncture.

Indocyanine green (ICG) emits strong near-infrared fluorescence, whereas methylene blue (MB) provides visible surface staining.The combination of these agents may provide complementary fluorescence and visible-light signals, which can assist intraoperative identification of the marked area [12–14]. However, comparative data between bronchoscopic ICG + MB localization and CT-guided autologous blood + MB localization remain limited, particularly in propensity score–matched cohorts. This study aimed to compare the safety and feasibility of these two localization strategies for selected pulmonary nodules and to identify factors associated with post-localization pneumothorax.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective, single-center study included patients who underwent preoperative localization of pulmonary nodules in the Department of Thoracic Surgery at The First Hospital of Putian between October 2019 and October 2025.

Inclusion criteria were: (I) solid nodules with a maximum diameter ≤ 1.5 cm and a distance ≥ 0.5 cm from the visceral pleura; (II) pure GGNs; (III) mixed GGNs with a maximum diameter ≤ 2 cm and a distance ≥ 0.5 cm from the pleura.Preoperative localization was considered when the operating surgeon judged that the lesion might be difficult to identify intraoperatively because of its subsolid appearance, small size, limited pleural surface change, insufficient tactile feedback during VATS, or the need to ensure an adequate margin during planned sublobar resection.

Exclusion criteria included: (I) vascular pulmonary lesions or nodules near the pulmonary hilum or major blood vessels; (II) severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction or bleeding tendencies; (III) history of thoracic surgery (thoracotomy), pleural infection, or ipsilateral pleural thickening and adhesions.

Between January 2019 and October 2025, 127 patients met the inclusion criteria and underwent CT-guided autologous blood + methylene blue (MB) localization, which has been routinely performed at our center since 2019. During the same study period,17patients met the inclusion criteria and underwent bronchoscopic indocyanine green (ICG) + MB localization after the technique was introduced at our institution. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of The First Hospital of Putian (Approval No.2018-008). Given its retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Variables and outcomes

Collected variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), nodule size, shortest distance from the nodule to the pleura, pulmonary function parameters including forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC), maximal voluntary ventilation (MVV), white blood cell (WBC) count, presence of emphysema, localization time, patient position, operative duration, surgical procedure, pathological diagnosis, resection margin status, and intraoperative visibility score.

Intraoperative visibility was assessed using a 6-point scale as follows: 0 = unidentifiable; 1 = identifiable but blurred; 2 = distinct without central erythema; 3 = distinct with central erythema; 4 = target-like appearance; and 5 = excessive dye diffusion or dispersion. Grades 0, 1, and 5 were considered localization failure. For this study, the scores were retrospectively assigned based on operative records, intraoperative photographs, and available thoracoscopic videos. Two thoracic surgeons independently reviewed these materials and assigned the visibility score. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a senior thoracic surgeon until consensus was reached.

Successful localization was defined as clear intraoperative identification of the marking area under thoracoscopic view, either by direct visualization of autologous blood or methylene blue staining, or by near-infrared fluorescence detection of indocyanine green, enabling accurate resection of the target lesion. Pneumothorax was defined as the presence of air within the pleural cavity on post-localization CT imaging. Pulmonary hemorrhage was defined as hemoptysis after localization or intraoperative evidence of bloody pleural effusion during VATS.

CT-guided localization procedure

Preoperative CT-guided localization was performed using a mixture of methylene blue and autologous blood. On the day before or on the day of surgery, 5–10 mL of peripheral venous blood was collected and mixed with 1 mL of methylene blue. After the patient was positioned in the CT suite, the puncture route was planned according to the location of the nodule on preoperative CT images.

An 18-G PTC needle (Hakko Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was gradually advanced to within approximately 1 cm of the target lesion under CT guidance. After confirming that the needle tip was not located within a bronchus or blood vessel, approximately 3 mL of the methylene blue–autologous blood mixture was injected during needle withdrawal. No additional iodinated contrast material was used. Because methylene blue and autologous blood are not inherently radiopaque, successful post-procedural marking was confirmed indirectly on CT by identifying a newly developed localized parenchymal ground-glass opacity, patchy increased attenuation, or small hematoma-like change along the intended puncture route or adjacent to the target lesion. Post-procedural CT was also used to assess complications, including pneumothorax, pulmonary hemorrhage, and hemothorax.

Bronchoscopic localization procedure

Thin-slice CT images were imported into the virtual bronchoscopy navigation system to reconstruct a three-dimensional bronchial pathway to the target lesion. The target bronchus and the estimated insertion depth were determined before the procedure according to the reconstructed navigation route. Bronchoscopic localization was performed on the same day as surgery in the bronchoscopy suite under local anesthesia and conscious sedation.

A thin flexible electronic bronchoscope (VBC-Q30; Shanghai Aohua Photoelectricity Endoscope Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) with an outer diameter of 3.7 mm and a working channel diameter of 2.0 mm was advanced along the planned bronchial route toward the target bronchus. Fluoroscopy was not routinely used during the bronchoscopic localization procedure. Target confirmation was based on the preoperative virtual bronchoscopic pathway, bronchoscopic anatomical landmarks, estimated insertion depth, and subsequent post-localization CT findings.

When the bronchoscope could no longer be advanced into the planned distal bronchus, the tip was considered to have reached the bronchial branch closest to the target lesion. A mixture of indocyanine green (ICG, 0.2 mL, 1 mg/mL) and methylene blue (MB, 1 mL) was injected through the working channel, followed by 10 mL of air to promote peripheral distribution of the dye. No balloon occlusion catheter was routinely used in this cohort. To reduce reflux into adjacent bronchi, the dye was injected slowly with the bronchoscope tip wedged as distally as possible, and the bronchoscope was kept in position briefly after injection before withdrawal (Fig. 1).

Post-localization CT was performed to confirm the location of the marking and to exclude pneumothorax. Because ICG and MB are not radiopaque, successful marking was inferred from newly appeared localized parenchymal ground-glass opacity, faint patchy increased attenuation, or focal air-containing change near the target region, corresponding to the planned bronchoscopic route.

Surgical procedure

After completion of localization and post-localization CT confirmation, all patients were transferred to the operating room for thoracoscopic resection. All procedures were performed using video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery. No planned thoracotomy was performed. Conversion to thoracotomy was recorded if required because of severe adhesions, bleeding, inability to identify the target lesion, or technical difficulty.

The surgical procedure, including wedge resection, segmentectomy, subsegmentectomy, or lobectomy, was selected according to nodule size, location, radiological features, pulmonary function, and the surgeon’s judgment. The marked area was first identified under thoracoscopic white-light visualization. For patients in the bronchoscopic ICG + MB group, near-infrared fluorescence imaging was additionally used to identify the ICG-marked area. After resection, the specimen was palpated and inspected to confirm the presence of the target lesion and marking area. Frozen section examination was selectively performed when intraoperative pathological confirmation was required to guide the extent of resection. Additional resection was performed if the surgical margin was considered insufficient or if invasive malignancy requiring anatomical resection was confirmed.

Statistical analysis

Propensity score matching was performed using age, sex, BMI, nodule size, and shortest distance to the pleura as prespecified covariates because these factors were considered clinically relevant to the technical difficulty of localization and were available for all patients in both groups. In the 1:3 nearest-neighbor matching procedure, each bronchoscopic patient was matched with up to three CT-guided control patients. Because matching was performed with replacement, the same CT-guided control patient could be selected for more than one bronchoscopic patient. After removal of duplicate records, the final analytical cohort included 17 bronchoscopic patients and 41 unique CT-guided patients. Subsequent analyses were conducted using one record per unique patient. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an SMD < 0.2 indicating acceptable balance for the variables included in the matching model.

To maintain the stability of the propensity score model and avoid overfitting, additional variables, including emphysema, pulmonary function parameters, lesion location, patient position, and operator experience, were not included in the matching model. These variables were compared descriptively after matching, and residual differences were reported when present. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were compared using Welch’s t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analyses were entered into multivariable Firth logistic regression to identify factors associated with post-localization pneumothorax.Odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Model discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and quantified by the area under the curve (AUC). All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.2).

Results

Baseline characteristics

After 1:3 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching with replacement, the five prespecified variables included in the matching model—age, sex, BMI, nodule size, and shortest distance to the pleura—achieved standardized mean differences below 0.2, indicating acceptable covariate balance (Fig. 2). The final analytical cohort included 17 patients in the bronchoscopic ICG + MB group and 41 unique patients in the CT-guided blood + MB group. The two groups were comparable with respect to the prespecified matching covariates. FEV1/FVC and patient position differed significantly between groups. Most lesions in both groups were ground-glass or subsolid nodules, accounting for 73.2% of lesions in the CT-guided blood + MB group and 76.5% in the bronchoscopic ICG + MB group.

Localization outcomes

Localization outcomes are summarized in Table 1. The localization success rate was comparable between the two groups (94.1% [16/17] vs. 90.2% [37/41], p = 1.000). Post-localization pneumothorax occurred in 22.0% (9/41) of patients in the CT-guided blood + MB group, whereas no pneumothorax was observed in the bronchoscopic ICG + MB group (0/17, p = 0.047). The median localization time was longer in the bronchoscopic ICG + MB group than in the CT-guided blood + MB group (15.0 [15.0–16.0] min vs. 10.0 [8.0–12.0] min, p < 0.001). Operative duration and intraoperative visibility scores did not differ significantly between groups.

Localization failure occurred in one patient in the bronchoscopic ICG + MB group and in four patients in the CT-guided blood + MB group. In the bronchoscopic group, failure was attributed to excessive dye diffusion, resulting in unclear localization during surgery. In the CT-guided group, two failures were due to intraoperative invisibility of the marker, and two were related to poor lung surface quality with extensive anthracotic pigmentation, which interfered with visual identification of the dye marking.

Pathological outcomes

Among the 58 resected nodules, 48 (82.8%) were malignant and 10 (17.2%) were benign. Malignant lesions included atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (n = 2), adenocarcinoma in situ (n = 17), minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (n = 19), and invasive adenocarcinoma (n = 10). All resected specimens achieved microscopically negative margins, and no patient required additional resection because of inadequate margins, indicating that both localization strategies provided adequate immediate surgical guidance for margin-secure resection.

Visualization scores

The distribution of visualization scores (0–5 grades) between the two localization methods is shown in Fig. 3. The bronchoscopic ICG + MB group demonstrated a right-shifted pattern toward higher scores, with a greater proportion of grades 3–4, whereas the CT-guided blood + MB group was predominantly grade 2. However, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test indicated that the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.210).

Risk factors for pneumothorax

Univariate Firth logistic regression identified the localization method (p = 0.032) and white blood cell (WBC) count (p = 0.026) as significant predictors of post-localization pneumothorax (Table 2), whereas age, sex, BMI, nodule size, distance to the pleura, pulmonary function parameters, and presence of emphysema were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

In the multivariable Firth logistic regression model, CT-guided blood + MB localization was associated with an increased likelihood of post-localization pneumothorax compared with bronchoscopic ICG + MB localization (OR = 9.85, 95% CI: 1.09–1307.06; p = 0.039). WBC count was also associated with pneumothorax (OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.03–2.24; p = 0.036). The Firth-corrected model showed acceptable discrimination, with an AUC of 0.810, and the calibration curve is shown in Fig. 4.

Discussion

This propensity score–matched study compared two clinically used localization strategies for pulmonary nodules: bronchoscopic ICG + MB localization and CT-guided autologous blood + MB localization. The main findings were that bronchoscopic ICG + MB achieved a localization success rate and intraoperative visibility comparable to those of CT-guided blood + MB localization, while no post-localization pneumothorax occurred in the bronchoscopic group. In addition, CT-guided localization and WBC count were associated with post-localization pneumothorax in the Firth logistic regression model. These results suggest that bronchoscopic ICG + MB localization may serve as a feasible airway-based option when preoperative localization is considered necessary, particularly for subsolid, small, or poorly palpable nodules in which avoidance of pleural puncture is preferred.

The indication for preoperative localization should be carefully selected. We agree that not all small peripheral nodules require localization. Solid nodules that are superficial and easily palpable may be managed by direct thoracoscopic palpation, biopsy-first strategies, or standard diagnostic wedge resection when appropriate. Kohman et al. emphasized the importance of obtaining a tissue diagnosis before randomization in CALGB 140,503, reflecting the principle that histological confirmation remains central to the management of small peripheral lung cancers [6]. The purpose of localization in the present study was not to replace biopsy or oncologic decision-making, but to facilitate intraoperative identification of lesions that were expected to be difficult to detect during VATS, particularly ground-glass or part-solid nodules with limited tactile feedback. In our cohort, most lesions were ground-glass or subsolid nodules, and all resections achieved negative margins without additional resection for inadequate margins. Therefore, localization may still have practical value in selected patients undergoing minimally invasive sublobar resection.

Recent evidence has refined the role of sublobar resection in early-stage lung cancer.JCOG0802/WJOG4607L showed favorable survival outcomes with segmentectomy in selected patients with small peripheral NSCLC [3]. CALGB 140,503 further demonstrated that sublobar resection was noninferior to lobectomy with respect to disease-free survival in patients with peripheral NSCLC measuring 2 cm or less after pathological confirmation of node-negative disease [4]. These findings highlight the importance of accurate lesion identification and adequate margin assessment when sublobar resection is selected [5]. In this context, preoperative localization remains clinically relevant for selected nodules that are difficult to visualize or palpate during minimally invasive surgery [7].

CT-guided percutaneous localization is widely used because of its technical simplicity and direct access to the target lesion. However, this approach requires transpleural puncture and may therefore be associated with procedure-related complications, including pneumothorax and pulmonary hemorrhage. In the present study, pneumothorax occurred in 22.0% of patients in the CT-guided blood + MB group, which is consistent with previously reported rates after percutaneous localization [15, 16]. In contrast, no pneumothorax was observed in the bronchoscopic group. We speculate that this difference may be partly related to the airway-based nature of bronchoscopic localization, which allows dye delivery without direct pleural puncture. Accordingly, bronchoscopic localization may be a useful option for selected patients, particularly those with limited pulmonary reserve, emphysema, or lesions requiring a potentially high-risk percutaneous puncture route.

The bronchoscopic ICG + MB technique combines near-infrared fluorescence and visible surface staining, providing two complementary intraoperative signals [17, 18]. In the present cohort, the distribution of visualization scores showed a trend toward higher scores in the bronchoscopic group, although the difference was not statistically significant. This finding suggests that the combination of fluorescence and visible staining may provide useful intraoperative guidance, especially when surface color change is subtle or when lung pigmentation interferes with visual identification. Previous studies have also reported that ICG-based fluorescence can improve the intraoperative detectability of small pulmonary nodules and provide clear target-like markings [19, 20]. The complementary optical properties of ICG and MB—near-infrared fluorescence and visible blue coloration—may enhance both depth perception and surface contrast, thereby helping surgeons identify the marked area during thoracoscopic resection [21].

Appropriate dye concentration, injection volume, and injection technique are important for achieving a clear and stable marking. In the bronchoscopic approach, excessive injection volume, insufficient distal wedging of the bronchoscope, or reflux of dye into adjacent bronchi may lead to a broad or poorly defined marking area [22–24]. In this study, one localization failure in the bronchoscopic group was attributed to excessive dye diffusion. This finding highlights the importance of standardized injection control, including accurate selection of the target bronchus, slow injection under a wedged position, and careful post-localization CT confirmation. Further refinement of dye concentration, injection volume, and bronchoscopic delivery technique may improve marking precision and reproducibility.

In our cohort, the median localization time for the bronchoscopic ICG + MB group was significantly longer compared with the CT-guided blood + MB group, with a median value of 15 min for bronchoscopic ICG + MB; this figure was also slightly higher than the previously published times of 10 ± 5 min and 10 ± 7 min reported by Bolton et al. and Zhu et al., respectively [25, 26]. This longer procedural duration may be explained by two factors. First, bronchoscopic localization requires additional time for virtual bronchoscopic pathway planning, bronchial route identification, and advancement toward the distal bronchi, which is not required for CT-guided blood + MB marking. Second, bronchoscopic ICG + MB localization was introduced relatively recently at our institution, and the observed procedure time may reflect an early learning-curve effect. With increasing operator experience, optimization of navigation equipment, and improved coordination between the bronchoscopy suite and operating room, the localization time of bronchoscopic ICG + MB localization is expected to decrease, and the workflow may become more efficient.

WBC count was also associated with post-localization pneumothorax in the multivariable Firth logistic regression model. Although most WBC values were within or close to the normal clinical range, WBC was analyzed as a continuous variable, and its relative increase may still reflect subtle differences in baseline patient condition. This finding suggests that routinely available preoperative laboratory parameters may provide additional information for pneumothorax risk assessment. In clinical practice, WBC count should not be interpreted in isolation, but may be considered together with procedural and radiological factors, such as localization route, puncture path, lesion depth, patient position, and emphysema status. Further large-sample studies are required to validate its predictive value and determine clinically meaningful thresholds for risk stratification.

Propensity score matching was performed using prespecified variables considered most directly related to localization difficulty and clinical comparability, including age, sex, BMI, nodule size, and shortest distance to the pleura. After matching, these covariates achieved acceptable balance, indicating improved comparability between the two groups for the main variables included in the matching model. FEV1/FVC and patient position remained different between groups. We speculate that the difference in patient position was partly related to the procedural characteristics of the two localization methods: patient position during CT-guided localization was mainly determined by the planned percutaneous puncture route, whereas bronchoscopic localization was generally performed in the supine position. Pulmonary function parameters, emphysema severity, lesion location, and operator-related factors may still influence outcomes and should be further evaluated in future studies with larger cohorts and more comprehensive adjustment.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, and the sample size of the bronchoscopic ICG + MB group was limited because this technique was introduced relatively recently at our institution. Second, although propensity score matching improved the balance of key baseline variables, some clinical factors were not fully adjusted, and larger cohorts are needed for further validation. Third, the visibility score was retrospectively assessed using operative records, intraoperative photographs, and available thoracoscopic videos, although independent review by thoracic surgeons was performed to reduce assessment bias. Finally, this study focused on perioperative localization outcomes and resection margin status; long-term follow-up data were not available. Despite these limitations, the present study provides preliminary comparative evidence supporting the feasibility and safety of bronchoscopic ICG + MB localization for selected pulmonary nodules.

Conclusions

Bronchoscopic ICG + MB localization demonstrated localization success and intraoperative visibility comparable to CT-guided autologous blood + MB localization in this propensity score–matched cohort. Pneumothorax occurred less frequently in the bronchoscopic group, while both techniques enabled thoracoscopic resection with negative margins. These findings suggest that bronchoscopic ICG + MB localization may represent a feasible airway-based option when preoperative localization is considered necessary, particularly for subsolid, small, or poorly palpable nodules in which avoidance of pleural puncture is preferred.
