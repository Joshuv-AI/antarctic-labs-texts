Barriers to childhood obesity prevention in the school food environment: a qualitative study from Indonesia
Authors: David Colozza, Astrid Citra Padmita, Mamadou Ndiaye, Siti Nadia Tarmizi, Esti Widiastuti, Aria Kekalih, Indriya L Pramesthi, Luh Ade Wiradnyani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchools play a crucial role in shaping children’s growth and development. In Indonesia, however, school food environments face major challenges in promoting nutrition and healthy lifestyles. These environments are increasingly dominated by unhealthy ultra-processed products (UPPs), limited nutritious options and inadequate opportunities for physical activity—key contributors to rising childhood obesity rates.MethodsWe explored the drivers of childhood obesity and barriers to prevention in selected school food environments using qualitative data from in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with 165 purposefully selected participants across four study areas, field observations and a desk review of relevant regulations. Data were analysed through an iterative content analysis approach, using a predefined variable indicator matrix outlining key study themes.ResultsFindings show frequent consumption of unhealthy snacks and sweetened drinks in and around schools, driven by a lack of food environment regulations. Barriers to healthier diets include children’s food preferences, limited parental time and the widespread availability of UPPs. While school-based physical activity was generally adequate, sedentary behaviours at home were common. Key gaps include limited teacher capacity, inadequate health education materials and weak data sharing and referral mechanisms for obesity between schools and community health services.ConclusionStrengthening school food environments regulations in Indonesia is urgently needed. Priorities include developing national canteen guidelines, restricting the sale and marketing of UPPs and high-fat, salt and sugar foods, adopting interpretative front-of-pack labelling schemes and promoting equitable physical activity opportunities. Improving knowledge, capacity and coordination among schools, parents and health workers is also critical.Keywords: nutrition, obesity, child health, qualitative research, child

Introduction

Indonesia is experiencing a rapid rise in childhood overweight and obesity rates (hereafter: ‘obesity’), alongside an increase in related non-communicable diseases (NCDs).1 In 2023, nearly one in five (19.7%) children aged 5–12 years and one in seven (14.3%) adolescents aged 13–18 years were affected by overweight or obesity.2 These trends compound persistent undernutrition challenges and place additional pressure on health and socio-economic systems, which remain generally unprepared to implement effective early life prevention strategies to mitigate the long-term impacts of obesity and related NCDs.3

Schools play a critical role in shaping children’s nutritional health, offering a unique platform for encouraging healthy habits early in life through nutrition education, and opportunities to access healthy foods and physical activity.4 5 However, schools in Indonesia, as in many other countries, often fail to support healthy diets and lifestyles. Reflecting broader shifts towards increasingly obesogenic food environments in the country,1 6 evidence on Indonesian school settings shows pervasive unhealthy ultra-processed products (UPPs) availability, limited nutritious food access, low nutrition literacy and a lack of regulations to monitor the nutritional quality of foods sold and marketed in and around schools.6 7

Similarly, although schools represent an ideal platform to deliver nutrition services, in Indonesia significant gaps remain. Lack of coordination between schools and community health centres limits the effectiveness of early detection and referral mechanisms for malnutrition cases,6 including for children with overweight or obesity.8 Delivery of essential nutrition services like deworming, iron-folic acid supplementation,9 10 nutrition education and counselling11 12 also remains suboptimal, with substantial variations across regions.13 Overall, awareness and capacity specific to chilhood obesity prevention remain limited.7 8

This study draws on qualitative data from 165 stakeholders, including teachers, parents, canteen managers and health workers, to address three objectives: (1) identify barriers and enablers for strengthening school-based obesity prevention initiatives in Indonesia; (2) examine nutrition knowledge gaps among these stakeholders and (3) explore challenges within early detection and referral systems for childhood obesity. Findings contribute to a limited evidence base on Indonesian school food environments, offering insights to help shape school-based policies and programmes to prevent childhood obesity, alongside other forms of malnutrition.

Methods

Study design and sample

The study draws on qualitative data from four cities/districts (Semarang, Surakarta, Tegal, Kudus) in Java, one of Indonesia’s most populous regions. Data were collected as part of a broader project documenting a school nutrition programme, through 16 semi-structured in-person focus group discussions (FGDs), four per location (60–90 min each). A total of 165 participants were purposively selected, including teachers involved in the School Health Programme (UKS), school committee members, canteen managers and representatives from local government authorities. Additionally, 12 in-person semi-structured interviews were conducted with primary healthcare workers (30–60 min each). Participant selection was done with schools and local authorities, based on predefined criteria (table 1).

LAAW and ILP, trained public health nutritionists, conducted all sessions using structured guides. Both facilitators are Indonesian, share a similar educational and professional background with many of the participants (eg, health workers and government staff) and have extensive knowledge of the study areas, also due to previous research and work engagements with local stakeholders. This helped build rapport and trust before and during data collection; meanwhile, the facilitators were not directly involved in implementing the intervention, which reduced the risk of bias arising from personal interest in programme outcomes.

Field observations and a document review focused on childhood obesity referral systems complemented FGDs/interview data. Instruments were field-tested prior to roll-out. FGD and interview guides are included in online supplemental tables S1 and S2. Manuscript preparation followed the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ).14

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted in two stages. First, participant responses were summarised and organised into matrices by respondent type. These were developed during data collection with ongoing team review to ensure completeness, alignment with the variable indicator matrix (VIM) and to identify emerging patterns. To support reflexivity throughout the data collection process, the facilitators held regular debriefing sessions with the broader study team in the field, to discuss the information collected and emerging findings. The iterative process of data collection-interpretation also helped identify information gaps and determine data saturation. The second stage involved desk-based analysis and interpretation, guided by the VIM, using standard word and data processing software (Excel, Microsoft, 2024) and following content analysis principles.15 Findings were grouped under three predefined themes: (1) perceived obesity drivers and barriers to prevention in school environments; (2) childhood obesity prevention knowledge gaps and (3) detection and referral systems gaps. Using an iterative approach, recurring themes were identified, and representative quotes selected. Two coders (LAW and ILP) led data analysis, with input from the study team. Sample characteristics

The study included 165 participants among teachers (39), school committee members (39), school canteen managers (40), local government representatives from the UKS (20) and community health workers (27). Most participants were female (n=114, 69%), reflecting the gender distribution in Indonesia’s workforce, where women comprise about 70.9% of the health sector16 and 72% of primary school teachers.17

Perceived obesity drivers and barriers to prevention in the school and home environments

Teachers and school committee members generally reported unhealthy diets and limited physical activity as key drivers of childhood obesity in their areas. Frequent consumption of UPPs was commonly mentioned, with teachers reporting that children frequently consumed sweetened drinks, sugary and fried snacks, at or after school. Some schools reportedly had canteen guidelines in place, but these were isolated good practices, dependent on the school management’s own initiative. Also, guidelines were often not strictly enforced, or focused on food safety rather than nutritional content, rendering them ineffective for obesity prevention.

Consumption of unhealthy foods during the school day was a common concern. Respondents noted that school canteens and nearby vendors often sold UPPs and other items high in sugar, salt and fat, with limited healthy options. Children typically chose based on taste and price, while healthier alternatives were seen as less appealing, accessible or affordable. Instant noodles, sweetened drinks and fried foods were especially popular. The widespread availability of such items in and around schools was seen as a major barrier to healthy diets. Food safety was also a concern, with some canteen items reportedly testing positive for textile dyes.

The snacks available to elementary school students are unhealthy, often high in sugar, salt, and fat. Vendors outside the school provide limited healthy options, and students prioritize taste and price. (District health officer, Tegal)

Teachers and school committee members identified two main challenges to implementing balanced nutrition practices at home: children’s food preferences and busy parental schedules. Children’s food preferences were considered a significant barrier to healthy eating, as most children showed reluctance towards vegetables, favouring carbohydrate-rich dishes and UPPs instead. Parents often struggled to persuade their children to eat nutritious options, with many children opting for convenient, familiar and tasty foods. This resulted in several respondents reporting concerns about lack of dietary diversity among local children.

It’s hard to persuade children; they want things their own way. Even when we offer nutritious vegetables, they often prefer instant meals. (School committee member, Surakarta)

Busy parental schedules also posed a challenge. Respondents noted that some parents often gave their children pocket money instead of preparing breakfast. This practice was reportedly common among children from lower socio-economic backgrounds, whose parents, employed as factory workers or farmers, start work early in the morning, leaving little time to home-cook breakfast. The packed meals that children brought from home frequently lacked vegetables. Teachers also mentioned that parental practices at home did not always align with school teachings on nutrition; some parents still allowed their children to buy snacks from vendors, even though schools discouraged this habit.

Parents working in factories who leave as early as 5 a.m. often don’t have time to prepare healthy meals. (UKS/M teacher, Kudus)

Parents still tend not to prioritise consistency between what children learn at school and practices at home, such as providing breakfast. (UKS/M teacher, Tegal)

Physical activity and sedentary behaviours were also perceived as influential. Teachers noted that, while physical activity levels within schools were generally adequate, sedentary behaviours outside school hours presented a challenge. In Tegal, teachers reported that children often spent free time on their phones or other devices instead of engaging in physical activities, with limited parental encouragement to remain active. Moreover, while most school communities saw minimal differences in dietary habits between boys and girls, some teachers observed that male students tended to be more active during school hours, with girls less engaged in physical activity.

After classes, children often play outside before going home. Teachers are aware that their homes may lack space, so they prefer the school’s open areas. (UKS/M teacher, Surakarta)

Respondents also reported how children’s physical activity levels were influenced by their parents’ habits. Teachers in Tegal observed that children often emulated their parents’ sedentary behaviours, such as video gaming. Teachers highlighted the importance of encouraging children to be active, whether through exercise or household chores. However, this engagement was reportedly rare, especially among younger parents, who often chose screen-based activities with their children over promoting physical activity.

Gaps in knowledge and information sources on childhood obesity prevention

Respondents identified several key sources of nutrition information. They often received training from the District Education Office or participated in seminars and webinars, particularly during events like National Nutrition Day. When unable to attend these events, they obtained materials through WhatsApp groups shared by fellow health workers or accessed information online from credible sources such as the Ministry of Health. Social media platforms were also frequently used, although respondents were mindful of the potential unreliability of online information.

Despite these resources, respondents noted significant gaps in the availability and quality of information specific to obesity prevention. Health education materials from community health centres (puskesmas) reportedly focused mainly on general topics such as balanced nutrition principles, oral and dental health, handwashing and reproductive health, with little to no content addressing obesity. Most teachers relied heavily on internet platforms like YouTube and social media for information on childhood obesity. Some teachers acknowledged they had not actively sought such information, often because UKS programmes in their schools were still in the early stages, which led them to prioritise broader health topics over specific nutrition issues, including obesity.

We usually provide training alongside other school programmes, like deworming tablet distribution and immunization. The focus is mostly on hygiene and sanitation, handwashing, and reproductive health. We never specifically covered obesity, and when nutrition topics are included, they are general, like the importance of eating nutritious foods. (Puskesmas UKS/M staff, Kudus)

Training availability and content varied across study sites. In Tegal, five schools involved in a UNICEF programme received targeted training on healthy diets and obesity prevention. In contrast, schools in Kudus received training from puskesmas or the District Education Office’s Health Promotion Unit, but with little focus on obesity. In Semarang, a teacher noted that training was often linked to specific events or school health competitions, leading to uneven access. In Surakarta, one school received training on healthy canteens from the Indonesian Food and Drug Authority (BPOM). Across all four areas, there was a consistent lack of regular, dedicated training for teachers and puskesmas coordinators on obesity prevention. For example, in Tegal, sessions mainly covered bullying, sexual harassment and puberty, with minimal focus on nutrition.

There was no specific training on health and nutrition provided by Puskesmas. However, they did encourage us to report if any students experience bullying or sexual harassment at school. (UKS/M teacher, Tegal).

Gaps in detection and referral systems for childhood obesity cases

Schools and puskesmas reported conducting regular health screenings with the involvement of puskesmas staff, including nutritionists and UKS coordinators. These covered anthropometric measurements, general health checks (eyes, ears, teeth) and physical fitness assessments. Anaemia screenings were only conducted when clinical symptoms were present. Most school stakeholders reported low rates of both obesity and undernutrition, typically identifying only one or two cases per school. In Kudus, some schools independently measured students’ nutritional status and shared the data with puskesmas. Teachers there were trained on nutritional assessments during coordination meetings. In Semarang, schools consistently received screening results, while in Surakarta, BMI was recorded in students’ ‘Health Report Books’.

We use a BMI calculator specifically designed for elementary school children. We can immediately identify if a child is underweight, overweight, or obese. We record the information in the child’s report book. During screenings or check-ups, the child needs to bring their report book. (Puskeskmas nutritionist, Surakarta)

However, discrepancies emerged around data sharing. While some puskesmas provided nutritional data in various formats, many schools reported not receiving it. Where shared, data often focused only on severe cases. Teachers frequently lacked the training or tools to assess nutrition independently, and in some cases, puskesmas assigned this task to schools without adequate support, affecting data quality. Teachers also noted the absence of a clear referral system for obesity. Screenings mostly involved weight and height checks, with limited follow-up. Puskesmas coordinators confirmed that formal referrals were not fully operational, although some efforts existed. For example, in Tegal and Surakarta, puskesmas conducted follow-ups involving re-measurements, reassessments and counselling on diet, physical activity and healthy eating. For overweight or obesity cases, verbal agreements with parents were made, with monthly monitoring visits.

For students with overweight or obesity, we typically offer counselling involving both the child and the parents. However, some parents do not bring their children back for reassessment at the health centre because they don’t feel their child is sick. They often say things like, ‘Oh, my child isn’t sick, why should we go back?’ It’s a common occurrence. (Puskesmas UKS/M coordinator, Surakarta)

Despite these efforts, challenges persisted. Tracking follow-up care was difficult, as referral facilities were often separate from screening sites. Puskesmas relied on schools to confirm if students accessed care, but parental compliance with recommendations was inconsistent. Respondents noted that parents were more responsive to advice on issues like dental or ear health, suggesting that childhood obesity and related risks may not yet be well understood or prioritised. Health centre accreditation influenced the effectiveness of referral systems. In Tegal, for example, puskesmas accreditation included nutritional referrals as a performance indicator, encouraging better follow-up. However, such practices were not consistently applied across all study areas.

Discussion

Schools can play a vital role in protecting and promoting child nutrition, setting children on a positive trajectory for lifelong healthy growth and development. However, children in Indonesia face significant challenges within their school food environments, as reflected in rising childhood obesity rates alongside other forms of malnutrition. The qualitative nature of the study limits findings’ generalisability. Nevertheless, results highlight multiple gaps and opportunities to improve school children’s nutrition in Indonesia.

First, findings highlight major challenges related to the immediate drivers of childhood obesity: unhealthy diets and insufficient physical activity. In the areas studied, children were regularly exposed to UPPs and foods high in fat, sugar and salt, leading to frequent consumption of unhealthy options over nutritious ones. This aligns with existing evidence showing that food environments in Indonesia, both in and around schools6 9 18 and within the broader community,19,22 have become increasingly obesogenic. These findings highlight the urgent need to strengthen food environment regulations and ensure their effective enforcement. Addressing the drivers of childhood obesity in schools will require a suite of evidence-based policy measures, many of which are WHO-endorsed ‘best buys’ for tackling obesity and NCDs.23

Priority policy actions include developing national school canteen guidelines to regulate the nutritional standards of foods sold in schools and implementing bans on the marketing and sale of unhealthy UPPs and other foods high in fats, sugar and salt, in and around schools.23 24 These regulations should target both formal outlets, such as convenience stores, and informal vendors operating near schools. Additionally, introducing robust interpretative front-of-pack labelling schemes that clearly identify unhealthy products can support the enforcement of these measures, making it easier for school communities to identify products that should not be sold or promoted to children in or around school premises.25 The establishment of an Indonesia-specific nutrient profile model, which is currently not available, would significantly help these efforts.26 27 The launch of the Free Nutritious Meals programme in 2025 presents a strong opportunity to integrate and scale up these measures nationwide, complementing the provision of meals with targeted efforts to improve children’s school food environments and protect them from unhealthy UPPs.

Findings suggest suboptimal physical activity levels among school children, consistent with survey data showing that nine in 10 Indonesian children (aged 11–19 years) do not meet the WHO-recommended 60 min of daily physical activity.25 While respondents reported sufficient activity during school hours, they noted that children often reverted to sedentary behaviours afterwards, with excessive screen time flagged as a concern. This highlights the influence of environments beyond school (home, neighbourhood and community) on children’s lifestyles.26 The study also found higher physical activity among boys than girls. Although not generalisable, this finding aligns with existing literature on gender disparities in physical activity,27 including limited evidence from Indonesia.28 This suggests that girls may be particularly vulnerable to physical inactivity, even during school hours; interventions should explicitly address this gap, and further research on the drivers of inactivity among Indonesian girls is needed to inform targeted solutions.

Additional barriers to childhood obesity prevention were linked to parental behaviours outside the school setting. Many respondents noted a disconnect between school and home practices. While teachers promoted healthy eating and limited unhealthy snacks during school hours, parents’ busy schedules, economic constraints or reluctance to challenge children’s preferences often led to skipped breakfasts or the consumption of poor-quality foods at home. Conflicting parental priorities and convenience are well-established drivers behind the increased consumption of UPPs and ready-to-eat meals, both globally29 30 and in Indonesia.19 21 31 Affordability also plays a key role, as lower cost but less nutritious UPPs often become default choices.29 In Indonesia, the cost of a nutritious diet varies widely by region, with up to 53% of households in the poorest provinces unable to afford one.32 These findings highlight the need for policies that both discourage UPP consumption (eg, through taxation) and promote healthier alternatives (eg, through subsidies for the production and purchase of nutritious foods).23

The study also highlights major gaps in knowledge and information on childhood obesity prevention among local school and health communities. Respondents noted that training and counselling materials from health centres rarely addressed obesity, prompting many to seek information from online sources, including social media. Training opportunities also varied widely across study areas, indicating a lack of standardisation and limited reach of capacity-building efforts. These findings align with broader evidence of gaps in nutritional knowledge related to healthy diets and malnutrition in Indonesia,9 20 while also pointing to a specific gap in obesity prevention that could be addressed through targeted programmes to develop and disseminate educational resources for parents, caregivers, school staff and health workers. Addressing this gap is critical. Existing literature shows that teachers’ knowledge is closely linked to their nutrition-related attitudes and beliefs,33 that the effectiveness of school-based nutrition education depends on teachers’ capacity34 and that children’s improved nutrition knowledge is associated with healthier dietary outcomes.35 Similarly, education interventions focused on healthcare workers have proven effective in improving service delivery, including for obesity prevention among both adults and children.36 37

Finally, the study reveals significant gaps in community-based early detection and referral mechanisms for childhood obesity. While many schools reported conducting regular health and nutrition screenings in line with national policies, challenges persisted, particularly limited data sharing between schools and community health centres. Local health systems still often prioritise undernutrition, with obesity data frequently overlooked.1 Although some schools and areas made efforts to bridge these gaps, progress was inconsistent and typically driven by local initiatives, such as health centre accreditation, rather than standardised practices. Indonesia’s decentralised health system13 offers a unique opportunity to reach all children, including those in remote or vulnerable communities. To realise this potential, there is a pressing need to expand and improve the coverage and quality of school-based early detection and referral services. Evidence from the global literature highlights the central role of primary-level healthcare providers working together with schools and families to mitigate obesity risk, implement community-based interventions and ensure timely referral of children with, or at risk of, overweight and obesity to specialist services.38 39 Strengthening these linkages across schools, primary healthcare facilities and families will be essential for Indonesia to advance a more holistic approach to the prevention and management of childhood obesity.

We acknowledge two key limitations to this study. First, data collection prioritised depth of insight over scale. Although the sample size is relatively large for a qualitative study and included perspectives from multiple stakeholder groups, the findings cannot be readily generalised to other contexts. While this is inherent to qualitative research, it is particularly important to note in the Indonesian context, given the country’s striking socio-economic, cultural and biophysical diversity across regions. Second, our study did not capture first-hand accounts from children. This is a considerable limitation, as it restricts understanding of possible nuances in barriers and opportunities for obesity prevention in both school and home environments. Including children’s voices represents an important avenue for future research in the target areas and beyond.

Conclusions

Schools have a critical role in promoting adequate nutrition and laying the foundation for lifelong health and development. However, this study reveals persistent gaps in Indonesia’s school food environments, health systems and home environments, along with parental behaviours and community practices that contribute to the growing burden of childhood obesity. Key challenges include the widespread availability and marketing of unhealthy UPPs, limited opportunities for physical activity, low nutrition literacy, inconducive home food environments and parental influences and weak referral and support systems. Priority intervention areas include strengthening regulatory frameworks, such as national canteen guidelines, restricting unhealthy food sales and marketing near schools and implementing interpretative front-of-pack labelling. Equally important are efforts to promote physical activity, improve early detection and referral services and build the capacity of teachers, health workers and parents. Targeted policies and school-based interventions that address these gaps are vital to creating healthier school environments and, in turn, ensuring better nutrition outcomes for Indonesian children.
