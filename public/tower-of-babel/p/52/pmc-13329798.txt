A real-world implementation study of a TDM-guided dalbavancin model for bone and joint infections (101 Dalbatians)
Authors: Andrea Rabbione, Francesco Petri, Aurora Civati, Chiara Mariani, Simona Landonio, Letizia Scandiffio, Riccardo Giorgi, Antonella Foschi, Martina Offer, Maria Vittoria Cossu, Aaron J Tande, Dario Cattaneo, Alberto Dolci, Gina A Suh, Andrea Gori, Matteo Passerini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDalbavancin is increasingly used off-label for chronic Gram-positive bone and joint infections (BJIs) requiring prolonged antimicrobial therapy, but optimal dosing strategies and the role of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) remain unclear. We evaluated the real-world implementation of a proactive TDM-guided dalbavancin workflow within an outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy (OPAT) programme.MethodsSingle-centre prospective observational implementation study including adult patients with BJIs consecutively treated with dalbavancin in OPAT service between July 2022 and August 2025. A multidisciplinary team developed a workflow incorporating TDM-guided pharmacokinetic modelling to individualize dosing intervals after fixed initial administrations (Days 1, 8 and 43). Target Cmin concentrations were ≥8 mg/L, later increased to ≥15 mg/L after European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) breakpoint revision. Primary outcomes included implementation metrics (feasibility, fidelity and appropriateness). Secondary outcomes were clinical effectiveness, safety and overall workflow success.ResultsThe workflow was applied to 101 patients accounting for 418 TDM-guided dalbavancin administrations (feasibility). Three patient-driven treatment discontinuations were observed (3/101, 3.0%), and scheduled administrations occurred within the predefined time window in 335/418 (83.5%) of cases (fidelity). Pharmacological target attainment was achieved in 285/342 (89.6%) of administrations without dosing delays (appropriateness). Clinical effectiveness was achieved in 55/73 (75.3%) patients completing therapy, with 28/101 (28%) still on treatment. Adverse events–related discontinuation occurred in 3/101 (3%) patients. Overall workflow success was observed in 74/101 (73.2%) patients.ConclusionsA multidisciplinary TDM-guided dalbavancin workflow within an OPAT programme was feasible and safe, achieving high target attainment and favourable clinical outcomes. This approach supports individualized long-acting dalbavancin therapy for complex chronic BJIs.

Introduction

Dalbavancin is a long-acting semisynthetic lipoglycopeptide approved for the intravenous treatment of acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections caused by susceptible Gram-positive pathogens.1 Owing to its extensive tissue distribution, high plasma protein binding and prolonged terminal half-life, it has a favourable pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) profile, good penetration into bone and articular tissue, minimal drug–drug interactions and an overall good safety profile.2,3

Beyond its approved indication, dalbavancin has been increasingly used off-label for chronic and deep-seated Gram-positive infections, including cardiovascular and bone and joint infections (BJIs) requiring prolonged or suppressive antibiotic therapy (SAT).4–6 Its infrequent dosing reduces the need for daily outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy (OPAT), shortens hospitalization and improves adherence, particularly in frail patients or those with limited mobility or prior antibiotic intolerance.5–7

The prolonged off-label use of dalbavancin is characterized by substantial variability in dosing strategies, monitoring practices and clinical follow-up, further complicated by inter- and intra-individual PK/PD variability.4,8 In this context, therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) has emerged as a useful strategy to optimize dalbavancin dosing, allowing individualized dosing intervals and minimizing subtherapeutic exposure and drug accumulation, especially in patients with altered PK.9 However, real-life data on TDM-guided dalbavancin therapy in large cohorts remain limited, and uncertainties persist regarding optimal dosing strategies and access to specialized pharmacological support.

Limited data are available on how structured TDM-based programmes can be implemented, sustained and scaled within OPAT services and multidisciplinary care pathways. As a result, the translation of pharmacological evidence into consistent clinical practice remains suboptimal across centres.

Implementation science provides a conceptual framework to address this gap by systematically evaluating how evidence-based interventions are adopted, delivered and maintained in real-world healthcare settings.

Based on principles of implementation science10 and research for off-label drugs,11 and building on previous experiences,12 we developed and implemented a structured, multidisciplinary PK/PD-based, TDM-guided workflow to personalize dalbavancin dosing intervals for the treatment of BJIs.

Therefore, the aim of this study is to evaluate the implementation of this TDM-guided dalbavancin workflow in routine clinical practice for the treatment of BJIs, focusing on key implementation outcomes to provide a replicable model for the integration into clinical practice of such workflow.

Methods

This was a single-centre, prospective observational implementation study evaluating a structured TDM-guided dalbavancin workflow for the management of complex BJIs within an established OPAT service. Clinical data were collected as part of routine care and analysed to assess the performance of the clinical workflow.

The implemented workflow was developed by a multidisciplinary team comprising infectious disease (ID) specialists, clinical pharmacologists, microbiologists, OPAT physicians and nurses. It was progressively integrated into routine clinical practice starting in July 2022, following internal consensus meetings, staff training and standardization of pharmacological consultation procedures.

Application of implementation science

Following the principles of implementation science, we systematically incorporated previous research findings and other practices and applied them into routine practice.13 The findings and practices of interest were as follows: (i) the emerging evidence of the effectiveness of dalbavancin for BJIs derived from a small randomized clinical trial and larger observational data14,15; (ii) the benefit of the use of TDM in glycopeptides to minimize the risk of toxicity and maximize antimicrobial effectiveness by ensuring optimal drug exposure16,17; (iii) the advantages of a personalized approach for patients in need of long-term antibiotic treatment to ensure adequate compliance18; (iv) the emerging need of appropriate strategies for patients requiring long-term antibiotic therapy to control infection in complex BJIs19 and (v) the observation that long-term oral therapy may sometimes be infeasible due to antimicrobial resistance, the occurrence of adverse events (AEs), patients’ values or impairment of drug absorption.20

Implemented workflow in clinical practice

Our workflow was implemented and proposed for any patient requiring long-term dalbavancin and has already been described in a subset of patients.12 Briefly, all patients received the first three dalbavancin administrations according to a predefined schedule on Days 1, 8 and 43 to ensure approximately 12 weeks of antimicrobial exposure.21 From the third administration onward, dosing intervals were individualized based on TDM results. Practically, we collected through concentrations (Cmin; immediately before each infusion) and peak concentrations (Cmax; 30 minutes after infusion completion). Then, Cmin and Cmax values were entered into individual log-linear regression models obtained by plotting log-transformed dalbavancin concentrations against time. For each patient, the model estimated the number of days required for dalbavancin concentrations to decline to the predefined target Cmin, thereby determining the timing of the subsequent administration. TDM samples were also collected at the second administration; however, as the third dose was predefined (Day 43), these measurements were used to improve model estimation rather than to guide dosing.

The standard dalbavancin dose for patients without renal impairment [estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) according to Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) >60 mL/min/1.73 m2] was 1500 mg per administration. In patients with moderate renal impairment (eGFR 30–60 mL/min/1.73 m2), a 1500 mg loading dose was followed by 1000 mg for subsequent administrations. For patients with severe renal impairment (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2), the dose was 1000 mg at each administration.

The target Cmin concentration (≥8 mg/L) was considered as the surrogate endpoint of target attainment. The 8 mg/L cut-off was chosen based on published data indicating that these levels correspond to an ∼90% probability of achieving pharmacodynamic exposure targets against S. aureus for minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) up to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) dalbavancin susceptibility breakpoint (0.125 mg/L).22 The model was further refined following a recent EUCAST revision (October 2024) of the Staphylococcus species susceptibility breakpoints from 0.125 to 0.25 mg/L,23 therefore updating target Cmin concentrations (≥15 mg/L). Further details on the TDM assessment are available in the Supplementary material (available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).

The individualized time interval was calculated by the service of clinical pharmacological consultation (CPC) and reported to the treating physicians with a formal written report. Then, the patients were contacted by telephone to communicate the scheduled date of the subsequent dalbavancin infusion.

Each OPAT visit for dalbavancin administration included a comprehensive clinical evaluation by the treating ID consultants, integrating clinical and laboratory parameters according to the predefined treatment programme and informing decisions on treatment continuation or discontinuation. This integrated assessment also enabled concurrent evaluation of safety, adherence and model performance.

Patient enrolment

In this study, focused on BJIs, we considered all consecutive patients managed according to the predefined workflow from 7 July 2022 to 22 August 2025 (37 months) with (i) diagnosis of BJIs, (ii) unsuitability for long-term oral therapy due to antimicrobial resistance, intolerance, impaired absorption or expected poor adherence, (iii) admission to the OPAT service of our hospital, (iv) indication for the use of dalbavancin, (v) age ≥18 years and (vi) provision of written informed consent for off-label dalbavancin use.

At the time of database lock (15 October 2025), we identified two groups of patients: (i) ‘ongoing group’ for patients still receiving dalbavancin and (ii) ‘completion group’ for patients who discontinued treatment for any reason.

Primary and secondary outcomes with the corresponding endpoints

As for implementation science,10 our primary outcomes included the following: (i) feasibility, defined as the number of people treated with our proposed workflow during the study period and their characteristics; (ii) fidelity, defined as the proportion of loss to follow-up (or other patient-driven discontinuations) and the proportion of Cmin performed at the correct timing (i.e. within four days from the scheduled administration) and (iii) appropriateness, defined as the number of measurements where target Cmin was obtained.

We evaluated effectiveness and safety as secondary outcomes, as suggested by recommendations for off-label medicines research.11 Specifically, effectiveness was defined as the absence of clinical failure during treatment course and the follow-up period. For ‘completion group’ the follow-up was defined as the interval between dalbavancin discontinuation and either the occurrence of clinical failure or the time of database lock, whichever came first. The definition of clinical failure was adapted from previous literature, especially the OVIVA trial24,25 (Supplementary material). Treatment completion was decided by the treating physician.

Outcome assessment was performed through review of patient clinical records and direct consultation with the treating ID physicians, who had at least 5 years of experience in BJIs. In cases of uncertainty regarding outcome classification, anonymized case summaries were independently reviewed by two international expert ID physicians with extensive experience in BJIs (A.J.T. and G.A.S.), who were not involved in the clinical management of the cases.

Safety was defined as the proportion of AEs deemed possibly related to the study drug and resulting in treatment discontinuation.

Lastly, we described the outcome workflow success, defined as the proportion of patients without any of the following, whichever occurred first:

Patients in the ongoing group were not considered to have experienced clinical or workflow failure solely because dalbavancin therapy was still ongoing at the time of database lock.

Statistical analysis

We extracted information from electronic medical records (NOEMALIFE Galileo) and by manual review of OPAT clinic charts. Collected variables included demographic data [age, sex and body mass index (BMI), geographic origins], comorbidities, types of BJI infection, bacterial isolates, previous and concomitant antibiotic exposure, baseline laboratory biomarkers (eGFR and albumin), TDM results and intervals between dalbavancin administrations. Data were entered into Microsoft Excel v16.0 (Microsoft Corp., USA) and subsequently analysed using R software (R 4.5.2). Continuous variables are expressed as median and interquartile range, and categorical variables as absolute frequency and percentage.

Ethical statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the dictates of the Declaration of Helsinki and the standards of Good Clinical Practice (E6: Good Clinical Practice: Consolidated Guideline (CPMP/ICH/135/95) (D.M. 27 April 1992) and subsequent amendments (D.M., 15 July 1997 and D.Lvo 211/2003). The use of off-label drugs and the current study were registered with the local ethical committee (RFOC approval 06/09/2023; MONTALBANO 444-2024). Written informed consent for off-label use and for study participation was obtained from all participants.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the population

A total of 101 consecutive patients with BJIs were included during the study period (Figure 1). Median age was 68 years [interquartile range (IQR) 56–77], and the majority were male. Prosthetic joint infections and hardware-associated osteomyelitis accounted for most cases (42/101, 41.6% and 28/101, 27.7%, respectively). Staphylococcus species were the predominant pathogens, with methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) being the most frequently identified organism, detected in 31 patients (30.7%). Dalbavancin Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) values for the causative pathogens were available only in a limited number of cases (n = 14) and ranged from 0.047 to 0.5 mg/L (Supplementary material). Detailed demographic, clinical, microbiological and laboratory characteristics at baseline are reported in Table 1. The median interval between TDM-guided administrations was 41 days (IQR 35–46) with a wide variability, ranging from 16 to 78 days (Table 2).

Appropriateness

Across all administrations, the predefined Cmin clinical cut-off was achieved in 322 of 418 determinations (82.4%, missing = 27). After excluding delayed administrations (>4 days from the scheduled date), target attainment among on-time administrations was 285/342 (89.6%, missing = 24).

Secondary outcomes

Effectiveness

At database lock, 28/101 (27.7%) patients were still receiving dalbavancin (‘ongoing group’), whereas 73/101 (72.3%) patients had completed treatment (‘completion group’). Among the latter, the median follow-up after treatment discontinuation was 175 days (IQR 22–394), with approximately half of the patients having more than 6 months of follow-up (36/73, 49.3%) (Supplementary material).

Clinical effectiveness was achieved in 55/73 patients (75.3%) in the completion group. Among the 15 clinical failures, 5 were due to the emergence of a newly isolated Gram-negative bacterium and 2 due to the occurrence of Gram-positive bacteria isolation (Supplementary material). In one of these cases, the baseline pathogen (MSSA) showed newly documented resistance to dalbavancin.

Safety

Treatment discontinuation due to AEs potentially related to ongoing treatment occurred in 3/101 (3%) cases: two gastrointestinal intolerance events and one cutaneous exanthema. Two events occurred during or immediately after the second administration and one during the third administration, which was not completed. All events subsided within a few days after discontinuation, and none were classified as severe. No clinically relevant decline in renal function or platelet count leading to treatment interruption was observed.

Workflow success

At database lock, overall workflow success, as previously defined, was observed in 74/101 (73.2%) patients: 28/28 (100%) in the ongoing group (as per definition) and 46/73 (63%) in the completion group.

Overall, 27 workflow failures were observed. The most frequent cause was clinical failure (n = 15), followed by non-BJI-related death (n = 4), interruption for AEs (n = 3) and patient-related discontinuation (n = 3), including personal choice due to logistic constraints (n = 1), poor adherence to dosing schedule (n = 1) and loss to follow-up (n = 1) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this implementation study, we evaluated a structured proactive TDM-guided dalbavancin workflow for complex BJIs within a real-world OPAT pathway. Implementation indicators showed good performance: the workflow proved feasible over a 37-month period, involving 101 patients and 418 proactive and individualized dalbavancin administrations; scheduled administrations occurred at the planned timing in 335/418 (83.5%) of cases, and pharmacological target attainment was achieved in 322/418 (82.4%) of administrations overall and in 285/342 (89.6%) when doses were delivered not delayed. Patient-driven discontinuation and loss to follow-up were uncommon (3/101, 3%), supporting a high level of retention in care. Workflow success was achieved in 73/101 (73.2%) of patients, and AEs leading to treatment discontinuation were infrequent (3/101, 3%).

Taken together, these findings suggest that a proactive TDM-guided dalbavancin workflow can be effectively integrated into routine clinical practice for the long-term treatment of complex BJIs. Within our model, TDM and subsequent CPC represented a structural component of care, enabling individualized dosing intervals. The observed variability in administration intervals (median 42 days, range 16–78) highlights the marked inter-individual pharmacokinetic variability of dalbavancin and suggests that fixed dosing schedules may not adequately reflect real-world PK behaviour during prolonged treatment courses.

Implementation outcomes supported the feasibility and the fidelity of the workflow. Patient retention was high, with only one patient lost to follow-up and two discontinuations due to logistical reasons, indicating good compliance despite the prolonged treatment duration. Additional fidelity indicators were similarly favourable: scheduled administrations occurred at the planned timing in 83% of cases, a relevant finding given that delays in administration could potentially result in transient drug underexposure. This adherence likely reflects both the pre-selection of patients suitable for long-acting therapy and the practical advantages of extended dosing intervals, which facilitate treatment organization. Moreover, pharmacological appropriateness was supported by TDM results, with concentrations above the predefined threshold in nearly 90% of administrations performed at the scheduled timing.

Implementation followed a stepwise pattern, with an initial phase of gradual uptake allowing clinical and logistical adaptation, followed by accelerated adoption as the workflow became integrated into routine OPAT practice, and finally a plateau consistent with a steady-state balance between new enrolments and treatment completions (Figures 2 and S1 and S2).

Clinical outcomes should be interpreted in the context of the marked clinical complexity of the treated population. Patients frequently presented with advanced age, substantial comorbidity burden, retained orthopaedic devices and limited feasibility of definitive source control (Tables 1 and S2). In such scenarios, strict definitions of clinical failure may not fully reflect the therapeutic objectives of prolonged antimicrobial strategies. Although criteria derived from the OVIVA Trial were used to provide a standardized framework for outcome assessment,24 the OVIVA population differs from our cohort, which largely consisted of chronic osteomyelitis cases with high clinical complexity and frequent indications for prolonged or suppressive antimicrobial strategies. Microbiological breakthrough attributable to Gram-positive pathogens during dalbavancin exposure was uncommon (2/101), suggesting that most unfavourable outcomes were more likely related to host factors, infection chronicity or incomplete source control rather than reduced antimicrobial activity. In 5/15 cases classified as clinical failure, subsequent infections were caused by Gram-negative organisms. This shift in the microbiological milieu likely reflects the ecological dynamics typical of chronic infections—particularly in the presence of difficult-to-treat wounds or skin lesions26—rather than failure of dalbavancin activity.

Our experience complements the recent TDM programme reported by Cojutti et al.,27 which applied Bayesian pharmacokinetic monitoring to chronic staphylococcal infections. While the study by Cojutti et al.,27 described a scalable hub-and-spoke advisory model, our workflow represents a fully integrated single-centre OPAT pathway in which TDM sampling and dalbavancin administration occurred during the same visit, minimizing additional hospital accesses. These approaches may be viewed as complementary implementation models for incorporating dalbavancin PK monitoring into clinical practice.

Long-acting antimicrobials are increasingly recognized as tools to simplify the management of chronic and deep-seated infections, particularly in older or comorbid patients in whom prolonged intravenous therapy or frequent hospital access may be challenging. Within this evolving framework, dalbavancin is progressively defining its role in the management of BJIs. In our cohort, it was frequently used in chronic or complex infections requiring prolonged therapy or SAT, including situations in which oral therapy would have been microbiologically appropriate (Table 1) but logistically difficult to maintain. From this perspective, dalbavancin may function not only as curative therapy but also as a pragmatic option to support long-term infection control and treatment adherence in patients with substantial barriers to conventional antimicrobial strategies.

Within the class of long-acting lipoglycopeptides, dalbavancin is often considered alongside oritavancin.28 Although oritavancin theoretically offers some pharmacological advantages, including broader antimicrobial spectrum and high bone and biofilm penetration,29 available clinical data suggest comparable effectiveness between the two agents in complicated infections.30 In real-world settings, practical considerations may influence agent selection. In our experience and in recent studies, dalbavancin proved operationally convenient within OPAT workflows, whereas oritavancin may involve greater logistical complexity related to infusion management, tolerability and PK modelling for individualized scheduling.30,31

Our study has notable strengths. The prolonged real-world implementation period supports the feasibility and sustainability of the workflow, while the large number of individualized administrations provides detailed insight into dalbavancin use in complex BJIs. The integration of TDM within routine OPAT visits minimized additional hospital access, and most microbiological diagnoses were supported by deep intraoperative or biopsy specimens, strengthening diagnostic reliability. A further strength was the close multidisciplinary collaboration among infectious disease specialists, clinical pharmacologists and microbiologists, which facilitated the rapid implementation of emerging evidence and guideline updates, including EUCAST breakpoint revisions for staphylococci (Table S4).

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the current workflow relies on specialized pharmacological expertise and TDM infrastructure that may not be available in all centres. Future work will focus on developing simplified PK models derived from our dataset to estimate individualized dosing intervals without mandatory TDM and to validate these models in external cohorts. Second, the observational design and clinical heterogeneity of BJIs preclude definitive conclusions regarding comparative effectiveness. Third, dalbavancin MICs were available only in a minority of isolates, reflecting that susceptibility testing is not routinely performed in many laboratories; systematic dalbavancin susceptibility testing has now been implemented in our microbiology laboratory as part of ongoing workflow refinement. Fourth, from an implementation science perspective, not all implementation outcomes were formally assessed. Adoption was not systematically quantified, and a comprehensive economic evaluation was not performed. Preliminary pilot data from our institution suggest that TDM-guided scheduling, compared with fixed dosing strategies, may reduce the number of dalbavancin administrations and OPAT visits, potentially improving resource utilization.32 Fifth, patient-reported outcome measures, which are gaining increasing relevance in BJIs, were also not systematically collected but may be particularly informative for long-acting antimicrobial strategies.33 Finally, our study was not specifically designed to evaluate SAT, a field in which indications and outcome definitions remain heterogeneous.34 In addition, although a clear distinction between curative and suppressive treatment strategies would have facilitated data interpretation, such a classification was often difficult to apply in this complex real-world cohort, where therapeutic goals and management plans frequently evolved over the course of the disease.

In conclusion, our findings contribute to defining the emerging role of dalbavancin in complex BJIs. Within structured OPAT pathways, proactive TDM-guided dalbavancin administration appears to represent a feasible and pragmatic strategy to support prolonged antimicrobial therapy in patients with chronic infection, limited surgical options or substantial barriers to conventional treatment approaches.
