The performance optimization model of adjusting technical and tactical decisions with ideal usages for elite female table tennis players
Authors: Xiangtong Chu, Wenxuan Yu, Xiao Xie, Yingcai Wu, Hui Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnalyzing technical and tactical behaviors in table tennis and developing optimization strategies are essential for improving player performance. However, most existing studies focus on evaluating the effectiveness of individual behaviors, but offer limited insight into how these behaviors should be strategically used. This highlights the need for data-driven methods that guide practical usage decisions.MethodThis study proposes a model to support decision-making for elite female table tennis players using three indicators: Behavior Scoring Performance (BSP), Ideal Usage Rate (IUR), and Behavior Usage Adjustability (BUA). These indicators identify optimal behavior patterns and suggest adjustments to improve scoring. For each technical-tactical attribute (stroke technique, placement, position, and game action), the model fits a performance curve to characterize the BSP-IUR relationship and guide tactical refinement. The approach operates on two levels: macro (group-level across matches) and micro (match-specific optimization).ResultsAnalysis of 59 matches between Chinese and Japanese players yielded actionable recommendations, such as increasing Neutral play while reducing Defense against Neutral and Offense actions, refining Topspin and Block usage, and enhancing serve variety among Japanese players. The most effective adjustment of a single stroke technique behavior yielded an average scoring rate increase of 1.10%. When applying all IURs of stroke techniques in a match, the average improvement reached 6.4% (SD = 4.3%). The model also supported tailored decision-making when applied to individual matches, highlighting its utility for data-driven, match-specific performance optimization.ConclusionsThis study introduces a data-driven framework to address the practical gap in optimizing behavior usage in elite women’s table tennis. By incorporating scoring performance, it identifies ideal usage patterns and informs effective adjustments. The framework outperforms baseline strategies and demonstrates robustness, supporting its value for tactical improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01347-4.

Background

Table tennis, a sport requiring two players to counter each other’s technical and tactical decisions, demands a blend of skillful stroke techniques, ball placements, shot positions, and spin effects to secure victory [1]. Each behavior distinctly affects the ball’s trajectory and, by extension, helps players create strategic advantages, disrupt opponents’ rhythm, and exploit weaknesses [2]. In competitive matches, players must continually adapt their behaviors based on their and their opponent’s strengths and specific match scenarios [3]. A skillful mix of different behaviors introduces uncertainty, often forcing opponents into suboptimal positions, thus improving chances to score. Consequently, a well-structured arrangement of behaviors is crucial, enabling players to adapt to different styles and counter opposing strategies effectively.

Analyzing a player’s technical and tactical behaviors is essential to understanding their performance and strategic approach, providing insights for optimization. Previous studies have evaluated behaviors primarily by usage rate (UR) and scoring rate (SR) to identify high-impact behaviors [4, 5]. For example, Xu [6, 7] compared Ma Long and Fan Zhendong’s performances in men’s singles finals, demonstrating Ma’s advantage in forehand strokes and serve versatility while recommending Fan focus on receiving and stroke placement. Liu et al. [8] also introduced stroke performance relevance (SPR) to assess the importance of specific behaviors across match phases.

While UR, SR, and SPR provide useful descriptive insights, they have clear limitations when applied to tactical decision-making. UR captures how often a behavior is used, and SR reflects its scoring efficiency, but when considered alone each offers only a partial view, and taken together they still fail to explain how behaviors should be adjusted in dynamic match contexts. Specifically, neither accounts for the interaction among different techniques nor the way an opponent’s adaptive strategies can quickly diminish the effectiveness of repeated actions. SPR integrates UR and SR statistically, yet it remains a static description and lacks practical interpretability for immediate tactical adjustment. These limitations highlight the necessity of introducing the ideal usage rate (IUR), which better balances effectiveness and adaptability for practical application.

Beyond these descriptive evaluations, data mining techniques such as the Apriori algorithm and Ant-Miner have revealed multidimensional patterns in players’ behavioral sequences, providing deeper insights into effective tactical combinations [9, 10]. Additionally, interactive visual analytics systems [11] can assist coaches and analysts in identifying key sequential patterns to better understand match dynamics. Artificial neural networks and regression models have also been used to analyze and predict optimal behaviors in various game scenarios [12, 13].

However, most of these models offer limited guidance for real-time or match-specific tactical adjustments, as they emphasize retrospective description rather than forward-looking behavior optimization. While these studies have identified effective behaviors, they typically emphasize individual behaviors or specific phases without addressing how to integrate multiple behaviors to both amplify a player’s strengths and maintain strategic diversity. Wu and Li’s [14] three-phase method provided UR recommendations for different match phases. For example, they suggested a UR of 25–30% for the “attack after serve” phase and 45–55% for the “stalemate” phase. Similarly, Zhang et al. [15] introduced a technique effectiveness metric and observed that increasing the UR in phases where the SR exceeds 0.5 could improve match outcomes. However, the challenge of determining how players should balance multiple behaviors to form an optimal usage mix remains unresolved.

Given the inherent competitiveness in table tennis, game theory offers a promising framework for decision optimization, and some studies have modeled table tennis decision-making as a game, finding optimal URs through Nash equilibrium in simultaneous and sequential game models [16, 17]. These studies, however, often rely on theoretical frameworks without validation from actual match data, as players aim to maximize immediate benefits rather than anticipate their opponent’s future behaviors. Whether their decision-making for the behavioral usages aligns with game theory and what the actual game process looks like still requires further investigation.

In recent years, sports analytics has adopted increasingly sophisticated frameworks, like reinforcement learning, player tracking, and predictive simulations, to guide behavior selection and performance evaluation [18, 19]. While these models offer strong theoretical precision, their reliance on large-scale, high-resolution tracking or sensor data often limits their deployment in elite competitive settings.

Complementing these data-driven approaches, computer vision systems have enabled fine-grained extraction of ball trajectories, stroke classifications, and spatial positioning in racket sports [20, 21]. When integrated with biomechanical models or reinforcement learning, they support real-time tactical simulations and opponent modeling [22, 23]. However, such systems typically require multi-angle or sensor-rich environments, which are rarely feasible in publicly broadcasted elite matches.

In contrast, this study introduces a low-granularity and interpretable framework based on behavior coding from broadcast match footage, allowing coaches and players to make informed tactical adjustments without requiring specialized sensors or equipment. The framework incorporates three core indicators: behavior scoring performance (BSP), ideal usage rate (IUR), and behavior usage adjustability (BUA), which help identify behaviors in need of adjustment. BSP measures the scoring efficiency of each behavior, where higher values suggest increased usage. IUR is derived from BSP and reflects the optimal usage rate for maximizing overall scoring. BUA quantifies the deviation between a behavior’s current and ideal usage rates. Performance outcomes are simulated using a first-order Markov chain model [24], with adjustments prioritized based on UR-IUR gaps. The method is applied to elite women’s singles matches between Chinese and Japanese players, generating recommendations at both group and individual levels.

Method

Samples

This study analyzed 59 women’s singles matches between Chinese and Japanese players from 2021 to 2024, focusing on major international tournaments such as the Olympic Games, WTT Championships, and the Table Tennis World Cup. These two countries have dominated women’s table tennis, with their players consistently ranked in the global top 30 (ITTF rankings, November 2024). Eight players were included in total, with four representing each country. They all use shake-hand grips and double-sided pimple-in rubbers. Their comparable playing styles allow for meaningful comparisons in skill and tactical decision-making. Match videos were collected from broadcast footage and online platforms. The full match list is provided in Supplemental Material S1.

Data collection and preprocessing

Data collection utilized a table tennis system to analyze four attributes: stroke technique, placement, position, and game action. Observation indices (Table 1), based on Pfeiffer et al. [24] and refined by Zhou and Zhang [25], ensured high reliability. To assess the objectivity and consistency of the annotation process, Zhou and Zhang [25] conducted an inter-rater reliability test based on 10 matches, where two trained analysts independently annotated match videos. The resulting Cohen’s kappa values were k = 0.925 for stroke technique, k = 0.977 for placement, k = 0.969 for position, and k = 0.966 for game action, indicating a high level of agreement [26, 27]. Since this study uses the same observation system, coding protocol, and analyst team, the reliability validation is directly extended to the present data.

Evaluation and analysis methods

Statistical analysis

We first determined the appropriate statistical approach by evaluating data distribution. Shapiro-Wilk tests [29] revealed significant non-normality in all behavioral UR samples (all p < 0.05), prompting the use of Mann-Whitney U tests [30] for all match-level comparisons, including cross-national differences and pre-post optimization effects in SR and UR. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to control for false discovery rates [31]. Effect sizes followed the thresholds of Karadimitriou and Marshall [32]: small (r < 0.3), medium (0.3 ≤ r < 0.5), and large (r ≥ 0.5).

A power analysis showed that with n = 59, α = 0.05, and power = 0.80, the minimum detectable effect size was r = 0.252. As optimization was applied independently per match, no nested structure adjustment was required. All statistical analyses were conducted in Python 3.9 using scipy.stats and statsmodels.

Evaluation against baseline optimization

We compared our IUR-based optimization model to a widely used baseline method [24, 33] that adjusts behavior usage purely based on observed transition frequencies, without accounting for scoring performance.

In the baseline method, each behavior’s usage probability is increased by 1–6% according to a fixed formula derived from its original transition rate [24], and the rates of other behaviors in the same state are proportionally reduced to maintain a valid probability distribution. A Markov simulation is then used to estimate the resulting SR. If the SR increases, the behavior is considered a successful optimization.

Since the two strategies rely on different optimization principles-one score-driven, the other frequency-driven-the behaviors selected, UR changes applied, and SR outcomes may differ considerably. To enable fair comparison, we report for each method: (1) the number of improved behaviors, (2) average SR gain, (3) average UR change, and (4) their ratio, which reflects optimization efficiency. This offers a unified basis to evaluate practical effectiveness across both approaches.

Sensitivity analysis

In addition to baseline comparison, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the optimization results under small perturbations. Specifically, we introduced random fluctuations of 1%-5% to behavior URs and recalculated the resulting SRs. This analysis tested whether the identified optimization solutions were stable under minor changes in input values, which is particularly important given the potential variability in real match conditions. Stable SR gains across perturbations support the reliability of the proposed method.

Results

Behavior performance comparison between players

Fig. 2 compares the BSP values of Chinese and Japanese players across different states. Significant differences were observed across all four technical-tactical attributes. Chinese players demonstrated higher BSP in several key scenarios, including Pendulum during serving (p = 0.001, r = 0.224), backhand Topspin against Pendulum (p = 0.004, r = 0.268), and Push techniques against short forehand (p < 0.001, r = 0.309; p = 0.033, r = 0.175 for forehand and backhand, respectively). Japanese players showed stronger performance in Reverse and Tomahawk serves, as well as in most behaviors responding to Pendulum.

While many differences reached statistical significance, only a subset exceeded the minimum detectable effect size (r = 0.252) under our sample size (n = 59) with 80% power and α = 0.05. For example, the Chinese advantage in Push and backhand Topspin usage met or surpassed this threshold, suggesting practically meaningful differences. In contrast, results with smaller r values (e.g., r < 0.2), though statistically significant, may have limited real-world impact and require cautious interpretation.

Discussion

Developing optimal strategies for table tennis players’ decision-making is crucial yet challenging. While studies like [23] have utilized ball and racket data to improve robot response strategies, capturing such data in real matches has hindered personalized optimization for elite players, creating a gap in tailored performance enhancement.

This study bridges this gap by analyzing abstract decision-making data, emphasizing how the relative performance of behaviors should influence their usage frequency. We introduce three key indicators, BSP, IUR, and BUA, to evaluate scoring performance and behavioral usage, the approach offers actionable insights for improvement, with simulations demonstrating its potential to enhance players’ SR. While the observed SR improvements from behavioral adjustments are often modest, even small percentage gains can prove meaningful in elite-level matches, where each point carries substantial weight. Thus, our method aims not to induce strategic overhauls, but to guide targeted and feasible refinements to enhance competitive consistency.

Integration of behavioral usage for performance evaluation

This study’s approach to calculating BSP combines behavioral usages with points scored or lost, contrasting with prior methods focused mainly on scoring like SPR [8]. Here, UR reflects the effectiveness of behaviors with specific URs [15, 35]. This combination differentiates behaviors by performance while maintaining the player’s general style. In real matches, players sometimes use rare behaviors to surprise opponents, creating unexpected scoring opportunities. Averaging these rare events might overlook strategic nuances; thus, our BSP indicator captures usage patterns while emphasizing context-specific performance. For example, Japanese players used the Tomahawk technique only twice in one notable match, but it created a scoring or neutral position, adding pressure. Avoiding such behaviors would miss this potential edge. For more common techniques, our results align with previous findings, such as the strength of Chinese players in Topspin [8]. Additionally, transition matrix analysis offers further insights into enhancing specific behaviors in varied match contexts.

Comparison of optimization strategies based on different technical-tactical attributes

This study’s primary contribution lies in introducing the IUR, which provides players with a structured framework for behavioral optimization. Recognizing that decision-making patterns vary significantly across technical-tactical attributes, we modeled distinct relationships between BSP and IUR for each attribute. Aligning behavioral usage with IUR recommendations led to improved SR. The best-fit relationships show that stroke techniques, which involve distinct biomechanics and affect ball trajectory differently [36, 37], enable the highest SR increase, averaging 6.4% ± 4.3%. This highlights IUR’s effectiveness in guiding players toward high-value behaviors within an optimal range.

In contrast, adjustments in stroke placement improved the SR in only 69.5% of matches, with a relatively modest average gain of 0.8% ± 2.1%. This may reflect inherent characteristics of placement behaviors. First, this limited effect may be due to the coarse granularity of placement categories, which are typically grouped into long, half-long, and short zones. At the elite level, players often exhibit similar stroke motions across these zones, making it difficult to differentiate and optimize effectively. Then, the scoring impact of placement often depends on contextual variables such as the opponent’s position, anticipation, and tempo, which are not captured in our usage-based model. Lastly, modifying placement alone may not lead to substantial SR changes unless accompanied by coordinated changes in stroke technique or timing. These factors suggest that stroke placement adjustment offers limited independent influence on scoring and may require more detailed data, like precise 3D spatial data of both players and the ball [23], to fully exploit their tactical value.

Rational analysis of optimization strategies

The UR-IUR gap, referred to as BUA, is central to optimizing behavior usage. A large BUA suggests either underuse of effective behaviors with high BSP or overuse of ineffective ones with low BSP. Although aligning UR with IUR generally improves SR, simulations revealed exceptions. For example, increasing stroke placement variety in Chinese players’ serves based on IUR did not lead to better SR. Possible explanations include: (1) greater flexibility in receivers’ responses during serves compared to rallies; (2) changes in UR may shift game states in ways that favor opponents; and (3) optimizing one behavior can inadvertently reduce the effectiveness of others. These findings highlight the need for a structured two-step process, where initial optimization is followed by simulation-based validation to ensure reliable results.

To demonstrate the method’s effectiveness, we further evaluate the found optimization solutions with domain knowledge. For general optimizations, emphasizing offensive techniques [38], such as Topspin, over defensive ones like Block aligns with the strategic preference for offense. From a biomechanical perspective, the Topspin enable stable racket acceleration and greater spin production, which not only increase direct scoring chances but also reduce the opponent’s reaction time [28]. In contrast, Block is typically a reactive technique that absorbs rather than generates forces, which can stabilize rallies but often leaves the initiative with the opponent. Additionally, continuing with backhand Topspin in response to an opponent’s backhand Topspin, a widely observed tactic [39], is reasonable. Then, switching from defensive to more aggressive, Neutral actions when facing Offense states aligns with maintaining tactical flexibility. Last, increasing serve variety of ball placements for Japanese players, as shown in Fig. 2, can enhance match unpredictability, challenging opponents’ response strategies.

In the analyzed match, part of the optimizations aligned with general strategies, and match video observations validated their effectiveness. The increase in backhand Topspin usage was prompted by different factors for each player. For P1, the main reason was that P2 often used Topspin to quickly change the ball’s trajectory to P1’s forehand side, forcing P1 into a forehand stance and leaving them vulnerable. To counter this, P1 needed to either anticipate these changes or adopt a more aggressive approach to reduce P2’s ability to alter the ball’s direction. In this case, increasing backhand Topspin usage in advance would allow P1 to press the opponent. For P2, the adjustment stemmed from multiple failures in responding to balls landing in the middle of the table due to poor positioning while using a pivot stance. P2 stood too close to the middle, making forehand strikes difficult. By using her backhand more effectively in such situations, P2 could put more pressure on P1. This adjustment is reasonable because backhand Topspin allows quicker recovery and more stable control against mid-table placements.

Comprehensive evaluation of the optimization framework

The proposed IUR-guided optimization framework demonstrates consistent effectiveness across different technical-tactical attributes. Among these, stroke technique showed the most significant and stable improvement in SR for both Chinese and Japanese players. This suggests that stroke technique behaviors are particularly responsive to targeted usage adjustments and should be prioritized in tactical planning. While other attributes exhibited minor or non-significant improvements, their numerical gains still indicate potential benefits under certain conditions.

Compared to the frequency-based baseline method [24], our framework identified more effective behavior adjustments and produced greater average gains in SRs. This advantage stems from incorporating scoring information into the adjustment process, allowing for more performance-oriented and context-aware refinements. Although our method required slightly larger changes in URs, it achieved higher scoring gains per unit of adjustment, indicating greater efficiency in translating behavior modifications into competitive advantage.

The robustness analysis further supports the reliability of the optimization outputs. Across most attributes, the SR declined gradually as random perturbations were introduced, demonstrating the sensitivity of optimized behaviors to deviations. This confirms that the optimized solution is behavior-specific and designed to operate under precise tactical conditions. Notably, in stroke placement, performance improved slightly under perturbation, suggesting that some local optima might be surpassed through additional fine-tuning.

Taken together, these findings highlight the framework’s contribution in enabling data-driven, attribute-specific tactical guidance. It not only improves scoring potential under realistic constraints but also ensures robustness under behavioral fluctuation. These characteristics make the framework valuable for coaching applications and match preparation, especially when tailored adjustments are needed for individual players or high-stakes competitions. Because it relies only on broadcast footage, the framework can be applied in the post-match review without requiring specialized equipment. In practice, the UR-IUR analysis can support coaches by quickly identifying behaviors that require adjustment. It can also inform the design of targeted training tasks, ensuring that practice priorities are consistent with competition demands. Its outcomes can also be delivered through visual interfaces or visualizations embedded in the match videos that provide intuitive tactical feedback for training and preparation.

Limitation

The optimization model proposed for guiding players’ technical and tactical decisions remains exploratory and presents several limitations. First, the analysis was conducted on a relatively small sample, limited to elite female players from China and Japan. Although the matches were drawn from recent top-tier competitions between 2021 and 2024, the temporal scope is still narrow, and broader generalizability requires validation with more diverse datasets, including different levels of play, genders, and regions.

Second, the optimization of stroke placement remains imprecise due to the lack of fine-grained spatial information. Including additional features such as exact landing coordinates of the ball and the player’s position at each moment could enable more context-sensitive recommendations. Third, the current framework does not consider contextual variables such as match importance, opponent strategy adaptation, or physical fatigue, which may affect decision-making. Integrating such dynamic factors into the model may enhance the realism and applicability of optimization outputs.

Fourth, while simulation results demonstrated improvements in SRs, these evaluations were based on probabilistic modeling rather than actual match interventions. The observed performance gains under perturbation conditions indicate that the optimized strategies are sensitive to execution fidelity and may require careful implementation to achieve real-world benefits. In some attributes, performance even improved under random perturbation, suggesting that the current optimization may not always represent a global optimum.

Fifth, although the comparison with baseline methods showed higher adjustment efficiency and better scoring outcomes, our framework still relies on behavior-level aggregation without capturing the full sequence of tactical exchanges. A more fine-grained, sequence-aware modeling could improve the precision of suggested adjustments.

Lastly, result interpretation depends heavily on reviewing complete match footage, which is labor-intensive and time-consuming. Future work should develop semiautomated tools that link behavioral indicators with annotated videos, enabling more scalable and accessible analysis.

Conclusion

This study developed an optimization framework to improve decision-making in technical and tactical behaviors for elite female table tennis players. Its core innovation lies in introducing the ideal usage rate derived from behavior scoring performance, integrating it with behavior usage adjustability, and validating adjustments through Markov chain simulation. Together, these steps provide a data-driven and interpretable approach for identifying optimal behavioral usage.

Application to Chinese and Japanese women’s singles players demonstrated that the framework can reveal meaningful adjustments, such as reducing passive blocks and enhancing proactive topspin usage. More broadly, the framework offers practical value for post-match review, where coaches can identify usage gaps, design targeted training tasks, and translate optimization results into tactical planning.

While the present study is limited to simulation-based evaluation, future work should test on-court effectiveness and explore integration into coaching systems to strengthen the model’s practical deployment.
