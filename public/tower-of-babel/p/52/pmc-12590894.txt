A comparative analysis of transposable element diversity and evolution across 75 bee genomes
Authors: Hannah L. Cook, John S. Sproul, Elizabeth A. Murray, Silas Bossert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransposable elements (TEs) are repetitive DNA sequences that can alter their position and abundance within genomes. While TEs are known to have various impacts on genome structure and function, our understanding of how their evolution has shaped genome diversity across the Tree of Life remains limited. Characterizing repetitive DNA profiles in a phylogenomic comparative context provides valuable insight into the evolutionary roles of these important genomic features. Bees are a well-studied and species-rich lineage which makes them an effective tool for exploring the evolution of TEs and their relationship to functional traits in non-model organisms.ResultsHere, we characterize TE dynamics across high-quality genome assemblies of 75 bee species to quantify their diversity in an evolutionary context. We find that bees show a striking amount of variation in repetitive element diversity and abundance, comprising between 4.4% to 82.1% of their genomes. We determine this variation is largely responsible for genome size differences, and lineages have unique signatures of TE accumulation with many genomes containing TE copies with a putatively recent history of activity. Additionally, we find a reduction of repetitive DNA to be associated with species that produce multiple generations per year.ConclusionsOur study provides the most comprehensive analysis of TE evolution across bees to date, allowing deep insight into genome evolution of the most species rich lineage of pollinating insects. This work will provide the foundation for future research on the evolution of TEs across bees, and the impact of TEs on genome evolution in general.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12190-9.

Background

Transposable elements (TEs) are DNA sequences that can move to new loci within genomes, often duplicating themselves in the process. This act of transposition introduces genomic novelty ranging from small-scale insertional mutations [1–3] to large-scale structural transformations [4–6]. Mutations generated by TEs contribute substantially to the variability of genomes both across and within species. Despite their ubiquity among eukaryotic life [7], our understanding of how TEs persist in diversity and abundance across evolutionary time remains largely unknown in non-model organisms.

TEs have historically been disregarded as “junk DNA” that persists in genomes without function, but more recent research has highlighted their significance as facilitators of evolution [8, 9]. Mutations induced by TEs can result in disruptive, deleterious changes [10, 11] but they may also generate modifications that promote adaptability and diversification [12]. The idea that TEs are capable of significant phenotypic effects is a hypothesis that went hand in hand with the discovery of TEs themselves. When Barbara McClintock initially discovered TEs in the 1940s [13], she deemed these features to be “controlling elements” due to their regulatory effects on nearby genes [14]. McClintock also found the activation of TEs to be a coordinated genomic response induced by shock, implicating that TEs have the potential to promote evolutionary adaptability during times of organismal stress [15]. Despite the importance of McClintock’s findings, our understanding of TEs has lagged behind our knowledge on sequences traditionally considered to be functional (e.g., genic sequences). While evidence substantiating McClintock’s early work has been accumulating for model organisms like maize or mice [16–18], characterizing TEs in a biodiversity framework provides broader insight into their evolutionary contributions across species.

TEs are a major component of repetitive DNA, which also includes tandem repeats such as satellite DNA, simple repeats, and other multicopy sequences. While our analyses generally focus specifically on TEs, we will reference “repetitive elements” in places where dynamics of these other categories of repetitive DNA is relevant. The repetitive nature of TEs has historically made them challenging to sequence, assemble, and characterize. Technological advancements now allow for the sequencing of long, continuous segments of DNA, improving our understanding of genome architecture [19]. Specifically, long reads allow us to recover highly repetitive genomic regions with increased accuracy [20, 21]. Concurrently, the development and improvement of repeat annotation software has increased the accessibility of studying repetitive DNA across a broad range of non-model taxa [22].

TEs are characterized by their replication strategies and insertion mechanisms [23], and repeat annotation software uses a variety of library-based, signature-based, and/or de novo approaches to identify and classify these elements [24]. Class I elements (i.e., retrotransposons) replicate through a copy-and-paste mechanism using reverse transcriptase and an RNA intermediate. Within Class I elements, LTR retrotransposons are characterized by their long terminal repeats while non-LTR retrotransposons (i.e., LINEs and SINEs) lack these repeats. Class II elements (i.e., DNA transposons) alter their location in the genome through cut-and-paste or peel-and-paste mechanisms. Cut-and-paste DNA transposons are characterized by their terminal inverted repeats and the presence of a transposase gene. Transposase enzymes excise the TE, followed by integrase enzymes reinserting the sequence elsewhere in the genome. Because these DNA transposons are not self-replicative by nature, their duplication is an indirect result of their hosts’ DNA repair functions [25]. Alternatively, peel-and-paste DNA transposons (i.e., Helitrons) lack terminal inverted repeats, encode a RepHel protein, and amplify by a rolling-circle mechanism.

As copies of TEs accumulate over time, they can comprise major proportions of genomes. Eukaryotic genomes differ drastically in size with the largest known genome, the fork fern Tmesipteris oblanceolata [26] having a 61,000-fold difference from the smallest known eukaryotic genome, that of the microsporidian Encephalitozoon intestinalis [27]. A long-standing question of evolutionary biology is why these differences in genome size occur. Large genomes do not consistently correlate with gene number or biological complexity, a phenomenon known as the C-value enigma [28–30]. Significant correlations between genome size and repeat abundance have been found across a variety of taxonomic groups including mammals [31], plants [32], fish [33], and insects [34]. The evolutionary forces that drive repeat accumulation and deletion remain an area of open research. Past theoretical and empirical work emphasizes the impact of drift versus selection and deleterious effects versus neutrality of TEs in driving patterns of their accumulation or loss (see review by Blommaert 2020). Whether selection or drift predominates, several studies highlight the role effective population size in driving TE dynamics [35–38]. Leveraging the increasing accessibility of high-resolution genomic data across clades enables identification and development of new models that can offer greater insights into connections between repeat dynamics, genome size, and the life history and other phenotypic traits within lineages [39]. Analyzing TE abundance and diversity within insects, the most diverse group of living animals, represents a particularly rich source of new models for understanding how TEs shape diverse species, their genomes, and phenotypes [40–42].

Bees are the most species-rich lineage of pollinivorous insects [43] and an emerging model system for genomics research [44, 45] due to this species diversity, as well as their agricultural and economic significance. Bees are also unique in that they are exceptionally well-studied across their taxonomic breadth in the context of their ecology and behavior. Despite the rapidly growing availability of sequenced genomes, our understanding of bee repeatomes has been limited to descriptions of individual genome assemblies, surveys of one repeat superfamily [46, 47] or comparative analyses using a subset of primarily eusocial species that exhibit reduced TE content [41, 48–50]. This has led to the impression that bees overall have reduced TE content, but eusocial species are not representative of the complex diversity found across bees. In fact, 90% of all bee species are either solitary or parasitic [51] with a vast array of behaviors, morphologies, and life histories. Now that genome assemblies have increased in quality and taxonomic representation, we can use this data to greatly build upon our current knowledge of TE dynamics across the lineage.

In the present study, we characterize the TE profiles of high-quality genome assemblies for 75 bee species, encompassing a broad phylogenetic diversity in our sampling. We consider the role of TEs in contribution to genome size, infer the evolutionary histories of TE activity across species, and explore the potential associations between TEs on nearby genes. Lastly, we consider the relationship between repetitive element abundance and functional traits including voltinism, sociality, diet breadth, nesting location, and body size. We intend for this study to provide the groundwork to support further research in this rapidly developing field of TE biology.

Results

Phylogenetic reconstruction

We studied the evolutionary history of repetitive DNA in bees in light of a robust phylogenetic hypothesis. To this end, we utilized high quality genome assemblies of 75 publicly available bee species, as well as six closely related apoid wasp relatives, representing all bee families except the small and geographically restricted Stenotritidae (Additional File 1: Table S1). Our phylogenetic reconstruction is based on 2,508 aligned ultraconserved elements (UCEs) which were extracted using the Phyluce pipeline [52], a phylogenomics approach that has found wide adoption for reconstructing bee phylogeny in recent years [53–55]. Maximum likelihood analysis of the resulting 3,643,193 aligned base pairs (2,490,493 informative sites) in IQ-Tree2 [56] recovered a well-supported phylogenetic hypothesis that is largely congruent with recent research on higher-level phylogenetic studies of Apoidea [57, 58] (Additional File 2: Figure S1). Diversity of TE content in bee genomes

Our repeat annotation analysis showed repetitive element abundance and diversity to differ substantially across distantly related bees, as well as among species within the same genus (Fig. 1). Repetitive element content includes TEs, simple repeats, satellite DNA, multicopy RNAs, and unclassified repeats. Furthermore, TEs encompass repeats classified as SINEs, LINEs, Penelope-like elements, LTRs, and DNA transposons (including Helitrons). We annotated repeats de novo using two pipelines, Earl Grey and RepeatModeler2/RepeatMasker. Both pipelines yielded consistent estimates of total repeat content across genome assemblies (paired t-test: t = −1.01, p = 0.316). However, we report the results from Earl Grey (Additional File 4: Table S6) as this pipeline classified a significantly greater proportion of repetitive elements (paired t-test: t = −4.91, p = 4.70e-06).

Of the bee species included in this study, total repetitive element content ranges from 4.4% in Apis dorsata (Apidae) to 82.1% in Xylocopa violacea (Apidae) for the respective genomes. These two species encompass the greatest differences in repeat content and are both members of the same family, Apidae, which exemplifies the dynamic evolution of repetitive DNA in apids. Interestingly, despite significant variation found within the bee families, we detect a pattern that the bee families Andrenidae, Halictidae, and Colletidae, which are traditionally referred to as ‘short-tongued’ bee families [61, 62], have a consistently higher amount of repetitive elements compared to the ‘long-tongued’ bees (i.e., Apidae and Megachilidae) (Welch’s t-test: t = 5.80, p = 1.74e-07).

We found evidence of dynamic evolution of major TE categories across bees (Figs. 1b and 2). LTRs are the most commonly classified TE group among bees (mean = 8.1%) with notable expansions in Macropis europaea (Melittidae), Megachile leachella (Megachilidae), Lasioglossum lativentre (Halictidae), and Bombus terrestris (Apidae) (Figs. 1b and 2). Gypsy is the most prevalent superfamily of LTR retrotransposons overall, making up an average of 5.9% of bee genomes. DIRS LTRs are relatively uncommon with a patchy distribution across the bee phylogeny (Fig. 2). DNA transposons are the second most commonly classified TE group found in bee genomes (mean = 4.6%) (Figs. 1b and 2). DNA transposons, PiggyBac and TcMar, are consistently present across lineages while Maverick transposons have undergone a notable expansion among short-tongued bees. One superfamily of TEs, En-Spm (or CACTA) was not detected in any genomes, despite being found in the genomes of other insects spanning multiple insect orders (Mengenilla moldrzyki (Strepsiptera), Rhodnius prolixus (Hemiptera), and Bombyx mori (Lepidoptera)) [63].

LINEs are generally uncommon across bees (mean = 4.1%) with the notable exception of a large expansion of R1 elements in Xylocopa violacea, encompassing 71.5% of this genome assembly (Figs. 1b and 2). Given these unexpected findings, we further studied the length and distribution of these repetitive sequences. Upon closer inspection, we find these abundant R1 repeats in Xylocopa violacea are often short, tandem satellite DNA (satDNA) repeats found across multiple scaffolds. This finding suggests that R1 LINEs were the parent sequence that gave rise to satDNA repeats which subsequently underwent proliferation that can account for some of the genome size increase in this lineage. Among all bees, R2 and RTE elements exhibit a patchy distribution similar to DIRS LTRs but display different patterns of presence and absence among species (Fig. 2). SINEs are uncommon in bees, with only two genomes of closely related species consisting of approximately only 0.01% of SINEs, Nomada ruficornis (Apidae) and Nomada hirtipes (Apidae). SINEs are non-autonomous and require the presence of proteins encoded in LINEs for their transposition. The overall reduced presence of LINEs likely influences the lack of SINEs found in bees.

Similarly, the abundance of one TE group is often positively correlated with the abundance of another TE group. LTR and DNA transposon abundances are significantly positively correlated (PGLS: slope = 0.530, R2pred = 0.334, p = 0.018), as are LTRs with LINEs (PGLS: slope = 0.412, R2pred = 0.078, p < 0.0001). This finding supports the idea that most bee genomes are not typically dominated by a single group of TE but are host to a diversity of genomic features.

The discovery of this substantial variation in TE abundance led us to investigate the phylogenetic signal of each TE group. We determined whether closely related species have more similar TE content compared to distantly related species by measuring phylogenetic signal using Pagel’s λ [64] and Blomberg’s K [65]. Specifically, this allows us to quantify whether closely related species share similar TE abundance compared to species drawn at random from our tree. We find DNA transposons to have a strong phylogenetic signal, followed by LTRs, and lastly LINEs with a relatively weak signal (Table 1). Contribution of TEs to genome assembly size

A long-standing question in evolutionary biology is the source of variation across genome size. Here we use genome assembly size as a proxy for genome size, which is common practice [33, 40]. We find genome assembly size to be strongly, positively correlated with both total repetitive element content (PGLS: slope = 0.365, R2pred = 0.876, p < 0.0001) and total TE content (PGLS: slope = 0.181, R2pred = 0.639, p < 0.0001) when accounting for phylogenetic relatedness (Fig. 3). To ensure genome assembly size variation is not driven by differences in sequencing or assembly methods, we evaluated the role of sequencing metrics on genome assembly size. The average Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) [66] completeness across genome assemblies was 96.58% (sd: ± 1.07). We further detected no significant relationship between genome assembly size and BUSCO completeness nor contig N50 values. Genome assembly size can potentially be overestimated in individuals with high heterozygosity [67]. To evaluate the likelihood of artificially inflated genome size estimates due to assembly error, we use the number of duplicated BUSCO genes as a proxy for unresolved heterozygous regions. These results underline that genome size evolution in bees is influenced by a number of complex factors and relationships across these genomic features.

Transposition history and activity

In addition to characterizing TE diversity and abundance, we considered the recent history of TE activity for each bee species. TE landscape plots show evidence of recent TE activity in all analyzed species because all genomes contain TEs without any substitutions from the consensus sequence (Fig. 4). The presence of several nearly identical copies within species is often interpreted as TEs that have experienced a recent or ongoing expansion and provides initial insights into which TEs are driving differentiation within lineages [68, 69]. LTRs are the most commonly occurring TE group with few substitutions, with the majority of assemblies hosting Gypsy elements that contain copies with zero substitutions. Our analysis revealed evidence of a recent Gypsy expansion that accounts for notable differences between recently diverged sister taxa, Bombus bifarius and Bombus vancouverensis (Fig. 4, note y-axis differences). Species with the highest proportion of TE content (Fig. 4A) tend to harbor more diverged TE copies compared to their closely related counterparts. This is demonstrated in the divergence of K2P distance peaks in species pairs such as Lasioglossum lativentre and Lasioglossum morio, Megachile leachella and Megachile rotundata, and Xylocopa violacaea and Ceratina calcarata (Fig. 4). This pattern shows that TEs have rapidly accumulated early in the divergence of some species and subsequently degraded over time, leading to very different TE profiles in comparison to their close relatives. We hypothesize that the TEs which are responsible for the major genome size expansions may have quickly accumulated mutations, leading to their inactivation. Overall, our landscape plots indicate a dynamic history of both gradual and abrupt TE expansion and decay throughout the evolution of bee genomes.

Associations between TEs and protein-coding genes

There is increasing evidence that TEs can have important effects on genic regions which can impact phenotypes [16, 18]. To conduct an initial investigation of associations between TEs and protein-coding genes, we use BUSCO genes as a proxy for understanding where TEs occur in relation to protein-coding DNA. This approach was recently developed by Heckenhauer et al., [70] and scripts were adapted in Sproul et al., [42]. Briefly, when TEs with open reading frames are present adjacent to, or inserted within BUSCO loci, the BUSCO algorithm often extends the BUSCO gene boundaries to include these TE sequences. Thus, quantification of cases where BUSCOs include TE annotations can serve as a surrogate measure for genome-wide association between TEs and protein-coding genes. Hymenopteran BUSCO genes in which adjacent or interrupting TE element sequences were detected are hereafter referred to as TE-associated BUSCOs. Evaluating 5,991 Hymenopteran BUSCO genes per genome, we detected between 5 and 112 TE-associated BUSCOs, indicating a large range in the number of genes occurring nearby TEs among bee species (Fig. 1). This BUSCO dataset constitutes a nontrivial 39% of the 15,314 genes predicted to occur in the most recent, high-quality Apis mellifera genome assembly [71]. The overall uniformity in the amount of low-copy DNA across bee species (Fig. 1C) suggests this BUSCO-based approach provides robust estimates of TE-gene associations for bees as a whole.

While we recovered TE-associated BUSCOs in all examined species, only two species have more than 30 TE-associated BUSCOs, and interestingly, both are in the genus Lasioglossum (Halictidae). In their evolutionary history, sweat bees of the genus Lasioglossum have repeatedly transitioned from solitary to eusocial behaviors and vice versa, rendering this lineage an attractive model system for understanding the molecular mechanisms that underpin gains and losses of eusociality [72–74]. We find that Lasioglossum calceatum and Lasioglossum pauxillum contain the highest number of TE-associated BUSCOs and both species display eusocial behaviors, followed by Seladonia tumulorum (Halictidae), which is another, relatively closely related eusocial halictine bee that diverged from the most recent common ancestor with Lasioglossum around 45 million years ago [57]. Interestingly, another clade with elevated levels of TE-associated BUSCOs is the genus Bombus, despite having an overall reduced proportion of TE content. Similar to halictines, Bombus species also display a variety of behaviors in regard to sociality and parasitism. Our finding of wide variation in the location and abundance of repeat-associated BUSCOs across bee species provides another illustration of how TEs drive variation in genome content in the group. These results suggest that bees have good potential as a model for understanding potential functional impacts of TE activity on gene activity and phenotypes.

Correlation of functional traits to total repeat abundance

Because of centuries of study, bees stand out as one of the very few insect groups for which both behaviors and ecology are comparatively well known across the entirety of their taxonomic diversity. This provides an opportunity to investigate relationships between functional traits and repetitive DNA. We gathered information about bee morphology and behavior and tested whether five biological traits are associated with total repetitive element abundance (Fig. 5). We find lower levels of repeat abundance to be strongly associated with multivoltinism, a polylectic diet, and advanced eusociality. Alternatively, we find an expansion of repeat abundance to be associated with univoltinism, below-ground nesting, oligolecty, and solitary living. Discussion

TEs are critical components of genomes due to their structural and regulatory roles, and their ability to generate genomic novelty. Despite their ubiquity, the study of TEs has been historically hindered by the difficulty of sequencing, assembling, and annotating highly repetitive DNA with accuracy. In the current era of biodiversity genomics, our ability to generate high quality genome assemblies is surpassing our capacity to analyze them meaningfully and in a phylogenetic comparative framework. Utilizing this abundance of high-quality data, we characterize the repeat profiles of 75 bee species sequenced with long-read technology in the most extensive analysis of TE composition among bees to date, representing six families, 24 genera, and 125 million years of evolution [57]. These efforts shed light on the variation of TE abundance and diversity for these economically and ecologically important pollinators, as well as provide insight into the mechanisms that shape repetitive element evolution generally.

Insects are characteristic in their variability of repeat landscapes within and across insect orders [40–42] and we demonstrate that bees exemplify this diversity. This research contradicts the previous findings that bee genomes contain low proportions of repetitive DNA overall [41, 75]. The inclusion of primarily eusocial bee species with reduced TE content informed this inference in prior studies, and our increased sampling has rectified this misconception. Here we show that repetitive elements in bee species range from 4.4% to 82.1% genomic proportion, with the largest bee genome consisting of up to 15 repetitive elements for every one gene (Figs. 1 and 2). Our findings emphasize the role this variation plays in generating genome size differences. TE and total repetitive element content are both significantly positively correlated with genome assembly size, and among TEs, we find evidence that expansions of LINEs, LTRs, and DNA transposons all contribute significantly (Fig. 3). Our study adds to the growing evidence that the proliferation of repetitive DNA contributes substantially to the expansion of genomes [31–34], and that bees are no exception with respect to the C-value enigma: the genomic content of low copy DNA is surprisingly similarly sized across all bee species, despite great variation in genome size (Fig. 1c).

Highly conserved TE insertions within an assembly indicate the existence of copies with a putatively recent history of activity. We found evidence that TEs are not only a relict within the evolutionary history of bee genomes, but that they have recently given rise to the differences we see among extant species. Prior work in Apis mellifera (Apidae) identified intact Mariner DNA transposons [71] despite its overall lack of TE content. Our TE landscape plots support this finding and showed evidence of recent activity from a variety of other TE types (Fig. 4). Most notably, recent LTR Gypsy element activity was broadly evident across bee species (Fig. 4). High overall levels of Gypsy elements are consistent with our annotation results that showed LTRs are the most abundant classified TEs across all species (Figs. 1 and 2). At finer scales, Bombus bifarius and Bombus vancouverensis are two recently diverged species of bumblebees in our sampling [76] for which we estimated a crown age of just 0.34 mya. Interestingly, Bombus vancouverensis has elevated levels of recent TE activity (especially Gypsy elements) relative to Bombus bifarius (Fig. 4), which underlines dynamic TE evolution within comparatively short evolutionary time frames. These findings corroborate the idea that TEs can bear great utility in delimiting species boundaries [77, 78]. Further, as agents of rapid evolution, TEs have been documented to generate phenotypic variation important for environmental adaptation [9, 79, 80] and promoting speciation [81–83]. Bombus vancouverensis is a bumblebee species restricted to high elevations in the Sierra-Cascade mountains of North America and exhibits strong signatures of selection related to thermal and precipitation adaptation [84]. Further study of active elements in bees can clarify their potential roles in adaptation, speciation, and response to environmental stressors.

Additionally, the genomic variation generated by repetitive elements among bees has strong associations with behavioral adaptations. We confirmed the previous findings that highly eusocial species have reduced repeat content [48], even when increasing the taxonomic representation from 10 species [48] to 75 (present study). This could be considered a paradoxical finding given that both eusociality and the unique haplodiploidy sex determination system in hymenopterans are expected to result in reduced effective population sizes compared to other insect species [85]. Theory predicts that low effective population size reduces the efficacy of natural selection at purging deleterious mutations (e.g., TE insertions) such that genome size should be positively correlated with low effective population size [86]. The observation that eusocial bees do not follow this trend, corroborated here with increased sampling, suggests genome size in these lineages is not determined by neutral, but rather, adaptive processes. Hypothesized explanations for small genome size despite low effective population size in hymenopterans also tie back to the details of their haplodiploid and eusocial traits. Haploid males expose recessive mutations (e.g., TEs) leading to the removal of lethal alleles and fixation of beneficial mutations at a faster rate than their diploid counterparts [87, 88], and a lack of recombination in males reduces opportunities for TE expansion [89]. High relatedness in eusocial colonies lends to genetic diversity further limiting expansion opportunities of those TEs which are present [90]. In addition, eusocial insects are thought to experience selective pressure for streamlined development which should favor small genomes that require less time for DNA replication [91, 92]. Consistent with this, we show that multivoltinism, i.e., the production of more than two generations per year (a trait highly correlated with eusocial behavior), is significantly correlated with reduced repeat content both before, and after accounting for phylogenetic relatedness among samples (Fig. 5). Consequently, small genome size could provide an adaptive advantage for species that need to produce new generations more rapidly, thereby immediately benefitting from faster rates of development. Conversely, the pace of development may not face strong selective pressures for species whose immatures remain underground for nearly a year or even multiple years [51, 93], as is the case for the majority of all bee species. Consistent with this, we find that greater repeat content and larger genome size is correlated with univoltine species (Fig. 5). Genome streamlining has been predicted to be influenced by a variety of evolutionary pressures and mechanisms among arthropods [94, 95]. Our findings that repetitive element abundance correlates with well-studied life history traits in bees suggests they have excellent potential to shed light on the interplay between evolutionary, ecological, and developmental forces shaping genome size.

Ongoing challenges to high-resolution TE studies in a biodiversity framework are due to the lack of well-curated TE libraries and limited taxonomic representation in TE databases [42, 96]. Despite Apis mellifera being a well-studied genomic model system, Apis genomes are characteristically composed of very few TEs, and consequently do not fully eliminate the barrier of TE classification in bees. In our study, we report the average bee genome containing 15.1% of unclassified repeats. Some of these unclassified elements are likely satellite DNAs which can be abundant in hymenopterans [97] and evolve so rapidly they are not expected to be conserved across broad phylogenetic scales. Others are likely TEs which become increasingly difficult to annotate and classify as putatively inactive insertions accumulate mutations. Though these challenges are somewhat reduced in bees compared to other insect lineages [42], they highlight the need for continued effort of curating repetitive DNA in non-model study systems. Despite these known annotation challenges, our analyses demonstrate detection of recurring patterns of repetitive element dynamics that further elucidate hypotheses that connect genome size evolution to well-studied ecological traits in bees.

As we forge further into this era of biodiversity genomics, we have the opportunity to explore genetic composition across an increasingly broader taxonomic range and at a progressively finer scale. Not only do we now better understand the regulatory and structural importance of TEs in the genome, but we are coming to appreciate how much these features contribute to genomic variation even among closely related taxa. This has vast implications for studying adaptation, speciation, and resilience, particularly as we recognize the activation of transposition to be a coordinated response to organismal stress. As genomic data accumulates at unprecedented rates, we must become more perceptive to the role of TEs to further discern patterns of their presence across the Tree of Life. To understand how organisms function and evolve, we must continue to look into what is often the largest component of their genetic constitution: the repeatome. The present study contributes yet another piece to the puzzle of understanding the role of transposable elements in genome evolution.

Conclusions

To shed light on a major component of genomic dark matter, our study provides insight into 125 million years of TE evolution across bees. We characterized the repeat landscapes of 75 bee species and found great variation in repeat abundance and diversity. As high-quality genomes become increasingly available, we will likely continue to recover variation across the richness of repeat evolution and encourage repeat library curation to further illuminate our understanding TE evolution. Among bees, TEs have accumulated gradually and in short spurts to generate the genomic variation largely responsible for differences in genome size. Many bee genomes contain conserved TEs as well as TE copies near protein-coding genes, signaling the potential TEs have in shaping the genetic composition of their host. This research opens the door for greater characterization of these features, which will be necessary for understanding their contributions to genomic diversity. This is particularly crucial during an era of biodiversity loss where adaptability to changing environments is critical.

Methods

Taxon sampling

We downloaded assemblies of 81 genomes from NCBI GenBank (last accessed 2024-v-08) using the ncbi-genome-download tool version 0.3.1 [98]. We only included assemblies that were generated using long-read sequencing technology to ensure balanced comparisons between species, due to the bias short-read sequencers have on accurate TE detection [42]. Six of the seven bee families are represented in this dataset, excluding Stenotritidae, a rare family of bees that is endemic to Australia. Our taxon sampling includes 41 species of apids, 10 megachilids, 14 andrenids, 7 halictids, 2 colletids, and 1 melittid. 24 different bee genera are represented by these 75 species, providing comparisons between closely related species within the same genus, as well as distantly related species across families. Our outgroup consists of 6 species of apoid wasps.

Phylogenomic methods

To ensure all interpretation and statistical analyses of our research on repetitive DNA is grounded in the evolutionary histories of the studied taxa, we constructed a genome-scale phylogeny using ultraconserved elements (UCEs). Analysis of these conserved genomic markers for phylogenetic reconstruction has become standard practice in the field of insect phylogenetics [99, 100], including for reconstructing bee phylogeny [53–55]. To this end, we used the Phyluce pipeline [52] to extract DNA that corresponds to UCEs of the Hymenoptera V2 bait set [101] from each individual genome, requiring an 80% overlap and identity. We aligned the sequences using MAFFT [102] and trimmed each aligned locus with Gblocks [103] following the parameters of Bossert et al. [104]. We filtered loci to discard alignments with less than 70% taxa completeness. We implemented a sliding window approach for partitioning UCE loci [105] and used CURE [55] and its UCERegion script to parallelize this process. We carried out a maximum likelihood (ML) analysis in IQ-Tree2 (v2.3.6) [56] and assessed branch support using 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap approximations (UFBoot2; [106]) and 1,000 Shimodaira-Hasegawa-like approximate likelihood ratio tests (SH-aLRT; [107]). ModelFinder [108] was implemented to merge partitions with similar substitution patterns (MFP + MERGE) while relaxing the hierarchical clustering algorithm to 20% (-rcluster 20). Interestingly, in a preliminary ML analysis, the sole representative of the family Melittidae, Macropis europaea, was recovered as sister lineage to the short tongue bees (Andrenidae, Halictidae, and Colletidae). However, recent phylogenomic work on bees with much more comprehensive sampling found Melittidae as sister group to all other bee families [57, 109], but also found that Melittidae can act as a rogue taxon in analyses using gene trees [57]. Given our sparse taxon sampling for Melittidae, the comparatively long branch of Macropis in the phylogeny (Additional File 2: Figure S1), and the history of Melittidae being potentially challenging to resolve, we repeated the IQ-Tree2 analysis with a constrained tree topology to place Macropis europaea as sister lineage to all other bees.

Repeat identification and annotation

We initially identified repeats de novo across genomes assemblies using RepeatModeler2.0 version 2.0.4 [110]. We used the ncbi search engine (-engine ncbi) and the LTRStruct parameter (-LTRStruct) to run the LTR structural discovery pipeline. We then annotated the repeats in two rounds using RepeatMasker version 4.1.4 [111]. The first round used the repeat library created de novo using RepeatModeler2 with the ncbi search engine and slow (-s) search settings for increased sensitivity. For the second round, we repeated the annotation on the masked genome assembly using RepeatMasker’s Arthropoda repeat library (-species Arthropoda) and output (.tbl) and alignment files (.align) were merged. To corroborate our findings, we also identified repeats de novo across genome assemblies using Earl Grey version 6.0.1 [112]. We used RepeatMasker’s Hymenoptera repeat library (-r Hymenoptera) and default parameters for the number of BLAST, Extract, Extend iterations (-i 10) and number of flanking base pairs to extract (-f 1000). High-level and family-level output statistics were merged for downstream analyses and visualization. Data visualization was performed using R version 4.1.0 and the 'ggplot2' R package [113].

TE divergence distribution landscapes

To infer relative ages of TE insertions, we constructed repeat landscapes using the calcDivergenceFromAlign.pl RepeatMasker utility as incorporated in the Earl Grey pipeline. We infer TE activity based on the number of mutations of each TE copy compared to a consensus sequence, with conserved TEs being more recently capable of transposition. TEs may become active during periods of organismal stress and these bursts of activation are then typically followed by an accumulation of mutations that silence transposition [9]. Consequently, transposons with a greater number of mutations are likely to have been active earlier in the evolutionary history of their hosts. However, it should be noted that conserved TEs may not just be young and/or active, but potentially conserved through positive selection [114]. This approach uses the alignment file (.align) output from RepeatMasker to calculate the Kimura two-parameter (K2P) distance of each TE insertion to its respective consensus sequence. K2P values were extracted from Earl Grey’s resulting general feature format (gff) file and then merged into categories of Penelope-like elements, LINEs, LTRs, DNA, and unclassified repeats. Landscape plots were generated manually in R using Earl Grey’s divergence_plot.R script.

Transposable element-related BUSCOs

We considered the relationship between TEs and protein-coding genes using methods developed by Heckenhauer and colleagues [70] and scripts adapted from Sproul and colleagues [42]. This method uses BUSCO genes as a proxy for detecting genome-wide TE-gene associations. We ran BUSCO version 5.4.6 [66] on each genome assembly using the Odb10 Hymenopteran BUSCO data set, consisting of 5,991 genes and the optimized Augustus self-training mode (-long) [115]. The BUSCO algorithm detects open reading frames from TEs that are embedded within or are immediately adjacent to BUSCO genes, extending the BUSCO gene boundary to include TE sequences. We used BLAST version 2.12.0 [116] to identify resulting BUSCO sequences that occur repetitively across the genome. BLAST hit counts were normalized as described in the supplemental materials of Sproul et al. [42]. We verified results for a subset of species by manually visualizing the overlap of detected TE-associated BUSCOs with repetitive DNA using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) [117]. Repeat annotation files were generated with the rm2gff3.sh script [118] and viewed in relation to BUSCO BLAST matches with the IGV browser for a subset of species.

Comparative analyses

To investigate the possible associations of repeat abundance and functional traits, we conducted a literature review to compile available information about bee host breadth, nesting biology, voltinism, body size, parasitism, and sociality for all species included in the study. References for functional traits can be found in the supplemental material (Additional File 3: Table S3).

All statistical analyses were performed using R [119]. Continuous trait data was log-transformed for all correlational analyses. We measured the correlation of traits using phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS) from the ‘nlme’ R package [120] under Brownian motion to account for phylogenetic relatedness. We generated an ultrametric tree using the chronos function from the ‘ape’ R package [60], supplying age ranges for 25 nodes for improved accuracy. These secondary calibrations were taken from a recent, comprehensive analysis of bee divergence times [57] and the specific values can be found in the supplementary material (Additional File 2: Table S2). For each PGLS, we report the slope to quantify the expected change in Y per unit X, after correcting for non-independence due to shared phylogenetic history. We also use the 'rr2' R package [121, 122] to provide partial R2pred values, representing the amount of variance explained by our models.

To determine whether repeat abundance was more similar for closely related species than for distantly related species, we performed continuous trait mapping and measured phylogenetic signal using the 'phytools' R package [123]. We used the phylosig function to measure both Pagel’s λ and Blomberg’s K. Blomberg’s K was run with 10,000 iterations and is expressed as a variation ratio. If K < 1 then traits are independent of the phylogeny, and if K > 1 then trait evolution is dependent of the phylogeny. Pagel’s λ ranges from 0 and 1, and λ = 0 indicates a random distribution of traits across the phylogeny while λ = 1 indicates a strong, nonrandom phylogenetic pattern.
