Predictors of Exercise Performance in Type 1 Diabetes: The Role of Hypoglycaemia Fear, Glycaemic Control, and Vascular Health: A Retrospective Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Arne Kooistra, Klaske van Kammen, Isabel Bekker, Peter R van Dijk, Helen L Lutgers, Rienk Dekker, André P van Beek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimCardiorespiratory fitness is often impaired in individuals with type 1 diabetes. While physiological determinants have been studied, psychological barriers such as fear of hypoglycaemia remain underexplored. We hypothesised that glycaemic factors including HbA1c, diabetes duration, the presence of microvascular complications, and fear of hypoglycaemia, would predict lower exercise performance.MethodsThis retrospective cross‐sectional single‐centre study included adults with type 1 diabetes who participated in a multidisciplinary rehabilitation program at the University Medical Centre Groningen. Outcomes were oxygen uptake, power output and heart rate at ventilatory thresholds and peak exercise. Predictors included age, sex, BMI, smoking history, diabetes duration, HbA1c, microvascular complications and fear of hypoglycaemia. Analyses comprised group comparisons and stepwise linear regression.ResultsThis study included 122 individuals with type 1 diabetes (median age 33 years; 76 females). Median HbA1c was 75 mmol/mol [IQR 63–93]. Hypoglycaemia fear survey data were available for 106 participants (median Worry score 23.5; Behaviour score 20.0). Higher HbA1c was negatively associated with VO2 at second ventilatory threshold (β = −0.085) and peak VO2 (β = −0.089), as well as power output and heart rate. Greater fear of hypoglycaemia predicted lower power output at second threshold (β = −21.95) and peak exercise (β = −28.97). Microvascular complications were linked to reduced heart rate at ventilatory thresholds, with stronger effects for multiple complications.ConclusionFear of hypoglycaemia, HbA1c and microvascular complications are key determinants of reduced cardiopulmonary exercise performance in type 1 diabetes, highlighting the need for integrated physiological and psychological interventions.Keywords: cohort study, database research, exercise intervention, type 1 diabetes

Introduction

Aerobic and resistance exercise are considered cornerstones in the management of type 1 diabetes and are widely recommended due to their broad physiological benefits [1, 2]. Among these, improving cardiorespiratory fitness is a key benefit and can be objectively assessed using cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET). CPET evaluates the integrated performance of the cardiovascular, pulmonary and muscular systems during exercise, with peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak) serving as a central metric and strong predictor of cardiovascular health [3]. In people without diabetes, higher VO2peak is strongly and independently associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular disease and all‐cause mortality [4, 5]. Benefits are also observed in individuals with type 1 diabetes, where higher VO2peak is linked to improved insulin sensitivity, body composition [6, 7] and reduced morbidity and mortality [7, 8]. Nevertheless, individuals with type 1 diabetes consistently exhibit lower VO2peak values of approximately 15%–30% compared to their non‐diabetic peers [9, 10]. The underlying mechanisms of this reduced exercise capacity are partly known and appear to be multifactorial. Prior studies have identified central cardiac impairments [11, 12], muscle limitations such as mitochondrial dysfunction and diminished microvascular muscle perfusion [13, 14, 15], inefficient substrate utilization [11, 12, 16] and compromised respiratory function [12, 17] as contributing factors. Additionally, fear of hypoglycaemia can reduce exercise participation and thereby may negative influence the VO2peak [18, 19]. Despite its relevance, this behavioural factor has not been examined in relation to VO2peak. Furthermore, exercise capacity it not solely defined by VO2peak, it also encompasses submaximal performance and physiological thresholds. CPET evaluates these aspects by determining the first (VT1) and second ventilatory thresholds (VT2), as well as peak responses for heart rate, power output and oxygen uptake (VO2). These measures provide a detailed assessment of exercise tolerance and are clinically relevant for individualizing training intensity, as VT1, for example, is a robust marker of functional capacity and is commonly used for training zone prescription and risk stratification [20, 21, 22]. A comprehensive understanding of both maximal and submaximal physiological markers, together with behavioural contributors, is therefore critical for optimizing exercise recommendations in type 1 diabetes.

Previous research has identified total daily insulin dose, diabetes duration and C‐peptide levels as predictors of CPET performance [10]. However, these findings were derived from a cohort with very short diabetes duration, limiting generalisability. Higher HbA1c has also been linked to lower VO2peak, highlighting physiological determinants of fitness [13]. Yet, fear of hypoglycaemia remain largely unexplored.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine hypoglycaemia fear, glycaemic, lifestyle and demographic predictors of CPET outcomes, specifically oxygen uptake, power output and heart rate at VT1, VT2 and peak exercise, in individuals with type 1 diabetes. A secondary aim was to evaluate differences in these variables between individuals with high fear of hypoglycaemia and those with low fear of hypoglycaemia. Ultimately, this knowledge may inform more personalised strategies to optimise cardiorespiratory fitness and long‐term health outcomes.

Methods

This study, conducted in 2025, reports in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) [23].

Design

This study is a retrospective cross‐sectional single‐centre study.

Setting

Data originally collected for healthcare purposes were used. Data from participants who participated in a diabetes rehabilitation program at the Centre for Rehabilitation of the University Medical Centre Groningen were collected. In the Dutch healthcare system, referral to such a programme occurs when participants fail to meet glycaemic targets or experience persistent psychosocial complains despite usual care. The program is a 14‐week multidisciplinary intervention combining medical, psychosocial and lifestyle treatments, including insulin management education, cognitive‐behavioural therapy and structured physical activity. Eligibility for the diabetes rehabilitation program required adults with type 1 and suboptimal glycaemic control, HbA1c > 58 mmol/mol and/or significant diabetes related psychosocial issues despite prior optimal care; exclusions were active substance addiction, except nicotine, and psychiatric conditions interfering with participation. For each participant, data were collected from the start of their rehabilitation program. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Central Ethical Committee of the University Medical Centre Groningen (approval number 22167). Informed consent was not required, as the study involved only temporary care contacts and did not include ongoing medical involvement.

Within the University Medical Centre Groningen patients can indicate their objection of the re‐use of clinical data for research purposes. As alternative to the informed consent, the objection registry was checked to verify if a patient objected prior to inclusion in the study.

Participants

This retrospective study included all individuals who participated in a diabetes rehabilitation program and underwent a CPET on a stationary bicycle at the start of their diabetes rehabilitation program between 2018 and 2025. To be eligible for inclusion in the current study, participants had a documented diagnosis of type 1 diabetes, underwent a CPET before the rehabilitation program, and had a recorded HbA1c value before or after 1 month of the CPET. Individuals with documented macrovascular complications were excluded from the analysis, because the observed reductions in heart rate and power output among this subgroups may still reflect underlying cardiac impairments or the use of medications such as beta‐blockers, which are known to affect exercise capacity [3]. Participants were classified as having achieved maximal effort during CPET if they met at least one of the following internationally accepted criteria, a respiratory exchange ratio ≥ 1.10 or more than 85% of their age‐predicted maximum heart rate [3].

CPET Measurements

CPET was performed during clinical care using a stationary cycle ergometer (Ergoselect 200, Ergoline GmbH, Bitz, Germany) and a ramp protocol with incremental workloads ranging from 5 to 25 watts per minute depending on the participant. The workload increment was selected based on a brief clinical anamnesis between the participant and the CPET administrator, considering self‐reported physical activity, perceived exercise tolerance and typical occupational and leisure activities, this assessment was qualitative and not based on predefined numerical criteria, and the rationale was not systematically documented. Breath‐by‐breath gas exchange analysis was conducted using the Vyntus CPX system (CPX Professional, Vyaire; currently Jaeger Medical GmbH, Hoechberg, Germany), with data averaged over every eight breaths. Heart rate was continuously monitored via electrocardiography (ECG; Cardiosoft 6, GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI, USA).

All CPET data were extracted from Sentrysuite 4 software (Jaeger Medical GmbH, Hoechberg, Germany). VO2peak was defined as the highest recorded oxygen uptake during the test and expressed relative to body weight (mL/min/kg) to reflect functional capacity. VT1 and VT2 were determined according to the V‐slope method and were determined by a single assessor due to the clinical nature of the procedure [24, 25]. To ensure participant safety, CPET was only conducted when baseline blood glucose levels were within the range of 4.0 to 20.0 mmol/L. Although international guidelines recommend a pre‐exercise range of 7–10 mmol/L and advise ketone testing when glucose exceeds 13.9 mmol/L, the wide range used in this study reflects routine clinical practice in the Netherlands, where ketone meters are not routinely reimbursed or widely available [1]. Ketone testing is therefore typically performed only when clinically indicated, and applying stricter cut‐offs would have markedly reduced the feasibility of conducting CPET in this rehabilitation setting. All tests were conducted under direct medical supervision. For participants using a continuous glucose monitor, these values were obtained directly from their sensor, whereas individuals relying on capillary blood glucose monitoring were asked to perform a finger‐prick measurement prior to testing.

Variables

For the descriptive characteristics, data on insulin therapy method (continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion or multiple daily insulin injections) were collected to provide transparency regarding potential implications for generalizability. The primary outcomes of this study were derived from CPET. These included oxygen uptake (VO2), power output (Watt) and heart rate (bpm) measured at three key time points, VT1, VT2 and peak exercise performance. Independent variables comprised anthropometric measures such as age, sex and BMI. Lifestyle variables included smoking status and cumulative lifetime smoking exposure, expressed as pack‐years. Pack‐years were modelled for all participants as a continuous measure, including former smokers. Current smoking status was included separately as a dichotomous variable to distinguish cumulative effects from the potential acute effects of active smoking. Furthermore, to account for clinical characteristics specific to type 1 diabetes, diabetes‐related variables were incorporated, including diabetes duration, HbA1c levels measured from venous blood samples and the presence of microvascular complications (nephropathy, retinopathy and/or neuropathy). The number of microvascular complications was recorded and categorised as none, one, two, or three. Information on microvascular complications was obtained from electronic health records. Only the presence of nephropathy, retinopathy and/or neuropathy was documented, the severity of these complications was not recorded. Fear of hypoglycaemia was assessed using the hypoglycaemia fear survey (HFS), a validated instrument that measures fear of hypoglycaemia [26, 27]. Both the Behaviour and Worry subscales were included independent from each other. Because no validated threshold exists for HFS scores, participants were dichotomised into high and low fear groups based on the median score for each subscale. Additionally, item 10 from the Behaviour subscale, ‘assessing exercise avoidance due to fear of hypoglycaemia’, was analysed separately as a categorical variable. The questionnaire was administered digitally and in Dutch [28]. As with all other variables, HFS data were originally collected for routine clinical care.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM Corporation). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non‐normally distributed variables are reported as median with interquartile range [IQR].

The primary objective of the statistical analysis was to identify predictors of CPET outcomes, specifically oxygen uptake, power output and heart rate at VT1, VT2 and peak exercise. To address this aim, relationships between independent and dependent variables were first examined visually using estimation plots to confirm approximate linearity for continuous predictors. Subsequently, initial associations were explored using Pearson correlation for continuous independent variables with normal distribution, Spearman rank correlation for continuous independent variables with non‐normal distribution, independent samples t‐tests for binary categorical variables with normal distribution, one‐way ANOVA for multi‐category variables with normal distribution, and Kruskal–Wallis tests for non‐normally distributed data. Independent variables showing an association with dependent variables at p < 0.20 were retained for regression modelling.

Manual backward stepwise linear regression was then performed for each CPET outcome, with variables removed stepwise based on statistical significance (p > 0.05) at each repetition. In total, nine models were constructed: VO2, power output, and heart rate at VT1, VT2 and peak exercise. For VO2 outcomes, models were adjusted for BMI and, when significantly correlated, also for age and sex. For power output outcomes, BMI, age and sex were included only if significantly associated with the dependent variable. The number of microvascular complications (0, 1, 2 or 3) was included as a categorical variable, with each level compared to the reference group without complications. Model diagnostics included assessments for multicollinearity and homoscedasticity, and independence of residuals was evaluated using the Durbin–Watson statistic.

Secondary Analyses

Secondary analyses were conducted. First, because HFS was completed only by a subgroup of participants, potential selection bias was evaluated by comparing demographic and clinical characteristics between this subgroup and the full cohort using appropriate parametric or non‐parametric tests. Second, differences in characteristics, anthropometric measures and CPET outcomes between individuals with low versus high fear of hypoglycaemia were examined to address the secondary aim. Data from both the Worry and Behaviour subscales were compared using independent samples t‐tests for normally distributed continuous variables, Mann–Whitney U tests for non‐normally distributed variables and Chi‐square tests for categorical variables.

To complement the median‐split analyses, we additionally examined associations between continuous HFS scores and CPET outcomes. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the continuous Worry and Behaviour subscales of the HFS and all CPET variables.

Results

Participants

This retrospective cross‐sectional study included data of 157 participants completing the CPET at the start of the diabetes rehabilitation program. No participants were excluded on the basis of an objection recorded in the registry. Of these 157 participants, 27 participants did not meet the criteria for maximal effort. Eight participants were excluded based on macrovascular complications in their medical history, resulting in a total cohort of 122 participants. A subset of 106 participants had data available from the HFS, and this group was used to examine whether fear of hypoglycaemia influenced CPET outcomes. Apart from the HFS data, all other variables were complete. The remaining independent variables were analysed using data from the full group of 122 participants who achieved maximal effort. Discussion

This study is, to our knowledge, the first to examine fear of hypoglycaemia, glycaemic, lifestyle and demographic predictors associated with CPET outcomes, specifically oxygen uptake, power output and heart rate at VT1, VT2 and peak exercise in individuals with type 1 diabetes.

Our findings reveal that diabetes‐specific factors, rather than general demographic characteristics, are primarily associated with exercise performance. When other predictors were accounted for, HbA1c and severe microvascular burden were associated with substantially lower VO2peak. Fear of hypoglycaemia, particularly worry scores, influenced power output at higher intensities, independent of glycaemic control and other clinical variables. Heart rate responses were consistently lower in participants with microvascular complications when all included predictors were accounted for. In this study, microvascular damage was a stronger predictor for heart rate limitations than VO2 responses at different stages of CPET. These findings highlight that psychological, glycaemic and vascular factors have stage‐specific effects on exercise performance.

Our finding that HbA1c is inversely associated with VO2peak is consistent with previous literature [18, 29]. While chronic hyperglycaemia is known to impair endothelial function and contribute to vascular dysfunction [29], we did not observe a significant association between one or two microvascular complications and VO2peak in our cohort. This indicates that the negative impact of elevated HbA1c on the VO2peak may be mediated by other physiological alterations. Possible explanations for the association between higher HbA1c and lower VO2peak, independent of microvascular damage, include mitochondrial dysfunction. As described by Goulding et al., no differences in mitochondrial function were found between individuals with type 1 diabetes and those without, provided they had similar VO2peak values [30]. However, their study reported increased mitochondrial impairment in individuals with higher HbA1c levels, suggesting a glycaemic‐dependent effect on mitochondrial efficiency. Another contributing factor that influences the VO2peak by a higher HbA1c could be a blunted heart rate to performance curve [10, 31]. Such impairment may reduce the heart rate's ability to respond adequately to increasing metabolic demands, thereby limiting exercise capacity. Additionally, elevated HbA1c may increase the likelihood of hyperglycaemia at the onset of CPET. Acute hyperglycaemia could induce a shift in primary fuel metabolism, favouring glucose over fat oxidation, which may negatively affect VO2peak [32]. However, because baseline glucose values were not consistently available for all participants, the extent to which acute glycaemic status influenced CPET performance cannot be fully determined, representing a limitation of this study.

Taken together, these findings suggest that HbA1c may serve as a broader marker of metabolic and cardiovascular inefficiency, influencing oxygen delivery and utilization even in the absence of microvascular complications. However, despite the lack of a significant association between microvascular complications and VO2peak, we observed that microvascular damage, when other predictors were held constant, was more associated with reduced power output and especially with the heart rate at the different thresholds. Building on this, we also explored predictors of exercise performance at submaximal stages of the CPET, including VT1 and VT2. These thresholds are clinically relevant because they reflect submaximal exercise capacity, which is crucial for activities of daily living, and are commonly used for exercise prescription and risk stratification [20, 21]. Despite this importance, there is, to our knowledge, no literature examining predictors of VT1 in individuals with type 1 diabetes. In our analysis, gender and the presence of microvascular complications were consistent predictors of VO2 at VT1, while HbA1c additionally emerged as a predictor when expressed relative to power output. For heart rate at VT1, age and the presence of one or more microvascular complications were associated with greater limitations. These findings suggest that microvascular damage may influence submaximal exercise capacity, which is particularly relevant for activities of daily living. Although Tagougui et al. focused on maximal exercise responses, their observations of impaired oxygen utilisation and altered muscle blood flow regulation in poorly controlled patients highlight underlying mechanisms that may also explain our findings at submaximal thresholds [15]. Together, this suggests that microvascular dysfunction could affect exercise performance across different stages of effort, from daily activities to maximal exertion.

Beyond physiological mechanisms, we also explored whether fear of hypoglycaemia might influence exercise performance. To better understand the role of fear of hypoglycaemia, we intentionally dichotomised HFS scores using median splits, as no validated clinical cut‐off exists to distinguish high from low fear. This categorical approach allowed us to examine group differences reflecting the presence versus absence of elevated fear. However, complementary analyses using the full continuous score range confirmed that fear of hypoglycaemia has limited relevance for maximal exercise capacity. In light of these findings, we next considered how hypoglycaemia‐related anxiety has been positioned within the broader literature on exercise behaviour and performance.

As far as we are aware, this is the first study to examine the potential association between hypoglycaemia‐related anxiety and CPET variables, based on the hypothesis that fear of hypoglycaemia is frequently cited as a barrier to exercise participation among individuals with type 1 diabetes [19]. In our analysis, we observed only an association between scores on the HFS, specifically the worry subscale, and power output during CPET. The observed relationship between hypoglycaemia fear and CPET outcomes may be explained by behavioural pathways rather than physiological mechanisms alone. Fear of hypoglycaemia is well documented as a barrier to exercise participation in type 1 diabetes, which can lead to avoidance of physical activity and lower overall fitness. This behavioural pattern may translate into voluntary limitation of effort during maximal testing, particularly at higher intensities, where the perceived risk of hypoglycaemia increases. Such anticipatory behaviour could explain the association with reduced power output observed in our study. Building on these behavioural considerations, it is important to note that evidence directly linking fear of hypoglycaemia to CPET‐derived exercise capacity is lacking. Given this gap, we explored daily physical activity in relation to HFS. This approach aimed to examine whether psychological barriers to everyday movement could also translate into reduced performance under controlled conditions. Previous research has shown mixed results regarding the relationship between hypoglycaemia‐related anxiety and physical activity levels. Some studies have reported a paradoxical correlation, where individuals with higher fear of hypoglycaemia also report higher levels of daily activity [33]. This apparent paradox needs further investigation into how psychological factors interact with physiological responses during exercise, and whether fear‐related behaviours influence maximal performance differently than general activity patterns. Finally, our findings regarding the associations of age, BMI and gender with CPET parameters align with established evidence, whereas the relatively minor influence of smoking history and pack‐years contrasts with their stronger impact reported in the general literature [21, 34]. These factors are well‐established determinants of exercise capacity and cardiovascular health, and their observed relationships in our cohort further support their relevance in the context of type 1 diabetes.

A key strength of this study is the inclusion of fear of hypoglycaemia variables, as potential determinants of exercise capacity. Another strength is the use of CPET outcomes in a relatively large cohort of individuals with type 1 diabetes. CPET provides objective and comprehensive measures of cardiorespiratory fitness, and according to the American College of Sports Medicine, it represents the most accurate approach for tailoring exercise intensity in clinical populations such as type 1 diabetes [20].

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross‐sectional design does not allow for causal inference, and unmeasured confounders such as habitual physical activity, c‐peptide levels, body composition, the heart rate to performance curve and baseline glucose levels at the CPET may have influenced the results [32]. Moreover, although HbA1c provides an estimate of average glycaemia, it was obtained either 1 month before or 1 month after the CPET, which limits its precision as an indicator of glycaemic status at the time of testing.

Furthermore, the median age of 33 years indicates a relatively young cohort, which should be considered when interpreting the findings. Additionally, the data used in this study were derived from individuals who participated in a specialised diabetes rehabilitation programme, a group that likely represents individuals with suboptimal glycaemic control or psychosocial challenges. This context may limit generalisability to the broader type 1 diabetes population, particularly those who are well‐controlled or highly physically active. Another limitation of this study is that impaired awareness of hypoglycaemia was not assessed in the dataset. Impaired awareness of hypoglycaemia occurs in approximately 20% of adults with type 1 diabetes and is associated with increased fear of hypoglycaemia, higher scores on the HFS, and a greater risk of severe hypoglycaemic events [35]. Factors such as longer diabetes duration, older age, recurrent hypoglycaemia and psychological characteristics are known to contribute to impaired awareness of hypoglycaemia. While fear of hypoglycaemia was a key variable in our analysis, it may partly reflect underlying impaired awareness of hypoglycaemia in some participants. However, the primary aim of this study was to examine whether higher fear of hypoglycaemia, regardless of its origin, was associated with differences in CPET outcomes. Future research should consider including measures of impaired awareness of hypoglycaemia to disentangle its specific contribution to exercise performance. Furthermore, the use of clinical data in this study comes with inherent limitations, such as missing values for certain variables. In particular, information on medication use, which could influence exercise responses, was not available beyond insulin treatment. Data on drugs affecting heart rate, such as beta‐blockers, would have been valuable to include. Although this gap could not be fully addressed, we attempted to mitigate its impact by excluding individuals with macrovascular complications, thereby reducing the likelihood of beta‐blocker use in the cohort. Additionally, detailed information on the nature and severity of microvascular complications was not available. Complications were operationalised based on their presence and cumulative burden, which does not allow differentiation between early and advanced pathology. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings and underscore the need for future studies to include detailed complication severity and autonomic function measures.

In addition to these considerations, future research should also incorporate objective measures of physical activity, glycaemic status at CPET initiation, glucose dynamics during the test, a more detailed assessment of diabetes‐related complications including hypoglycaemia unawareness, and detailed metabolic profiling to better clarify the mechanisms underlying reduced VO2peak in individuals with type 1 diabetes. Although psychological assessments are often considered relevant, our findings showed no association between hypoglycaemia fear variables and VO2peak, but revealed significant associations between fear of hypoglycaemia, HFS worry subscale and power output at peak exercise. This suggests that psychological factors play a role in specific aspects of exercise performance and should not be disregarded in future research.

Additionally, future studies could explore which psychological and glycaemic variables are associated with improvements in VO2peak following exercise interventions, highlighting the need for longitudinal research designs.

Conclusion

This study highlights the multifactorial nature of exercise performance in individuals with type 1 diabetes, with microvascular complications, along with glycaemic, demographic and hypoglycaemia fear factors all associated with variation in CPET outcomes. Variables such as age, sex, HbA1c, smoking history and the presence of microvascular complications were consistently associated with reduced performance across multiple domains. While increased fear of hypoglycaemia was not linked to VO2peak, it was associated with reduced power output, suggesting a role in exercise performance. These findings underscore the importance of an integrated approach to understanding and improving cardiorespiratory fitness in this population. Future studies should include detailed assessments of hypoglycaemia unawareness, acute glycaemic control and the severity of microvascular complications to better clarify the mechanisms underlying reduced exercise capacity.
