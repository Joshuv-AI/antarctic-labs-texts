Macroheterocycle-functionalized PVDF meshes with hybrid nanocomposite coatings for antibacterial wound care
Authors: Aygun Israyilova, Alibala Aliyev, Alakbar Huseynzada, Ulviyya Hasanova, Eldar Gasimov, Fuad Rzayev, Sevil Yucel, Farah Mehtiyeva, Gulnara Abdullayeva, Lala Huseynova, Nushaba Aliyeva, Vagif Abbasov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We herein report the preparation of a novel wound healing material by functionalizing polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) meshes with a hybrid nanocomposite system consisting of a 40-membered macroheterocycle (MHC), Dashsalahli bentonite nanoparticles (BN), silver nanoparticles (AgNP), and propolis (P), which were chosen according to their complementary roles: MHC serves as an antibacterial scaffold, BN serve as a stabilizer and release controller agent, AgNP serve as a potent bactericidal agent and P serves as a natural bioactive product. The functionalization and stability of the designed novel hybrid nanocomposite systems were evaluated using SEM, EDAX, TEM, and zeta potential analysis. Antibacterial activity was evaluated against various strains of S. aureus using MIC, agar diffusion and time-kill kinetic assays, which revealed a potent bactericidal effect within 8 hours of treatment with the MHC@BN@AgNP nanocomposite in the case of S. aureus ATCC 6538, with an MIC value of 0.25 µg mL−1. TEM analysis revealed that the sample caused bacterial cell wall disruption and intracellular degradation. In addition, hemolysis assays showed low hemolytic activity for most hybrid nanocomposites, and ex vivo porcine skin decontamination assays revealed an effective reduction in S. aureus 1199B strain colonization under relevant biological conditions. The obtained results suggest that the designed nanocomposite coated with PVDF meshes is a potential candidate for advanced wound care applications targeting S. aureus infections.

Introduction

Skin and soft tissue infections remain a major clinical challenge in the world because of the increasing antimicrobial resistance among pathogenic bacteria. Among the pathogenic bacteria, Staphylococcus aureus plays a major role in wound-associated infections. These bacteria can form biofilms, which provide them with strong adhesion capacity and resistance to different antibiotic classes.1,2 It is already known that S. aureus isolates rapidly acquire resistance through many mechanisms, such as enzymatic drug degradation, target site alteration, efflux pumps, and biofilm formation, which cause traditional antibiotics to become ineffective.3–5 The rise in multidrug-resistant bacteria poses a significant threat to public health and healthcare environments.6–8 Recently, the development and study of multifunctional wound dressing materials with antibacterial activity have become an actual and significant priority in the field of biomaterials research.9–11

Traditional wound dressings create a physiological barrier and regulate moisture around the wound, but they lack sufficient antibacterial properties. For this reason, in modern science, nanotechnology-based approaches are widely applied to overcome these limitations. In some studies, the incorporation of metal nanoparticles, clay nanomaterials and bioactive organic molecules into polymeric matrices has shown promising antibacterial activity.12

Macroheterocycles (MHCs), a diverse category of macrocyclic compounds containing heteroatoms, are among the most promising frameworks for antimicrobial research. Robust and precise interactions with biomolecular targets, including bacterial enzymes and membranes, are facilitated by their rigid, three-dimensional structures.13–15 Reports indicate that macrocycles with notable antibacterial and antifungal characteristics, which include calixarenes, pillararenes, porphyrins, and crown ethers, either directly inhibit pathogens or function as carriers that enhance antibiotic absorption.16–18 Some recent studies have shown that macroheterocyclic systems are able to mimic siderophores, cause iron chelation and enhance penetration across bacterial membranes to overcome prevalent resistance mechanisms.19–21 Moreover, the structural versatility of macroheterocycles allows for their functionalization with hydrophobic substituents or ionic groups, hence improving their solubility and antibacterial effectiveness.22–24 Despite these advantages, their stability, bioavailability, and tendency to aggregate in physiological media sometimes limit their practical application, highlighting the necessity for composite systems to overcome these challenges.25

Bentonite is a natural clay mineral predominantly made of montmorillonite and has been studied as a carrier material due to its high cation-exchange capacity, layered structure, substantial surface area, and the advantage of biocompatibility.26,27 Bentonite has attracted researchers' attention in drug loading systems because of its nanoscale adsorption and excellent dispersibility functions.28,29 Recent studies have shown that bentonite-based nanocomposites not only stabilize structural activity but also regulate drug release by improving their antibacterial features.30–35 Bentonite-based nano platforms incorporated with levofloxacin, silver nanoparticles (AgNP), and propolis (P) have demonstrated promising antibacterial activity, supporting their potential for use in wound healing and infection prevention.36,37

Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) meshes are frequently applied in biomedical applications, especially in wound treatment, due to their chemical stability, mechanical strength and electroactive properties. The fibrous mesh structure in PVDF provides a high surface area that is suitable for surface modification and immobilization of nanocomposites/hybrids.38

Taking into account the above-mentioned factors, this study introduces a novel approach that combines a MHC with Dashsalahli bentonite nanoparticles, AgNP, and P to develop multifunctional PVDF-based nanocomposites. The mentioned alterations were selected based on the idea that these components can demonstrate improved efficacy within the overall system: MHC serves as an antibacterial scaffold, BN function as a dispersive and stabilizing support, AgNP serve as a potent bactericidal agent, and P is a bioactive natural substance that promotes wound healing.

The efficacy of the modifications and physicochemical stability were studied through scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDAX), and zeta potential analysis of the resultant composites. We develop a diffusion-based model to simulate MHC release from MHC@BN and its transport in the surrounding medium. The simulation results suggest that bentonite can be a release-regulating reservoir supporting sustained MHC availability over time. The antibacterial effect of the designed macroheterocycle-functionalized PVDF meshes coated with a hybrid nanocomposite was evaluated against S. aureus ATCC 6538 and NorA efflux pump overexpressing strains S. aureus 1199 and 1199B. Antibacterial activity was determined using the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), agar diffusion and time-kill kinetic assays to investigate both inhibitory and bactericidal effects. Along with this, an ex vivo skin model for bacterial decontamination and hemolysis assays was used. In order to study the ultrastructural changes in bacterial cells after treatment with the nanocomposite, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis was performed. The present work aims to contribute to the development of antibacterial wound materials by combining organic molecules with nanoparticles on stable polymeric meshes, which have enhanced efficacy against S. aureus strains.

Experimental

Materials and reagents

The primary material for this study was Dashsalahli bentonite, sourced from AzRosPromInvest. Anfarm Hellas S. A. provided us with levofloxacin. All the solvents and other reagents were purchased from commercial suppliers, were of analytical grade, and were used without further purification.

Synthesis of BN

Due to its structural and compositional appropriateness for biomedical applications, Dashsalahli bentonite, a naturally occurring clay mineral abundant in montmorillonite, was utilized as the foundational material. Approximately 75% of the bentonite consists of montmorillonite (Mt), 13% comprises quartz (Q), and roughly 10% includes calcite (Cal) and iron impurities.30 A rigorous multi-step purification method was employed to produce BN, aimed at eliminating impurities and enhancing the material's physicochemical properties for further applications.

To disaggregate the clay particles, 10 g of raw bentonite was initially combined with 400 mL of deionized water and physically agitated for one hour at 800 rpm. The suspension was transferred to a sedimentation vessel and allowed to settle undisturbed for 12 hours to isolate the finer clay particles from the coarser contaminants. The clay fraction was included in the supernatant, which was subsequently decanted and vacuum filtered to eliminate any residual fine clay particles. The refined dispersion underwent magnetic separation to remove iron-containing contaminants. This process was conducted iteratively using a magnetic bullet until all the contaminants were eliminated. The sample was subsequently centrifuged at 4000 rpm to consolidate the clay fraction, and residual moisture was eliminated by drying in a laboratory oven at 100 °C for 24 hours.30

Synthesis of AgNP

We employed a green synthesis approach to produce AgNP, utilizing starch as both a stabilizing and reducing agent.35 Initially, 25 mg of starch was dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water combined with 30 mg of silver nitrate, which was dissolved in an additional 50 mL of deionized water. A 25% ammonia solution was gradually introduced to facilitate the reduction process until the pH reached around 11. The reaction mixture was thereafter placed in a bath and subjected to ultrasonic agitation for one hour. This ensured thorough mixing and expedited the formation of AgNP. The reaction was conducted under continuous cooling to prevent an excessive temperature increase. Upon completion of the reaction, the brown colloidal solution was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for five minutes to isolate the produced AgNP. The precipitate was rinsed three times with deionized water to eliminate any residual ammonium nitrate present in the solution. The pure AgNP were ultimately dried at ambient temperature under a nitrogen stream. This maintained their stability and prevented oxidation.

Synthesis of the MHC

The synthesis of the 40-membered MHC (6,7,15,16,18,19,21,22,30,31,39,40,42,43,45,46-hexadecahydrotetrabenzo[c1,i,i1,o][1,4,11,14,21,24,31,34]octaoxa[7,18,27,38]tetraazacyclotetra-contine) was carried out based on our previous work.39 The NMR data agree with those reported in the literature.39

Preparation of nanostructures and mesh surface modification with the obtained hybrid nanostructures

Preparation of MHC@BN@AgNP nanostructures for surface modification of PVDF meshes

For the preparation of the nanocomposite coating, 1 mg of BN was dispersed in 10 mL of DMSO and ultrasonicated for 15 minutes with cooling for uniform dispersion. 1 µg of the MHC was dissolved in 1 mL of DMSO and gradually added to the bentonite suspension. A solution containing 3 µg of AgNP was added to the bentonite solution, and the mixture was stirred at 150 rpm for 12 hours at room temperature using a magnetic stirrer for better adhesion of components to the surface of BN.

PVDF meshes were functionalized with MHC@BN@AgNP using the dip coating technique due to its ability to uniformly cover the mesh surface. To increase the mesh fiber-nanocomposite solution interaction, immersion was performed under typical room conditions. The parameters were meticulously tuned to ensure uniformity and consistency during the dip-coating process.

After immersion, the coated meshes were extracted from the mixture at a consistent rate to facilitate uniform distribution of the nanocomposite material across the mesh surface, hence reducing the likelihood of clumps and abnormalities. Subsequently, the coated samples were dried using a mild flow of nitrogen gas, which facilitated the removal of moisture and enhanced the adhesion of the nanostructures to the polyvinylidene fluoride substrate. The nanocomposite and mesh polymeric fibers adhered better after drying, resulting in a durable covering.

Preparation of MHC@BN@P nanostructures for surface modification of PVDF meshes

First, conjugation of the MHC with BN was performed according to the previous procedure (Subheading 2.5.1). Further, we employed the previously discussed dip-coating technique to modify the surface of the PVDF meshes. PVDF mesh samples (1 × 1 cm) were immersed in the MHC@BN@P suspension for 30 min to allow for the adsorption of the nanostructures onto the fiber surface. After immersion, the samples were maintained in the suspension for an additional 5 min to ensure coating equilibration and then withdrawn at a controlled rate of approximately 1 mm s−1 to obtain a uniform coating layer. After the initial coating, 1 mL of a 4.62% ethanol solution of P was included in the suspension. This enabled the incorporation of bioactive chemicals into the nanostructured coating. Following the coating process, the meshes were subjected to a nitrogen gas stream to facilitate solvent evaporation, resulting in a stable adhesive coating.

Preparation of MHC@AgNP@P nanostructures for surface modification of PVDF meshes

MHC@AgNP@P nanostructures were synthesized by mixing 1 µg of the MHC and 3 µg of AgNP in 10 mL of DMSO. The solution was agitated for 12 hours at ambient temperature using a magnetic stirrer operating at 150 rpm. This ensured that the components were uniformly blended and engaged with one another to form stable nanostructures. A dip-coating technique was employed to modify the surfaces of the PVDF meshes. We immersed 1 × 1 cm polyvinylidene fluoride mesh samples in the prepared MHC@AgNP suspension to ensure uniform coating. Controlled immersion and extraction were employed once more to ensure the uniform placement of the nanostructures on the polymer substrate. After the coating process, 1 mL of a 4.62% ethanol solution of P was poured into the suspension to add the bioactive ingredient into the coating. The obtained coated meshes were dried in a nitrogen gas stream to eliminate residual solvents.

Preparation of MHC@BN@AgNP@P nanostructures for surface modification of PVDF meshes

First, conjugation of the MHC with BN and AgNP was performed according to a previously described procedure. The previously described dip-coating process was employed to modify the surface of the PVDF meshes. PVDF meshes with a 1 × 1 cm size were immersed in the MHC@BN@AgNP@P suspension for surface coating. After the coating process, 1 mL of a 4.62% ethanol solution of P was poured into the suspension to add the bioactive ingredient into the coating. The obtained coated meshes were dried in a nitrogen gas stream to eliminate residual solvents.

UV-vis spectroscopy

The loading efficiency of the MHC and other composite systems on PVDF meshes was quantitatively investigated using UV-vis spectroscopy. After the preparation of the coating suspension, the meshes were immersed in the corresponding solution (deep coating process) to allow adsorption and deposition processes to occur. After modification, the meshes were removed, and the supernatant solutions were collected for UV-vis analyses. The initial concentration of the active compound before the loading process was designated as C0, while the concentration remaining in the supernatant after equilibrium was designated as Ce. The loading efficiency (LE %) was calculated according to the following equation:LE (%) = [(C0 − Ce)/C0] × 100.

C 0 is known for coating solutions, Ce is detected using UV-vis, and C0 − Ce shows the loading concentration. Adsorption mechanisms were analyzed using Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm models, while adsorption kinetics were evaluated using pseudo-first-order (PFO) and pseudo-second-order (PSO) models. The release behaviour was investigated using the Higuchi and Korsmeyer–Peppas models to evaluate the diffusion- and interaction-controlled release mechanisms.

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are reported as mean ± standard deviation.

FTIR spectroscopy

FTIR spectra were recorded using a Bruker Alpha FTIR spectrometer with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory on a zinc-selenide crystal at room temperature in the wave number range of 400–4000 cm−1.

TGA analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis was conducted using a “NETZSCH Synchronous Thermal Analyzer (STA) 449 F3 Jupiter” under dynamic conditions. The measurements were carried out in the range of 25–650 °C at a heating rate of 20 °C min−1 under N2 flow. A corundum crucible assay was used for the measurements. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed under N2 flow using a PerkinElmer Differential Scanning Calorimeter DSC 4000.

Quantification of silver content by ICP-OES

The silver content in Ag-containing composite systems was quantified using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES). After deep-coating, the supernatants were collected and analyzed to determine the Ag content.

The amount of Ag in the initial deep-coating solutions was 3 µg. The amount of loaded Ag was calculated as follows:Agloaded = Aginitial − Agremaining.

TEM analysis

The surface morphology and structural characteristics of the samples were examined using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) JEM-1400 (JEOL, Japan) at 80–120 kV. All the samples were prepared using a drop-dry method on carbon-coated copper grids (CF150-Cu-50 Carbon Film 150 Mesh, Electron Microscopy Science, USA).

SEM analysis

The surface morphology and structural characteristics of the samples were examined using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) (TESCAN MIRA3 field emission microscope, operating at an accelerating voltage of 8.0–10.0 kV). Before imaging, all samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold (Au) to enhance their conductivity and improve the image resolution.

The elemental composition was investigated by Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDAX), coupled to the SEM system, using a silicon drift detector (SDD) at 10.0 kV accelerating voltage and 30 seconds of live acquisition time per spot. The analysis was performed on multiple regions (area 1, spots 1–3) to ensure the representativeness of the elemental distribution. Quantification was carried out using the eZAF Smart Quant model, which corrects for atomic number, absorption, and fluorescence effects.

Zeta potential analysis

The clay and nanocomposite's zeta potential (ζ) was measured in an aqueous solution with a concentration of 0.05% w/v using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 apparatus (Malvern Panalytical, USA).

Mechanistic modelling of MHC release and bacterial uptake

To describe the release of the MHC from MHC@BN, its transport in the surrounding aqueous medium, and subsequent uptake by bacterial cells, a diffusion-based mechanistic model was constructed.40 The model was implemented using a finite-element framework and aimed to capture time-dependent release, spatial diffusion, and membrane-limited transport phenomena.

The computational domain consisted of three coupled regions: (a) the MHC@BN reservoir representing the bentonite carrier surface, (b) the extracellular aqueous medium, and (c) the bacterial compartment separated from the medium by a semipermeable membrane. To resolve the concentration gradients toward the bacterial membrane, both one-dimensional (1D) penetration models and two-dimensional (2D) diffusion geometries were employed.

The transport of the MHC in the extracellular medium and within the bacterial cytoplasm is described by Fick's second law:where C is the concentration of the MHC, and D is the effective diffusion coefficient of the domain. The diffusion coefficients were selected based on the literature values for small organic molecules in aqueous and cytoplasmic environments and adjusted to ensure numerical stability.

Release kinetics from MHC@BN

The liberation of the MHC from MHC@BN was conceptualized as a time-dependent flux boundary condition at the interface between the carrier and the medium. This denotes the release regulated by desorption from the layered silicate structures. The initial MHC loading was assigned to the BN domain, and the release rate was constrained to prevent simultaneous release, as observed in trials using continuous release profiles.

Parameters of diffusion and membrane transport

The diffusion coefficient of the MHC in the extracellular aqueous media was established as Daq = 4.5 × 10−10 m2 s−1, consistent with documented results for tiny aromatic and heterocyclic organic compounds of similar molecular weight in water. This range has been extensively utilized for antibiotic-like and bioactive small compounds in diffusion-transport modelling investigations, ensuring realistic mass transport without exaggerating molecular mobility. Diffusion in the bacterial cytoplasm was simulated using a diminished diffusion coefficient Dcyto = 1.0 × 10−10 m2 s−1, considering macromolecular crowding and elevated intracellular viscosity compared to aquatic settings. Reduction factors for small-molecule transport within bacterial cells are well-documented and have been empirically proven using fluorescence recovery and tracer investigations. Transport across the bacterial membrane was established as a permeability-limited boundary condition with an effective membrane permeability of Pm = 1.0 × 10−7 m s−1, indicating the passive diffusion of moderately lipophilic small compounds through Gram-negative bacterial membranes. This methodology elucidates the membrane's barrier function without presuming active transport and has been previously utilized in models of antibiotic absorption and small-molecule penetration.

All diffusion and permeability parameters remained fixed during the simulation to isolate the mechanistic effects of prolonged release and membrane resistance while maintaining numerical stability and mass conservation.41–44

Initial and boundary conditions

At time t = 0, MHC was exclusively restricted to the MHC@BN domain. No-flux conditions were employed at the external boundaries to maintain a constant mass. At all internal interfaces, except for the bacterial membrane, where permeability restrictions were established, the continuity of concentration and flux was mandated.

Simulation parameters and time scales

Time-variant simulations were conducted over 24 hours to replicate the experimental antibacterial and release assessments. Concentration patterns, cumulative release fractions, and intracellular MHC accumulation were measured over time. Spatial concentration maps were created to illustrate the mechanisms of diffusion gradients and membrane penetration.

Antibacterial activity screening

Initially, the minimum inhibitory concentration for the prepared nanosuspensions was determined using a two-fold microdilution assay on S.aureus ATCC 6538, S. aureus 1199 and S. aureus 1199B in accordance with CLSI guidelines M07-Ed12.45–47 The bacterial strains used in the microbiological studies were obtained from the culture collection of Baku State University (Azerbaijan), and overnight-grown cultures in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) were used in the experiments. The nano suspensions were prepared as described above, and the samples were diluted in 96-well microtiter plates in cation-adjusted Muller–Hinton broth (CAMHB) (“Liofilchem’’). The concentrations of the samples ranged from 256 to 0.125 µg mL−1. After inoculation of the bacterial strains into each well, the plates were incubated at 37 °C. The turbidity of the test cultures was standardized to 0.5 McFarland (1.5 × 106 CFU mL−1) using a digital densitometer. The plate reader X-mark (Bio-Rad) was used to read the experimental plates before (0 h) and after incubation (18 h). The growth inhibition percentage was calculated relative to the vehicle control (DMSO). The MIC was determined as the lowest concentration with ≥95% inhibition. After incubation, in order to confirm turbidity results, 30 µL of resazurin dye (0, 01%) (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each well, and microplates were again incubated for 3–4 hours. The change in color from blue to pink was considered bacterial growth. The tests were repeated in triplicate with three technical replicates per treatment on different days.

The antibacterial activity of the functionalized PVDF mesh was evaluated against Staphylococcus aureus 1199, Staphylococcus aureus 1199B, and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 6538 using the agar diffusion method, as described previously.31,48,49 The agar diffusion assay was performed to study the antibacterial effects of PVDF mesh combined with BN, AgNP, MHC and P. Bacterial strains were cultivated in Tryptic Soy Broth (“Liofilchem”), and freshly grown overnight cultures were used for each experiment. The PVDF meshes (10 × 10 ± 0.8 mm) with nanocomposites were placed on Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA) plates (“Liofilchem”), which had already been incubated with 100 µL of 0.5 McFarland standardized bacterial suspensions. The plates were kept for 20 minutes at room temperature, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 hours. The control plates contained only bacteria without functionalized meshes. All assays were repeated in triplicate, and the inhibition zones surrounding the meshes were measured using a ruler scale. Statistical analysis and graphical representation of the results were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.6.2. Software (USA).

Time kill assay

A time-killing assay was performed in order to determine the killing activity for the potent active combination – MHC@BN@AgNP.46,49–51 Briefly, different multiples of 0.5, 1 and 2 × MIC of samples were used to determine the effect on the growth of the S. aureus ATCC 6538 strain, depending on time. The exponential bacterial cultures were inoculated in MHB at a cell density of 5 × 105 CFU mL−1. Non-treated cells were used as a control for bacterial growth. The samples were incubated by shaking at 37 °C (150 rpm). Bacterial counts were measured at seven points (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 24 h) by spreading 10-fold serial dilutions on MHA plates. Serial dilutions (in triplicate) were grown overnight to determine the number of colonies in the colony-forming units (CFU mL−1). The experiments were repeated in triplicate, and the mean values were calculated. Time kill curves were generated by plotting the logarithm (log10) of CFU mL−1 against time.45,52 Bactericidal activity was considered the reduction ≥99.9% in the total number of colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU mL−1) relative to the initial inoculum.

Preparation of bacterial samples for TEM study

In order to study the ultrastructure changes in the bacterial cells after treatment with the nano suspensions, samples for the transmission electron microscope were prepared, as described in previous studies.46,53,54 The S. aureus bacterial cells from the mid-log phase were treated with BN and MHC@BN at 1 µg mL−1 and incubated for 8 hours at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm. The samples were grown in 100 mL conical flasks to obtain the required cell mass for TEM examination. The cell pellets were obtained after centrifugation of the samples at 5000 rpm for 5 min. The cell pellets were fixed with the phosphate buffer (pH = 7.4), which contained 2% paraformaldehyde, 2% glutaraldehyde, 0.1% picric acid and 1% caffeine. Then, the samples were fixed in 1% osmic tetroxide solution for 1.5 h, and Araldite-Epon blocks were prepared according to the standard accepted methods for electron microscopy. EM UG7 (Leica, Germany) ultramicrotome was used to prepare semithin sections (1–2 µm). Semithin sections were stained with methylene blue, azure II and fuchsin for investigation using a light microscope (Primo Star, Zeiss, Germany) and photographed with a digital camera (EOS D650, Canon, Japan). Ultrathin sections (50–60 nm) were double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The stained ultrathin sections were examined under a transmission electron microscope.

Hemolysis assay

Hemolytic analyses were carried out with minor modifications, as reported previously.55–58 Briefly, human blood was collected from a healthy, non-smoking volunteer in a sodium citrate-supplemented tube. The collected blood samples were diluted in a DPBS solution under sterile conditions. Distilled water was used as the positive control, which induced erythrocyte lysis, whereas DPBS was applied as a negative control to represent the baseline of the hemolysis process. The test samples were incubated at 37 °C for 60 minutes for thermal equilibration. Subsequently, 1 mL of the diluted blood suspension was gently added to each tube and re-incubated for an additional 60 minutes under static conditions. The samples were centrifuged after incubation to separate intact erythrocytes from the supernatant. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 577 nm using a UV-visible microplate reader (Xmark, Bio-Rad). The percentage of hemolysis was calculated according to the following equation: hemolysis ratio (%) = (OD(test) − OD(negative control))/(OD(positive control) − OD(negative control)) × 100%. Each experiment was performed in triplicate for each sample to confirm its reproducibility, and the standard deviation was calculated to measure the amount of variation in hemolytic percentage for each sample.

Ex vivo skin model for bacteria decontamination

The ex vivo pig skin decontamination model was performed according to the protocol established by Fenton et al., with slight modifications.59,60 Briefly, commercially purchased pig skin was carefully shaved with sterile scissors and cut into 2.5 × 2.5 cm explants. Further, the skin samples were cleaned with 70% isopropyl alcohol, dried at room temperature for 30 min and kept under UV light for 10 min. Then, sterile explants were transferred to Petri plates (60 mm) containing 5 mL physiological saline solution (0.9% NaCl, pH 5.5) supplemented with 0.5% agar in order to simulate physiological skin conditions and maintain tissue hydration. Each explant was then inoculated with 105 CFU mL−1 of S. aureus 1199B strain diluted in PBS. The bacterial suspension was spread on the surface of the explants and allowed to adhere for 30 min at room temperature. Then, 200 µL of the hybrid systems was sprayed onto the contaminated skin surfaces. The explants inoculated with bacterial suspension but not treated with the test samples were considered the positive control. In addition, non-inoculated explants were used to verify sterility and the absence of contamination. The skin samples were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Following incubation, bacterial cells were taken from the skin surface with a cotton swab, and serial dilutions were performed. Colony-forming units were determined by plating on TSA plates and incubating for 18 h at 37 °C. The experiment was performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

All experiments related to antibacterial studies were repeated in triplicate, and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: ns (non-significant); ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. The theoretical and experimental data of the adsorption process were illustrated using TableCurve 2D. The Zeta-Sizers graphs were prepared using OriginPro 9.0 software.

Results and discussion

Solid-state characterization of nanostructures

Characterization of bentonite using transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to investigate the morphology of the obtained BN. The TEM image in Fig. 1 illustrates the presence of thin, sheet-like structures characteristic of layered silicate clays. The shown particle possesses an essentially rectangular form with lateral dimensions potentially reaching several hundred nanometers. The thickness, which is within the nanometer scale, proves that bentonite layers have exfoliated and formed nanosheets.

TEM image of bentonite.

The dark spherical contrast around the bentonite nanosheets is probably caused by the agglomerated bentonite particles and the variation in the thickness of the layered clay structure. Bentonite-treated nanorods have an increased surface-to-volume ratio and elongated lamellar morphology. This provides a substantial interfacial surface suitable for further functionalization and interaction with biological or chemical surroundings. The sheet-like morphology proves that these nanoparticles may function effectively as carriers in nanocomposite systems. Their ultrathin layered architecture facilitates ion exchange and the intercalation of active substances. Their nanometric size distribution facilitates uniform integration into polymeric or biological matrices. The structural features, revealed by TEM, highlight the importance of further investigations on BN, which are able to find applications in various areas, such as drug administration, wound healing, and antimicrobial nanocomposites.

Characterization of BN and MHC@BN nanostructures using a zeta-sizer technique

The Z-average hydrodynamic diameter of the unaltered BN was around 213 nm, with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.278, indicating a reasonably narrow size distribution. The examination of the size distribution revealed distinct groups with primary maxima at 177 nm and 52 nm. This indicates that bentonite possesses a layered and partially exfoliated structure when suspended in water. The zeta potential of the unbound BN (Fig. 2) was recorded at −30.4 mV, signifying a pronounced negative surface charge characteristic of aluminosilicate clays, hence imparting electrostatic stability to the colloidal solution. BN functionalized with MHC exhibited markedly distinct physicochemical characteristics.

Zeta potential distributions of (a) BN and (b) MHC@BN.

The Z-average hydrodynamic diameter increased markedly to around 28.61 nm, accompanied by a PDI of 1.0, indicating particle aggregation and a broad size distribution. The emergence of larger particle populations (up to 2248 nm) suggests that the integration of MHC enhanced interactions among particles perhaps due to π–π stacking or electrostatic bridging between bentonite platelets and MHC molecules. Bentonite modified with MHC reached a likely neutral zeta potential (−2.0 mV), showing a sharp reduction in surface charge. The reduction in zeta potential proves that the MHC has effectively modified the bentonite surface, resulting in decreased stability of the colloidal dispersion and an increased propensity for aggregation. Overall, these findings indicate that pristine BN exhibits significant colloidal stability due to its markedly negative zeta potential. Conversely, altering bentonite nanoparticles with MHC modifies their surface charge characteristics and hydrodynamic dimensions, hence promoting aggregation.

Characterization of nanostructures using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and elemental analyses

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed (Fig. 3) to examine the surface morphology of the PVDF meshes before and after modification. The pristine PVDF mesh exhibited a smooth, uniform fiber surface devoid of any visible imperfections or irregularities, indicating its structural integrity and unchanged condition (Fig. 3a).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of (a) pristine PVDF mesh, (b) PVDF/MHC, and (c) PVDF/MHC@BN.

Upon the addition of the MHC, the surface roughness of the fibers exhibited a minor increase, accompanied by the emergence of subtly distributed characteristics, signifying the successful deposition of the organic modifier (Fig. 3b). The alterations in shape indicate that the MHC and the PVDF substrate engage with one another on the surface without compromising the fibrous network.

The PVDF mesh exhibited a significantly rougher and more irregular surface when functionalized with both BN and MHC (Fig. 3c). The existence of particle aggregates and irregular coatings indicates that BN has been absorbed and immobilized on the fiber surface, with the MHC matrix maintaining its stability.

The PVDF/MHC@P sample had a relatively smoother surface with selective spots at the edges and deposits. This indicates that the P layer was only partially covered by the MHC (Fig. 4a). These deposits demonstrate efficient adherence to bioactive chemicals without much agglomeration.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of (a) PVDF/MHC@P, (b) PVDF/MHC@AgNP, and (c) PVDF/MHC@BN@AgNP.

The PVDF/MHC@AgNP mesh exhibited more pronounced surface imperfections and granular characteristics distributed across the fiber surfaces (Fig. 4b). The addition of AgNP led to the formation of a non-uniform coating due to their nucleation and immobilization on the polymer substrate. This is believed to impart antibacterial qualities to the coating.

The PVDF/MHC@BN@AgNP composite exhibited a markedly rough and textured surface, characterized by numerous tiny particles (Fig. 4c). BN, together with AgNP, formed dense, irregular coatings with strong immobilization and integration.

The PVDF/MHC@BN@P mesh exhibited a less uniform surface, characterized by larger and more irregular deposits of bentonite integrated within the propolis-MHC matrix (Fig. 5a). The clay particles were distributed along the fiber outlines, resulting in a rougher texture. This indicates that the inorganic component was highly immobilized.

The PVDF/MHC@AgNP@P sample exhibited relatively smooth fibers and uniformly distributed tiny particles (Fig. 5b). The amalgamation of silver and propolis, stabilized by MHC, produced a uniform, thin layer that adhered firmly to the fiber surface without aggregating into big clumps.

In the PVDF/MHC@BN@AgNP@P composite, a substantial and continuous layer of aggregated particles nearly enveloped the fibers entirely (Fig. 5c). The most notable alteration in shape transpired in the presence of BN, AgNP, and P. The dense coating nearly concealed the underlying PVDF structure.

After the addition of MHC, oxygen (4.7 wt%) and nitrogen (1 wt%) peaks appeared, proving the attachment of heteroatom-containing organic moieties on the surface.

Characterization of samples using FTIR and TGA

The obtained samples MHC, MHC@BN, MHC@AgNP, MHC@P, MHC@BN@AgNP, MHC@BN@P, MHC@AgNP@P, and MHC@BN@AgNP@P were also analyzed by FTIR (Fig. S2–S9, SI material) and TGA (Fig. S10S–S17, SI material) methods. As can be observed (Fig. S2, SI material), the FTIR spectrum of MHC shows a characteristic band of the imine bond at 1680 cm−1, which is also confirmed by NMR (8.58 ppm).39 In the case of ensembles, the shifting of the signals is observed, which is caused by the noncovalent interaction between MHC, P, BN and AgNP.

Along with all performed analyses, TGA was also performed to investigate the thermal behaviour of the samples. As shown in Fig. S10 (SI material), a considerable mass loss of 91.06% occurs in the temperature range of 100–200 °C, which is caused by bond cleavage and decomposition of the MHC molecule. Further, small-scale mass loss is observed, and the residual 2.49% is coke. Comparison of the curves of other samples ’with the curve of the MHC demonstrates that significant mass loss also occurs until 200 °C, which is caused by the presence of MHC in all the samples. However, in the case of samples containing P, the mass loss, along with the decomposition of MHC, is also caused by the release of volatile/low molecular weight/organic compounds in the above-mentioned temperature range; in the case of samples containing bentonite, cleavage of hydroxyl groups was observed.

Diffusion-based mechanistic model of MHC release and bacterial uptake

A diffusion-based mechanistic model was developed to describe the release of MHC from MHC@BN, its transport in the surrounding medium, and subsequent accumulation within bacterial cells. Moreover, time-dependent release, spatial diffusion, and membrane-limited uptake processes were integrated into the model.

As shown in Fig. S18 (SI material), the release of MHC from MHC@BN proceeds gradually, with a continuous decrease in the MHC@BN fraction and a corresponding increase in the concentration of the MHC over time. After 24 h, ∼60% of MHC was released, while a significant fraction remained on BN, indicating sustained, non-burst release behavior characteristic of layered silicate carriers.

The intercellular accumulation profile (Fig. S19, SI material) shows a delayed but progressive increase in MHC concentration within bacterial cells, reaching ∼0.55–0.60 µg at 24 h. This indicates that membrane transport, rather than release, constitutes the rate-limiting step for intercellular delivery.

Spatial concentration profiles also support this interpretation. The 1D penetration simulation (Fig. 6) shows a concentration drop across the bacterial membrane, confirming its barrier role, while sustained release from BN maintains a driving concentration gradient that enables gradual cytoplasm entry.

Spatial transport of the MHC illustrated by one-dimensional penetration profiles and two-dimensional diffusion maps showing time-dependent concentration gradients toward the bacterial membrane.

Overall, the model shows that BN function as a release-regulating reservoir, ensuring persistent extracellular availability of the MHC and facilitating time-dependent membrane penetration. These findings provide a mechanistic framework that rationalizes the observed sustained antibacterial activity and complements the experimental release kinetics and PK/PD analyses.

Adsorption behavior and isotherm analysis

Depending on the composition, there is a significant difference in loading between the PVDF-based composite systems. As shown in Fig. S20–S23 (SI material), all systems exhibit significant uptake with loading capacities between 1.6 and 3.2 microgram mL−1. The highest loading was observed in MHC@BN@AgNP@P, indicating a synergetic role of bentonite. This can be attributed to the increased surface area of the BN, and the system lacking bentonite showed comparatively lower loading.

The adsorption behavior in various composite systems was evaluated using Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm models. The Langmuir model (Fig. S24, SI material) showed linearity for all samples correlation coefficients (R2) ranging from 0.9976 to 0.9998 (Table S1, SI material), indicating that adsorption mainly occurs as a single-layer coating on relatively uniform surfaces. This indicates the presence of well-defined active sites, especially in bentonite-containing systems.

The Freundlich model (Fig. S25, SI material) also showed good agreement (R2 = 0.9954–0.9992; Table S2, SI material), confirming the presence of surface heterogeneity and multilayer adsorption behavior. The coexistence of Langmuir and Freundlich properties suggests that adsorption is driven by a combined mechanism involving both homogeneous adsorption sites and energetically inhomogeneous regions.

The fact that the composite systems containing BN and AgNP exhibited slightly lower R2 values compared to the original systems is quite normal, reflecting the increased structural complexity and diversity of interactions. This supports the hypothesis that adsorption is not purely physical but involves multiple interaction mechanisms, including electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and possible coordination interactions.61,62

Releasing kinetics and mechanism

The release behavior of MHC from the composite systems was analyzed using several kinetic models to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Higuchi model

The Higuchi model (Fig. S26, SI material) showed high linearity (R2 = 0.9879–0.9968; Table S3, SI material), indicating that the release process is mainly determined by diffusion-controlled mechanisms. The higher R2 values observed in the bentonite-containing systems indicate that the porous structure of the clay enhances the diffusion pathways and simultaneously modulates the release rate.

Korsmeyer–Peppas model

The Korsmeyer–Peppas model (Fig. S27, SI material) showed excellent fit (R2 = 0.9976–0.9995; Table S4, SI material) for all systems, confirming that the release process follows non-Fickian (anomalous) transport behavior. This indicates that the release is not driven by simple diffusion alone but by a combination of diffusion and matrix*drug interactions.

Weibull model

The Weibull model (Fig. S28, SI material) showed the highest correlation coefficients (R2 = 0.9985–0.9997; Table S5, SI material) and demonstrated excellent ability to describe the overall release behavior. This excellent fit confirms that the release process is multi-mechanistic and involves an initial rapid release phase followed by a sustained release phase driven by structural and interaction-based constraints.

Based on the combined analysis of adsorption isotherms and release kinetics, the interaction between MHC and composite systems can be described as a complex multi-step process:

➢ Initial adsorption occurs through surface interactions, including electrostatic attraction and chemisorption.

➢ The structural heterogeneity created by the BN and AgNP leads to the heterogeneity of the binding sites. Release is controlled by diffusion through the composite matrix coupled with interaction-driven desorption.

➢ The overall behavior of the system is best described as non-Fickian and multi-mechanistic, as supported by the Weibull model.

These results indicate that compounds are not simply physically precipitated but are mainly tightly bound to the composite matrix and provide a controlled and sustained release.63–65

Silver incorporation and interaction

The loading of the AgNP into the composite system was analyzed using ICP-OES. The results clearly demonstrate that the loading of the AgNP strongly depends on the composition of the mixture.66

The loading efficiency for systems with only AgNP was 54.67%, while systems with MHC but no BN had slightly lower values (about 50–51%) (Fig. S29, SI material). This means that AgNP and the polymer matrix did not interact very well.

The systems with bentonite, on the other hand, had much higher silver incorporation, with loading efficiencies of 70.33% (BN@AgNP), 74.90% (MHC@BN@AgNP), and 79.33% (MHC@BN@AgNP@P). This significant increase shows how important bentonite is for improving silver retention. The better loading can be explained by the fact that bentonite has a layered structure, a large surface area, and a cation exchange capacity, which gives AgNP more active sites to stick to and stabilize them. The further increase observed in the multicomponent systems shows that bentonite and organic components work together to trap silver more effectively in the composite matrix. These findings offer quantitative validation of the effective integration of AgNP and affirm the existence of robust interfacial interactions within the composite systems. The markedly enhanced retention of silver in the bentonite-containing formulations correlates with the improved controlled release demonstrated in kinetic studies, thereby reinforcing the functional role of bentonite as a carrier and stabilizing matrix.

Antibacterial activity of the designed nanocomposites

Initially, a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for the MHC and its hybrid nanocomposites was determined against S. aureus ATCC 6538 and the NorA efflux pump-overexpressing strains S. aureus 1199 and S. aureus 1199B using a two-fold microdilution assay. In previous works, it was reported that BN alone do not significantly affect bacterial cells.28,29 As summarized in Table 2, the MHC showed relatively weak activity against the tested strains. The MIC value was 32 µg mL−1 in the case of S. aureus ATCC 6538 and 64 µg mL−1 when tested with the S. aureus 1199 and 1199B strains. Similarly, P alone demonstrated moderate effects against reference strain S. aureus ATCC 6538, while weak susceptibility was detected against resistant strains. In contrast, the functionalization with propolis (MHC@P) moderately affected the activity, and a two-fold reduction was observed in MIC values across all strains (Fig. 7). Along with this, an enhancement in the antibacterial potency was observed when the MHC was incorporated with the silver nanoparticles (MHC@AgNP). Compared with MHC results alone, MIC values ranged from 1 to 2 µg mL−1 against strains, which indicated a 32–64-fold improvement in the antibacterial activity of the hybrid system.

The MIC and agar diffusion assays demonstrated that bentonite-containing hybrid nanostructures showed improved antibacterial activity.

Time-kill and growth kinetics analysis of MHC@BN@AgNP

The time-kill assay was performed to investigate the antibacterial kinetics of the MHC@BN@AgNP nanosystems against S. aureus at different concentrations of the MIC (0.5× MIC, MIC and 2× MIC) for 24 h. The growth of the bacteria was studied by optical density measurements (OD600) (Fig. 8A) and by colony counts expressed as log10 CFU mL−1 (Fig. 8B).45,46,49–52 Normal exponential growth of S. aureus was exhibited in the untreated control, and the cell number steadily increased during the incubation period. At 0.5× MIC, the nanocomposite affected bacterial growth moderately, and compared with the control, a decrease was observed in CFU counts. A notable suppression in bacterial growth was exhibited in the treatment of the sample at the MIC level, and the results suggested the sustainable inhibition of bacterial cells. The rapid and significant reduction in bacterial growth was observed at the 2× MIC level of MHC@BN@AgNP. After 8 h of treatment, a decrease of more than 3 log10 CFU mL−1 was observed, and according to the CLSI criteria, the results suggested bactericidal activity. The killing effect remained for up to 24 h; no bacterial growth was observed. In conclusion, the obtained results highlighted MHC@BN@AgNP as a potent bactericidal nanocomposite against S. aureus.

Study of ultrastructure changes in S. aureus induced by MHC@BN

The structural changes in S. aureus ATCC 6538 (SA) cells in the case of MHC@BN were studied using transmission electron microscopy (TEM).46,53,54 Initially, analysis in the light microscope confirmed that the samples were properly prepared for TEM, and because of low magnification (1000×), it was difficult to differentiate treated and non-treated cells. The results obtained using MHC@BN were compared with those obtained using non-treated and BN-treated cells. The untreated control cells demonstrated typical S. aureus morphology, a well-organized cell wall, homogenous cytoplasmic content, and a regular spherical shape (Fig. 9A and B). No structural modifications, such as membrane disruption, cytoplasmic condensation or structural abnormalities, were observed. A similar effect was detected in the cells treated with BN alone (Fig. 9C and D). Therefore, the results indicated that BN did not cause any morphological damage (disruption in the cell envelope and cytoplasmic material) in the tested bacterial cells.

Ultrastructural analysis of Staphylococcus aureus cells: (A and B) non-treated and (C and D) BN-treated.

In contrast, major ultrastructural alterations were observed in S. aureus cells when treated with the MHC@BN. These changes include damage and disruption of the cell wall and loss of structural context, as indicated by arrows (Fig. 10). Moreover, different intracellular alterations were detected, such as cytoplasmic leakages, formation of dense aggregates and degeneration of internal structures highlighted by snowflake symbols. Overall, these pathological modifications may support enhanced antibacterial effects of the MHC@BN hybrid nanocomposite.

TEM images of S. aureus cells after treatment with the MHC@BN nanohybrid system. The damaged cells are indicated by arrows, while the disruption in cytoplasmic structures is marked by asterisks.

Among all the tested samples, the BN- and AgNP-containing hybrid nanosystems exhibited strong antibacterial activity against bacterial strains. The obtained results indicate enhanced antibacterial effects of BN and AgNP on bacterial growth inhibition. It is already known that AgNP possess bactericidal effects through different mechanisms, such as disruption of cell membrane permeability, generation of reactive oxygen species and interference with the DNA replication process in the cell.67,68 On the other hand, BN have a high surface area and support electrostatic interaction with the bacterial envelope, which is negatively charged.33,69,70 These features of the BN enhance the sustained exposure of bioactive compounds against bacterial cells. For this reason, superior antibacterial activity was detected for MHC@BN@AgNP, which can be attributed to the enhanced interaction between AgNP and BN.

As shown in Table 2, the activity of the AgNP- and BN-based nanocomposites slightly decreased by adding P to the hybrid systems. However, P alone possesses antimicrobial and antioxidant features;71,72 the complex polyphenolic composition may cause an antagonistic effect in the hybrid systems with nanomaterials. P is rich in polyphenols, such as flavonoids and phenolic acids, which demonstrate interaction with metal ions through chelation or coordination bonding. Polyphenols are able to bind to metal nanoparticles because of their hydroxyl groups and aromatic rings, which can coat or cap the nanoparticles by changing the surface charge and reactivity.73,74 According to the literature, one plausible explanation for this effect is that components of the P, as capping agents, may generate an organic layer around the AgNP by limiting their direct contact with the bacterial cell. In addition, this interaction may reduce ROS formation, which leads to a moderate decrease in the activity of the nanosystems.

A similar effect was observed in bentonite-based hybrid systems. Bentonite is widely used for the adsorption of polyphenols and phenolic compounds because of its high surface area and charge.75 This is another explanation that polyphenols, which dominate constituents in the P, adsorb on the bentonite surfaces and modify the surface charge properties. These surface changes may affect the reduction of electrostatic interactions and direct contact with bacterial cells.

Hemolysis analysis of the nanocomposites

The hemolysis properties of the functionalized PVDF meshes were investigated, and the obtained results were compared with those of the positive control (distilled water). The results showed clear differences in blood compatibility between the tested samples (Fig. 11). The positive control demonstrated complete hemolysis (∼100%), while the negative control hemolysis rate was below 1%, which indicates erythrocyte stability. From the results, it seems that among the samples, AgNP showed the highest hemolytic activity (∼25%), which indicated that free Ag nanoparticles may cause damage to the erythrocyte membrane.76–79 As reported previously, this effect can be associated with nanoparticle membrane interaction and a possible oxidative stress-related mechanism. In contrast, BN, P and MHC alone exhibited low hemolysis (<5%). The samples containing AgNP without protective modifications, MHC@AgNP and MHC@AgNP@P showed a lower hemolysis rate compared with the AgNP alone. This reduction can be attributed to the incorporation of AgNP into a hybrid composition, which decreases the direct connection of the nanoparticle with the cell membrane. The bentonite-containing samples exhibited minimal hemolysis, and the rate ranged between 1% and 5% depending on the nanocomposites. According to the ISO 10993-4 guidelines, materials exhibiting hemolysis below 5% are considered hemocompatible.80,81 The exact mechanism that highlighted the reduced hemolytic activity was not investigated in the present study, but the obtained results indicated improved blood cell compatibility of the designed nanocomposites. Further, mechanism investigations, such as oxidative stress and membrane integrity analysis, are important for understanding nanostructure-blood cell interactions.

Ex vivo skin model assay for bacterial decontamination

The ex vivo assay was performed in order to investigate the bacterial decontamination efficacy of the hybrid nanostructures against S. aureus 1199B. The infected explants were treated with the MIC concentrations of the tested nanocomposites for 24 h. The untreated control reached the highest bacterial load, which confirms the successful bacterial colonization on the skin explants (Fig. 12). BN alone did not show any antibacterial activity, whereas AgNP demonstrated promising bacterial decontamination. The BN@AgNP hybrid system produced a stronger effect than AgNP. This result suggests that the bentonite matrix may enhance the stability of the nanoparticle and the interaction of the bacterial surface. The incorporation of P into the AgNP and BN-containing hybrid systems resulted in a partial reduction in the antibacterial efficacy, as observed with MHC@AgNP@P, MHC@BN@P and MHC@BN@AgNP@P (Fig. 12A). As mentioned above, this effect can be correlated with surface capping or a reduction in the availability of active antibacterial components caused by P interactions with the hybrid nanostructures. The strongest antibacterial effect was observed in the case of MHC@BN@AgNP, which reduced the bacterial colonization to approximately 2 log10 CFU mL−1, indicating the pronounced decontamination activity of the sample against the NorA efflux pump-overexpressing S. aureus 1199B strain (Fig. 12B). The hemolysis and ex vivo assays results provide preliminary data, which further can support the improved biological compatibility and practical antibacterial activity of the hybrid nanostructures for the development of wound care related materials.

Conclusion

In this study, PVDF meshes were functionalized with a hybrid nanocomposite system consisting of MHC, BN, AgNP and P. Structural and physicochemical analyses confirmed effective surface modification and stable binding of the nanocomponents, resulting in the creation of a multifunctional platform on the polymeric substrate. Microbiological assays revealed that bentonite- and silver-containing hybrid nanocomposites showed notably enhanced antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 6538 and NorA efflux pump overexpressing strains. Among the designed nanostructures, the MHC@BN@AgNP sample exhibited the most promising bactericidal activity, with a substantial reduction in bacterial growth, low MIC values and extensive inhibition zones. Ultrastructural modifications, including disruption of the bacterial cell wall and intracellular degradation, were revealed using TEM analyses. Moreover, P is considered a bioactive substrate; its incorporation moderately decreases the antibacterial effect of the AgNP- and BN-based systems because of its polyphenol-mediated surface interactions and nanoparticle capping effects. The hemolysis and ex vivo assays provide preliminary data supporting the biological compatibility and antibacterial potency of the designed hybrid nanostructures. In conclusion, the interaction of MHC with BN and AgNP on PVDF resulted in enhanced antibacterial activity against S. aureus infections, which can represent a potential candidate for future antimicrobial and wound-related applications.
