MOF-based fluorescent molecularly imprinted polymer nanosensor for the determination of aflatoxin M1 in water and cow milk with exceptional sensitivity
Authors: Osman Gamal, Ahmed S. Abo Dena, Shaimaa A. Khalid, Tanvir Ahmad, Yang Liu, Ibrahim M. El-Sherbiny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Contamination of food and feed with fungal aflatoxins is a major problem. Even extremely minute amounts of aflatoxins can be fatal to humans and animals if ingested with food or feed. Therefore, presenting analytical methods for the detection/determination of aflatoxins (e.g., aflatoxin M1, AFM1) is highly required for food contamination control. Here, we report a novel, highly sensitive fluorometric nanosensor for the rapid determination of AFM1 in water and cow milk. The proposed nanosensor is based on a fluorescent molecularly imprinted polymer (FMIP) comprising a molecularly imprinted polymer coating onto a chitosan-coated fluorescent metal organic framework (MOF). The FMIP was fabricated from a newly synthesized functional monomer, which was then conjugated with chitosan and used to coat the fluorescent MOF prior to its polymerization. The synthesized materials, including functional monomer, MOF, and FMIP were characterized using FTIR, 1H-NMR, DSC, XRD, dynamic light scattering, zeta potential measurement, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging. The analytical performance of the nanosensor was investigated and the incubation time was found to be 20 min, indicating the rapid analytical procedure. The nanosensor exhibited a limit of detection of 2.89 pg L−1 and a limit of quantification of 9.63 pg L−1, supporting the ultra-high sensitivity of the nanosensor. In addition, the fluorescence vs. concentration relationship is linear (r2 = 0.9945) over a wide concentration range of 3.3–63.3 pg L−1. Furthermore, the obtained recovery values are in the range 95.76–105.41% with a standard deviation of 1.72–2.65, indicating the high accuracy and precision of the nanosensor. Therefore, the FMIP-based nanosensor can be successfully applied to the determination of AFM1 in profoundly dilute water and cow milk samples.

Introduction

Mycotoxins have serious potential negative impacts on both animal and human health. They are secondary metabolites secreted by some fungi such as Aspergillus spp., Penicillium spp., and Fusarium spp. Some of the most dangerous and most abundant groups of natural fungal toxins are aflatoxins (AFs). These toxins are produced by Aspergillus parasiticus, Aspergillus nominus, and Aspergillus flavus.1 AFs have immunosuppressive, hepatotoxic and carcinogenic effects, and are found in many agricultural products such as cotton seeds, wheat, corn, and corn gluten. The most hazardous type of AFs is aflatoxin B1 (AFB1), which can exert severe toxic effects on animals. Moreover, the metabolites of AFB1 are extremely dangerous, and these can contaminate feed. After being consumed by animals, the metabolism of AFB1 produces aflatoxin M1 (AFM1), which is then secreted in milk and exhibits serious carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, teratogenicity, genotoxicity, and immunosuppression.2,3

AFM1 is the hydroxylated derivative of AFB1 (Fig. 1). It is generated via biotransformation by liver microsomal cytochrome P450 when animals ingest a meal contaminated with AFB1. The biotransformation does not alter the structure of the difuran ring and oxanaphthone (coumarin); hence, AFM1 remains highly toxic. AFM1 is often hundreds of times more dangerous than other highly toxic substances, such as arsenic and potassium cyanide. It may disrupt RNA and DNA synthesis, modify protein synthesis, further affect cellular metabolism, and damage animal organs, including the gastrointestinal tract, liver, and kidneys.4,5

Chemical structures of AFB1 and AFM1.

Individuals often face the danger of exposure to AFs via the consumption of contaminated crops (e.g., maize, peanuts, wheat, ginger, etc), or by ingesting dairy products or meat derived from animals that have consumed AF-contaminated feed. Additionally, farmers and the majority of agricultural workers may be exposed to inhalation of dust generated by the processing and handling of contaminated feed and crops.6 Multiple global food safety agencies have set maximum residue limits (MRLs) for the presence of AFM1 in milk and other dairy products owing to their high consumption on a daily basis. For instance, the European Union (EU) specified a maximum amount of 50 ng L−1 in milk to protect consumers;7 however, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the China Food and Drug Administration (CFDA) set an MRL of 500 ng L−1, which is significantly higher.8,9

The significant stability of AFM1 makes it resistant to conventional food processing techniques, such as pasteurization, ultra-high temperature treatment, ionizing radiation, and cooking; therefore, it is often present in final dairy products, highlighting the need for quick and accurate assay methods.10 The current traditional assay methods for AFs include high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and gas chromatography (GC), which are accurate, albeit expensive and usually require well-trained operators.11,12 In order to improve the selectivity of AF detection and avoid the above-mentioned drawbacks, molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) with different signal transduction mechanisms were introduced.13

MIPs are synthetic polymers synthesized from specific functional monomers and cross-linkers in the presence of a specified template for a target analyte molecule. The voids in the MIP designs must precisely imitate the form of the target molecules after the template is removed/washed out, exhibiting increased selective affinity and specificity.14 MIPs are involved in numerous quick, non-destructive, cheap, and simple analytical techniques that paved the way to highly selective analytical methods for molecules such as pharmaceuticals, pesticides, biomarkers, etc. Moreover, one of the advantages of MIPs is that they are resistant to extreme conditions. Along with their outstanding molecular identification abilities, MIPs withstand high temperature and pressure, as well as mechanical strain, and are stable in various solvents, acids, and alkalis.15 Due to their relatively low chemical inertia, the sensitivity of MIPs can be even enhanced by the introduction of fluorescent species, resulting in real-time and label-free detection capabilities.16 The synergy between molecularly imprinted cavities and fluorescence signalling not only improves the selectivity and sensitivity of traditional MIPs and reduces their limits of detection (LOD) to very low levels, but also it provides them with exceptional response dynamics, making fluorescent MIPs (FMIPs) indispensable tools for a wide range of applications.17

The aim of the present work is the preparation of a novel and superior FMIP-based nanosensor for the detection of AFM1 in water and cow milk samples. The designed FMIP incorporates a novel MIP synthesized for the first time via the grafting of chitosan (CS) polymer with 4-oxo-4-(phenylamino) but-2-enoic acid (OPEA). The fluorescence source herein is a Cr/Ga/Zn-based metal organic frame work (MOF).18,19 The novelty of the present study is the utilization of newly synthesized functional monomer for the fabrication of the MIP, and Cr/Ga/Zn-based MOF as a source of fluorescence, and their integration in a fluorescent nanosensor for the highly sensitive and selective spectrofluorometric assay of AFM1.18,20 All chemicals and reagents were used as received from the suppliers without any further purification. Aqueous solutions were prepared using double distilled water. The synthesis, characterization and performance evaluation of the proposed nanosensor are introduced in the following sections.

Materials and methods

Materials

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, CAS number: 10 196-18-6), 2-methylimidazole (2-MI, CAS number: 693-98-1), gallium(iii) nitrate hydrate (Ga(NO3)3·xH2O, CAS number: 69 365-72-6), 1-hydroxybenzotriazole hydrate (HOBt, CAS number: 123 333-53-9), 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP, CAS number: 1122-58-3), aniline (CAS number: 62-53-3), maleic anhydride (CAS number: 108-31-6), chromium(iii) chloride hexahydrate (CrCl3·6H2O, CAS number: 10 060-12-5), and chitosan (poly(d-glucosamine), molecular weight: 600–800 kDa, CAS number: 9012-76-4) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Germany). Hydrochloric acid (HCl, CAS number: 7647-01-0), ammonium hydroxide solution (NH4OH, CAS number: 1336-21-6), 2-propanol (CAS number: 67-63-0), diethyl ether (CAS number: 60-29-7), and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, CAS number: 68-12-2) were obtained from Fisher Scientific (USA).

Preparation of fluorescent MOF (ZGC/ZIF-8-NH2)

The fluorescent MOF was prepared according to the synthesis protocol reported elsewhere21 with minor modification. The ZGC was prepared with a one-pot hydrothermal method. Briefly, 148.8 mg of Zn(NO3)3·6H2O were mixed with 182.0 mg of Ga(NO3)3·xH2O and 266.5 mg of CrCl3·6H2O in 15 mL of ultrapure water and stirred thoroughly. Thereafter, the pH of the mixture was adjusted to 9.0 by quickly adding 28% ammonium hydroxide solution and stirring for 30 min. The obtained mixture was poured into a 50 mL hydrothermal reactor with Teflon lining and left in the oven at 220 °C overnight. After cooling to room temperature, the previous mixture was centrifuged and washed with 0.01 mol L−1 HCl, and then mixed with isopropanol and centrifuged to obtain the desired ZGC. A weight of 500.0 mg of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O was taken and mixed with 12 mL of ultrapure water. Following, 828.0 mg of 2-MI was mixed with 48.5 mL of deionized water, and then 750 µL of triethylamine was added. The Zn2+ solution was slowly dropped into the 2-MI solution, mixed well, stirred for 30 min, centrifuged thrice with deionized water, and dried at 50 °C for 12 hours to obtain the ZIF-8.

Regarding the fabrication of ZGC/ZIF-8-NH2 (abbreviated as MOF in the following sections), 1.0 g of the dried ZIF-8 powder was added to 25 mL of 25% ammonium hydroxide solution and sonicated for 60 min using a probe sonicator. The suspension was then stirred vigorously for 24 h at 20 °C and centrifuged. The obtained pellet was repeatedly washed with deionized water and then dried at 60 °C for 24 h. MOF was synthesized by the co-precipitation method by redispersing 400 mg of ZGC and 100 mg of ZIF-8-NH2 in 50 mL of deionized water and stirring for 4 hours. The powder was washed with ultrapure water and dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C.

Synthesis of the functional monomer

A volume of 1.0 mL of aniline was added to 40 mL of diethyl ether and stirred for 15 min. In a separate glass beaker, a weight of 1.42 g of maleic anhydride was dissolved in 60 mL of diethyl ether. Thereafter, the maleic anhydride solution was dropped onto the aniline solution over a period of 2 hours. The obtained monomer powder was centrifuged, washed thoroughly with diethyl ether, characterized, and stored for further use in the subsequent experiments. This monomer was designed to be easily conjugated with chitosan prior to being polymerized. This strategy is supposed to allow for coating single MOF particles with the designed MIP, so that no agglomerations could be formed; hence, the dispersion ability and suspension stability of the nanosensor in the measurement solution will be enhanced. The actual yield of the reaction was ca. 98%. Fig. 2 shows a scheme illustrating the chemical equations used in the synthesis procedures.

Scheme illustrating the chemical equations used in the synthesis of the functional monomer, chitosan–monomer conjugate and the molecular imprinting process.

Monomer conjugation with chitosan

N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC, CAS number: 25 952-53-8, 0.29 g, ca. 1.5 mmol) and HOBt (0.15 g, ca. 1.1 mmol) were sequentially added in batches to a stirred DMF solution of the functional monomer synthesized in the above section (0.18, 1.0 mmol) and chitosan (0.43 g, 1.05 mmol) in 3 mL of dry DMF at 0 °C under an argon atmosphere. The reaction mixture was stirred for 10 min at 0 °C and an additional 1 hour at 25 °C. Thereafter, DMAP (0.024 g, ca. 0.20 mmol) was added to the reaction medium, and the reaction mixture was stirred for 2 h at 25 °C. Eventually, the excess solvent was removed via dialysis against deionized water. Fig. 3 shows a graphical illustration of the composition of the synthesized nanosensor material.

Schematic diagram showing the methodology of the present study.

Molecular imprinting

A weight of 0.333 g of the modified chitosan (modified functional monomer) was mixed in 15 mL of DMSO and sonicated for 10 min. Following, 125 mg of the synthesized MOF (fluorescence source) was added to the above mixture and stirred for 10 min. Thereafter, a volume of 1.3 mL of AFM1 standard (template, 70 ng L−1) was added to the former mixture and stirred for 30 min. The polymerization reaction was initiated via the addition of 5 mL of DMSO containing 0.135 g of potassium persulfate (initiator). The obtained solution was stirred for 5 min at 60 °C. Thereafter, a weight of 3.585 g of the synthesized functional monomer (free monomer) and 250 mg of methylene bisacrylamide (cross linker) were mixed with 30 mL of DMSO and added to the previous mixture and stirred (400 rpm) for 2 hours at 60 °C. Finally, the resulting mixture was centrifuged and washed with 1 : 9 (v/v) acetic acid/methanol to remove the template molecules (i.e., AFM1 molecules). The obtained powder was dried at 60 °C for 24 hours.

Characterization of the synthesized materials

Infrared absorption scans

FTIR spectroscopy was used to identify new functional groups and/or chemical bonds in the chemical structure of the synthesized materials to confirm their successful synthesis. Absorption measurements were performed using a Frontier PerkinElmer FTIR spectrometer, coupled with a universal ATR module (Diamond/KR-5 crystal), at each stage to ascertain the successful synthesis of the monomer and the sensor materials. Spectral scans were recorded over the 400–4000 cm−1 range.

1H-NMR spectroscopy

This technique was employed to verify the successful synthesis of the monomer and the monomer-modified chitosan using a Bruker Avance II 400 MHz spectrometer operating at 300 K. A specimen of the previously mentioned samples was dissolved in deuterated DMSO, and the results were documented in ppm relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS).

Particle size and zeta potential analysis

The particle size (PS, in nm) of the synthesized MOF, MIP, and NIP was evaluated by dynamic light scattering (DLS, Zetasizer 2000 Malvern Instrument). The materials were examined at 25 °C in deionized water as a dispersing medium. In addition, the surface zeta potential (ZP, in mV) of the particles was assessed to measure the overall surface charge. All measurements were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility of the results.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging was utilized to measure the size and morphology of the synthesized nanomaterials. A single drop of the sample solution was placed onto the copper grid, thereafter treated with ethanol for morphological fixation prior to microscopic analysis. The morphology of the selected samples was examined with the aid of a transmission electron microscope (CEM 902A; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

X-ray diffraction analysis was employed to investigate the crystallinity of the materials of interest. The crystal structures of the materials were elucidated via recording the diffractograms within the range of 5–70° (2θ). XRD measurements were performed at room temperature using a Siemens diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation at 35 kV.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The thermal stability and phase transitions of the synthesized materials were analyzed with the aid of a DSC instrument (Linseis-L63/45, Germany). A sample mass of 5–10 mg was accurately weighed and loaded into the DSC aluminium pans. The thermal behavior of the samples was investigated over the temperature range of 25–420 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 under inert nitrogen atmosphere (using a N2 gas flow rate of 50 mL min−1), thus preventing oxidative degradation of the samples.

Evaluating the nanosensor performance

Wavelength selection

The excitation and emission wavelengths were selected by changing the excitation wavelength and investigating the corresponding emission wavelengths over the spectral window of 200–900 nm. The highest fluorescence intensity emission wavelength was selected for fluorescence measurement. The measurement wavelength should demonstrate the widest Stoke's shift from the excitation wavelength to avoid spectral interference and fluorescence loss. The selected wavelengths are λex = 425 nm and λem = 853 nm. The emission maximum at 853 nm (excited at 425 nm) indeed yields a large Stokes shift of 428 nm, which is unusual but well-documented for certain organic fluorophores and metal–organic frameworks involving excited-state intramolecular proton transfer (ESIPT) or charge-transfer (CT) mechanisms. For instance, similar large Stokes shifts (>400 nm) have been reported in ESIPT-based fluorophores like benzothiazole derivatives22 and CT-active MOFs with ligand-to-metal charge transfer.23,24 It is worth mentioning that Pan et al. reported that the MOF used herein emits purple fluorescence at 454 and 700 nm when excited at 254 nm, thus confirming the extraordinarily large Stoke's shift in the present work. However, we highly recommend that the full mechanism of emission should be studied in the future to explain this large Stoke's shift, since this is not in the scope of the present work.

Effect of incubation time

To investigate the effect of incubation time on the fluorescence signal of the suggested nanosensor, an accurately measured volume of 2.9 mL of the MMIP suspension (236.22 µg mL−1) was added to 100 µL of AFM1 solution (2.0 ng L−1). The mixtures were left for different time periods (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min), and then the fluorescence emission spectra of the resulting solutions were recorded.

Calibration curve procedure

To reach the appropriate intensity of the fluorescent nanosensor, 12 mg of the nanosensor was suspended in 50.8 mL of water and left covered on the magnetic stirrer (300 rpm) for 24 h to achieve maximum dispersion. Thereafter, a standard stock solution of AFM1 was prepared by dissolving a vial containing 1.3 mL of 20 ng L−1 in 11.7 mL of water. Subsequently, a series of solutions comprising different concentrations of AFM1 (3.33–63.3 pg L−1) were prepared by adding different volumes (5–95 µL) of the AFM1 standard solution to 2905 µL of the nanosensor suspension. The volume was completed to 3000 µL with double distilled water and stirred at 300 rpm for 20 min. Following, the fluorescence intensity of the nanosensor was measured at an emission wavelength (λem) of 853 nm using an excitation wavelength (λex) of 425 nm. A calibration curve was constructed via plotting the fluorescence intensity change (as the abscissa) versus AFM1 concentration (as the ordinate). Analysis of spiked water and cow milk samples

To ensure the highest performance of the fluorescent nanosensor, spiked samples were prepared by adding different amounts of the standard AFM1 solution to different amounts of double distilled water. The corresponding sample concentrations were calculated and the resulting solutions were analyzed using the proposed nanosensor. All samples were measured in triplicate to calculate the relative standard deviation for investigating the precision of the proposed nanosensor.

Skimmed milk (LAMAR lactose-free skimmed cow milk and Juhayna skimmed cow milk) was obtained from a local market (Giza, Egypt). The milk samples were spiked with known amounts of AFM1 to prepare concentrations lying within the linear range of the calibration curve (the taken amounts are 58.3, 125.0 and 158.3 pg). Thereafter, the milk samples were centrifuged at 22 × 103 rpm for 10 min to remove all the suspended contents. The supernatant was analyzed as mentioned above in the analysis of the spiked water samples and the found masses of AFM1 were calculated from the calibration curve. Results and discussion

Physicochemical characterization of the synthesized materials

Infrared absorption spectroscopy

Infrared (IR) spectroscopy is a pivotal technique used frequently for the detection and identification of the functional groups in organic and inorganic substances. It works by directing an infrared light toward the sample, which results in the vibration of the bonds at a certain wavenumber, resulting in the formation of a complete and unique spectrum for each molecule. Recently, the used instrument, specifically Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer (FTIR), produced special data characterized by high speed, better sensitivity and noteworthy accuracy, which in turn facilitated the comprehensive detection of various functional groups and subsequently, the identification of molecular architecture and sample purity.25

The monomer used in the present study, synthesized from maleic anhydride and aniline, exhibited strong IR absorption bands at 1735 and 1751 cm−1 (Fig. 4A) corresponding to stretching of the carbonyl group of maleic anhydride. Furthermore, the carbonyl of the amide bond appeared at 1655 cm−1, which is evidence of the successful synthesis of the monomer. Additionally, an absorption band appeared in the region of 3350–3460 cm−1, corresponding to the N–H bond vibration.26,27

The spectrum of the polymer after conjugation showed a clearly sharpened peak near 1655 cm−1. Therefore, this sharpening confirms the successful connection between the monomer and chitosan via amide bond formation. 1H-NMR spectroscopy

The 1H-NMR spectrum of the functional monomer shown in Fig. 4B, known as (Z)-4-oxo-4-(phenylamino) but-2-enoic acid, also confirms the successful synthesis of the functional monomer. The spectrum shows characteristic peaks such as the five aromatic protons of the benzene ring, which were observed as a multiplet between 7.0 and 7.6 ppm. Moreover, the vinylic protons of the maleic acid moiety appear in the region of 6.2–6.5 ppm as doublets, which confirms the presence of the double bond on which the polymerization will be conducted. Furthermore, the amide proton appears as a singlet peak at 10.3 ppm, which confirms the successful formation of the amide bond between aniline and maleic anhydride. Finally, the acidic proton of the maleic acid moiety appears as a singlet peak near 13 ppm.

The second 1H-NMR spectrum demonstrated in Fig. 4C corresponds to the chitosan after conjugation with the monomer, where it confirms the successful linking of the monomer to the chitosan through EDC/HOBt-activated coupling reaction. The spectrum shows characteristic multiplet peaks in the region of 7.4–7.9 ppm, which are related to the aromatic protons. Meanwhile, the vinylic proton of the monomer appears at 6.96 ppm, confirming the presence of the active centre at which polymerization will occur. Moreover, the multiplet peaks appearing in the range of 4.17–3.20 ppm correspond to the protons of the chitosan rings. Furthermore, additional multiplets related to the aliphatic protons of the residual N-acetyl group and the polymer side chain appear in the region of 2.85–0.43 ppm. Finally, the absence of the signal of the carboxylic acid proton of the monomer is clear evidence of the completion of the reaction between the monomer and chitosan, confirming the reaction of the carboxylic groups of the monomer with the amino groups of chitosan.

XRD crystallography

X-ray diffraction (XRD) is an analytical and non-destructive method utilized to detect and determine the phase composition, lattice characteristics and crystalline structure of substances. This device directs a monochromatic X-ray beam onto the sample, which results in the diffraction of the X-ray by the atomic planes in the crystal lattice, resulting in a diffraction pattern dictated by Bragg's formula. The resultant pattern is a fingerprint for each crystalline phase. The current XRD systems have sophisticated software and sensitive detectors, allowing rapid and accurate study of powders, thin films, and bulk materials in both research and industry.32 The XRD pattern of the MOF present in Fig. 5A shows a broad band containing sharp peaks in the range 20–60° 2θ, indicating its amorphous structure with a degree of crystallinity, which may be attributed to the synthetic conditions that favour fast nucleation. However, after the imprinting process (either in the case of FNIP or FMIP), at which the MOF was coated with the monomer-conjugated chitosan followed by monomer polymerization, the structure was converted into crystalline with clear and sharp peaks indicating either the template-driven organization. The relatively amorphous structure of the MOF can be attributed to template-driven organization, or contamination or crystallization of the unremoved template molecules. The polymerization not only provides molecular selectivity but can also modulate the packing and microstructure of the material.

Differential scanning calorimetry

The thermal behaviours of MOF, FMIP, and FNIP were examined using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis, Fig. 5B–D. The thermogram of the MOF (Fig. 5D) exhibits a wide endothermic peak at around 120 °C, which is due to the loss of adsorbed and coordinated water molecules from the porous network. This peak is followed by structural degradation, which is characterized by a gradual decrease in the heat flow. On the contrary, FNIP and FMIP (Fig. 5B and C, respectively) showed sharp exothermic peaks at ca. 210.5 and 209.5 °C, respectively, which correspond to melting or the major relaxation of the crosslinked chains of both systems. These peaks are also followed by a decline in the heat flow due to structural decomposition of the organic matrix. The presence of these distinct peaks confirms the formation of a stable, crosslinked network within the FNIP and FMIP. It is worth mentioning that the observed slight variations in peak temperature and energy further suggest the structural influence of the template molecule (AFM1) during the synthesis process. Furthermore, the FNIP demonstrated a greater enthalpy of transition (ΔH = 375.8 J g−1) relative to the FMIP (ΔH = 308.2 J g−1), implying that the imprinting procedure generates a network with a distinct organization compared to the FNIP, which lacks the reactive sites introduced by imprinting, and consequently, necessitating a higher energy input.

Transmission electron microscopy

The TEM image of MOF (shown in Fig. 5E) depicts cuboidal aggregates with rough edges. The difference in contrast in the shown particle may be attributed to regions of different thicknesses or porosity, which is a real characteristic of MOFs resulting in an enormous surface area. Moreover, the TEM image of the FMIP (Fig. 5F) shows a core–shell structure where the dark and dense area represents the MOF and the outer crystalline shell is the crosslinked polymer. The higher opacity and relatively smooth surface of the MOF nanoparticle shown in the FNIP image might indicate decreased porosity as a result of coating of the MOF with the AFM1-imprinted polymer. The TEM image of FNIP (Fig. 5G) is very similar to that of the FMIP discussed above, indicating that the polymer coating was successfully achieved either in the presence or absence of the template AFM1. From the above characterization techniques, XRD and TEM images provide clear evidence that the molecular imprinting process impacts the organization of the composite, introducing new order or crystalline fragments not present in the pure MOF. This change from amorphous to crystalline domains would greatly and efficiently affect the sensing and adsorption performance of the nanosensor system.

DLS and zeta potential measurements

The hydrodynamic nanoparticle size and surface charge were measured using dynamic light scattering, which highlights the differences between FMIP, FNIP, and pristine MOF. The MOF shown in Fig. 5E–G exhibits the main narrow peak of size (Fig. 6A) cantered around 68 nm, while the MIP showed a narrow peak cantered around 183.8 nm, indicating the successful and mostly controlled polymerization process being applied during the molecular imprinting process. On the other hand, the NIP exhibited a slightly broader size profile, giving rise to a peak at 390 nm, indicating the relative increase in heterogeneity and tendency to agglomeration in the absence of the template molecule.

The inset in panel D shows the calibration curve of AFM1 in aqueous meida using the investigated nanosensor.

Zeta potential values in Fig. 6B indicate that all the prepared materials exhibited a net negative surface charge of +15.1 mV for the MOF, −5.53 mV for the MIP, and −5.46 mV for the NIP, reflecting favourable colloidal stability and limited aggregation during synthesis. The zeta potential value of the prepared MOF is in coincidence with the results published elsewhere.33 Interestingly, this positive zeta potential was shifted to a slight negative one after coating with imprinted or the non-imprinted polymer. The polymer coating contains chitosan (with its characteristic positively charged ammonium groups); however, the amino groups of chitosan are linked with the functional monomer via an amide bond. This may have resulted in the significantly reduced number of positive charges, leading to the slight negative zeta potential. The results collectively indicate that molecular imprinting with MOF inclusion produces sensor particles with acceptable size uniformity and surface characteristics compared to non-imprinted counterparts, thereby offering an ideal platform for selective sensing applications. It is worth mentioning that the NIP showed a zeta potential value comparable to that of MIP. This may indicate that the FMIP and FNIP have relatively lower solution stability than the MOF. This clearly confirms the coating of the MOF with MIP or NIP.

Nanosensor performance evaluation

Effect of time

The performance of the sensor strongly depends on the kinetics of analyte binding dictated by the incubation period during which the sensor interacts with the target molecule. In the present work, the impact of incubation time on the sensor's performance was investigated by carefully monitoring the fluorescence response at different time intervals. The experiment was conducted, where the sensor was incubated with AFM1 for different time intervals (5–45 min). The fluorescence response was measured at each time interval as the ratio I0/I, where I0 is the initial fluorescence intensity and I is the post-incubation intensity (Fig. 6C). Initially, at 5 and 10 min, fluorescence ratios were moderately high, indicating lower binding of AFM1 to the surface binding sites of the FMIP sensor. However, a significant decrease in the I0/I ratio culminates at 20 min, indicating the binding of the AFM1 to the imprinted sites (i.e., indicated by the increase of the post-incubation fluorescence intensity). This finding is relevant to literature reports where mostly the imprinted polymers reach equilibrium within several to tens of minutes, depending on matrix porosity, analyte size, and sensor surface chemistry.33,34

After 20 min, the fluorescence response did not change, and a plateau was obtained, which may indicate that AFM1 molecules have saturated the FMIP binding sites. The incubation for a short time leads to incomplete binding, as shown with the high ratios at 5 and 10 min. On the contrary, incubation for long periods may result in decreased selectivity or signal saturation because of rebinding phenomena, possible sensor fatigue or nonspecific interactions.35,36 Therefore, 20 min was selected as the best incubation period for sensitive and reliable measurement.

Calibration curve and analytical parameters

The quantitative performance of the nanosensor was determined by calibration curve construction and recovery study utilizing a series of standard solutions with increasing concentrations of AFM1. The nanosensor's fluorescence response was measured for each concentration, and the ratio I0/I was calculated to construct the calibration curve.

The nanosensor exhibited a clear, concentration-dependent decrease in the I0/I ratio as the toxin concentration increased from 3.3–63.3 pg L−1 shown in Fig. 6D. This inverse relationship indicates effective enhancement of the nanosensor's fluorescence signal upon binding with the analyte molecules, which is typical for competitive binding in molecularly imprinted systems. The measured samples within the tested range showed excellent linearity as depicted from the r2 value of the calibration curve (r2 = 0.9945), confirming that the sensor is suitable for quantitative analysis.

This superior sensitivity may be due to the presence of the well-known porous MOF material that comprise an enormous number of pores that facilitate the adsorption of AFM1 molecules even at extremely low concentration. In addition, the present work provides a facile large-scale synthesis method of the FMIP-based nanosensor as a very minute quantity of the nanosensor is needed to prepare a large volume of the suspension utilized in sample measurements. Moreover, a microliter volume of this nanosensor suspension is required for the analysis of an AFM1-containing sample. Furthermore, the long-term stability of our nanosensor material is much better than other sensors comprising antibodies or nucleic acids that are liable to be affected by temperature changes and storage conditions, in addition to being very expensive and unaffordable. Finally, the dependence on fluorometry facilitates the analytical procedures since it does not need well-trained personnel like other sophisticated instruments like chromatography or electrochemistry. Despite all these advantages, the application of the proposed nanosensor to real samples, including food, feed and cereals is still under investigation. Albeit, our nanosensor clearly demonstrated its successful use for the assay of AFM1 in spiked water samples with exceptional sensitivity, simple analytical procedure, wide applicability range, affordable price, and minimal energy consumption.

The analysis of spiked skimmed cow milk samples confirmed the high sensitivity of the proposed sensor in biological/food matrices. A complex matrix like that present in milk usually needs reliable separation procedures prior to the analysis of the sample to avoid interference from matrix components. However, in the present study, the proposed nanosensor showed an excellent selectivity due to the utilization of molecular imprinting approach. This was proved by the acceptable recovery values of the spiked commercial skimmed cow milk samples given in Table 2. In addition, the sensor demonstrated an acceptable precision revealed by the low RSD values.

Conclusions

The present study provided a novel analytical method for the determination of picogram amounts of aflatoxin M1 in aqueous solutions and cow milk samples. The proposed analytical method is based on integrating nanotechnology (in the form of a fluorescent MOF nanomaterial) and molecular imprinting technology to provide a highly sensitive fluorescent molecularly imprinted polymer-based fluorometric nanosensor. The nanosensor demonstrated a wide linear range and a substantially low limit of detection, indicating its high sensitivity and wide applicability. This study reports for the first time a novel strategy for the preparation of a Cr/Ga/Zn-based fluorescent MOF with thin molecularly imprinted polymeric coating for the selective binding with AFM1. Recovery studies showed that the proposed nanosensor demonstrates high accuracy (revealed by the acceptable recovery values) and precision (indicated by the low standard deviation values obtained). Moreover, the analytical method requires tiny amounts of the prepared nanosensor as well as minute AFM1 sample volumes. The incubation time is 20 min, which guarantee rapid but accurate analytical procedure. Accordingly, the proposed fluorometric nanosensor is thought to be suitable for routine analysis of AFM1 samples in water and cow milk. It can be concluded that the present study provides a fluorometric nanosensor that clearly demonstrated its successful use for the assay of AFM1 in spiked water and cow milk samples with exceptional sensitivity, simple analytical procedure, wide applicability range, affordable price, and minimal energy consumption.
