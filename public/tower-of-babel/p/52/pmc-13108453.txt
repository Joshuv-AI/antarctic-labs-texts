Prolonged Paradoxical TB-IRIS: 2 Case Reports and a Review of the Literature
Authors: Max McClure, John Szumowski, Gabriel Chamie, Janice Louie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough paradoxical tuberculosis immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (TB-IRIS) in HIV-TB coinfection is typically thought of as occurring within several months of starting antiretroviral therapy, more prolonged manifestations have been reported. We describe 2 cases in which manifestations of TB-IRIS continued for longer than 1 year after the end of TB treatment and review the literature on prolonged TB-IRIS. These cases are often characterized by a history of extrapulmonary TB and initially low CD4 counts and commonly demonstrate lymphadenitis or abscesses. In the absence of clear consensus around its appropriate management, prolonged TB-IRIS presents a significant diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma.Keywords: HIV, IRIS, paradoxical reaction, prolonged, tuberculosis

Paradoxical immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) complicates approximately 18% of HIV-associated tuberculosis (TB) cases in patients receiving antiretroviral (ART) and TB therapy [1]. The syndrome is characterized by initial improvement with TB therapy followed by paradoxical worsening typically coincident with initiation of ART [2–6] and is a diagnosis of exclusion. Although TB-IRIS is commonly a self-limited condition occurring within the first 2 to 3 months after starting ART, more prolonged manifestations are possible [7]. We report 2 cases of prolonged posttreatment TB-IRIS in individuals with coinfection of HIV-1 and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) occurring greater than 1 year after the completion of TB treatment and review the literature on prolonged TB-IRIS. In both cases, adherence to TB therapy and ART was assessed during daily directly observed therapy and regularly scheduled provider visits.

A 39-year-old man from Ethiopia presented with cough, fevers, and night sweats. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest demonstrated numerous pulmonary nodules. He was newly diagnosed with HIV (CD4 count of 14 cells/µL [19%] and viral load [VL] of 269 663 copies/mL) and initiated dolutegravir, tenofovir alafenamide (TAF), and emtricitabine. Two days later, he was also diagnosed with acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear-negative pulmonary TB, later found to be pan-susceptible. Treatment with isoniazid, rifampin (RIF), pyrazinamide, and ethambutol was started, with prednisone 40 mg daily added to reduce risk of TB-IRIS, and TAF replaced with tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) [8].

Two weeks later, he presented with fevers and vomiting. A CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis (CAP) demonstrated abdominal lymphadenopathy and bilateral psoas abscesses (maximum, 4.6 × 3.6 cm). HIV VL was undetectable. Fine-needle aspirate (FNA) of a retroperitoneal lymph node was AFB smear- and culture-positive. An attempted prednisone taper was complicated by lymphadenopathy, and the patient’s dose was increased to 60 mg daily.

Surveillance CT CAP after 2 months of therapy demonstrated necrotic left supraclavicular lymphadenopathy, a right upper lobe cavity, right pleural effusion, and hepatic lesions. Pleural fluid was exudative, with a lymphocyte predominance, elevated adenosine deaminase, and negative AFB smear and culture. Cervical lymph node FNA was notable for positive AFB smear and negative culture. These findings were felt to be consistent with TB-IRIS, and prednisone was tapered off.

Surveillance CT CAP 12 months into therapy showed worsening supraclavicular lymphadenopathy and psoas abscesses (maximum, 3.4 × 5.7 cm). Bilateral drains were placed; psoas fluid had positive AFB smear and gram-positive cocci on gram stain but negative AFB and bacterial cultures. He received 1 week of empiric amoxicillin-clavulanate for presumed bacterial superinfection following adequate source control.

The patient completed 15 months of TB treatment but was rehospitalized 3 months later after developing abdominal pain, with a CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis showing worsening psoas abscesses (maximum, 5.0 × 3. × 6.7 cm). HIV VL remained suppressed, and CD4 count was 113 cells/µL (14%). He underwent aspiration of multiple abscesses and drain placement. Psoas fluid showed polymicrobial bacterial growth, but AFB smears and cultures remained negative. He was retreated with vancomycin and ertapenem followed by amoxicillin-clavulanate to complete 2 weeks of therapy following adequate source control. Approximately 5, 8, 11, 54, and 60 months after end of treatment, he re-presented with cervical and supraclavicular lymphadenitis, bilateral psoas abscesses, and exudative left pleural effusion. All samples remained AFB smear- and culture-negative. At 61 months, he underwent biopsy of an enlarging left supraclavicular lymph node; pathology showed granulomatous changes with necrosis and the tissue was positive on Xpert MTB/RIF for MTB without any rpoB mutation detected. He started isoniazid, pyrazinamide, rifabutin, and ethambutol, but opted to stop after lymph node AFB cultures were negative.

An 18-year-old man with a history of tobacco use was treated for pulmonary TB in Ukraine. Nine years later, he underwent an appendectomy with pathology demonstrating granulomatous inflammation. He restarted TB therapy for approximately 6 weeks, stopping after AFB tissue cultures were negative. The same year, he was diagnosed with HIV (CD4 count approximately 100 cells/µL, VL unknown) and chronic HBV and started dolutegravir, TDF, and emtricitabine. After medication shortages that year, his HIV VL remained detectable and ART was switched to darunavir/cobicistat, TAF, and emtricitabine. Two months later, he was found to have an HIV VL of 772 c/mL, with a CD4 count of 473 cells/µL (24%).

After moving to the United States that year, he developed a neck mass, with a CT scan of the neck and chest demonstrating a left neck fluid collection and nodules in bilateral lung apices. Sputum was AFB smear- and culture-negative, whereas lymph node biopsy was AFB smear-positive, culture-negative, and Xpert MTB/RIF-positive. Pyrosequencing from the lymph node sample demonstrated resistance to isoniazid and rifampin, and he started bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid, and moxifloxacin at standard doses for 6 months (adequate dosing confirmed by therapeutic drug monitoring). At that time his HIV VL was 73 c/mL, and ART was switched to dolutegravir, TDF, and emtricitabine to minimize drug–drug interactions.

Weeks after starting TB therapy, he developed right cervical lymphadenopathy. Right lymph node FNA was AFB smear- and culture-negative, whereas left cervical lymph node FNA was again AFB smear-positive but culture-negative. His symptoms were believed to be consistent with IRIS, and he started prednisone 60 mg daily, tapered over 4 months. Two months later, left cervical lymphadenopathy recurred. He was treated with ibuprofen, with improvement after spontaneous drainage. A follow-up CT scan of the chest demonstrated stable to improving pulmonary nodules.

Three, 6, and 12 months after completing his TB regimen, the patient developed recurrent right cervical lymph node swelling. AFB smear and culture of FNA were repeatedly negative, but 16s polymerase chain reaction was positive for MTB on 1 occasion. Two more prednisone tapers from 60 mg daily were started without benefit.

Fourteen months after completing TB therapy, he developed groin lymphadenopathy. A CT scan demonstrated intraabdominal lymphadenopathy and left psoas and anterior rectus sheath fluid collections (maximum, 4.6 × 3.4 × 1.7 cm) believed to represent IRIS. Twenty-three months after completing TB therapy, he had additional episodes of right cervical and anterior neck lymph node swelling, with most recent imaging demonstrating cervical lymphadenopathy, stable abdominal fluid collections, and a lesion of the manubrium. He has been referred for sampling of his neck and manubrial lesions.

DISCUSSION

Our cases share risk factors for TB-IRIS: CD4 counts below 200 cells/µL at time of TB diagnosis [1, 2, 9–12], initial evidence of extrapulmonary TB [13–15], and early ART initiation [10]. Both are notable for their duration—up to 7 years after the start of TB therapy in case 1—and their protean nature, with IRIS affecting sites not known to have TB disease. Together, they illustrate the frustrations patients and clinicians experience with current diagnostic and management strategies for TB-IRIS.

Rates of posttreatment reactions to TB in both people with HIV (PWH) and people without HIV range from 3% to 14% [7, 16], with TB-IRIS in PWH more common than paradoxical reactions (PR) among people without HIV [17–19]. TB-IRIS in PWH occurring 1 year or more after completion of TB therapy, as in our 2 reports, is relatively rare (Table 1) [7, 20, 21].

Pathophysiology

While TB-IRIS occurring after ART initiation (within 2 [3, 4] or 3 [5] months) is attributable to immune reconstitution, the pathophysiology underlying prolonged reactions is unclear. Proposed mechanisms include immune reconstitution resulting from recovery from TB infection [25, 26], delayed clearance of MTB antigen [27], or contained microbiological relapses [7].

Specific immunologic profiles may correlate with TB-IRIS. Preceding IRIS, these include elevated plasma interleukin-6 levels [28] and a composite score of inflammatory markers [29], though these require validation. Elevated plasma interferon gamma and ferritin have been reported during PR episodes in people without HIV [30], but these results have not been replicated in HIV-associated TB-IRIS [31], and their ability to distinguish IRIS from TB recurrence has not been evaluated.

Diagnosis

The diagnosis of TB-IRIS is one of exclusion. Paradoxical TB-IRIS is indistinguishable from progression of TB and can manifest at any potential TB site, including those not originally implicated [32]. Our cases were both characterized by abscesses and/or lymphadenitis, which are among the most commonly reported manifestations of TB-IRIS in general [1, 33, 34] and of cases lasting longer than 1 year in particular [20]. Fortunately, most cases of prolonged paradoxical TB-IRIS resolve spontaneously [7], and death is uncommon without central nervous system involvement [1, 35].

The differential diagnosis for clinical deterioration following TB therapy includes TB recurrence, non-TB infection, and malignancy. TB recurrence may represent reinfection or relapse (with or without new drug resistance). Multiple etiologies may cooccur: paradoxical responses have been reported in the setting of drug resistance [36, 37], whereas bacterial infections may superinfect cold abscesses (see case 1).

In both cases presented here, evaluation of suspected TB-IRIS consisted of imaging and specimen collection for AFB smear and culture, additional microbiological studies as needed, and pathology/cytology. We noted that smears and nucleic acid amplification tests were occasionally positive during suspected episodes of IRIS, as previously described [34]. Detection of nonviable bacilli has been reported with both Xpert and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assays [38] up to 7 years after end of treatment [39, 40], with 14% of retreatment cases were Xpert false positive in 1 cohort [41]. Positive AFB culture remains the sole microbiologic indicator of recurrence.

Management

The decision of how aggressively to evaluate possible IRIS must be individualized and patient centered (see case 2). With isolated lymphadenopathy, watchful waiting may be considered [7]. With involvement of a new site (cases 1 and 2), risk of severe disease, severe symptoms (case 1), or significant risk of reinfection (case 1), a more comprehensive approach should be considered. If clinical concern for recurrence is high, it may be reasonable to empirically restart TB therapy (case 1). For central nervous system lesions, some authors recommend empiric treatment of multiple etiologies simultaneously [42].

Beyond therapeutic aspiration (or surgical drainage) as necessary for control of symptoms, management of suspected TB-IRIS commonly consists of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and/or corticosteroids. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are in common use [1, 43], consistent with their first-line role for managing mild TB-IRIS [44], though their efficacy has not been demonstrated in clinical trials. Corticosteroids remain widely used for TB-IRIS after immune reconstitution [45], but supporting data are also lacking. Corticosteroids did not shorten symptom duration in a non–HIV-infected retrospective cohort of patients with PR [33]. However, 4 weeks of prednisone to treat moderately severe paradoxical TB-IRIS in PWH is supported by a randomized clinical trial demonstrating a significant reduction in the combination of cumulative days hospitalized and number of outpatient therapeutic procedures at 12 weeks [46]. Notably, 12 of 110 patients in the trial benefited from a longer duration (median 84 days [IQR 60–126]). The use of prednisone at the time of ART initiation in PLWH with CD4 <100 cells/µL to prevent TB-IRIS is also supported by a randomized clinical trial demonstrating an association with lower risk of TB-IRIS through 12 weeks [8]. Case 1 received prophylactic prednisone, but corticosteroids were deferred in case 2 because of higher CD4 count on re-presentation and concurrent HBV infection. The paradoxical worsening observed during multiple prednisone tapers in these cases illustrates the difficulty in withdrawing corticosteroids. There are no data to inform optimal steroid tapering, but slow tapers may be needed.

Such prolonged corticosteroid courses carry the risk of infectious and noninfectious complications [47], lead to decreased glucocorticoid receptor expression over time [48], and may have decreased efficacy in the setting of interleukin-6 induced by MTB disease [49]. There has consequently been interest in alternative immunomodulatory agents in TB-IRIS and PR, with particular focus on tumor necrosis factor inhibitors because of their ability to control mechanisms of inflammation unique to excess tumor necrosis factor [50, 51], including inhibiting T-cell apoptosis and promoting lymphocyte accumulation [21, 52–54] (see Table 2).

CONCLUSION

Patients with HIV-TB coinfection may present with paradoxical TB-IRIS long after TB therapy and without a clear relationship to CD4 recovery or ART initiation. These cases are often characterized by a history of extrapulmonary TB and initially low CD4 counts and commonly demonstrate lymph node involvement and/or abscesses, including at sites not thought to have TB disease. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for alternative etiologies. There is no clear consensus around appropriate management for prolonged TB-IRIS. An improved understanding of the immunopathogenesis of these cases may inform more effective immunomodulatory therapy.
