Iranian mothers’ perceptions of key nursing safety practices during first chemotherapy treatment of children with leukaemia: a longitudinal study
Authors: Hosna Ghorbani, Akram Ghahramanian, Arefeh Davoodi, Leila Valizadeh, Farzaneh Bagheriyeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesUnderstanding maternal perceptions of the quality and safety of care serves as a crucial management tool for the planning and enhancement of health interventions. In Iran, where cultural norms emphasise mothers’ central role in caregiving and healthcare resources are often limited, incorporating their perspectives into nursing care strategies is essential. This study aimed to assess the perception of key nursing safety practices among Iranian mothers of children with leukaemia undergoing their first chemotherapy course.DesignA longitudinal study.SettingOncology wards of hospitals affiliated with Tabriz University of Medical Sciences.ParticipantsMothers of children with leukaemia.ResultsThe mean perception score increased from 2.75 (out of 4) at baseline to 2.99 by week 4. The largest increases in perception were observed in patient identification and hand hygiene, with mean differences of 0.41 and 0.38, respectively. A significant increase in overall perception and subscales was observed (p<0.001). Mothers’ education level, economic status, child’s age, family size and the child’s leukaemia type (acute lymphocytic leukaemia vs acute myeloid leukaemia) were significantly associated with their perceptions.ConclusionsActively involving mothers in the care process enhances their perceptions of safety and quality during hospitalisation. Supportive and educational interventions are needed to further improve the safety and quality of nursing care.

Introduction

Cancer remains a significant global health concern.1 The challenges associated with cancer for the family and the uncertain prognosis of the disease distinguish it from other chronic childhood diseases.2 Among childhood cancers, leukaemias are the most common malignancy.3 The increasing survival rate of children with cancer, coupled with the growing complexity of treatment regimens, presents patients and their families with complex decisions.4 5

Children with cancer require frequent hospitalisation and undergo invasive and painful treatments and procedures with many side effects.6 During the first course of chemotherapy, mothers of children with cancer face highly stressful experiences, including uncertainty about treatment outcomes, concerns about side effects and navigating complex hospital environments.7 In these circumstances, they want to receive quality and safe care for their child. Maintaining patient safety is one of the professional nursing standards that must be considered in providing nursing care in order to improve the quality of care.8 Safety and quality of nursing care mean preventing all avoidable harm—whether intentional or unintentional—to patients. This includes mitigating risks such as medication errors, diagnostic inaccuracies, healthcare-associated infections or other failures that could lead to physical or psychological injury or death.9 In the context of child healthcare services, the perception of safety and quality of nursing care encompasses mothers’ subjective assessment of how effectively nursing staff prevent avoidable harm, provide compassionate and competent care and support both the child and family throughout treatment. These perceptions play a critical role in shaping mothers’ confidence in the care provided, the emotional well-being and their involvement as active participants in their child’s treatment process.10

The knowledge that parents have about the treatment and procedures needed for their children can contribute to safety, reduce negative emotions, prevent adverse events and enable them to play an active role in the child’s care.11 Children who are hospitalised in paediatric wards may not be able to express their opinions about the care they receive.12 Therefore, caregivers or family members who are responsible for the care of the child in the hospital can play an important role in expressing their perception of the care provided to the child. Mothers’ perception of the nursing care their child receives may be an indication of whether their expectations for the child’s health and recovery during hospitalisation are being met.13 When patient expectations are not met, the perceived quality of care is often diminished. This may make them anxious and cause aggressive behaviours during nurse-patient interactions.14 In this way, mothers’ perceptions help measure the quality of care provided and provide opportunities for improvement and provide an opportunity for physicians and nurses to review the feedback and suggestions of families of sick children with the aim of improving the quality of nursing care they receive, thus ultimately affecting the outcomes of care performed for the patient and nursing performance.15

Understanding users’ perceptions, opinions and satisfaction regarding the quality and safety of care serves as a valuable management tool for planning and improving health interventions.16 Studies on patient safety and the participation of nurses in the implementation of strategies to improve the quality and safety of care are essential and innovative at the same time and can help professionals in this field to become familiar with the causes and effects on patient health, in addition to providing adequate training to prevent new events in health services.17 Khan et al found that parents are a valuable, yet underutilised, source of information regarding preventable safety events in hospitalised children.18 Insight into parents’ perspectives is crucial when examining the quality of care for children with serious illness, especially since parents themselves are at risk for adverse outcomes. Parents’ reports of their children’s care can also effectively provide information about care processes compared with resource-focused methods such as medical record review or videotaping.19

Given that the patient’s perspective has recently gained greater significance and its role in decision-making is increasingly emphasised—alongside the ultimate goal of healthcare organisations, which is ‘providing desirable services from the customers’ point of view’,20 further research is needed to explore the quality and safety of nursing care from the perspective of parents of children and adolescents with cancer, as this perspective is a critical factor in the patients’ recovery process.

A review of the literature shows that studies in the field of examining the perception of safety and quality of nursing care of mothers with children with leukaemia who are undergoing chemotherapy for the first time are limited. Mothers of children admitted to oncology wards for the first time experience significant stress in this environment due to the specific conditions of the ward and insufficient awareness of the side effects of chemotherapy drugs.21 Understanding these mothers’ perspectives on the safety and quality of nursing care is a fundamental step towards understanding their unique needs and concerns. It is also possible to witness positive changes in favour of child safety in these wards and the positive experience of mothers, which in turn facilitates the implementation of solutions.22 On the other hand, considering the excessive prevalence of cancer in society, avoiding errors and observing the safety of patients in performing safe and effective radiation therapy, chemotherapy and other high-risk treatment methods, paying attention to the challenges and opportunities to ensure access of cancer patients to safe, affordable and high-quality cancer care in the health policy landscape, seems necessary.23

Conversely, inadequate or substandard care can have devastating physical, psychological and economic consequences for patients. Assessing user perception is one way to assess the quality and safety of services, through which a set of concepts and attitudes related to received assistance can be obtained.24 This information increases the knowledge of oncology nurses’ care and ultimately helps to facilitate the planning of nursing care based on the priorities of care behaviours identified by patients.22 Therefore, given the importance of assessing safety and quality from the perspective of service users, this study aimed to determine the perception of key nursing safety practices among Iranian mothers of children with leukaemia undergoing their first chemotherapy course.

Methods

Aims

This study aimed to assess the perception of key nursing safety practices among Iranian mothers of children with leukaemia undergoing their first chemotherapy course.

Study design

This descriptive longitudinal study was conducted to explore Iranian mothers’ perceptions of safety and quality of nursing care during their children’s first chemotherapy course for leukaemia. Guideline of Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) was used for conducting and reporting study (online supplemental file 1).

In this context, perception of safety and quality of nursing care refers to the mothers’ subjective observations and evaluations of the nursing care practices related to patient safety measures and quality standards experienced by their children during hospitalisation. This includes mothers’ assessments of nursing behaviours such as medication administration, prevention of falls, hygiene practices and communication, as reflected in their responses to a standardised instrument.

Demographic and clinical data for mothers and children were collected using a questionnaire at baseline. Mothers’ perceptions of safety and quality of nursing care were assessed using a separate questionnaire during interviews conducted in the first week of chemotherapy and again at the end of the fourth week. The goal was to find out whether nurses’ interactions with patients in the care process increase their perception of the quality and safety of care in the fourth week compared with the baseline (first week). Longitudinal designs are more robust for measuring variables that change over time compared with cross-sectional studies, which compare different individuals or groups at a single point in time.25

Setting and participants

The study setting was the oncology wards of hospitals affiliated with Tabriz University of Medical Sciences. Mothers of children who were newly diagnosed with leukaemia and undergoing their first course of chemotherapy were included in the study. The inclusion criteria were having a child aged 6 to 18 years with leukaemia, not having another child with cancer and no history of psychiatric disorder. Based on data from a similar study by Carvalho de Lima et al16 the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 110 people.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited between April 2022 and March 2023. With the assistance of oncologists and nurses, eligible patients were identified, and their mothers were invited to participate in the study after obtaining informed consent.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data

Mothers’ demographic data (age, marital status, education level, number of family members and economic status) and the children’s clinical characteristics (gender, age and leukaemia type) were collected through interviews and a review of medical records.

Perceptions of safety and quality of nursing care scale

Mothers’ perceptions of key nursing safety practices were assessed using a 28-item questionnaire developed by Carvalho de Lima et al (online supplemental file 2).16 Safety and quality of nursing care mean preventing all avoidable harm—whether intentional or unintentional—to patients. This includes mitigating risks such as medication errors, diagnostic inaccuracies, healthcare-associated infections or other failures that could lead to physical or psychological injury or death.

After obtaining permission to use the developed tool, the scale was translated into Persian by two bilingual experts. The linguistic/cultural adaptation of the translated version was then reviewed and confirmed by six faculty members at the School of Nursing and Midwifery of Tabriz. Based on their recommendations, the original binary (yes/no) response format was modified to a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from 0=never to 4=always) for enhancing measurement sensitivity.

Given that the original scale was developed based on safety and quality care guidelines in Brazil, we compared its items with the standards issued by Iran’s Ministry of Health for educational and medical centres. This step was crucial to ensure conceptual alignment with Iran’s healthcare framework and to address potential differences in contextual adequacy between the two countries. The evaluation confirmed that the scale’s content coverage was fully consistent with Iran’s national guidelines, thereby supporting its content equivalence for this study.

The scale comprised five subscales: patient identification (4 items), hand hygiene (3 items), fall prevention (10 items), skin damage prevention (6 items), and medication administration (5 items). In Carvalho de Lima et al.’s study,16 the face and content validity of the scale had been previously confirmed by four experts. In this study, the face validity of the adapted scale was subsequently confirmed by 20 mothers of hospitalised children at Tabriz Children’s Hospital. Internal consistency reliability, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.81 for the overall scale and ranged from 0.61 to 0.85 for the subscales.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V. 24. The analytical plan was structured around the study’s research questions as follows. Paired t-tests were employed to assess whether there was a statistically significant difference in mothers’ perception scores over time at both the total scale and subscale levels, comparing week 1 and week 4. To examine differences in perception scores among demographic subgroups, independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were applied depending on the number of groups within each demographic category (eg, education levels and economic status). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate associations between continuous background variables such as mothers’ age, child’s age and family size with perception scores. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

This study was conducted on 110 mothers of children and adolescents with leukaemia hospitalised for chemotherapy for the first time. The average age of the mothers was 37.46±6.65 years (table 1). Most mothers (44%) held a diploma, and 85% were married and living with their spouses. The majority (63%) reported a middle-level economic status, and the average number of family members was 4. The children’s average age was 11.61±3.51 years, with most (77%) diagnosed with ALL. Slightly over half the patients were girls (51%).

Discussion

This longitudinal study aimed to determine the perception of safety and quality of nursing care among mothers with children suffering from leukaemia undergoing their first course of chemotherapy, both in the first and fourth weeks of the chemotherapy period. Our findings revealed that mothers, during the first week, had a moderate perception of the safety and quality of the nursing care they received. In line with this, a study by Gholami et al20 aimed at assessing the quality of nursing care showed that, from the patients’ perspective, the quality of nursing care falls into the ‘moderate’ category. Both studies were conducted within Iran’s healthcare system, which faces common systemic challenges, including low nurse-to-patient ratios, an overemphasis on technical tasks at the expense of holistic care, and performance evaluation systems that prioritise quantitative metrics (eg, the number of procedures performed) over humanistic aspects of care (eg, the quality of interpersonal interactions). However, a study by Evans et al15 contradicted our findings, showing that only about 25% of mothers rated the nursing care they received as ‘good’. This may be due to differences in patient populations. In the study by Evans et al, the mothers studied had children hospitalised in a general paediatric ward, not necessarily children with leukaemia undergoing chemotherapy. Parents of children with chronic or life-threatening illnesses (such as leukaemia) may perceive nursing care differently because of increased emotional stress or expectations of specialised care.

One crucial aspect of mothers’ perception of safety and quality of nursing care was patient identification. The perception of mothers participating in the study in this dimension increased from an average of 2.63 in the first week to 3.04, an increase of approximately 0.41. Although the lowest perception of safety and quality of care was in this dimension, the most significant increase in perception from the first to the fourth week occurred here. Because the process was clearly visible, systematically implemented by nurses and had immediate and serious consequences if an error occurred. Identifying patients accurately during preparation and medication administration is a critical part of the nurse’s role. Incorrect patient identification is associated with adverse events and, consequently, impacts patient safety. In 2007, the WHO emphasised that healthcare institutions and professionals must pay special attention to this issue.26 Regarding the importance of patient identification, Souza et al, in their study aiming to determine the status of patient identification based on WHO guidelines during drug prescription and preparation in a paediatric inpatient unit in Brazil, found that when the nursing team understands the complexities involved in patient identification, medications are more likely to be prepared and administered correctly, thus facilitating patient treatment and reducing the risk of errors.27 Companions of children with cancer in the Carvalho et al study stated that they received poor nursing care in the areas of patient identification, hand hygiene, medication administration, fall prevention and skin damage prevention.16 The findings of Hoffmann et al.’s research on safety incidents reported by relatives of hospitalised children revealed that the incidents were mostly related to medication administration, hand hygiene and environment, patient identification, falls, treatment regimen, access to services, communication between professional caregivers and family members, and care procedures.28

Findings regarding mothers’ perception in the dimension of hand hygiene indicated that their perception of this aspect of care increased by a score of 0.38, from 1.84 to 2.22. Although this showed an upward trend, it remained at a moderate level until the fourth week. Although nurses sometimes perform hand hygiene correctly and at the required times, they occasionally neglect to do so or perform it incompletely due to rushing, forgetfulness or inattention. This inconsistent adherence to handwashing protocols—such as before and after patient contact—leads mothers to perceive little improvement in this practice. One of the recommended measures to reduce healthcare-associated infections is hand hygiene. Poor hand hygiene leads to the death of many hospitalised patients, with 15% of hospitalised patients in Brazil and 10% in the USA and Europe contracting hospital-acquired infections.29 The increased length of hospital stays, increased costs to health systems and increased mortality associated with these infections make hand hygiene a critical issue.30 Unfortunately, although healthcare providers have sufficient perception of the importance of handwashing and are aware of it, this is not reflected in their practice.31

According to the findings of the current study, the perception of mothers participating in the study in the dimension of fall prevention increased from an average of 2.77 in the first week to 2.91, an increase of approximately 0.13, with the least increase in mothers’ perception occurring in this dimension. In this regard, Alsowailmi et al found the incidence of falls among 4860 hospitalised children under 14 years to be 9.9 per 1000 patients. Most falls occurred in boys and in the 1–5 year age group.32 In Gurgel et al.’s study, considering nurses’ performance in fall prevention measures, six actions that were not performed satisfactorily included: daily fall risk assessment, providing appropriate transportation for children less than or equal to 6 months, confirming prescriptions for medications that cause changes in mobility and balance, placing children with a previous history of falls near the nursing station and recording the results of assessments and reporting incidents. Ongoing education, along with the use of specific protocols, is an effective strategy for preventing and reducing risks and injuries, such as falls in healthcare settings.33

The findings regarding mothers’ perception in the dimension of skin damage prevention revealed that their perception of this aspect of care increased from an average of 3.07 in the first week to 3.25 in the fourth week, an increase of approximately 0.18. Children who are hospitalised may experience various types of skin damage resulting from pressure, moisture and friction.34

According to the findings of the present study, mothers’ perception in the dimension of medication administration was higher in the first week than in the other dimensions of safety and quality of nursing care, and the increased score in mothers’ perception in this dimension from the first to the fourth week showed that mothers have a moderate perception in this dimension. Chemotherapy is a high-risk clinical activity, meaning that the potential to harm the patient can be very high. Using standardised protocols is essential to ensure the accurate administration of chemotherapy treatments. In Dedefo et al.’s study, out of 233 patients included, 175 were exposed to medication errors. Of the 1115 medication actions reviewed, 513 medication errors, 75 potential adverse drug events and 17 actual adverse drug events were identified. Of the 17 adverse drug events, eight were preventable while nine were not. Most medication errors were dose errors, followed by wrong drug and wrong time errors.35 Nursing care behaviours are central to the quality of care for patients undergoing complex chemotherapy protocols. However, there is little research on these behaviours. Parents of children with cancer in Khan et al.’s study in Turkey also reported errors related to disease diagnosis, medication administration and patient care methods. The length of stay of children whose families reported safety incidents was, on average, longer than that of children whose families did not report a safety incident.18

Regarding the relationship between background factors and mothers’ perception of safety and quality of care, our results showed a significant relationship between mothers’ education and perception of safety and quality of nursing care. Mothers with academic education had a significantly greater perception of safety and quality of care than other groups, both at the time of admission and discharge. Similarly, in the study by Almis et al36 of 117 children hospitalised, 39 had a fall incident. Analysis of risk factors related to caregivers for falls of hospitalised children showed that lower education levels of caregivers were associated with a higher risk of falls for hospitalised children.

In addition, mothers with good economic status had a higher perception of safety and quality compared with the moderate and weak economic groups, both in the first and fourth weeks. In this regard, the study by Motlagh et al37 demonstrated that income from work dropped to the lowest level around the time of a cancer diagnosis. Also, it has been noted that childhood cancer is implicated in a family’s financial difficulties and its effects.38

One possible reason for errors in children’s care may be the failure of healthcare professionals to listen to them. Knowing the perceptions, opinions and satisfaction of users regarding the quality and safety of care can become a management tool and can be used in planning and improving health measures.16 This information increases the knowledge of oncology nurses’ care and ultimately helps to facilitate the planning of nursing care based on the priorities of care behaviours identified by patients.39

The first step in providing quality services to patients and gaining their satisfaction is to understand the dimensions of service quality and the importance of each of these dimensions to the patient, as well as to analyse their expectations and perceptions regarding each of these dimensions. One of the most basic and important goals in providing health and medical services is to prevent harm to the patient and endanger their safety as a result of providing health services. Unsafe services have unpleasant consequences for the patient and their family, cause psychological stress on healthcare system employees and members of society, and impose a huge economic burden on the healthcare system and society.40

Study limitations

Despite the strengths of using a validated instrument to assess mothers’ perceptions of nursing safety practices, a major limitation of this study must be acknowledged. The questionnaire primarily focused on a narrow range of observable nursing behaviours, specifically patient identification, hand hygiene, medication administration, fall prevention and skin care, and did not capture the broader, more complex dimensions of parental perceptions of safety and quality. Important aspects such as emotional support, cognitive understanding, communication quality and holistic care experiences were beyond the scope of this tool. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within this limited context, and their generalisability to overall parental perception of nursing care safety and quality is restricted. This limitation underscores the need for the development and use of more comprehensive assessment tools that encompass these broader constructs. Accordingly, the results of this study reflect only the spectrum of observable nursing practices.

This study was a small-scale survey conducted among mothers of children with leukaemia in hospitals affiliated with Tabriz University of Medical Sciences. Therefore, the perception captured does not represent those of mothers receiving care at other cancer treatment centres. Additionally, since the study was conducted in public hospitals where most participants were from low- to middle-income groups; therefore, our findings should be generalised with caution to patients admitted to private hospitals. Finally, although the longitudinal design of this study allowed for the examination of changes in perception over time, its observational nature limits the ability to draw definitive cause-and-effect conclusions between variables. Future research is recommended to explore perceptions among mothers of children with other common childhood cancers, such as brain tumours and lymphoma.

Conclusion

The present study showed that most mothers had a moderate perception of the safety and quality of nursing care received during their first hospitalisation period. Mothers’ perception of safety and quality of nursing care increased from the first to the fourth week of chemotherapy. The findings of this study highlight several actionable strategies to enhance the safety and quality of nursing care from the perspective of mothers of children with leukaemia. To address the moderate initial perceptions of care, structured interventions such as comprehensive orientation programmes for families at admission (including safety protocols, chemotherapy side effects and nursing roles) should be implemented. Over time, maintaining consistent patient-centred communication through weekly feedback sessions can sustain and improve satisfaction levels. An interdisciplinary approach is crucial, involving physicians, nurses, psychologists and social workers in joint family education sessions to align expectations and address anxiety, thereby indirectly improving perceived care quality. Targeted nurse training programmes (such as simulation-based safety drills) and (checklist systems) for medication administration and patient identification can reduce errors and build trust. Hospitals should adopt systematic feedback mechanisms, including real-time digital surveys (eg, mobile apps or kiosks) and unit-specific quarterly evaluations, to capture patient and family concerns. A dedicated quality improvement committee should analyse this feedback to revise policies (eg, staffing ratios) and design new services (eg, parental counselling). Additionally, a transparent complaint resolution system with tracked responses and root-cause analysis of recurring issues can ensure accountability.
