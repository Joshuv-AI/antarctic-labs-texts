Thumb Duplication (Wassel Type IIB): A Rare Case Report Highlighting Surgical Strategy and Functional Outcomes
Authors: Maha D. Hanawi, Kayan F. Alotaibi, Rakan H. Alelyani, Abdulrahman I. Alhadlaq, Ovais Habib, Shabeer A. Wani, Loai A. Alsalmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Polydactyly occurs in 1 in 1000 live births and is one of the most common congenital anomalies of the hands and feet, according to a thorough literature review. It may be an isolated anomaly, part of a syndromic condition, or associated with other congenital disorders. Clinical presentation can range from a fully functional extra digit to a small, underdeveloped appendage. Different genetic and embryological factors affect limb development, resulting in diverse phenotypic presentations in affected individuals. We present a rare case of type IIB radial polydactyly, discuss its treatment and complexities, and emphasize the need for more research and clinical reporting.

Polydactyly is a congenital malformation characterized by supernumerary digits on the hands or feet. It may present as an isolated anomaly, in association with other congenital disorders, or as a feature of a syndromic condition.1 Clinical presentation exhibits considerable variability, ranging from a well-developed, fully functional extra digit to a small, underdeveloped appendage. The degree of formation can differ based on underlying genetic and embryological factors that influence limb development, contributing to the diverse phenotypic presentations observed in affected individuals.1

Hand polydactyly is classified into 3 distinct types: preaxial, central, and postaxial.2 Preaxial polydactyly, also referred to as radial polydactyly, involves duplication of the thumb; postaxial polydactyly, or ulnar polydactyly, is characterized by an extra digit on the ulnar aspect of the hand; and central polydactyly, the least common subtype, involves duplication of one of the central digits, typically affecting the index, middle, or ring finger.3 Radial polydactyly most commonly manifests as an isolated congenital anomaly; however, it can also be associated with other congenital abnormalities affecting the hands and feet or occur as a feature of a syndromic condition, such as Werner mesomelic syndrome.4 From a genetic perspective, preaxial polydactyly may arise sporadically; however, it is more frequently inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. Research indicates that preaxial polydactyly types II and III are associated with a gene located on chromosome 7q36.5

A widely used classification system for preaxial polydactyly is the Wassel classification, which is the universal standard for classifying thumb duplication, first introduced by Wassel in 1969. This system was developed based on the patient series reported by Flatt.2

A thorough review of the literature identified that polydactyly is among the most frequently occurring congenital anomalies of the hands and feet, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 1000 live births.1 The Wassel type II thumb duplication is the second most prevalent type, observed in approximately 20% of cases.3 However, subtype IIB represents a rare form of radial polydactyly characterized by symphalangism, accounting for only 3% of thumb polydactyly cases.6 Due to its rarity, type IIB remains less documented in the literature, which calls for further study and clinical reporting. This case report presents and discusses the management of a rare case of type IIB radial polydactyly, highlighting the complexities and factors influencing the treatment approach.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 15-year-old medically fit male patient presented to our clinic with a right thumb duplication at the level of the interphalangeal (IP) joint. The patient had not sought medical attention previously, but at the presentation, he was asking for correction. Physical examination showed symphalangism of the radial digit, an absent IP joint crease, and joint stiffness. However, there was an IP joint crease and intact passive and mildly active flexion of the ulnar digit (Fig. 1). An x-ray showed fusion between the radial aspects of the proximal and the distal phalanges (Fig. 2).

Intraoperative photograph of the right hand in dorsal view showing a duplicated thumb.

Preoperative radiograph of the right hand in oblique view showing type IIB radial polydactyly.

After securing consent from the patient’s parent, we took the patient to the operating room. We decided to excise the radial digit, given that the ulnar digit was functional. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays a schematic highlighting the cartilage preservation and resection planes: modified procedure with preservation of the cartilage on the radial side of the proximal phalanx to enhance the stability of the IP joint, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E667.) We marked a tennis racquet incision over the metacarpophalangeal joint. A novel technique was performed by excising the radial digit while preserving the cartilage on the radial side of the proximal phalanx to enhance the stability of the IP joint (Fig. 3), and a K-wire was inserted to centralize the ulnar digit. Four weeks later, the K-wire was removed, and the patient began physiotherapy (Fig. 4). At 5 months postoperatively, the patient was doing fine and was happy about the result, with no issues. Furthermore, the patient’s postoperative thumb functional status includes full passive IP joint flexion, active flexion of about 90 degrees, full extension, slight residual angular ulnar deviation, and the ability to perform a lateral pinch grip.

DISCUSSION

Although minor complications may arise following thumb polydactyly reconstruction, the operated thumb achieves acceptable aesthetic and functional outcomes when surgical guidelines are meticulously followed. In this case, the patient expressed satisfaction with the procedure, and the surgical outcome seemed to meet both patient and parental expectations, given that the primary concern was the removal of the supernumerary digit.

The Wassel classification was used for clinical categorization and surgical planning. Our case was classified as Wassel type IIB, characterized by cartilaginous fusion between the distal phalanx of the radial thumb and the head of the proximal phalanx.6

Several surgical approaches can be used for thumb polydactyly reconstruction, all of which should ultimately ensure sufficient joint stability, strength, and mobility, while ensuring a satisfactory aesthetic outcome.7 The Bilhaut-Cloquet procedure is one of the widely suggested methods, especially recommended for Wassel type I and II thumbs with a bifid distal phalanx. This procedure involves the fusion of equal portions of bone, soft tissue, and nail tissue following excision of the central duplicated segment of the thumb.1

In a case series involving 34 patients, 2 of the patients who underwent the Bilhaut-Cloquet procedure demonstrated joint stability. This outcome is attributed to the preservation of both the radial and ulnar collateral ligaments of the IP joint.8 However, the outcomes of the Bilhaut-Cloquet procedure remain variable, as the technique exhibits significant technical challenges in combining structures of both thumbs. Over time, the reconstructed thumb is susceptible to complications such as physeal growth disturbances, joint stiffness, and nail plate abnormalities.9 To address the associated complications, a modified Bilhaut-Cloquet technique was introduced in 2007 by Goo Hyun.10 This procedure has been demonstrated to be an effective surgical approach for the management of type II and III thumb polydactyly, offering the advantages of preserving IP joint mobility, mitigating the risk of physeal growth arrest, and reducing the incidence of nail deformities.11 A limitation of using this technique is its applicability to cases with asymmetrical thumbs and malformed or unstable IP joints, and potential postoperative complications such as nail deformities. Therefore, a symmetrical duplication is a prerequisite for its use.11 As a result, this technique is not the preferred option for our case.

In this case, the supernumerary radial digit was excised while preserving the cartilage on the radial side of the proximal phalanx to enhance the stability of the IP joint. A K-wire was inserted to centralize the ulnar digit, which was removed later on. Following the procedure, the patient began physiotherapy without complications. The outcome was favorable, with the patient demonstrating good functional recovery, stable joint motion, and satisfactory aesthetic results. The IP joint maintained stability, and no significant deformities or issues were observed in the nail plate.

CONCLUSIONS

This case highlighted the successful surgical management of a 15-year-old male patient with right thumb duplication, classified as Wassel type IIB. Through a novel surgical technique that involved excising the radial digit while preserving the cartilage on the radial side of the proximal phalanx, we achieved enhanced stability of the IP joint. Further research and case studies are warranted to refine surgical techniques and improve patient satisfaction in similar cases.
