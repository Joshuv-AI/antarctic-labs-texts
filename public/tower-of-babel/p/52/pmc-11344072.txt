Effects of Limosilactobacillus reuteri strains PTA-126787 and PTA-126788 on intestinal barrier integrity and immune homeostasis in an alcohol-induced leaky gut model
Authors: Dharanesh Gangaiah, Min Gu, Aline Zaparte, Olaf Will, Laurie C Dolan, Anthony Goering, Jason Pillai, Shrinivasrao P Mane, German Plata, Emily B Helmes, David A Welsh, Arvind Kumar Mahajan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntestinal barrier is a first line of defense that prevents entry of various harmful substances from the lumen into the systemic environment. Impaired barrier function with consequent translocation of harmful substances into systemic circulation (“leaky gut”) is a central theme in many gastrointestinal, autoimmune, mental, and metabolic diseases. Probiotics have emerged as a promising strategy to maintain intestinal integrity and address “leaky gut”. Using in silico, in vitro and avian in vivo analyses, we previously showed that two novel L. reuteri strains, PTA-126787 (L. reuteri 3630) and PTA-126788 (L. reuteri 3632), isolated from broiler chickens possess favorable safety profiles. Consistent with a recent study, here we show that L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 are phylogenetically similar to human L. reuteri strains. Daily administration of high doses of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 to Sprague Dawley rats for 28 days was found to be safe with no adverse effects. More importantly, administration of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 significantly reduced markers associated with alcohol-induced leaky gut, by downregulating inflammatory cytokines and upregulating anti-inflammatory cytokines in an alcohol model of leaky gut in mice. While L. reuteri 3630 cells and supernatant showed no activation, L. reuteri 3632 cells but not supernatant showed activation of AhR, a key transcription factor that regulates gut and immune homeostasis. L. reuteri 3630 is creamish white in morphology typical of Lactobacillus species and L. reuteri 3632 displays a unique orange pigmentation, which was stable even after passaging for 480 generations. We identified a rare polyketide biosynthetic gene cluster in L. reuteri 3632 that likely encodes for the orange-pigmented secondary metabolite. Similar to L. reuteri 3632 cells, the purified orange metabolite activated AhR. All together, these data provide evidence on the phylogenetic relatedness, safety, efficacy, and one of the likely mechanisms of action of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 for potential probiotic applications to address “leaky gut” and associated pathologies in humans.Subject terms: Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Intestinal barrier, a semipermeable structure that lines the luminal surface of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract plays key role in maintaining homeostasis along the GI tract1. In addition to allowing for discrimination of self from non-self and facilitating selective transport of nutrients, water, and electrolytes, intestinal barrier serves as a front line of defense against injurious factors such as pathogens and toxins preventing their diffusion from the gastrointestinal lumen into the tissue and subsequently into systemic circulation2,3. This important function of the multifunctional epithelial monolayer is commonly referred to as “barrier function”2,3. Impaired intestinal barrier function results in increased permeability to allergens, pathogens, toxins, and microbiota-derived inflammatory mediators such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), leading to “leaky gut”4,5. Leaky gut is associated with a variety of gastrointestinal diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), celiac disease and colon cancer6,7. Piling evidence suggests that leaky gut predisposes to multiple autoimmune, mental, and metabolic diseases, including schizophrenia, autism, diabetes, obesity, heart disease and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease6,8–17. Probiotics targeting intestinal structure and functionality offer promising solutions to mitigate gut leakiness and the consequent diseases18–21.

Species belonging to Lactobacillus are among the most used probiotic species in both human and animal health22. First isolated in 1962, L. reuteri is one of the most widely studied Lactobacillus species with a long history of safe use and wide-ranging health benefits22. L. reuteri naturally occurs in several foods (e.g., milk, cheese, sourdough) as well as in the GI tract of a variety of vertebrates and is considered as a truly indigenous bacteria of the human GI tract22,23. The L. reuteri species is recognized as safe based on submissions made to the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), inclusion in the European Food Safety Agency (EFSA) BIOHAZ Panel Qualified Presumption of Safety (QPS) List, and incorporation in the Health Canada Probiotics Monograph. At least five L. reuteri strains have been successfully notified to FDA as Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) substances for use in human food or as New Dietary Ingredients (NDI) for use in dietary supplements. L. reuteri strains have been evaluated for safety in adults, children, infants and even in immunocompromised population and found to be well tolerated, safe and efficacious at doses as high as 1010 colony forming units (CFUs)/day22,24–28. As a probiotic, L. reuteri has been shown to confer wide-ranging benefits, including promoting overall health, reducing infections, improving feed tolerance, enhancing nutrient absorption, modulating host immune response, promoting gut mucosal integrity and preventing translocation of inflammatory mediators into systemic circulation22,29–35.

L. reuteri is known for its ability to enhance gut barrier function22,36,37. For example, L. reuteri prevents bacterial translocation from the GI tract to the mesenteric lymph nodes in a DSS-induced colitis model38. Treatment with a mixture of probiotics containing L. reuteri increased tight junction proteins in intestinal epithelial cells, reduced LPS translocation and attenuated disease in lupus-prone mice39. L. reuteri protects the colon from Salmonella Typhimurium infection by enhancing gut microbiota stability and barrier integrity and reducing inflammation40. In another study, L. reuteri was shown to be effective in maintaining intestinal epithelial regeneration and homeostasis as well as at repairing intestinal damage after pathological injury41. Similar roles for L. reuteri in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity have also been shown in pigs42,43. Human studies with L. reuteri DSM 12246 significantly reduced the frequency of GI symptoms, suggesting potential ability of L. reuteri to reverse leaky gut44–46.

L. reuteri strains produce several metabolites that contribute to host-microbe crosstalk for maintenance of gut and immune homeostasis47,48. For example, L. reuteri strains R21c and 2010 are known to produce a secondary metabolite that activates aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and is important for intraspecies antagonism47,49. AhR is a ligand-activated transcription factor that plays a key role in regulating immunity, inflammation, and gut barrier functions50–56. Inefficient activation of AhR by inadequate AhR ligands leads to increased intestinal inflammation and susceptibility to infection and AhR activation has been shown to protect against a variety of diseases, including colitis50–56. AhR activation is needed for IL-22 production; a deficient IL-22 production is believed to be one of the underlying mechanisms for increased inflammation in AhR deficiency53.

We previously showed that two novel L. reuteri isolates, ATCC PTA‐126,787 (L. reuteri 3630) and ATCC PTA‐126,788 (L. reuteri 3632), possess favorable safety properties based on the results from in silico, in vitro and in vivo analyses57. In this study, we evaluated L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 for: 1) phylogenetic relatedness to human L. reuteri strains, 2) safety in a mammalian species using a 28-day OECD Guideline 407 toxicity study in rats, 3) efficacy against alcohol-induced leaky gut in mice, and 4) potential underlying mechanisms of effect against leaky gut using in vitro and in silico approaches.

Results

L. reuteri strains 3630 and 3632 are phylogenetically similar to human L. reuteri strains

L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 were originally isolated from poultry57. A recent comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of 94 L. reuteri strains isolated from fresh feces or gut samples of multiple vertebrate hosts (n = 19 from poultry, n = 32 from nonhuman primates, n = 23 from rodents, and n = 20 from humans) along with 88 L. reuteri genomes available in public databases showed that L. reuteri from poultry and humans cluster together in a lineage58. Phylogenetic analysis of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 with 182 L. reuteri strains from different host origin reported in the above study showed that L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 cluster with human L. reuteri strains in lineage VI (Fig. 1). L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 showed closest relatedness to commercial L. reuteri strains SD2112 and RC-14 (Fig. 1).

L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 showed no adverse effects in a 28-day repeat dose rat toxicity study

As L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 showed phylogenetic similarity with human L. reuteri strains, we next evaluated the safety of the two strains in a mammalian species using a 28-day OECD Guideline 407 toxicity study in rats. In this study, 80 rats were administered with both strains together (referred to as “test substance” here afterwards) by oral gavage at three different doses. Group 1 served as a control and was administered with normal saline. Group 2, Group 3, and Group 4 were administered with 1.7E + 10 CFUs/kg body weight (BW)/day, 3.3E + 10 CFUs/kg BW/day and 1.0E + 11 CFUs/kg BW/day of 1:1 mixture of the two L. reuteri strains, respectively. The results showed that there were no test substance-related deaths or clinical observations in rats. There were no effects of the test material on body weight (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2) or body weight gain (Supplementary Table 1) of either sex. There was no effect of the test articles on food consumption other than a significant increase in Group 4 males from Days 15–22 (Supplementary Table 2). The results also showed that there were no test substance-related adverse effects on clinical chemistry (Table 1), blood clotting (Supplementary Table 3), and urinalysis (Supplementary Table 4). Organ weights were not affected by administration of the test substance, except for a significant increase in the spleen to body weight ratio in Group 2 males (Supplementary Table 5). Results of the translocation study showed a low incidence of bacteria in blood of 3 out of 40 animals (one from the female control, one low dose male and one high dose male) (Supplementary Table 6). The findings were not dose-dependent and may have resulted from contamination during necropsy. Because pathological findings or other treatment related toxicity were not observed in these animals, the translocation findings in the blood do not indicate that test strains are harmful. There was no statistically significant effect of any dose of the test material on bacterial translocation to the mesentery or liver of male or female rats (Supplementary Tables 7, 8, 9 and 10). More details on the results of rat toxicity study can be found in Supplementary Data under “Supplementary Results”.

Treatment with L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 reduced alcohol-induced leaky gut in mice

L. reuteri is known for its ability to enhance gut barrier function and protect against leaky gut22,36–46. Alcohol induces dramatic alterations in gut microbiota composition, impairs intestinal epithelial barrier and triggers inflammation, causing leaky gut59–61. Hence, we used a binge-on-chronic alcohol model of mice for evaluating L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 for potential efficacy against leaky gut.

To this effect, as a marker of leaky gut, we measured the translocation of orally gavaged fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran into systemic circulation. As expected, AF (Alcohol fed) group showed a significant higher concentration of FITC-dextran in serum compared to PF group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). While there was no significant difference in the FITC-dextran levels in the serum between AFBP (Alcohol fed and treated with L. reuteri 3630 and 3632) and PF (Pair fed) groups (P > 0.5), treatment with L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 (AFBP) significantly reduced FITC-dextran levels in serum compared to AF group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2A).

In addition, we also quantified serum intestinal fatty acid binding protein (iFABP), which is another marker of intestinal wall integrity. As expected, iFABP levels significantly increased in AF group compared to PF group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2B). While there was no significant difference in the iFABP levels between AFBP and PF groups (P > 0.5), treatment with L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 (AFBP) significantly reduced iFABP levels in serum compared to AF group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2B).

Treatment with L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 reduced alcohol-induced inflammatory response in mice

We also evaluated the effect of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 on intestinal inflammatory response by measuring cytokines from intestinal samples using qRT-PCR. As expected, the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9; a key pathogenic factor known to induce increase in intestinal permeability62) were significantly increased in AF group compared to PF group (IL-1β, P < 0.01; IL-6, P < 0.0001; TNF-α, not significant but trended towards significance; MMP-9, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C–F). The proinflammatory cytokines IFN-γ showed significant increase (P < 0.05) and TGF-β showed trend towards increase in AF group compared to PF group (Fig. 2G,H). While there was no significant difference in the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α and MMP-9 between AFBP and PF groups (P > 0.5), treatment with L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 (AFBP) significantly reduced the levels of IL-1β (P < 0.001), IL-6 (P < 0.0001), TNF-α (P < 0.05) and MMP-9 (P < 0.01) compared to AF group (Fig. 2C–F). While there was no significant difference in the levels IFN-γ and TGF-β between AFBP and PF groups, treatment with L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 (AFBP) showed a trend towards reduced levels of IFN-γ and TGF-β levels in AFBP group compared to AF group (Fig. 2G,H).

Similarly, the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-22 significantly decreased in AF group compared to PF group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2I). The level of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 did not statistically differ between groups (Fig. 2J). Treatment with L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 (AFBP) significantly increased the levels of IL-22 compared to AF group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2I) and PF group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2I).

L. reuteri 3632 whole cells, but not cell-free supernatant, activate mammalian AhR

AhR plays a key role in regulating immunity, inflammation, and gut barrier functions50–56. AhR activation is also needed for IL-22 production; a deficient IL-22 production is believed to be one of the underlying mechanisms for increased inflammation in AhR deficiency53. As L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 reduced alcohol-induced leaky gut and associated inflammatory response, and increased IL-22 expression, we hypothesized that the L. reuteri strains likely mediate these effects via activating AhR. We tested our strains for their ability to activate AhR using HepG2-Lucia™ AhR reporter cells. As shown in Fig. 3, the overnight grown L. reuteri 3632 whole cells, but not L. reuteri 3630 whole cells, activated AhR compared to media control. In contrast, cell-free bacterial supernatants from both L. reuteri 3632 and 3630 showed no activation of AhR (Fig. 3).

In silico analysis of L. reuteri 3632 and 3630 genomes and plasmid sequences identified a putative biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) in L. reuteri 3632 related to aryl polyene class

To better understand the underlying mechanisms of efficacy against leaky gut and AhR activation, we performed comparative genome analysis of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 with other human L. reuteri probiotic strains. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis showed that L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 are closest to human L. reuteri strain DSM17938 and its parent SD2112 (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Comparative genomics analyses of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 with other L. reuteri strains DSM17938, SD2112 (parent of DSM17938), DSM20016, ATCC53608, CF48-3A and CECT5711 as well as L. rhamnosus GG identified 12 unique genes in L. reuteri 3630 and 178 unique genes in L. reuteri 3632 (Supplementary Fig. 3B and 3C). Most of the unique genes in L. reuteri 3630 were annotated as hypothetical proteins with no known function. Analysis of the unique genes present in L. reuteri 3632 identified several genes that seemed to be part of two BGCs involved in the production of a secondary metabolite belonging to polyene class and bacteriocin belonging to the mersacidin class (Supplementary Fig. 3D).

For a deeper understanding of the BGC-related unique genes present in L. reuteri 3632, we set out to identify all the BGCs present in the two L. reuteri strains by comparative analysis with BGCs present in 206 publicly available L. reuteri genomes. Comparative genomics analysis identified 355 BGCs and grouped them into 20 distinct BGC families (Supplementary Table 11). Consistent with the above data, while absent in L. reuteri 3630, L. reuteri 3632 possessed two rare intact BGCs: 1) A PKS BGC with homology to the aryl-polyene class present in 3 out of 208 strains, including L. reuteri isolates R2lc and 201047,49, and 2) A two-component bacteriocin most closely related to carnolysin/ mersacidin that is present in 2 out of 208 strains.

L. reuteri strains R21c and 2010 have been previously shown to produce an orange-pigmented secondary metabolite that activates AhR and is important for intraspecies antagonism47,49. The authors associated orange pigmentation and AhR activation to a polyketide (PKS) BGC located on a 21.4-kb plasmid47,49. While L. reuteri 3630 is creamish white in color (typical of strains belonging to Lactobacillus species), L. reuteri 3632 displays a distinct orange to yellow pigmentation similar to R21c and 2010 (Fig. 4A). Further sequence analysis confirmed that the PKS BGC present in L. reuteri 3632 is identical to the PKS BGC present in R2lc previously described by Özçam et al.47,49. Contrary to R21c, where the PKS BGC is located on a 21.4-kb plasmid, the PKS BGC in L. reuteri 3632 is located on a 165.9-kb plasmid with 34.3% GC content (Fig. 4B,C)4,47.

Putative structure of the orange pigmented PKS metabolite

Aryl-polyenes are one of the most common and widespread class of specialized metabolites produced by bacteria63. To better understand its role in host-microbiota crosstalk, we set out to isolate and characterize the structure of the orange pigmented polyene metabolite. Adapting methods that have been previously used to isolate aryl polyenes, we extracted and purified the pigment from saponified cellular material and then fractionated on a normal-phase silica-gel column followed by reversed-phase using Diaion HP-20 resin. The structure of the purified pigment was analyzed using NMR and HRMS. Dodecapentaenoic acid (DDPA), a 12-carbon polyunsaturated carboxylic acid, was identified as the putative structure of the orange-pigmented PKS metabolite (Fig. 4D). Given that the PKS BGC has been associated with the orange pigmented secondary metabolite in R21c and both L. reuteri 3632 and R21c are orange pigmented and contain identical PKS BGCs, we propose that DDPA is likely the product of the PKS BGC from L. reuteri 3632. DDPA is novel as a natural product produced by bacteria, however the compound has been previously described as an intermediate in the biosynthesis of fumagillin64. The NMR and MS results of DDPA were consistent with the description of the fumagillin intermediary compound.

The purified PKS metabolite activates AhR similar to L. reuteri 3632 whole cells

As shown in Fig. 3, the purified PKS metabolite from L. reuteri 3632 significantly activated AhR compared to media control and the activation was comparable to that of L. reuteri 3632 whole cells.

Proposed biosynthesis of PKS metabolite

We propose a biosynthesis based on the structure of DDPA and analysis of the genes in the PKS BGC. We were able to propose a role for each gene with predicted biosynthetic function (Fig. 4E; Table 2). The biosynthesis of DDPA likely follows the standard mechanisms of iterative polyketide synthesis that involves chain extension, reduction, and dehydration to form aryl polyene chain (Fig. 4E). The BGC encodes a multi-subunit biotin-dependent acetyl-CoA caryboxylase that we propose biosynthesizes malonate monomers that are iteratively incorporated into the natural product. A biosynthetic domain capable of terminating the biosynthesis of the polyketide such as thioesterase or reductase is notably absent from the BGC. This thioesterase function may be performed instead by a glyoxalase-like enzyme encoded by pksG (Fig. 4E). The BGC also does not encode a chain-length factor protein, which is found in many aryl polyene BGCs.

Discussion

Impaired intestinal barrier function is a common theme in many gastrointestinal, autoimmune, mental, and metabolic diseases, including IBD, celiac disease, colon cancer, schizophrenia, autism, diabetes, obesity, heart disease and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease6–17. Precision probiotics with targeted effect on intestinal integrity have emerged as one promising strategy to maintain gut health homeostasis, primarily by attenuating inflammation and strengthening intestinal barrier function. We previously showed that both L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 possess favorable safety profiles based on the results from in silico, in vitro and in vivo analyses in chickens57. Here, using phylogenetic analyses, we showed that L. reuteri strains 3630 and 3632 cluster with human L. reuteri strains. In a 28-day OECD Guideline 407 toxicity study, rats tolerated high levels of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 (3.65E + 10 CFUs combined/kg BW/day) over a period of 28 days with no adverse effects. Oral administration of the two strains in 1:1 ratio prevented gut leakiness, reduced proinflammatory cytokines and increased anti-inflammatory cytokines in a binge-on-chronic alcohol model of leaky gut in mice. While L. reuteri 3630 whole cells and cell-free supernatant showed no activation, L. reuteri 3632 whole cells but not cell-free supernatant showed activation of AhR, which is a key transcription factor that regulates gut and immune homeostasis. Morphological analysis showed that L. reuteri 3630 is creamish white, which is typical of Lactobacillus species, and L. reuteri 3632 displays a unique orange pigmentation, which was found to be stable even after passaging for 480 generations. Using in silico approaches, we identified a rare polyketide BGC in L. reuteri 3632 that likely encodes for the orange-pigmented secondary metabolite and showed that, similar to L. reuteri 3632 whole cells, the purified, orange-pigmented metabolite activates AhR.

Compared to allochthonous probiotics, autochthonous (native) probiotics are believed to be more effective as they are better adapted to the gut environment. A recent comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of 94 L. reuteri strains isolated from fresh feces or gut samples of multiple vertebrate hosts (n = 19 from poultry, n = 32 from nonhuman primates, n = 23 from rodents, and n = 20 from humans) along with 88 L. reuteri genomes available in public databases showed that L. reuteri from poultry and humans cluster together in a lineage58. Based on extensive phylogenetic and gene content analyses, the authors concluded that the human strains (lineage VI) do not differ in their lifestyle or ecological strategy from those occurring in poultry and that these strains likely were acquired from domestic poultry or rodents58. L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 were isolated from poultry57. Phylogenetic analysis of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 with 180 L. reuteri strains from different host origin reported in the above study showed that our strains cluster with human L. reuteri strains in lineage VI. These data suggest that L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 are phylogenetically similar to human L. reuteri strains.

Given their phylogenetic relatedness to human L. reuteri strains, we evaluated L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 for safety in a mammalian species. When administered in combination to Sprague Dawley rats over a period of 28 days, the strains did not show evidence of toxicity or apparent pathogenicity. There was no effect of any dose of the test material on clinical condition, body weight, food consumption, clinical chemistry, hematology, urinalysis, or organ weights. Findings, morphologies, severities observed, and incidences of microscopic changes are consistent with those expected in untreated rats of this age, sex, and strain65. The mild hepatic vacuolization observed in one Group 4 male is considered to be adaptive and not adverse in the absence of necrosis66, and the increased creatinine in Group 4 females was not associated with any change in urinalysis or histopathology of the kidney. Under the conditions of the study, the no-observed-adverse-effect-level (NOAEL) for oral administration of the combined test article, L. reuteri 3630 & L. reuteri 3632, is 1.6E + 10 CFUs of L. reuteri 3630/kg BW/day plus 5.7E + 10 CFUs of L. reuteri 3632/kg BW/day for male and female Sprague Dawley rats, the highest doses of each strain administered. These results suggest that rats tolerate high oral levels of L. reuteri 3630 and L. reuteri 3632 over a period of 28 days with no adverse effects.

Despite no adverse effects, dose dependence or correlation with clinical or histopathological findings, administration of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 resulted in a small number of translocated bacteria in rats. Translocation of bacteria to internal tissues of rodents administered high doses of L. reuteri is not a unique finding. A study by Taranto, et al.67 showed translocation of lactobacilli, enterobacteria and anaerobes to liver and spleen in mice provided 1.0E + 6 – 1.0E + 8 CFUs/day of L. reuteri CRL 1098 in drinking water for two days, but not in mice provided 1.0E + 4 CFUs/day for 7 days. The mean numbers of translocated bacteria in the liver in rats receiving L. reuteri 3630 and L. reuteri 3632 are comparable to or lower than those reported by Taranto, et al.67, with the exception of one high dose male that exhibited 147 colonies in the liver (4900 CFUs/g). Uptake of bacteria by Kupffer cells lining the sinusoids of the liver is a normal physiological response during bacterial translocation. Kupffer cells of the liver represent 80–90% of all tissue macrophages and are well known to take up bacteria delivered via the portal circulation where they can help orchestrate tolerance or inflammation68. It is also possible that the results are due to bacterial contamination during sampling. The results are also consistent with the observation that animals that are subject to either physical or chronic psychological stress experience extra intestinal bacterial translocation69,70. The finding of translocated facultative anaerobes across the vehicle control as well as treatment groups is not unexpected in animals subjected to daily gavage over multiple rounds71. Further, the fact that the apparent test strains contributed to a small number of the translocated bacteria is expected based on the high levels of L. reuteri test strains administered by gavage in this study, coupled with evidence of translocation of other Limosilactobacillus/Lactobacillus strains in rodents. These data suggest that L. reuteri 3630 and L. reuteri 3632 are not pathogenic and did not cause toxicity to rats over 28 days of administration by gavage.

In addition to their long history of safe use, strains belonging to L. reuteri are known for their ability to improve gut barrier function. L. reuteri exert these improvements via multiple mechanisms, including modulating immune system, increasing tight junction proteins, improving oxidative stress, producing beneficial metabolites, inhibiting pathogens, and modulating gut microbiota22,36–46. Administration of 1:1 ratio of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 significantly attenuated leaky gut in a binge-on-chronic alcohol model of leaky gut in mice. Treatment with the two strains downregulated several proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ and TGF-β and MMP-9 (one of the key pathogenic factors known to induce increased intestinal permeability62) and upregulated the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-22. Treatment with L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 had no effect on the expression of tight junction proteins, zonulin, occludin and claudin, and markers of oxidative stress, NRF2 and iNOS (data not shown). The two strains produce several metabolites, which likely could improve gut barrier function72. The strains also showed in vitro inhibitory activity against various Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens and this inhibition was only seen when L. reuteri strains were cocultured with pathogens but not with cell-free culture supernatants (data not shown). A role of the two strains in modulating the microbiota to effect barrier functions also cannot be ruled out. Although the protective effect of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 against leaky gut is likely mediated by multiple mechanisms, the data presented here suggest immunomodulation as one key underlying mechanism of efficacy against leaky gut.

AhR is a ligand-activated transcription factor that plays a key role in regulating immunity, inflammation, and gut barrier functions50–56. Inefficient activation of AhR leads to increased intestinal inflammation and susceptibility to infection50–56. AhR activation is needed for IL-22 production; a deficient IL-22 production is believed to be one of the underlying mechanisms for increased inflammation in AhR deficiency53. Treatment with L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 reduced several proinflammatory cytokines and increased the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-22 and protected the mice against leaky gut. L. reuteri 3632 showed activation of AhR in vitro. These data suggest that L. reuteri 3632-mediated AhR activation likely contributes to the observed in vivo efficacy.

L. reuteri strains R21c and 2010 have been previously shown to produce an orange-pigmented secondary metabolite that activates AhR47,49. The authors associated orange pigmentation and AhR activation to a PKS BGC located on a 21.4-kb plasmid47,49. Similar to R21c and 2010, L. reuteri 3632 displays a unique orange pigmentation and showed activation of AhR in vitro. In silico analysis identified a rare PKS BGC, which was found to be identical to the PKS BGC in R21c. The purified orange PKS metabolite from L. reuteri 3632 activated AhR and the level of AhR activation with purified PKS metabolite was comparable to that of L. reuteri 3632 whole cells. In contrast to L. reuteri 3632, L. reuteri 3630 is creamish white in color, showed no activation of AhR and lacked any BGCs similar to PKS BGC present in L. reuteri 3632. Together, these data suggest that the orange pigmented secondary metabolite likely contributes to AhR activation seen in L. reuteri 3632.

Tryptophan metabolites produced via aromatic amino acid amino transferase (AraT) have also been shown to activate AhR48,53,73. L. reuteri 3632 produces several tryptophan metabolites72, which could potentially activate AhR. However, the cell-free supernatant from L. reuteri 3632 resulted in no activation of AhR. Both L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 contain a homolog of AraT and only L. reuteri 3632, but not L. reuteri 3630, activated AhR consistent with the presence of orange pigmented secondary metabolite and PKS BGC. Purified PKS metabolite of L. reuteri 3632 activated AhR similar to L. reuteri 3632 whole cells. Deletion of araT in L. reuteri R21c, which contains a PKS BGC identical to that of L. reuteri 3632 and presents similar orange pigmentation as L. reuteri 3632, had no additional effect on AhR activation47,49. Together, these data suggest that the AhR activation seen with L. reuteri 3632 whole cells is less likely mediated by tryptophan metabolites.

In conclusion, the results presented in this study provide evidence on the phylogenetic relatedness, safety, efficacy, and potential mechanism of action of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632, opening new avenues for further exploring these strains as potential probiotics to address leaky gut and associated pathologies in humans. Future studies will evaluate these candidates for safety and potential beneficial effects in humans. Given that only L. reuteri 3632, but not L. reuteri 3630, was able to activate AhR in vitro, it is plausible that L. reuteri 3632 primarily contributes to the observed in vivo efficacy; studies to delineate the individual contributions of two strains to in vivo efficacy are worth exploring. Although we showed that L. reuteri 3632 activated AhR in vitro, investigating AhR activation in vivo might provide further insights into the mechanisms of action of these probiotic candidates. Future studies will also focus on further defining the contribution of the orange-pigmented secondary metabolite in L. reuteri 3632 to in vivo efficacy. A better understanding of the individual contributions of genes involved in PKS production, through analyses of transcripts, protein, or individual gene deletions and complementation, might open new ways to enhance its production in L. reuteri 3632 as well as facilitate heterologous production of this metabolite in other microbial platforms.

Materials and methods

Phylogenetic analysis

The UBCG274 pipeline was used to build a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree using a recent collection of 182 L. reuteri strains isolated or curated by Li and collaborators from a wide variety of hosts58. UBCG2 uses 81 universal bacterial single copy genes to build the phylogeny. The GTRCAT model in RAxML v 8.2.12 was used to build the final tree75.

Ethics statement

All rats and mice were housed and maintained under the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, NRC, 201176 and all local standard operating procedures. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the institution conducting the study. All in vivo experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Test substance

The test substance used for both rat and mice studies was a blend containing 2.7E + 11 CFUs/g of L. reuteri ATCC PTA-126787 (L. reuteri 3630, lot #201123LRE3630) and 4.3E + 11 CFUs/g of L. reuteri ATCC PTA-126788 (L. reuteri 3632, lot #201123LRE3632) produced at Biosource Cultures and Flavors (Muskego, WI).

Bacterial strains, and culture conditions

For rat and mice studies, the L. reuteri strains in the test material were propagated anaerobically on Lactobacilli de Man Rogosa Sharpe (MRS, BD Difco) medium at 37 °C and identified as L. reuteri according to Gangaiah et al.57. Samples of the neat test article were collected each week for rat study and Day 1 and Day 10 for mice study to evaluate stability and for enumeration of CFUs. Samples were also collected from the dose formulations to verify homogeneity on Day 1, and dose concentration every week for rats and on Day 10 for mice. The two strains were differentiated based on the colony morphology and color. L. reuteri 3632 produces orange to yellow-colored colonies and L. reuteri 3630 produces creamish white colonies.

For isolation of PKS metabolite, L. reuteri 3632 seed culture was grown anaerobically from glycerol stock in 3 mL of commercial MRS broth (BD Difco) for 2 days with no agitation and light. Seed culture was used to inoculate modified MRS broth consisting of glucose at 20 g/L, vegetable proteose peptone (Sigma-Aldrich, Prod. No. 29185) at 10 g/L, yeast extract at 5 g/L, C2H3NaO2 at 3.01 g/L, K2HPO4 at 2 g/L, MgSO4 · 7H2O at 0.2 g/L, and MnSO4 · H2O at 0.05 g/L. Glucose was combined with reverse osmosis (RO) water, filter sterilized, and added after autoclaving media to prevent caramelization. Immediately after sterilization, media was flushed with an anaerobic mixture of analytical grade N2 (85%)/CO2 (10%)/H2 (5%) (AirGas Part No. X03NI85C2001383) at a rate of 5 psi for 15–20 min with constant agitation to decrease dissolved oxygen and increase amount of dissolved anaerobic gas mixture.

Rats used in the study

Rats used in the study were CRL Sprague–Dawley CD® IGS Rats, obtained from Charles River Laboratories, Inc., Raleigh, NC. The animals were acclimated for 19 days prior to testing and were approximately nine weeks of age at study initiation. Animals were housed 2 per cage. The animal room was maintained under a 12 h light/dark cycle. Temperature and relative humidity were 20–22 °C and 43–58%, respectively. Rats had ad libitum access to basal diet (2016 Certified Teklad Rodent Diet® from Envigo Teklad, Inc.) and filtered tap water except for the day before study termination, when food was withdrawn. Animals were selected for use in the study based on lack of clinical signs of disease or injury, adequate body weight gain, and a body weight within ± 20% of the mean within a sex. Rats used in the study were stratified by body weight and randomly distributed into treatment groups. During the 28-day study, 3 sentinel animals that were housed with study animals but were not used in the study were evaluated for the presence of common rat pathogens. Because the sentinel samples tested negative for all pathogens evaluated, the study animals were considered healthy and reasonably free of common rat pathogens. Details of the rat toxicity study design and execution as well as bacterial translocation analysis have been described in Supplemental Data under “Supplementary Materials and Methods”.

Binge-on-chronic alcohol model of leaky gut in mice

C57BL/6 mice (female, 10- to 12-week-old with body weight ~ 20 g) were obtained from Charles River (Wilmington, MA) and maintained in the specific-pathogen-free facility at LSU Health Science Center. Mice were housed in filter-topped cages (5 mice/cage), fed sterile water and diet, and kept under standard environment conditions (25 °C, 56% moisture, day/night 12-h shifts). All experiments were reviewed and approved by the LSUHSC Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were acclimated to Lieber-DeCarli liquid control diet (Bioserv, Flemington, NJ) for 5 days. Animals were then randomized into alcohol-fed (AF; Lieber-DeCarli ethanol [EtOH] liquid diet) and PF groups (control diet) (n = 10/group). AF mice were fed with EtOH liquid diet (5.0%, vol/vol) for 10 days and received 4 g/ kg BW EtOH by gavage at day 5 and day 10. Blood alcohol concentrations averaged 200 mg/dl before the binge and reached ~ 400 mg/dl 6 h post binge60,77. The amount of control liquid diet for PF mice was adjusted daily according to the food intake of AF mice during the experiment. Mice were sacrificed 24 h following the last binge EtOH administration. AFBP group was fed with 3.0 × 109CFUs/mice/day of L. reuteri 3630 and 3632 (1:1 ratio) for 14 days.

Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran measurements in mice

Mice fasted overnight were orally gavaged with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran (60 mg/ml in phosphate buffered saline, 4 kDa, Millipore Sigma, MA) with a dose of 600 mg/kg body weight. Mice were then anesthetized for 4 h and blood was collected, centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Plasma was collected and fluorescence was quantified at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and emission wavelength of 535 nm.

iFABP in mice

iFABP was quantified from serum samples using the Mouse FABP2 ELISA Kit (Boster, Pleasanton, CA) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Quantitative RT-PCR of mice intestinal samples

Small intestinal samples were harvested at sacrifice and placed directly into Trizol Reagent (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). Total RNA was extracted, and the cDNA was synthesized using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). The real-time quantitative PCR was performed using the iQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

AhR activation assay

AhR activity assay was performed using HepG2-Lucia™ AhR reporter cells (Invivogen) following manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the reporter cells were grown in Eagle’s minimal essential media (EMEM, ThermoFisher) with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (iFBS), 1X non-essential amino acids (NEAA), 100 μg/ml of Pen-Strep and 100 μg/ml Normocin™. For selection, the cell culture medium was supplemented with 100 μg /ml of zeocin (Invivogen). The cells were grown for 70–80% confluency, washed with PBS, detached with trypsin, and centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the cells were resuspended to 1.5 × 105 cells /ml in the growth medium without Normocin™ and Zeocin™. The AhR agonist 6-Formylindolo[3,2-b]carbazole (FICZ, 1μg final concentration) was used as a positive control. The test articles included L. reuteri 3632 and 3630 whole cells and cell-free supernatants and purified PKS metabolite [50μl in phosphate buffered saline (PBS)]. For preparing whole cells, overnight grown L. reuteri 3632 and 3630 were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were filter sterilized with 0.22μM filter, the whole cells were resuspended in 10 ml of PBS. The media control, whole cells and cell-free supernatant samples were further diluted seven-fold in PBS and twenty microliters of the sample was mixed with 180μl of cells containing 1.5 × 105 cells/ml in a flat-bottom 96-well plate and incubated for 36 h in a CO2 incubator. Following 36 h of incubation, 20μl of the sample was mixed with 50μl of Quanti-Luc™ and luciferase activity was measured immediately. The RLUs for whole cells, cell-free supernatants and purified PKS metabolite were normalized to either MRS media or PBS.

Comparative genomics analysis

OrthoFinder v 2.3.1178 was used to determine ortholog relationships between protein sequences predicted by PROKKA on the L. reuteri 3630, 3632, DSM17938, SD2112, DSM20016, CF48-3A, ATCC53608, and CECT5711 as well as L. rhamnosus GG genomes. Orthogroups containing genes from L. reuteri 3630 and/or L. reuteri 3632 but no genes from L. reuteri DSM17938, SD2112, DSM20016, CF48-3A, ATCC53608, and CECT5711 as well as L. rhamnosus GG genomes were considered to be unique to the former two strains. ANI values were calculated all-against-all, using FastANI v 1.3279.

In silico analysis of L. reuteri 3632 and L. reuteri 3632 genome and plasmid sequences for putative BGCs

To identify a potential BGC encoding the orange pigmented metabolite, we performed comparative genomics analysis of the BGCs present in 206 publicly available L. reuteri genomes using the free and open-source secondary metabolite analysis tools antiSMASH and BiG-SCAPE80–82.

Purification and structural elucidation of PKS metabolite

Extraction of PKS metabolite was performed using saponification under low light conditions to protect the pigment. Details of saponification, extraction, column chromatography, resin purification, HPLC purification and structural elucidation of PKS metabolite have been described in Supplementary Data under “Supplementary Materials and Methods”.

Statistical analyses

All the data related to 28-day rat toxicity study were analyzed statistically with Provantis® version 10 (Instem Life Science Software). The means and standard deviations were calculated for all quantitative data, and results for males and females were analyzed separately. When an individual value was recorded as being below the lower limit of quantitation, calculations were performed on half the recorded value. When an individual observation was recorded as being greater than the upper limit of quantitation, calculations were performed on the recorded value. Bartlett’s test or Levene’s test for homogeneity and Shapiro–Wilk’s test for normality were used to determine whether data should be analyzed parametrically or nonparametrically. Data that were normally distributed and homogenous were analyzed using a two-way (continuous data such as body weight, food consumption, etc.) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by comparisons of the treated groups to control by Dunnett’s t-test for multiple comparisons. Data that were not homogenous or normally distributed were analyzed using a Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric ANOVA, followed by a Dunn’s test. Data were evaluated at various levels of significance (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001 and P < 0.001), with P < 0.05 chosen as the minimum criterion for statistical significance.

Statistical analyses of mice and AhR data were executed using GraphPad Prism 9 (La Jolla, CA). Results were shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed using a one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD for multiple comparisons. Data were evaluated at various levels of significance (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001 and P < 0.001), with P < 0.05 chosen as the minimum criterion for statistical significance.
