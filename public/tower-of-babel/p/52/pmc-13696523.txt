Feasibility and Preliminary Effectiveness of a Data-to-Care Intervention for HIV-Positive People Who Inject Drugs in Ukraine: A Pilot Cluster-Randomized Trial
Authors: Kostyantyn Dumchev, Brian Weir, Khushee Prasad, Shannon Fuller, Farren Rodrigues, Julia Burlaka, Olena Makarenko, Tetiana Kiriazova, Jill Owczarzak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntiretroviral therapy (ART) interruptions are common among people who inject drugs (PWID) and contribute to viral rebound, disease progression, and onward transmission. Data-to-Care (D2C) strategies, which use routine health data to identify and re-engage people living with HIV who have fallen out of care, are widely used in high-income settings but rarely evaluated in low- and middle-income countries. We piloted a Ukrainian Data-to-Care (UD2C) intervention integrated into the national HIV Medical Information System among HIV-positive PWID recently out of care. Four specialized HIV clinics were randomized to UD2C or care as usual (CAU), with 40 participants enrolled per clinic (n = 160). UD2C combined an optimized data query to identify out-of-care patients with multidisciplinary, acuity-based case management. Recruitment, data extraction, and intervention delivery were feasible across all clinics. Because recruitment required clinic attendance, the pilot assessed UD2C’s post-linkage care component rather than its re-engagement function. The primary analysis accounted for clinic-level clustering using cluster-robust standard errors. Median ART coverage during follow-up was higher in UD2C than CAU (97% vs. 89%; adjusted rate ratio 1.15, 95% CI 0.87–1.51); this difference was significant in the model-based sensitivity analysis but not in the primary clustered analysis. Viral suppression was achieved or maintained more often in UD2C than CAU (58.8% vs. 28.8%; adjusted odds ratio 4.6, 95% CI 1.76–12.1). The UD2C model was feasible to implement and evaluate using a cluster-randomized design and showed promising improvements in longitudinal viral suppression. A fully powered definitive trial is warranted to confirm effectiveness and inform national scale-up. The trial is registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov as NCT05821413.Keywords: Antiretroviral therapy adherence, Care continuum, Viral suppression, Health information systems, Eastern Europe, Implementation science

Introduction

Retention in HIV care and continuous treatment adherence are critical for successful long-term HIV management, yet antiretroviral therapy (ART) interruptions remain common globally [1, 2]. Treatment interruption leads to loss of virological control, reversal of immune recovery, increased risk of opportunistic infections, higher morbidity and mortality, and increased risk of onward HIV transmission [3]. Even short-term interruptions cause measurable CD4 loss [4], and treatment resumption within 16 weeks is critical to avoiding adverse outcomes [5].

Re-engaging people who have fallen out of care remains a persistent challenge, requiring innovative, systems-level, and cross-sectoral approaches. Data-to-Care (D2C) is one promising strategy that leverages routine data, often from surveillance, clinical, or administrative systems, to find disengaged people living with HIV (PLWH) and support their re-linkage to HIV-related medical services [6–8]. D2C interventions may include provider-mediated data sharing, electronic linkages between health information systems, and direct outreach by trained staff to patients flagged as out-of-care [6]. D2C approaches have been widely implemented in high-income countries, particularly in the United States, where they have demonstrated promising outcomes, including improvements in re-linkage to care, ART uptake, and viral suppression among out-of-care populations [9–12]. However, evidence on the effectiveness and adaptation of D2C strategies in low- and middle-income countries remains scarce [8, 13].

Ukraine continues to face a concentrated and complex HIV epidemic, further intensified by the full-scale invasion by the Russian Federation in February 2022 [14, 15]. The epidemic remains concentrated among people who inject drugs (PWID), who accounted for over 30% of PLWH enrolled in care in 2020 [16]. According to survey-based estimates, 77% of HIV-positive PWID know their status, 92% of those diagnosed are on ART, and 82% of ART patients are virally suppressed [17]. Despite existing case management and linkage programs, including those tailored to PWID, implementation across Ukraine has been inconsistent [18]. Service fragmentation persists, with suboptimal coordination across HIV testing sites, outpatient clinics, and community-based organizations, often resulting in delays or failures in linkage and re-engagement [15, 19]. Addressing these barriers requires a shift toward data-driven systems that combine clinical data and service delivery.

Ukraine has made important progress in developing health information infrastructure, including routine collection of clinical and surveillance data through HIV Medical Information System (MIS) such as HIV case notifications, laboratory results, and service utilization records [20]. In 2022, the WHO identified key strengths in Ukraine’s health information infrastructure, including the widespread use of MIS, strong connectivity to the national e-health database, and efforts to centralize health data for policy and public health use. However, the assessment also highlighted gaps in system integration, data quality, legal frameworks, and the ability to use data for clinical care and decision-making [19]. This offers a unique and timely opportunity to adapt D2C strategies to this context to strengthen outcomes across the HIV care continuum.

To address these gaps and build on the existing health information infrastructure, we adapted a D2C strategy to the Ukrainian context (UD2C intervention), integrating acuity-based case management into MIS workflows to facilitate identification, re-engagement, and retention in care for newly out-of-care PWID living with HIV in Ukraine. This paper reports the results from a pilot cluster-randomized trial designed to assess the feasibility of a future definitive trial and the preliminary clinical effectiveness of the UD2C intervention compared to care as usual (CAU), focusing on ART coverage and viral suppression.

Methods

Study Design and Aims

UD2C is a structural-level intervention designed to facilitate information exchange and promote sustainable changes in HIV care practices at the facility level. As a facility-level intervention, UD2C involves training multiple staff members, changing workflows and care procedures, and introducing integrated services. Because structural interventions alter organizational practices, resources, or environments, exposure cannot be meaningfully restricted to individuals within the same setting. Therefore, an efficacy trial should employ cluster randomization to minimize contamination, preserve intervention fidelity, and align with the ecological level at which the intervention produces effects [21].

Accordingly, we conducted a pilot cluster-randomized controlled trial mirroring the design planned for the definitive trial, to assess feasibility in two domains: (a) intervention-level feasibility, including integration of the data-driven query and acuity-based case management workflows into routine clinic operations, staff acceptance, and fidelity to the recommended contact frequency by acuity level; and (b) research-level feasibility, including cluster size, recruitment yield, eligibility criteria, MIS data extraction, participant retention through 12 months, and operational procedures across sites. Although not powered to determine effectiveness, the pilot also aimed to estimate the preliminary intervention effect size for key clinical outcomes, providing essential parameters for sample size estimation and planning of a larger cluster-randomized trial. The present paper reports research-level feasibility and preliminary clinical effectiveness. Intervention-level feasibility, staff and client experiences, and implementation outcomes are the focus of a separate, in-progress mixed-methods analysis [22].

Study Sites and Population

In Ukraine, HIV care is delivered exclusively in the public sector, through a national network of approximately 370 specialized HIV clinics and selected primary care facilities; private-sector HIV care does not exist. Antiretroviral therapy, viral load and CD4 monitoring, and routine clinical visits are provided to all patients free of charge, with medication procurement coordinated nationally by the Public Health Center of the Ministry of Health. Reporting to the MIS is mandatory for all HIV care providers, and the system captures clinical, laboratory, and pharmacy data for all patients in HIV care nationwide.

The number of clusters (N = 4, 2 per arm) for the pilot was determined by feasibility considerations, while the target cluster size (N = 40 per cluster, 80 per arm) was selected to balance statistical precision and implementation efficiency for the planned definitive trial [23].

The UD2C study was conducted in four HIV clinics in Odesa, Dnipro, Kyiv, and Poltava, Ukraine, selected based on their willingness and administrative readiness to participate. These public-sector specialized HIV care centers, each serving between 1,500 and 14,000 patients. Clinics were randomized (using simple randomization function in MS Excel) to either the intervention (UD2C - Dnipro and Odesa) or the control arm (CAU - Kyiv and Poltava). Forty participants were enrolled at each clinic, for a total sample size of 160.

Eligibility criteria for patients included: (i) age 18 years or older; (ii) confirmed HIV diagnosis documented in the Ukrainian Medical Information System for Socially Significant Diseases (MIS); (iii) being registered as a patient at one of the participating clinics; (iv) newly out-of-care, defined as having missed an ART pick-up visit more than 7 days ago; (v) no prior contact by clinical staff following the missed visit; (vi) injecting drug use (IDU) as the probable mode of HIV transmission or a documented history of IDU in the MIS.

The same recruitment procedure was applied across all sites. At the start of enrollment, eligible patient lists were generated and randomly sorted. Outreach specialists contacted patients sequentially in the order they appeared and conducted brief scripted counseling to encourage them to visit the clinic, mentioning the opportunity to enroll in the study. Up to three contact attempts were made for each patient, spaced one to two weeks apart. The patients were excluded if they: (i) had already visited the clinic; (ii) had not interrupted ART due to having a carryover of medications; (iii) had moved to another city or were incarcerated; (iv) were deceased.

Eligible patients who visited the clinics before recruitment reached the target sample size and provided written informed consent were enrolled as study participants. ART dispensing could occur during the enrollment visit at the discretion of the clinical staff if the patient agreed; however, it was not mandatory.

Recruitment took place between June and October 2023.

Intervention

Clinics randomized to the control arm continued CAU case management practices with participants, while clinics randomized to the intervention arm implemented the D2C strategy.

The UD2C intervention has two integrated components [22]. First, an optimized data query and exchange procedure links the MIS to the clinic care team, that consists of physicians, nurses, case managers, and outreach specialists. A team member with MIS access (typically a nurse or physician) generates lists of patients newly out of care and initiates the recruitment procedure described above.

Second, after returning to care, patients receive 12 months of active, holistic case management. At the first session, the case manager conducts a structured acuity assessment across four domains: basic needs (housing, financial situation, legal issues, transportation), interpersonal (support systems, domestic violence, caregiver burden), HIV-related (retention in HIV care, ART adherence, current HIV health status, hospitalizations and opportunistic infections, health literacy), and other health (comorbidities, oral health, mental health, alcohol and drug use, women’s health). Each item is scored 0–3, and the total score (range 0–51) classifies patients into one of four management levels: Self-Management (0–6), Basic Management (7–17), Moderate Management (18–33), or Intensive Management (34–51). Acuity level determined the recommended contact intensity: Self-Management — in-person meetings as needed and telephone contact at least every two months; Basic Management — an in-person meeting once per quarter and telephone contact at least monthly; Moderate Management — monthly in-person meetings and telephone contact every other week; and Intensive Management — monthly in-person meetings and weekly telephone contact. Case managers use the acuity findings to set goals with the patient, provide tailored counseling (e.g., on ART adherence, harm reduction, drug treatment), and arrange referrals to available social, legal, or medical services. Acuity is reassessed every three months and goals are refined accordingly. Optimized data query procedures are used to obtain MIS data to assess acuity in the HIV-related domain.

CAU clinics continued the existing case management practice based on the Steps Toward Health program, a fivesession structured intervention covering HIV transmission, treatment, and care-related modules [24]. CAU did not include a structured acuity assessment or an acuity-based contact schedule.

Study Procedures

Participants were followed from baseline (the date of their enrollment visit) to 12 months after baseline. Data collection began in May 2023 and concluded in November 2024 and included: (1) clinical outcomes data from monthly MIS queries, (2) behavioral and health outcomes data from participant surveys, and (3) acuity assessment data from participants in the intervention arm. MIS data included date of HIV confirmation; date and result of the most recent viral load and CD4 tests; and date, regimen, and amount of most recent ART dispensation. In-person participant surveys at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months collected data on demographics, HIV treatment experience, current and past substance use and treatment, mental health status, and physical health comorbidities.

Outcome Measures

The clinical outcomes were derived from data recorded in the MIS on viral load (VL) testing and ART dispensing. MIS data included the dates and results of the last VL test before baseline and all VL tests conducted during follow-up, as well as the dates of ART dispensing and the calculated ART run-out dates. The ART run-out date is generated automatically by the MIS at each dispensing event, based on the number of days of ART supply dispensed plus any days of carryover medication reported by the patient.

For each timepoint, the VL test validity window was defined as the period from 6 months before to 15 days after the timepoint; results older than 6 months were considered outdated.

Because written informed consent had to be obtained in person, eligible patients were required to visit the clinic before enrollment. To ensure comparability between groups, the same recruitment procedure (the optimized MIS data query, outreach, and brief scripted counseling) was applied at all four sites. As a consequence, the pilot did not evaluate the re-engagement (linkage) function of UD2C and instead isolates its post-linkage, longitudinal care component. Re-engagement was nonetheless measured (outcome 1, below), both to characterize the recruitment-period dynamic and to refine outcome definitions for the definitive trial.

The primary clinical outcomes were:

Re-initiation of ART, defined as time to the first ART dispensing after baseline;

Re-initiation of ART, defined as time to the first ART dispensing after baseline;

Retention on ART at 12 months, defined as having an ART run-out date (plus a 28-day grace period) later than the 12-month timepoint;

Retention on ART at 12 months, defined as having an ART run-out date (plus a 28-day grace period) later than the 12-month timepoint;

Proportion of follow-up days with ART coverage, calculated as the total days covered by ART medication divided by the total number of follow-up days;

Proportion of follow-up days with ART coverage, calculated as the total days covered by ART medication divided by the total number of follow-up days;

Viral suppression at 12 months, defined as a VL result of ≤ 1,000 copies/mL from the last test within the validity window of 12-month timepoint;

Viral suppression at 12 months, defined as a VL result of ≤ 1,000 copies/mL from the last test within the validity window of 12-month timepoint;

Viral suppression achieved or maintained during follow-up, defined as having at least two separate VL tests within follow-up windows, with the most recent result ≤ 1,000 copies/mL.

Viral suppression achieved or maintained during follow-up, defined as having at least two separate VL tests within follow-up windows, with the most recent result ≤ 1,000 copies/mL.

Complete outcome definitions and rationale are presented in Supplementary Material S1. Participants who died during follow-up were excluded from analyses of outcomes 2 and 4. Patients lost to follow-up and those transferred out were not excluded because their data remained available through the MIS.

Statistical Analysis

Differences between intervention and control participants at baseline were evaluated using Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Pearson’s chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The association between group assignment and outcomes 1–2 and 4–5 was evaluated using logistic regression. The exponentiated coefficient for group assignment provided the odds ratio of viral suppression among UD2C vs. CAU participants. The association between group assignment and outcome 3 was evaluated using negative binomial regression with days of ART coverage as the outcome, and days of follow-up specified as the person-time exposure. The exponentiated coefficient for group assignment provided the rate ratio of (or proportional increase in) daily ART possession between UD2C vs. CAU participants. For all four outcomes, models were adjusted for variables selected a priori based on hypothesized potential association with the intervention and HIV treatment and the outcome, including basic sociodemographics, IDU, MOUD treatment, drug abuse severity, depression, duration of ART, presence of chronic conditions, baseline HIV treatment satisfaction, and past-30-day ART adherence.

Because the clinic was the unit of randomization, the primary analysis accounted for clinic-level clustering using cluster-robust standard errors with the CR2 small-sample correction and Satterthwaite degrees of freedom [25]. Standard model-based estimates, which do not account for clustering, are reported as a sensitivity analysis. Given that only four clusters were randomized, both sets of estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Analyses were conducted in R statistical computing environment [26] and Stata 17.0 SE. Negative binomial regression analysis was conducted using the ‘MASS’ package [27], cluster-robust standard errors were calculated using the ‘clubSandwich’ R package [28].

We followed the CONSORT statement for conducting and reporting pilot and feasibility trials [29]. The CONSORT checklist is included in Supplementary Material S2.

Results

Recruitment and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 347 patients were included in the recruitment lists across four HIV clinics. Of them, 131 could not be reached, 30 refused to participate in the study, 18 were ineligible, and 168 (48.4%) scheduled a visit (Fig. 1). The median number of days between the date of last missed visit and study enrollment was 19 days (range 8–1019).

CONSORT diagram

A total of 160 participants were enrolled, with 40 participants per site. Participants were predominantly male (67%), and the median age was 43.8 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 39.0–49.6 years). Educational attainment was high overall, with more than 70% of the sample having completed vocational education or university. Half of participants were married or in a relationship, 38.8% were not employed, and 11% had temporary housing. There were no significant differences in sociodemographic characteristics between the study arms, except in housing (Table 1).

Clinical Outcomes

A majority of participants were dispensed ART at the enrollment visit (74.4%; median time to reinitiation: 0 days, IQR: 0–1 days). Within seven days, 88.8% of the UD2C group and 80.0% of the CAU group reinitiated ART (χ2=1.7, p = 0.191, Table 2). ART uptake at each monthly timepoint is presented in Supplementary Material Figure S4, showing gradual dropout from ART due to death and other reasons, starting from month 3. At 12 months, 4.4% of all participants were deceased, 7.5% stopped ART, 5% missed the scheduled and were past the grace period, 5% were within the grace period, and 78.1% were on ART, with no difference between the groups. In the multivariable regression models, neither the study arm nor any covariates were associated with ART reinitiation or retention at 12 months (Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

Sustained re-engagement in HIV care is crucial for successful, long-term HIV treatment, particularly for people who inject drugs, who experience high rates of care interruptions and viral rebound. D2C approaches have emerged as a promising strategy to improve re-engagement by using routinely collected health data to identify, trace, and return out-of-care individuals to treatment. This pilot cluster-randomized trial represents one of the first evaluations of a D2C strategy adapted for implementation in an Eastern European setting, specifically designed to rapidly re-engage PLWH who injected drugs in care.

The pilot study demonstrated the feasibility of conducting a larger definitive trial. The cluster size of 40 participants per site proved manageable and allowed for efficient recruitment within the study timeframe. Eligibility criteria and recruitment procedures were effective, with a recruitment rate of 46% after accounting for those who could not be reached, declined participation, or were ineligible. This rate was lower than the average recruitment rate of 64% in a recent methodological review of pilot and feasibility trials [30], which may reflect the additional challenges of enrolling participants with a history of injection drug use as well as the broader war-related constraints [31–33]. Data collection procedures, specifically the monthly MIS queries, provided objective data on the clinical outcomes.

As acknowledged in the Methods, due to the in-person consent requirement, the same UD2C recruitment procedure, including data-driven query and outreach, had to be applied across all four sites, resulting in the majority of patients receiving ART on the day of enrollment. This precluded our ability to isolate the effect of the intervention on the first pre-specified outcome, ART re-initiation, which represents a meaningful limitation of the design. Nonetheless, re-engagement, as a defining function of data-to-care interventions, remains a key component of UD2C; therefore the definitive trial will be redesigned to allow evaluation of this outcome (see below).

Despite this design limitation, our analysis was able to evaluate the post-linkage, longitudinal care component of UD2C, informing the choice of endpoints for the definitive RCT. The cross-sectional outcomes assessed at the 12-month timepoint, i.e. retention on ART and viral suppression at 12 months, were less sensitive in determining intervention effects, with no significant differences detected between study arms in adjusted models. In contrast, the ‘longitudinal’ outcomes that captured treatment dynamics over the entire study period (proportion of follow-up days with ART coverage and viral suppression achieved or maintained during follow-up) were substantially more sensitive to intervention effects. This suggests that longitudinal measures more accurately reflect real-world patterns of HIV care engagement and treatment success, as they account for fluctuations in adherence and viral load over time rather than relying on a single timepoint assessment. Several studies have demonstrated that longitudinal approaches to assessing HIV care effectiveness, which track individual progress in ART uptake and viral suppression over extended periods, provide more nuanced understanding of treatment trajectories than single-timepoint measurements alone [34–36]. The dynamic outcome assessment is particularly note-worthy given the progressive decline in ART retention we observed in our sample, also reported by other studies [37].

UD2C Effectiveness

The UD2C intervention showed promising preliminary effects on longitudinal clinical outcomes, though these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the pilot nature of the study. Participants receiving the UD2C intervention were over four times more likely to achieve or maintain viral suppression during follow-up, an effect that remained statistically significant in the primary analysis accounting for clinic-level clustering. They also had a 15% higher daily probability of ART coverage; this effect was significant in the sensitivity analysis that did not account for clustering. A distinctive feature of our study is its focus on patients recently out of care (the majority within the 28-day grace period) among whom the intervention nonetheless yielded measurable clinical improvements. These results are particularly notable given the higher levels of IDU and lower baseline adherence in the UD2C group. It is also important to note that a substantial proportion of patients who were included in recruitment lists (38%, 131/347) could not be reached after three attempts, suggesting that our observed effects may not fully capture outcomes among the hardest-to-reach PLWH, who may require more intensive re-engagement efforts or alternative strategies. An in-progress mixed-methods analysis of intervention implementation, including staff and client experiences and the distribution and dynamics of participant acuity over follow-up, will provide additional context on how UD2C was delivered.

These results contribute to the growing evidence on the effectiveness of D2C interventions, although direct comparisons with other studies may be limited by differences in study populations, settings, and intervention components. A meta-analysis that included 14 U.S. D2C interventions [8] reported that such approaches significantly increased the odds of re-engagement in care (OR 1.79) and retention in care (OR 2.01), although evidence for viral suppression was limited, likely due to short follow-up durations or incomplete laboratory data. Another review of re-engagement interventions reported mixed results for re-engagement and found no significant effects on viral suppression in any of the five studies reporting that outcome [38]. A more recent systematic review of 34 D2C studies with 30 unique interventions also found that D2C interventions have a strong potential in improving HIV care outcomes [7], although the statistical significance was marginal, likely due to heterogeneity in intervention components and outcome definitions.

Limitations

Several factors may limit the interpretation of this pilot study’s findings. With only four randomized clusters and two clusters per arm, randomization could not ensure balance across important prognostic variables, and substantial baseline imbalances were observed in recent injecting drug use, MOUD treatment, ART history, baseline ART status, recent adherence, depression, self-reported chronic conditions, and HIV treatment satisfaction. We addressed these imbalances by employing multivariable regression models adjusted for all key variables that differed between groups. Nonetheless, with N = 4 clusters no inferential correction can fully account for between-clinic heterogeneity, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors remains possible.

VL testing coverage was limited, with 44.4% and 46.3% of participants having VL results older than 6 months at baseline and at the 12-month timepoint, respectively, which may hinder full assessment of the intervention’s effect on viral suppression. We did not anticipate this degree of under-testing and therefore did not plan study-funded VL measurements. The lower-than-expected coverage may reflect adoption of the WHO recommendation for annual VL testing [39] and the disruptions in reagent supply caused by the full-scale war in Ukraine [40]. Another reason for relying on routine clinical testing was methodological: consistent with an implementation-science approach, we sought to evaluate UD2C under real-world conditions, and introducing study-specific VL testing would have constituted an additional clinical contact for all participants, contaminating both the intervention and, especially, the CAU condition. Under-testing in this design therefore reflects routine care patterns and may itself indicate suboptimal engagement in care. Notably, participants in the UD2C group had more frequent VL testing during follow-up, suggesting that improved engagement in care under the intervention also facilitated more regular monitoring.

ART-related outcomes were derived from MIS-recorded ART “run-out” dates, which depend on patients’ self-reported carryover medication amounts. Our recruitment data indicate that some patients may still possess ART after the recorded run-out date, implying intentional or unintentional inaccuracy in self-reported ART possession. This limitation may reduce the validity of ART-related outcomes. To account for possible under-reporting of carryover, we incorporated a 28-day grace period into the ART retention outcome. On the other hand, PWID may over-report antiretroviral medications uptake for both treatment [41] and prevention [42], likely due to social desirability bias; therefore no grace period was applied to the more sensitive ART coverage outcome.

These limitations will be addressed in the definitive trial. Increasing the number of clusters and employing matched randomization should reduce baseline imbalances. To ensure more robust evaluation of viral suppression outcomes while minimizing the contamination, a single study-funded VL test will be added at the final timepoint to complement MIS-recorded clinical data. Additional investigation of carryover-reporting accuracy and the use of more objective measures (e.g., pill counts) will inform refinement of ART-related outcome definitions. To permit evaluation of UD2C’s re-engagement function, recruitment will be redesigned so that participants are enrolled at the point of first outreach contact (by telephone or in the community) rather than at the clinic. Finally, recruitment procedures and eligibility criteria may be revised to ensure participation of harder-to-reach subgroups of PLWH.

Conclusions

This pilot cluster-randomized trial contributes to the evidence on data-to-care interventions in low- and middle-income countries by demonstrating the feasibility of adapting and implementing a D2C-type intervention within Ukraine’s expanding national HIV Medical Information System framework. The study also yielded preliminary signals of improved ART coverage and viral suppression, supporting the potential effectiveness of the UD2C model. A larger, definitive randomized trial is warranted to confirm the intervention’s clinical effectiveness and cost-effectiveness and to inform its potential scale-up in Ukraine and other resource-constrained health systems where similar data infrastructures are emerging.
