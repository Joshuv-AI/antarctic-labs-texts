Impact of combined unilateral mastectomy and ovariohysterectomy on acute phase response and wound healing in dogs with mammary gland tumors
Authors: Grisnarong Wongbandue, Phirom Prompiram, Panyakamol Chandrasakha, Nawarus Prapaiwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCanine mammary gland tumors (CMTs) are among the most common neoplasms in female dogs, and surgical excision is the standard modality for treatment of CMTs. Unilateral mastectomy (UM) is frequently combined with ovariohysterectomy (OVH), which may lead to different levels of systemic inflammation and wound-healing. This study aimed to evaluate pain intensity, serum acute phase proteins (APPs), proinflammatory cytokines, and wound healing in dogs submitted to OVH (control), UM, or UM concomitant with OVH (BT). Twenty-nine female dogs were prospectively enrolled and divided into three groups: control (n = 8), UM (n = 10), and BT (n = 11). Pain was measured using the Glasgow Composite Pain Scale. Serum C-reactive protein (CRP), amyloid A (SAA), haptoglobin, interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-6 levels were determined at five time points, including pre-surgery, after emergence from anesthesia, and on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7, using enzyme-linked immunosorbent and fluorescent immunoassays.ResultsPain scores peaked after emergence from anesthesia in both the UM and BT groups compared to controls (P < 0.05), with non-significant differences between UM and BT groups. In the UM and BT groups, serum CRP and SAA levels rose significantly following surgery, peaking on day 1 and gradually dropping subsequently. On day 1, both the UM and BT groups exhibited significantly higher CRP and SAA levels than the control group (P < 0.05), whereas the difference between the UM and BT groups was non-significant. Wound healing was slower in the BT group, which had a significantly higher wound score on day 3 than the control group (P < 0.05).ConclusionsUM, performed alone or in combination with OVH, was associated with transient increases in postoperative pain, APP responses, and wound scores compared with OVH alone, particularly during the early postoperative period. SAA and CRP were the most consistent and sensitive biomarkers for detecting postoperative inflammation under the present sampling schedule, whereas IL-1β and IL-6 showed limited discriminatory capacity, likely due to their rapid and transient kinetics. Therefore, monitoring blood CRP and SAA levels provides supportive information for assessing postoperative inflammatory responses in dogs undergoing mammary tumor surgery.

Background

Canine mammary gland tumors (CMTs) are one of the most common tumors in female dogs. Several studies suggest that CMTs account for 24–54% of all neoplasms identified in female dogs, ranking second only to skin tumors, while some registries include mammary tumors as the single most prevalent neoplasm in females of this species [1]. Surgical excision remains the gold standard treatment for non-metastatic CMTs, with the primary goal of removing the tumor completely and leaving histologically clean margins to reduce the risk of recurrence [2]. Unilateral mastectomy (UM), which involves the complete removal of all ipsilateral mammary glands and accompanying lymph nodes, is a preferred procedure for patients who present with numerous breast tumors on the same side [3]. This widespread surgical approach, in which the entire mammary chain is dissected, is almost always run in parallel to spaying or ovariohysterectomy [4]. One of the previous studies supports the rationale behind this combined procedure, and with that, up to 64% of female dogs developed new mammary gland cancers after having had mastectomy without ovariohysterectomy (OVH), compared with approximately 36% for females being mastectomised together with OVH [5]. In addition, OVH not only prevents the occurrence of CMT but also reduces the risk for age-associated ovarian and uterine disorders, such as pyometra, that become more common in older intact female dogs [6].

The role of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-6 in mediating the inflammatory response has been implicated to be a result of immune cells from the blood that are activated during this process [7]. These cytokines circulate in the blood and act on many organs, with a wide range of subsequent effects. One significant systemic response is activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, which leads to enhanced release of adrenocorticotropic hormone and elevation of plasma glucocorticoids [8]. In addition, the liver is stimulated by IL-6 and other mediators to synthesize acute phase proteins (APPs), including C-reactive protein (CRP), as part of the acute phase response (APR). This APR is characterized by measurable alterations in the concentrations of specific proteins, the so-called positive or negative APPs, depending on whether they change in an increasing (positive) or decreasing (negative) sense during an inflammatory process [9]. Positive APPs are produced in response to inflammation and include molecules such as CRP, serum amyloid A (SAA), and haptoglobin (Hp) [10]. In dogs, CRP and SAA are considered major APPs, whereas Hp is a minor one [11]. CRP has become a major marker in the field of canine medicine, with reference values in healthy dogs below 1.0 mg/L, and it is capable of increasing rapidly above 100 mg/L due to infection, inflammation, or trauma [12]. Although much less is known about SAA in dogs, it has also been established as a highly sensitive biomarker of local tissue damage and systemic inflammation in this species, with an induction similar to cats, such as that seen following challenge with Bordetella bronchiseptica [13] and canine leishmaniosis [14]. Hp is at the lower end of its response, with increases in multiple inflammatory and infectious conditions being detected, but on a longer time scale and with less amplitude than those observed for CRP or SAA [9]. Reports on the relationship between APPs and surgery in companion animals have demonstrated that CRP concentrations in dogs depend on the extent of tissue injury [15]. Moreover, the inflammatory burden can be monitored using sensitive biomarkers, and this may provide veterinarians with an indication of how they should modulate surgical surveillance, analgesic duration, and follow-up regimens for individual patients [16].

Even though mastectomy with OVH is a common practice in female dogs, there is no comprehensive data on postoperative serum biomarkers, pain scores, and wound complications. Therefore, the goal of the present study was to compare the postoperative pain scores, APPs, and proinflammatory cytokine serum levels, and wound score in female dogs after three different surgeries: OVH, UM, and BT (UM performed with OVH), and to record their levels on the preoperative day and at several time points after surgery. It was hypothesized that the combination of UM and OVH would result in a more intense and extended inflammatory response than each surgery alone. In addition, it was also hypothesized that the intensive and prolonged activity of inflammatory markers would be associated with slower wound healing and increased risk of complications. Additionally, the results of the study would help to identify the reliable inflammatory markers for postoperative monitoring and an individual approach to the perioperative treatment of dogs with mammary gland tumors.

Methods

Animals and experimental grouping

This study included 29 client-owned female dogs presented for clinical treatment and undergoing surgical procedures at Prasu-Arthorn Animal Hospital, Faculty of Veterinary Science, Mahidol University, Nakhon Pathom, Thailand. All dogs were privately owned by individual owners and were enrolled in the study during routine clinical care. Sample size was determined using G*Power version 3.1 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). An a priori power analysis was conducted for a repeated-measures within–between interaction design, assuming three groups and five measurement time points. A medium effect size (f = 0.32), α = 0.05, desired power (1 – β) = 0.95, a correlation among repeated measures of 0.5, and a nonsphericity correction (ε) of 1.0 were specified. Under these assumptions, the estimated minimum total sample size required was 27 animals. A total of 29 animals were ultimately enrolled, exceeding the minimum required sample size. All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institute Animal Care and Use Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Science, Mahidol University, Nakhon Pathom, Thailand (Protocol No. MUVS-2023-10-65) in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for animal welfare.

All dogs included in the study were chosen based on well-defined inclusion criteria. Both the UM and BT groups required non-inflammatory mammary gland tumors, with inflammatory mammary carcinoma specifically excluded. Eligible tumors were non-ulcerated, had no secondary infection, were not adherent to underlying muscles, and showed no signs of metastasis to the lungs or intra-abdominal organs, as determined by clinical and diagnostic evaluations. Furthermore, only tumors deemed suitable for complete surgical excision and primary skin closure without advanced reconstructive techniques, such as skin flaps or grafts, were included. All dogs were screened out for systemic diseases that could impair surgical outcomes or healing processes, such as renal, endocrine, or infectious conditions. According to the history and medical records, none of the animals had received anti-inflammatory medications for at least 1 week prior to surgery. Dogs in the BT group were only intact females with no reproductive tract abnormalities, such as pyometra or genital neoplasms. Similarly, the control group consisted entirely of clinically healthy dogs with no history of anti-inflammatory drug use in the week preceding surgery and no detectable reproductive system abnormalities.

The animals (N = 29) were divided into three groups according to the type of surgery they underwent. The control group (n = 8) consisted of clinically healthy intact female dogs aged 1–9 years, weighing between 9.20 and 21.00 kg, who underwent routine OVH. This group included five mongrels (n = 5), Beagles (n = 2), and Labrador Retrievers (n = 1). Their overall health status was confirmed by a clinical examination and hematological profiles. The UM group consisted of ten female dogs (n = 10) who had been diagnosed with mammary gland tumors that required unilateral mastectomy. The BT group consisted of twelve intact female dogs (n = 11) who underwent both unilateral mastectomy and OVH. Dogs in the UM and BT groups underwent preoperative clinical staging using the modified TNM system for canine mammary gland tumors [17], which classifies tumors by T (primary tumor size), N (regional lymph node involvement), and M (distant metastasis). Stage I was defined as a tumor < 3 cm; stage II, 3–5 cm; and stage III, > 5 cm. Regardless of tumor size, regional lymph node metastasis was classified as stage IV and distant metastasis as stage V. Tumor size was determined by physical examination and confirmed intraoperatively. Regional lymph nodes were assessed by palpation and cytology when indicated, and distant metastases were evaluated by thoracic radiography and abdominal ultrasonography. The same veterinarian performed all staging procedures to minimize inter-observer variability. Table 1 summarizes detailed patient characteristics for the UM and BT groups.

Anesthesia and surgical procedures

All dogs received identical perioperative management protocols to minimize potential treatment-related variability among groups. Preoperative preparation consisted of a 12-hour food fast and a 6-hour water fast before anesthesia induction. Anesthetic and surgical protocols were standardized and applied uniformly across all experimental groups [2, 18]. Premedication was accomplished with diazepam (Diazepam injection, the government pharmaceutical organization, Bangkok, Thailand) at 0.2–0.5 mg/kg, followed by anesthetic induction with propofol (Propofol-Lipuro 1%, B. Braun Melsungen AG, Melsungen, Germany) at 4–6 mg/kg. Intraoperatively, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (dose 20 mg/kg; Cavumox®, Siam Bheasach Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) was given intravenously as an antibiotic, and morphine (dose 0.2–0.5 mg/kg; Morphine sulfate injection, the government pharmaceutical organization, Bangkok, Thailand) was given for peri- or intraoperative pain control. Anesthesia was maintained by administering isoflurane (Aerrane, Baxter Healthcare Corporation, Puerto Rico, USA) in 100% oxygen. All procedures were performed by certified veterinary surgeons following a standardized surgical protocol to minimize technical variability. Prior to incision, local anesthetics, lidocaine (0.5–2 mg/kg; Locana, L.B.S. Laboratory Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) and bupivacaine (0.5–2 mg/kg; Marcaine 0.5%, AstraZeneca AB, Sodertalje, Sweden), were administered subcutaneously at the planned surgical site.

Hemostasis was achieved using bipolar electrocautery in combination with absorbable monofilament ligatures (polydioxanone). Closure of the abdominal wall and subcutaneous tissues was performed using absorbable monofilament sutures (polydioxanone), while the skin was closed with non-absorbable monofilament polyamide sutures. In the OVH group, a standardized ventral midline approach was used. In the UM group, an elliptical incision was made to allow en bloc resection of the ipsilateral mammary chain (glands 1–5). In the BT group, OVH was performed first via a ventral midline incision, followed by unilateral mammary chain excision through a separate elliptical incision. Although incision length and operative time were not prospectively recorded, the anatomical location of incision, the extent of mammary tissue removal (glands 1–5 on one side), and the surgeon were consistent across cases within each group. Variability in incision length and procedure duration was primarily influenced by individual body size and mammary chain dimensions rather than differences in surgical technique.

Postoperative pain management and medical treatment were also standardized across groups. After surgery, a transdermal fentanyl patch (dose 3 − 6 mcg/kg; Sandoz fentanyl patch, Sandoz AG, Basel, Switzerland) was applied for ongoing pain relief. Carprofen (Rimadyl®, Zoetis, Girona, Spain) was also given subcutaneously at a dose of 2.2 mg/kg after complete recovery from anesthesia to provide anti-inflammatory support. During the recovery period at home, owners were given oral medications for ongoing care. These included the antibiotic amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (Cavumox®, Siam Bheasach Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) at a dose of 13–25 mg/kg given twice daily before meals for seven days. Carprofen (Rimadyl®, Zoetis, Girona, Spain) at 2.2 mg/kg was administered twice daily after meals for three days. Dogs were discharged on the same day after complete recovery from anesthesia and stabilization of vital parameters. Standardized postoperative care was provided for all animals. Owners were instructed to enforce strict rest and activity restriction for 7–10 days postoperatively. Continuous use of an Elizabethan collar was required until suture removal to prevent self-trauma. All dogs returned to the hospital for wound management on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7. At each visit, the surgical site was cleaned using sterile normal saline followed by 10% povidone–iodine solution, and a protective bandage was applied. The same clinical team performed wound evaluation and dressing to ensure consistency of care across groups.

All perioperative analgesic protocols were standardized and weight-adjusted according to institutional guidelines. Morphine was administered within a predefined dosage range based on body weight, and fentanyl transdermal patches were selected according to manufacturer recommendations to achieve comparable plasma exposure across individuals. Carprofen dosing and duration were identical in all groups. No additional rescue analgesia was required in any dog during the study period. Because analgesic regimens, administration timing, and treatment duration were consistent across groups, analgesic exposure was considered equivalent and was therefore not included as a covariate in the statistical model.

Pain assessment

Pain was assessed in all dogs using the Glasgow Composite Pain Scale (GCPS) [19]. This validated composite pain scale measures pain using six behavioral categories: vocalization, attention to the surgical site, mobility, response to touch, demeanor, and posture/activity. Each parameter is scored individually, and the total composite score ranges from 0 (minimal or no pain) to a maximum of 23, reflecting severe pain. In the vocalization category, dogs were scored from 0 to 3 based on their sounds, with 0 representing quietness, 1 denoting whimpering or crying, 2 referring to groaning, and 3 representing screaming. Attention to the surgical wound was graded as follows: 0 if the dog ignored the area, 1 if it looked at it, 2 if it rubbed it, and 3 if it chewed on it. The mobility scores ranged from 0 (normal mobility), 1 (mild lameness), 2 (slow or reluctant movement), 3 (stiffness), and 4 (complete refusal to move). The touch response was graded as 0 (no reaction), 1 (looking around), 2 (flinching), 3 (growling or guarding the area), 4 (snapping), and 5 (crying in response to palpation). Demeanor was graded on a scale of 0–4, with 0 representing a happy and active dog, 1 representing a quiet demeanor, 2 reflecting indifference to the surroundings, 3 corresponding to anxious or fearful behavior, and 4 indicating depression or a lack of responsiveness to stimulation. Finally, posture and activity were scored as 0 if the dog appeared relaxed and 1 if it appeared unsettled. All dogs were assessed 5 times (pre-surgery, after emergence from anesthesia, and on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7). One observer performed all evaluations to control inter-observer bias.

Collection of blood samples

Whole blood (2 − 4 mL per dog) was aseptically collected from the cephalic vein. Samples were obtained at 5 time points: pre-surgery, after the emergence from anesthesia, and on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7. The blood sampled was placed in plain tubes, maintained at room temperature for 1 h, and subsequently centrifuged at 1,000×g for 3 min to obtain the serum. The serum was subsequently shifted to sterile microcentrifuge tubes and stored at − 20 °C until analysis.

Serum analysis

Serum CRP levels were determined using a fluorescence immunoassay (Vcheck Canine CRP 2.0 Test Kit; Bionote, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea), as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The assay showed adequate intra- and inter-assay precision for canine serum analysis. The CRP concentration was measured using the V200 Analyzer (Bionote, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea). Serum CRP levels greater than 30 mg/L were classified as elevated. Serum concentrations of SAA, Hp, IL-1β, and IL-6 were measured using canine-specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (ab205072, ab137978, ab273170, and ab193686; Abcam, Cambridge, UK, respectively), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Analyte concentrations were determined using standard curves created for each assay. The intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation, used as indicators of assay precision, were within acceptable limits for veterinary diagnostic use: SAA, < 10 and < 10%; Hp, 5.60 and 10.40%; IL-1β, < 10 and < 12%; IL-6, < 10 and < 12%, respectively. The minimum detection limits of the assays were 6.25 pg/mL for SAA, 0.31 pg/mL for Hp, 10.97 pg/mL for IL-1β, and 0.1 pg/mL for IL-6.

Surgical wound assessment

All dogs had their postoperative wound healing evaluated using a standardized scoring system previously described [22]. The same observer performed the evaluation to minimize inter-observer bias. The wounds were evaluated at days 1, 3, and 7 post-surgery. The wound score ranged from 0 to 4, depending on the appearance and integrity of the surgical site. A score of 0 demonstrated a wound with good apposition of surgical edges but absence of skin erythema and hematoma. A score of 1 indicated mild erythema or ecchymosis surrounding the wound. A score of 2 indicated the presence of swelling, discharge, hematoma, or exposed subcutaneous tissue. A score of 3 represented partial wound dehiscence, while a score of 4 indicated complete wound dehiscence. Additional interventions or complications that developed in the postoperative course were also recorded.

Statistical data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 30.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Repeated measures data were analyzed using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) to account for within-subject correlations across time points. Group and time were included as categorical fixed effects; tumor stage was a covariate; and group-by-time interaction terms were included to evaluate differences in temporal patterns among groups. An appropriate distribution and link function were specified for each dependent variable based on its characteristics. An exchangeable working correlation structure was applied to model within-subject dependence. Descriptive data are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Model results are reported as regression coefficients (β) with 95% Wald confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance of model parameters was assessed using Wald chi-square tests. The control group and the predefined baseline time point were used as reference categories. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Pain level

Pain scores among dogs in the three groups at different time points are presented in Table 2. Before surgery, pain scores did not differ significantly between the BT and control groups. In contrast, the UM group showed a significantly higher pain score than the control group (β = 0.69, 95% CI 0.02 to 1.37, P = 0.04). Within the control group, pain score increased significantly after emergence from anesthesia (β = 2.67, 95% CI 2.29 to 3.04, P < 0.001), postoperative day 1 (β = 2.00, 95% CI 2.00 to 2.00, P < 0.001), and postoperative day 3 (β = 0.83, 95% CI 0.28 to 1.38, P = 0.003) compared with before surgery. By postoperative day 7, the pain score was not significantly different from baseline. Significant group-by-time interaction effects were observed after emergence from anesthesia for both the BT (β = 1.25, 95% CI 0.48 to 2.02, P = 0.001) and UM groups (β = 0.93, 95% CI 0.31 to 1.56, P = 0.003), indicating that the increase in pain score after emergence from anesthesia was significantly greater in these groups compared with the control group. No statistically significant interaction effects were observed on postoperative day 1, postoperative day 3, or postoperative day 7. Tumor stage was not significantly associated with pain score.

Serum APPs levels

Serum APP levels among dogs in the three groups at different time points are presented in Table 3. Pre-surgery, CRP levels did not differ significantly between the BT and control groups or between the UM and control groups. Within the control group, compared with pre-surgery, CRP levels increased significantly on postoperative day 1 (β = 37.13, 95% CI 24.09 to 50.18, P < 0.001) and postoperative day 3 (β = 18.47, 95% CI 4.27 to 32.67, P = 0.01). There was no significant difference after emergence from anesthesia or on postoperative day 7. Significant group-by-time interaction effects were observed on postoperative days 1 and 3. At postoperative day 1, CRP levels were significantly higher in both the BT group (β = 24.53, 95% CI 8.56 to 40.51, P = 0.003) and the UM group (β = 19.85, 95% CI 3.34 to 36.35, P = 0.02) compared with the control group. Similarly, at postoperative day 3, CRP levels were significantly higher in the BT group (β = 29.05, 95% CI 11.66 to 46.44, P = 0.001). In contrast, the increase in the UM group did not reach statistical significance. No significant interaction effects were observed after emergence from anesthesia or on postoperative day 7. Tumor stage was not significantly associated with CRP levels. SAA levels were analyzed using a GEE model with a gamma distribution and log link. In the control group, SAA levels after emergence from anesthesia did not differ significantly from pre-surgery. However, SAA levels were significantly elevated on postoperative day 1 (β = 6.56, 95% CI 5.84 to 7.28, P < 0.001) and day 3 (β = 4.39, 95% CI 3.66 to 5.12, P < 0.001) compared with pre-surgery. Significant group-by-day interaction effects were observed on postoperative day 1 for both the UM (β = −1.74, 95% CI − 2.74 to − 0.74, P < 0.001) and BT groups (β = −0.97, 95% CI − 1.93 to − 0.02, P = 0.04), indicating that the relative increase in SAA on postoperative day 1 was significantly attenuated in these groups compared with the control group. No other interaction effects were statistically significant. Tumor stage was not significantly associated with SAA levels. For Hp levels, no significant intergroup differences were observed at any time point. Discussion

The present study showed that UM, particularly in combination with OVH, was associated with numerically higher and more prolonged inflammatory responses compared with OVH alone. Of the measured biomarkers, both CRP and SAA exhibited increases in the UM and BT groups on postoperative day 1, supporting their classification as major canine APPs. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that CRP and SAA are major circulating APPs that undergo measurable changes following surgical intervention in dogs [20–22]. On the other hand, IL-6, IL-1β, and Hp showed non-significant changes among groups, probably because the samples were collected late since cytokine levels attain peak levels within a few hours after injury [23]. The non-significant change in Hp also supports the classification of Hp as a moderate, slow-acting APP [24].

Postoperative pain differed among groups in a time-dependent manner, with significantly higher pain scores observed in the UM and BT groups after emergence from anesthesia than in the control group. These findings are consistent with the concept that greater surgical extent may be associated with increased postoperative pain. The increased pain scores observed in the UM and BT groups, particularly after emergence from anesthesia, are likely attributable to the more extensive tissue excision and manipulation, leading to a local inflammation and nociceptive sensitization response for the excision of mammary glands [25, 26]. For the UM in dogs, the procedure probably generated additive nociceptive input from a wide surgical site, prolonging surgery and intensifying pain, consistent with reports linking multisite procedures to increased inflammatory and neural activation [27]. In this study, pain scores of the UM group remained elevated on postoperative day 1 and subsequently declined over time, which is consistent with the typical postoperative recovery pattern observed in moderate to major canine surgeries. The high pain scores on postoperative day 1 coincided with significantly elevated serum CRP and SAA levels in these groups, suggesting a temporal association between postoperative pain and the acute-phase inflammatory response. Therefore, these APPs may serve as biomarkers of both inflammation and pain severity [24, 28, 29]. All dogs received multimodal analgesia, including opioids and NSAID drugs, which may have attenuated observable differences in early postoperative pain scores and inflammatory biomarkers. Opioids and NSAIDs are known to modulate inflammatory mediators and neuroendocrine stress responses. Although analgesic protocols were standardized across groups to ensure ethical pain management and minimize treatment bias, their pharmacological effects may have reduced the magnitude of detectable intergroup differences. Therefore, the absence of significant differences at certain time points should be interpreted in light of potential analgesic modulation.

Of the APPs tested, SAA and CRP demonstrated significant time-dependent change, with Hp remaining relatively stable over the study period. The rapid and pronounced changes observed in SAA further support its classification as a major APP in dogs, characterized by a swift induction and resolution following inflammatory stimuli. This dynamic profile underscores the utility of SAA as a sensitive biomarker for the early detection and short-term monitoring of acute inflammatory responses, such as perioperative stress [30]. Moreover, CRP remains a clinically valuable indicator for monitoring the progression and resolution of postoperative inflammation and is therefore widely used in both clinical and research settings [31, 32]. Whereas, Hp levels showed minimal variation irrespective of surgical approach or time point. This limited responsiveness is likely attributable to its lower synthesis rate and longer half-life, supporting its classification as a moderate APP with a delayed, less pronounced response to acute inflammatory stimuli [10, 24]. Consequently, Hp may be less suitable for detecting short-term inflammatory changes following surgical trauma but may retain relevance in chronic or subacute inflammatory conditions. Collectively, the distinct response patterns of SAA and CRP highlight their complementary roles in assessing systemic inflammation and postoperative recovery. Therefore, combining these APP assessments may provide complementary information for postoperative monitoring [30, 33].

In the present study, serum IL-1β and IL-6 levels did not differ between groups or change over time despite the fact that they are key inducers of liver APP synthesis [34]. This is in contrast to the high increases of CRP and SAA, which are both downstream effectors of cytokine-induced hepatic stimulation. A possible cause could be the sampling time point. Previous studies have shown that proinflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and IL-1β bind quickly after tissue damage or surgery, and blood levels peak within 4–12 h before decreasing to baseline within 24 h [35, 36]. Although these cytokine levels were measured immediately after surgery and at 24 h postoperatively (postoperative day 1), previous studies indicate that IL-6 and IL-1β may peak within a few hours following tissue injury. Therefore, it is possible that the peak cytokine response occurred between the immediate postoperative sampling and the 24-hour time point, and was not fully captured by the present sampling schedule. The fact that there was no significant increase in the serum level of IL-6 at any time point tested is also in agreement with other studies on dogs, where transient increases in IL-6 have been reported within hours after surgery but only up to 24 h post-surgery [37]. Analogous profiles were observed in humans undergoing surgery in which IL-6 levels reached a maximum the day following surgery, followed by rapid waning [38]. Inter-individual differences in cytokine production might also be important. These differences can also be influenced by variability in immune responses, genetic background, existing inflammation, and perioperative analgesic or anesthetic regimens, among other factors affecting cytokine release and duration of clearance [23].

The postoperative wound score was highest in the UM and BT groups on postoperative day 3, which may reflect greater surgical manipulation in this group. This paralleled the continued high levels of SAA and CRP in the group, reflecting an ongoing systemic inflammation. Chronic inflammation can inhibit the healing process through interfering with hemostasis, cellular proliferation, and remodeling [39]. Continued elevation of cytokines and APPs, even if originally protective, can inhibit fibroblast growth, retard angiogenesis, and encourage matrix breakdown [40]. The increased duration and extent of tissue manipulation in BT dogs may have enhanced inflammation and tissue damage. Hormonal effects due to parallel OVH may also be involved; a sudden fall of estrogen and progesterone affects collagen synthesis, angiogenesis, and cytokine modulation [41, 42]. Delayed wound healing and increased complication rates have been reported in dogs undergoing combined mastectomy and OVH when compared with single-site procedures [43, 44]. Our results imply that the overall trauma from surgery in the UM group and the combined trauma of surgery and hormonal changes in the BT group might have resulted in a systemic and local environment that inhibits normal wound healing, which could be improved with proper postoperative care.

Serial monitoring of APP, particularly SAA and CRP, has been proposed as a valuable adjunct for postoperative assessment [15]. In this study, inflammatory and clinical parameters increased during the first 3 postoperative days, consistent with previous reports of early APP activation following soft tissue surgery [45]. These findings suggest that the initial 72 h represent a period of heightened inflammatory activity during which closer clinical monitoring may be warranted. However, this study was not designed to evaluate discharge protocols or outcome-based APP guidance; therefore, the clinical utility of APP-directed management remains exploratory and requires prospective validation, including cost-effectiveness analysis [46]. Inflammatory markers tended to be numerically higher in dogs undergoing combined unilateral mastectomy and OVH compared with mastectomy alone, although most differences did not reach statistical significance. This pattern may indicate a modest increase in surgical stress associated with the combined procedure; however, the absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as procedural equivalence. Previous reports suggest that concurrent OVH in intact females may be feasible and could reduce the risk of subsequent reproductive tract disease while avoiding repeated anesthesia [5, 6, 47]. Within the limitations of the present cohort, our findings provide preliminary, but not definitive evidence that combined surgery does not appear to induce disproportionate systemic inflammatory responses.

An important structural limitation concerns the composition of groups. The OVH group consisted of clinically healthy bitches, whereas the other groups included dogs with mammary gland tumors. Because neoplastic disease itself may influence baseline metabolism, systemic inflammation, and wound healing capacity, intergroup differences cannot be attributed solely to surgical type. Although the inclusion of non-oncological controls provided a reference for surgical trauma in healthy animals, it limits causal inference regarding the isolated impact of surgical complexity. Future investigations incorporating tumor-bearing controls or randomized allocation would better distinguish the effects of tumor presence from those of procedural extent. Dogs with mammary gland tumors underwent clinical staging; however, histological grading was not performed or incorporated into subgroup analyses. While clinical staging allowed basic characterization of disease extent, mammary gland tumors exhibit heterogeneous biological behavior, and histological grade may substantially influence systemic inflammatory activity. The absence of grading data limits the interpretation of baseline variability and postoperative inflammatory kinetics. Stratified analyses based on tumor grade in larger cohorts would strengthen future studies. Moreover, larger tumors may be associated with greater surgical dissection and tissue trauma, potentially influencing the magnitude of the APR. Although tumor size was incorporated into the clinical staging (modified TNM), the study was not powered to perform stratified analyses by T category. Previous studies have demonstrated that dogs with naturally occurring mammary tumors exhibit increased CRP levels compared with healthy controls, particularly in cases with larger tumors or metastatic disease, supporting the concept that tumor burden itself contributes to systemic inflammation [48, 49]. These findings suggest that tumor-associated inflammatory activity may act as a biological confounder when interpreting perioperative APR in oncologic patients. From an interpretive standpoint, baseline inflammatory variability between groups may reasonably reflect tumor-associated biological activity rather than procedural differences. In contrast, the postoperative temporal increases observed within each group, particularly the day 1 peaks in CRP and SAA, are more plausibly attributable to surgical trauma. The inclusion of group-by-time interaction terms in the statistical model was intended to partially distinguish baseline disease-related effects from differences in postoperative inflammatory dynamics across surgical procedures. In addition, incision length and operative duration were not recorded as fixed parameters, as these variables inherently depend on body size and mammary chain dimensions in individual dogs. However, the surgical approach, the anatomical extent of tissue excision, and the surgeon’s experience were standardized across groups. Therefore, differences in postoperative inflammatory responses are unlikely to be attributable to technical variability, although the influence of individual surgical magnitude cannot be completely excluded.

Methodological considerations should also be acknowledged. Cytokine levels often peak within a few hours following tissue injury, with reported IL-6 and IL-1β maxima occurring approximately 4–12 h postoperatively. Although cytokines were measured immediately after surgery and again at 24 h in the present study, peak levels may have occurred between these sampling points and were therefore not fully captured. Consequently, the absence of marked postoperative elevation in IL-6 and IL-1β should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect sampling kinetics rather than true biological inactivity. In addition, variability in ELISA sensitivity may have influenced the detection of low circulating levels. The earliest postoperative assessment was defined as the time of emergence from anesthesia in order to capture the immediate recovery phase following surgical intervention. However, variability in surgical duration across groups may have led to differences in total anesthetic exposure and perioperative drug metabolism at this time point. In addition, perioperative morphine administration may have influenced early postoperative pain scores and potentially modulated inflammatory markers, introducing measurement bias. Therefore, findings obtained at the first postoperative time point should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect residual anesthetic or opioid effects rather than purely procedure-related inflammatory responses.

Several important limitations must be considered when interpreting these findings. The non-randomized design, the relatively small sample size, tumor heterogeneity, and the limited sampling schedule reduce statistical power and limit generalizability. The study may have been underpowered to detect modest but clinically meaningful differences between surgical approaches. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as limited but preliminary evidence that may have clinical implications. Larger, randomized, and stratified studies with earlier and more frequent sampling intervals are required to more precisely define cytokine kinetics and clarify the clinical value of APP monitoring in dogs undergoing this oncologic surgery.

Conclusions

UM, performed either alone or in combination with OVH, was associated with postoperative changes in pain scores, APP responses, and wound scores compared with OVH alone, most notably during the early postoperative period. Although some inflammatory and clinical parameters were numerically higher in dogs undergoing combined surgery, most comparisons between combined and UM surgery did not reach statistical significance. Of all the biomarkers evaluated, SAA and CRP demonstrated greater consistency and sensitivity in detecting postoperative inflammatory responses under the present sampling schedule. In contrast, IL-1β and IL-6 showed limited discriminatory capacity at the selected time points, possibly due to their rapid, transient kinetic profiles. These findings suggest that APPs, particularly SAA and CRP, provide supportive information for monitoring postoperative inflammatory responses under similar clinical conditions. Prospective long-term follow-up studies are required to further clarify their potential role in perioperative monitoring and clinical decision-making.
