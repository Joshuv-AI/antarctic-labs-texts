PINCH proteins orchestrate vascular mural cell homeostasis through integrated signaling and transcriptional networks
Authors: Chunxiao Wang, Yao Jin, Yuanfeng Xin, Qianke Xing, Hongming Zhu, Qicheng Zou, Jie Yan, Lina Luo, Xingqun Liang, Yunfu Sun, Zhongmin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVascular mural cells (VMCs) are crucial for vascular stability, and their dysfunction underlies cardiovascular pathologies including atherosclerosis and aortic aneurysms. PINCH proteins are core focal adhesion components mediating integrin signaling, yet their roles in VMC development remain elusive. Here, we generated mice with conditional deletion of both PINCH1 and PINCH2 in Pdgfrb-lineage VMCs, which resulted in perinatal lethality accompanied by severe arterial enlargement, hemorrhage and defective angiogenesis. Mutant VMCs exhibited profound defects in cytoskeletal organization, proliferation, differentiation, adhesion and extracellular matrix assembly. Multi-omics analyses revealed that PINCH deficiency dysregulated phospho-signaling networks, hyperactivating PDGFR/EGFR/AKT/ERK and STAT/NF-κB pathways while impairing integrin-FAK-SRC and cell cycle-associated pathways (p53, p27). RNA-seq demonstrated altered expression of genes enriched in immune response (CD74, Tlr2), cytoskeleton (TUBB3, ACTA2) and VMC differentiation (Rgs5, Kcnj8, ABCC9). Importantly, we identified PINCH1 as a nuclear transcriptional coregulator that directly represses proliferative-inflammatory programs while promoting contractile-adhesive and cytoskeletal organization signatures. The clinical relevance of these findings is underscored by downregulation of PINCH genes in human atherosclerosis and Marfan syndrome aneurysms, with conserved dysregulation of key PINCH targets including CD74 and RGS5. Introduction

Vascular development and homeostasis depend on tightly coordinated interactions among endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), pericytes, and immune cells [1–3]. Vascular mural cells (VMCs), including VSMCs and pericytes, play an essential role in vessel stability by generating contractile force, producing extracellular matrix (ECM), and sensing mechanical cues [4]. During vascular development, VMCs are specified, recruited, proliferate, differentiate, and adhere to endothelial cells to build stable, functional vessels [5, 6]. Disruption of these steps causes hemorrhage, barrier dysfunction, and abnormal vascular patterning [7, 8]. VMC dysfunction critically contributes to major cardiovascular diseases, such as atherosclerosis and aortic aneurysm, highlighting the need to elucidate novel molecular mechanisms that maintain VMC homeostasis [9, 10].

Phenotypic plasticity of VSMCs is a hallmark of vascular remodeling. In response to injury, metabolic stress, or inflammation, VSMCs transition from a contractile phenotype, marked by α-SMA, SM22α, and MYH11, to a synthetic state characterized by enhanced proliferation, migration, and inflammation [11, 12]. Single-cell transcriptomic studies have revealed multiple intermediate states in diseased vessels, including macrophage-like, foam cell-like, and osteochondral-like phenotypes, highlighting the complexity of VSMC fate transitions in vivo. While such plasticity can be adaptive, its dysregulation drives neurinoma formation, plaque instability, and vessel wall weakening [12]. The mechanisms that restrain VSMC phenotypic switching under physiological conditions and become dysregulated in disease remain incompletely understood.

Multiple signaling pathways orchestrate mural cell development and function [13, 14]. PDGFR signaling mediates mural cell recruitment, while TGFβ promotes maturation through Smad2 phosphorylation and Notch signaling enhances contractile function by upregulating smooth muscle-specific proteins [15]. VEGF signaling through VEGFR2 and neuropilin-1 regulates arterial growth and patterning [16]. Integrin-mediated signaling is a central mechanism by which cells couple to the ECM and transduce mechanical and biochemical cues. Integrins are heterodimeric transmembrane receptors that physically link the ECM to the intracellular cytoskeleton via focal adhesions (FAs), which serve as anchorage points and signaling hubs modulating cell adhesion, migration, proliferation and differentiation [17]. Integrin-FA complexes crosstalk with receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) through adaptors such as NCK2, thus integrate growth factor inputs (for example, PDGF, VEGF, EGF and FGF) with mechanical signals. Studies have revealed distinct roles of specific integrins in vascular pathophysiology. For instance, integrin α5β1 is upregulated in atherosclerotic plaques and promotes VSMC migration and proliferation, and mediates inflammatory responses [18]. Genetic perturbations of integrins or their adaptors disrupt vascular integrity. Specific integrin subtypes exhibit distinct functional specializations: integrin α1β1 and α7β1 are primarily implicated in the regulation of vascular contractility, whereas integrin α5β1 and αvβ3 critically mediate cellular adhesion processes [19]. More evidence further underscores the essential roles of integrins in vascular homeostasis. Notably, mice lacking α5/αv integrins develop aneurysmal lesions, β1-deficient models exhibit impaired vasomotor function, and ILK knockout mice display progressive aortic dilatation [20–23]. How integrin signaling fidelity is maintained in VMCs—and how its dysregulation feeds into disease-associated phenotypic switching—remains an open question.

PINCH proteins are key components of focal adhesion complex, and play essential roles in multiple cellular processes and organ development [24–27]. Studies have shown that, beyond their canonical cytoplasmic roles, several cytoskeletal and mechanosensitive factors such as YAP/TAZ and αSMA shuttle to the nucleus to regulate gene expression through distinct genetic and epigenetic mechanisms [28–30]. PINCH1 has been shown to translocate into the nucleus in podocytes treated with TGFβ, where it interacts with WT1 to repress podocalyxin expression [31]. Our previous study has shown that PINCH1 is expressed in the nucleus in neural crest cells, where it is required for neural crest cell migration and smooth muscle differentiation [32]. It remains unclear whether PINCH proteins similarly operate in the nucleus of VMCs, how they might coordinate integrin-growth factor/RTK crosstalk with transcriptional control, and whether such mechanisms are perturbed in human vascular disease.

Here, we investigated the roles of PINCH proteins in VMCs using conditional knockout mice and multi-omics approaches. We demonstrate that PINCH deficiency disrupts integrin signaling, leading to hyperactivation of growth factor pathways and aberrant phosphorylation of key regulators of proliferation and differentiation. Furthermore, we identify a novel nuclear role for PINCH1 as a transcriptional co-activator that promotes expression of contractile and adhesive genes while repressing pro-inflammatory and proliferative programs. Finally, we establish clinical relevance by demonstrating conserved downregulation of PINCH1 and PINCH2 and concordant dysregulation of key PINCH target genes in human Marfan syndrome aortas and atherosclerotic plaques.

Materials and methods

Animal models

PINCH1 conditional knockout (PINCH1f/f) and PINCH2 global knockout (PINCH2−/−) mouse lines have previously been established and characterized in detail [25, 27]. We used Pdgfrb-Cre to generate vascular mural cell (VMC) specific double-knockout mouse line. Pdgfrb-Cre mice were crossed with PINCH1f/f; PINCH2−/− mouse line to generate a Pdgfrb-Cre; PINCH1f/+; PINCH2± mouse line, which were back-crossed to PINCH1f/f; PINCH2−/− mouse line to generated VMC-specific PINCH1 and PINCH2 double-knockout (dmut) mice (Pdgfrb-Cre; PINCH1f/f; PINCH2−/−). For lineage tracing, PINCH1f/f; PINCH2−/− mouse line was crossed on a Rosa-tdTomato background (PINCH1f/f; PINCH2−/−; Rosa-tdTomato+/+). All animals were maintained and generated in the Tongji University Laboratory Animal Center under standardized conditions [33]. Experimental protocols complied with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Ministry of Health, China, 1998) and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji University School of Medicine.

Histological analysis

Mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia, and tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h, and paraffin-embedded (San GO-BP N, A601891). Sections (8–10 μm) were prepared and stained with hematoxylin (Sigma, 51275) and eosin (Sigma, HT110132) following established protocols [26, 34]. Elastic fibers were visualized by Verhoeff’s Van Gieson (EVG) staining according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Sigma, HT25A).

Immunostaining analyses

For immunostaining, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h, cryoprotected in graded sucrose solutions (Sigma, V900116), embedded in OCT compound (Sakura, 4583), and sectioned at 8 μm. Sections were blocked with 5% BSA (Sigma, V900933) and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, including α-smooth muscle actin (1:200, Abcam, ab5694), CD31 (1:100, BD, 550,274), laminin (1:200, Abcam, ab11575), elastin (1:200, Abcam, ab21610), Fibronectin (1:200, Abcam, ab45688), Paxillin (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology), Phalloidin (1:500, Abcam, ab176753), BrdU (1:200, Abcam, ab6326). After washing, sections were incubated with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher) for 2 h and mounted with DAPI-containing medium (Vector Labs, H-1200).

For immunofluorescence staining of primary arterial smooth muscle cells, cells were dissociated from mesenteric vessels of dmut and ctrl mice using 50% KOH for 16 h at 4 °C. Cells were stained with antibodies against α-Parvin (1:200, Cell Signaling, 4026) and Ki67 (1:200, Abcam, ab15580).

Evans blue dye

A 2% Evans Blue dye solution was prepared in saline and administered intravenously at a dose of 50 µL per mice. After a 30-min circulation period to allow the dye to distribute, the mices were euthanized, and transcardial perfusion with saline was performed to remove any unbound Evans Blue dye from the vasculature[35]. The brain was then quickly extracted and rinsed with cold saline. Gross inspection of the brain tissue was carried out to observe the distribution of the Evans Blue dye, which stains areas where the blood–brain barrier is compromised. Visible blue staining in specific brain regions, such as the cortex, hippocampus, or cerebellum, indicates Evans Blue dye leakage, suggesting disruption of the blood–brain barrier.

RNA-seq analysis and qPCR quantification

RNA-Seq samples were derived from mice and humans. Mice tissue consisted of aortic media cells isolated from dmut and ctrl mice at E18.5 under dissecting microscopes. Aortic specimens from Marfan syndrome patients for RNA-seq analysis were obtained from Shanghai East Hospital. The three patients in question all met the following criteria: aortic root dilatation with acute type A aortic dissection and a systemic score of ≥ 7 points. Three biological replicates were analyzed as previously described [36]. Raw and processed RNA-seq data are available in the NCBI GEO database (accession number GSE271525). Quality control of the transcriptomics shows in Supplementary Fig. S7. Briefly, total RNA (100 ng) was enriched for poly(A) + RNA using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads, and libraries were generated with the SMARTer cDNA library construction kit (Contech). Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (BGI). The differentially expressed genes were identified by FPKM ≥ 1 in either condition, | the change in FPKM |≥ 1.5-fold and p < 0.05. The DEG threshold was applied to capture pathway-level effects. Gene Ontology—Biological Process (Go-BP) enrichment analysis was performed using the DAVID (v6.7) gene annotation tool.

For qPCR, total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The synthesis of complementary DNA (cDNA) was accomplished through the utilisation of the Superscript™ II Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Invitrogen, 18064014). QPCR was performed employing the Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Life Technologies, 4367659).

Phosphorylated protein chip (PEX100)

Phosphorylated protein chip (pex100) analysis was performed on aortic medial layer cells isolated from dmut and ctrl mice at E18.5 under a dissecting microscope, as previously described [33]. To enrich the mural cell population for subsequent analysis, endothelial cells (ECs) were depleted from freshly isolated aortic cell suspensions. Cells were incubated with anti-CD31 MicroBeads at 4 °C for 15 min, followed by separation using a magnetic column. The flow-through, enriched in EC-depleted cells, was collected. The resulting cell population was then processed for protein extraction. Protein yields were 280.9 μg in ctrl and 312.2 μg in dmut samples. Quality control of the phosphoproteomics shows in Supplementary Fig. S6. Samples were analyzed using PEX100 phosphorylated protein chips and scanned with the SureScan Dx Microarray Scanner. Raw data were processed with GenePix Pro v6.0, and signal intensities from background, positive, and internal reference spots were quantified. Data were normalized, and phosphorylation rates were calculated. The differential phosphorylation ratio was identified by |Phos/Unphos of dmut vs Phos/Unphos of ctrl |≥ 1.5-fold and p < 0.05 to capture the pathway effects closely associated with vascular development. GO-BP enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID (v6.7) gene annotation tool.

CUT&Tag analysis

CUT&Tag was performed on aortic medial layer cells from ctrl mice at E18.5, as previously described [37]. DNA libraries were prepared using the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (Vazyme, TD904). The average peak size was 413 bp, and qPCR quantification yielded 17.86 nmol/L DNA. Quality control of CUT&Tag shows in Supplementary Fig. S8. Sequencing quality was assessed with FastQC, and adapters and low-quality reads were trimmed. Reads were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner [38] with mapping quality > 13. Duplicates were removed, and peaks were called with MACS2. Narrow peaks (n = 50,746) and total peaks (n = 50,878) were identified. Sequences spanning ± 250 bp from peak summits were analyzed with HOMER to determine conserved motifs. Peak distribution across genomic features was annotated with ChIPseeker, and GO-BP enrichment analyses were performed using DAVID (v6.7). The PINCH1 antibody (clone 49, BD Biosciences) used for CUT&Tag has been used for application in IP assays in prior studies [39].

Western blot

For protein extraction, ctrl and dmut mice at E18.5 were euthanized under general anesthesia, and the medial and adventitial layers of the aorta were carefully separated. Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (Sigma, R0278) supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Thermo, 36978) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 049068450011). Western blotting was conducted according to standard protocols. The following primary antibodies were used: phospho-FAK (1:1000, Abcam, ab4792), FAK (1:1000, Abcam, ab40794), phospho-P27 (1:1000, Abcam, ab32034), P27 (1:1000, Abcam, ab32034), phospho-Src (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 2105), Src (1:1000, Abcam, ab133283), AKT (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4685), phospho-AKT (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 9271), ERK (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4695), phospho-ERK (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4370), GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech, HRP6004), SM22α(1:1000, Sigma-Aldrich, MABT167), PINCH1 (clone 49, BD Biosciences) and PCNA (1:1000, Invitrogen, 13-3900). Relative protein expression was quantified using ImageJ software (v1.8.0).

Primary mouse vascular cell culture

Primary vascular medial cells were isolated from ctrl and dmut aortae at E18.5, as previously described [40]. Tissues were digested twice at 37 °C for 15 min with collagenase/dispase (1 mg/mL, Roche, 10.269.638.001) and papain (Worthington, LS003119). Endothelial cells were enriched using CD31+ magnetic beads (Miltenyi, 120-009-894) and plated in uncoated 60-mm Corning dishes for ≤ 1 h. Non-adherent fibroblasts were removed by washing, and adherent endothelial cells were cultured on fibronectin-coated coverslips in DMEM/F-12 medium (GIBCO, 11965 and 11765) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (GIBCO, 10099141) for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in several independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were evaluated using unpaired Student’s t-tests (n ≥ 3). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with p value < 0.05, 0.01 or 0.001 denoted by one to three asterisks (*).

Results

Loss of PINCH in Pdgfrb-lineage cells causes severe arterial enlargement and defective angiogenesis

To investigate the role of PINCH in vascular mural cells (vascular smooth muscle cells/pericytes) during vascular development, we generated mice with conditional deletion of both PINCH1 and PINCH2 specifically in the Pdgfrb-Cre lineage (Pdgfrb-Cre; PINCH1/2f/f; hereafter termed double mutants or dmut). Western blot and qPCR analysis confirmed efficient and specific knockout of PINCH1, with significantly reduced PINCH1 protein levels in the aorta of dmut mice relative to controls (Supplementary Fig. S1A, B). A majority of dmut mice died within 24 h after birth, with none surviving beyond 3.5 days. Wholemount imaging and quantitative analysis at postnatal day 0.5 (P0.5) revealed pronounced enlargement of the aorta in dmut neonates compared to control littermates. This aortic enlargement was particularly severe within the abdominal segment (Fig. 1A–C, arrows).

H&E staining and morphometric quantification confirmed the aortic dilation, characterized by a significantly increased luminal diameter and thickened vascular wall in mutants (Fig. 1D–G). The organization of aortic VSMCs were profoundly disrupted in mutants. While control VSMCs displayed a characteristic flattened morphology with elongated, spindle-like nuclei oriented circumferentially along the vessel wall, mutant VSMCs were significantly increased in number, adopted a rounded shape, and were marked disorganization (Fig. 1D–G). Wholemount analysis of the P0.5 forebrain parenchyma revealed numerous hemorrhagic foci in dmut mice (Fig. 1H, I), indicating vascular fragility and compromised blood–brain barrier integrity. To directly evaluate blood–brain barrier integrity, we conducted Evans Blue dye extravasation assays. Consistent with the observed hemorrhagic foci, dmut mice showed significantly increased dye leakage into the brain parenchyma compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. S1C, D), further confirming the disruption of the blood–brain barrier. Examination of the Pdgfrb-Cre lineage-labeled (Tomato+) cerebral vasculature revealed significant arterial abnormalities in dmut brains (Fig. 1J, K). The basilar artery (ba), Circle of Willis and middle cerebral arteries (mca) in mutants were consistently enlarged overall and displayed frequent localized narrowing and truncation of branches (Fig. 1J, K, yellow arrowheads; Fig. 1J1, J2, K1, K2). The diameter of the mutant basilar artery was significantly increased (Fig. 1L).

Lineage tracing within the heart highlighted defects in coronary artery development. Control coronary arteries branched extensively and formed a well-developed, hierarchical capillary network (Fig. 1M, M1). In contrast, dmut coronary arteries were significantly dilated and exhibited a variety of malformations, including excessive shortened branches, narrowing, beading, and truncation of the vessels (Fig. 1N, N1), suggesting impaired yet active ectopic angiogenesis. Co-immunostaining for BrdU and PECAM revealed significantly increased proliferation of coronary endothelial cells in mutant hearts at E17.5 (Fig. 1O, P), suggesting that VSMC defects non-cell-autonomously resulted in aberrant EC proliferation and dysregulated angiogenesis. In addition, histological analysis of carotid arteries revealed the similar arterial defects. Mutant carotid arteries displayed significantly increased luminal diameter, thickened vessel walls, and an increased number and disorganization of VSMCs compared to controls (Fig. 1Q–T). We performed histological analysis to determine when the aortic enlargement phenotype first appears. Significant aortic dilation and an increased number of associated mural cells were already detectable in mutants at E11.5 (Supplementary Fig. S2A-F) and persisted at E13.5 (Supplementary Fig. S2G-J). Analysis of venous structures showed the cardinal vein (CV) at E11.5 and inferior vena cava (IVC) at E13.5 were grossly normal with no evidence of dilation.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that deletion of PINCH1 and PINCH2 in mural cells leads to enlargement of major arteries (aorta, basilar, carotid, coronary), disorganization and rounding of VSMCs, vascular wall thickening, hemorrhage, and defective arterial patterning, indicative of impaired angiogenesis. The early onset of these structural defects suggests a primary cellular origin, although we cannot rule out subsequent contributions from altered hemodynamics.

PINCH deficiency disrupts VSMC differentiation, ECM assembly, and promotes proliferation

We next examined cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying the vascular malformations in PINCH mutant mice. Co-immunostaining for Pecam (endothelium) and αSMA (VSMCs) at P0.5 revealed that mutant aortic VSMCs lost their characteristic spindle shape and circumferential alignment, becoming rounded, disorganized with increased cellularity (Fig. 2A, B; inset 1, 2). Quantification showed significantly elevated αSMA expression and a loss of cellular orientation in mutant VSMCs (Fig. 2A’, B’). Similarly, control basilar and coronary arteries displayed a continuous monolayer of spindle-shaped VSMCs closely associated with the endothelium. In contrast, mutant VSMCs were rounded, disorganized and forming aberrant multilayered foci (2–3 layers) interspersed with regions of layer disruption (Fig. 2C–F, arrowheads and insets). Quantification showed significantly elevated αSMA expression and a loss of cellular orientation in mutant VSMCs (Supplementary Fig. S3A, B). We examined VSMC development at early stage. At E12.5, control aortic VSMCs were flattened and organized into 2–3 concentric layers, while mutant cells already exhibited rounded/disorganized morphology and disrupted layer assembly (Fig. 2G, H), indicating a failure in early differentiation.

Given the role of integrin signaling in extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, we assessed, ECM expression and organization in PINCH mutant vessels. Co-immunostaining for αSMA and laminin or fibronectin in mutant aortas showed defective assembly of laminin and fibronectin fibers (Fig. 2I–L; insets). Similarly, co-immunostaining for Pecam and elastin (ELN) demonstrated disrupted elastin fiber formation in mutant aortas (Fig. 2R, S). Consistent with this, EVG staining of dorsal aorta sections at P0.5 revealed severe elastin fiber defects in mutants. Quantification of elastin fiber breakpoints per cross-section showed a significant increase in mutants (Supplementary Fig. S3C-E). Defective laminin basement membrane formation was also observed in mutant cerebral and coronary arteries (Fig. 2M–P; insets and arrowheads). Quantification revealed a significantly increase in laminin and fibronectin expression in mutant aortas (Fig. 2Q).

BrdU incorporation assays demonstrated markedly increased proliferation of Pdgfrb-Cre lineage (Tomato+) VSMCs in mutant aortas at E12.5 (Supplementary Fig. S3F, G) and P0.5 (Fig. 2T–V), consistent with hypercellularity observed in mutant vessels.

PINCH orchestrates vascular morphogenesis by coordinating integrin and growth factor signaling networks

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying PINCH-dependent vascular morphogenesis, we performed phosphoproteomic profiling of E18.5 control and dmut aortas. We identified 168 significantly dysregulated phosphorylation sites (96 upregulated, 72 downregulated; fold-change ≥ 1.5, p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 1). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that upregulated phosphoproteins were involved in proliferation, muscle stretch, ERK signaling, angiogenesis and actin filament assembly, while downregulated phosphoproteins were associated with blood vessel remodeling, adhesion, integrin signaling pathway, cell differentiation (Fig. 3A–C). STRING analysis revealed a complex interactive signaling network, consisting of multiple signaling pathways including integrin (ITGB1/B3) and several growth factor (PDGF/EGF/VEGF) and immune (STAT1/5a, NF-κB, mTOR) signaling pathways. We observed antagonistic phosphorylation states within key signaling hubs: ITGB3 was hyperphosphorylated at Y785 but hypophosphorylated at Y773, ITGB1 was hyperphosphorylated at T788, coupled with reduced phosphorylation of FAK and SRC, but increased phosphorylation of RAC1, the key downstream mediators of integrin signaling, indicating dysregulated integrin and cytoskeleton signaling. Concurrently, hyperphosphorylation of EGFR at T678, a phosphorylation site that regulates kinase dimerization and downstream signaling pathways, such as MAPK/ERK, PI3K-AKT and JAK-STAT signaling pathways involved in mitogenic and immune responses [41]. PDGFRB hyperphosphorylation at Y751, a docking site for PI3K, leading to increased proliferation [42]. Hyperphosphorylation of VEGFR2/KDR at Y1175 leads to activation of ERK and Akt, promoting proliferation and angiogenesis [43], but concomitant defective integrin signaling likely resulted in impaired mural cell recruitment and adhesion, causing nonproductive angiogenesis, vascular leakage and hemorrhage observed in PINCH mutants (Fig. 3C, D). AKT and MAPK/ERK are key hubs downstream of multiple signaling pathways, critical for cell survival, proliferation, metabolism and inflammation. We observed increased phosphorylation of AKT(S246) and MAPK8/14, alongside altered phosphorylation of TP53 (T18/S315) and NF-κB pathway components (e.g., NFKBIA at S32/S36), and hyperphosphorylation of STAT1 (T701), mTOR (S2448) and Elk1 (S383) [44], promoted a state of unchecked proliferation and inflammation (Fig. 3C, D).

Further analysis identified dysregulated subnetworks controlling cytoskeleton /adhesion (INGB1/B3, CTTN, VAV2, EZR, PYK2, FAK, JUNB, MERLIN2, p130CAS, MARCKS) and mitotic cell cycle (Tp53, p27, CHK1/2, CDC25A/CCDK2) (Fig. 3E, E’, F, F’). For instance, reduced phosphorylation of p27 impaired differentiation, while hyperphosphorylation of TP53 at T18/S315 reduced its stability and transcriptional activity, further exacerbating proliferation (Fig. 3F, F’).

Key changes in protein phosphorylation and expression were verified by Western blot (Fig. 3G–K, K’). Phosphorylation of SRC and FAK were significantly reduced, confirming impaired integrin signaling (Fig. 3G, G’, H, H’). Conversely, phosphorylation of AKT and ERK were increased, consistent with hyperactivated mitogenic signaling (Fig. 3I, I’ J, J’). p27 expression and phosphorylation were diminished, aligning with defective VSMC differentiation. To assess differentiation impairment in PINCH-deficient vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), we examined the expression of the key contractile marker SM22α/TAGLN. Western blot analysis confirmed its protein level was significantly downregulated. (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B).

We further examine alterations in cell proliferation, cytoskeleton and focal adhesion organization in dmut VSMCs in culture. EdU staining revealed a greater than twofold increase in proliferating Pdgfrb-lineage VSMCs (Tomato+) in mutants (Fig. 3L, M, arrow; inset 1, 2). Phalloidin staining revealed disorganized cortical actin fibers and a failure to extend into cellular protrusions in mutants (Fig. 3O, P; inset 1, 2). Immunostaining further showed a marked reduction of Parvin and Paxillin in focal adhesions, indicating defective adhesion site assembly (Fig. 3Q–T; insets). In addition, mutant VSMCs exhibited excessive elongated filopodia-like protrusions (Fig. 3L–P), suggesting increased cell motility and disorientation, consistent with ectopic angiogenesis observed in vivo (Fig. 2I–N).

Together, these results implicated PINCH as a key mediator of signaling crosstalk between integrin and other growth factor pathways essential for mural cell cytoskeleton organization, migration, proliferation, differentiation and immune response during mural cell development.

Integrated transcriptomics implicates PINCH in pericyte dysfunction and conserved vascular pathogenesis

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms of PINCH in mural cells, we performed RNA-seq analysis of E18.5 mutant and control aortas. Identified and 356 differentially expressed genes in mutants (DEGs; 94 upregulated, 262 downregulated; |log₂FC|≥ 0.58, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table 2). Heatmap visualization showed DEGs involved in adhesion, cytoskeletal organization, ECM dynamics, immune signaling and innervation (Fig. 4B). GO analysis revealed that upregulated genes were enriched for inflammation, cell cycle, chromosome organization and ERK pathway, while downregulated genes were involved in cell junctions, synapses and innervation, vessel development, and muscle contractile and actin-cytoskeleton (Fig. 4C, D). STRING analysis of DEGs revealed three core modules: an inflammatory cluster (CD74, TLR2 and TYROBP), a cytoskeletal module (TUBB3, Acta2 and Col1a1) and a cluster of Wnt and ion channel signaling pathway (Rgs5, Kcnj8 and ABCC9) (Fig. 4E). Notably, CD74, a key regulator of immune response and VSMC proliferation upregulated in human atherosclerosis and aortic artery aneurysm [45, 46], was a top hub gene in PINCH mutants. Conversely, Rgs5 (an inhibitor of mural cell proliferation [47–49] and the pericyte-enriched K-ATP channel components Kcnj8 and Abcc9 (critical for VMC differentiation and BBB integrity) [50] were downregulated. Changes in selected genes were validated by qPCR, including those involved in cytoskeleton organization and adhesion (decreased: Stmn3, Capg, Plec, Fat3/4, Col1a1), immune/proliferation (increased: CD74, Tlr2, NPY, Acta2; decreased: Lrp1, Ubc, Crebbp), signaling and neural innervation (decreased: Rgs5, Ephb2, Ntrk3, Ntn1, Fgf1, Sdk1, Syp, Nefm) and Wnt pathway-related genes (decreased: Sfrp4, Ptpro) (Fig. 4F, G).

The marked reduction of pericyte markers (Rgs5, Kcnj8, and Abcc9) in PINCH mutants prompted us to examine pericyte morphogenesis. Co-immunostaining of NG2 and Pecam showed that NG2⁺ pericytes in mutants were poorly attached, failed to spread along endothelium, and frequently detached, leaving vasculature exposed (Fig. 4H, I; inset 1 and 2, arrows). Quantification showed an increase in total pericytes and a higher proportion of immature, “attached-yet-unspread” cells, suggesting impaired pericyte differentiation and maturation (Fig. 4J, K). Consistent with significant decreased genes of innervation in mutants, TH immunostaining revealed markedly reduced sympathetic innervation along mutant mesenteric vessels (Fig. 4L, M) The concurrent downregulation of FGF1 suggests a potential mechanism contributing to this disrupted neurovascular unit.

To further investigate the potential roles of PINCH in pathogenesis of human atherosclerosis and aneurysm, we performed RNA-seq analysis of aortas from human Marfan syndrome (Fig. 4N) and utilized published datasets of human atherosclerosis [51] (Fig. 4O) to examine expression of PINCH1/PINCH2 (LIMS1/LIMS2) and key PINCH target genes. We found significant downregulation of PINCH1 and PINCH2, accompanied by a conserved pattern of dysregulation in key downstream genes, including CD74 (3.84), TLR2 (1.48) and ACTA2 (3.93), RGS5 (-0.71), UBC (-0.78) and FGF1 (-0.51), suggesting conserved disease mechanisms.

To better understand how PINCH-regulated signaling pathways were interconnected to downstream transcriptional gene expression, we performed an integrated analysis of transcriptomic and phosphoproteomic datasets and identified hub genes bridging signaling cascades to transcriptional outputs, including Crebbp, Ubc, Col1a1 and FGF1 (decreased); Tlr2, Acta2 and FGF15 (increased) (Fig. 4P).

These findings establish that PINCH deficiency orchestrates a broad transcriptional reprogramming that destabilizes vascular homeostasis through impaired VSMC and pericyte function, dysregulated matrix dynamics, and disrupted neurovascular crosstalk, with direct relevance to human vascular pathologies.

Nuclear PINCH1 binds chromatin and directly regulates a transcriptional program for vascular maturation

Recent studies have highlighted the crucial role of the nuclear translocation of cytoskeletal proteins, such as YAP and αSMA, in mechanotransduction, gene expression, and cellular differentiation [30, 52]. PINCH1 has been shown to translocate into the nucleus in podocytes treated with TGFβ, where it interacts with WT1 to repress podocalyxin expression [31]. Our previous study has shown that PINCH1 is expressed in the nucleus in neural crest cells, where it is required for cell migration and smooth muscle differentiation [32].We found PINCH1 expression within nuclear fraction of aortic VSMCs by Western Blot (Fig. 5A). To investigate whether nuclear PINCH1 plays a role in transcription and define the genome-wide binding landscape of nuclear PINCH1, we performed ChIP-seq (Cut&Tag) analysis on E18.5 aortas using PINCH1 antibody. The analysis identified 10,222 high-confidence PINCH1-binding peaks genome-wide (Score > 40; SignalValue > 3; -log10 p-value > 4) (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Table 3). Genomic annotation revealed these peaks were distributed across promoter (18.12%), intronic (36.88%), and or intergenic regions (41.54%) (Fig. 5B). We performed a bioinformatic analysis of the intergenic PINCH1 binding regions (~ 41%) and found a substantial portion of these intergenic peaks mapped to established regulatory elements, of which approximately 19 intergenic fragments reside within VISTA enhancers, and 50 fragments are in regulatory elements annotated with vascular development functions. Motif analysis within PINCH1-bound regions uncovered significant enrichment for the binding sites of transcription factors with broad roles in general transcription initiation (TATA-box), proliferation and cell cycle control (E2F8, TOP2), stress response (ATF4, Esrra), and differentiation (KLF10, ZEB1, Foxh1, SP1, HIF2a) (Fig. 5C). This suggests that PINCH1 may function as a co-regulator within core transcriptional complexes governing basic cellular processes. GO analysis of genes associated with PINCH1 binding sites revealed enrichment for biological processes critical for vascular development, including cell differentiation, adhesion, MAPK cascade signaling, cytoskeleton and ECM organization, and angiogenesis (Fig. 5D). Intersection of Cut&Tag and RNA-seq datasets identified 180 direct transcriptional targets of PINCH1, with 152 significantly downregulated and 28 upregulated in mutant VSMCs, suggesting that PINCH1 predominantly functions as a transcriptional co-activator, necessary for promoting the expression of genes controlling cytoskeleton organization and adhesion (Capg, Stmn2, Fat3, Sdk1) and signaling for neuronal guidance and vascular development (Ntrk3, Ephb2, Fgf1, Kcnj8). Conversely, PINCH1 acts as a repressor for a specific subset of targets involved in proliferation and immune signaling (CD74, ulk4, fgf15) (Fig. 5E). GO analysis of these direct targets confirmed their involvement in axonogenesis, immune response, differentiation, migration and ECM organization (Fig. 5F). Notably, key hub genes identified in our network analyses, including CD74, Fgf1, Fgf15, and Crebbp, were confirmed as direct targets. IGV visualization showed PINCH1 occupancy at regulatory elements of these critical genes, including CD74, Fgf15, Cntn4, Crebbp, Kcnj8-Abcc9 and Col25a1 (Fig. 5G). These findings suggest that, in addition to its canonical role in focal adhesions, nuclear PINCH1 serves as a transcriptional coregulator in VSMCs. It represses genes involved in immune response and proliferation while promoting programs associated with cytoskeleton organization, muscle contractility, cell adhesion, and neuronal guidance, thereby playing a dual role in fine-tuning vascular maturation.

Together, we propose a unified model wherein PINCH orchestrates vascular maturation through dual mechanisms in the cytoplasm and nucleus (Fig. 5H). Cytoplasmic PINCH-Integrin signaling is essential for maintaining VSMC quiescence, ECM organization, and focal adhesion stability. Nuclear PINCH1 directly represses a pro-inflammatory, proliferative transcriptional program while promoting a gene signature for differentiation and contractility. Loss of PINCH disrupts both arms of this regulation, leading to a hyperproliferative, pro-inflammatory phenotypic switch that drives vascular pathogenesis.

Discussion

Vascular mural cells (VMCs), including vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) and pericytes, are fundamental to vascular development and stability. Their proper differentiation, recruitment, and adhesion are critical for maintaining vascular integrity, and dysregulation of these processes underpins the pathogenesis of common cardiovascular diseases like atherosclerosis and aortic aneurysms [2, 7, 10]. Our study demonstrates that conditional deletion of PINCH1 and PINCH2 in Pdgfrb-lineage cells causes profound vascular defects. These defects include impaired VMC differentiation and organization, aberrant proliferation and defective extracellular matrix (ECM) assembly, ultimately leading to arterial enlargement and hemorrhage. By integrating multi-omics approaches, we uncover a dual mechanistic role for PINCH as a scaffold for integrin signaling in the cytoplasm and as a transcriptional coregulator in the nucleus. This coordinated regulation is essential for maintaining VMC differentiation and preventing a pathological shift towards a pro-inflammatory, proliferative state.

PINCH-integrin signaling axis is a master regulator of VSMC phenotype and vascular homeostasis

The severe vascular phenotypes in PINCH mutants align with established models of integrin dysfunction, underscoring a shared requirement for a functional integrin-adhesion complex in transmitting mechanical and biochemical signals for vessel wall formation and stability [7, 20, 21, 23, 46, 53–56]. For instance, Itgb1 and Itga4 are essential for VMC adhesion, spreading and vascular stability. Deletion of those integrin subunits caused VMC defects similar to that observed in PINCH mutants. VSMC-specific deletion of ILK, a key component of the FA complex, leads to aortic aneurysm formation [21]. Despite these findings, underlying molecular mechanisms are not fully understood. Our phosphoproteomic analysis revealed that PINCH deficiency creates a profound imbalance in integrin-mediated signaling pathways. We observed an antagonistic phosphorylation pattern across multiple integrin subunits, with ITGB3 showing Y785 hyperphosphorylation versus Y773 hypophosphorylation, accompanied by increased ITGB1 (T788) phosphorylation (Fig. 3E’, Table S2). These integrin-signaling abnormalities were coupled with significant alterations in key downstream effectors, including reduced phosphorylation of FAK and SRC (Fig. 3G, H), but increased phosphorylation of RAC1 (S71). This altered phosphorylation pattern is consistent with disrupted adhesion and cytoskeleton organization, but increased cell motility and excessive ectopic angiogenesis observed in PINCH mutants.

Crosstalk between the integrin adhesion complex (IAC) and growth factor receptors (RTK) can potentiate mitogenic signaling, yet the precise mechanisms governing this interplay remain incompletely understood. Our findings reveal that PINCH deficiency results in constitutive activation of multiple growth factor pathways, evidenced by hyperphosphorylation of PDGFRB (Y751), EGFR (T678), VEGFR2 (Y1175), and their downstream effectors AKT and ERK (Fig. 3I, J; Table S2). These results suggest that PINCH proteins normally function as critical regulators or "brakes" on RTK signaling in VMCs, and that loss of PINCH eliminates these constraints. The proliferative drive was further amplified by concurrent dysregulation of key cell cycle regulators. This included reduced phosphorylation and expression of the cell cycle inhibitor p27, alongside altered phosphorylation of c-JUN (T93, S243) and JUNB (S259), modifications known to stabilize the AP-1 transcription factor complex and enhance its pro-mitotic activity [57]. Additionally, we observed altered phosphorylation of core cell cycle components CDK2, CDC25A/C, and CHK1/2, coupled with reduced phosphorylation of TP53 (T18), a modification known to enhance TP53 degradation and proliferation. Collectively, these changes create a permissive cellular environment that promotes unchecked proliferation while simultaneously impairing differentiation, providing a molecular explanation for the hypercellular yet dysfunctional vessel walls observed in PINCH mutants. While the functional necessity of these individual pathway alterations was not tested here, our data position PINCH as an essential upstream node. Its loss creates a concerted imbalance across the integrated adhesion and growth factor signaling network. Therefore, rescuing individual downstream effectors like AKT or ERK is less likely to restore the complex homeostatic state.

Nuclear PINCH1 as a transcriptional coregulator: fine-tuning the gene programs for VMC stability

Beyond its cytoplasmic roles, our study reveals a novel nuclear function for PINCH1 as a transcriptional coregulator in VMCs. Genome-wide mapping identified 10,222 PINCH1-binding sites, with binding motif analysis revealing enrichment for transcription factors regulating proliferation (E2F8, TOP2), differentiation (KLF10, ZEB1), and stress response (ATF4, ESRRA). Integrative analysis of chromatin binding and transcriptomic data demonstrated that PINCH1 functions predominantly as a transcriptional activator. Loss of PINCH1 resulting in significant downregulation of 152 out of 180 direct target genes, which mainly fall into three coregulated gene clusters: a cytoskeletal/contractile cluster (ACTA2, TUBB3, COL1A1), a VMC-specific signaling cluster (RGS5, KCNJ8, ABCC9) and an inflammatory/proliferative cluster (increased: CD74, TLR2, TYROBP). The repression of the RGS5, KCNJ8, and ABCC9 cluster is particularly significant given their VSMC-specific expression pattern and established roles in vascular morphogenesis and blood–brain barrier integrity [47–50]. This repression provides a direct mechanistic link to the observed phenotypes, including immature VSMC/pericyte morphology, poor vascular coverage, and hemorrhage in our mutants. Conversely, the derepression of CD74, a shared hub gene upregulated in human atherosclerosis and aortic aneurysms [45, 46, 51, 58] that activates AKT signaling to promote proliferation and inflammatory responses, may directly contributing to the vascular pathogenesis upon PINCH ablation.

Our discovery of nuclear PINCH1 aligns with emerging evidence that multiple cytoskeletal proteins, including αSMA [30] and YAP/TAZ [28, 29], translocate to the nucleus to regulate vascular smooth muscle cell differentiation and maintain vascular integrity. Analogous to the mechanosensitive shuttling of YAP/TAZ, PINCH1's nucleocytoplasmic distribution may similarly be regulated by biomechanical cues, suggesting that their coordinated nuclear functions likely represent a sophisticated mechanism for maintaining vascular homeostasis in response to mechanical and biochemical cues.

Integrated signaling and transcriptional network dysregulation in vascular pathogenesis

Further analysis identified several hub genes that coordinated the phospho-signaling networks to transcriptional programs, including FGF1, CREBBP, TLR2, UBC, ACTA2 and COL1A1. These hub genes represent critical points of convergence where signaling changes translate to sustained transcriptional reprogramming, and explain how PINCH deficiency orchestrates a coordinated pathogenic response across cellular compartments.

Interestingly, while the canonical TGF-β/SMAD pathway and its classic transcriptional targets (e.g., MYOCD, TAGLN) appeared largely intact at the mRNA level, the significant downregulation of SM22α/TAGLN protein points to post-transcriptional dysregulation. This supports a model where PINCH regulates a complementary, parallel network essential for VMC homeostasis. Furthermore, PINCH deficiency led to pericyte immaturity and loss of sympathetic innervation. The observed downregulation of FGF1 may contribute to these phenotypes, as FGF signaling can stimulate pericyte recruitment via PDGFRβ [59, 60]. Notably, the imbalance between FGF and TGF-β signaling, where FGF antagonizes TGF-β's contractile program [61, 62], could also underlie the discordant gene expression (e.g., elevated ACTA2). These interconnected mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Clinical implications and conclusion

The clinical significance of our findings is underscored by the conserved dysregulation observed in human vascular diseases. We found significant downregulation of PINCH1 and PINCH2 in human atherosclerotic plaques and Marfan syndrome aortas, accompanied by parallel dysregulation of PINCH1 target genes, particularly CD74 and RGS5 clusters, across species and diseases. Additionally, similar phosphorylation changes have been observed in clinical vascular pathological conditions, particularly in integrin signaling, growth factor receptors, and inflammatory pathways [2, 7, 54, 63], suggesting that the mechanisms identified in our study have direct relevance to human pathogenesis and represent potential promising therapeutic targets.

In conclusion, our study suggests that PINCH proteins maintain VMC homeostasis through an integrated mechanism. In the cytoplasm, they scaffold integrin signaling to constrain growth factor activation and promote mechanical integrity. In the nucleus, PINCH1 directly or indirectly activates transcriptional programs for differentiation while repressing programs for proliferation and inflammation. The coordination of these dual functions ensures VMC quiescence and vascular stability. Their disruption leads to a coordinated signaling-transcriptional dysregulation that drives vascular pathogenesis, revealing new potential therapeutic nodes for treating vascular diseases.
