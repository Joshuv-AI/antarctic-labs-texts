Prognostic scoring model incorporating clinical characteristics for pediatric hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Shu-yi Guo, Ru Tang, Shi-lin Liu, Li-hua Yang, Yun-yan He, Liu-hua Liao, Hong Wen, Sen-lin Luo, Xiang Lan, Ya-wei Zou, Hui-qin Chen, Xing-jiang Long, Qi-wen Chen, Hai-xia Guo, Cong Liang, Hao-yan Ren, Si-xi Liu, Dun-hua Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediatric hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) lacks reliable risk models for personalized treatment. We aimed to develop a survival nomogram to address this gap. We retrospectively analyzed 404 pediatric HLH patients from multiple centers. Independent prognostic variables identified by Cox regression were incorporated into a nomogram. Five predictors of mortality were identified: central nervous system involvement, gastrointestinal bleeding, WBC < 2.3 × 10⁹/L, Hb < 80 g/L, and IL-10 > 69pg/mL. The nomogram demonstrated strong predictive accuracy, with validation cohort AUCs of 0.83, 0.78, and 0.79 for 1-, 2-, and 3-year overall survival (OS). Calibration and decision curve analysis confirmed accuracy. Using an X-tile-determined cutoff of 261, patients were stratified into low- and high-risk groups with significantly different 3-year OS (training cohort: 94.0% vs. 47.2%; validation cohort: 88.2% vs. 43.2%; p < 0.0001). This multicenter study established and externally validated a nomogram-based model that enables early risk stratification and may inform individualized therapy for pediatric HLH. Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome resulting from genetic or acquired immune dysregulation, leading to multi-organ dysfunction. It can occur at any age and is broadly classified as primary (pHLH), associated with genetic defects, or secondary (sHLH), triggered by autoimmune diseases, infections, or malignancies [1]. The diagnosis is typically confirmed using the HLH-2004 criteria [2], which continue to be the clinical benchmark.

The HLH-1994 and HLH-2004 guidelines, which primarily include corticosteroids and etoposide, have improved the 5-year overall survival rate (OS) to 54 ± 6% [3]. Despite this progress, standard immunotherapy is not suitable for all patients, and significant heterogeneity remains in the initial treatment strategies. In some critically ill children, standard therapy may worsen outcomes by increasing the risk of infection or treatment-related mortality. Therefore, the development of precise risk prediction models is critically important for informing personalized treatment strategies and enhancing patient outcomes.

Previous studies have demonstrated that etiology, clinical characteristics, and early treatment response are key determinants of prognosis in patients with HLH. For example, Verkamp et al. [4] reported that elevated soluble CD25 levels and poor response to etoposide on day 7 were associated with increased mortality risk. Pegoraro et al. [5] identified elevated serum ferritin at onset as a predictor of poor prognosis in primary HLH. In recent years, several prognostic scoring systems have used nomograms or machine learning approaches. Wang Zhao et al. [6] proposed the CEPHO-PSS, which stratified patients based on chronic active Epstein-Barr virus (CAEBV) infection, plasma EBV-DNA load, gastrointestinal and pulmonary bleeding, infection, and hypoxemia. Similarly, Wei et al. [7] introduced the HHLWG-HPI score for adult HLH, incorporating six variables: age, serum creatinine, albumin, platelet count, lymphocyte ratio, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). Despite these advances, large-scale, robust studies focusing on pediatric HLH remain scarce.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed pediatric HLH cases from multiple centers in South China, focusing on epidemiology, triggers, and prognostic factors. Our primary objective was to develop a clinically applicable survival prediction model and assess the prognostic relevance of key risk factors for pediatric HLH.

Materials and methods

Patients

Between January 2020 and July 2024, 424 children diagnosed with HLH from 18 institutions were recruited. Inclusion criteria included: (1) diagnosis of HLH meeting the HLH-2004 criteria; (2) age under 18 years; (3) parental or legal guardian consent; and (4) complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria were: (1) insufficient diagnostic evidence for HLH; and (2) missing essential clinical data.

HLH subtypes and diagnostic approach

HLH encompasses diverse subtypes. Immunodeficiency-associated HLH arises from congenital or acquired defects—often genetic mutations impairing NK and CTL function—leading to uncontrolled inflammation. Infection-associated HLH is most frequently EBV-related in Asia, confirmed by ≥ 10³ EBV-DNA copies/mL on PCR. Malignancy-associated HLH occurs mainly in hematologic cancers, particularly T-cell lymphoma, while rheumatologic disease–associated HLH is linked to systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis, lupus, or necrotizing lymphadenitis. Cases of unknown etiology met HLH-2004 and HScore criteria but lacked an identifiable trigger despite extensive testing. Diagnosis combined HLH-2004 criteria with HScore (≥ 169 for secondary HLH) [8] and PRINTO 2016 MAS criteria for rheumatologic cases [9]. All patients underwent multidisciplinary review (hematology, rheumatology, immunology) to exclude HLH-like disorders, ensuring diagnostic accuracy.

Cohort division and ethical considerations

A total of 424 pediatric patients were collected, among whom 20 cases had missing key clinical data. Ultimately, 404 patients were included and randomly divided into a training cohort (n = 282) and a validation cohort (n = 122) at a 7:3 ratio. The study received ethical approval from the institutional ethics committee (approval number: SYSKY-2024-962−01), and all procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Baseline characteristics and follow-up

Baseline data from 404 pediatric patients at admission were collected, including demographics, underlying etiologies, and clinical manifestations such as fever, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, central nervous system (CNS) symptoms, and gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding. Laboratory assessments comprised routine blood counts, biochemical markers such as serum triglycerides (TG) and albumin (Ab), coagulation parameters, ferritin (SF), Epstein-Barr virus DNA (EBV-DNA) quantification, and liver enzymes. Cytokine levels were also measured. Treatment regimens were recorded, and survival time was tracked from diagnosis to death, censoring patients who remained alive at the final follow-up or study conclusion.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n %), and continuous variables as medians (range). Group comparisons were conducted using t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, chi-square tests, or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Prognostic factors were determined in the training cohort using both univariate and multivariate Cox regression. Optimal cutoff values were determined utilizing the Youden’ index and X-title software. Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan-Meier (KM) and log-rank tests.

A nomogram was developed using R (v4.4.3) to predict OS in pediatric HLH patients. Risk stratification was illustrated using scatter plots of risk scores and survival time. Model discrimination and calibration were assessed via the concordance index (C-index) and calibration plots, respectively, using 2000 bootstrap resamples. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was applied to evaluate clinical benefit. A p-value of < 0.05 (two-sided) was regarded as statistically significant.

Treatment regimens and survival outcomes in HLH based on different etiological classifications

In the overall cohort, 85 patients received the HLH-1994 regimen, 35 received the HLH-2004 regimen, 174 were treated with a modified HLH-1994 regimen (in which dexamethasone was replaced with high-dose methylprednisolone), 10 received treatment targeting the underlying hematologic malignancy, 5 were treated with the DEP regimen, and 110 patients received other treatments (including anti-infective therapy, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), ruxolitinib monotherapy, or rheumatologic-associated therapies). The 3-year OS rate were 82.2 ± 4.2% for the HLH-1994 regimen, 85.2 ± 6.1% for the HLH-2004 regimen, 80.9 ± 4.1% for the modified HLH-1994 regimen, 80 ± 12.6% for malignancy-directed therapy, 75 ± 21.7% for the DEP regimen, and 83.1 ± 4.0% for other treatments, with no significant differences among regimens (p = 0.994, Figure S1a). As shown in Figure S1b, no notable variation in 3-year overall survival was observed among patients receiving regimens with or without VP-16 (83.5%±4.9% vs. 80.8%±2.6%, p = 0.217).

Regarding etiology, 37 (9.2%) were classified as pHLH, 260 (64.4%) as infection-associated HLH, including 166 (41.1%) EBV-related and 94 (23.3%) non-EBV-related, 32 (7.9%) as rheumatologic-associated HLH (rHLH), 52 (12.9%) as malignancy-associated HLH (mHLH), and 23 (5.7%) as HLH of undetermined cause. The 3-year OS rates by etiology were 69.7% for pHLH, 84.3% for EBV-HLH, 88.1% for non-EBV HLH, 96.3% for rHLH, 67.2% for mHLH, and 77.3% for undetermined HLH. Survival differences among the various etiologic groups were statistically significant (Fig. 1a). Similarly, in the training cohort, survival outcomes varied significantly by etiology (Fig. 1b), with 3-year OS rates of 75.6%±8.7% for pHLH, 83.4%±4.8% for EBV-HLH, 91.0%±3.5% for non-EBV HLH, 100% for rHLH, 67.1%±8.1% for mHLH, and 93.8%±6.1% for undetermined HLH. These findings suggested that underlying disease status might be a prognostic factor for survival. The comparison of etiological distribution between the training and validation cohorts indicated no statistically significant difference (p = 0.944, χ² =1.211), as shown in Table S2.

Independent prognostic indicators selection in the training cohort

Clinical and laboratory profiles varied significantly between survivors and non-survivors in the training cohort. The mortality group exhibited a higher prevalence of splenomegaly, CNS involvement, and GI hemorrhage, along with lower WBC counts, elevated levels of sCD25 and IL-10 (all p < 0.05) (Table 1). To facilitate risk stratification for predictive modeling, optimal cutoff values for continuous variables were determined using Youden’s J statistic, allowing conversion into binary variables for analysis using Fisher’s exact test. Significant cut-off values for WBC (2.3 × 10⁹/L), Hb (80 g/L), TG (3 mmol/L), sCD25 (2500 U/mL), IFN-γ (360 pg/mL), and IL-10 (69 pg/mL) were identified (Table 2).

Construction of the prognostic nomogram

To estimate the survival probabilities at diagnosis for pediatric HLH patients, we developed a nomogram derived from multivariate Cox regression analysis. The nomogram visualizes individualized 1-, 2-and 3-year OS rate probabilities. For example, as shown in Fig. 3, a pediatric HLH patient (patient 31 in the training cohort) had an IL-10 level of 8495pg/mL (92 points), WBC count of 1.14 × 10⁹/L (81 points), Hb level of 70 g/L (58 points), and CNS involvement (100 points), resulting in a total score of 367. Her estimated 1-, 2-and 3-year OS rate probabilities were approximately 26.3%, 22.7%, and 22.7%, respectively. In reality, the patient died 42 days after diagnosis.

Assessment of the nomogram’s discriminatory ability and prognostic accuracy in the training cohort

The nomogram’s calibration curves in the training cohort showed a good correspondence between the predicted and observed OS rates over 1, 2, and 3 years (Fig. 4a). As demonstrated by the DCA, the nomogram resulted in a superior net benefit across various threshold probabilities, particularly in the 0.2 to 0.6 range, compared to the ‘treat-all’ and ‘treat-none’ strategies (Fig. 4b). Notably, the net benefit for 2- and 3-year OS rates exceeded that of the 1-year mark, indicating improved utility for long-term prognostic assessment.

Using an optimal cutoff of 261 points identified by the X-tile software, patients were categorized into high-risk (n = 60) and low-risk (n = 222) cohorts. The risk score and survival distribution plots illustrate patient outcomes and scores, clearly highlighting the prognostic differences (Fig. 4c and d). Moreover, the 3-year OS rate was 0.940 (95% CI: 0.909–0.972) for the low-risk group and 0.472 (95% CI: 0.310–0.719) for the high-risk group. KM analysis further demonstrated a markedly lower survival rate in the high-risk cohort (p < 0.0001, Fig. 4e). Finally, ROC curves analysis showed AUCs of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.76–0.90), 0.827 (95% CI: 0.75–0.89), and 0.836 (95% CI: 0.76–0.90) for the OS rates at 1, 2 and 3 years respectively, demonstrating the nomogram model’s strong predictive performance (Fig. 4f).

DCA indicated that the nomogram provided a greater net benefit in predicting OS at 1, 2, and 3 years within a risk threshold range of 0.1 to 0.4 (Fig. 5b).

Using the same cutoff of 261 points, the validation cohort stratified pediatric patients into high-risk (n = 25) and low-risk (n = 97) groups. Survival time distribution plots based on risk scores clearly reflected the patients’ prognosis (Fig. 5c and d). The 3-year OS rate was 0.882 (95% CI: 0.818–0.95) for the low-risk group and 0.432 (95% CI: 0.273–0.682) for the high-risk group, respectively. The KM analysis confirmed significantly poorer survival in the high-risk group (p < 0.0001, Fig. 5e). ROC curve analysis revealed AUCs of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.73–0.93), 0.78 (95% CI: 0.68–0.88), and 0.79 (95% CI: 0.69–0.89) for predicting 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS, respectively, indicating strong prognostic accuracy of the nomogram model (Fig. 5f).

Validation of retrospective regimens and etiologies with nomogram model

Risk stratification using the nomogram divided 319 patients into the low-risk group and 85 into the high-risk group. Based on the model, the distribution and prognostic differences of patients with HLH varied across different etiologies (Table 3). Significant differences in 3-year OS were observed between the low- and high-risk groups in EBV-HLH (72.3% vs. 27.7%, p < 0.0001) and malignancy-associated HLH (73.1% vs. 26.9%, p = 0.017), indicating that the model had strong discriminatory power in these subgroups. In contrast, for primary HLH, non-EBV HLH, rheumatologic-associated HLH, and HLH of undetermined cause, survival differences between risk groups did not reach statistical significance, which may be attributable to limited sample sizes or relatively homogeneous outcomes within these subtypes. Overall, these findings support the applicability of the prognostic model across etiologic subgroups, particularly in EBV-associated and malignancy-associated HLH, where it demonstrated notable clinical utility.

Discussion

Pediatric HLH is a rare but life-threatening disorder with heterogeneous outcomes [1, 10]. In this study, we developed and validated a prognostic nomogram that demonstrated good discriminatory performance for predicting long-term OS and may assist in individualized risk stratification and clinical decision-making.

Regarding etiology, mHLH showed the poorest prognosis, whereas infection- and rheumatology-associated HLH demonstrated relatively favorable outcomes, consistent with adult cohorts [11, 12]. However, etiology was not significant in univariate analysis and was therefore excluded from the final model, suggesting that shared high-risk features across etiologies may be more informative. In our study, EBV-HLH, the most common subtype among Asian children, demonstrated clear risk stratification, whereas other subgroups did not, likely due to limited sample sizes. Given the high prevalence of EBV-HLH in Asian populations, future studies are warranted to develop subtype-specific prognostic models. Previous work, such as that by Wang et al., has already established prognostic models for pediatric EBV-HLH and identified key risk factors, aand our findings may serve as an extension of this foundation.

Among clinical manifestations, CNS involvement and GI bleeding were important predictors of mortality. CNS disease, as reported in previous studies [13–18], was associated with poor outcomes and may also reflect underlying genetic predisposition. Previous adult studies [19] have shown that gastrointestinal bleeding is an important fatal complication of HLH, typically occurring during uncontrolled cytokine storms. It provides prognostic information beyond conventional coagulation markers, underscoring the importance of early recognition and monitoring of relevant cytokines.

Cytokine dysregulation plays a central role in HLH pathogenesis [20]. IL-10 is particularly elevated and has been consistently associated with disease severity and poor outcomes [21, 22]. Although inter-laboratory variability and limited assay availability constrain the widespread use of IL-10, our findings, together with previous evidence, underscore its role as a key adverse prognostic factor in HLH. Broader implementation of IL-10 testing should be encouraged when feasible, while future studies should also aim to establish simplified prognostic models applicable in resource-limited settings. Severe cytopenia also strongly predicted mortality, likely reflecting systemic inflammation and bone marrow suppression.

Risk stratification based on our model also provided insights into treatment response. VP-16, a core drug in standard HLH protocols, was associated with improved OS in the high-risk cohort, though the benefit did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to limited sample size. No benefit was observed in the low-risk group, suggesting that risk-adapted treatment strategies may help balance therapeutic benefit and treatment-related toxicity.

Several prognostic models for pediatric HLH have been reported [23–25]. Luo et al. proposed a model for early mortality (AUC = 0.88), and Tang et al. reported a 28-day mortality model (AUC = 0.933) with superior net benefit over HSCORE, PELOD-2, P-MODS, and PSOFA. Unlike these models focused on short-term outcomes, our nomogram targeted 3-year OS and achieved robust performance (AUC = 0.82), with good discrimination in EBV-HLH. Despite only moderate-to-high predictive indices, its focus on long-term prognosis, inclusion of multiple etiologies, and independent validation support its clinical applicability.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be noted. First, the retrospective and multicenter design may introduce selection and reporting bias. Second, the model was developed using data from a Chinese pediatric cohort, which may limit its generalizability. Third, dynamic changes in biomarkers during treatment were not evaluated. Fourth, heterogeneity in treatment strategies precluded the inclusion of therapy-related variables. Finally, given regional differences in etiology, further external validation in international cohorts is warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our prognostic nomogram based on GI bleeding, CNS involvement, WBC < 2.3 × 10⁹/L, Hb < 80 g/L, and IL-10 > 69 pg/mL holds significant clinical value in the risk stratification and personalized management of pediatric HLH. This predictive model facilitates early risk assessment and guides the development of individualized treatment plans, thereby helping to avoid overtreatment, related side effects, and delays in therapy. Future prospective studies incorporating dynamic monitoring and external validation in diverse populations are warranted to further refine and validate this tool.
