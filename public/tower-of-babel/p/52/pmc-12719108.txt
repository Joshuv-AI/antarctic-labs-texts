Elevated osteopontin reflects systemic inflammation in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis
Authors: Sungmoo Hong, Kyungsook Jung, Taeyoung Kang, Meejung Ahn, Changjong Moon, Jeongtae Kim, Taekyun Shin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe examined the expression and localization of osteopontin (OPN) in various organs in mice with experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE). To evaluate the level of OPN in blood and various tissues, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and western blot analysis of OPN were performed. The serum level of OPN was significantly increased in mice with EAE, and OPN was upregulated in all tissues examined, including the liver, kidneys, intestines, and spinal cord. OPN immunoreactivity was noted in inflammatory cells (mainly macrophages) and was enhanced in constitutively expressed cell types in the examined organs. In sum, OPN, a pro-inflammatory and immunomodulatory mediator, was elevated in all tissues following EAE induction, resulting in increased blood concentrations. These findings suggest that OPN may function as a key extracellular matrix component contributing to systemic disorders in autoimmune disease models.Keywords: Osteopontin, Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, Experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, Immunohistochemistry

Introduction

Experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) is a widely used animal model for multiple sclerosis (MS), a chronic autoimmune demyelinating disease of the central nervous system (CNS) [1]. It is characterized by the infiltration of autoreactive T lymphocytes into CNS tissues of genetically susceptible animals [2]. Following immunization with encephalitogenic antigens, these autoimmune T cells are activated in the peripheral immune system, subsequently entering the circulation and infiltrating the CNS, where they trigger neuroinflammation and demyelination. Although MS and EAE have been associated with pathological changes in the lungs and liver, the systemic effects of EAE on circulating pro-inflammatory mediators remain poorly understood [3-9].

Osteopontin (OPN), a pro-inflammatory mediator, exhibits diverse biological activities, aiding cell adhesion, facilitating cell migration and survival, and activating immune cells, partly by binding to CD44 ligand [1, 10, 11]. It also plays a role in initiating neuroinflammation in the spinal cords of animals with EAE [12-14]. MS patients show elevated serum levels of OPN, suggesting that peripherally produced OPN in such patients circulates in the blood, eventually infiltrating various tissues in the course of autoimmune neuroinflammation [15, 16]. However, the systemic distribution and levels of OPN in various tissues in EAE remain poorly understood.

Here, we used enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), western blotting, and immunohistochemistry to examine the serum levels of OPN, its distribution among various organs in animals with EAE, and whether its expression changes after EAE induction compared to normal controls.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Female C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old) were obtained from Central Laboratory Animal Inc. and maintained at our animal facility under normal conditions (12 hours light/dark cycle, temperature, 23°C±2°C). All animal protocols conformed to international laws and the policies of the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health publication no. 85-23, 1985, revised 1996). The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jeju National University approved the study protocol (approval no.: 2022-0062). All efforts were made to minimize the number and suffering of animals used in this study.

Induction of EAE

To induce EAE, experimental animals were subcutaneously injected with 200 μl myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) 35–55 peptide at 1 mg/ml (Koma Biotech) emulsified in complete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich) and supplemented with Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Ra (5 mg/ml; Difco Laboratories). All mice were intraperitoneally injected with 500 ng pertussis toxin (List Biological Laboratories) on the day of induction and again 2 days later. After immunization, all mice were weighed and observed daily for clinical signs of EAE, which were divided into six clinical stages as follows: 0, asymptomatic; 1, floppy tail; 2, mild paraparesis; 3, severe paraparesis; 4, tetraparesis; 5, moribund condition or death due to EAE.

Tissue preparation

The mice were euthanized with 95% CO2 gas on day 17 after immunization (D17PI). The tissues, including the spinal cord, liver, pancreas, small intestine, large intestine, and kidney (n=3 mice per group), were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline for histopathological examination. The samples were stored at −80°C until analysis via western blot (n=3 mice per group).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Blood was collected (n=4 mice per group) on D17PI under isoflurane anesthesia, and serum was stored at −80°C prior to analysis. OPN levels in serum samples diluted 100-fold were measured using mouse OPN ELISA kits (ab100734; Abcam) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols, with duplicate measurements for each sample.

Immunohistochemical staining with diaminobenzidine

Immunohistochemistry was performed as described in previous studies [17, 18]. Briefly, 5-μm-thick sections of paraffin-embedded tissues were deparaffinized. To minimize nonspecific background staining in mouse tissue, the Mouse on Mouse (M.O.M.) Immunodetection Kit (Vector Laboratories) was used. Next, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with a mouse anti-OPN primary antibody (1:200; sc-21742; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Subsequently, the sections were incubated for 45 minutes with the appropriate secondary antibody provided in the M.O.M. Immunodetection Kit. An avidin–biotin complex kit (Vectastain Elite ABC Kit; Vector Laboratories) was applied for 45 minutes. The peroxidase reaction was visualized using a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine substrate kit (Vector Laboratories), and the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 30 seconds. Semiquantitative evaluation of OPN immunohistochemistry was performed independently by three researchers using light microscopy. Staining intensity was scored as follows: – indicated no detectable staining; + represented weak staining in up to 33% of cells; ++ denoted weak to moderate staining in 34%–67% of cells; and +++ indicated moderate to strong staining in more than 67% of cells.

Western blotting

Homogenized tissue samples were subjected to gel electrophoresis and then immunoblotted onto a nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk solution (v/v) in Tris-buffered saline for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by incubation overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: mouse anti-OPN monoclonal antibody (1:1,000) and mouse anti-β-actin monoclonal antibody as a control (1:10,000; A5441; Sigma-Aldrich). After washing the nitrocellulose membranes three times with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20, the membranes were reacted with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated horse anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) secondary antibody (1:1,000; Vector Laboratories) for 1 hour at room temperature. Bound proteins were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (BS ECL Plus Kit, W6002; Biosesang). Western blot signals were detected using the Fusion Solo 6X imaging system (Vilber Lourmat) and quantified using Image J software (v1.54p; National Institutes of Health).

Double immunofluorescence staining

To investigate the phenotype of OPN-positive cells, double immunofluorescence staining was performed as previously described [18]. Following blocking with normal horse serum (M.O.M. Immunodetection Kit), sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with mouse anti-OPN antibody. After washing, the sections were incubated with an appropriate secondary antibody, followed by labeling with fluorescein isothiocyanate-conjugated streptavidin (1:500; Zymed). The sections were incubated with rabbit anti-ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 antibody (Iba1; 019-1974; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), followed by goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa 594 (1:500; ab150080; Abcam). Images were captured using an Axio Imager A2 microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy) and merged using ZEN v3.7 software (ZEN lite; Carl Zeiss Microscopy).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc.), employing the unpaired Student’s t-test. Significance was evaluated at a threshold of P<0.05.

Results

At D17PI, the peak stage after MOG immunization, the serum levels of OPN were significantly higher in EAE mice (138.60±19.68 ng/ml) than in normal control mice (72.82±8.35 ng/ml) (P<0.05, Fig. 1).

OPN levels were significantly elevated in EAE mice compared to normal controls. Data are presented as means±standard error of the mean. *P<0.05 vs. normal controls.

To quantify the changes in OPN levels, western blot analysis was performed on various tissues, including in the spinal cord, liver, kidney, small intestine, large intestine, heart, and pancreas, and compared between EAE and control mice. The band densities for OPN precursor (predicted size: 66 kDa) and OPN cleavage products (predicted sizes: 44 kDa) were higher in the tissues of EAE mice (Fig. 2), indicating increased OPN levels in the organs of such mice (Fig. 2). Protein levels of the OPN precursor (predicted molecular weight: 66 kDa) and its cleavage products (predicted molecular weight: 44 kDa) were significantly elevated in most tissues examined (Fig. 2B). These findings suggest that OPN expression is upregulated in multiple organs affected by EAE.

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed constitutive OPN expression in specific cells within the spinal cord, liver, kidney, and small intestine, consistent with the observed increase in OPN levels in EAE mice (Fig. 3). In the spinal cords of control mice (Fig. 3A), weak OPN immunoreactivity was observed in neurons, and certain glial cells. In contrast, it was enhanced in the neurons, glial cells, and nerve fibers of the spinal cord in EAE mice (Fig. 3B). No OPN-positive immunoreactivity was observed in the negative control (Fig. 3C). In the livers of controls (Fig. 3D), OPN immunoreactivity was detected in endothelial cells, periportal hepatocytes, pericentral hepatocytes, scattered hepatocytes, and bile ducts; in EAE mice (Fig. 3E), enhanced immunoreactivity was noted in endothelial cells and Kupffer cells (infiltrated macrophages) (Table 1 and Fig. 3). In the kidneys, weak OPN immunoreactivity was observed in renal tubules of controls (Fig. 3F) whereas enhanced immunoreactivity was found in glomeruli, Bowman’s capsule, and renal tubules in EAE mice (Fig. 3G). In the small intestine, OPN immunoreactivity was detected in intestinal absorptive cells of both control and EAE mice (Fig. 3H, I). More OPN-positive cells were present in the lamina propria of EAE mice vs. controls, although the latter also had such cells. Notably, most inflammatory cells in the spinal cord and small intestine co-expressed the macrophage marker Iba-1 and OPN (arrowheads; Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study provides the first evidence of OPN upregulation in both serum and non-neuronal organs of animals with EAE, indicating a systemic increase in OPN. We propose that this may synergistically enhance cell mobility in various tissues, including the spinal cord and intestines, thereby contributing to systemic inflammation. Given the role of OPN in extracellular matrix interactions and cell adhesion, it likely plays a critical role in cell migration [11, 19].

The source of circulating OPN in serum remains to be elucidated. OPN is a major product of osteoclasts and is widely expressed in various tissues, including the brain, saliva, muscles, and kidneys [11]. Because OPN was originally identified in the kidneys, particularly in the renal tubules, it has been hypothesized that the elevated serum OPN levels observed in EAE may partly originate from renal tubular production following disease induction [20, 21]. Further studies are needed to investigate the interplay between autoimmune disease and renal OPN expression [20]. In patients with inflammatory bowel disease, such as ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease, plasma OPN levels are significantly elevated compared to healthy controls and are positively correlated with clinical activity indices [22]. In liver injury models, OPN has been localized to Kupffer cells and stellate cells in necrotic regions induced by carbon tetrachloride administration [23]. Similarly, in experimental autoimmune myocarditis, upregulated OPN expression has been observed in ED1-positive macrophages in the rat heart [24]. Notably, OPN was upregulated in all tissues examined, suggesting that it may contribute to homeostasis regulation and pro-inflammatory processes in non-neuronal tissues.

Regarding OPN expression in various tissues affected by EAE, OPN-positive macrophages were identified in the spinal cord, liver, kidney, small intestine, and other organs (data not shown). The elevated OPN levels in EAE-affected organs likely originate from serum OPN and OPN-positive macrophages. Although most infiltrating macrophages in EAE-affected organs are of hematogenous origin, the contribution of tissue-resident macrophages such as Kupffer cells in the liver should not be overlooked, as demonstrated by microglial and macrophage activation in CNS inflammation models [14, 23, 25]. Following its expression in renal tubules, OPN may enter the circulation and subsequently accumulate in the lungs [11, 26, 27]. In the context of EAE, increased OPN expression in renal tubules, in conjunction with OPN-positive macrophage infiltration, likely contributes to elevated renal OPN levels. The inflammatory response in the small intestine of EAE animals warrants further attention. Although EAE studies have traditionally focused on CNS inflammation, recent studies have emphasized the role of the gut–brain axis in the progression of neuroinflammation; in particular, the accumulation of Iba1-positive macrophage in the intestinal lamina propria has been demonstrated in EAE [9, 14]. In patients with MS, alterations in gut microbiota composition have been reported based on treatment status, and show strong associations with blood inflammatory biomarkers [28]. Immune activation is driven by myeloid cells derived from the intestine and mesenteric lymph nodes, and the small intestine has been identified as a key site for the activation of effector Th17 cells relevant to MS pathogenesis [9]. Consistent with these findings, we observed OPN-positive cells in the intestinal lamina propria, suggesting that intestine-derived OPN and OPN-positive immune cells may contribute to the modulation of EAE. The precise mechanisms by which these cells influence CNS inflammation require further investigation.

OPN plays a critical role in the regulation of inflammatory responses, particularly through its actions on macrophages and T cells. Functioning as a pro-inflammatory cytokine, OPN enhances the expression of Th1 cytokines and modulates immune activity [11]. It acts as a potent chemoattractant, promoting the recruitment of immune cells, such as macrophages and T cells, and further amplifies the inflammatory response by stimulating cytokine production [11]. The involvement of OPN in the pathogenesis of EAE and MS is well established. In patients with neuromyelitis optica and MS, OPN is significantly upregulated in the cerebrospinal fluid and brain white matter, where it is produced by astrocytes, neurons, oligodendroglia, and infiltrating macrophages [29]. Experimental studies have shown that OPN-knockout mice exhibit reduced spinal cord pathology, and treatment with anti-OPN antibodies alleviates EAE-related paralysis [30, 31]. Notably, vaccination of EAE mice with anti-OPN antibodies confers protection through the modulation of interleukin-17 and interferon-γ secretion [30]. These findings support a central pro-inflammatory role for OPN in the development and progression of EAE [14].

While the CNS is the primary target of EAE, autoreactive T cells and macrophages circulate systemically before reaching target organs. This systemic circulation enables inflammatory cells to migrate through blood vessels to other organs, where some infiltrate the parenchyma. This infiltration triggers a cascade of inflammatory reactions, resulting in increased OPN production. Consequently, we expect elevated OPN concentrations in both parenchyma and serum in various organs, including the lungs and spinal cord, as previously reported [13, 26].

In summary, OPN is systemically upregulated in the extra-neuronal tissues of animals with EAE and enters the bloodstream, thereby promoting cell infiltration into various organs, including the CNS. Notably, OPN may function as a key extracellular matrix component contributing to systemic disorders in autoimmune disease models.
