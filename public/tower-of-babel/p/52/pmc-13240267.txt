Bevacizumab for Radiation‐Induced (Radiculo)plexopathy
Authors: Alberto Picca, Timothée Lenglet, Christophe Vandendries, Jean‐Marc Simon, Mathilde Ducloie, Thierry Maisonobe, Damien Ricard, Dimitri Psimaras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesRadiation‐induced (radiculo)plexopathy [RI(R)P] is a debilitating delayed neurological adverse event occurring in cancer survivors years after the irradiation of the brachial or lumbar plexuses. No effective treatments have been identified to date. The objective of this work was to evaluate the effectiveness of the anti‐VEGF bevacizumab in the treatment of RI(R)P.MethodsWe retrospectively identified patients diagnosed with contrast‐enhancing RI(R)P and treated with bevacizumab in the setting of an institutionally sponsored expanded access program over the last 10 years. Clinical, radiological, and neurophysiological records were reviewed.ResultsWe identified nine patients manifesting with neurophysiological‐confirmed RI(R)P (brachial plexopathy, 4; lumbosacral radiculoplexopathy, 5) after a median of 13 years from radiotherapy for their cancer. Symptoms included focal weakness, paresthesia, cramps, hypoesthesia, amyotrophy, and pain. Patients received bevacizumab at 7.5 mg/kg/q2 weeks for a median of six cycles (range, three to nine cycles). Clinical improvement was noted in three cases and clinical stabilization in two. Patients treated within 2 years since symptom onset appeared as most likely to benefit. Enhancement reduction on follow‐up MRI was associated with the clinical benefit.DiscussionBevacizumab could be effective in the treatment of RI(R)P, particularly when patients are managed early in their disease. Further prospective clinical trials are warranted to validate these results.Keywords: bevacizumab, brachial plexopathy, lumbosacral radiculoplexopathy, radiation therapy, radiation‐induced radiculoplexopathy

Introduction

Radiation‐induced (radiculo)plexopathy [RI(R)P] is a rare, debilitating delayed neurological adverse event that affects either the brachial or the lumbar plexuses years after radiation treatment in cancer survivors [1, 2, 3]. Although radiation‐induced nerve injury in the upper limbs is usually limited to the brachial plexus (radiation‐induced brachial plexopathy), radiation therapy in the lumbar and/or pelvic zones often involves both the lumbosacral plexus and the correspondent nerve roots [4]; the term radiation‐induced lumbosacral radiculoplexopathy is therefore often preferred [1].

The onset of RI(R)P is insidious, with paresthesia, muscle weakness, amyotrophy, areflexia, and/or pain in the affected territory. The diagnosis relies on clinical features, a history of radiation therapy involving the plexus, neurophysiological studies, and dedicated MRI [1, 2, 3]. The proposed pathophysiology involves a multi‐step process, beginning with an initial microvascular injury [1, 5, 6] with the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and neo‐angiogenetic factors from reactive endothelial cells [1, 6]. These early changes promote active fibrosis. In later stages, the disease progresses toward end‐stage fibroatrophy, in the absence of active inflammation [1, 3, 5, 6].

To date, management of RI(R)P is supportive, with no effective disease‐modifying treatments available [1, 2]. Explored therapeutic options include hyperbaric oxygen (HBO) [7] and a pentoxifylline‐vitamin E‐clodronate combination (PENTOCLO) [8], but randomized trials were negative [7, 8]. New therapeutic options for RI(R)P therefore represent an unmet medical need.

Bevacizumab (BEV) is an anti‐vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) monoclonal antibody that showed efficacy in treating radiation‐induced CNS necrosis [9] by reducing abnormal vascularization and the development of brain edema [10]. VEGF has been proposed as a mediator of neo‐angiogenesis and transition from the initial pro‐inflammatory phase to the end‐stage fibrosis in RI(R)P [1, 6], justifying the potential use of anti‐VEGF, such as BEV in the treatment of RI(R)P.

In this report, we present our experience on nine patients diagnosed with RI(R)P who were treated with BEV under an institutional expanded access program.

Patients and Methods

We retrospectively identified patients diagnosed with RI(R)P and treated with BEV as part of an institutionally sponsored expanded access program at the Neurology Department of Pitié‐Salpêtrière Hospital (Paris, France) over the last 10 years (January 2014–November 2024). The diagnosis of RI(R)P was established based on suggestive clinical, radiological, and neurophysiological features, after excluding alternative causes. The treatment was initiated by the treating physician upon the presence of at least one of the two conditions: either clinical severity or recent disease evolution. The presence of plexus and/or nerve roots contrast enhancement (CE) on MRI (considered as a proxy of an active process) was a condition required to start BEV treatment. To avoid the inclusion of cases of early transient RI(R)P [1], patients whose symptoms began within 1 year of completing radiation therapy were excluded. Although no formal protocol was established, typically patients were given four BEV infusions at 7.5 mg/kg every 2 weeks and reevaluated at the time of the fourth infusion. Two additional BEV infusions were administered on the judgment of the treating physician.

Clinical records were reviewed by two neurologists (AP, DP). Improved strength was defined as an increase of at least one point on the MRC scale. Improved sensation was defined as subjective, meaningful improvement as reported by the patient. Clinical improvement was defined as improvement of at least one of the two. Stabilization was defined as halting an ongoing clinical deterioration, whereas stability indicated no evident effect on the disease course. Neurophysiological data were reviewed by two experienced neurologists (TL, DP). MRI scans were reviewed by an experienced neuroradiologist (CV). No formal statistical comparisons were performed given the reduced sample size. All patients gave their informed consent to disclose.

Results

We identified nine patients (four men, five women, Table 1) who presented with RI(R)P after a median of 13 years (range 1–34) from radiotherapy for their cancer. The median age at symptoms onset was 45 years (range 23–68 years). Five patients had involvement of the upper limbs (unilateral in all but one case), whereas four patients exhibited symptoms in the lower limbs, typically bilateral and asymmetric. All patients experienced focal weakness (9/9), accompanied by paresthesia (6/9), cramps (4/9), hypoesthesia (3/9), amyotrophy (2/9), and pain (1/9). Neurophysiological studies were conducted in all cases to confirm the diagnosis. Dedicated MRI was performed in all patients, revealing post‐contrast enhancement of affected nervous structures (Figures 1a and 2a). Two patients with lumbosacral radiculoplexopathy showed micronodular enhancement of the cauda equina nerve roots, corresponding to probable radiation‐induced cavernomas [11] (Figure 1a).

Two patients received glucocorticoids as first‐line treatment, with clinical stabilization in one case and no response in the other. All patients received BEV for the treatment of RI(R)P on a compassionate use basis. They received BEV at 7.5 mg/kg every 2 weeks for a median of six cycles (range, three to nine cycles). No treatment‐related adverse events were noted. Clinical improvement was seen in three cases, including an improvement in strength in two patients, and a subjective reduction in paresthesias in one. All three had brachial plexopathy. In two patients with previously progressing symptoms, BEV led to clinical stabilization. No benefit was seen in the remaining four cases. Among the four patients treated within 2 years of symptoms onset (< 1 year in all but one case), three showed clinical improvement, and the fourth disease stabilization. Conversely, of the five patients treated after a longer interval (7–8 years post‐symptoms onset), only one experienced stabilization, whereas the remaining four showed no response. No patient with lumbosacral radiculoplexopathy showed clinical response, but they tended to be treated later (only 1 out of 4 was treated within 2 years from symptoms onset and showed disease stabilization).

Follow‐up MRIs showed a reduction in enhancing lesions in four patients (Figures 1b and 2b), more evident on the intradural roots of the cauda equina compared with the postganglionic roots and trunks. MRI improvement was always associated with some degree of clinical benefit (two patients each showed clinical improvement and stabilization). Follow‐up EMG did not show a detectable improvement in any of the responding cases. After a median follow‐up of 2 years (range 0.5–7), two patients presented a recurrence of the symptoms: One case recurred shortly after BEV withdrawal and the other relapsed 9 months later. Clinical management of these recurrences is ongoing.

Discussion

In this report, we describe our monocentric experience with BEV for the treatment of RI(R)P, a rare delayed neurological toxicity of cancer radiotherapy for which no disease‐modifying treatments are currently available. Remarkably, three out of nine patients in our cohort showed some degree of symptoms improvement, and two had a prolonged, ongoing stabilization. The treatment was well‐tolerated, with no reported adverse events. For most responders, three to six bi‐weekly cycles at 7.5 mg/kg were sufficient to achieve clinical improvement.

Time from symptoms onset emerged as a potential predictor of clinical benefit, as all patients treated within 2 years since symptoms onset responded to BEV. This is in accordance with the proposed pathophysiological model of RI(R)P, consisting of a dynamic process with an initial, reversible prefibrotic phase followed by irreversible fibrosis [1, 6]. In the early stage, radiation injury leads to endothelial cell activation and inflammation with the release of pleiotropic cytokines, including VEGF [6]. Indeed, VEGF inhibition in the active hypoxic/inflammatory phase could interrupt the proposed vicious circle that ultimately leads to the end‐stage fibrosis/atrophy [1]. This could also explain the negative results seen in PENTOCLO and HBO trials, where patients were treated significantly later since symptoms onset (5.5 and 11 years in median, respectively) [7, 8].

The presence of nervous structures enhancement on MRI, used as a marker of an ongoing active process, was considered a criterion to initiate BEV therapy. However, it appeared to be a less reliable predictor of treatment response than the time from symptom onset. Conversely, a radiological response characterized by reduced enhancement on follow‐up MRI was consistently associated with clinical benefit. The absence of EMG improvement even in clinically improving patients is understandable in the context of an examination whose sensitivity is probably not sufficient to demonstrate marginal modifications of proximal nerve conduction nor the axonal impact of RI(R)P, which remains limited in its prefibrotic phase.

We acknowledge the limitations of this study, primarily the inherent risk of bias due to its retrospective nature and the lack of a comparative control arm. The study cohort was heterogeneous, with different irradiated zones and treatment schedules. As most patients received radiotherapy decades ago, we were unable to retrieve dosimetry charts.

Whether the association of glucocorticoids, which have a broader anti‐inflammatory effect, to BEV could further improve treatment results has not been tested and warrants exploration in the future. Furthermore, it remains to be established the risk of symptoms recurrence after BEV withdrawal and the efficacy of treatment rechallenge. In our cohort, after a median 2‐year follow‐up, two cases recurred, both within 1 year since last BEV administration, and no data are available yet on the subsequent management. Finally, most cases treated after several years since symptoms onset did not respond to treatment; better therapeutical options are required for this population.

In conclusion, we report here the potential clinical efficacy of bevacizumab in a subset of patients with radiation‐induced (radiculo)plexopathy. The patients treated within 2 years since symptoms onset, in particular, appeared to be the most likely to benefit from this treatment. These results should be validated in further prospective trials.
