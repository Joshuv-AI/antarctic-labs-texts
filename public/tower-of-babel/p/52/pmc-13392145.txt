High-quality NiOx nanoparticles synthesized via low temperature chemical precipitation method for high-performance inverted perovskite photovoltaics
Authors: Ziyuan Liu, Dongmei He, Zuolin Zhang, Kun Zhang, Ziyi Tang, Shaokuan Gong, Xilai He, Xinxing Liu, Xuxia Shai, Yue Yu, Jiajia Zhang, Xihan Chen, Xuanhua Li, Yang Wang, Cong Chen, Jianhong Yi, Jiangzhao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDual hole transport layers consisting of NiOx and self-assembled molecules are widely adopted in inverted perovskite solar cells, yet plagued by high impurity content, inefficient hole transport, low molecular coverage, weak interfacial binding, unstable buried interface and energy level mismatch. Herein, a low-temperature chemical precipitation strategy is developed to synthesize high-quality NiOx nanoparticles as hole transport layers. Compared with the room-temperature route, the low-temperature prepared NiOx films deliver an elevated Ni3+/Ni2+ ratio, reduced impurities, higher electrical conductivity and hole mobility. Moreover, this strategy improves molecular coverage, alleviates energy level mismatch, accelerates hole extraction and strengthens buried interface stability. The optimized cells achieve a certified power conversion efficiency of 27.1%, and the 14 cm2 minimodule reaches an efficiency of 23.18%. The devices retain 91.5% efficiency after 2100 h of continuous operation, and 91.4% efficiency after 2000 h of damp-heat aging.Subject terms: Solar cells, Energy

Introduction

Inverted (p-i-n) perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have gained worldwide attention, as it achieved a remarkable power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) exceeding 27%.1–5 In inverted PSCs, hole transport layer (HTL) plays a critical role in determining the final device performance, capturing intensive attention in recent 2 years6–14. Presently, high-efficiency inverted PSCs usually adopt nickel oxide (NiOx)15–17 or self-assembled molecules (SAMs)7,10,12–14,18,19 as efficient HTL, which accomplishes salient advancements in stability and PCE. However, the interface between NiOx HTL and perovskite layer suffers from severe redox reaction, thereby deteriorating device long-term longevity20–23. Besides, the relatively weak bonding between SAMs and indium tin oxide (ITO) or fluoride-doped tin oxide (FTO) causes SAMs to easily detach from the transparent electrodes under high-temperature conditions, thereby reducing long-term operational lifespan of PSCs8,24. It was revealed that SAMs bond far more strongly to NiOx nanoparticles (NPs) than ITO or FTO8,24. To address the above tough issue, SAMs and NiOx NPs were leveraged to construct a double-layer HTL, overcoming the instability challenges of either NiOx or SAMs as a single HTL, which prominently elevated the long-term durability of the HTL/perovskite interface (called buried interface) and devices1,8,11,23,24. Nevertheless, there are some ongoing key challenges for double layer HTL formed by NiOx and SAMs. First, the NiOx nanoparticles have the disadvantages of poor dispersion, high impurity content, low electrical conductivity and low hole mobility, which will diminish the hole transport and extraction ability of NiOx film16,24. Second, the low coverage, prone to aggregation and insufficient adhesion of SAMs on the NiOx film would not only lower the charge transport capability of the SAMs layer, but also lead to poor perovskite crystallization and low quality of buried interface8,11. Finally, the energy level mismatch between double HTL and perovskite layer would result in serious carrier non-radiative recombination8,11,24. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop simple yet effective strategy to simultaneously tackle the above intricate problems, aiming to synchronously enhance the PCE and stability of inverted PSCs.

Over the past few years, researchers have proposed a variety of strategies to figure out the problems faced by double-layer HTL consisting of NiOx and SAMs, such as the regulation of SAMs/perovskite interface25,26, doping and modification of SAMs layer11,13,27,28, and development of new SAMs7,9,10,12,14,18,19,29,30. By introducing modification molecules at SAMs/perovskite interface, they can fill the un-covered areas of SAMs, passivate the defects at the bottom surface of perovskite films, promote perovskite crystallization and reduce interface energy barrier, which can facilitate interfacial charge transfer, inhibit interfacial non-radiative recombination and raise the stability of buried interface25,26. However, this method is difficult to effectively modulate the underlying NiOx and SAMs layers. In addition, developing new types of SAMs and optimizing commonly used SAMs could inhibit the aggregation of SAMs, increase SAMs coverage rate, strengthen the ability to passivate interface defects, improve perovskite crystallization and reduce interface energy level mismatch, thereby boosting the photovoltaic performance and stability of PSCs7,9,11–14,19,27,28. However, this strategy is unable to address the problems existing in the NiOx layer. Overall, it is unfulfillable to simultaneously address the above all issues through SAMs doping and SAMs/perovskite interface modulation. It should be noted that, considering the layered stacking structure of PSCs, the underlying functional layers will significantly affect the growth and quality of the subsequent functional layers7,8,11,23. Thus, it is highly preferred to simultaneously settle all of the above problems through a bottom-up modulation strategy. In recent years, most research efforts have focused on SAMs and the regulation of their interfaces with perovskites11,13,25–27, but there is very limited research on the regulation of NiOx layer. For instance, doping or post-treatment of NiOx NPs can heighten the efficiency and stability of PSCs16,24, while this method may introduce new impurity phases, which is not conducive to device long-term stability. Therefore, it is urgent to fulfill the simultaneous modulation of the bottom-up multi-functional layers and interfaces through simple synthesis process regulation of NiOx NPs, synchronously addressing all the issues existing at the HTL and buried interface, ultimately realizing long-term operationally stable efficient inverted PSCs.

In this study, we report a low temperature chemical precipitation (LTCP) method to successfully synthesize high-quality NiOx NPs used as HTL in inverted PSCs. Compared with room temperature chemical precipitation (RTCP) approach, the NiOx NPs prepared by LTCP exhibit lower impurity content, superior dispersibility, higher electrical conductivity and hole mobility, because of increased Ni3+ content and suppressed secondary chemical reaction under low temperature condition. Besides, the NiOx NPs based on LTCP method increase the coverage of SAMs, promote perovskite crystallization, and ameliorate the interface contact between HTL and the perovskite layer, thus reinforcing the long-term stability of buried interface and devices. Finally, the double-layer HTL constructed by LTCP-processed NiOx NPs and SAMs possess a reduced interface energy barrier with perovskite layer than RTCP-processed counterpart, which facilitates interfacial hole transfer and extraction, and thereby inhibits interfacial carrier non-radiative recombination. The above multiple benefits are translated into substantially enhanced photovoltaic performance and long-term stability. The 1.55 eV, 1.68 eV, and 1.78 eV inverted PSCs fabricated using LTCP NiOx NPs deliver a PCE of 27.37% (certified PCE 27.09%), 24.15% and 20.36%, respectively. The large-area module with LTCP NiOx NPs achieves a PCE of 23.18% (14 cm2). The 1.55 eV target PSCs maintain 91.5% and 91.4% of the initial efficiencies under maximum power point tracking (MPPT) for 2100 h, and at 85 °C and 85% relative humidity (RH) for 2000 h, respectively.

Results

Chemical precipitation is one very effective approach to synthesize high-quality NiOx NPs16,31,32. Detailed process of NiOx NPs was described in experimental section, which involves two critical steps. Firstly, the reaction between Ni(NO3)2 and NaOH generates Ni(OH)2, and in the second step, the Ni(OH)2 is calcined to obtain NiOx NPs. In the first step reaction, NO3− impurity ions are prone to form and adsorbed on Ni(OH)2 and final NiOx NPs, which has a detrimental effect on device PCE and stability. Moreover, the first step reaction would also affect the Ni3+/Ni2+ ratio, particle size, surface hydroxyl number of NiOx NPs, thereby affecting electrical properties of NiOx films and adsorption and packing of SAMs. Therefore, modulating the first step reaction is of crucial importance for acquiring high-quality NiOx NPs and films. Since the reaction between Ni(NO3)2 and NaOH is exothermic, high temperature or room temperature reaction should be not beneficial for the formation of Ni(OH)2 and suppressing the adsorption of impurity ions at the surface of Ni(OH)2 precipitation. Consequently, in this work, LTCP was innovatively proposed to synthesize high-quality NiOx NPs, which was compared with commonly used RTCP. For simplicity, henceforward, the NiOx NPs and films prepared by RTCP (~25 °C) and LTCP (~0 °C) were denoted as RT-NiOx and LT-NiOx, respectively. The perovskite films were denoted as PVK.

Subsequently, various characterizations were employed to investigate the influence of reaction temperature on quality and stability of NiOx NPs and films. First, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that the RT-NiOx NPs aggregated obviously with a typical size of 6.96 nm (Fig. 1a), while the LT-NiOx NPs exhibited much suppressed agglomeration with a particle size of 5.31 nm (Fig. 1b). This indicates that LT-NiOx NPs had a reduced average particle size and narrower particle size distribution (Fig. 1c), which is conducive to homogeneous dispersion and fabrication of uniform and compact NiOx films. The results of zeta potential and dynamic light scattering (DLS) revealed that LT-NiOx dispersion solution exhibited a higher zeta potential (from 32.3 mV to 46.5 mV) and a decreased average colloidal size (from 32.01 nm to 26.77 nm) as well as narrower colloidal size distribution compared to RT-NiOx dispersion solution (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). It suggests that LT-NiOx NPs is superior to RT-NiOx NPs in dispersity. The poor stability of NiOx NPs dispersion solution can endow the corresponding PSCs poor reproducibility, which is deleterious for large-scale commercial deployment. From Fig. 1d, after ambient aging for 960 h, much higher zeta potential can be observed in LT-NiOx dispersion solution in comparison with RT-NiOx dispersion solution. In addition, after 960 h of ambient aging, the average colloidal size of RT-NiOx was enlarged much more significantly than LT-NiOx (Fig. 1e). These results imply that LT-NiOx dispersion solution showed much better durability compared with RT-NiOx dispersion solution. The LT-NiOx film is more compact and smoother compared to RT-NiOx films (Supplementary Fig. 2), which is ascribed to smaller particle size and more homogeneous particle size distribution of LT-NiOx. It is worth noting that after 960 h of aging, the RT-NiOx-based film underwent significant agglomeration (Fig. 1f), whereas the morphology of LT-NiOx-based film was almost invariant under the same aging conditions (Fig. 1g). This once again proved excellent stability of LT-NiOx NPs, which is advantageous for device long-term lifespan.

Subsequently, we used X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to systematically investigate the chemical states of cations in NiOx films. From Fig. 1h, i, both RT-NiOx film and LT-NiOx film contained nickel ions in three different valence states (i.e., Ni2+, Ni3+ and Ni4+). Usually, the generation of nickel vacancies disrupts the electrical neutrality of nonstoichiometric oxygen-rich NiOx, resulting in the oxidation of Ni2⁺ to Ni3+ to maintain electrical neutrality33. It has been widely demonstrated that the formation of Ni3+ is favorable for increasing conductivity of NiOx film34,35. As revealed by quantitative analysis of the Ni 2p spectra, the Ni3+/Ni2+ ratio was calculated to be 1.97 for RT-NiOx film, whereas it was increased to 2.41 for LT-NiOx film. To further understand the origin of higher content of Ni3+ of LT-NiOx film, density functional theory (DFT) was performed to calculate the formation energy of Ni vacancies in the bulk and at the surface of NiOx. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, the formation energy of Ni vacancies on the surface of NiOx (1.14 eV) was considerably lower than that in the bulk phase (1.50 eV). Due to the smaller particle size of NiOx prepared by LTCP method, the LT-NiOx film had a larger specific surface area and can form more Ni vacancies and thus Ni3+, thereby contributing to improved electrical performance. The electrical conductivity improvement of the LT-NiOx film was further validated through the O 1 s spectra (Supplementary Fig. 4). Compared with RT-NiOx film, the proportion of hydroxyl (-OH) groups in LT-NiOx film became smaller, while the proportion of Ni3+-O became larger, which may be caused by transformation of more Ni(OH)2 to NiOOH during the process of NiOx NPs formation using our LTCP method. As everyone knows, the presence of NiOOH can both improve the conductivity of NiOx films and provide more surface -OH groups for the bonding with SAMs24.

To further gain insights into the effect of low temperature synthesis on impurity phase of NiOx NPs and films, we conducted purity analysis on NiOx films by XPS. As exhibited in Fig. 1j, k, though both NiOx films showed the peak of NO3− around 406.5 eV, the intensity of LT-NiOx sample was much lower than that of RT-NiOx sample, indicating that the low-temperature synthesis process can effectively suppress the generation of NO3− impurities. Upon the addition of NaOH solution for RTCP method, the reactants solution experienced a rapid increase in temperature, accompanied by the formation of large flake-like precipitates (Supplementary Fig. 5a). In contrast, the temperature rise was very slow by the LTCP process, and the resulting precipitate exhibited a more dispersed particulate morphology (Supplementary Fig. 5b). This result indicates that the reaction between Ni(NO3)2 and NaOH is exothermic, and thus lowering reaction temperature can effectively restrain the adsorption of NO3− on Ni(OH)2 precipitates. In addition, we tracked the pH changes with the addition time of NaOH solution for RTCP and LTCP process. It was found that longer time was needed to reach target pH of 10 for RTCP than LTCP (Supplementary Fig. 6), which should be accelerated chemical precipitation exothermic reaction by low temperature. From X-ray diffraction (XRD) in Supplementary Fig. 7a, b, we observed higher content of NaNO3 in LT-Ni(OH)2 precipitates than RT-Ni(OH)2 precipitates. It should be stressed that NO3− existed in Ni(OH)2 precipitates in the form of either NaNO3 or chemical adsorption at the surface of Ni(OH)2 precipitates. Thus, it can be inferred that considering the same molar content of NO3− in both reaction solutions, less NO3− was chemically adsorbed at the surface of LT-Ni(OH)2 precipitates compared with RT-Ni(OH)2 precipitates. After reaction stopped, we performed centrifugation of RT-Ni(OH)2 and LT-Ni(OH)2 solutions, and then collected the remaining solution after centrifugation. It was revealed that Ni2+ ions concentration was much lower in remaining LTCP solution than remaining RTCP solution (Supplementary Fig. 7c). This suggests that Ni2+ ions in Ni(NO3)2 reacted more completely with OH− in NaOH using LTCP compared with RTCP. In addition, remaining LTCP solution possessed a much higher concentration of NO3− than remaining RTCP solution (Supplementary Fig. 7d), again confirming less chemical adsorption of NO3− at the surface of LT-Ni(OH)2 precipitates, in agreement with the above XRD results. The Raman and Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra in Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9 further corroborated much reduced chemical adsorption of NO3− and CO32− impurities at the surface of LT-Ni(OH)2 and LT-NiOx than RT-Ni(OH)2 and RT-NiOx. As revealed in Supplementary Fig. 10, the thermogravimetric-differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC) demonstrated that the decomposition temperature of LT-Ni(OH)2 was slightly higher than that of RT-Ni(OH)2, indicating that LT-Ni(OH)2 is more stable compared with RT-Ni(OH)2 possibly due to diminished impurities.

The XRD results in Supplementary Fig. 11 indicate that for RT-NiOx and LT-NiOx, same diffraction peak position and similar crystallinity of NiOx were found regardless of power or films. The average grain size of RT-NiOx calculated by XRD is well consistent with TEM results. In Supplementary Fig. 12, compared with RT-NiOx film, the LT-NiOx film displayed an increased optical transmittance, which may be primarily put down to reduced impurity and defects. The hole mobility was improved from 1.32 × 10−4 cm2·V−1·s−1 of RT-NiOx film to 2.34 × 10−4 cm2·V−1·s−1 of LT-NiOx film (Fig. 1l). Meanwhile, increased conductivity was fulfilled in LT-NiOx film as compared with RT-NiOx film (Supplementary Fig. 13). The enhanced hole mobility and conductivity should be due to film improved homogeneity and compactness, reduced impurity and defect density, and increased Ni3+/Ni2+ ratio.

To sum up, RTCP and LTCP in Fig. 2a, b were adopted to synthesize NiOx NPs. The only difference between two synthetic methods is reaction temperature of generating Ni(OH)2. First, LT-NiOx had reduced particle size, improved dispersity and stability than RT-NiOx. Second, LT-NiOx exhibited a higher Ni3+/Ni2+ ratio compared with RT-NiOx, resulting in ameliorated conductivity and hole mobility. Finally, the low temperature can promote the formation of Ni(OH)2 and suppress the chemical adsorption of NO3− and CO32− impurities at the surface of Ni(OH)2 and thus NiOx NPs, thereby contributing to improved electrical properties and stability of NiOx NPs and films.

Since NiOx substrate is underneath SAM and perovskite film, it should have a substantial impact on growth and quality of subsequent functional layers. In view of this, we systematically studied the effects of different NiOx substrates on buried interface quality. The contact angle of the SAM solution on LT-NiOx film is slightly smaller than that on RT-NiOx (Supplementary Fig. 14), beneficial for deposition of subsequent SAM layer. The roughness was reduced upon depositing SAMs on LT-NiOx than RT-NiOx films (Supplementary Fig. 15). From conductive atomic force microscopy (C-AFM) in Fig. 3a, b, it was obviously seen that the SAM film on LT-NiOx substrate was more uniform and exhibited higher coverage than that on RT-NiOx substrate, which was further affirmed by significantly reduced current in the former film relative to the latter (Fig. 3c). By fitting the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves (Fig. 3d, e and Supplementary Fig. 16)36, it can be concluded that the coverage rate of SAMs on LT-NiOx film reached 2.75 × 1013 cm−2, which is significantly higher than that of SAMs on RT-NiOx film (2.26 × 1012 cm−2). Subsequently, we exploited DFT to calculate the fraction of coverage and adsorption energies (Ead) of SAM molecules on ideal NiO, RT-NiOx and LT-NiOx. The coverage rate was gradually increased in the sequence of ideal NiO (36%), RT-NiOx (41%) and LT-NiOx (43%) (Supplementary Fig. 17). It shows that SAMs realized a higher coverage rate on LT-NiOx than RT-NiOx, which is attributed to increased Ni3+/Ni2+ ratio, reduced particle size, lowered impurities, improved uniformity and compactness of LT-NiOx film. Besides, the larger integrated area of P 2p peaks again corroborated the enhanced coverage rate of SAMs on LT-NiOx film (Supplementary Fig. 18). The Ead of SAMs on ideal NiO, RT-NiOx and LT-NiOx was −2.27 eV, −2.52 eV, and −2.64 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 19). Evidently, SAMs had a larger adsorption energy with LT-NiOx than RT-NiOx, which is attributed to increased number of -OH groups and thus SAM binding sites induced by the higher concentration of Ni3+ in LT-NiOx. Following aging at 85 °C after 240 h, the SAMs on RT-NiOx became more inhomogeneous, while the SAMs on LT-NiOx were still very uniform (Supplementary Fig. 20). It certified that the SAMs bonded more strongly with LT-NiOx in comparison with RT-NiOx, in line with the theoretical calculation results. The stronger bonding force should be partially responsible for improved coverage rate of SAMs on LT-NiOx. In short, the SAMs on LT-NiOx possessed higher coverage rate and larger bonding force as compared to RT-NiOx.

The reduced contact angle of perovskite precursor solutions on LT-NiOx/SAM than RT-NiOx/SAM should be favorable for perovskite crystallization (Supplementary Fig. 21). To explore the effect of different NiOx NPs on buried interface quality, we peeled off perovskite films from ITO/NiOx/SAM substrate with ultraviolet (UV)-activated glue for bottom surface characterization. Obviously, numerous pinholes and nanovoids were seen at the bottom surface of perovskite film on RT-NiOx/SAM (Fig. 3f), whereas the LT-NiOx/SAM/perovskite film exhibited a pyknotic and smooth buried surface (Fig. 3g), owing to improved morphology and wettability of SAM and promoted perovskite crystallization resulting from high-quality LT-NiOx films, thereby contributing to ameliorated electric property and robustness of buried interface. The average grain size of bottom surface of perovskite films prepared on LT-NiOx/SAM was 1.45 μm, larger than that on RT-NiOx/SAM (1.32 μm) (Supplementary Fig. 22). Moreover, the average grain size difference between bottom surface and top surface of the perovskite films on LT-NiOx/SAM was much smaller than that on RT-NiOx/SAM. The above result further demonstrated improved buried interface quality and vertically homogeneous crystallization of perovskite films on LT-NiOx/SAM. This improved morphology was also evidenced by the cross-sectional SEM images (Fig. 3h, i). According to AFM in Fig. 3j, k, the grain boundary groove (GBG) side angle at the bottom surface of the perovskite film grown on LT-NiOx/SAM substrates was measured to be 130°, significantly larger than that formed on RT-NiOx/SAM substrates, again confirming improved structural continuity and smoothness due to intimately packed neighboring perovskite grains13.

To evaluate the lattice strain of perovskite films, grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) technique was employed. The improvement of HTL quality effectively mitigated tensile stress within perovskite film, leading to enhanced crystalline lattice ordering (Supplementary Fig. 23). Apparently, the internal stress in the perovskite film deposited on LT-NiOx/SAM substrate was dramatically smaller than that deposited on RT-NiOx/SAM (Fig. 3l), principally due to the enhanced crystallinity and decreased defect density stemming from the improved buried interface quality. In response to improved buried interface morphology, the electric properties of perovskite films on LT-NiOx/SAM should also be improved. As displayed in Fig. 3m and Supplementary Fig. 24, the perovskite film on LT-NiOx/SAM (0.83 × 1015 cm−3) verified a much lower trap density compared with that RT-NiOx/SAM (1.99 × 1015 cm−3). It can be clearly seen from Fig. 3n, o that NiOx/SAM/perovskite/PC61BM sample showed a higher photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) and quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) than NiOx/SAM/perovskite sample regardless of RT-NiOx or LT-NiOx, which could be effective defect passivation of perovskite films via PC61BM37,38. Additionally, the perovskite films deposited on LT-NiOx/SAM exhibited higher PLQY and QFLS values compared to that on RT-NiOx/SAM no matter whether PC61BM was exploited, which should be mostly ascribed to reduced defect density and improved buried interface contact, ultimately resulting in enhanced open-circuit voltage (VOC) and fill factor (FF).

We studied the effect of RT-NiOx and LT-NiOx on interfacial carrier dynamics. Kelvin probe force microscope (KPFM) demonstrated that compared with RT-NiOx/SAM film, LT-NiOx/SAM film exhibited a lower work function (Supplementary Fig. 25), which is indicative of its more P-type. The ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) revealed that the Fermi level (EF) values of RT-NiOx/SAM and LT-NiOx/SAM films were −4.61 eV and −4.99 eV, respectively, and the valence band maximums (EVBM) were estimated to be −4.96 eV and −5.41 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 26). Next, we also calculated the bandgaps (Eg) of different HTLs and perovskite film by UV–vis absorption spectra (Supplementary Fig. 27). Evidently, a slightly increased Eg was achieved for LT-NiOx/SAM films (3.71 eV) than RT-NiOx/SAM films (3.64 eV), which may be closely correlated with increased Ni3+/Ni2+ ratio and reduced impurity. However, the Eg of perovskite films deposited on RT-NiOx/SAM and LT-NiOx/SAM substrates was unchanged and was not dependent on HTL substrates. Therefore, the energy level alignment at HTL/perovskite interface was schematically plotted (Supplementary Fig. 28). Compared with RT-NiOx/SAM films, the LT-NiOx/SAM films showed considerably lowered EVBM, much closer to that of perovskite films (−5.48 eV), minimizing interfacial energy barrier and accordingly accelerating hole extraction. Ultrafast reflection spectroscopy (TRS) showed that the hole extraction rate was increased from 3870 ± 240 cm·s−1 of RT-NiOx/SAM films to 8090 ± 290 cm·s−1 of LT-NiOx/SAM films, with an increment by more than twice (Fig. 4a, b), largely owing to improved energy band arrangement, reduced interface defects, and reinforced interface contact. Through steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL), the dropped PL intensity and shortened carrier lifetime further validated the swifter hole extraction rate of LT-NiOx/SAM HTL (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 29). Furthermore, from transient photocurrent (TPC), we can observe a markedly reduced carrier decay lifetime in the PSCs with LT-NiOx (0.18 μs) as compared to the PSCs with RT-NiOx (0.36 μs), again confirming facilitated hole extraction (Fig. 4d).

Transient photovoltage (TPV) demonstrated a longer carrier decay lifetime in the PSC using LT-NiOx (1.22 μs) than one using RT-NiOx (0.96 μs), suggesting inhibited interfacial non-radiative recombination (Supplementary Fig. 30). The Mott-Schottky analysis revealed that the LT-NiOₓ devices exhibited a higher built-in potential (1.13 V), which can enhance the driving force for charge carrier separation and effectively suppress interfacial recombination (Supplementary Fig. 31). The restricted non-radiative recombination was also evidenced by a reduced dark current density (Fig. 4e) and a markedly increased recombination resistance in the impedance spectra (Supplementary Fig. 32). In addition, the LT-NiOₓ-based devices exhibited an ideality factor of 1.31, smaller than that of RT-NiOₓ-based devices (1.43), which once again corroborated diminished non-radiative recombination (Fig. 4f). In brief, reduced interfacial energy level mismatch, reduced interface defects, improved electric properties of NiOₓ films and augmented buried interface quality collectively contributed to mitigated non-radiative recombination.

The long-term work stability of the device heavily depends on the stability of the buried interface. The perovskite films based on RT-NiOx and LT-NiOx were fabricated and aged under one sun illumination and 85 °C for 480 h. The SEM and PL mapping were leveraged to characterize the bottom surface of the perovskite films peeled off from the ITO/NiOx/SAM substrates, aiming to assess the buried interface stability. From SEM in Fig. 4g, h, it can be observed that after aging, a large number of pores and cracks appeared at the bottom surface of the control perovskite film, whereas the target film still exhibited a continuous and compact surface morphology, similar with fresh perovskite film. Moreover, the cross-sectional SEM images showed that the target film could maintain its initial state after aging (Fig. 4i), while the control perovskite film collapsed and disintegrated under the same aging conditions (Fig. 4j). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 33a, b, the perovskite films based on LT-HTL demonstrated better stability when compared with those based on RT-HTL. From Supplementary Fig. 33c, on one hand, after aging for 480 h, the RT-HTL - based perovskite film displayed a significantly higher diffraction peak intensity of PbI2 compared with the LT-HTL - based perovskite film, which indicates more rapid decomposition of the former. On the other hand, regardless of the aging time, the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the PbI2 peak in the LT-HTL-based perovskite film is larger than that in the RT-HTL-based perovskite film, suggesting the formation of much less PbI2. These results collectively confirm the improved stability of the LT-HTL-based perovskite film compared to the RT-HTL-based one. The UV–vis absorption spectra revealed distinct optical stability behaviors between the LT-NiOx and RT-NiOx based perovskite films. The fresh LT-NiOx-based perovskite film showed a slightly higher absorption intensity. Importantly, it was readily observed that the light absorption intensity of the RT-NiOx based perovskite film decreased significantly after aging for 480 h, while that of the LT-NiOx based perovskite film only declined slightly (Supplementary Fig. 34). These results confirm that the perovskite film on LT-NiOx is more stable than that on RT-NiOx, owing to the improved morphology and crystallinity. PL mapping results are in keeping with these morphological observations. The control perovskite film displayed prominently reduced and non-uniform PL intensity after aging, indicative of a high density of non-radiative recombination centers caused by film decomposition (Fig. 4k). Conversely, the perovskite film on LT-NiOₓ/SAM could retain strong and spatially homogeneous PL emission (Fig. 4l). The ameliorated buried interface robustness should primarily result from strong chemical anchoring of SAMs on LT-NiOₓ, increased coverage rate of SAMs, improved morphology of bottom surface of perovskite films and released interface residual stress.

We fabricated inverted PSCs employing RT-NiOx and LT-NiOx, where 1.55 eV, 1.68 eV or 1.78 eV perovskite compositions were used as light-harvesting layer. For the devices with a 1.55 eV bandgap, the devices with LT-NiOx achieved a champion PCE of 27.37% with negligible hysteresis, corresponding to a VOC of 1.184 V, a short-circuit current density (JSC) of 26.55 mA·cm−2, and an FF of 86.96%, whereas the best-performing device with RT-NiOx yielded a PCE of 26.31% with a VOC of 1.175 V, a JSC of 26.33 mA·cm−2 and an FF of 85.01% (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 1). We attributed the enhancement in photovoltaic performance to improved energy level alignment, reduced interface defects, promoted hole extraction and transport, released residual stress, and thus suppressed non-radiative recombination provoked by high-quality LT-NiOx. The integrated current densities of the PSCs with RT-NiOx and LT-NiOx from external quantum efficiency (EQE) were 25.59 and 25.92 mA·cm−2, matching well with that derived from J–V curves (Supplementary Fig. 35). We also corroborated the excellent reproducibility of the PSCs with RT-NiOx and LT-NiOx (Supplementary Figs. 36 and 37). We sent one of our representative high-efficiency devices with LT-NiOx to an accredited third-party institution of Tianjin Institute of Metrological Supervision and Testing for independent certification. The certified steady-state PCE was 27.09% (Supplementary Fig. 38), which is one of the highest PCEs ever reported for single-junction PSCs (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 2).

Subsequently, we verified the universality of LT-NiOₓ by utilizing 1.68 eV and 1.78 eV bandgap of perovskites. Specifically, the champion 1.68 eV inverted PSCs with LT-NiOₓ obtained a PCE of 24.15%, which is among the highest PCE values reported for 1.68 eV inverted PSCs up to now (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Table 3). Besides, the 1.78 eV inverted PSCs with LT-NiOₓ accomplished a maximum PCE of 20.36%, which is one of the highest PCE values reported for 1.78 eV inverted PSCs (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 4).

Then, we fabricated modules using blade coating to demonstrate the scalability of our developed LT-NiOₓ. Minimodules consisting of six sub-cells with a total active area of 14 cm2 were constructed. The minimodules based on LT- NiOₓ achieved a PCE of 23.18% with a VOC of 6.57 V, a JSC of 4.55 mA·cm−2, and an FF of 77.61% (Fig. 5e), which is among state-of-the-art PSC modules with active area of 10-20 cm2 (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Table 5).

Work stability is another important indicator of device performance. For this purpose, we measured the MPP output characteristics of unencapsulated PSCs. The measurement was implemented under continuous one sun light illumination (100 mW/cm2). The LT-NiOx-based devices with 1.55 eV and 1.78 eV bandgap could maintain 91.90% and 92.75% of their initial PCEs (26.33% and 19.32%) after 2100 h and 500 h, respectively, which is among the most sophisticated p-i-n PSCs (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 39). In contrast, the 1.55 eV and 1.78 eV inverted PSCs based on RT-NiOx degraded to 79.44% and 80.00% of their initial PCEs after 1000 h and 250 h, respectively. The light-thermal operational stability of devices was further investigated on the basis of the ISOS-L-2 protocol (MPP and 70 °C). Following 1055 h, the LT-NiOx device only decreased by 9.60%, while the RT-NiOx device only maintained 59.72% of the initial value (Supplementary Fig. 40). To further investigate the stability of the devices under damp and heat conditions, the encapsulated RT-NiOx and LT-NiOx-based devices were subjected to long-term stress under 85 °C and 85% humidity. The results showed that after 2000 h, the LT-NiOx based device still maintained 91.40% of its original efficiency, while the RT-NiOx based device only retained 64.70% of its initial efficiency (Fig. 5h). Obviously, the light and damp-heat stabilities were substantially enhanced, which is due to improved durability of NiOx layer, SAM layer and buried interface induced by high-quality NiOx NPs synthesized via LTCP method. Under a relative humidity of 20–30%, the LT-NiOx-based devices retained 96.55% of its initial efficiency after 2300 h, whereas the RT-NiOx-based devices degraded to 83.13% of its initial value (Supplementary Fig. 41). In a word, our developed LT-NiOx can elevate not only PCE but also long-term operational stability by strengthened HTL and buried interface via bottom-up manipulation.

Discussion

We developed a LTCP method to successfully fabricate high-quality NiOx NPs for HTL in inverted PSCs. It was revealed that the NiOx NPs prepared by LTCP was superior to that prepared by RTCP in size homogeneity, dispersibility and stability. The LT-NiOx films showed increased Ni3+/Ni2+ ratio, lower impurity content, higher electrical conductivity and hole mobility than the RT-NiOx films. In addition, using LT-NiOx NPs can enhance the coverage rate of SAMs, promote perovskite crystallization, reduce interfacial energy level mismatch, accelerate hole extraction and transport, as well as reinforce the stability of buried interface. The 1.55 eV inverted PSCs based on RT-NiOx realized a PCE of 27.37% (certified PCE of 27.09%), which is one of the highest efficiency values ever reported for single-junction PSCs. The adoption of LT-NiOx resulted in efficient large-area module with a PCE of 23.18% (14 cm2). The 1.55 eV target PSCs maintained 91.5% and 91.4% of the initial PCEs after aging at MPPT for 2100 h, and under 85 °C/85% RH for 2000 h, respectively. This work synchronously addressed the critical challenges of NiOx film, SAM film, perovskite film and buried interface through bottom-up modulation strategy, which paves the way for the commercial application of perovskite photovoltaics and perovskite-based other optoelectronic devices.

Methods

Materials

All chemicals and solvents were used as received without further purification. The nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, 99.99%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 99.99%) and potassium chloride (KCl,99.99%) were purchased from Aladdin. N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.99%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.99%), chlorobenzene (CB, 99.99%), isopropanol (IPA, 99.99%), absolute ethyl alcohol (EtOH, 99.995%) and ether (99.99%) were bought from Sigma-Aldrich. Methylamine chloride (MACl, 99.99%), methylammonium iodide (MAI, 99.99%), formamidium iodide (FAI, 99.99%), cesium iodide (CsI, 99.99%), lead (II) iodide (PbI2, 99.99%), [6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PC61BM, 99.99%), buckminsterfullerene (C60, 99.99%), lead (II) bromide (PbBr2, 99.99%) and bathocuproine (BCP, 99.99%) were purchased from Advanced Election Technology. [4-(3,6-dimethyl-9-hydro-carbazole-9-yl)butyl]phosphonic acid (Me-4PACz, 99.99%) was obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industy (TCI). Ethanediamine dihydroiodide (EDAI2) was obtained from Xi’an Yuri Solar Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of NiOx nanoparticles by RTCP

9.0 g of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O was dissolved in a beaker containing 180 mL of water, and stirred at room temperature for 30 min. 3.0 g of NaOH was dissolved in 75 mL of deionized water and stirred at room temperature for 30 min. Then, using a peristaltic pump, the NaOH solution was slowly added into the Ni(NO3)2·6H2O solution at a rate of 20 drop·min−1. The addition of NaOH solution was stopped when the pH value reached 10. After continuous reaction for 30 min, a suspension of nickel hydroxide precipitate was obtained. Subsequently, the suspension was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, and the process of centrifugal washing was repeated three times to remove impurities and by-products. The obtained product was frozen in a freeze-drying machine at −100 °C for 3 h. Then, the sample was taken out and placed in a vacuum chamber with a vacuum degree of 5 Pa. After vacuuming for 15 h, nickel hydroxide (Ni(OH)2) was obtained. Next, Ni(OH)2 was placed in a muffle furnace and calcined at 270 °C for 2 h to obtain room-temperature nickel oxide nanoparticles (RT-NiOx).

Synthesis of NiOx nanoparticles by LTCP

9.0 g of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O was dissolved in a beaker containing 180 mL of water, and placed in an ice-water bath for stirring for 30 min. 3.0 g of NaOH was dissolved in 75 mL of deionized water and placed in an ice-water bath for stirring for 30 min. The other experimental procedures are the same as synthesizing RT-NiOx nanoparticles.

Fabrication of 1.55 eV inverted perovskite solar cells

The ITO substates were successively washed by detergent, deionized water, acetone and ethyl alcohol for 20 min. Then, the ITO substates were dried by nitrogen, followed by ultraviolet ozone treatment for 15 min. 10 mg of RT-NiOx or LT-NiOx NPs was dispersed in 1 mL of deionized water and sonicated for 10 min. Then, NiOx NPs dispersion solution was filtered by using PVDF filter. 50 μL of NiOx NPs dispersion solution was spin-coated on ITO at 5000 rpm for 30 s. Subsequently, the NiOx films were annealed at 150 °C for 10 min. 0.3 mg of Me-4PACz was dissolved in 1 mL of anhydrous ethanol and was shaked for 30 min. The Me-4PACz solution was filtered through using PTFE filter. After that, 50 μL of Me-4PACz solution was spin-coated on NiOx film at 5000 rpm for 30 s. The SAM films were annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. 1.73 M 1.55 eV Cs0.05MA0.05FA0.9PbI3 perovskite precursor solution was obtained by dissolving 20.78 mg of CsI, 247.68 mg of FAI, 12.72 mg of MAI, 15.00 mg of MACl, 796.55 mg of PbI2 (excess 10%) and in 1 mL of mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO with a volume ratio of 4:1. Then, additional 5 mol% MAPbCl3 was added into the above perovskite precursor solution. The perovskite films were deposited by spin-coating the perovskite precursor solution on glass/ITO/NiOx/Me-4PACz substrate at 2000 rpm for 10 s and 4000 rpm for 10 s. Then, the perovskite wet film was placed in a vacuum chamber and evacuated to 5 Pa for 11 s, followed by annealing at 150 °C for 1 min and 100 °C for 10 min. 1.4 mg·mL−1 EDAI2 solution in IPA was spin-coated on perovskite films at 5000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. 10 mg PC61BM was dissolved in chlorobenzene. PC61BM solution was filtered using PTFE filter. 30 μL of PC61BM solution was spin-coated on perovskite film at 2500 rpm for 40 s. Subsequently, 21 nm of C60, 6 nm of BCP and 100 nm of Ag were successively deposited on PC61BM film by thermal evaporation.

Fabrication of 1.68 eV inverted perovskite solar cells

The glass ITO/RT-NiOx or LT-NiOx/SAM was prepared as 1.55 eV inverted PSCs. 1.4 M 1.68 eV Cs0.05MA0.15FA0.8Pb(I0.76Br0.24)3 perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 18.20 mg of CsI, 192.60 mg of FAI, 536.00 mg of PbI2 (excess 10%), 131.00 mg of PbBr2 and 23.50 mg MABr in 1 mL of mixed solvents of DMF and DMSO with a volume ratio of 4:1. The perovskite films were deposited by spin-coating the perovskite precursor solution on glass/ITO/NiOx/Me-4PACz substrate at 2000 rpm for 10 s and 4000 rpm for 10 s. Then, the perovskite wet film was placed in a vacuum chamber and evacuated to 5 Pa for 15 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 15 min. 1 mg/mL EDAI2 solution in IPA was spin-coated on perovskite films at 5000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, 15 nm of C60, 6 nm of BCP and 100 nm of Ag were successively thermally evaporated on perovskite film.

Fabrication of 1.78 eV inverted perovskite solar cells

The glass ITO/RT-NiOx or LT-NiOx films were prepared as 1.55 eV inverted PSCs. 0.5 mg of Me-4PACz was dissolved in 1 mL of anhydrous ethanol and was shaked for 30 min. The Me-4PACz solution was filtered through using PTFE filter. After that, 50 μL of Me-4PACz solution was spin-coated on NiOx film at 4000 rpm for 40 s. The SAM films were annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. 1.2 M 1.78 eV Cs0.2FA0.8Pb(I0.6Br0.4)3 perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 62.35 mg of CsI, 165.07 mg of FAI, 221.30 mg of PbI2 and 264.25 mg of PbBr2 in 1 ml mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO with a volume ratio of 4:1. The perovskite solution was spin-coated on SAM films at 1000 rpm for 5 s and 5000 rpm for 30 s, and 700 μL of ether was dripped on the perovskite films at 10 s before ending program. Then, the perovskite films were annealed at 100 °C for 15 min. 0.5 mg·mL−1 EDAI2 solution in IPA was spin-coated on perovskite films at 5000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. 23 mg PC61BM was dissolved in chlorobenzene. PC61BM solution was filtered using PTFE filter. 30 μL of PC61BM solution was spin-coated on perovskite film at 2500 rpm for 40 s. The 0.5 mg·mL−1 BCP solution in IPA was spin-coated on perovskite films at 5000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, 100 nm of Ag were thermally evaporated on BCP film.

Fabrication of large-area perovskite solar modules

The process for preparing 1.55 eV Cs0.05 MA0.05FA0.9PbI3 perovskite large-area modules is similar with that of 1.55 eV Cs0.05 MA0.05FA0.9PbI3 small-area devices. Specifically, 1 mL of Cs0.05MA0.05FA0.9PbI3 precursor solution evenly spread on a 5×5 cm2 ITO substrate, followed by a 10-s resting period to ensure uniform dispersion of the solution before proceeding with spin-coating at 2500 rpm for 20 s. The assembly of subcells within the modules involved a three-step laser etching technique (P1, P2, P3) on a 2 × 7 cm2 ITO substrate, resulting in the formation of 11 subcells. Initially, the P1 step etched the ITO substrate with a 1064 nm, nanosecond laser, achieving a width of 30 ± 3 µm through single-path etching. This ensures that the insulation resistance between adjacent conductive layers exceeds 10 MΩ post-etching. Crucially, the P1 process does not leave residual TCO film within the etched tracks and preserves the integrity of the underlying glass. In the second step, P2, the ITO/NiOx/Me-4PACz/perovskite/C60/BCP was etched using a 532 nm, picosecond laser to a width of 50 ± 5 µm, ensuring the bottom TCO layer remains intact and maintaining a precise 30 µm gap between the P1 and P2 etch lines. The final stage, P3, employed a 532 nm, picosecond laser to etch the Au electrodes, matching the width of P1 (30 ± 3 µm) while also avoiding penetration of the bottom TCO layer and preserving a 30 µm interval between the P2 and P3 etchings.

Device performance characterizations

Current density-voltage (J-V) curves were measured using a Keithley 2400 Source Meter under simulated AM1.5 G illumination (Enli SS-X100R; Enli Technology, Taiwan), and the light intensity was calibrated using a standard silicon reference cell (Enli Technology Co., Ltd., Taiwan). The EQE curves were collected by an Enlitech EQE measurement system. The device area is 0.0535 cm2, which was determined by metal aperture mask. TPV and TPC were obtained by Tektronix oscilloscope as follows: the devices were illuminated with a laser pulses (MDL-NS-405-500mW, 6 ns width from an arbitrary function generator (AFG 31000 series)), and then the decay of signals was collected through a 3 series mixed domain oscilloscope.

Device stability measurements

For MPPT stability characterization, encapsulated devices were tested under continuous 1 sun illumination provided by a UV-free white LED lamp under ambient environment. All measurements were performed via an MPP tracking system (YH-VMPP-16, Yanghua, Suzhou). In the test, 16 photovoltaic cell samples were simultaneously loaded into the aging chamber, with the ambient temperature surrounding the cells stabilized at approximately 25 °C by a refrigeration system. The aging setup realizes MPP tracking and testing by continuously scanning and analyzing the MPP of each device. The white LED light source was calibrated to an irradiance of 100 mW/cm2. The long-term stability data of unencapsulated devices were collected with a Keithley 2400 digital source meter, and raw data were acquired through the Zeal Young K2400 Experimental Platform-Professional2450V3.5 software.

For the stability measurement under 85 °C and 85% RH, the encapsulated samples were placed in a JHLS-80 programmable temperature and humidity test chamber (China). The samples were removed every 200 h for J–V characterization under precisely controlled environmental conditions. The device encapsulation process involved the uniform application of a UV-curable adhesive onto a white glass substrate, followed by precise alignment and lamination of the glass onto a pre-fabricated device. Controlled pressure was applied to ensure full surface contact and eliminate entrapped air. The assembled structure was then subjected to UV irradiation for 5 min, after which it was transferred to a glove box for a 2-day post-curing period to ensure complete solidification of the encapsulant. Upon completion of curing, the device underwent visual inspection to confirm the absence of defects, including air bubbles, cracks, and edge delamination.

For the MPPT stability measurement at 70 °C, the encapsulated devices was assessed under continuous one-sun conditions using a white LED lamp without a UV filter in ambient conditions. These tests were conducted using an MPP tracking system (YH-VMPP-16, Yanghua, Suzhou). Specifically, 16 solar cell samples were simultaneously placed in aging equipment. The environment around solar cells were maintained at around 70 °C through a heating system. Aging equipment is tracked and tested by continuously scanning and analyzing the maximum power point of the device. White LED light sources have a light intensity energy of 100 mW·cm−2. The recording of long-term stability of the sealed devices was performed using the Keithley 2400 digital source meter, and the data was obtained using the Zeal Young software K2400 Experimental Platform-Professional2450V3.5.

Density functional theory calculation

First-principles calculations based on DFT were performed to investigate the Ni vacancy formation energy in NiOx and the adsorption energy of SAMs on NiOx surfaces with varying Ni vacancy concentrations. The calculations were carried out using a plane-wave basis set code. The electron-ion interactions were described by the projector augmented-wave method, and the exchange-correlation functional was treated within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof parameterization. A plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff of 400 eV was used.

Three distinct NiOx (001) surface slab models were constructed to represent different synthesis conditions: P-NiO, RT-NiOx and LT-NiOx. P-NiO represents a pristine surface with 64 Ni2+ ions in the top layer, serving as the reference. RT-NiOx is a model for NiOx synthesized at room temperature, featuring a low concentration of Ni vacancies. This was created by introducing 16 Ni2+ vacancies into the P-NiO model. To maintain charge neutrality, each Ni2+ vacancy necessitates the formation of two Ni3+ ions, resulting in a Ni3+: Ni2+ ratio of 2:1 in the top layer. LT-NiOx is a model for NiOx synthesized at low temperature, featuring a high concentration of Ni vacancies. This was created by introducing 17 Ni2+ vacancies, resulting in a Ni3+: Ni2+ ratio of 2.6:1.

To model the ground-state antiferromagnetic (AFM) order of NiOx, a spin-polarized calculation with the appropriate magnetic configuration was employed. The strongly correlated nature of the Ni 3 d electrons was addressed using the GGA + U approach within the Dudarev formalism, with an effective Hubbard U parameter (Ueff) of 8.0 eV applied to Ni atoms. The electronic self-consistent iteration and the ionic relaxation were considered converged when the energy change and the residual forces on atoms were below 1 × 10−4 eV and 0.03 eV/Å, respectively.

The formation energy of a Ni vacancy was calculated as Ef(VNi) = E(d-NiOx) − E(NiOx) + E(Ni), where E(d-NiOx) is the energy of the NiOx supercell with a Ni vacancy, E(NiOx) is the energy of the pristine NiOx supercell, and E(Ni) is the chemical of Ni. The adsorption energy of a SAM on the NiOx surface was calculated as Ead = E(NiOx@SAM) − E(NiOx) − E(SAM), where E(NiOx@SAM) is the energy of the optimized adsorption system, E(NiOx) is the energy of the respective NiOx surface slab (P-NiOx, RT-NiOx, or LT-NiOx) before adsorption, and E(SAM) is the energy of an isolated SAM in a large vacuum box.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted to study the surface coverage of SAMs on NiOx using the LAMMPS software package. A large NiOx surface model with lateral dimensions of 11.8 × 11.8 nm was constructed. The interatomic interactions were described by the CVFF force field. The simulations were performed in the NVT ensemble at a temperature of 300 K, maintained using a Nosé-Hoover thermostat. A time step of 0.5 fs was used, and the total simulation time for each run was 5 ps, which was confirmed to be sufficient for the system to reach equilibrium and for the results to converge.
