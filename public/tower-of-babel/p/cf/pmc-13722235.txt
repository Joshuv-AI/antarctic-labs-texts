Intracranial hemorrhage in a patient with Guillain–Barré syndrome following intravenous immunoglobulin therapy: A rare complication
Authors: Preeti Singh Dhoat, Amandeep Kaur, Rohit Raina, Navdeep Kaur, Sonali Mina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGuillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) is an acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy characterized by progressive weakness, areflexia, and variable autonomic dysfunction. Intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is an extremely uncommon neurological complication associated with GBS and has been rarely described in the literature. We report a case of a 53-year-old female who presented with rapidly progressive quadriparesis and respiratory failure. Clinical findings and nerve conduction studies were suggestive of pure motor axonal GBS. The patient was treated with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a dose of 0.4 g/kg/day for five days. During treatment, she developed sudden deterioration in sensorium, and computed tomography of the brain revealed pontine hemorrhage with perilesional edema. Platelet count and coagulation parameters remained within normal limits. Despite intensive supportive management, the patient developed cardiac arrest and could not be revived. Intracranial hemorrhage is a rare but potentially fatal complication in patients with GBS. Sudden neurological deterioration during the course of disease or IVIG therapy should prompt immediate neuroimaging evaluation.

Introduction

Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) is an immune-mediated peripheral neuropathy characterized by rapidly progressive weakness, usually symmetrical and associated with hyporeflexia or areflexia, and a variable amount of sensory and autonomic dysfunction. Respiratory failure requiring ventilatory support may occur in severe cases. Electrophysiological studies classify GBS into demyelinating and axonal variants, with axonal forms generally associated with more severe disease and poorer outcomes.[12]

Autonomic dysfunction is a recognized manifestation of GBS and may result in fluctuations in blood pressure, cardiac arrhythmias, and other systemic complications. However, central nervous system complications such as intracranial hemorrhage are exceedingly rare. Only isolated cases of intracranial hemorrhage associated with GBS have been reported in the literature.[345] Previous studies have suggested that autonomic instability, endothelial dysfunction, vasospasm, and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)-related hemodynamic changes can contribute to cerebrovascular events in these patients.[456]

We report a rare case of fatal pontine hemorrhage occurring during treatment of axonal GBS with intravenous immunoglobulin.

Case Presentation

A 53-year-old female with no known prior medical comorbidities presented with progressive weakness of both lower limbs for four days, which subsequently involved both upper limbs and neck muscles. She also developed respiratory distress and was intubated at an outside facility before referral to our institute.

At presentation, the patient was hemodynamically unstable with a blood pressure of 82/54 mmHg and pulse rate of 53 beats/minute. Oxygen saturation was maintained on mechanical ventilation. Neurological examination revealed a Glasgow Coma Scale score of E4VtM6 with bilaterally equal and reactive pupils. Motor examination demonstrated muscle power of 1/5 in all four limbs according to the Medical Research Council grading system. Deep tendon reflexes were absent in both upper and lower limbs.

Nerve conduction studies demonstrated markedly reduced compound muscle action potential amplitudes in sampled nerves, suggestive of pure motor axonal polyneuropathy. GBS was diagnosed based on the electrophysiological and clinical results.

IVIG was administered to the patient for five days at a dose of 0.4 g/kg/day. By the third day of treatment, there had been a little increase in muscle strength and a 2/5 improvement in motor power across all limbs.

However, on the fifth day of IVIG therapy, the patient developed sudden deterioration in sensorium. Urgent computed tomography (CT) scan of the brain revealed pontine hemorrhage with perilesional edema. Platelet count and coagulation parameters remained within normal limits. CT angiography could not be performed at the time of the event to evaluate for reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome or vascular abnormalities.

Neurosurgical consultation was obtained, and conservative management was advised. Despite intensive supportive care and ventilatory support, the patient’s neurological condition progressively deteriorated. She subsequently developed sudden cardiac arrest on the seventh day of admission and could not be revived.

Discussion

GBS is primarily a disorder of the peripheral nervous system; however, severe autonomic dysfunction may occur during the disease course and contribute to life-threatening systemic and neurological complications. Only isolated reports of intracranial hemorrhage complicating GBS are available in the literature.[345]

The exact mechanism underlying intracranial hemorrhage in GBS remains uncertain. Proposed explanations include autonomic dysregulation causing marked blood pressure fluctuations, impaired cerebral autoregulation, endothelial injury, and cerebral vasospasm.[4]

Another potential contributing factor is IVIG therapy. Although IVIG remains one of the standard treatments for GBS and is generally considered safe, cerebrovascular complications, including ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome, have occasionally been reported.[56] Proposed mechanisms include transient hyperviscosity, hemodynamic alterations, endothelial dysfunction, and immune-mediated vascular changes.

Our patient had rapidly progressive axonal GBS requiring ventilatory support, suggesting severe systemic and autonomic involvement.

In the present case, the patient developed pontine hemorrhage during the course of IVIG therapy in the absence of thrombocytopenia or coagulation abnormalities. Although a direct causal relationship cannot be conclusively established, the temporal association suggests that autonomic instability and IVIG-related vascular changes contributed to the event.

This case also highlights the importance of considering intracranial complications in patients with GBS who develop acute neurological deterioration. Altered sensorium, pupillary abnormalities, focal neurological deficits, or unexplained hemodynamic instability should prompt urgent neuroimaging evaluation.

The prognosis of intracranial hemorrhage complicating GBS appears poor based on the limited literature available. Despite aggressive supportive management, our patient had a fatal outcome.

Limitations

This report has certain limitations. CT angiography could not be performed at the time of neurological deterioration; therefore, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome or occult vascular abnormalities could not be definitively excluded. In addition, the rarity of reported cases limits the ability to establish a definite causal relationship between GBS, IVIG therapy, and intracranial hemorrhage.

Conclusion

Intracranial hemorrhage is a rare but serious complication that may occur during the course of GBS or its treatment. Sudden neurological deterioration in patients with GBS should not be attributed solely to disease progression, and prompt neuroimaging evaluation should be considered to exclude intracranial complications.
