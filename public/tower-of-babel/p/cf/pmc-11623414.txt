Verruciform Xanthoma Presenting Within an Ear Piercing
Authors: Hannah E. Myers, Subhapradha Rangarajan, Michael Roberts, Christopher Buckley, Josh Hammel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Verruciform xanthomas are rare lesions affecting 0.025-0.5% of the population. They present as an asymptomatic, slow growing solitary lesion on the oral mucosa. Commonly misdiagnose as squamous cell carcinoma, verruca vulgaris, leukoplakia, and lichen planus. Presented is a 22 year old male with a painful verruciform xanthoma on the posterior ear lobe in a prior ear piercing.

Introduction

Verruciform xanthomas are rare lesions affecting 0.025–0.5% of the population.[1] They present as an asymptomatic, slow-growing solitary lesion on the oral mucosa. Rarely, cases involving the anogenital region and extremities have been documented.[12] Verruciform xanthomas are commonly a pale white or red color with a papillary appearance.[1] However, polypoid, sessile and pedunculated morphologies with a rough or a pebbly surface have also been recorded.[12] This case focuses on the unusual presentation of a verruciform xanthoma as a new-onset, painful lesion of the ear at a previous ear piercing site.

Case Presentation

A 22-year-old male presented with a painful growth on the left posterior ear lobe. The lesion was a 0.8 cm symmetric peculated nodule with a skin-colored stalk. The center was yellow with slight crusting [Figure 1]. Three years prior, the patient had an ear piercing in the area. Three months prior to presentation in the clinic, the patient noticed pain and bleeding at the piercing site, along with a new bump. The patient denied any previous similar lesions and was unaware of any family history of such lesions. Differential diagnoses at this time included verruca v. xanthoma v. irritated nevus v. basal cell carcinoma v. keloid. Shave biopsy with electrodessication was performed. Shave biopsy was chosen for its ability to test the area without interference with the piercing site or the need for subsequent suture removal.

Pedunculated papule on the posterior lobule of the ear

Pathology revealed a verrucous-like lesion with digitated epidermal hyperplasia with hyperkeratosis and columns of parakeratotic cornified cells within invaginations of epithelium upon low magnification [Figure 2]. Upon higher magnification, there were lipid-laden macrophages within dermal papillae [Figure 3]. Histopathological differential included verruca versus xanthoma but was most consistent with xanthoma due to the presence of lipid-laden macrophages. The patient was informed of the results and instructed to follow up with their primary care provider for any necessary lab work.

Discussion

Due to the wide variety of potential presentations, differential diagnoses of verruciform xanthoma is broad and includes papilloma, fibroepithelial polyp, squamous cell carcinoma, verruca vulgaris, leukoplakia, lichen planus and more.[12] In our patient, due to the presentation of the lesion on the ear in a previous piercing site, the primary diagnosis prior to biopsy was a keloid. Histopathology of verruciform xanthoma is pathognomonic for the lesion and commonly reveals the underlying diagnosis. Features include the presence of lipid-laden foamy histiocytes within the connective-tissue papillae and elongated dermal papillae.[12]

Verruciform xanthoma, though rare, has been shown to be associated with genodermatoses such as recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa, keratitis-ichthyosis-deafness syndrome and congenital hemidysplasia with ichthyosiform erythroderma and limb defects (CHILD) syndrome.[345] These associations are serious, with significant morbidity and mortality, and result in the recommendation for genetic testing for those diagnosed with even mild cases of verruciform xanthomas.[2] However, comorbidities are not limited to rare, hereditary conditions. Verruciform Xanthoma has also been associated with more common conditions including hypercholesterolemia, immunocompromised states, fungal and viral infection.[1] Care should be taken to ensure the patient is receiving proper screening and treatment for these potential comorbidities.

The pathogenesis of verruciform xanthoma is not completely known; however, several theories propose this condition to be a chronic reactive process in the epithelium. According to this theory, trauma causes keratinocyte damage and degenerates epithelial cell membranes, attracting neutrophils and monocytes to the injured site.[2] Damaged keratinocytes are degraded by the oxidative processes within neutrophils rendering them necrotic. These necrotic cells are then phagocytosed by macrophages with the eventual formation of foam cells due to the accumulation of lipids.[12] Examples of such trauma include carcinoma in situ, local immunological disorder, viral or candida infections.[16] Trauma-induced xanthomas are more likely to be eruptive in nature, compared to their spontaneous counterparts.[7]

There are multiple treatment options for verruciform xanthoma which vary in favor due to the variety of presentations and number of lesions. The most common treatment options include cryosurgery, surgical excision, electrosurgery, pulsed dye laser and carbon dioxide laser. Surgical excision has traditionally been favored for single, larger lesions, while cryosurgery is favored for a more numerous, widespread distribution.[8] In our patient, the lesion resolved with electrocautery at the time of biopsy.

Verruciform xanthoma is challenging to diagnose clinically due to the wide variety of presentations and difficulty distinguishing from more common lesions, including papilloma, verrucous carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma.[2] For example, in a case series of 24 patients ultimately diagnosed with verruciform xanthoma, the correct diagnosis was not suspected in any of the patients from clinical presentation alone.[29] Furthermore, the presentation of a verruciform xanthoma at a piercing site on the ear is even more rare, with authors finding only one recorded case.[10] Biopsy is therefore the preferred method of diagnosis. The prognosis of verruciform xanthoma is excellent compared to some of its look-alikes with malignant potential; however, care should be taken in recommending appropriate genetic testing and screening to rule out more serious conditions.
