Exploring Gender Disparities and Advances in Transcatheter Structural Heart Interventions
Authors: Mirvat Alasnag
Published: 2026

The field of transcatheter structural interventions has evolved significantly over the last decade, permitting us to offer solutions to patients with structural diseases who were previously deemed inoperable and assigned to conservative therapy alone. For example, MITRAL prospectively evaluated the safety and feasibility of balloon-expandable aortic transcatheter heart valve implantation in those with mitral annulus calcification. Previously, these patients were excluded from such therapies owing to the high risk of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. The MITRAL cohort was pre-emptively treated with alcohol septal ablation to decrease that risk.1

Along with developments in technology, mounting evidence has placed transcatheter therapy as a frontline strategy. In fact, the indication for use of such technologies continues to expand every day. Yet the sex-specific differences in the safety, feasibility and long-term outcomes have not been sufficiently studied in women. Over the course of the last two decades, trials examining outcomes for the various structural interventions have expanded with increased trial-level mean age.2 However, there are persistent sex disparities in enrolment that fail to inform equitable transcatheter solutions.2

We have ascertained that the physiological and biological responses of women to the various structural heart pathologies differ from those of men. For instance, women with severe aortic stenosis (AS) are less likely to have underlying bicuspid disease, less leaflet calcification, more myocardial fibrosis and concentric hypertrophy and remodelling, and hence more paradoxical low-flow AS and more patient-prosthesis mismatch.3 Crousillat and colleagues have underscored the low rates of clinical recognition ofAS using transthoracic echocardiography in women, including assessment of AS severity, which contributes to the late detection and treatment of women, which could ultimately impact outcomes.4

Reassuringly, two important trials addressed outcomes of women undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVI) in recent years. The RHEIA trial explored outcomes of TAVI in women compared with surgical aortic valve replacement. The 1-year outcomes noted that the primary composite endpoint of all-cause mortality, stroke, or rehospitalisation for valve- or procedure-related symptoms or worsening of heart failure was 45% lower with TAVI, meeting criteria for both noninferiority and superiority.5 The other trial was the SMART trial, which enrolled symptomatic severe AS patients and an aortic-valve annulus area of 430 mm2 or less in a 1:1 ratio. Patients were randomised to undergo TAVI with either a self-expanding supra-annular valve or a balloon-expandable valve. The study met its coprimary end points (a composite of death, disabling stroke, or rehospitalisation for heart failure for noninferiority as well as a composite end point measuring bioprosthetic valve dysfunction for superiority) at 1 year (9.4% versus 11.8%; p=0.35).6 A subgroup analysis of women enrolled in the SMART trial (621 in total, constituting 87% of those enrolled) reported a significantly lower rate of bioprosthetic valve dysfunction at one year following TAVI with self-expanding compared with balloon-expandable valves (8.4% versus 41.8%; p<0.001). Additionally, the effective orifice area was higher (1.97 versus 1.49 cm2), and the mean gradient was lower (7.7 versus 15.8 mmHg) for those who received self-expanding devices (p<0.001 for both).7

Similar to the aortic valve disorders, there are notable differences in mitral and tricuspid valve interventions in women. The international, multicentre real-world EuroSMR registry noted that women were significantly older with fewer comorbidities than male patients undergoing transcatheter therapy of the mitral valve. Women also had a higher left ventricular ejection fraction with a significantly smaller left ventricle compared with men, even after adjustment for body surface area.8 Despite the aforementioned differences, the registry demonstrated a similar reduction in mitral regurgitation in women and men with no sex-related differences in clinical outcomes at two years.8 As for the tricuspid valve, women have isolated tricuspid regurgitation more commonly, which is often non-ischaemic in aetiology.9 Early observational data suggest that women have marginally better procedural success rates with edge-to-edge repair, as well as long-term survival and greater quality of life improvement. Although the tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion/mean pulmonary arterial pressure ratio may serve as a valuable prognosticator following transcatheter tricuspid valve implantation, sex-specific thresholds are yet to be identified to guide ideal procedural timing.9

The scope of this special collection is to dissect the current body of evidence for patients undergoing transcatheter procedures. Mitral and tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair are becoming common interventions in modern cath labs. As such, the first article in the edition by Arnautovic and colleagues focuses on outcomes and complications associated with both procedures. Understanding the salient differences in baseline characteristics between men and women and a review of the literature will allow us to identify and address the gaps in evidence.10

Currently, there are no trials that have identified sex differences in the incidence and management of stroke following TAVI. In our article, we examine potential sex differences in the pathophysiology, preventive strategies and therapeutic options for stroke following TAVI, including embolic protection devices.11

Despite this increase in disease burden in women, imaging indices tailored for women are understudied and underused in both research and clinical practice. Attar et al. summarise the conundrum emphasising the delayed referral for intervention, suboptimal risk stratification and arbitrary procedural and device selection noted in heart team discussions. They review the current evidence and focus on disparities in imaging in women with valvular heart disease.12

Individuals with structural heart diseases often present with heart failure (HF). HF is a life-threatening condition that affects women and men differently. Wijesinghe et al. provide an overview of the sex differences in those with HF reduced ejection fraction and preserved ejection fraction. The authors also summarise sex differences in the cardiomyopathies and modern therapies, including support devices and transplantation.13

Finally, pregnancy is a unique condition during which women may require valvular or structural interventions. Frogoudaki and colleagues present a practical approach to the decision-making process for pregnant women.14 Balancing foetal safety and the mother’s survival is paramount, and this article advocates for the use of transcatheter interventions during pregnancy in select cases to improve outcomes of both mother and child. It also highlights the striking paucity of evidence on transcatheter valve therapies during pregnancy.
