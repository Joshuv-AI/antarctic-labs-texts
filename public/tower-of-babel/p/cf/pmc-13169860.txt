Cross-algorithm machine learning and consensus features for early disseminated intravascular coagulation risk prediction in sepsis-induced coagulopathy
Authors: Fanxuan Chen, Xuewen Mei, Dongren Yang, Darong Hai, Xueying Bao, Hao Chen, Ruoyun Wang, Zefei Mo, Yu Xia, Li Zeng, Ziyang Zhang, Yan Zu, Qi Zhao, Jingye Pan, Xiaowei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis-induced coagulopathy (SIC) represents an early and potentially reversible stage of coagulation dysfunction in sepsis, whereas progression to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is associated with multi-organ failure and markedly increased mortality. Although existing diagnostic criteria and machine learning (ML) models have demonstrated utility in broad sepsis populations, they lack specificity for identifying high-risk subgroups within SIC patients who are most likely to deteriorate into DIC. Timely and precise risk stratification at this critical transition point remains an unmet clinical need.MethodsWe conduct a retrospective, multicenter study integrating data from MIMIC-IV v3.1 database and an independent cohort from the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. A total of 4,874 SIC patients are included, comprising 1,629 patients from the local cohort and 3,245 patients from MIMIC-IV. Using 61 routinely available clinical variables collected within 48 h of ICU admission, we design a two-stage ML framework. In phase I, 16 ML algorithms are combined with 11 feature importance ranking methods and backward elimination to identify algorithm-specific optimal feature subsets. In phase II, a cross-algorithm consensus strategy is applied to extract globally stable features and construct a minimalist, interpretable predictive model. Model performance is evaluated using accuracy (ACC), area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and F1-score. External validation was performed using the MIMIC-IV cohort. Model interpretability was assessed through permutation feature importance, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), and a clinically deployable nomogram, with calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) used to assess clinical utility.ResultsAcross single-algorithm optimization, the Gradient Boosting Classifier (GB) model achieved the best balance between performance and robustness. Cross-algorithm consensus analysis identified seven highly stable predictors: intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay (LOS), vasopressor, albumin (ALB) level, Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) estimate, Logistic Organ Dysfunction System (LODS) score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy (CRRT). The final GB model incorporating these seven features achieved an F1-score of 0.71 and an AUC of 0.75 in the internal validation cohort, while demonstrating excellent generalization in the external validation cohort (F1-score 0.92, AUC 0.74, ACC 0.92). Interpretability analyses confirmed that disease severity, organ dysfunction, and treatment intensity were the primary drivers of DIC progression. The nomogram exhibited good calibration and provided consistent net clinical benefit across a wide range of risk thresholds in DCA.ConclusionsWe propose a robust and interpretable ML framework for early identification of SIC patients at high risk of progression to DIC, based on only seven routinely available clinical indicators. By transitioning from algorithm-specific optimization to cross-algorithm consensus feature selection, this study achieves a favorable balance between predictive performance, generalizability, and clinical usability. This model offers a practical decision-support tool for precision risk stratification and targeted intervention in sepsis-associated coagulopathy. Prospective multicenter studies incorporating dynamic time-series data are warranted to further validate and extend its real-time clinical applicability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08067-4.Keywords: Sepsis-induced coagulopathy, Disseminated intravascular coagulation, Machine learning, Two-stage feature selection, Interpretability

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening multiorgan dysfunction syndrome caused by dysregulated host responses to infection, representing a major global public health challenge [1]. Statistics indicate approximately 49 million new sepsis cases and 11 million related deaths annually worldwide, accounting for 20% of global mortality [2]. Coagulation dysfunction is a common severe complication of sepsis. Globally, approximately 24.0%–60.0% of sepsis patients develop SIC [3, 4], with reported incidence rates as high as 67.9% in the Chinese population [5, 6]. Among these, 30%–50% of SIC patients further progress to DIC, elevating the mortality rate to 28%–43% [7, 8]. SIC represents the early reversible stage of coagulation abnormalities in sepsis, characterized by thrombocytopenia, prolonged coagulation times, and organ dysfunction, whereas DIC signifies the coagulation system entering a decompensated phase, highly prone to triggering multiple organ failure, with limited treatment options and extremely poor prognosis [9–12]. Therefore, early identification of high-risk DIC patients during the SIC stage is crucial for improving outcomes.

However, early DIC identification faces multiple challenges in clinical practice. First, its pathophysiology involves highly coupled and dynamically imbalanced interactions among inflammation, coagulation, and fibrinolysis systems, exhibiting significant heterogeneity and time dependency. Traditional static coagulation markers struggle to capture this complex progression [13–15]. Second, clinical symptoms lack specificity and are easily confused with severe liver disease or thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) [9, 16]. Existing DIC diagnostic criteria (e.g., the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) overt DIC score, the Japanese Association for Acute Medicine (JAAM) criteria) primarily rely on late markers such as decreased fibrinogen and elevated fibrin/fibrinogen degradation products (FDP)/D-dimer, often lagging behind organ damage; compounded by low clinical detection rates for certain key indicators, further exacerbating diagnostic delays [17, 18]. Although SIC scores exhibit high sensitivity (predicting overt DIC with sensitivity ranging from 84.4% to 98.7%), their low specificity leads to high false-positive rates, increasing unnecessary anticoagulation risks and amplifying healthcare burdens [10]. Given that anticoagulation therapy yields the greatest benefit during the SIC phase, while efficacy plummets and bleeding risks surge once DIC develops [5, 19], there is an urgent need for a tool to precisely identify high-risk subgroups within the SIC population who are likely to progress to DIC, enabling targeted intervention within the critical “time window.”

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have offered new opportunities for precision prediction in critical care medicine [20–24]. Previous studies have employed machine learning algorithms (e.g., eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), GB) to construct predictive models for SIC risk or mortality, demonstrating superior performance (AUC = 0.81) [25–28]. However, most existing studies adopt a broad-spectrum prediction strategy across the entire sepsis population. They ignored the specific transitional stage from SIC to DIC. Since only a subset of SIC patients ultimately progress to DIC, such “one-size-fits-all” approaches may lead to reduced specificity and limited clinical applicability for sequential risk stratification [29].

Therefore, this study aims to develop and validate a ML model specifically designed for early identification of the risk of SIC patients progressing to DIC. We systematically integrate 16 ML algorithms, 11 feature importance evaluation methods, and cross-model consensus strategies to construct a two-stage feature selection and model optimization scheme. This study focuses on identifying a cross-model stable, clinically interpretable, and minimalist key feature set, providing clinicians with an efficient and reliable early warning tool to optimize precision stratified management of sepsis-associated coagulopathy (SAC).

Materials and methods

Ethics and quality standards

Data is sourced from the publicly available Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, version 3.1) database and the local dataset for First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. This study has obtained authorization to use MIMIC-IV v3.1 database (Authorization ID: 66528040). The research protocol is approved by the Ethics Committee of First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Ethics Approval No.: KY2025-R201, 2008–March 2025), with informed consent waived. Our methods and reporting strictly follow the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) guidelines, meeting ethical and quality standards for this quality improvement study.

Data sources and dataset construction

MIMIC-IV is a large-scale, publicly available, de-identified clinical database for intensive care. The study is based on the latest released version, MIMIC-IV v3.1, covering clinical information from over 65,000 intensive care unit (ICU) patients and more than 200,000 emergency department patients from 2008 to 2022 [30–32]. The First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University is a comprehensive Grade A tertiary hospital in Wenzhou, Zhejiang Province, China. Clinical data are collected from SIC patients admitted to its ICU between May 2008 and March 2025. Following established inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final cohort includes 3245 SIC patients from MIMIC-IV v3.1 database (DIC incidence approximately 3.9%) and 1629 SIC patients from First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (DIC incidence approximately 40.8%). SIC and DIC diagnoses are determined according to the sepsis 3.0 criteria published in 2016, the SIC and overt DIC criteria published by ISTH in 2017 (Supplementary Table S1) [9, 33]. Dataset cleaning and preprocessing

Data for the study are extracted using Structured Query Language (SQL) and managed with PostgreSQL (version 13.7.2) and Navicat Premium (version 16). The worst values of various clinical indicators within 48 h of the patient’s initial ICU admission are collected (i.e., the values deviating most significantly from the normal reference range, reflecting the most severe pathophysiological state in the early disease phase). A total of 61 candidate features are collected for model construction, including: (1) demographic information (e.g., age, gender); (2) vital signs (e.g., heart rate (HR), blood pressure); (3) laboratory indicators (e.g., complete blood count, hepatic/renal function, coagulation parameters); (4) scoring systems (e.g., SOFA, Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII)); (5) therapeutic interventions (e.g., mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use); (6) comorbidities (e.g., myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure), as detailed in supplementary Table S2.

Data preprocessing is performed using Python 3.9. (1) All continuous variables are retained with four decimal places. Missing values in continuous variables are imputed using the column mean from the same group. All continuous variables undergo Z-score standardization to eliminate the influence of different measurement scales. (2) No categorical variables are missing in this study, eliminating the need for imputation. For categorical variables, “0” indicates no comorbidity or no treatment intervention, while “1” indicates presence of comorbidity or corresponding treatment measures. In the gender variable, “1” denotes male and “0” denotes female. For outcome labels, “0” represents no progression to DIC, and “1” represents progression to DIC.

Dataset division and external verification

To ensure the scientific rigor of the study and the reliability of model training results, the local dataset is randomly split into a training set (n = 977) and an internal validation set (n = 652) at a 6:4 ratio. The MIMIC-IV dataset serves as an independent external validation set (n = 3,245) to evaluate the model’s generalization ability (Fig. 1a).

To assess the balance of baseline feature distributions between the local and public datasets, we conduct systematic intergroup comparisons. Continuous variables undergo Shapiro-Wilk normality testing. Normally distributed variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with intergroup comparisons using independent samples t-tests; non-normally distributed variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) [median (Q1, Q3)], with group comparisons using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are reported as frequency and percentage [n (%)], where group comparisons for unordered categorical variables used Pearson’s ꭕ² test or Fisher’s exact test, and for ordered categorical variables used the Mann-Whitney U test. All statistical analyses are performed using R version 4.2.2. Statistical tests are two-tailed, and P value < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Two-stage feature selection framework

To balance predictive accuracy for SIC patients progressing to DIC with model robustness, this study designs a two-stage feature selection and model optimization framework which are shown in Fig. 1b. This framework extends from “intra-algorithm optimization” to “cross-algorithm consensus aggregation”, aiming to overcome the randomness of single-model feature selection and identify the most reliable core feature subset.

Phase I: Single-algorithm feature optimization

This phase explores the performance boundaries of individual algorithms across different feature combinations. We select 16 representative ML algorithms, encompassing linear models (e.g., Logistic Regression (LR), Ridge), tree models (e.g., Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, Extra Trees), and kernel methods support vector machine (SVM) to ensure broad coverage of the model space (see Appendix Table S3). To systematically evaluate feature importance and mitigate inherent biases of individual methods, we employ 11 feature importance assessment techniques categorized into four paradigms: filtering (e.g., Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), Mutual Information (MI)), wrapping-based (e.g., Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE)), embedding-based (e.g., XGBoost built-in importance), and collinearity diagnostics (Variance Inflation Factor (VIF)). These aim to generate feature value rankings from diverse perspectives (see Appendix Table S4).

We combine 16 algorithms with each of the 11 feature importance evaluation methods. For each “algorithm + ranking method” combination, we employ a backward sequential feature elimination strategy, progressively removing bottom-ranked features based on their importance scores, reducing the feature dimension from 61 to 3. After each dimension reduction, we employ 5-fold cross-validation and grid search to reconfigure model hyperparameters. Using F1 score as the core metric, we capture each algorithm’s peak performance achieved through coupling with the optimal feature subset and best ranking method.

Phase II: consensus feature selection and optimal model

This phase aims to distill “global consensus features” from the multiple “local optimal solutions” identified in phase I. We first aggregate the optimal feature subsets selected by 16 algorithms and calculates the selection frequency for each feature. High frequency indicates robust predictive value across different algorithmic assumptions, representing a “consensus metric.” Features are then ranked by frequency, followed by repeated backward elimination. This dynamically monitors model performance as feature count decreases, achieving model refinement. Finally, we gain the model combination yielding the highest F1-score on our internal test set while adhering to Occam’s Razor (i.e., incorporating the minimal number of features) as the final optimal predictive model.

Indicator setting and model evaluation

Considering the class imbalance in our datasets, we select F1-score as the primary evaluation metric. The F1-score can balance precision and recall, providing a more informative measure of model performance when positive events are rare. In addition to F1-score, we systematically report other common model evaluation metrics, including ACC, AUC and confusion matrix elements (True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), and False Negatives (FN)), to provide a comprehensive assessment of model performance.

Clinically applicable simplified assessment tool

To enhance the model’s interpretability and operational feasibility in real clinical settings, we construct a personalized risk assessment tool based on the key features incorporated into the final optimized model. This tool takes the form of a nomogram that quantifies the contribution of measured features. It aims to provide clinicians with an intuitive and transparent risk assessment method, thereby assisting them in making more precise individualized decisions.

We evaluate the nomogram’s discriminative efficacy using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and their corresponding AUC. ROC curves visually reflect the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity, while AUC quantifies a model’s classification performance, facilitating cross-model comparisons. Considering the class imbalance in the dataset, we further plot calibration curves to more sensitively capture performance variations in minority class identification. Additionally, DCA is employed to evaluate the potential clinical net benefit at different risk thresholds, thereby measuring the model’s practical application value.

Permutation feature importance (PFI) analysis and SHAP explainability analysis

To address the “black box” effect of complex models, this study incorporates multi-level interpretability analysis. First, Permutation Importance (PI) quantifies and ranks the global contribution of each variable to assess feature stability across different evaluation algorithms. Second, recognizing that the selected optimal model may contain nonlinear structures, we employ SHAP for in-depth analysis. SHAP swarm plots reveal the positive/negative impact and intensity distribution of features across all samples, while SHAP waterfall plots decompose decision paths for representative cases, visually illustrating the incremental accumulation from baseline risk to final prediction probability. This multi-level explanatory mechanism provides transparent, traceable, and clinically intuitive interpretations of the model’s predictive logic.

Results

Population statistics and baseline analysis of datasets

Significant heterogeneity exists between the local and public datasets in patient baseline characteristics and internal variable distributions (see Fig. 2 and Appendix Table S5). Results indicate extensive and significant differences in disease severity and outcomes between patients in the two datasets (P < 0.01 for all comparisons). The local dataset represents a cohort of patients with more severe illness and greater pathophysiological disturbance, exhibiting a significantly higher incidence of DIC compared to the public dataset (40.8% vs. 3.9%). The local dataset exhibits longer and more variable ICU LOS (P < 0.001), higher SOFA scores (7 [5, 9] vs. 3 [2, 4], P < 0.001), higher LODS scores (7 [5, 9] vs. 5 [4, 8], P < 0.001), and higher APSIII scores (66 [53, 80] vs. 47 [34, 62], P < 0.001). The results of these statistical differences are presented in Fig. 2a and c. Patients exhibit more unstable hemodynamics, manifested by lower systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressures, along with compensatory increases in HR and respiratory rate (all P < 0.001). More severe renal impairment, inflammatory responses, and electrolyte disturbances are also present (P < 0.001) (see Appendix Table S5). Intragroup comparisons reveal that the core characteristic of the DIC group is severe coagulation dysfunction, manifested by significantly prolonged Prothrombin Time (PT), Partial Thromboplastin Time (PTT), International Normalized Ratio (INR), and markedly decreased platelet counts (P < 0.001). Across all datasets, DIC occurrence is strongly associated with higher disease severity scores, acute kidney injury (AKI), hemodynamic instability, and greater dependence on advanced life support (see Appendix Table S5).

Correlation analysis among features reveals two pairs exhibiting the strongest linear relationships, demonstrating highly consistent stability across the training, internal validation, and external validation datasets. Specifically, Pearson correlation coefficients between ICU LOS and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) are 0.94, 0.95, and 0.94 across the three datasets, respectively. Similarly, the correlation coefficient between absolute neutrophil count and respiratory rate remains consistently high at 0.93, 0.99, and 0.93 (Fig. 2d, Appendix Fig. S1a-c). Feature distribution heatmaps reveal more pronounced differences in feature distributions between DIC-positive and DIC-negative groups in the external test set compared to the training set (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. S1a-c). This indicates that external validation not only tests the model but also rigorously assesses its generalization capability.

Model performance evaluation and consensus feature selection

Phase I: internal feature selection for single algorithms

We explore the performance boundaries of 16 algorithms as the number of features decreases from 61 to 3. When all features are included, the Random Forest model based on variance analysis for feature importance evaluation achieves optimal performance on the internal test set (F1 = 0.70, AUC = 0.75, ACC = 0.71) (Supplementary Fig. S2c). Detailed performance metrics for all models on the training and external test sets are presented in Supplementary Fig. S2a-c. As the number of features decreased, all models show a trend of initial improvement followed by deterioration. This pattern is further confirmed by the average performance changes across all models (Supplementary Fig. S2a). This indicates that the original feature set contained substantial redundant or noisy features. Our feature pruning strategy effectively removes noise and mitigated overfitting, thereby identifying an optimal feature subset and enhancing model generalization (Supplementary Fig. S2c).

We analyze the distribution of optimal F1 scores across different datasets (Fig. 3a). Results show that model performance on the internal test set (median = 0.673) is significantly lower than on the training set (median = 0.763, p = 0.001). Concurrently, model performance on the training set exhibits high dispersion (IQR: 0.687–0.997), indicating our feature selection strategy effectively prevents the selection of overfitted model configurations. Surprisingly, model performance on the external test set shows highly concentrated distribution (IQR: 0.93858–0.94214), with performance (median = 0.941) significantly superior to the internal validation set (p < 0.001) but not statistically different from the training set (p = 0.593). This atypical generalization pattern may relate to differences in data distribution between internal and external test sets.

Ultimately, we identify the optimal feature subset and feature importance evaluation method unique to each model (Fig. 3b and c and Appendix Table S6). Results reveal significant variations in algorithm sensitivity to feature redundancy, with the minimum number of features required to achieve peak F1 scores spanning a wide range (5 to 47) (Fig. 3b). Specifically, linear models exhibit diverse performance, tending to achieve optimal results with fewer features—e.g., LR under Spearman ranking strategy requires only 11 features for optimal F1 = 0.67; while more complex linear variants like Support Vector Classifier (Support Vector Classifier (SVC), Linear support vector classifier (LinearSVC)) and Ridge Classifier required up to 18–43 features to fully realize their potential, achieving F1 scores of 0.68. Models based on single decision trees, including standard Decision Trees Classifier (DT) and Extremely Randomized Trees (Extra Tree), exhibit high sensitivity to features, both achieving peak performance (F1 ≈ 0.67) with minimal subsets of just 5 features, where adding more features yields no further benefit. In contrast, ensemble models can accommodate more feature information. For instance, RF requires 47 features using VIF strategy to achieve F1 = 0.71. Among all combinations, the GB model with RFE strategy demonstrates exceptional efficiency and performance balance. Requiring only 20 features, it achieves the highest overall performance on the internal test set (F1 = 0.72, AUC = 0.77, ACC = 0.72) (Fig. 3c), proving that algorithmic feature selection effectively mitigates overfitting.

Phase II: cross-algorithm feature consensus extraction and optimal model

We integrate features appearing across the 16 optimal feature subsets from Phase I to construct a cross-algorithm feature frequency analysis, identifying globally most consistent features. Results reveal a right-skewed feature frequency distribution (range: 1–16; median: 5; IQR:3–8), indicating that the vast majority of features exhibit relatively limited importance, concentrated in the low-frequency region. Conversely, a small number of key features appear with extremely high frequency at the tail end of the distribution, demonstrating their irreplaceable core value. Among these, ICU LOS is repeatedly selected across all optimal models, exhibiting the highest cross-algorithm consistency. ALB, MDRD estimate, LODS score, SOFA score, platelet count (PLT), HR, vasopressor use, CRRT treatment, mechanical ventilation, and presence of peptic ulcer or cerebrovascular disease (11 indicators). Each indicators appears more than 10 times. These consistently and stably recur in the optimal configurations of different models, strongly indicating robust and stable associations with the prediction target (Fig. 3d). After comprehensive comparison of model performance under the consensus feature reduction strategy, the GB model incorporating 7 consensus features (LOS, Vasopressors, ALB, MDRD, LODS, SOFA, CRRT) is identified as the cross-algorithm optimal model (Fig. 3e). Confusion matrix analysis on the external test set revealed that TP + TN (representing “total correct predictions”) exhibit a distinct hump-shaped fluctuation pattern: initially declining, then rising, before falling again. False positives (FP) are initially high but decreased significantly as features are reduced, indicating a reduction in the model’s false alarm rate. At seven features, FP is controlled at a low level while TP + TN reached its peak (Fig. 3f). It achieves the highest overall predictive performance on the internal test set (F1 = 0.71, AUC = 0.75) and maintains excellent generalization capability on the external test set (F1 = 0.92, AUC = 0.74, ACC = 0.92) (Fig. 3g).

This two-stage outcome confirms that by progressively converging from “algorithm-specific optimality” to “cross-algorithm consensus optimality,” we not only significantly reduce feature dimensions but also identify key clinical predictors with cross-model robustness. This ultimately constructs an optimal model with superior performance, streamlined structure, and clinical deployability.

Nomogram for clinical guidance based on 7 consensus features

To enable direct translation of predictive features into clinical decision-making, this study constructs an individualized risk nomogram based on 7 key consensus features (CRRT, LODS, SOFA, Vasopressors, LOS, MDRD, ALB) identified through cross-algorithm consensus screening. This tool quantifies the risk of DIC in SIC patients using a logistic regression model. Among the features, CRRT, Vasopressors, ALB, and SOFA score contribute most significantly to the overall risk score, while LOS and MDRD contribute moderately, and LODS has the smallest impact (Fig. 4a). The nomogram visually displays the specific contribution of each feature value through a scoring bar. By accumulating the scores of all items, the patient’s individualized risk probability can be directly obtained, enabling a smooth transition from ML models to an interpretable clinical tool.

Permutation feature importance analysis

To further validate the importance of the seven high-frequency features identified for model prediction, we employ Permutation Feature Importance analysis. Results indicate that replacing any consensus feature in the optimal GB model significantly degrades model performance, confirming their critical role in prediction. Feature importance for model prediction, ranked from highest to lowest, is: CRRT, LODS, SOFA score, vasopressors, LOS, MDRD, and ALB (Fig. 4b). These exhibit strong clinical interpretability. Top-ranked features (e.g., CRRT, SOFA score) directly correlate with more critical clinical states, aligning with established critical care medicine principles and providing a reasonable explanation for the model’s stability and generalization.

Model performance evaluation

To comprehensively evaluate the overall performance and clinical applicability of constructed nomogram model, our study systematically validates its three core dimensions: discriminatory power, calibration, and clinical utility (Fig. 4c and e). Regarding discriminatory power, the nomogram achieves an AUC of 0.71 on the internal test set, maintaining high consistency with the overall performance of the optimal model and demonstrating robust classification capability (Fig. 4c). Given the imbalance in patient proportions between the two groups, we evaluate the prediction consistency of the nomogram using calibration curves. The overall calibration curve closely approximates the ideal diagonal line, suggesting that the model’s predicted probabilities align well with actual occurrence rates without significant systematic bias (Fig. 4d). Regarding potential clinical utility, the DCA demonstrates that within the risk threshold range of 0.18–0.72, this decision curve consistently yielded greater net benefit than both “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies (Fig. 4e). This implies that in real-world clinical decision-making, the tool can effectively reduce unnecessary interventions while more accurately identifying genuinely high-risk patients, providing reliable evidence for early intervention in critical care management.

SHAP interpretability analysis

To further understand the model’s decision-making basis, we systematically evaluate the mechanisms of the seven consensus features using SHAP method.

Global SHAP analysis reveals the direction and magnitude of each feature’s contribution to the prediction outcome, with features ranked top-down based on their average absolute impact on model output (i.e., SHAP values) (Fig. 4f). Longer ICU LOS, more severe organ dysfunction (higher SOFA and LODS scores), vasopressor use, and CRRT treatment are all positively correlated with SHAP values, indicating they are risk factors promoting progression to DIC. Conversely, higher albumin levels and more favorable MDRD-estimated renal function show negative contributions, exerting a protective effect. This reveals that the model’s decision logic is primarily driven by two characteristics: disease severity and treatment intervention intensity.

To enhance healthcare providers’ trust and acceptance of the model in specific clinical scenarios, we further analyze the DIC risk prediction process for each patient using SHAP waterfall plots. This provides a clear interpretative pathway for each patient’s high-risk determination. As illustrated (using high-risk patient case = 1 as an example), the model’s final predicted value (f(x) = 1.381) is derived by summing the baseline value (E[f(x)] at the bottom of the figure) with the contribution values of each feature (Fig. 4g). In this high-risk prediction case, LOS (+ 1.22) and MDRD (+ 0.59) are the primary positive drivers elevating the patient’s DIC risk, while SOFA, LODS, and Vasopressors also contribute positive driving forces (+ 0.16, + 0.1, + 0.08). Conversely, relatively normal albumin levels (ALB = 3.06, -0.35) and absence of CRRT treatment (-0.05) exerts negative protective effects. This visual interpretability mechanism decomposes model outputs into clinically meaningful combinations of feature contributions, enhancing model transparency and credibility in real-world clinical settings (Fig. 4g).

Discussion and conclusion

DIC represents the terminal stage of uncompensated coagulation dysfunction in sepsis [34]. SIC is its precursor stage, which exhibits a degree of reversibility and is considered a critical intervention window for DIC [35]. However, the transition from SIC to DIC involves complex pathological network regulation, coupled with significant clinical diagnostic delays, making early DIC identification extremely challenging. ML technologies, with their robust capabilities for multidimensional feature integration and capturing nonlinear risk patterns, offer new opportunities for early prediction of sepsis-related DIC [36–39]. Nevertheless, existing ML models predominantly target broad sepsis populations, lacking specificity for the high-risk SIC cohort. This results in insufficient predictive accuracy, hindering precise decision-making for anticoagulation strategies [27, 40–42]. Therefore, to address this research gap, this study aims to develop and validate a ML model specifically tailored for SIC patients, with the goal of achieving early identification of DIC risk and enabling stratified interventions.

The transition from SIC to DIC involves the interaction of multiple pathways, including inflammatory responses, endothelial injury, activation of the coagulation system, and inhibition of the fibrinolytic system. To comprehensively capture the complexity of the disease, we incorporate 61 clinical variables covering multidimensional pathophysiological states. However, high-dimensional data readily introduces redundant information, increasing the risk of model overfitting [43, 44]. This is evidenced by models like Extremely Randomized Trees Classifier (ExtraTrees) and DT achieving F1 = 1.0 on the training set but showing significant decline on the internal test set. To address this issue, we design a systematic two-stage feature selection process: The first stage employs 11 feature importance ranking methods combined with 16 mainstream ML algorithms to progressively reduce the number of features, constructing a series of feature subsets from “61→3” to identify the optimal subset. The second stage performs consensus feature frequency statistics across the 16 optimal subsets, selecting features with the highest cross-algorithm consistency to build a streamlined model. This strategy demonstrates significant advantages in enhancing model generalization capabilities and improving interpretability.

Feature screening in Phase I reveals the prevalence of algorithm dependency and feature redundancy. During feature reduction, nearly all models exhibit a “first-rise-then-decline” performance trend. This indicates substantial low-information entropy in the original feature set; moderate pruning enhances information density among features and mitigates overfitting, but excessive reduction may discard critical clinical clues. Notably, ensemble models (e.g., Random Forest, Gradient Boosting) exhibit greater robustness during feature reduction, suggesting their superior ability to uncover nonlinear relationships from a smaller set of core features, making them well-suited for complex clinical scenarios. Simultaneously, we observe significant variation in the optimal number of features across models (ranging from 5 to 47), highlighting the necessity and foresight of designing multi-algorithm comparisons to seek cross-algorithm consensus. Among these, the GB model incorporating 20 features selects via RFE achieved the best performance in internal testing (F1 = 0.72, AUC = 0.77) and maintains stability in external validation. Furthermore, the results show that almost all models performed significantly better on the external test set than on the internal test set, exhibiting an “atypical” generalization pattern. This may be related to systematic differences in disease severity and the distribution of key predictors between the two datasets. The extremely low event rate in the external dataset poses a significant challenge for the model’s generalization, particularly in achieving a high recall rate, a common problem in validation with heterogeneous data. We recognize that relying solely on traditional classification metrics may not fully assess the model’s value in a real-world clinical setting, which lays the groundwork for our subsequent evaluation of its clinical utility and decision-support capabilities. The results demonstrate that our model has learned disease patterns that are generalizable across populations, rather than statistical noise specific to one dataset. Its strength lies not in “catching all” cases, but in issuing high-fidelity high-risk alerts that can provide clinicians with the most valuable decision-making reference.

The second-stage consensus feature analysis identifies a robust “final feature solution” with high consensus across different algorithms. Based on feature frequency statistics within the optimal feature subsets of 16 ML algorithms, low-frequency features are progressively eliminated. Ultimately, guided by Occam’s Razor, a GB model incorporating seven features (LOS, vasopressors, ALB, MDRD, LODS, SOFA, and CRRT) is identified as the optimal model. This model achieves performance comparable to complex models on the internal test set (F1 = 0.71, AUC = 0.75) and demonstrates exceptional generalization capability in external validation (F1 = 0.92, AUC = 0.74), indicating these seven features are core biomarkers driving the transition from SIC to DIC with cross-dataset stability.

To advance the model from theory to clinical utility, we construct a visual column chart based on these seven key features, enabling personalized risk assessment without complex algorithms. These features derive from routine ICU monitoring or treatment information, imposing no additional testing burden and demonstrating strong clinical applicability. Although the calibration curve indicates mild overestimation in the high-risk range, the DCA demonstrates significant positive net benefit across a broad threshold range of 0.1–0.7. This suggests the model’s practical value in risk stratification far outweighs its absolute probability error, highlighting its substantial clinical utility. Furthermore, to address the “black box”issue in ML, we employs SHAP and PI analysis to dissect the model’s decision-making mechanism from global to individual levels. Results indicate its predictive mechanism aligns closely with the core pathophysiological pathways of DIC, where disease duration and cumulative damage to organ function serve as the primary drivers of SIC progression to DIC. First, ICU LOS is identified as the key predictor with the strongest cross-algorithm consensus. Studies have shown that sepsis patients who develop coagulopathy have a significantly longer ICU stay than those with normal coagulation function. A longer ICU stay signifies a prolonged and unresolved illness, during which patients are continuously exposed to various stressors (e.g., hospital-acquired infections, invasive procedures). This not only increases the risk of hospital-acquired infections and delirium but is also associated with a higher risk of in-hospital mortality, imposing a heavy economic burden on patients’ families and the healthcare system. Renal impairment (low MDRD values and CRRT use), hypoalbuminemia, and elevated organ failure scores (SOFA and LODS) serve as key drivers, mapping the core mechanisms of DIC: systemic inflammatory response, endothelial injury, and coagulation activation ultimately lead to widespread microthrombosis throughout the body, causing multi-organ dysfunction [45–48]. As the central organ maintaining balance in the coagulation-inflammation network, impaired renal function directly leads to accumulation of procoagulants and impaired clearance of inflammatory mediators [49]. Hypoalbuminemia serves as a direct marker of severe inflammation and capillary leakage, reflecting extensive damage to the vascular endothelium—a critical platform for coagulation activation [50, 51]. The use of vasopressors reflects the severity of circulatory failure, aligning with the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines emphasizing hemodynamic stability as the core management principle [8, 52]. The above analysis systematically validates our model as a clinically coherent reasoning engine. By quantifying pathophysiological damage across multiple dimensions, it provides transparent and traceable explanations for each prediction outcome, laying a solid foundation for clinical application and trust in the model.

Translating the model’s risk score into concrete clinical decision guidance is crucial for realizing its applied value. The nomogram provides each patient with a specific, quantitative probability of developing DIC. The DCA further supports risk stratification for DIC, where the risk threshold points on the net benefit curve serve as action thresholds for initiating clinical interventions. Based on existing clinical management guidelines and expert consensuses for SIC, we can adopt different response strategies for SIC patients at varying risk levels. For low-risk DIC patients, we recommend continuing with standard sepsis treatment and routine monitoring in the hospital to avoid over-intervention, which aligns with the fundamental principles of current sepsis management. For moderate-to-high-risk patients, the coagulation function of SIC patients should be dynamically assessed using a full range of laboratory coagulation indicators (Recommendation strength: II, evidence level: C). In addition to routine laboratory tests, viscoelastic tests such as thromboelastography (TEG) or rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) are recommended when available to provide a real-time, comprehensive picture of the patient’s coagulation status, distinguishing between hypercoagulable and consumptive/hypocoagulable states to guide subsequent treatment. The efficacy of anticoagulant therapy in sepsis-associated DIC remains controversial, but our model offers a new approach for precisely selecting patient populations who may benefit. Czempik et al. discussed the potential for maximum benefit from anticoagulant drugs in the early stages of DIC. The latest 2024 Chinese expert consensus recommends proactively initiating anticoagulant therapy for high-risk DIC patients (Recommendation strength: II, evidence level: C). Furthermore, the patient’s bleeding risk must be carefully assessed before selecting an anticoagulant. According to international sepsis guidelines, for anemia, red blood cell transfusion is recommended when hemoglobin concentration is < 7.0 g/dL (strong recommendation); the use of fresh frozen plasma to correct coagulation abnormalities is not recommended in the absence of active bleeding or planned invasive procedures (weak recommendation). Ultimately, by aiding clinical decision-making, we aim for the model to guide a tiered response strategy, thereby facilitating the rational allocation of medical resources.

The highlights of this study can be summarized in four points. Firstly, this study focuses on patients with SIC, a high-risk group for DIC, thereby filling a previous gap in this field. Secondly, our proposed two-stage feature selection framework establishes a replicable paradigm for building “lightweight, robust, and interpretable” AI models in complex clinical settings. Rigorous external validation across regions and multi-source data confirms its robustness and generalization capabilities in addressing real-world clinical heterogeneity. Beyond its predictive performance, the clinical value of this model lies in its potential application in ICU management and decision support. In practical ICU settings, such risk stratification could assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients earlier, prompting closer monitoring of coagulation function, timely reassessment of organ dysfunction, and more cautious management of invasive procedures or anticoagulation strategies. This approach can facilitate more individualized treatment planning and optimized allocation of critical care resources.

Nevertheless, this study still has several limitations: the retrospective data may introduce selection bias; The low incidence rate of DIC (approximately 3.9%) in the external validation cohort may impact the positive predictive value, suggesting the model is better suited as a “risk stratification tool” rather than an absolute probability predictor when deployed across different regions. Furthermore, a limitation shared by most ML models in current clinical applications is the static processing of clinical data. We extract the “worst values” within 48 h of ICU admission as a feature snapshot. While this reflects the patient’s most critical physiological state during the early phase, the progression from SIC to DIC is a highly dynamic pathophysiological process. This static modeling approach limits our ability to delve deeper into the patterns of disease progression and capture earlier warning signals. Therefore, future research urgently requires large-scale, prospective, multi-center studies to validate its external validity. The research direction should focus on shifting the paradigm from “static risk assessment” to “dynamic real-time early warning.” We recommend that subsequent work should further integrate multi-timepoint sequential data (time-series data) and utilize advanced machine learning models capable of handling temporal dependencies, such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) or their variant, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. Through these models, we aim to capture the complex nonlinear dynamics of the disease across the temporal dimension, thereby improving prediction accuracy and achieving early, continuous warning for DIC risk. This represents not only an enhancement in model performance but also a crucial step towards achieving genuine clinical foreseeability [53–55].

In summary, our study successfully constructs and validates a streamlined predictive model using only seven routine clinical indicators, providing a reliable quantitative tool for DIC risk stratification in SIC patients. Its broader significance lies in systematically demonstrating how to achieve a delicate balance between model performance, feature stability, clinical applicability, and decision transparency, offering a crucial practical example for developing clinically trustworthy AI decision support systems for critical care.
