Unraveling the roles of network and tunnels in the conductivity of carbon nanofiber composites
Authors: Yasser Zare, Muhammad Naqvi, Kyong Yop Rhee, Soo-Jin Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerein, an advanced conductivity model for polymer-carbon nanofiber (CNF) samples is introduced, stated as PCNFs. This model considers the length (l), radius (R) and amount of CNFs, interphase depth, percolation onset (PT), waviness, network portion, and tunneling length (d), by reasonable and meaningful equations. The proposed model is verified through the measured conductivity of samples and by studying the features’ influences on the PCNF conductivity. The model calculations display respectable fitting with the experimented facts from numerous CNF samples. Additionally, all factors sensibly affect the conductivity of PCNFs. Longer and thinner CNFs, higher CNF amount, thicker interphase, shorter tunnels, and lower percolation onset lead to higher conductivity in PCNF. R > 50 nm and l < 15 μm produce an insulative composite, but the top conductivity of 0.21 S/m is displayed at R = 10 nm and l = 25 μm. Accordingly, narrower and bigger nanofibers can improve the conductivity. Furthermore, an insulative material is produced by PT = 0.04 nm and d > 5 nm, nevertheless the conductivity maximizes to 0.25 S/m at the least values of PT = 0.01 nm and d = 2 nm. Introduction

Carbon nanofiber (CNF) is auspicious filler in the development of multifunctional composites, as it has a high aspect ratio and good electrical resistivity1–4. CNFs can increase the stiffness and thermal features, besides the electric conductivity of composites, by actually low amounts5–7. Both carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and CNFs show the near sameness of microstructure, CNTs are extra inclined by van der Waals bonds causing the dispersion for a shorter time8–10. However, restricted studies have focused on the description of electrical properties in PCNFs. This inspires the current study, justifying the deep examination of the CNF in the nanocomposite conductivity.

The charge can be conveyed among the nanoparticles, and the contact zones among the nanofibers influences the electron tunneling11–15. A short tunneling distance among neighboring nanofibers can confidently increase the conductivity of composites. The tunneling phenomenon among neighboring particles was reported in various composites16–19. Figure 1 displays a schematic for tunneling region among CNFs in nanocomposites. The tunneling zone has been considered in many reports, which affects the conductivity of CNF/polymer system (PCNF)20–22. However, tunneling studies in PCNF are limited in the literature.

A schematic of tunneling region in PCNFs.

A distinguished alteration in the conductivity occurs at the percolation threshold (PT)23–27. The large aspect ratio of CNFs leads to the production of a network in the PCNF, which influences the conductivity. In fact, the nanocomposite conductivity relies on the conductive net that forms at a determinate concentration known as the PT28,29. PT is chiefly based on the size and shape of CNF30,31. Growing the aspect ratio of the particles causes a lower PT32,33. The conductive nets can be made more easily in PCNFs with big aspect ratios, which enhance the charge transfer.

The interphase among nanofiller and polymer can govern the PCNF properties. The impressions of interphase on the nanocomposite performance were described34–38. It was reported that the interphase can modify the PT, the conductivity, and stiffness of nanocomposites39,40. However, there is no model to reflect the stimuli of the tunnels and interphase zones on the PCNF conductivity, even though these terms can help to achieve a higher conductivity and smaller PT.

This article introduces an advanced model for predicting the electrical conductivity of PCNF that incorporates several advanced aspects previously unaddressed in the literature. The model uniquely integrates factors such as interphase depth, tunneling length, and the wettability between the polymer and carbon nanofibers, offering a comprehensive analysis of how these elements influence conductivity. By factoring in the role of tunneling effect and PT, the model provides deeper insights into the nano-scale interactions within PCNFs, which are critical for optimizing material properties. This holistic approach not only fills a significant gap in existing conductivity models but also enhances the ability to design the efficient and high-performance nanocomposites tailored to specific applications such as biosensors41,42.

Thin nanofibers enhance the interphase area between CNFs and the polymer matrix according to Eq. 8, which proves their positive efficacy in the composites. Reedy nanofibers also generate a lower level of PT according to Eq. 5. Additionally, thin CNFs encourage a high effective filler fraction and a high percentage of networked CNFs based on Eqs. 9 and 10. Therefore, thinner CNFs positively handle the interphase volume fraction and network percentage, improving the nanocomposite conductivity. Nevertheless, thick CNFs cannot surge the conductivity since they cause the small interphase and poor networks, weakening the conductivity of PCNF. This explanation shows that the CNF radius truly affects the nanocomposite conductivity by the advanced model.

Better conductivity in PCNF is achieved with longer CNFs, while short CNFs cause poor conductivity. Larger CNFs produce a lower PT (Eq. 5). Moreover, long CNFs rise the aspect ratio, which yields a high operative conductivity59. Additionally, the net fraction of CNFs is enhanced by a lower PT. Accordingly, nanocomposites containing long CNFs can form large networks with interphase zones, which significantly transfer electrons. Conversely, the small networks created by short CNFs cause low effective charge moving. Consequently, the proposed model truly correlates the PCNF conductivity to CNF length.

The nanocomposite shows better conductivity at higher CNF concentration, and there is a direct relation between the nanocomposite conductivity and CNF concentration. This trend demonstrates the appropriate competences of high CNF volume on the sample conductivity. A higher sum of CNF can progress the conductivity since the CNF has a conductivity of 10,000 S/m, extremely bigger than the polymer conduction60. In fact, CNF is the conducting component in the nanocomposites, and thus its amount handles the conductivity of PCNF. Though, a small CNF volume share cannot manage the conductivity since it may not yield the CNF net for electron transferring. The experimental data in Fig. 2 have confirmed the direct relation of conduction to fiber amount. Accordingly, the CNF sum directly regulates the conductivity supporting the model.

The optimistic roles of a denser interphase in the PT and the net percentage are clearly shown in Sect. 2. A dense interphase can form and swell the filler net by reducing the space between CNFs. So, a thicker interphase produces a larger network, easing the electron transfer. Contrariwise, a skinnier interphase generates a reduced network, limiting the charge transfer. This means the confident efficiency of a dense interphase in the PCNF conductivity.

A low PT indicates that the CNFs are proficient to form the large networks. Additionally, a lower PT produces larger networks (Eq. 10), facilitating electron transfer. As a result, a lower PT has a positive role in the PCNF conductivity. However, a high PT causes small networks in the PCNFs, limiting the charge transfer and electrical conductivity. So, the PT inversely handles the PCNF conductivity, confirming the outputs.

The charge transference in the PCNFs relates to the space among the nearby nanofibers because largely-detached nanofibers cannot conduct the charge transfer. Therefore, larger tunneling gaps result in poor conductivity. Indeed, the separation length between CNFs influences the tunneling effect because the tunneling distance is an important factor in tunneling resistance40,47. Thus, the significant role of tunneling length in the conductivity is properly recognized by the developed model.

The surface tensions of polymer and particles control the wettability, governing the dispersion and agglomeration of nanofiller in the sample. Great wettability eases the neighboring of CNFs by the polymer, limiting the nanoparticle contacts43. In contrast, lower wettability encourages direct contacts between nanoparticles, promoting their agglomeration in the samples. Since the contacts of nanoparticles and filler aggregation positively handle the conductivity of PCNFs, low wettability is beneficial in creating networks of CNFs. cos(θ) in Eq. 3 shows the wettability, and a higher γp reduces the wettability. Thus, a higher γp limits the CNF dispersion and expands the network, which improves the PCNF conductivity. Instead, a high γf increases the wetting process, which enhances the filler circulation and limits the CNF network. According to these results, Eq. 13 appropriately associates the conductivity of PCNF with the γp and γf.

Conclusions

An advanced model was advised for PCNF electrical conduction by the interphase part, tunnels, and wettability parameter. The planned model effectively predicted the conductivity of numerous PCNFs. The PCNF conductivity was rationally motivated by all factors. A thinner and larger CNF, higher CNF concentration, thicker interphase, shorter tunnels, lower PT, higher γp and lower γf resulted in higher conductivity for PCNFs. R > 50 nm and l < 15 μm produce the lowest conductivity, but the supreme result of 0.21 S/m is shown at R = 10 nm and l = 25 μm. Consequently, narrower and bigger nanofibers improve the conductivity. An insulative material is detected at ϕf < 0.008, whereas the maximum conduction of 0.43 S/m is realized by ϕf = 0.04 and t = 30 nm. So, high fiber amount and a denser interphase yield higher conductivity. An insulative material is produced by PT = 0.04 nm and d > 5 nm, nevertheless the conductivity maximizes to 0.25 S/m at the least PT = 0.01 nm and d = 2 nm. The lowest PT and narrowest tunnels yield super-conductive. The lowest conductivity as 0.034 is publicized at γp = 30 mN/m and γf = 60 mN/m, though the extreme result of 0.08 S/m is recognized at γp = 55 mN/m and γf = 35 mN/m. Hence, a higher γp and a lower γf improve the conductivity. The results exposed that the CNF content and size of interphase significantly impact the conductivity, ranging from 0 to 0.43 S/m.
