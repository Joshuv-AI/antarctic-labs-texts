The influence of socioeconomic disparities on the association of physical behavior with incident type 2 diabetes
Authors: Puchen Zhou, Xin Geng, Chengjing Zhang, Tongtong Li, Fengchang Li, Yaying Cao, Dou Mao, Yunan Liu, Nan Wang, Kaixuan Li, Zilin Xiao, Xiliang Shang, Chengwu Feng, Geng Zong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate the influence of socioeconomic status (SES) on the associations of moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA), light intensity physical activity (LIPA), sedentary behavior, and sleep duration with incident type 2 diabetes (T2D).MethodsThe analysis included 69,461 participants from the UK Biobank who completed an accelerometer-based physical activity measurement. Individual-level SES was assessed via latent class analysis of household income, employment status, and education qualifications. The area-level SES was determined through the Townsend Deprivation Index. The additive hazard model and Cox proportional hazards model were employed to estimate the risk of T2D.ResultsAfter a median follow-up of 6.7 years, 1,252 cases of incident T2D were documented. The associations between physical behaviors and T2D risk differed significantly by SES. Compared with individuals with higher SES, those with lower SES presented greater increases in T2D risk associated with low MVPA (68 vs. 31 cases per 10,000 person-years; p for interaction = 0.030), high sedentary behavior (24 vs. 1 cases per 10,000 person-years; p for interaction < 0.001), and short sleep duration (23 vs. 5 cases per 10,000 person-years; p for interaction = 0.003). Area-level SES also modified the relationship between low MVPA and T2D risk on both additive and multiplicative scales (p for interaction < 0.001), with stronger effects observed in socioeconomically disadvantaged groups.ConclusionsAddressing insufficient MVPA, prolonged sedentary behavior, and inadequate sleep among individuals with lower SES could significantly reduce the number of T2D cases, highlighting the importance of targeted public health interventions to mitigate disparities in T2D incidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24359-8.

Background

Diabetes prevention remains a major public health challenge, especially among socioeconomically deprived populations [1, 2]. Socioeconomic inequality is a known determinant of health-related behaviors and has been shown to modify the impacts of overall lifestyle scores on long-term health outcomes [3, 4]. While prior research highlights similar patterns for individual factors, including alcohol consumption, smoking, and physical activity [5, 6], studies examining the potential influence of socioeconomic status (SES) on the relationships between physical behaviors and type 2 diabetes (T2D) risk are still lacking.

Physical behaviors, including insufficient moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA) [7], prolonged sedentary behavior [6, 8], and short sleep duration [9], are consistently associated with an elevated risk of T2D. However, SESs are linked to both differential exposure to and vulnerability to these behaviors [10]. For example, individuals with lower SES are more likely to have reduced MVPA, greater sedentary behavior, and poorer sleep patterns, while these findings may vary by demographic factors and behavior domains [11–15]. Moreover, findings on how SES modifies the associations between physical behaviors and long-term health outcomes are limited and inconsistent [6, 16, 17]. A key limitation of previous research lies in the reliance on self-report questionnaires to measure physical behaviors, which are prone to greater measurement errors, especially among lower SES groups [18]. Additionally, previous studies have used single SES variables, such as household income, education, or occupational position, which capture only a narrow aspect of social inequalities [19]. More recent studies have adopted a composite individual SES indicator and further included neighborhood SES for comprehensive assessments [3, 6, 20], with independent and differential impacts of these SES factors on physical activities and mortality associations reported [6].

In this study, we use data from a large cohort with accelerometer-based physical activity measurements to explore how individual-level and area-level SES modify the associations between physical behaviors—specifically MVPA, light intensity activity (LIPA), sedentary behavior, and sleep duration—and T2D. By calculating incident risk differences (IRDs) and additive interactions, we aim to provide actionable public health insights, identify how many T2D cases could be prevented through improvements in physical behaviors, and determine which subgroups would benefit most from targeted interventions [21].

Methods

Study design and population

The current study was based on data from the UK Biobank, which recruited approximately 500,000 participants between 2006 and 2010. A subsample of 103,695 individuals responded to an e-mail invitation to wear a wrist-worn accelerometer between 2013 and 2015 [22]. The average time difference between the baseline survey and accelerometer measurements was 5.7 (Standard deviation [SD]: 1.1, range: 2.8–8.7) years. After excluding participants without valid accelerometer data (< 72 h of wear or no wear data for every hour of the 24-hour cycle), those with incomplete SES or covariate data, and those with diabetes before accelerometer measurements, 69,461 participants were included in the analysis. (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Assessment of physical behaviors

The participants wore an Axivity AX3 accelerometer (Newcastle upon Tyne, UK) on their dominant hand for a period of 7 days to capture data on physical activity [22].

The raw accelerometer data were processed by the R package GGIR (v 2.7.1). Acceleration signals were calibrated to local gravity to ensure consistency across devices. The original data were combined into 5-second epochs, which represented the average vector magnitude. Nonwear time was identified as consecutive time episodes of 60 min or more with a 3-axis acceleration standard deviation < 13.0 mg and was imputed using the averages from similar time-of-day data [23, 24]. MVPA was defined as the sum of bouts ≥ 5 min with acceleration ≥ 100 mg to avoid misclassifying the artifact as MVPA. LIPA was defined as the sum of 5-second epochs with acceleration between 40 and 100 mg. Sedentary behavior was defined as the sum of 5-second epochs with a mean acceleration < 40 mg [25]. The GGIR HDCZA algorithm was used to detect the time window between sleep onset and waking after the last sleep episode [26]. The GGIR sleep detection algorithm was then used to identify sleep episodes within this window [27]. MVPA, LIPA and sedentary behavior were each divided into tertiles—low (< 15.13, < 238.14, < 571.57 min/day, respectively), medium (15.13–36.40, 238.14–286.58, 571.57–654.82 min/day) and high (> 36.40, >286.58, >654.82 min/day). Sleep duration was divided into short (< 6 h per day), normal (6–8 h per day), and long (> 8 h per day) groups [28].

Assessment of outcome

Prevalent T2D was identified from two sources: self-reported data collected at baseline and hospital inpatient records obtained before accelerometer wear. Incident T2D was derived from hospital inpatient data linked to UK Biobank after the accelerometer measurement period [29], with data available up to 30th September 2021 in England, 31st July 2021 in Scotland, and 28th February 2018 in Wales. T2D cases were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, E11, which included primary and secondary diagnoses.

Definition of socioeconomic status

Supplemental Text S1 provides detailed information on the SES. Briefly, individual-level SES status was calculated using average total household income, education qualifications, and employment status by latent class analysis using R package poLCA (v 1.6.0.1) with maximum times of iterations of 104 and a tolerance value for judging convergence of 10−6 [30]. For employment and education status which may have multiple responses, we assigned the highest level of response for each of these variables in the main analysis. We used the model with three classes because the model with four classes did not converge. The participants were classified into three groups: high (college or university degrees, household income ≥£52,000), medium, and low (unemployed, household income <£18,000; Supplemental Table 1).

The area-level SES was defined using the Townsend deprivation index (TDI) based on the participant’s residential postcode. The TDI was censused in 2011, which included the most recent data and was close to the accelerometer measurements. A higher TDI indicates a more deprived area. The participants were categorized into three groups (high, medium, or low) according to the tertiles of TDI.

Definition of covariates

All covariates were collected during the baseline survey from 2006 to 2010, about 5.7 (SD: 1.1, range: 2.8–8.7) years before accelerometer measurements. Age, race, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, food intake, family history of T2D, and overall health rating were self-reported at the baseline survey [31]. The healthy dietary score was derived using the following factors: fruit intake ≥ 3 servings/day, vegetable intake ≥ 3 servings/day, fish intake ≥ 2 servings/week, red meat ≤ 2 servings/week, and processed meat intake ≤ 1 serving/week [32, 33]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using height and weight obtained from the study visit preceding wearing an accelerometer.

Statistical analysis

Participants’ characteristics according to SES were described using means and SDs for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables.

To examine whether SES levels were correlated with physical behaviors, we calculated Spearman’s partial correlation between SES and physical behaviors. Models were adjusted for age at wearing an accelerometer (continuous), sex (male/female), the season in which the accelerometer was worn (spring/summer/autumn/winter), and area-level SES (continuous) for individual-level SES (low/medium/high) and vice versa.

Additive hazard models were used to estimate the IRD of MVPA, LIPA, sedentary behavior, and sleep duration with T2D [34]. The age at which the accelerometer was worn was used as the time scale. The main model was adjusted for sex (male/female), race (White/others), season in which the accelerometer was worn (spring/summer/autumn/winter), individual-level SES (low/medium/high), TDI (continuous), smoking status (current/past/never), alcohol consumption (current/past/never), family history of T2D (yes/no), overall health rating (excellent/good/fair/poor), and healthy diet score (continuous) where appropriate. Additional adjustments included sedentary behavior for the MVPA models and vice versa, and MVPA for the sedentary behavior and sleep models. Additive interactions were evaluated using product terms for exposures and SES variables [34]. In this study, we assessed multicollinearity among covariates using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and found no evidence of collinearity concerns (all VIFs < 5, the threshold for potential multicollinearity).

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for T2D, with similar adjustments. The proportional hazard assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals and no violation was found. Multiplicative-scale interactions between exposures and SESs were evaluated by comparing models with and without the product term of exposures and SESs via the likelihood ratio tests.

We also applied an isotemporal substitution model (ISM) to estimate the IRD associated with reallocating 30 min per day from one physical behavior to another. For example, when estimating the IRD for substituting 30 min/day of sedentary behavior with MVPA, LIPA, or sleep, sedentary time was omitted from the model; the coefficients for MVPA, LIPA and sleep therefore quantify the effect of replacing sedentary behavior with each respective activity. Additive interactions were evaluated as described above.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the results. First, we further adjusted for BMI based on the main model to evaluate whether the association was independent of obesity. Second, we censored the first 2-year cases to avoid possible reverse causation. Third, we replicated the analysis for individual-level SES using average total household income, education qualifications, and employment status, to test whether a single factor could drive possible interactions. Fourth, we redefined incident T2D cases using field IDs 130708 and 130709 to identify T2D cases from various sources including self-reports, primary care, hospital admission data, and death register. Sixty-six healthy participants were reclassified as incident cases, and 139 cases were reclassified as healthy because of different censor dates. Finally, we examined whether our results were affected by the potential misclassification of individual-level SES. We repeated latent class analyses after including numbers in households (recoded into 1, 2, 3, and ≥ 4) and using the lowest qualification and the unemployment option for those who selected multiple answers.

All analyses were performed using R statistical software. Two-sided p values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Spearman’s partial correlations showed no strong relationship between physical behaviors and SES, with coefficients ranging from − 0.07 (sedentary behavior/area-level SES) to 0.07 (sleep duration/area-level SES) (Supplemental Table 2).

Physical behaviors and incident T2D

During a median follow-up of 6.7 years (IQR, 6.3–7.3 years), 1,252 cases of incident T2D were documented. After adjusting for sex and accelerometer-wear season, low MVPA and LIPA, high sedentary behavior, and short sleep duration were all associated with a higher risk of T2D in additive hazard models and Cox proportional hazard models (Supplemental Tables 3–6). Further adjustment for race, SES, lifestyle factors, family history of T2D, and overall health rating did not alter the associations. In the main model, the IRD (95% CI) and HR (95% CI) for the low-MVPA group, comparing high MVPA group, were 42 (38, 45) per 10,000 person-years and 2.04 (1.72, 2.41); the IRD (95% CI) and HR (95% CI) for the low LIPA group, comparing with high LIPA group, were -2 (-5, 2) per 10,000 person-years and 0.85 (0.73, 0.98); the IRD (95% CI) and HR (95% CI) for the high-sedentary behavior group, comparing low sedentary behavior group, were 11 (6, 15) per 10,000 person-years 1.37 (1.14, 1.66); and the IRD (95% CI) and HR (95% CI) for the short sleep duration group, comparing with normal sleep duration group, were 15 (11, 19) per 10,000 person-years and 1.56 (1.39, 1.76).

Effect modification by SES

The associations of low MVPA, prolonged sedentary behavior, and short sleep duration with higher incident T2D risk were stronger in participants with lower individual-level SES, while null association between LIPA and T2D were observed across SES status (all p values for interactions < 0.05; Fig. 1; Tables 2 and 3, and Supplemental Table 7). The IRDs (95% CIs) for low MVPA compared with high MVPA were 68 (59, 77), 42 (38, 47), and 31 (25, 37) cases per 10,000 person-years from lower to higher individual-level SES. For sedentary behavior, significant associations were observed only among individuals with lower and medium individual-level SES. The IRDs (95% CIs) for high sedentary behavior were 24 (11, 37) and 12 (6, 17) cases per 10,000 person-years among individuals with lower and medium individual-level SES respectively. The IRDs (95% CIs) for short sleep duration compared with normal sleep duration were 23 (10, 35), 18 (12, 23), and 5 (-1, 11) cases per 10,000 person-years from lower to higher individual-level SES. We also observed a significant interaction between area-level SES and MVPA (p for interaction < 0.001). The IRDs (95% CIs) for low MVPA compared with high MVPA were 61 (54, 68), 45 (39, 50), and 33 (28, 38) cases per 10,000 person-years from lower to higher area-level SES. Supplemental Tables 8–11 showed the results of the multiplicative-scale interaction in the Cox proportional hazard models, and only MVPA and area-level SES showed multiplicative-scale interactions (p for interaction = 0.034).

Secondary analyses

Supplemental Tables 12–15 showed the results of ISM. Among participants with low individual-level SES, substituting 30 min/day of sedentary behavior for 30 min/day of other physical activities were associated with lower T2D risks (IRDs: -9 to -4 cases/10,000 person years) compared to their counterparts (-4 to 0 cases/10,000 person years; p for interactions ≤ 0.005). Moreover, among those with lower area-level SES, substituting 30 min/day of other physical behaviors for 30 min/day MVPA also associated with lower T2D risk (-9 to -5 cases/10,000 person years) compared to those with high area-level SES (-3 to 0 cases/10,000 person years).

Supplemental Tables 16–19 showed the results of sensitivity analyses. Overall, the results were independent of further adjustment of BMI (Supplemental Tables 16 and 17) but slightly attenuated after eliminating the first 2-year cases (Supplemental Tables 18 and 19). Education qualifications and household income significantly interacted with all 3 physical behaviors (Supplemental Tables 20 and 21), whereas employment status only interacted with sedentary behavior (Supplemental Table 22). The results remained largely consistent when T2D cases were identified from self-reports, primary care, hospital admission data, and death registers (Supplemental Tables 23 and 24). The results were robust when further including the number of people in households in the definition of SES (Supplemental Table 25), or when redefining SES with the lowest qualification and the unemployment option (Supplemental Table 26).

Discussion

In the current study, insufficient MVPA, extended sedentary behavior, and short sleep duration measured by accelerometers were associated with greater differences in the risk of T2D among participants with lower SES than among their counterparts. These interactions were more pronounced for individual-level SES than for area-level SES, underscoring the role of social inequalities in health outcomes.

To our knowledge, this is among the first studies reporting that SES factors may modify the associations of MVPA with T2D. Our data align with a previous study in the UK Biobank, which demonstrated that lower SES groups had a stronger association between MVPA and all-cause mortality [6]. Similarly, in the National Health Interview Survey, people with lower physical activity and lower education attainment had 31.9 (21.4, 42.3) to 34.1 (26.1, 42.1) more deaths per 10,000 person-years than those with sufficient physical activity (MVPA ≥ 150 minutes per week) and higher education levels [11]. The findings of the present study are also consistent with recent findings showing stronger associations between overall healthy behaviors (including MVPA and sedentary behavior) and T2D risk among those with lower social risk scores, which were defined as a combination of individual-level SES, neighborhood and living environments, and psychosocial stress (p for additive interaction = 0.030) [35]. These findings align with social vulnerability theory, which posits that individuals with lower SES are more affected by unhealthy lifestyle factors due to heightened stressors, limited health knowledge, and reduced access to healthcare [4]. Conversely, higher SES individuals are more likely to attend strenuous aerobic sports, which are more strongly inversely associated with T2D risk than other sports [12, 36]. Meanwhile, we did not find significant correlations between SES factors and MVPA duration. However, the differential exposure theory cannot be rejected as SES may affect the pattern, quality, and other aspects of MVPA that cannot be obtained using accelerometers. Collectively, these results suggest that promoting physical activity in lower SES populations could prevent more T2D cases, supporting targeted public health interventions.

Our study also demonstrated stronger associations of sedentary behavior itself and reallocating sedentary behavior to other behaviors with incident T2D in the lower individual-level SES group. This aligns with the Prospective Urban and Rural Epidemiological study, which found a stronger relationship between sitting time and all-cause mortality in lower-income countries [37]. Nevertheless, stronger associations of screen time with mortality were observed in the high-education group of the Multiethnic Cohort Study [17] and higher individual-level SES in a British population [6]. These discrepancies could be partly attributed to the inaccuracy of self-reported sedentary behavior caused by differential recall bias related to SES factors, as well as the choices of sedentary behaviors and SES indicators in specific studies [38, 39]. Previous studies have reported a stronger association between watching TV and T2D than sedentary behaviors in other domains, suggesting that the type of sedentary behavior can impact health outcomes [8, 40]. Lower SES individuals may rely on watching TV as a form of stress-coping [12], but its long-term health implications might exacerbate stress [41]. The null association between sedentary behavior and T2D in higher individual SES groups could be attributed to the confounding of other healthy behaviors in this group, the confounding of more dominating risk factors (such as MVPA) derived from accelerators, and lower incidence rates of disease (16 vs. 47 cases per 10,000 person-years in higher and lower SES groups), which collectively call for studies with larger sample sizes to detect the potential associations of a moderate-effect risk factor.

For the first time, we reported that individual-level SES modified the association between insufficient sleep and T2D, with stronger associations observed in lower SES groups. Similar trends have been documented in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey and the Health and Retirement Study, where individuals with lower SES and short sleep duration experienced worse mental and cognitive health outcomes [20, 42]. These health inequalities may be related to poor sleep quality, increased stress levels, and limited access to healthcare in individuals with lower SES. Studies utilizing accelerometers and polysomnography have shown that individuals with lower SES may experience longer sleep latency, increased wakefulness after sleep onset, and poorer sleep quality [14, 43, 44], all of which are associated with higher diabetes risk [45, 46]. These findings underscore the complex interplay between socioeconomic status, sleep, and T2D risk.

In our study, neither individual nor area SES factors were strongly correlated with MVPA, LIPA, sedentary behavior, or sleep duration. Furthermore, area-level SES only modified the associations of MVPA with T2D when individual-level SES was controlled for. One previous study in the UK Biobank reported no significant interaction effect between area-level SES and an overall lifestyle score including physical activity, TV watching, and sleep duration on incident CVD [4]. Another study found no interaction effect of MVPA or sedentary behavior with area-level SES on the risk of mortality or incident CVD [6]. Individual-level and area-level SES might influence physical behaviors and T2D risk in different pathways [12, 19]. Income and education might influence health outcomes and lifestyles through material resources, health-related knowledge, and literacy [12], whereas area-level SES might include community opportunities, social support, social cohesion, and peer influence deprivation [12]. It is also possible that the sensitivity of postcode-derived SES is low because of confounding by other environmental determinants of health and potential changes in participant residence [47].

Strengths and limitations

The key strengths of this study are the large sample size and prospective cohort design. However, some limitations need to be addressed. First, because of the observational design, we cannot establish causal inference, and residual confounding remains possible despite careful adjustment of covariates. Second, SES and some covariates were not measured when participants wore accelerometers, which may further attenuate the expected findings. Third, the identification of T2D in our study was based solely on hospital inpatient data, which may lead to significant underestimation of diabetes cases, as well as rate differences and the relative risk in our study. However, the results remained robust when T2D was identified using self-report, primary care, hospital admissions data, and death register. Fourth, although our definition of individual-level SES was widely used in previous studies [48, 49], misclassification by other SES indicators and the potential change of SES over time is still possible. According to our sensitivity analysis, results were unchanged when household size was further included as an indicator of poverty. A previous study reported that household income and educational qualifications remained stable between the baseline and additional visits in the UK Biobank [50]. Furthermore, employment status tended towards unemployment, which may slightly attenuate the expected findings, as employment is only a minor factor in the latent classification analysis of individual SES. Fifth, compared to thigh-wearing accelerometers, wrist-wearing accelerometers cannot distinguish different postures such as sitting, lying, and standing, therefore, sedentary behavior in this study may also include other stationary behaviors. Finally, the UK Biobank has a low response rate (5.5% of those invited), and participants in the current analysis were additionally selected. Future studies should validate these findings in cohorts with greater ethnic, cultural, and socioeconomic diversity, such as those from lower- and middle-income countries where SES disparities may exert even greater influence on health behaviors and outcomes. Additionally, incorporating immigrant populations and urban-rural comparisons could provide broader insights into global diabetes disparities.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we observed that low MVPA, long sedentary behavior, and short sleep duration were associated with higher incident T2D risk, and the findings were stronger among those with lower SES status. These findings highlight the need for tailored public health interventions targeting socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. Future research should evaluate the cost-effectiveness and scalability of these interventions to ensure they address health inequities.
