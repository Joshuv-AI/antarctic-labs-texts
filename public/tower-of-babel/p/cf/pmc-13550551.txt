Towards a clinically integrated artificial intelligence tool for triage of skin cancer
Authors: Andre G. C. Pacheco, Eduarda P. Magesk, Leonardo F. Moreira, Luis A. Souza, Rayane C. Martins, Brenda Comper, Pedro H. G. Bouzon, Rafael S. Altoe, Renan S. Vieira, Caio L. V. S. Silva, Renata S. Careta, Jose C. Frasson, Luciana Vieira, Tania R. P. Canuto, Patricia H. L. Frasson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skin cancer is one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, with early detection critical to improving outcomes. However, in low-resource settings such as rural regions, limited access to dermatologists and diagnostic tools delays diagnosis. This study presents a clinical validation of an artificial intelligence (AI)-based mobile application to assist generalist healthcare professionals in skin lesion triage. The tool classifies lesions into five priority levels according to malignancy risk, following a protocol developed by dermatologists. The AI model, based on a fine-tuned MobileNet-V3 architecture, was trained on the PAD-UFES-20+ dataset (13,569 images) and integrated into an offline-capable mobile app. Internal validation achieved a sensitivity of 0.79, specificity of 0.95, and AUC of 0.95. In clinical validation across two phases, 131 healthcare professionals from nine cities participated. In Phase 1, sensitivity and specificity improved from 0.64 and 0.90 (without AI) to 0.80 and 0.92 (with AI), matching the model’s standalone performance. In Phase 2, with 57 community health workers in rural areas, AI assistance increased triage effectiveness by 17% and reduced unnecessary referrals by 30%. Participant feedback was positive, highlighting increased safety and confidence. These results indicate that AI-assisted mobile triage tools can enhance diagnostic performance and healthcare efficiency in resource-limited environments.

Introduction

Skin cancer, including both melanoma and non-melanoma types, remains one of the most prevalent forms of dysplasia worldwide and represents a major public health concern1. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), more than 510,000 new cases of melanoma are expected by 2040, resulting in nearly 100,000 deaths2. While Australia and New Zealand report the highest overall incidence rates of skin cancer, Europe records the highest incidence and mortality rates specifically for melanoma3. In the United States, melanoma incidence has been rising steadily over the past three decades, having doubled between 1982 and 20114.

Early detection remains the most effective strategy for reducing skin cancer mortality5. However, diagnostic accuracy can vary considerably depending on the healthcare provider’s experience and expertise6,7. Dermatologists with extensive training typically achieve high diagnostic accuracy, whereas less experienced practitioners may have difficulty distinguishing between benign and malignant lesions. In emerging countries, early diagnosis is even more challenging due to limited resources—such as dermatoscopes—and lack of access to specialists. For example, in Brazil, skin cancer accounts for approximately 31.2% of all cancer cases8, and there are about 12,000 dermatologists serving a population of over 210 million people9. Also, the vast majority of these specialists are located in urban areas, which limits access to specialized care for populations in rural regions—where a significant number of skin cancer cases occur due to higher sun exposure10.

In this context, generalist healthcare professionals often serve as the first point of contact for patients with suspected skin cancer. However, their limited training in dermatology can lead to lower diagnostic accuracy11,12. As a result, many cases of skin cancer may go undetected or be diagnosed at more advanced stages, leading to treatments that, although potentially effective, are associated with greater morbidity for patients and increased costs to public health systems. As such, there is a need for accessible tools to support generalist healthcare professionals in the early detection and assessment of skin lesions, while ensuring the level of precision the task demands. Technological solutions, particularly those based on artificial intelligence (AI), have shown promise in assisting with image-based diagnosis, offering decision support that can enhance triage and improve diagnostic accuracy. These tools may be particularly valuable in remote areas and have the potential to improve patient prognosis through earlier intervention.

Significant progress has been made in the development of Artificial Intelligence (AI) algorithms for skin cancer detection, with many works demonstrating high diagnostic accuracy13–16. However, the clinical validation of these algorithms remains a critical step before they can be widely adopted in healthcare settings. Most of the proposed algorithms have been validated using retrospective datasets in controlled environments, which may not accurately reflect real-world clinical scenarios. For example, Jones et al.17 investigated 14,224 studies on AI for skin cancer detection and found that only two of them had used data from clinical settings for validation. Clinical validation involves testing the algorithm in real-world scenarios to ensure its reliability and effectiveness in diverse populations and settings. This process is essential to confirm that the AI tool can perform well across different demographics and clinical conditions, thereby ensuring its safety and efficacy for patient care.

In this work, we present a comprehensive clinical validation of an AI-based model designed to assist generalist healthcare professionals in the triage of skin lesions for early detection of skin cancer. The model was embedded in a user-friendly mobile application—that allows healthcare providers to capture images of skin lesions and receive immediate skin lesion triage support—and with the support of the Dermatological and Surgical Assistance Program of the Federal University of Espírito Santo (PAD-UFES)—which provides free monthly consultations for the diagnosis and treatment of skin cancer in rural areas—our system was tested in a real-world clinical setting in multiple clinical sites in Brazil. The primary objective was to evaluate the model’s diagnostic performance, including accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity in identifying malignant lesions compared to the assessments made by experienced dermatologists. Additionally, we aimed to assess the usability and acceptance of the mobile application among generalist healthcare professionals. The results of this study provide valuable insights into the potential of AI-assisted tools to enhance skin cancer detection in primary care settings, particularly in resource-limited environments. By validating the model in a clinical context, we aim to contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the integration of AI technologies in healthcare, ultimately helping to improve patient outcomes through earlier diagnosis and treatment of skin cancer. Results

In this section, we present the main results of this work. First, we report the internal validation of the AI model on the PAD-UFES-20+. Next, we present the results obtained in the two phases of the clinical validation study, including the performance of generalist healthcare professionals with and without the AI tool, as well as the usability feedback collected from the participants.

Internal validation of the AI model

As we may observe, the model presents a steady performance across all folds, indicating it is able to generalize to unseen validation data. For this phase, our goal was to investigate whether the use of the AI tool could help community health workers to make more accurate referrals to dermatologists. In this context, an effective triage could reduce the number of unnecessary appointments and/or increase the number of surgeries for suspected malignant lesions, which indicates that more high-risk cases were being correctly identified and referred. In contrast, during the period with AI assistance, they scheduled 214 appointments, resulting in 162 surgeries. Although the number of appointments scheduled decreased by 30% when using the AI tool, the number of surgeries performed remained almost the same. The effectiveness—calculated as the ratio between the number of surgeries and the number of appointments scheduled—increased from 58.57% to 75.70% with the use of the AI tool, indicating an improvement of over 17% in the triage process when comparing both periods. This suggests that the AI tool helped community health workers to make more accurate referrals, potentially reducing unnecessary appointments and focusing on high-risk cases.

To conclude, in phase 2, we also asked participants about their access to the internet during the patient assessments. The results showed that 55.6% of them reported never having internet access during the assessments, while 33.4% reported having internet access sometimes. This highlights the importance of having an AI tool that can operate offline, as it ensures that community health workers can utilize the tool effectively in remote areas with limited connectivity. We also allowed participants to provide additional comments about their experience using the tool. The feedback received was generally positive, with many participants expressing appreciation for the tool’s assistance in their clinical practice. Nonetheless, some participants also raised concerns about the accuracy of the AI tool. In the following, we present two representative comments from the participants.

Open feedback 1

"I used the application during my visits and felt that with its use, patients with skin lesions accepted the need for consultation better, which helped in their referral for treatment and prevention”.

In this comment, the participant highlights the positive impact of the AI tool on patients’ willingness to accept referrals to dermatologists. Community health workers often report that many patients are reluctant to follow such referrals, arguing that their skin lesions are not serious. In this case, however, the use of the AI tool appeared to increase patients’ trust in the referral process, potentially leading to better health outcomes. This is a surprising and encouraging finding, as patients typically prefer the judgment of a human healthcare professional over that of a machine. We believe that the recent hype surrounding AI technologies may have contributed to this effect, with patients perceiving AI tools as modern, advanced, and trustworthy sources of information.

Open feedback 2

"In my case, it suggested melanoma in two patients, but they had already been evaluated by a dermatologist in the past, and it was a nevus. I’m not 100% sure it works.

This comment reflects a concern about the accuracy of the AI tool, particularly regarding false positive results. The participant mentions two cases where the AI tool suggested a high priority level, but the lesions were previously evaluated by a dermatologist and found to be benign nevi. There are two important points to consider in this feedback. First, false positives are an inherent challenge in AI-based diagnostic tools, however, in this case, the tool was trained to consider melanocytic nevi as high priority (P1) due to their potential for malignant transformation. In other words, it was designed to prioritize sensitivity over specificity for this particular lesion type—which may lead to more false positives but aims to minimize the risk of missing potentially malignant cases. In addition, it is possible that the lesions changed over time, and what was previously a benign nevi could have developed suspicious features since the last evaluation by the dermatologist. Second, it important to invest in continuous user education and training to ensure that healthcare professionals understand the capabilities and limitations of the AI tool, enabling them to interpret its recommendations appropriately within the clinical context. There is no tool that works 100%,—not even experienced dermatologists—and it is important to set realistic expectations for users regarding the performance of AI-based systems.

Discussion

The results presented in the previous sections indicate that the proposed AI-based tool for skin lesion triage enhances the diagnostic performance of generalist healthcare professionals in real-world clinical settings. By integrating the AI system into an offline mobile application, the tool enables practical use in remote areas, demonstrating strong potential to improve the accuracy of skin lesion assessment in resource-limited environments, such as those found in rural regions of Brazil.

The AI model achieved promising results during internal validation, with a sensitivity of 0.790 ± 0.014 and specificity of 0.947 ± 0.002, attaining stable performance on our held-out dataset. The model’s AUC of 0.949 ± 0.006 indicates that it is able to discriminate between different lesion priority levels within the scope of the collected clinical images. Furthermore, the performance of the AI model in the clinical validation (sensitivity 0.815, specificity 0.931) closely matched the internal testing, suggesting that the model maintains its efficacy within the specific primary care workflow evaluated in this study. This finding addresses a common concern in AI applications in healthcare, where models often struggle to maintain performance when deployed outside of controlled environments18. This result indicates that careful model design, dataset representativeness, and context-aware triage protocols may be key factors in achieving consistent performance in targeted clinical deployments.

The results from Phase 1 reveal that generalist healthcare professionals notably improved their diagnostic performance when assisted by the AI tool. Sensitivity increased from 0.648 to 0.804, and specificity from 0.906 to 0.924. Nonetheless, observing the confusion matrices in Figure 2, we note that the AI tool primarily helped reduce misclassifications between priority levels P3, P4, and P5. From a strictly clinical perspective, correctly identifying the extreme priority levels—particularly P1 for urgent attention and P5 for benign cases—is the most critical task for patient safety. Nonetheless, accurate discrimination between intermediate categories such as P3 and P4 remains important for overall health system efficiency. For instance, P3 patients frequently require specific procedures such as cryotherapy; thus, overestimating or underestimating P3 cases can lead to unrealistic scheduling demands, bottlenecking the assistance program and reducing the number of available clinical appointments.

Conversely, for P2, the AI tool presented more stable results, whereas volunteers without AI assistance struggled to accurately classify these cases. These findings suggest that although the AI tool can improve overall triage performance, further research is needed to determine the most effective way to present AI-generated recommendations to users—particularly for high-priority cases where misclassification may have serious clinical implications. This finding is supported by the statistical analysis presented in Table 4, which indicates a significant difference between the AI tool and volunteers without assistance, but no significant difference between the AI tool and volunteers with assistance. Finding a balance between human judgment and AI recommendations is challenging, and future work should explore optimal strategies for integrating AI support into clinical workflows to maximize benefits while minimizing potential drawbacks. We align with prior studies19,20 that emphasize the role of AI in augmenting, rather than replacing, human decision-making in healthcare.

In Phase 2, the real-world deployment among community health workers produced a notable 17% improvement in triage effectiveness (from 58.57% to 75.70%), despite a 30% reduction in the total number of dermatology appointments. This suggests that the AI tool helped to improve individual decision-making and the overall efficiency of the healthcare workflow. In practice, this means that dermatologists received a higher proportion of high-risk cases, maximizing the impact of limited specialist resources. These results suggest that AI-assisted triage may contribute to the rational allocation of medical resources—a key challenge in public health systems worldwide. Also, the fact that these improvements were achieved in a setting with limited internet access—where over half of users reported working entirely offline—underscores the practicality of the proposed offline-capable solution for deployment in remote or underserved areas.

Participant feedback across both phases was overwhelmingly positive, with more than 90% of participants in Phase 1 and 85% in Phase 2 expressing that the tool should be used in clinical practice. Feelings of safety and confirmation were the most frequently reported, indicating that the AI tool provided reassurance and confidence to users during triage. Interestingly, open-ended responses revealed an unanticipated secondary benefit: the presence of the AI tool appeared to increase patient trust in referrals made by community health workers. This psychological and social dimension—where AI indirectly facilitates adherence to medical advice—represents a promising area for further investigation. However, some concerns about accuracy were raised, particularly regarding false positives. This highlights the importance of ongoing user education to ensure that healthcare professionals understand the capabilities and limitations of AI tools. Setting realistic expectations is important to prevent overreliance on AI recommendations and to maintain critical clinical judgment.

The small decrease in positive responses during Phase 2, along with a slight rise in neutral or negative feedback, may reflect a combination of factors: the challenges of using the tool in less controlled, rural settings; the inherent difficulties of triaging real patients compared to controlled cases; and user adaptation over time. These observations suggest that while initial enthusiasm for AI tools is high, sustained adoption may require addressing practical challenges, continuous training, regular feedback cycles, and transparent communication about the limitations of AI systems.

While the clinical validation presented here provides notable evidence for the real-world utility of the proposed tool, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, the AI model was trained and validated using data collected from multiple cities within a single state in Brazil, reflecting the demographic and epidemiological characteristics of the population served by the PAD-UFES program. Moreover, the evaluation was conducted in clinical environments and workflows closely aligned with this same context. As a result, the reported performance should be interpreted within this specific deployment scenario and may not generalize to regions with different population profiles, healthcare structures, or disease distributions. In particular, the dataset does not fully capture variability across different skin tones (e.g., Fitzpatrick types), which may affect model performance in more diverse populations. While the current objective is to deploy the tool within the public health system of this state, future work should include multi-center studies with broader demographic and clinical diversity to ensure fairness and generalizability.

Second, the model was designed for risk-based triage in primary care rather than diagnostic classification. While this supports practical decision-making in resource-limited settings, differences in intermediate categories (e.g., P3 vs. P4) may have limited impact on high-stakes clinical outcomes, despite contributing to system-level efficiency. In addition, as the protocol focuses on early detection of malignant lesions, the dataset does not fully represent severe inflammatory skin conditions. Although some common inflammatory diseases are included (e.g., dermatitis), other inflammatory cases are typically assigned to intermediate categories, reflecting a design choice aligned with the malignancy-focused triage protocol adopted in this clinical program, although this may not always capture the full clinical urgency of certain conditions. This limitation is partially mitigated by the broader clinical training received by healthcare professionals prior to participating in the program. Also, the relatively small sample size in Phase 1 (n = 87) limits statistical power, particularly for rare but critical categories such as P1.

Third, the system relies on a deep learning architecture, which inherently limits interpretability. As previously discussed, the practical impact of this black box nature is minimized by the system’s human-in-the-loop design and its specific focus on rapid risk-based triage rather than definitive diagnosis. While this clinical context justifies prioritizing operational efficiency, the lack of explicit explainability mechanisms remains an inherent architectural limitation. Future work may explore the integration of interpretability techniques to enhance transparency and user trust, particularly in more complex diagnostic settings.

Finally, in Phase 2, we acknowledge that external factors—such as seasonal variations in skin lesion incidence or changes in workforce availability—may have influenced the observed results. Although these effects were likely limited due to the relatively short interval between evaluation periods, future studies should incorporate control groups or longer observation windows to better isolate the impact of AI assistance.

Methods

This section outlines the key methodological components of the study, along with the theoretical foundations related to Artificial Intelligence, model development, and clinical validation that underpin the proposed methodology. Skin lesion triage

Skin lesion triage refers to the process of sorting and prioritizing skin lesions based on their potential malignancy. The goal is to identify lesions that require more medical attention, such as those that may be indicative of skin cancer, while distinguishing them from benign lesions that can be monitored or treated later. This process is commonly used in clinical settings and telemedicine to improve patient management and optimize resource allocation21. The triage process may differ depending on the healthcare setting and the specific protocols in place. Typically, it is performed by healthcare professionals, such as general practitioners or nurses, who assess the lesions based on visual examination, patient history, and sometimes additional diagnostic tools like dermoscopy.

In Brazil, community health workers play an important role in the triage process in the public health system, particularly in underserved areas where access to dermatologists is limited. These workers are often the first point of contact for patients with skin lesions and are trained to perform basic assessments and refer high-risk cases to specialized care. Nonetheless, their training in dermatology is usually limited, and they do not have access to advanced diagnostic tools, relying on visual inspection and basic clinical criteria to make decisions about referrals. As a result, the accuracy of the triage may vary significantly, potentially leading to missed diagnoses or unnecessary referrals. In this context, developing tools to assist these professionals in this task is of great importance in improving early detection of skin cancer in those regions, which are common in emerging countries.

Usually, Computer Aided Diagnosis (CAD) systems for dermatology are designed to identify specific types of skin lesions, such as melanoma or non-melanoma skin cancers15,22. However, in real-world clinical settings, healthcare professionals often need to make decisions based on the overall risk level of a lesion rather than its specific diagnosis. As such, dermatology experts from PAD-UFES designed a skin lesion triage protocol that classifies lesions into five different priority levels based on their potential malignancy and urgency for medical attention based on the local epidemiological context where the system will be deployed. Basically, priority level 1 (P1) includes lesions that are highly suspicious for malignancy and require immediate medical attention, while priority level 5 (P5) includes normal skin or skin conditions (e.g., acne) that do not require urgent care. The intermediate levels (P2, P3, and P4) represent varying degrees of risk, with P2 being more concerning than P3, and P4 being less concerning than P3. In clinical practice, these priority levels denote relative urgency for referral rather than rigid, time-based deadlines. Because the exact timeframe for dermatological evaluation depends heavily on fluctuating local healthcare capacities, patient demand, and logistical constraints, the protocol functions as a dynamic scheduling guide. Available appointments are allocated sequentially: P1 cases are scheduled first, followed by P2 cases, and so forth. This relative prioritization ensures that the highest-risk patients receive the earliest possible care, while remaining flexible enough to adapt to varying resource limitations across different public health programs. Dataset

In order to train the AI model for skin lesion triage, we employed the PAD-UFES-20+ dataset, an extended version of the PAD-UFES-20 dataset23. The PAD-UFES-20+ dataset comprises a total of 13,569 clinical images of skin lesions collected from 4067 patients who attended the Dermatological and Surgical Assistance Program of the Federal University of Espírito Santo (PAD-UFES) between 2018 and 2024. The mean age of the patients is 60.3 years ±16.4 SD; 57.1% of them are female, 43.9% is male, and 94.2% of the patients have Fitzpatrick skin types I to III, and 5.98% have Fitzpatrick skin types IV to VI, which reflects the demographic profile of the population served by PAD-UFES. The images were captured using smartphones and tablets, reflecting the real-world conditions under which the mobile application is intended to be used. The dataset includes a diverse range of skin lesion types, encompassing both benign and malignant conditions, and is annotated according to the five priority levels defined in the triage protocol described earlier. Artificial intelligence model

The AI model used in this work was developed to classify skin lesions into the five priority levels defined in the triage protocol. While recent advancements in computer vision have introduced Vision Transformers as state-of-the-art for various image classification tasks, these architectures typically require massive datasets for training and entail highly intensive computational demands. In contrast, Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architectures remain highly effective, particularly in resource-constrained environments. Consequently, we decided to use a CNN-based approach, which has been widely used in image classification tasks due to its ability to learn hierarchical features from raw pixel data15. In previous works16,24,25, we investigated several CNN architectures, including ResNet26, MobileNet27, and EfficientNet28 families. We found that MobileNet-V3 provided the best trade-off between performance and computational efficiency, which is essential since our model is designed to run locally on mobile devices. The clinical context of deployment also influenced our approach to model interpretability. While the black box nature of deep learning models is a well-known limitation, its practical impact is limited in our context, as the system is designed strictly as a decision-support tool for risk-based triage rather than diagnosis. The model assigns priority levels to guide primary care referrals, operating within a human-in-the-loop framework where healthcare professionals always perform the final clinical evaluation. Furthermore, as the system is intended to support triage by generalist practitioners in resource-limited settings, we prioritized operational efficiency, usability, and seamless integration into existing clinical workflows. In this context, complex explainability mechanisms may offer limited added value for end users. Nevertheless, we recognize that interpretability remains important—particularly in diagnostic settings—and represents a relevant direction for future work.

The model was implemented using PyTorch, and the MobileNet-V3 architecture was initialized with weights pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset29 obtained from the torchvision API. The final fully connected layer was replaced with a new layer containing five output neurons, corresponding to the five priority levels defined in the triage protocol. Next, the model was fine-tuned on the PAD-UFES-20+ for 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0001 and exponential decay rates of β1 = 0.9 and β2 = 0.999. To enhance training efficiency, the learning rate was reduced by a factor of 0.1 if no improvement was observed over 10 consecutive epochs, and early stopping was triggered after 15 epochs without improvement. To mitigate class imbalance, a weighted cross-entropy loss function was employed, assigning weights inversely proportional to the frequency of each class. All input images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels and augmented using standard techniques, including horizontal and vertical flipping, brightness, contrast, and saturation adjustments, image scaling, and the addition of random noise30,31. We employed 5-fold cross-validation using a stratified group scheme, ensuring stratification by label frequency while grouping by patient to prevent data leakage. The model’s performance was evaluated using the average and standard deviation of the sensitivity (also known as recall), specificity, and area under the curve (AUC).

The final model used in the clinical validation was obtained by training on the entire PAD-UFES-20+ dataset using the same hyperparameters and training procedures described above. This model was then converted to the ONNX format—which results in a model with around 8 MB in size—and integrated into a mobile application designed to assist healthcare professionals in skin lesion triage. In Figure 5, we present some screenshots of the mobile application, illustrating its user interface and key features. It was developed using Kotlin and is compatible with Android devices running version 8.0 (API level 26) or higher—which reaches over 95% of the Android devices. In order to use the application, healthcare professionals need to create an account and log in—only authorized users can access the system. Once logged in, they can capture images of skin lesions using the device’s camera or upload existing images from the gallery. The AI model processes the image locally on the device (in other words, the patient data is not sent to the cloud) and provides a triage recommendation based on the priority levels defined in the protocol. The results are displayed on the screen, along with additional information about the recommended next steps for patient management. In the Phase 2 of the study aimed to further evaluate the impact of the AI tool on the skin lesion triage process conducted by community health workers in remote areas served by PAD-UFES, who represent the primary target users of the proposed tool. Within the PAD-UFES workflow, these health workers are responsible for the initial assessment and triage of patients with skin lesions, referring suspected high-risk cases to dermatologists for further evaluation. Next, if the dermatologist identifies a lesion as suspicious for malignancy, the patient is referred to a surgeon for biopsy and treatment. In this context, the PAD-UFES team defines an effective triage phase as one in which most of the lesions referred to dermatologists are indeed suspicious for malignancy – that is, cases subsequently sent to surgical intervention. Accordingly, this phase of the study focused on assessing how the use of the AI tool influenced the referral decisions made by community health workers.

For this purpose, we selected the city of Santa Maria de Jetibá, a long-term partner of PAD-UFES, located in a mountainous region in the countryside of Espírito Santo State. The city has a high incidence of skin cancer due to its predominantly fair-skinned population and high sun exposure. The PAD-UFES triage process takes about three weeks to complete, during which community health workers actively visit the community, identifying and assessing skin lesions in patients and scheduling appointments with dermatologists from the program when necessary. As such, in this phase of the study, we conducted a real-world field evaluation to complement the Phase 1 investigation. We compared the performance of community health workers in skin lesion triage across two distinct periods: (i) in March 2024, without using the AI tool, and (ii) in December 2024, using the AI tool. For this stage, we recruited 57 community health workers who agreed to participate in the study and were instructed on how to use the mobile skin lesion triage tool. The average age of the community health workers was 43.7 years (±8.54 SD), with 96.4% women (n = 55) and 3.6% men (n = 2)—this is a gender distribution that reflects the typical composition of community health workers in Brazil.

During both periods ((i) and (ii)), the community health workers performed skin lesion triage in the community following the standard PAD-UFES protocol. However, during the second period, we gave access to the AI-based mobile application. They carried out their usual work without external interference and could use the AI tool to assist in skin lesion triage whenever they found it necessary. The goal was for them to act naturally and comfortably within their regular routine so that we could evaluate the results in a real-world care scenario. Finally, at the end of the second period, participants completed the same usability questionnaire applied in Phase 1 of the study. However, this time, an additional field was included where they could write open comments about their experience.
