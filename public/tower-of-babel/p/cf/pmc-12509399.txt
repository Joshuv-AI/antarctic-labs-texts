Healthcare providers’ perceptions on facility-based screening for intimate partner violence: a multi-center qualitative formative research at assisted reproductive technology clinics
Authors: Xinchen Li, Han Hu, Yu Fu, Xueqi Sun, Yuanyuan Wang, Fan Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo explore acceptable strategies and feasibility of intimate partner violence (IPV) screening at assisted reproductive technology (ART) hospitals, from the perspective of healthcare providers.MethodsDrawing on programmatic qualitative research design, this study used in-depth interviews to elicit experiences and opinions of clinicians who provided ART services in selected hospitals in Beijing, Anhui Province, Guangdong Province, and Ningxia Hui Autonomous Reigion.ResultsA total of 14 ART doctors and nurses joined our study. We found that clinicians encountered suspected cases of IPV victimization and that several reported having applied strategies to identify IPV in medical practice. Acceptable strategies mentioned by clinicians include identifying key patients of concern during ART service delivery, and thereafter following up with case management. Most healthcare providers mentioned that challenges to IPV screening at ART clinics would include its incompatibility between clinical workflow, clinic venue-specific disadvantages for routinized IPV screening, and limited capacity of the healthcare workforce to respond to IPV.ConclusionsHealthcare providers at ART clinics have commonly encountered infertility patients who fell victim to IPV. Screening for IPV is deemed possible only if risk stratification is implemented. Several health system-level challenges should be addressed to mobilize and incentive healthcare providers to engage more with IPV screening.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13362-9.

Background

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is the most widespread form of violence against women globally [1]. It can take forms as physical, sexual or psychological violence, as well as controlling behaviors, perpetrated by a current or former intimate partner [1–3]. According to recent estimates from the World Health Organization (WHO), 27% of women worldwide have been abused by their intimate partner at least once in their lifetime, while 13% have experienced IPV in the past 12 months [1]. The past 12-month prevalence was estimated even higher in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), with 37.2% of women reporting experiencing at least one type of IPV in recent surveys [4]. IPV is not only a violation to fundamental human rights but also causes numerous adverse health outcomes. For instance, women who have experienced IPV suffer from ensuing health problems, such as injury, dizziness, chronic pain, gastrointestinal disease, poor reproductive health outcomes, depression, and other emotional distress, and in many cases with suicidal ideation and attempts [5, 6].

Infertility can exacerbate multiple risk factors for IPV, including high levels of marital discord, separation, divorce, and multiple sexual partners [7]. In addition, it may also function as an independent risk factor for IPV, particularly in cultural contexts where fertility is closely tied to women’s social value and marital stability [8]. Women with infertility in LMICs may experience increased vulnerability to IPV [3, 7], which might be associated with gender inequality rooted in sociocultural norms and institutionalized through laws and policies that favor men over women [3, 9]. In China, the prevalence of IPV experienced by women with infertility was estimated to be 17.2% over a 12-month period, which may even be underestimated due to potential selection bias associated with healthcare facility-based samples [3]. Together, IPV and infertility operate as an intersected vulnerability threatening women’s human rights as well as their sexual and reproductive health.

IPV screening necessitates timely identification of cases in order to intervene in time. In line with this, several high-income countries (HIC), such as the United States, have established formal recommendations for IPV screening. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends that women of reproductive age be screened for IPV in healthcare settings and, when necessary, referred to appropriate support services [10]. Similarly, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises that IPV screening be routinely conducted by healthcare professionals, including nurses, midwives, and obstetrician–gynecologists (OB/GYN), at regular intervals for all female patients during visits in both primary care settings and OB/GYN departments [11]. However, such standardized practices or discussion thereof have been largely missing in LMICs. In China, an anti-domestic violence law (The Anti-Domestic Violence Law of the People’s Republic of China) has come into effect in 2016 [12], while health system has not been engaged for IPV screening. Additionally, subgroups of women at disproportionately higher risk of IPV victimization have rarely been surveyed about their unique needs in terms of IPV screening. To date, few official recommendations worldwide have tailored IPV screening guides for specific subgroups, except for one performed in HIV counseling and testing settings concerning potential patients living with HIV [13].

Assisted reproductive technology (ART) has been effective in treating certain infertility and faces increasing demand in China as the postponement of childbirth becomes a demographic trend [14]. Since the first in-vitro fertilization success in 1988, ART has expanded its coverage to all provinces in mainland China, totaling 517 accredited ART centers by the end of 2019 [15]. Most ART clinics in China are public healthcare facilities. A policy memorandum issued in 2022 outlined a plan to progressively incorporate ART treatments into the national health insurances system, with Beijing taking the lead by providing medical insurance for 16 ART items as of 2024 [16]. In the context of foreseeable increases in access to ART in the next years, ART facility-based screening for IPV merits not only scholarly attention but also increased clinical focus [3, 15]. Yet, IPV screening practices have not been formally integrated into ART medical practice.

Formative research involves gathering data useful for the development and implementation of intervention programs [17]. In our study, this refers to whether and how screening for IPV at ART settings is appropriate for further implementation. While numerous studies have explored the feasibility and acceptability of IPV screening in antenatal care settings [18–20], general reproductive health [21, 22], and emergency departments [23], none have focused specifically in the context of ART clinics. ART clinicians often develop extensive and close contact with patients over the lengthy fertility care process, which involves multiple clinic visits. This nature of physician-patient relationship provides an opportunity to screen for IPV among patients [24]. Since screening for IPV at ART service settings is unprecedented in China before, it calls for formative research to explore acceptable strategies and perceived feasibility among key stakeholders, for example, healthcare providers.

Evidence from other countries suggested that screening for IPV at clinic settings can be challenged by a lack of consensus regarding what constitutes best practices for detection [23, 25]. Several screening tools have been designed to identify IPV. These include the “Hurt, Insult, Threaten, and Scream” [26] and the “Woman Abuse Screening Tool/Woman Abuse Screening Tool- Short Form” [27], both tested in the US for use in family physicians’ practice; the “Extended - Hurt, Insult, Threaten, and Scream” tool tested in emergency department in Hong Kong, China [28]; the “Modified Extended - Hurt, Insult, Threaten, and Scream” tool tested among a U.S. sample of exclusively veteran women [29]; the “Partner Violence Screen” developed for use in emergency department in the US [30]; the “Abuse Assessment Screen” developed for prenatal clinics in the US [31]; the “Afraid/Controlled/Threatened/Slapped or physically hurt” tool tested in antenatal care in Australia [32]; and the “Infertile Women’s Exposure to Violence Determination Scale” tested in infertility clinic in Turkey [33]. The sensitivity and specificity of these standardized screening questionnaires remains inconclusive due to a lack of gold standard of IPV measurement [25, 34, 35], and the fact that formal screening procedures do not necessarily result in disclosure [36].

Screening for IPV at ART settings is planned to be empirically tested in China to evaluate its effectiveness. One proposed approach involves inviting infertile patients to voluntarily complete a self-administered questionnaire in a separate room during the course of ART treatment. Formative research is an essential step prior to the implementation of programs, as it gathers insights early on to help make informed decisions about program design, messaging, and expected user experience. Specifically, as screening for IPV in ART settings will rely on input from ART healthcare providers, their perceptions are crucial and should be considered and incorporated into designing IPV screening interventions. This study complements a previous qualitative research, which explored perceptions and experiences towards IPV of women undergoing ART [37]. Together with the current formative research focused on healthcare provider perspectives, these studies offer a triangulated evidence to inform the design of IPV screening interventions in ART settings. In this study, we aim to gather knowledge and elicit healthcare providers’ opinions on screening for IPV at ART settings among their patients, to inform subsequent intervention effort. To achieve this goal, programmatic qualitative research as a factual knowledge-driven method was used to ask purposeful and open-ended questions and to tap into insights and experiences of healthcare providers [38]. Their contribution to developing acceptable IPV screening strategies and identifying the factors affecting the feasibility of IPV screening from ART service providers’ perspectives will be valuable.

Methods

Study design and site selection

Experiencing infertility can amplify various underlying factors associated with, or directly contributing to IPV, which places individuals undergoing ART at greater susceptibility to IPV. Moreover, ART settings typically involve frequent, intensive, and prolonged interactions between healthcare providers and patients. This would foster continuity of care that facilitates trust-building and communication about sensitive topics such as IPV. Therefore, ART clinics could be a salient setting for IPV screening.

The study is underpinned by interpretivist paradigm, which prioritizes individuals’ subjective experiences and emphasizes the context in shaping individuals’ experiences [39]. This paradigm permitted us to gain an understanding of how healthcare providers interacted with patients to identify potential IPV victims at ART settings in representative regions of China, and how IPV screening could be conducted from the perspectives of healthcare providers at ART settings taking into account personal, clinical, and institutional factors. Conducted from September 2022 to August 2023, this study used in-depth interviews to gather healthcare providers’ experience and perceptions on the acceptability and feasibility of IPV screening at ART clinics in China. We first identified a list of healthcare facilities certified to provide full-range ART services. Among those health facilities, we chose four based on geographic variations (Northern, Central, Southern, and Western China), which yielded four study sites respectively in Beijing, Anhui Province, Guangdong Province and the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region (abbreviated as Ningxia hereafter). The selection of those four regions maximized the variations in terms of socioeconomic and culture context, and therefore enhanced sample’s representativeness for larger areas in China. More details about those geographic areas and their selection rationale are in Supplementary file.

Sampling and data collection

Participants in our interviews included healthcare providers working in reproductive medicine, who provided ART specialty care and services to women, women’s male partners, or both in the past six months, and voluntarily participated in the study with informed consent. To diversify the sample, we included participants from different cadres, such as associate chief physicians, attending physicians, and nurses, and with various work experience, in order to reflect differences in clinical duties, training backgrounds, and levels of seniority. These factors were expected to influence participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and confidence toward IPV screening. In the early stage of this study, five healthcare providers were recruited from Beijing by purposive sampling. Through iterative data analysis and collection guided by qualitative research methods [40], we proceeded to recruit additional participants from Anhui, Guangdong and Ningxia. Participants were recruited through China’s National Obstetrics and Gynecology Research Network (CNOGRN). The two corresponding authors (YW and FY) led the recruitment process by spreading information about this study and inviting voluntary participation from CNOGRN members. All participants voluntarily took part in this study and received monetary compensation ranging from 400 to 800 RMB after the interviews, based on the length of the interview.

Interview guides were developed to include the following four sections: first, participants were asked about their role and responsibilities in ART-related services, including their level of interaction with patients; secondly, we probed for those healthcare workers’ past encounters with IPV victims during ART treatment and services; thirdly, participants were invited to discuss what can be achieved in clinical settings to screen for IPV among women with infertility; lastly, we further elicited participants’ opinions on those suggested interventions. Prior to data collection, interviewers received qualitative research training on data collection and research ethics training. Detailed interview outline can be found in Supplementary Files.

One interview was conducted in Beijing at the reproductive medicine center in a separate meeting room, and the other 13 interviews were carried out on Tencent Meeting, an online video conference platform widely used in China [41]. Interviews were conducted using a hybrid approach (online and in-person) due to COVID-19 lockdowns and participants' scheduling preferences. Four researchers (XL, YF, XS, and HH) conducted the interviews in Mandarin Chinese, after obtaining oral consent from the participants. Interviews lasted between 25 and 60 min averaging 43 min. They were all audio-recorded and transcribed to text for analysis. Data collection ended when information saturation was reached whereby no new themes and codes emerged from data.

Data analysis

Interview transcripts were reviewed and anonymized. We employed template analysis, a form of thematic analysis which emphasizes hierarchical coding in the process of textual analysis [42]. Two researchers (XL, HH) independently conducted preliminary coding of all interviews to develop an initial coding template, which was subsequently refined through iterative review whereby sub-themes and quotations emerged from the data. Discrepancies in coding and template revisions were resolved through continuous discussion and consensus among the research team. Data saturation was considered reached when no new themes or codes emerged from additional interviews. The final coding template encompassed the following three overarching themes: (1) strategies already deployed by healthcare providers to identify IPV victims among patients; (2) proposed strategies to formulate IPV screening programs and their acceptability; and (3) potential challenges to incorporate IPV screening into routine clinical practices at ART settings. Finally, we synthesized the key findings and selected quotations based on their representativeness and relevance to the aim of this analysis.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (IRB00006761-M2022437). All sites in this study are participating members under the CNOGRN agreement.

Strategies already deployed by healthcare providers to identify IPV victims among patients

Encountering IPV victimization from patients’ account

Several healthcare providers mentioned that they had been explicitly informed about IPV victimization directly by patients’ disclosure. Nine out of 14 participants reported that they had encountered patients during medical consultation or treatment, who voluntarily disclosed IPV victimization including threats. As a doctor stated that “I have met infertile women who were threatened [by their partners], saying that if the women could not achieve pregnancy through ART, they would be divorced or cheated on.” (ID09, Associate chief physician). One nurse mentioned that she learned about participant’s IPV victimization experience through a questionnaire that she filled out (ID11, Nurse).

Suspecting IPV victimization from subtle hints

Healthcare providers also mentioned that there existed tacit knowledge to identify IPV through subtle hints revealed during medical practices, including bruises on patients during medical examinations, witnessing spouses’ verbal abuse during ART treatment, and observing spouses’ passive aggressiveness towards ART treatment at the ART centers. However, they also concurred that it cannot be confirmed as IPV cases without further probing. As one doctor stated:“Because women had to take some clothes off during ultrasound diagnosis, I noticed that some patients had something that seemed like bruises on their skins, but I wasn’t sure.”(ID03, Attending physician)

“Because women had to take some clothes off during ultrasound diagnosis, I noticed that some patients had something that seemed like bruises on their skins, but I wasn’t sure.”

(ID03, Attending physician)

Several participants mentioned that they witnessed abusive behaviors and attitudes from the spouses accompanying their patients at the hospitals. One nurse mentioned that she saw “many times of disrespectful behavior by men to women, including verbal and physical abuse” (ID10, Nurse) during her clinical work. Another nurse confirmed that spouse’s verbal abuse in ART clinics was a common manifestation of gender inequality and power asymmetry:“You can tell the woman’s status in her family by the man’s negative attitude or his abusive words when the couple show up.”(ID12, Nurse)

“You can tell the woman’s status in her family by the man’s negative attitude or his abusive words when the couple show up.”

(ID12, Nurse)

Partner’s passive aggressiveness towards ART treatment was regarded as another subtle indication of potential IPV victimization. One participant used the term “emotional abuse” to describe the partner’s lack of compliance towards ART treatment, as the participant reported:“Indeed, there are male partners who neglect their wives’ [effort during ART treatment]. Every time their cooperation is needed,…, for example, to sign consent documents required for ART procedure, they simply don’t show up. I think this can be seen as a form of violence, perhaps emotional abuse.”(ID14, Associate chief physician)

“Indeed, there are male partners who neglect their wives’ [effort during ART treatment]. Every time their cooperation is needed,…, for example, to sign consent documents required for ART procedure, they simply don’t show up. I think this can be seen as a form of violence, perhaps emotional abuse.”

(ID14, Associate chief physician)

One doctor also identified patients who were financially restricted by partners:“There were also a few men, who were unwilling to allocate money [for ART treatment]. Therefore, women [who had already quit their job for ART treatment] had to temporarily go to work on their own to make money for the treatment. I would interpret this as a sign that the woman’s status in the family is low.”(ID01, Associate chief physician)

“There were also a few men, who were unwilling to allocate money [for ART treatment]. Therefore, women [who had already quit their job for ART treatment] had to temporarily go to work on their own to make money for the treatment. I would interpret this as a sign that the woman’s status in the family is low.”

(ID01, Associate chief physician)

Proposed strategies to formulate IPV screening programs and their acceptability

Based on interview data, two strategies emerged to inform the screening for IPV victimization at ART clinics. They included: first, to triage suspected IPV victims from the large majority of patient flow, and from there, to provide concerted effort to systematically inquire about IPV risks.

Triage suspected IPV victims for further screening

According to participants, patients experiencing repeated ART treatment failures should be identified as persons at increased risk. One participant spoke based on her experience:“[If] a woman failed to be pregnant after rounds of treatment. If it [the infertility] is [due to] the woman’s factors, the man would certainly be angry, he might also abuse her.”(ID12, Nurse)

“[If] a woman failed to be pregnant after rounds of treatment. If it [the infertility] is [due to] the woman’s factors, the man would certainly be angry, he might also abuse her.”

(ID12, Nurse)

Healthcare providers emphasized several key timepoints of ART treatment which patients would not normally miss out. When patients suddenly became absent for the scheduled treatment at those critical time points, their healthcare providers should be wary to inquire about potential IPV victimization. One doctor recollected the past cases seen:“I think that in those cases where the ART treatment was in the final step and almost going to make the goal, some patients did not even show up. There must be some issues going on. It’s usually the husband cheating, or domestic violence happening.”(ID09, Associate chief physician)

“I think that in those cases where the ART treatment was in the final step and almost going to make the goal, some patients did not even show up. There must be some issues going on. It’s usually the husband cheating, or domestic violence happening.”

(ID09, Associate chief physician)

Also, five out of 14 participants mentioned that bruises, scars, and other physical indications of injury could serve as indications to initially alert clinicians to triage those patients for further screening. As a participant stated that “Some violence is reflected as physical abuse, …, then the doctor can ask and screen.” (ID02, Nurse).

A few participants believed that patients who appear in poor mental state (i.e. with apparent distress and anxiety) that seemed disproportionate to the clinical context, should be prioritized for IPV screening. Healthcare providers recognized that ART procedures can naturally cause emotional strain; however, they emphasized that their concerns were raised when patients’ reactions appeared unusually intense. As a participant noted:“Patient’s mental state can be told from her face [during clinical encounter], … If she appears extremely anxious,…,so much so that it stands out and we feel it is beyond normal stress, then we might consider [screening for] violent behavior. … We suspect violent behavior from anxious or unhappy patients at first.”(ID10, Nurse)

“Patient’s mental state can be told from her face [during clinical encounter], … If she appears extremely anxious,…,so much so that it stands out and we feel it is beyond normal stress, then we might consider [screening for] violent behavior. … We suspect violent behavior from anxious or unhappy patients at first.”

(ID10, Nurse)

Four participants mentioned that IPV screening triage could take place based on observing patients’ interactions with families. One doctor mentioned:“[Identifying potential IPV victims by] Whether she was not intimate with her husband or other relatives during treatment.”(ID03, Attending physician)

“[Identifying potential IPV victims by] Whether she was not intimate with her husband or other relatives during treatment.”

(ID03, Attending physician)

Systematic screening for IPV experience among those with increased risks

After triaging the patients and having identified individuals at increased risk, several participants suggested that healthcare providers may further inquire about potential IPV experience strategically, such as: (1) weaving the inquiry about IPV into an attentive conversation; (2) using anonymous self-administered assessment tools; and (3) involving mental health support staff to provide counseling services.

Firstly, participants discussed communication strategies to guide patients to disclose IPV by “spending more time to listen” (ID01, Associate chief physician) and potentially “using qualitative methods to lead” patients to narrate their life stories (ID10, Nurse).

Secondly, a few participants suggested using self-administered tools such as checklist or questionnaire to avoid confrontation on this sensitive matter. For instance, one participant mentioned, “Some patients are ashamed to tell us [about IPV victimization], but if they are asked to finish the questionnaire, they will have the courage to fill out” (ID04, Associate chief physician). Specifically, another doctor recommended using online questionnaires to screen for IPV to ensure patients’ safety and freedom from pressure when responding to IPV victimization questions:“I think online questionnaires are good, because we could know they are filled out by women themselves. Some women are easier to be influenced by their family members (being present) when filling out paper questionnaires…Thus, the results of (paper-based) questionnaire analysis might deviate from the truth.”(ID14, Associate chief physician)

“I think online questionnaires are good, because we could know they are filled out by women themselves. Some women are easier to be influenced by their family members (being present) when filling out paper questionnaires…Thus, the results of (paper-based) questionnaire analysis might deviate from the truth.”

(ID14, Associate chief physician)

Three participants suggested staffing professional counsellors in IPV screening at ART clinics, as follows:“Some clinics don’t have special psychological counselor. If there is a counselor, I think patients are more willing to tell their experiences [about IPV].”(ID12, Nurse)

“Some clinics don’t have special psychological counselor. If there is a counselor, I think patients are more willing to tell their experiences [about IPV].”

(ID12, Nurse)

Although the majority of participants perceived that it was acceptable for healthcare providers to implement IPV screening at ART clinics, they also expressed concerns about the challenges limiting the feasibility. As one participant noted, “many factors will influence the feasibility of IPV screening” (ID10, Nurse). The commonly reported challenges, as presented in following sections, included that routinized IPV screening being incompatible with current clinical workflow at ART clinics, venue-specific drawbacks for IPV screening at healthcare settings, and lack of preparedness among healthcare providers to address IPV issues.

Potential challenges to incorporate IPV screening into routine clinical practices at ART settings

Incompatibility between clinical workflow and routinized IPV screening

Insufficient time per patient during consultation was commonly viewed as a major challenge to routinize IPV screening at ART clinics according to most clinicians interviewed in our study. As mentioned by one doctor:“But there are too many outpatient visits (per day), even though doctors intended to talk more with patients (about IPV), more (medical) work is waiting for them, because time is limited and precious.”(ID08, Attending physician)

“But there are too many outpatient visits (per day), even though doctors intended to talk more with patients (about IPV), more (medical) work is waiting for them, because time is limited and precious.”

(ID08, Attending physician)

For several healthcare providers, they have witnessed intimate partner verbal violence but time shortage to some extent hinders any further inquiries to patients’ experience:“Actually, as for the reason why they fought, we have no idea, and it is partly due to little time for us to investigate [further].”(ID13, Associate chief physician)

“Actually, as for the reason why they fought, we have no idea, and it is partly due to little time for us to investigate [further].”

(ID13, Associate chief physician)

Besides time shortage, various cadres of healthcare providers reasoned that facility-based IPV screening is not part of their cadre-specific responsibilities. One nurse stated, “the screening depends on doctors’ willingness to do so.” (ID11, Nurse). Conversely, a doctor, considering the alignment between ART care provision and IPV screening, mentioned that the responsibility for IPV screening should be delegated to nurses as they spend more time with patients:“… I saw some patients suddenly crashed down outside the room, specifically, uncontrolled yelling or crying…but they didn’t complain to us but to nurses. … So sometimes the head nurse or senior nurse would invite them into the nurse station and talk with them. However, to tell the truth, we (the doctors) didn’t participate in the conversation as we were under time pressure making diagnosis.”(ID09, Associate chief physician)

“… I saw some patients suddenly crashed down outside the room, specifically, uncontrolled yelling or crying…but they didn’t complain to us but to nurses. … So sometimes the head nurse or senior nurse would invite them into the nurse station and talk with them. However, to tell the truth, we (the doctors) didn’t participate in the conversation as we were under time pressure making diagnosis.”

(ID09, Associate chief physician)

Furthermore, existing healthcare infrastructure lacks necessary incentives such that IPV screening can be included as a performance indicator for healthcare providers. Consequentially, some healthcare providers perceived limited motivation to put sustained effort in implementing IPV screening as a routine practice. As one participant questioned during the interview:“What does (IPV) screening contribute to doctors or assistants? For them, what benefits could they get, directly or indirectly?”(ID10, Nurse)

“What does (IPV) screening contribute to doctors or assistants? For them, what benefits could they get, directly or indirectly?”

(ID10, Nurse)

Venue-specific drawbacks for IPV screening at healthcare settings

Although ART clinics in general provide space for physician-patient communication on private matters related to sexual and reproductive health, several venue-specific drawbacks were mentioned by healthcare providers. Firstly, according to participants, ART clinics were perceived as public space where IPV perpetrators may disguise their violence tendency in public, which is disadvantageous for healthcare providers to efficiently discover signs for patients at risk of IPV. As a participant noted:“…Violent behaviors in outpatient department are less because the potential perpetrators still take others’ gaze into account.”(ID03, Attending physician)

“…Violent behaviors in outpatient department are less because the potential perpetrators still take others’ gaze into account.”

(ID03, Attending physician)

Besides, unlike most other clinics in health practices, ART clinics are usually set up with separate consultation rooms for male and female patients in many cities in China, which renders it difficult for healthcare providers to observe dyadic interaction between couples seeking treatment. This added to missed opportunities to identify key persons at risk of IPV, as one doctor stated:“We didn’t allow ladies to enter during male patients’ consultations… so the proportion [of IPV we could observe, among those actually happening] is quite low.”(ID07, Attending physician)

“We didn’t allow ladies to enter during male patients’ consultations… so the proportion [of IPV we could observe, among those actually happening] is quite low.”

(ID07, Attending physician)

Last, suboptimal privacy in ART clinics was also seen as a challenge to IPV screening. As a doctor noted that “There may be other people [residents and attending clinician] around listening, it may not be so private [for patients to disclose]” (ID04 Associate chief physician).

Lack of preparedness among healthcare providers to address IPV issues

Participants identified challenges related to healthcare providers’ awareness to screen IPV. One mentioned:

“Doctors didn’t use to think about this issue before, … because we initially believed there wasn’t that much violence. We felt like most cases were relatively acceptable. However, as we collected more data later on, … we realized that emotional abuse, such as mockery or belittling, and practices like manipulative behavior can result in more terrifying harm than physical violence.”(ID09, Associate chief physician)

“Doctors didn’t use to think about this issue before, … because we initially believed there wasn’t that much violence. We felt like most cases were relatively acceptable. Discussion

Clinical settings have been found to be key locations for identifying IPV in HIC context, whereas fertility treatment clinics in particular may offer opportunities to contribute to the screening of IPV against women with infertility. While our study findings suggest the potential for establishing IPV screening procedures within the current healthcare system, several contextual barriers remain, including incompatibility with clinical workflow, venue-specific constraints that limit privacy, and limited capacity within the healthcare workforce to respond to IPV. Further implementation research is needed to assess the feasibility of specific IPV screening programs in ART settings. In this study, it was revealed that healthcare providers at ART clinics in China have encountered patients who were victims of IPV and can be mobilized to engage in IPV screening processes if certain conditions are met. Several contextualized strategies were proposed to facilitate patient disclosure and triage individuals of key concern for further investigation and intervention. Specifically, to streamline IPV screening into fertility treatment, healthcare workers suggested clarifying roles between cadres, capacity building to raise awareness among healthcare providers, and developing an IPV screening protocol. These findings, taken together, provided initial directions for developing facility-based IPV screening programs and deepened our understanding on its feasibility and acceptability from the perspective of healthcare workers.

Shared features and unique opportunities for IPV screening in ART settings

Situating our findings in existing literature, we found that ART clinic, despite its distinct medical specialty, shared similar characteristics with perinatal care settings reported in other countries [19, 20, 43, 44]. Frequent clinician-patient interactions, built on trust, enabled patients to disclose private matters and struggles that would not otherwise be shared [43, 45]. Meanwhile, healthcare providers need additional training to competently perform regular routinized screening for IPV victimization among patients [19, 20, 43, 44]. When compared with other empirical studies on IPV screening during prenatal care of pregnant women, several barriers that emerged from our study demonstrate consistency with those elicited from midwives and prenatal care nurses in Norway [19, 20], Italy [43], and Turkey [44]. Furthermore, evidence converges on that reproductive health care services (including both ART and perinatal care) provide a relatively safe place built on trust that is conducive to IPV disclosure. Physical signs and patients’ behavioral clues during treatment were considered means to detect IPV cases in this study, aligning with findings from previous studies conducted in prenatal, primary care, OB/GYN, pediatrics, and emergency settings [23, 46].

Overall, this study contributes to the existing literature by providing evidence from a non-Western cultural context. In addition, it offers new insights for broad reproductive health care settings by highlighting interrupted fertility treatment as a potential indicator for IPV screening, based on the perspectives of healthcare providers. While this perception aligns with prior studies linking IPV with women’s poor mental health [47], which in turn affects ART success [48], no studies to date have explicitly connected treatment discontinuation with IPV risk as a potential actionable screening cue. However, this signal remains to be further investigated and should not be interpreted in isolation. Discontinuation of treatment may also stem from unrelated factors, such as financial hardship or treatment-related fatigue not linked to IPV. It is therefore essential to interpret these indicators in a holistic manner within each patient’s broader individual circumstances.

Potential tools for IPV screening in ART settings

Next, specific approaches were recommended by healthcare providers, including self-administered tool. Self-administered questionnaire was proposed by several participants as a means to detect IPV while reducing the impact from patients’ partners and ensuring safety and privacy of patients during IPV disclosure. Previous studies conducted in Canada [49] and the United States [50] showed that patients largely preferred self-administered or self-report screening tools, compared to face-to-face questioning by healthcare providers about spousal abuse. Conversely, a study conducted in Uganda reported that such self-report IPV screening tools were not commonly used and may not be feasible in medical practice due to low levels of literacy [18]. Therefore, the utility of self-administered tool for IPV screening largely depends on literacy level and demographic composition of the patient groups. While it offers convenience and usability to certain patient groups, it may not comprehensively capture all women at risk. According to the World Bank, the female literacy rate in China was 95% in 2020 [51], among the highest in the world, rendering it feasible to use self-administered questionnaire in such situation. Nevertheless, in our study, healthcare providers in Ningxia and Anhui mentioned that a small number of patients from remote areas were illiterate, and that foreign patients had communication barriers during ART treatment. These findings highlight the importance of considering patients who may face literacy or language challenges when implementing future IPV screening programs to ensure inclusivity.

Our study also revealed healthcare providers’ perception that self-administered screening tools could enhance anonymity and help circumvent the challenges of in-person conversation, thereby potentially facilitating IPV disclosure. This finding was corroborated by a randomized controlled trial in Japan that self-administered questionnaire method identified more abused women as compared to in-person interview in prenatal setting [52]. Similar to pregnant women, patients undergoing ART treatment commonly attend ART hospital with their family members, therefore, privacy should be guaranteed.

Challenges to implementing IPV screening in ART settings

A range of challenges to implementing IPV screening at ART clinic settings emerged in our study, such as time constraints, absence of clinic-level protocols, lack of facility-wide privacy, and lack of incentives. These findings were consistent with evidence from healthcare providers across different clinical settings [18, 21–23, 34, 35]. For example, studies conducted in antenatal care settings in Uganda [18] and general reproductive health clinics in the United States [21, 22] have reported similar systemic obstacles, such as limited time during consultations and infrastructural constraints that compromise patient privacy. Likewise, the lack of standardized screening tools and institutional support has been a common barrier across multiple healthcare contexts [23]. Task sharing for IPV screening between doctors and nurses was mentioned as a primary concern in our study, while similarly more professionals, including social workers and health educators, were suggested to be involved in screening in one study conducted in reproductive health clinics also in the United States [22]. Therefore, clearly delineating roles and responsibilities for IPV screening is essential for facility-based implementation. Based on findings from our study and previous evidence, we concluded that the responsibility of IPV screening should not be taken solely by healthcare providers, but should be shared across various key stakeholder sectors and professions at health facilities.

Lack of awareness and inadequate IPV training should also be addressed among healthcare workers. The need for and effectiveness of training on IPV are evident in a previous quantitative study which showed that reproductive endocrinology and infertility physicians appeared to underestimate the prevalence of IPV in their practices, and that those clinicians with a history of training on IPV tended to report fewer perceived barriers to IPV screening [21]. Specifically, training reduced perceived barriers such as time constraints. Therefore, training on IPV screening should be provided to healthcare providers at ART settings to systematically enhance their perceived efficacy of IPV screening.

IPV disclosure was also shaped by cultural norms, which influenced the effectiveness of IPV screening [18, 53, 54]. The risk and vulnerability for IPV can arise from gender inequity where women may experience restriction at multiple levels of the social ecology, including dyadic, community, and structural levels [55]. In some cultural contexts, including China, infertility is often viewed as a woman’s failure and a source of shame for her family. Sociocultural expectations around fertility, rooted in gendered norms that equate a woman’s value with her ability to bear children, can intensify women’s emotional vulnerability and dependency on their partners. Moreover, traditional norms that discourage discussing private family matters in public settings further inhibit disclosure. Taken together, cultural perception of infertility, which intersects with gendered expectations around childbearing, reinforces women’s vulnerability to IPV. These intersecting layers of social disadvantage made women fear stigmatization and tended to blame themselves for the abuse, hence refusing to disclose their IPV experience. Although violence perpetrated by women against men due to infertility was reported by healthcare providers in some cases, participants widely reported that the current situation persists whereby women were more susceptible to IPV. Therefore, IPV screening should be implemented in tandem with other interventions at multiple socio-ecological levels to change attitudes towards infertility, de-stigmatize fertility treatment, and create a supportive environment to advance individuals’ rights and reproductive health.

Limitations and strengths

Study findings should be considered with several limitations. First, most interviews were conducted online instead of in-person due in part to travel restrictions during the COVID-19 lockdown. Online interviews generally face challenges in terms of rapport building. To mitigate this shortcoming, we identified key personnel at the selected hospitals to build connections and trust before the interviews. Second, this study did not include triangulating views from other key informants, such as policymakers whose decisions may directly or indirectly impact the feasibility of IPV screening at ART clinics. However, we have captured the viewpoints of frontline healthcare workers, and study findings will be disseminated to inform relevant policymaking. Third, regarding the recruitment strategy, while the affiliation with CNOGRN facilitated logistical coordination and access to study sites, we acknowledge the potential selection bias, as participating institutions may have exhibited greater research engagement or clinical capacity compared to non-affiliated sites. This potential bias was partially mitigated by purposively selecting clinics from regions of various economic development level, and by including providers from diverse cadres and with different levels of seniority.

Nevertheless, this study provided an initial foundation for developing IPV screening mechanisms in reproductive health facilities in China, drawing on insights from ART healthcare providers. Additionally, our study covered several geographic regions in China, increasing the representativeness of the sample and enhancing its relevance to diverse settings in China. The shared knowledge and common challenges pertaining to IPV screening across ART clinics should have certain value in terms of transferability to the rest of China and similar contexts abroad.

Conclusions

Healthcare providers at ART clinics have commonly encountered IPV victims among infertile patients and suggested acceptable strategies to implement IPV screening during ART treatment and care. Implementing IPV screening at ART clinics should focus on addressing the incompatibility between clinical workflow and routinization of IPV screening, as well as increasing capacity of healthcare providers to participate in IPV screening. Future research should build on these findings by developing and piloting appropriate IPV screening interventions within ART settings. Such implementation research can help assess the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of tailored screening strategies and contribute to refining best practices for implementation in reproductive health contexts.
