Artificial intelligence‐assisted detection of epileptic spasms using electroencephalographic–video analysis
Authors: Lin Wan, Nan Lin, Wen Wang, Zi Liang, Yisu Dong, Haibo He, Jian Chen, Yuying Fan, Tong Liu, Hongjie Wang, Weike Cheng, Guangshuang Lu, Juan Wang, Bo Zhang, Qiang Lu, Guang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study was undertaken to develop and validate an artificial intelligence (AI) diagnostic tool using hybrid electroencephalographic (EEG)–video signals for automatic epileptic spasms (ES) detection.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study with internal cross‐validation and multicenter external validation was conducted from July 2022 to May 2025. It included 252 patients with ES from Chinese PLA General Hospital and 60 from three other medical centers. We developed a multimodal fusion approach combining video and electrophysiological signals. All EEG data were segmented into continuous 4‐s pages. The internal cohort consisted of 212 patients (723.4 h video‐EEG, 7348 ES, and 643 215 non‐ES segments). Clinical validation involved 100 patients across four datasets (212.2 h, 5709 ES and 185 207 non‐ES segments) plus 78 controls without ES (218.1 h, 196 249 segments, used for false alarm rate analysis). Primary outcomes included sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, and F1 score versus electroencephalographer interpretation.ResultsIn internal cross‐validation, the hybrid EEG–video model achieved superior performance compared to current EEG‐only models (area under the precision–recall curve = .7334, 95% confidence interval [CI] = .7118–.7539, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve = .9820, 95% CI = .9782–.9856). In the clinical validation dataset, the model demonstrated diagnostic sensitivity (.735, 95% CI = .673–.822) and specificity (.995, 95% CI = .992–.996) comparable to experienced electroencephalographers. With AI assistance, all electroencephalographers showed improved sensitivity trends, with one rater showing a marked improvement from .620 (95% CI = .532–.699) to .792 (95% CI = .757–.821). Notably, specificity did not decline significantly for any of the raters. In samples containing subtle ES, machine‐assisted recognition significantly improved sensitivity by 16%–21% for all electroencephalographers (p < .01). The model maintained low false alarm rates (.16‰) across different patient populations including healthy controls and epilepsy patients without ES.SignificanceThis AI diagnostic tool achieves clinical performance comparable to senior electroencephalographers when applied independently for ES detection, with greater robustness in detecting subtle ES. When used collaboratively with clinicians, it could enhance diagnostic sensitivity while maintaining high specificity. The technology addresses critical diagnostic challenges in pediatric epilepsy care and shows promise to reduce health care inequities in resource‐limited settings lacking specialized expertise.Keywords: artificial intelligence, automatic detection, epileptic spasm, multimodal analysis, video‐EEG monitoring

INTRODUCTION

Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS) is a severe developmental epileptic encephalopathy that may lead to poor neurodevelopmental outcomes. 1 , 2 Early diagnosis and timely intervention can potentially improve both short‐term and long‐term outcomes. Electroencephalography (EEG) remains an essential diagnostic tool for epilepsy. 3 Despite these well‐defined motor features, 4 the EEG patterns associated with epileptic spasms (ES) are highly heterogeneous in clinical practice. 5 ES recognition is not always straightforward. Multiple studies have demonstrated that even experienced health care professionals may fail to correctly identify ES, particularly subtle ES manifesting only as facial movements, 6 , 7 , 8 leading to diagnostic delays even after EEG examination. 9 This delay in diagnosis potentially reduces the likelihood of short‐term response and increases the risk of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes while also increasing health care costs and family financial burden. 10 Furthermore, diagnostic capabilities for ES vary significantly across different regions and even among different ethnic groups within the same region. 11 , 12 , 13 Notably, our previous study has demonstrated that the performance of electroencephalographers in ES detection is suboptimal. 14

Previous research has demonstrated the potential of artificial intelligence (AI) in epilepsy diagnosis. 15 Recent advances in biosignal processing methods have demonstrated great potential for automatic recognition models. Pei et al. 16 proposed an inference correction method that leverages dependencies within EEG series to improve classification accuracy. Li et al. 17 developed an ensemble voting classifier integrating multiple machine learning algorithms with Shapley Additive Explanations‐based interpretability for bioelectrical signal analysis. Shi and Liu 18 introduced B2‐ViT Net, a broad vision transformer network with broad attention mechanism that captures global spatial interactions between channels and models long‐range temporal dependencies. For IESS, AI technologies have been widely applied for predicting ES presence and automatic ES recognition. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 However, most of these studies were limited by small sample sizes and preselection of low‐noise data for modeling and validation, which may not reflect real clinical scenarios with inevitable artifacts from chewing, eye movements, and involuntary muscle activity. These limitations restrict their clinical applicability, particularly as these models 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 have never been compared against experienced electroencephalographers.

Recent studies have shown that EEG–electrocardiographic (ECG) fusion significantly outperforms unimodal EEG in seizure recognition, 27 , 28 underscoring the value of multimodal approaches. Compared to ECG, video offers richer clinical information by capturing seizure semiology, and its integration with EEG has demonstrated particular value in pediatric epilepsy diagnosis. 29 Given the importance of ES recognition and current identification challenges, particularly in resource‐limited regions, 13 developing a video‐EEG‐based clinical decision support tool to assist electroencephalographers has significant importance.

In this study, we developed an automatic ES detection system as confirmatory support tool based on multimodal fusion of video and electroencephalographic signals. The model achieved recognition performance comparable to that of senior human electroencephalographers. Furthermore, when used as a diagnostic assistance tool, the system could improve the sensitivity of human electroencephalographers in ES detection.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

In this retrospective study, video‐EEG data were collected from patients with ES and children without ES or characteristic EEG patterns who visited the First Medical Center of the People's Liberation Army (PLA) General Hospital from July 2022 to May 2025. Informed consent for participation was obtained from the patients' parents. The data were deidentified and protected by privacy safeguards. Ethical approval was granted by the ethics committee of the First Medical Center of PLA General Hospital (reference: S2023‐748‐01). The overall study design workflow is shown in Figure 1. All details can be found in the Appendix Protocol . In summary, all video‐EEG data were divided into nonoverlapping consecutive 4‐s windows. These windows were classified into ES pages and non‐ES pages based on whether ES onset occurred within the page range. The model aims to accurately identify ES pages.

Study flow. AI, artificial intelligence; EEG, electroencephalographic; ES, epileptic spasms; HIDM, human‐independent detecting module; MAHDM, machine‐assisted human‐driven module. FAR, false alarm rates; FC, fully connected; MLP, multilayer perceptrons; VS, versus.

Study cohort

The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients who were diagnosed with ES according to the International League Against Epilepsy diagnostic criteria, 4 specifically polyphasic slow waves followed by amplitude suppression and fast activity, with concurrent ES manifestations, including classic and subtle type. The exclusion criterion was patients who declined to provide data for research due to privacy or other reasons. The final study cohort confirmation (including 78 children without ES onset) was conducted by two electroencephalographers (J.C. and Y.F.) with >10 years of experience in pediatric epilepsy who independently reviewed >1500 video‐EEG examinations for ES. In cases of disagreement, a senior electroencephalographer (W.W.) with >15 years of experience and >3000 ES patient EEG reviews provided final arbitration.

Construction and validation of the model

This study established an automated ES recognition system based on multimodal fusion. Video‐EEG data were used to create an authoritative dataset annotated by electroencephalographers.

The system integrates EEG signal and video data features. EEG signals underwent 500‐Hz resampling and Butterworth filtering preprocessing, with EfficientNet‐B0 30 extracting 1024‐dimensional feature vectors (Figure 1A); video signals utilized the YOLOv5‐Patient model 31 for patient detection combined with Sapiens 32 model key point detection to generate 1024‐dimensional feature vectors (Figure 1B).

Multimodal feature fusion employed feature concatenation to form 2048‐dimensional vectors, with multilayer perceptrons (MLP) classifier performing binary classification using Sigmoid activation function (Figure 1C). The model was trained using the PyTorch framework with AdamW optimizer, comparing several mainstream baseline models for comprehensive evaluation as internal validation: EEGNet, 33 EEGNet‐Transformer, 34 CNN‐Transformer, 34 ResBiLSTM, 35 CBraMod, 36 and EEG‐DINO 37 ; all the models used three random seeds (42, 43, 44) for multiple experiments. Considering ES and non‐ES sample imbalance (1:50–1:100), we ensured that the specificity was above .99 and used the threshold corresponding to the maximum F1 score on the precision–recall (PR) curve for clinical application verification (Figure 1C).

Clinical validation mainly includes three aspects: (1) comparison of the ability between the model and senior electroencephalographers in ES recognition (using data from 20 ES patients from our hospital as internal human‐independent detecting module [HIDM] group and 30 patients from the other three hospitals as external HIDM group); (2) whether the recognition ability of senior electroencephalographers is improved with model assistance (using data from 20 ES patients from our hospital and 30 from the other three hospitals, conducted by the internal and external machine‐assisted human‐driven module [MAHDM] groups, respectively); and (3) false positive rate of the model in patients without ES (using data from 78 children without ES onset, 19 from our hospital, and 59 from the others; Figure 1D).

Multiple methods, including Grad‐CAM 38 visualization techniques, will be employed to explore the model's interpretability. Additionally, potential causes of false positives and false negatives generated during the model's identification process will be categorized and analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Model performance in the internal validation was evaluated using multiple metrics, including the area under the PR curve (PR‐AUC) and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC‐AUC). Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and precision were calculated for clinical application validation. False alarm rates were calculated only for the non‐ES group.

RESULTS

Derivation dataset characteristics

The final dataset comprised 212 patients with confirmed IESS, contributing 723.4 h of video‐EEG recordings. The cohort included 7348 manually annotated ES segments and 643 215 non‐ES segments. Among the patients, 83 had focal ES, and 129 had generalized ES. A total of 136 patients had typical ES, four had only subtle ES, and 72 had both. Additionally, 31 patients experienced clustered ES, 61 had isolated ES, and 120 had both. Furthermore, 34 patients had other seizure types in addition to ES (Appendix Table S1).

Model performance in internal validation

The model training set and validation set included 169 and 43 patients, respectively. The number of ES segments was 6346 in the training set and 1502 in the validation set, whereas the number of non‐ES segments was 513 113 and 130 102, respectively. The hybrid model achieved superior performance, with a PR‐AUC of .7334 (95% confidence interval [CI] = .7118–.7539) and an ROC‐AUC of .9820 (95% CI = .9782–.9856) at the individual page level, and a PR‐AUC of .7274 (95% CI = .6387–.7966) and an ROC‐AUC of .9819 (95% CI = .9719–.9891) at the patient level, significantly outperforming the best baseline models (Figure 2). Considering the extreme data imbalance, we evaluated the impact of class balancing using seizure‐to‐nonseizure ratios of 1:1, 1:10, and the natural clinical prevalence. Class balancing significantly upgraded model performance, with PR‐AUC increasing from .7334 to .9837 at the page level, and similar improvements were observed at the patient level (Figures S1 and S2). This data balancing method may overestimate model performance. Therefore, we did not use this strategy. We therefore trained on the imbalanced dataset and used the PR curve to guide threshold selection. Systematic evaluation across multiple thresholds revealed optimal performance at .20, yielding a sensitivity of .7443 (95% CI = .7227–.7648), specificity of .9952 (95% CI = .9948–.9955), and F1 score of .6892 (95% CI = .6723–.7058; Appendix Table S2). This threshold was applied to all subsequent validation experiments.

Clinical validation dataset characteristics

The 100 children in the independent validation dataset (40 from our hospital [internal] and 60 from three other hospitals [external]) had a total monitoring duration of 212.2 h (internal: 92.5 h, external: 119.7 h), with 5709 ES (internal: 2290, external: 3419), including 4915 typical ES (internal: 2056, external: 2859) and 794 subtle spasms (internal: 234, external: 560). Among them, 49 patients had typical ES, and 51 had both subtle and typical ES. Twenty‐seven had focal ES attacks, and 73 had generalized attacks. Thirteen patients had clustered ES, 22 had isolated ES, and 65 had both. Eighty‐eight patients had only ES, whereas 12 had other seizure types in addition to ES attacks. There were 5709 ES segments and 185 207 non‐ES segments. The subgroup classification of the validation dataset maintained a relative balance between the HIDM and MAHDM groups in both internal and external cohorts (see Appendix Table S1).

Performance comparison between the model and human electroencephalographers in the validation set

In the HIDM dataset, the model demonstrated sensitivity (.735, 95% CI = .673–.822) comparable to that of electroencephalographers, and even outperformed Rater 2. Both the model (.995, 95% CI = .992–.996) and electroencephalographers maintained excellent specificity; although electroencephalographers achieved slightly higher specificity, the difference was only approximately .003. No significant difference was observed between the model (.986, 95% CI = .982–.991) and electroencephalographers in terms of accuracy. Electroencephalographers demonstrated superior precision, indicating the model's (.730, 95% CI = .679–.800) relatively weak ability to avoid false positive detections. However, the F1 score (.755, 95% CI = .679–.795), which balances sensitivity and precision, was comparable between the model and electroencephalographers, suggesting equivalent overall performance (Figure 3). A heatmap illustrating the performance of all four raters, including the model, across all HIDM samples is presented in Figure S3.

When samples were stratified by the presence or absence of subtle ES, electroencephalographers exhibited a marked decline in sensitivity: Rater 1 decreased from .873 to .531 (p < .001), Rater 2 showed a declining trend from .737 to .557, and Rater 3 decreased from .875 to .667 (p < .001). In contrast, the model maintained relatively consistent performance (from .816 to .732, p = .055), demonstrating greater robustness to subtle seizure patterns (Figure S4).

For page‐level recognition, the model achieved sensitivities of .757 (95% CI = .741–.774), .783 (95% CI = .767–.799), and .603 (95% CI = .556–.650) for all, typical, and subtle epileptic spasms, respectively (McNemar test; see Figure 4), significantly outperforming electroencephalographers (p < .01).

Performance comparison between the human electroencephalographers with/without AI assistance

Compared with the HIDM dataset, machine‐assisted recognition significantly improved the sensitivity of Rater 2 (.792, 95% CI = .757–.821 vs. .620, 95% CI = .532–.699, p = .009), and the other two electroencephalographers also showed an increasing trend. No significant differences were observed in other parameters, although all three raters demonstrated a consistent trend toward improved F1 scores (Figure 6).

When samples were stratified by the presence or absence of subtle ES, machine‐assisted recognition significantly improved the sensitivity of all electroencephalographers by 16%–21% in samples containing subtle ES (Rater 1: .738 vs. .531, Rater 2: .768 vs. .557, Rater 3: .830 vs. .667, p < .01), as well as F1 scores by 14%–18% (Rater 1: .806 vs. .623, Rater 2: .797 vs. .655, Rater 3: .818 vs. .677, p < .05; Figure S5). In samples without subtle ES, no significant differences were observed between machine‐assisted and unassisted performance (Figure S6).

Additionally, compared with the HIDM dataset, the three electroencephalographers showed improved consistency (.504 vs. .667) in annotating ES in the MAHDM dataset, with particularly notable improvement in subtle ES recognition (.423 vs. .697; see Appendix Figure S7).

We further evaluated whether machine assistance could effectively reduce recognition time. No significant difference in reading time was observed among the three raters (p > .05; Figure S8). After adjusting for the presence of subtle ES, total number of pages, total number of ES pages, and number of subtle ES pages, no significant difference was still observed (see Appendix Figure S8).

Performance of the model's false alarm rates

In a validation cohort of 78 children without ES (28 healthy infants, 13 infants diagnosed with epilepsy but without seizure onset, 10 infants with focal seizures, and 27 children diagnosed with IESS but without ES attacks), a total of 218.1 h of monitoring yielded 196 249 pages (Appendix Table S1). The model produced false alarms in 10 patients (two from the internal cohort and eight from the external cohort), of whom seven were diagnosed with IESS without ES onset, with a total of 41 false alarms, corresponding to false alarm rates ranging from .034% to .985% (Figure S9). For all pages, the overall false alarm rate was .016% (Figure S9). A detailed review of all mislabeled pages revealed that false alarm pages from seven patients showed false alarms triggered by movements occurring within 1–2 s before or after the appearance of ES patterns (a typical false alarm incident video can be found in Appendix Video S4). The remaining three patients had rapid limb movements (resembling typical ES) and electrical artifact signals generated by these movements in the video. These artifacts resembled the ictal patterns of ES (a typical false alarm incident video can be found in Appendix Videos [Link], [Link], [Link]).

Model interpretability and calibration analysis

In the processing of electrophysiological signal data, attention mechanism analysis revealed that the model's attention weights were predominantly concentrated on regions exhibiting ES characteristic discharge patterns. Across low‐, mid‐, and high‐level feature attention distributions, the model consistently focused on these ES discharge pattern regions. When temporal shifts occurred in the input signals, the model's attention correspondingly shifted, dynamically tracking the spatiotemporal changes in discharge regions and demonstrating robust temporal adaptability (see Appendix Figure S10). Additionally, we demonstrated that key point‐based recognition can effectively quantify and analyze patients' limb movements and facial movement patterns (see Appendix Figure S11). The calibration curve demonstrated that the observed event rates (blue curve) closely aligned with the ideal calibration line (black dashed line), with most data points falling within the 95% CI. The model achieved a very low Brier score (Brier = .0054, 95% CI = .0051–.0057), indicating excellent agreement between machine‐annotated ES and actual outcomes (see Appendix Figure S12).

DISCUSSION

This study presents the first comprehensive evaluation of a hybrid EEG–video AI system for ES detection that has been validated against expert electroencephalographers in real clinical settings. Our findings demonstrate that the AI system achieves diagnostic performance comparable to that of experienced specialists (with similar sensitivity and specificity). The maintained high specificity (>99%) ensures that the system does not generate excessive false alarms, which is crucial for clinical acceptance. This improvement could translate to earlier treatment initiation and potentially better outcomes for patients with IESS. Meanwhile, it provides substantial assistance to human interpreters. The demonstrated improvement in sensitivity and F1 score when senior electroencephalographers used AI assistance represents a clinically meaningful enhancement, particularly for detecting subtle ES that are often missed in routine practice. Although these improvements may seem limited, they are still meaningful for these experienced electroencephalographers, as the closer you get to the top, the harder it is to climb. The sensitivity of our model may not demonstrate a clear advantage over previous studies; however, our results were validated on challenging real‐world clinical video‐EEG data and, for the first time, directly compared against human electroencephalographers, a rigorous benchmark not previously attempted. Differences in model architecture, sample size, and validation methodology between our study and prior research are summarized in the Appendix (see Appendix Table S3).

Beniczky and Ryvlin emphasized the need for continuous data, a sufficient sample size, independent validation sets, and other factors in the field of automatic epilepsy recognition. 39 To date, almost all studies focusing on automatic ES recognition suffer from two major problems: relatively small sample sizes—all these studies had very small sample sizes, and none of them included more than 1000 ES events—and data selection bias, focusing on clusters of typical ES (without difficult‐to‐identify isolated or subtle ES) 21 , 22 , 23 , 25 or using data with minimal initial interference (such as artificially selected low‐noise data or sleep data). 24 In this study, we utilized video‐EEG recordings from more than 250 children with ES, encompassing nearly 10 000 ES episodes; ES being a rare syndrome in infancy (with an incidence rate of only approximately 3 per 1000), 40 this represents the largest video‐EEG dataset for this condition to date. Additionally, in the model establishment, internal validation, and external independent dataset validation stages, we did not perform any selection of the data. All the data are continuous detection data, which means that various interferences in the entire monitoring process are considered. This allows full consideration of various influencing conditions that the model may encounter in actual clinical use, enabling the model to obtain stable generalization capabilities.

To determine whether the high efficacy achieved during internal validation can be reproduced on different datasets, greater robustness clearly means a greater possibility of entering clinical application. 41 There were two studies that performed external validation. One used the CHI‐MIT public dataset, which seem unsuitable, as none appeared to have ES as their seizure type (ES generally last less than 4 s per episode), and these children were older, with a median age of 10 years (range = 6–13), whereas ES generally occur in children under 2 years of age. 21 The other one used only one ES patient's video for independent validation, lacking sufficient numbers of ES confirmed by the gold standard for validation, which obviously currently limits its potential applicability as a diagnostic tool. 26 In our study, we observed that our model maintained relatively stable performance during both the internal and external validation processes. With performance comparable to that of human electroencephalographers, our model achieved human expert level, and as an assistive tool, it can significantly improve human electroencephalographers' accuracy, demonstrating clinical utility. Although one electroencephalographer showed a decrease in specificity and precision in the identification of all pages after using our model, there were no intergroup differences among individual patients. This may be attributed to the electroencephalographer's high recognition of the model's identification accuracy and the presence of a large number of electrographic‐only ES patterns in some patients.

Previous studies on ES identification have usually emphasized sensitivity, with a sensitivity of approximately 90%, and our model can achieve 91% sensitivity, whereas the specificity at this threshold was 95%. The latest international consensus guidelines on AI research in medicine mention that given the high‐risk nature of medical AI, human supervision is essential, and clinical utility and usability must be fully considered. 41 Considering the extremely imbalanced state of ES and non‐ES pages in actual clinical application scenarios (usually less than 1:50), we focused more on the comprehensive performance of sensitivity and precision and selected a threshold more suitable for clinical application scenarios. Our model showed excellent performance in terms of false positive rates, with an average false positive rate of less than 1% (this performance remained stable in an independent mixed sample dataset of 78 subjects without ES), meaning that the number of pages requiring additional review by human EEG technicians was greatly reduced, demonstrating its clinical usability.

Historically, the diagnosis of epileptic spasms has often been delayed. 42 , 43 , 44 Stéphane Auvin et al. reported that among 84 patients with misdiagnosed infantile epileptic spasms syndrome, the majority of consulted physicians (301/362, 83%) did not suggest any specific diagnosis. 42 Even today, this situation has not improved significantly, particularly in South Asian regions, where insufficient diagnostic capacity has led to treatment delays of more than 2 months. 13 , 44 The clinical utility and usability demonstrated by our model holds promise for addressing the regional imbalance issues faced in the diagnosis and treatment of this rare disease. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that even with such poor consistency, epilepsy professionals remain highly confident in their own results 45 , 46 ; our model holds promise to address this issue by improving consistency.

Given that current EEG training is usually insufficient, with gaps in exposure opportunities and training quality, leading many neurologists to be unable to independently interpret EEG readings, AI shows great potential in EEG result interpretation, highlighting the application value of AI in EEG education. 47 Although some studies have performed epileptic spasm prediction, suggesting that ES may occur within a certain period, 21 , 22 , 23 fundamentally, electroencephalographers need to be able to identify ES within this period. When human EEG technicians lack sufficient experience to complete this task, the role of prediction periods will obviously be limited. Our model can annotate a 4‐s ES page in real time. Moreover, because our model's recognition accuracy is sufficiently high, it not only can be used to identify ES in clinical work but also may play a teaching role in actual clinical applications. Some subtle ES are not easy to identify, and inexperienced human electroencephalographers may gain recognition experience from model‐annotated ES.

In our model, different threshold settings prioritize different capabilities. Considering clinical practicality, we sacrificed some sensitivity to ensure high specificity, positioning the model solely as an assistive recognition tool, with humans remaining the final arbiters. This approach aligns with perspectives expressed in previous reviews. However, depending on regional health care resources and application scenarios, different thresholds can provide electroencephalographers with more options. For instance, in resource‐limited settings, sensitivity may be prioritized at the expense of some specificity, enabling preliminary screening to identify patients potentially experiencing ES and facilitating referral to epilepsy‐center hospitals for definitive diagnosis.

Although this model shows promise in addressing the challenges of ES recognition, numerous obstacles may still exist in deploying it in clinical practice. Cross‐device variability represents a major challenge in clinical EEG machine learning applications. 48 Studies have shown that due to differences in amplifier characteristics, electrode configurations, and preprocessing pipelines, models trained on one device exhibit significant performance degradation when applied to other EEG systems. 49 Recent research has demonstrated that domain adaptation techniques can partially mitigate these effects; methods such as Euclidean alignment and Riemannian manifold approaches have shown promise. 50 , 51 Additionally, transfer learning through fine‐tuning models with small labeled datasets from target devices has proven effective in cross‐center studies. 52 These approaches hold promise for facilitating the generalization of the current model to other EEG acquisition platforms. Beyond these hardware‐related challenges concerning equipment and sampling settings, several other issues warrant consideration. Regional resource disparities may lead to fairness concerns in model accessibility; data leakage risks during patient data transfer and the “black box” nature of algorithms may raise patient privacy concerns and clinical trust issues; there are currently no clearly defined liability boundaries; and compliance regulations vary across regions. These challenges need to be addressed following the FUTURE‐AI framework 41 to facilitate trustworthy deployment of this AI model in clinical EEG workflows. Nevertheless, oversight by human electroencephalographers remains essential, as they bear the responsibility for reviewing the outputs of automated recognition models, a responsibility that may help mitigate potential automation bias.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all the data were collected during clinical EEG examinations, where occlusion from clothing or personnel was inevitable. We did not exclude such data, which may have caused some ES‐like pages to be misclassified as non‐ES. This might have reduced the model's recognition accuracy. However, because human electroencephalographers also rely on video footage for ES judgment, we believe that this conservative approach better simulates real‐world clinical scenarios. Second, although this study has conducted multicenter research for external validation, it still faces the problem of a single EEG device and has not yet been validated on other platforms. Therefore, its demonstrated performance may currently be limited to a single collection platform. The external validation cohort, although adequately powered, was relatively small and would benefit from larger multicenter validation. Although the false alarm rate in this study was impressively low, the sensitivity remained suboptimal. As an auxiliary diagnostic tool for clinical examinations, the model needs to improve sensitivity while maintaining the current false alarm rate. These issues will be addressed through multicenter and multiplatform data, and related work is currently underway. Additionally, the model's performance in patients with comorbid movement disorders or other epilepsy syndromes requires further investigation. Third, given the model's current capabilities, it should be used only as an auxiliary diagnostic tool. Users must possess basic electrophysiological knowledge, for example, to verify the model's annotations by examining concurrent video and electrical signal changes to ensure accuracy. Fourth, we did not conduct studies on the video‐only modality in the current work. Future research will explore this direction.

CONCLUSIONS

We developed and validated an AI‐assisted diagnostic tool that combines EEG and video analysis for automatic ES detection. The hybrid feature approach integrating different signal modalities may represent a viable method for automated ES detection.

The system demonstrated performance comparable to that of experienced electroencephalographers and significantly enhanced human diagnostic accuracy when used as a clinical decision support tool, particularly for patients with subtle ES. By improving recognition consistency, this technology shows promise for improving ES recognition both at specialized centers and in resource‐limited settings to alleviate the imbalance of medical resources across regions in ES identification, potentially leading to earlier treatment and better patient outcomes.
