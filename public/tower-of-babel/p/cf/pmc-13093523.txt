Occlusion and Vascular Malformation Solved by a Novel Recanalization Management in a Hemodialysis Patient: A Case Report
Authors: Guojun Hao, Aihua Xie, Bin Tang, Qiuling Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOur case report demonstrates that combined with percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and extraluminal recanalization, better blood flow was regained and the new vascular access was able to support regular hemodialysis. This may be another better choice for vascular access malformation for hemodialysis patients.Keywords: arteriovenous fistula, hemodialysis, percutaneous extraluminal recanalization, percutaneous transluminal angioplasty, vascular malformation

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is an urgent public health issue, accompanied by a rising prevalence of end‐stage renal disease (ESRD). Globally, there were 697.5 million CKD patients in 2017, with nearly one‐third in China and India [1]. In China during 2021–2022, CKD, impaired renal function, and proteinuria were reported to have considerable prevalence rate [2]. Additionally, the prevalence of CKD undergoing hemodialysis has risen in 2013–2017 and is predicted to escalate through 2025 [3]. Therefore, establishing an appropriate and effective dialysis access and maintaining its patency is an important measure to prolong the prognosis and ensure the quality of life of patients. Arteriovenous fistula (AVF) is the most preferred hemodialysis access. However, complications may happen, including thrombosis, central venous stenosis, and aneurysms [4]. A comprehensive clinical evaluation can uncover failed AVFs and possible associated complications, allowing for early examination and intervention to restore function, maintain access lifespan, and improve prognosis [5].

Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) is a well‐established treatment option for patients undergoing hemodialysis with vascular access stenosis, thrombosis, or occlusion [6, 7]. The earliest existing report of PTA in the treatment of AVFs was an observational study conducted by Saeed et al. [8] in 1987, which confirmed that PTA is safe and effective in the treatment of dialysis fistula stenosis with an overall success rate of 80%. Recent research has shown that improved patency rates occur when utilizing antiproliferative drug‐coated balloons to treat stenotic lesions in dialysis patients [9, 10, 11].

However, PTA may fail to solve the complicated and severe stenosis and vascular malformation. Therefore, additional treatment considerations must be taken into account when treating specific lesions. Percutaneous extraluminal recanalization is another choice applied in treating peripheral arterial disease, but few cases have been reported about treating vascular malformation in AVFs [12]. Compared to PTA, percutaneous extraluminal recanalization is innovatively presented in this case report. Similar to percutaneous intentional extraluminal recanalization in patients with chronic critical limb ischemia [12], it primarily involves creating a new extravascular pathway between two independent vessels, which may be a new management when an AVF with a single perforating venous return develops a blind end. The main procedural steps include puncturing through the blind end of the cephalic vein and exiting at the cephalic vein‐perforating vein turn, then puncturing into the brachial vein. After establishing the extravascular pathway from the cephalic vein to the brachial vein, dilation and stabilization with angioplasty of the extravascular channel, thereby literally establishing a new venous pathway. In this article, we reported a middle‐aged male on regular hemodialysis who developed complicated stenosis, occlusion, and vascular malformation in his right‐handed radiocephalic AVF. Satisfied outcome was obtained after percutaneous transluminal angioplasty, percutaneous extraluminal revascularization and medical therapy.

Case Presentation

Case History

A 55‐year‐old man with a previous medical history of polycystic kidney disease and ESRD had been hemodialysis‐dependent for 3.5 h 3 times per week for 5 years. He was administered erythropoietin 10,000 units subcutaneously per week for renal anemia, sodium bicarbonate for metabolic acidosis, and calcium carbonate for CKD mineral bone disease (CKD‐MBD) and received regular follow‐up. In addition, he suffered from hypertension and received surgery for cerebral aneurysm embolization. He has been smoking for over 20 years. Firstly, this patient underwent left‐handed radiocephalic arteriovenous graft surgery and received multiple percutaneous transluminal angioplasty surgeries for immuration or stenosis. However, the blood vessel was finally severely calcified and the AVG turned out dysfunctional, and the right‐handed radiocephalic arteriovenous fistula was constructed on October 6th, 2022 and became the vascular access during the regular hemodialysis.

On May 7th 2024, this patient was admitted into the hospital with the complaint of decreased tremor in the right‐handed arteriovenous fistula. There was no skin lesion, no swelling of the upper extremities, and no cyanosis or pain in the fingers. The physical examination at admission showed mild weak pulses of the cephalic vein in the right forearm AVF, but without tremor or bruit upon palpation or auscultation. He was diagnosed with stenosis and occlusion of the right forearm arteriovenous fistula. After admission, laboratory tests revealed an elevated serum creatinine level (SCr) of 1121 umol/L, blood urea nitrogen level (BUN) of 18.93 mmol/L, serum uric acid level (UA) of 482 umol/L, serum phosphate level of 2.89 mmol/L and intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH) of 411 pg/mL, but a lower calcium level of 1.95 mmol/L. Admission ultrasound (Affiniti 70 eL18‐4, Philips, Netherlands) showed severe, diffuse stenosis of the cephalic vein (Figure 1A). Usually, a radiocephalic AVF involves the direct anastomosis of the cephalic vein to the radial artery at the level of the wrist or distal forearm. The cephalic vein originates from the dorsal venous network on the radial side of the hand. It ascends along the radial aspect of the forearm, continuing into the anterolateral aspect of the arm within the deltopectoral groove. Its definitive termination occurs in the axillary vein usually [13]. However, in this patient, the proximal part of the fistula showed venous neointimal hyperplasia and stenosis with thrombotic accumulation. The cephalic vein of AVF formed a blind end at the branch of the vein (Figure 1B). The patient was diagnosed as having thrombosis of the right‐handed arteriovenous fistula and treated by percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and percutaneous extraluminal recanalization.

Methods

Under ultrasound guidance, we used a micropuncture needle to access the appropriate radial artery in the lower arm (Figure 2, point A). Next, 0.035 guidewire was then passed over the needle, and a 6–8‐Fr introducer (Terumo, Somerset, New York) was exchanged over the wire. The intravenous enoxaparin (60 U/kg, CLEXANE, French) was administered. However, due to severe stenosis and occlusion of cephalic vein, the guidewire failed to pass smoothly through the occlusion site. Therefore, the puncture site was changed to the occlusion lesion of the cephalic vein (Figure 2, point B). Likewise, the 0.035 guidewire was then passed over the needle, followed by a 6–8‐Fr introducer (Terumo, Somerset, New York) exchanging over the wire. The guidewire has been succeeded passing over the occlusion lesion and taking to the distal part of the radial artery. After that, the balloon (CONQUEST, BD, USA) was advanced into the occlusion and inflated to achieve complete effacement and revascularization.

The ultrasound images representing the procedure of the percutaneous angioplasty and extraluminal revascularization. (A) Point A represents the distal part of the radial artery; Point B represents the stenosis and occlusion lesion of the cephalic vein; Point C represents the blind end of the cephalic vein; Point D represents the proximal part of the brachial vein. (B) The arrow represents the blind end of the cephalic vein to the brachial vein.

Then, due to the poor condition of the patient's systemic vascular conditions and the malformation of a blind end at the proximal part of the cephalic vein, after sufficient assessment, we planned to reconstruct vascular access by percutaneous extraluminal recanalization. First, the puncture site was chosen in the blind end of the cephalic vein (Figure 2, point C) and taken over to the proximal part of the brachial vein (Figure 2, point D), then, a 0.035 guidewire was passed over the needle and taken to the axillary vein. Next, we chose the proximal site of the brachial vein (Figure 2, point D) as another puncture site, then the guidewire was passed and passed over to the distal part of the radial artery, and a 6–8‐Fr introducer (Terumo, Somerset, New York) was exchanged over the wire. Finally, a new vascular access connecting the cephalic vein and the brachial vein finally succee ded building (Figure 3B).

(A) Markedly decreased in length of severe, diffuse stenosis in the cephalic vein. (B) Improved blood flow of the cephalic vein after the surgery. BV, brachial vein; CV, Cephalic vein; RA, radial artery.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Ultrasound after PTA and extraluminal recanalization revealed a markedly decreased length of severe, diffuse stenosis in the vein (Figure 3A) and better blood flow with a speed of 230 mL/min and decreased venous pressure of 120 mmHg (Figure 4A). And the AVF was able to be used as vascular access to support dialysis. The patient was discharged the other day and returned to regular hemodialysis. After 2‐month follow‐up, adequate blood flow with a speed of 230–250 mL/min and venous pressure of 150–180 mmHg is present to support dialysis (Figure 4B).

Discussion

Stenosis and thrombosis secondary to stenosis are the most common complications of AVFs [14]. Venous stenosis is the most common reason for AVF loss after 3 months of use. Researches how that 83% of patients were treated with venous angioplasty for venous stenosis [15, 16]. Venous stenosis in AVFs most commonly occurs at the juxta‐anastomotic site (approximately 50%–60% of cases). The remaining stenosis develops more proximally in the venous system; among these, up to 20% involve the central veins [17, 18]. Angiography enables the assessment of stenosis severity, with a ≥ 50% narrowing in luminal diameter or an increase in the peak systolic velocity exceeding 2–3 times the baseline value generally indicating a clinically significant lesion [19]. Usually, the management of access failure employs both endovascular and open surgical strategies to address the underlying etiology. Endovascular management encompasses balloon angioplasty and stenting (including drug‐eluting stents). Open surgical revision is typically indicated for specific complications: recurrent stenosis, aneurysmal or pseudoaneurysmal degeneration, hemodialysis access‐induced distal ischemia, and infection [16].

PTA has already become the most widely used treatment to restore vascular function. The ultrasound‐guided PTA has been performed successfully in treating peripheral venous stenosis, thrombosis or occlusion [7]. Still, restenosis may occur after PTA, especially in older patients, comorbid with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and hypoalbuminemia [20]. Recent research revealed that elevated neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1 levels may be risk factors for restenosis after PTA surgery [21]. Our patient underwent several surgical treatments and long‐term anti‐platelet therapy for severe stenosis and occlusion in the vascular access, which contributed to hypertension, polycystic kidney disease, and poor vascular condition.

However, there are still many AVFs that are blind‐ended due to dysplasia, chronic atrophic occlusion, and other reasons. Traditional PTA does not allow for such lesions, and patients have to use other surgical modalities, including autologous vascular grafting, artificial vascular bypass, stent bridging, or even re‐establishing a new fistula. These surgical methods have obvious defects in terms of surgical trauma, cost burden, and waste of vascular resources. Percutaneous extraluminal recanalization is a minimally invasive surgical procedure usually performed to restore blood flow in occluded or stenosed arteries [22, 23]. Ultrasound‐guided extraluminal angioplasty breaks through the limitations of endovascular surgery and establishes an artificial extraluminal passage from the blind end of AVFs to the new vein through balloon dilation without the help of artificial blood vessels or stents, repairing the AVFs and reducing surgical costs for patients. Percutaneous extraluminal recanalization involves inserting a guidewire through an incision in the patient's skin and directing it towards the area of the blockage under imaging guidance. The guidewire is then used to create a new channel within the blocked area but outside the original lumen [12, 22]. In this case, we have detailed a case of severe occlusion and vascular malformation in the cephalic vein of AVF and combined PTA and percutaneous extraluminal recanalization as an innovative approach to regain vascular access. We have created a new channel between two separate veins. Following the creation of the new channel, typically, a balloon is inserted and inflated to expand the newly‐formed lumen, which made a big difference from the original percutaneous intraluminal recanalization. We have succeeded in providing a new pathway around the blind end in the vessel and restoring blood flow, which is able to support regular dialysis.

Besides, in maintaining AVF function to ensure treatment efficacy and quality of life for HD patients, a multidisciplinary approach is a critical strategy, especially in managing complex cases [16]. The nephrology team and vascular surgeons serve as core members in this model. The nephrology team is responsible for the daily monitoring of AVF, assessing dialysis adequacy, and acting as the frontline in identifying issues. The vascular surgeon, as the core specialist, oversees fistula creation, revision surgeries, open surgical interventions for complex stenosis or thrombosis, and makes final decisions regarding access strategy. Endovascular techniques play a significant role in the management of hemodialysis access and are often the preferred initial treatment approach. However, for certain complex cases, such as recalcitrant stenosis, hemodialysis access‐induced distal ischemia, and infection, early referral to vascular surgeons may still be necessary [24]. Treatment plans should be formulated based on individualized patient goals and require an effective multidisciplinary collaborative model to manage such complex situations.

Conclusion

Our case report demonstrates that combined with percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and extraluminal recanalization, better blood flow was regained and the new vascular access was able to support regular hemodialysis. Patients with hemodialysis fistulas may suffer from severe complications, including stenosis, thrombosis, or occlusion. Nephrologist must be able to identify these complications and deliver timely treatment.
