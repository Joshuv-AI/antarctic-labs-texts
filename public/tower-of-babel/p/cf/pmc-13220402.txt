Outcomes of emergency department and operating room preperitoneal pelvic packing in hemodynamically unstable severe pelvic fractures: a retrospective risk-adjusted observational study
Authors: Seong Hwa Lee, Jihoon T. Kim, Seongho Choi, Min Ae Keum, Eun Ji Lee, Hyeonjoon Lee, Kyu-Hyouck Kyoung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHemodynamically unstable patients with pelvic fractures have a high mortality rate. Most bleeding from pelvic fractures originates from venous and bony sources; therefore, direct compression of the pelvic wall can be effective, and preperitoneal pelvic packing (PPP) should be considered as a first-line intervention. PPP can be performed in both the emergency department (ED) and the operating room (OR). However, outcomes according to the location of PPP have not been clearly established. This study evaluated the outcomes of ED-PPP and OR-PPP based on the location where the surgery was performed.MethodsThis single-center, retrospective, observational study included patients who underwent PPP for pelvic fractures with an Abbreviated Injury Scale score of ≥ 4 from July 2015 to June 2025. Data were collected from a prospectively maintained trauma registry. Patients were categorized into ED-PPP and OR-PPP groups according to the site of PPP. Baseline characteristics, injury severity (Injury Severity Score, Revised Trauma Score, and Trauma and Injury Severity Score), and interventions, including ED thoracotomy, resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta, tranexamic acid administration, and time to PPP and transfusion, were compared. Mortality outcome measures were evaluated at 24 h, 7 days, and as overall in-hospital mortality and were risk-adjusted using W- and Z-statistic.ResultsFifty patients were included, with 17 and 33 in the ED-PPP and OR-PPP groups, respectively. Patients in the ED-PPP group were more critically ill at presentation, with lower systolic blood pressure, lower Glasgow Coma Scale scores, and a higher incidence of cardiac arrest before PPP. Time from scene to PPP (114.4 ± 68.6 vs. 284.3 ± 186.9 min) and to transfusion (84.5 ± 53.3 vs. 126.3 ± 72.5 min) was shorter in the ED-PPP group. The W-statistic demonstrated a greater number of actual survivors than expected in the ED-PPP and OR-PPP groups at 24 h (38.31 vs. 29.69) and 7 days (26.55 vs. 17.57), despite the high crude mortality rates (76.5% vs. 27.3%). Risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality showed no significant difference (W-statistic 3.02 vs. 5.45).ConclusionPPP is associated with improved early and intermediate survival. ED-PPP significantly reduces the time to hemorrhagic control and should be strongly considered as an important rescue damage control intervention for patients with life-threatening pelvic bleeding.

Background

Hemodynamically collapsed patients with unstable pelvic fractures have a high mortality rate, ranging from 25.5% to 46.5%, despite the implementation of early multidisciplinary treatment [1–3]. Pelvic bone fractures can cause severe hemorrhage from extensive bleeding foci, including the fracture site, lacerated surrounding tissues, and disrupted arteries and venous plexuses, predominantly within the retroperitoneal space [1, 4, 5]. Approximately 80–90% of bleeding associated with hemodynamically unstable pelvic fractures originates from venous and bony sources, in contrast to arterial bleeding, which accounts for only 10–20% [6, 7].

Therefore, direct compression of the pelvic wall can be an effective maneuver, and preperitoneal pelvic packing (PPP) should always be considered as a first-line modality. The time to hemorrhagic control is the most important factor influencing mortality, as it plays a critical role in reducing the time to achieve bleeding control [7, 8]. The First Italian Consensus Conference stated that PPP plays a critical role in unstable pelvic fractures with hemorrhagic shock and can be performed in both the emergency department (ED) and operating room (OR) [9]. However, the outcomes of PPP performed in the ED compared with those performed in the OR have not been clearly established.

Although it is commonly assumed that earlier hemorrhagic control leads to better survival, paradoxical results have been reported. Patients with greater illness severity or more severe injuries are more likely to receive early and aggressive interventions, thereby creating a bias in which cohorts undergoing earlier intensive treatment appear to have poorer outcomes, known as “confounding by severity” [10–12]. To adjust for this bias, the W- and Z-statistic have been developed and applied in trauma statistics; these measures are based on differences between the observed number of survivors and the estimated number of survivors [13, 14]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the outcomes of PPP according to the location of surgery, ED (ED-PPP) and OR (OR-PPP), using W- and Z-statistic.

Methods

Study population and patient data

This single-center, retrospective, observational study was conducted from July 2015 to June 2025 to evaluate the outcomes of ED-PPP and OR-PPP in patients with hemodynamically unstable pelvic fractures. Data were collected from a prospectively maintained trauma registry. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board, and informed consent was waived because no additional interventions were performed.

We included patients who underwent PPP for pelvic fractures with an Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) score ≥ 4. Patients < 16 years or with pelvic AIS < 4 were excluded. Those who died in the ED before transfer to the OR or trauma intensive care unit after PPP were also excluded to minimize confounding from nearly non-survivable injuries.

Patients were categorized into two groups (ED-PPP and OR-PPP) based on the site where PPP was performed. The initial status of the groups was analyzed using parameters including systolic blood pressure (SBP) and the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). Injury severity was assessed using the Revised Trauma Score (RTS), Trauma and Injury Severity Score (TRISS), Injury Severity Score (ISS), and the occurrence of cardiac arrest. Treatment factors that could influence outcomes, such as emergency department thoracotomy (EDT), resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA), administration of tranexamic acid, and the time from accident to PPP and transfusion, were also compared.

Outcome measures

Outcome measures were evaluated according to the timing of death: within 24 h for early mortality, within 7 days for intermediate mortality, and in-hospital mortality for late mortality. The impact of the location of PPP on survival was adjusted using the W- and Z-statistic, derived from the difference between the expected number of survivors and the actual number of survivors in the study population.

Methodologies for risk-adjustment

TRISS is a methodology used to predict the probability of survival (Ps) for an individual patient using a formula derived from the combination of the RTS, ISS, and age [13, 15]. The sum of Ps values for individual patients predicted by TRISS represents the expected number of survivors (E) in the study population.

The W-statistic quantifies the number of excess survivors per 100 patients by comparing the actual number of survivors (A) with the expected number (E) and serves as an indicator of institutional performance [13, 14].

Statistical analysis

Data for continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations or as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 24 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Patient enrollment and classification

During the study period, a total of 310 patients with severe pelvic fractures (pelvic AIS ≥ 4) were transported to the ED. PPP was performed in 55 patients; five patients who died at the ED were excluded from the analysis. Consequently, 50 patients who sustained blunt trauma were enrolled. Seventeen patients underwent PPP in the ED (ED-PPP) and 33 in the OR (OR-PPP) (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics and clinical presentation

As shown in Table 1, there were no significant differences between the ED-PPP and OR-PPP groups regarding age, sex, time from scene to ED, cardiac arrest before arrival, EDT, REBOA, or the administration of tranexamic acid.

However, the ED-PPP group was in a significantly more critical condition at initial presentation compared to the OR-PPP group in terms of SBP (66 ± 49 vs. 105 ± 37 mmHg, P = 0.028), GCS scores (4.7 ± 2.6 vs. 11.8 ± 4.1, P < 0.001), and the incidence of cardiac arrest before PPP (58.8% vs. 12.1%, P = 0.001). Surgery and transfusion were initiated more promptly in the ED-PPP group than in the OR-PPP group, specifically regarding time from scene to PPP (114.4 ± 68.6 vs. 284.3 ± 186.9 min, P < 0.001) and time from scene to transfusion (84.5 ± 53.3 vs. 126.3 ± 72.5 min, P = 0.035).

The ED-PPP group exhibited significantly higher injury severity in both anatomical and physiological aspects compared to the OR-PPP group (Table 2). ISS was significantly higher in the ED-PPP cohort (49.3 ± 11.3 vs. 40.3 ± 10.0, P = 0.016), alongside higher AIS for the abdomen and lumbar spine. Mortality in univariate and adjusted analysis

Twenty-four-hour outcomes: Mortality within 24 h was higher in the ED-PPP group than in the OR-PPP group (41.2% vs. 3.0%, P = 0.001). The W-statistic for ED-PPP and OR-PPP were 38.31 and 29.69, respectively, with statistical significance confirmed by the Z-statistic of 7.99 and 6.32.

Seven-day outcomes: Seven-day mortality was also higher in the ED-PPP group than in the OR-PPP group (52.9% vs. 15.2%, P = 0.008). The W-statistic demonstrated actual survival gain over the expected number of survivors in both the ED-PPP and OR-PPP groups (26.55 and 17.57, respectively), with clinically significant Z-statistic (5.54 and 3.74).

In-hospital outcomes: Overall in-hospital mortality was 44%, with crude mortality significantly higher in the ED-PPP group than in the OR-PPP group (76.5% vs. 27.3%, P = 0.001). The W-statistic showed a greater number of actual survivors than expected in both groups (3.02 vs. 5.45, respectively). However, these differences did not reach statistical significance, as the Z-statistic remained within the ± 1.96 range (0.63 and 1.16) (Table 3).

Discussion

Since Rotondo and Schwab described “damage control” in 1993 [17], intraperitoneal packing has been established as a temporizing, life-saving surgical technique for exsanguinating injuries. The modified technique of extraperitoneal packing for hemostasis in pelvic ring fractures was introduced by Pohlemann et al. [18]. Subsequently, Burlew et al. [19] and Tötterman et al. [20] established PPP as a life-saving damage control procedure for massive pelvic hemorrhage resulting from unstable pelvic fractures. The implementation of PPP has contributed to improved survival rates [8, 21] and is increasingly being used as the first hemostatic modality, often replacing angioembolization [8, 22–24].

Although PPP is widely recognized as an essential intervention and has been compared with other modalities, such as angioembolization, pelvic external fixation, and REBOA [25, 26], rapid hemorrhage control remains the most critical factor. The clinical imperative to minimize delays from CT scanning, angioembolization, and surgical intervention has led to the development of a hybrid ER system, which allows all procedures to be performed in a single suite adjacent to the ED [27, 28]. This novel system, pioneered and implemented in Japan, has shown favorable outcomes but is limited by high requirements for manpower and financial resources.

Institutions without access to such advanced resources often have no alternative but to omit conventional management pathways, such as ED-based operations. Damage control surgery in the ED has been accepted as a feasible rescue therapy; however, higher mortality has been reported in previous studies comparing ED laparotomy and OR laparotomy, despite shorter time to surgery [29–31]. Given the prevalence of selection bias in severe trauma and critical care, the interpretation of these outcomes should be performed using appropriate statistical adjustments that account for injury severity and physiologic factors. In this study, crude mortality was higher in the ED-PPP group across all time points.

However, risk-adjusted analyses using the W- and Z-statistic did not follow this trend. Both the ED-PPP and OR-PPP groups showed survival gains over the expected number of survivors at 24 h and 7 days, with statistical significance. Notably, the Z-statistic of ED-PPP at 24 h and 7 days were particularly remarkable considering the high crude mortality observed in this group. These findings suggest that ED-PPP plays a substantial life-saving role during the early phase of massive pelvic hemorrhage. Nevertheless, these results should be interpreted with caution. The cohort in the present study comprised high-risk patients, particularly in the ED-PPP group, and the TRISS model has been reported to overestimate survival among severely injured patients [14]. Although the W-statistic for ED-PPP appeared numerically higher than that for OR-PPP, this difference was not statistically significant and therefore should not be interpreted as evidence of superiority.

The findings for in-hospital mortality showed a different trend. Although crude mortality remained higher in the ED-PPP group, the W-statistic no longer demonstrated significant survival gain in either group. This suggests that PPP may not provide a benefit for late mortality. We hypothesize that the great injury severity and critical initial physiological status may have predisposed these patients to severe late complications, potentially increasing late mortality and attenuating the early survival benefit.

A noteworthy finding of the present study was the timing at which cardiac arrest occurred. There was no difference in the rate of cardiac arrest before ED arrival. However, the rate of cardiac arrest increased abruptly in the ED-PPP group before PPP initiation, although the ED-PPP group significantly shortened the time interval between ED arrival and PPP. This interval (36 min from ED arrival to PPP, corresponding to approximately 2 h from injury) may represent a critical time threshold at which the patient’s compensatory mechanisms become exhausted. Conversely, it may also represent the final therapeutic window in which last-resort intervention can be provided to achieve survival in patients with hemodynamically unstable pelvic fractures.

Although several studies have reported the availability of ED-PPP [9, 26, 32], large-scale studies evaluating the outcomes of ED-PPP versus OR-PPP have not yet been conducted. Reitano et al. [33] and Goldenshluger et al. [34] conducted comparative studies of ED-PPP and OR-PPP; however, these investigations focused primarily on the feasibility of ED-PPP, particularly regarding complications, such as surgical site infection and deep vein thrombosis, and did not demonstrate a difference in mortality. To our knowledge, the impact of ED-PPP on survival has not been clearly established due to a lack of relevant studies. In this context, although crude mortality appeared higher, evaluation based on the difference between expected and observed survival suggests a positive contribution of ED-PPP to survival. This finding supports the need for further investigation into the role of ED-PPP.

This study has some limitations. First, it is a retrospective study. Nevertheless, because the data were collected prospectively in a trauma registry and all patients transferred to our institution were comprehensively reviewed, the inherent limitations associated with a retrospective design may be mitigated. Second, a total of 50 patients is insufficient to provide conclusive recommendations. However, enrollment criteria were strictly confined to patients with pelvic AIS ≥ 4 to evaluate the feasibility of ED-PPP in a high-risk population, and the low incidence of this procedure was an inevitable limitation. Third, the indication for ED-PPP was not protocol-driven and depended on the surgeon’s discretion and the phase of the study period. Although treatment strategies were determined by attending trauma surgeons, in practice, they appeared to have limited alternatives, as many patients in the ED-PPP group were profoundly hypotensive, comatose, and at imminent risk of cardiac arrest unless immediate hemorrhage control was achieved. Particularly, the study period of 10 years was a time of rapid development for trauma centers due to governmental support. Various invasive rescue therapies, such as EDT, REBOA, and PPP, were adopted and spread across these trauma centers. Consequently, patients may not have received uniformly qualified treatment throughout the study period.

Pelvic injuries with an AIS ≥ 4 are often accompanied by damage to adjacent organs and tissues, including blood vessels, crushed muscles, and nerves crossing the sacroiliac joint. These injuries can lead patients to long-term complications and disability. We were curious about the difference between ED-PPP and OR-PPP. ED-PPP is performed under less optimal conditions than OR-PPP, including limitations in manpower, equipment, supplies, and even illumination. Moreover, surgeons’ hands may be more constrained and the operating environment more stressful in the ED. We speculate that these factors could affect long-term outcomes; however, this study did not evaluate this aspect, as the present analysis focused on survival outcomes. This represents another limitation of the study.

Damage control surgery, including PPP performed in the ED, faces challenges, such as infectious complications and the need for equipment and human resources, including anesthesiologists and OR nurses. Nonetheless, the short procedural time, minimal resource requirements, and potential for substantial hemostatic effect suggest that PPP may help mitigate these disadvantages.

Conclusion

Both ED-PPP and OR-PPP were associated with improved survival after risk-adjustment. Notably, the survival improvement observed in the ED-PPP group was achieved in an extremely high-risk population. In patients with pelvic fractures and life-threatening pelvic bleeding, particularly when immediate OR access is unavailable or transfer to the OR is not feasible, ED-PPP may be considered as a rescue damage control procedure, serving as an initial temporizing measure. Additionally, intensive care management and timely interventions to prevent complications should be emphasized to ensure that the early reduction in mortality translates into a reduction in late mortality. Large-scale multicenter studies are required to confirm the efficacy of ED-PPP.
