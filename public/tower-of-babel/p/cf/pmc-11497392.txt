Integrating Ayurveda and modern mainstream medicine
Authors: KP Mohanan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article is an attempt to understand the challenge of integrating the education provided by BAMS programs and MBBS programs, in order to initiate the process of integrating research and practice in Ayurveda and Modern Mainstream Medicine. The specific issues discussed in the article are framed within the broader context of the challenge of integrating any two bodies of knowledge, theories, or knowledge systems in education and research.

Preamble

What follows are my personal reflections on the challenge of integrating medical education in Ayurveda and Modern Mainstream Medicine, with the hope that such integration would shape research in the sciences of life and of human health, and the practice of healing human illness.

The trigger for these reflections lies in the following sources:

Article: “WHO’s first traditional summit splits opinions: The World Health Organization says the world-first summit will take an evidence-based approach — some are sceptical that much progress will be made.”

Nature 18 August 2023 [1].

Editorial: "Evidence-based traditional medicine for transforming global health and well-being." [2].

My primary thoughts here are about the specific challenge of integrating what is taught in BAMS programs (Bachelor of Ayurveda Medicine and Surgery) and in MBBS programs (Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery). From a general perspective, the challenge is one of integrating Ayurveda as a mainstream body of knowledge in Ancient India, and ‘modern medicine’ as a mainstream body of knowledge in the modern world. The former is concerned with the understanding of life, health and illness, and the latter with the current understanding of health and illness, with ‘biology’ covering the sciences of life. The core ideas in this article are embedded in the broader challenge of integrating bodies of knowledge, theories, and knowledge systems.

The meaning of the terms ‘knowledge system’ and ‘paradigm’, and even ‘ideology’, has considerable overlap. What lies at their core shapes the questions we ask, the nature of the explanations we legitimise, and hence guide the nature of education, research, and practice. I will use the term ‘knowledge system’ to refer to that core. What I wish to share are my thoughts on integrating the knowledge systems that shape the scope of The Journal of Ayurveda and Integrative Medicine and of the journal, Nature.

Integrating knowledge systems of life, health, and illness

The Nature article says:

“The expert panel that organized the summit published an editorial in the Journal of Ayurveda and Integrative Medicine where they contrasted the “reductionist” approach of Western medicine, which breaks down a phenomenon into its constituent parts, with traditional medicine, which stresses ‘the interconnectedness of mind, body and spirit’.”

The theme of integration appears in the JAIM editorial as well:

"The WHO is committed to exploring ways to integrate evidence-based traditional and complementary medicine services, particularly for primary health care, to achieve UHC and ensure healthy lives and well-being for all. The WHO global report on Traditional and Complementary Medicine 2019 and the WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy: 2014–2023 remain valuable resources for governments, system planners, and health practitioners. In this editorial, we use the broader concept involving IK and Traditional, Complementary, and Integrative Medicine (hereinafter referred to as TCIM) as a holistic system for planetary health and well-being."

I would like to go beyond the goals of TCIM and share my reflections on the challenges of integrating knowledge systems, theories, and knowledge fragments, with a focus on alternative/complementary systems of the ancient, modern mainstream, and modern non-mainstream medical practices, education, and research. I believe this is crucial for the success of the WHO project, and for convincing the skeptics that the summit (mentioned in the Nature article) will not “result in the often-before voiced platitudes and wishful thinking.”

Experiments, theories, and knowledge systems

Implicit in the design of experiments in science are theories that shape the design in terms of the questions they investigate, the methodology they use for looking for answers, and the kinds of conclusions they arrive at on the basis of the results. Researchers focussed solely on experiments, with little interest in theoretical issues, may not be aware of those implicit theories, even though they shape experimental research.

Implicit in research, whether in science, mathematics, philosophy, or alternative medical systems, are the axiomatic commitments to different sets of values, beliefs, and habits of thought that come under what we have called knowledge systems, which shape the modes of investigation, the nature of the theories, and ways of critically evaluating them. This would hold true in experimental research in the sciences of life, health and illness as well, whether ‘modern’ or ‘western’, and whether classified as mainstream or alternative.

Integrating theoretical frameworks

In pre-Daltonian chemistry, researchers investigated the nature of substances. In the context of health and illness, they investigated the efficacy of substances in healing. With the emergence of fundamental particles, atoms, inorganic molecules, and biomolecules, the attention shifted to the efficacy of molecules in healing, promoted by the pharma industry.

Any integration of ‘mainstream’ and ‘alternative’ systems of medicine would have to begin with what they share — the concept of substances. Given that substances like honey, blood, and saliva contain not only molecules but also cells, it would also require a careful investigation of the relation between the levels of molecules, of cells, and of substances. and their respective roles in healing.

To build a synergy between experimental inquiry within the ancient and modern systems of medicine, we need such a theory of molecules, cells, and substances, to engage with questions such as:

“Does garlic counter the effects of harmful bacteria, viruses, fungi?

“Does uncooked crushed garlic (as distinct from baked garlic cloves) counter the effects of multidrug-resistant strains of E.coli?”

“Does honey cause botulism that can be fatal to some infants?”

These questions are formulated in terms of the substances in plant parts (garlic), plant substances (crushed garlic), and substances that are animal products (honey). The current search in mainstream medicine for the ‘active ingredients’ of healing restricts the scope of our attention to the molecular level, with very little attention to interactions across the levels of cells, molecules, and atoms, and perhaps fundamental particles as well.

Take honey, a complex substance composed of many organic and inorganic compounds, including sugars, proteins, organic acids, pigments, and minerals. The following passage highlights the need investigate the medicinal properties of honey without necessarily reducing it to the search for the active ingredients in it:

“Honey use as a therapeutic agent is as old as human civilization itself. Prior to the appearance of present day drugs, honey was conventionally used for treating many diseases. At this instant, the modern research has proven the medicinal importance of honey. It has broad spectrum anti-biotic, anti-viral and anti-fungal activities. Honey prevents and kills microbes through different mechanism such as elevated pH and enzyme activities. Till now, no synthetic compound that works as anti-bacterial, anti-viral and anti-fungal drugs has been reported in honey yet it works against bacteria, viruses and fungi while no anti-protozoal activity has been reported. Potent anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-cancerous activities of honey have been reported. Honey is not only significant as anti-inflammatory drug that relieve inflammation but also protect liver by degenerative effects of synthetic anti-inflammatory drugs. This article reviews physico-chemical properties, traditional use of honey as medicine and mechanism of action of honey in the light of modern scientific medicinal knowledge.” [3].

Implicit in the issue of reducing a theory of substances to a theory of molecules is the more general issue of reductionism. Unnecessary disagreements on this issue can be avoided by clarifying the distinction between radical reductionism and moderate reductionism.

Moderate Reductionism: Many properties of the whole can be explained and predicted on the basis of properties of the parts, and the interaction among them.

Moderate reductionism recognises the value of the theory of molecules as a substrate for a theory of substances, a position that does not imply subscribing to radical reductionism. A keen awareness of this distinction is essential to any attempt to integrate Ayurveda with Modern Mainstream Medicine.

Integration would be impossible without an understanding of how the knowledge systems of academic inquiry shape theoretical inquiry and experimental inquiry. It follows that if curriculum designers in higher education wish to aim at integration at the level of research and practice, it would be useful to introduce undergraduate students to the different knowledge systems of academic inquiry in the past and in the present, such that when they become researchers and practitioners, they can strive to integrate what is currently transmitted as fragments of knowledge and application.

Foundation courses

A course on the conceptual foundations of the physical and biological sciences, offered in the first year of the program, would be a way of helping MBBS and BAMS students to grow into practitioners and researchers who strive towards the desired integration.

If we accept the aspiration outlined in the previous sections, we need to design and implement a set of compulsory foundation courses, not only for those who pursue degrees in BAMS and MBBS, but also the Bachelors students in mathematics, physical-biological-human sciences, humanities, engineering, law, and so on.

Such a foundation program would include a course on academic literacy to develop the capacity to read, understand, and critically evaluate books meant for educated nonspecialists. A good reading base for academic literacy would include books like Essays on Life itself (Rosen); What is Life? (Shrodinger); Evolution of Physics (by Einstein and Infeld); Animal Liberation (Peter Singer); Silent Spring (Rachel Carson); Concepts of Modern Mathematics (Ian Stewart), and Problems of Philosophy (Bertrand Russell).

Going beyond academic literacy, it would also include a course in transdisciplinary inquiry and critical thinking. This would help learners develop the capacity for higher order cognition, recommended by NEP 2020, and fleshed out in the NAAC white paper 2023.(http://naac.gov.in/images/docs/notification/Notification_regarding_WhitePaperpdf.pdf).

At the introductory level, students would learn the core abilities of academic inquiry and critical thinking, which include the ability.

At an advanced level, they would develop an understanding of concepts denoted by words like:

theory, law/constraint, paradigm, formalism, prediction, reasoning, logic, explanation, computation, calculation as a form of numerical computation, determinism, emergentism, radical reductionism, moderate reductionism, emergentism, mechanistic vs organismic, mechanism, correlation vs causation, nonlinearity, periodicity, complex adaptive systems, and so on.

Such foundation courses would generate an intellectual climate that responds to the skeptics who think that not much progress will be made by the WHO summit, mentioned in the Nature article. It will also respond to the fear that the summit “will result in the often-before voiced platitudes and wishful thinking.” Furthermore, the distinction between radical and moderate reductionism would resolve the apparent conflict between those who value reductionism (what they value is the moderate version), and those who reject reductionism (what they reject is the radical version).

Integrating the ancient and the modern mainstream

The passage quoted from the Nature article talks about traditional medicine, stressing the interconnectedness of mind, body and spirit. If this is not one of the “often-before voiced platitudes and wishful thinking,” we need to clearly articulate how the WHO project would stress the interconnectedness of body, mind, and spirit in education, research, and practice of the sciences of life, health, and illness.

Many ancient Indian systems of thought promise precisely what the WHO summit promises: the integration of the body, mind and spirit. But in order to deliver that promise through education and research, we need to understand the systems of thought in the ancient and modern mainstream medicine. For instance,

Do we expect our MBBS and BAMS graduates to be able to.

•give a clear exposition of the logical contradiction between the concept of Nirguna Brahman in Advaita Vedanta (a la Sankara) and the concepts of purusha and prakriti in the Saankhya system (e.g., what Krishna propounds to Arjuna on the battlefield)?

•take a position on whether or not the system of logic in modern medicine accepts catushkoti?

Why are such questions important? Because ancient treatises on Ayurveda were composed within a fairly extensive ontology, epistemology, and logic prevalent at that time. Carakasamhita, for instance, adopts the dualist ontology of Saankhya, and not the monistic ontology of Advaita or modern science. The integration of Ayurveda and modern mainstream medicine would demand that the logical contradiction between the two be resolved.

Likewise, the classical western epistemology from Plato till the twentieth century was that of totally certain, infallible knowledge. Its logic (whether syllogism or first order logic) was modelled on mathematical proofs. In contrast, the epistemology assumed in Carakasamhita was one that allows debates and self-correction in a fallibilistic epistemology (e.g., Nyaya logics and Buddhist logics) as in the case of defeasible logics modelled on scientific proofs.

If we want the WHO project to be successful in its goal of integrating Ancient and Modern sciences of life, health, and illness at the levels of practice, theory, and knowledge systems, issues of this kind need to be carefully investigated, and integrated in terms of experimental research, theoretical research, and knowledge systems.
