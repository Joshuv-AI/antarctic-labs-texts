Beta-blocker use and all-cause mortality in myocardial infarction survivors: A retrospective cohort study using propensity score methods in NHANES 1999 to 2018
Authors: Yingjie Sheng, Ying Han, Ye Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Beta-blockers are recommended for post-myocardial infarction (MI) care, but their long-term impact on all-cause mortality in real-world settings remains unclear. This study investigates the association using propensity score methods in a nationally representative cohort, with sensitivity analyses across age subgroups. This retrospective cohort study analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, restricted to 2308 MI survivors. Beta-blocker use was the exposure (0: nonuse, 1: use). All-cause mortality, assessed via mortality status (MORTSTAT), was the outcome, with follow-up time measured in person-months from interview (PERMTH_INT). Covariates included age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, poverty-income ratio, education, smoking, alcohol use, and body mass index. Cox proportional hazards regression, propensity score adjustment, matching, and inverse probability of treatment weighting were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Age-stratified sensitivity analyses were conducted for subgroups ≥30, ≥55, ≥60, ≥70, ≥75, and 30–80 years to assess the average treatment effect (ATE). Of 2308 participants (mean age 68.7 years, 65% male), 15% used β-blockers. The crude HR was 0.81 (95% CI: 0.68–0.96, P = .0178). After multivariable adjustment, the HR was 1.03 (95% CI: 0.86–1.24, P = .7296). Propensity score-matched ATE HR was 0.93 (95% CI: 0.70–1.23, P = .6029); inverse probability of treatment weighting ATE HR was 1.02 (95% CI: 0.83–1.24, P = .8786). Weighted Kaplan–Meier estimates showed modest but nonsignificant absolute risk reductions of 1.17% (95% CI: 0.2–2.1) at 5 years and 0.81% (95% CI: 0.1–1.5) at 10 years, as confidence intervals overlapped substantially with no statistical significance in the differences. Age-stratified analyses showed consistent null effects post-adjustment (e.g., ≥70 years adjusted HR 0.98, P = .8992). Subgroup analyses suggested potential benefits in females (HR 0.70, P = .0290), low-education, and low-income groups, though interactions were nonsignificant. Beta-blocker use was not associated with reduced all-cause mortality in MI survivors after confounder adjustment, with robust findings across age subgroups. These results support personalized therapy approaches rather than routine use. Limitations include potential residual confounding due to the absence of key clinical variables (e.g., left ventricular ejection fraction, revascularization status).

1. Introduction

MI, commonly known as a heart attack, affects approximately 8 to 9 million new cases annually worldwide and is the leading cause of cardiovascular death globally.[1] Beta-blockers, by antagonizing adrenergic receptors, reduce heart rate, blood pressure, and myocardial oxygen demand, forming a cornerstone of post-MI management per guidelines from the American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association.[2-4] Early randomized trials, such as the β-blocker heart attack trial, demonstrated short-term mortality benefits,[5] but real-world evidence on long-term all-cause mortality is inconsistent, with observational studies suggesting attenuated benefits due to comorbidities, adherence issues, or population heterogeneity.[6,7]

Recent trials have intensified this debate. The REBOOT trial (2024), involving 8438 MI patients with preserved LVEF > 40%, found no difference in the primary composite endpoint (all-cause death, recurrent MI, or heart failure hospitalization) between β-blocker users and nonusers over 3.7 years (HR 1.04, P = .63).[8] Similarly, the BETAMI-DANBLOCK trial (2024) randomized 5574 MI patients with LVEF ≥ 40% and reported a lower composite endpoint rate with β-blockers (14.2% vs 16.3%; HR 0.85, P = .03), driven by reduced new MI risk, though no significant effects on mortality or other outcomes were observed.[9] These findings highlight endpoint-specific benefits and variability, particularly in patients with preserved ejection fraction, where universal use may be unnecessary.[8-10] Age remains a key modifier, as older patients may experience differential responses due to polypharmacy or reduced physiological reserve.[11]

NHANES offers a unique opportunity to examine this association in a diverse, nationally representative US population.[12] Prior NHANES analyses have explored cardiovascular therapies, but few have focused on β-blockers in MI survivors using advanced causal inference methods or age-specific stratifications to mitigate confounding.[13] This study aims to evaluate the association between β-blocker use and all-cause mortality in MI survivors, employing PS techniques to estimate ATE, average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), and average treatment effect on the controls (ATC).[14] This study explores the association between β-blocker use and all-cause mortality in MI survivors, potentially modified by age and confounded by baseline differences[15]

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from NHANES 1999 to 2018 cycles, a cross-sectional survey conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) to assess US health and nutrition.[16] We included continuous NHANES cycles with available linked mortality data, restricting to participants with a self-reported history of heart attack. Exclusions included participants with missing data on mortality status, β-blocker treatment, or follow-up time (n = 976 removed). Participant flow is shown in Figure 1. The final analytic sample was 2308 participants. NHANES employs a complex, multistage probability sampling design; we accounted for the NHANES complex survey design using survey weights (e.g., WTINT2YR for interview weights, with adjustments for multi-cycle pooling as per NCHS guidelines), stratification, and clustering in descriptive statistics, unadjusted analyses, and adjusted models where appropriate to ensure nationally representative estimates. Weights were not reapplied in PS-matched subsets (unlike in PS adjustment models) to avoid bias, as recommended.[17,18] This ensures the integrity of the matched balance without distorting the survey design. (see statistical analysis for model-specific details). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

2.2. Exposure, outcome, and covariates

Exposure: β-blocker use was binary (BETABLOCKERS: 0 = nonuse, 1 = use), ascertained from the Prescription Medications Questionnaire (RXQ_RX file) based on self-reported current use of agents in the Multum therapeutic class for beta-adrenergic blocking agents (e.g., metoprolol, atenolol). We acknowledge that reliance on self-reported data may introduce misclassification bias, as participants could under- or overreport usage.[19]

Outcome: all-cause mortality was defined using MORTSTAT (1 = deceased, 0 = alive/censored) from the NHANES-Linked Mortality File, linked to the National Death Index through 2023. Survival time was PERMTH_INT (person-months from interview to death or censoring).

Covariates: selected based on clinical relevance and data availability: age (continuous, smoothed with restricted cubic splines (rcs)), sex (1 = male, 2 = female), ethnicity (1 = Non-Hispanic White, 2 = Non-Hispanic Black, 3 = Mexican American, 4 = Other Hispanic, 5 = Other), marital status (categorical), poverty-income ratio (categorical), education (categorical), smoking status (categorical), alcohol use (categorical), and BMI (continuous, rcs-smoothed). We note that key clinical variables (e.g., revascularization status, LVEF, and type of MI) were unavailable in NHANES, potentially introducing residual confounding.[20] Missing covariate data (primarily BMI, ~12% missing) were handled using multiple imputation with the missForest package in R (m = 5 imputations, random forest-based method) to reduce bias compared to indicator variables.[21] This approach ensures reproducibility for nonlinear missing patterns, with pooling via Rubin’s rules.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Weights handling varies by PS method, as detailed in Methods.

Descriptive statistics compared baseline characteristics by treatment group using t-tests, chi-square tests, and standardized mean differences (SMD; >0.1 indicating imbalance), incorporating NHANES survey weights for representativeness. Univariate Cox proportional hazards models assessed crude associations, with weights applied to account for sampling design.

Stratified analyses examined treatment effects by covariate subgroups, with interaction tests. To enhance result credibility, sensitivity analyses were performed in age subgroups (≥30, ≥55, ≥60, ≥70, ≥75, and 30–80 years), repeating crude, multivariable, and PS-adjusted models. Proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals (P > .05 indicating no violation). Statistical analysis was performed with R version 4.3.1 (https://www.R-project.org) and Empower Stats 6.0 (www.empowerstats.net, X&Y solutions, Inc. Boston, Massachusetts). P < .05 was considered significant. As NHANES data are publicly available and approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board with participant informed consent, no additional ethical approval was required.

2.4. Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this research, as it is a secondary analysis of existing public data.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

Of 3284 initial observations, 2308 were analyzed after removing those with missing treatment/outcome data (Table 1). Beta-blocker users (unweighted n = 345; weighted 14.5%) were younger (weighted mean age 64.7 years [95% CI: 63.3–66.1] vs 66.3 years [95% CI: 65.6–67.1], P = .0444), had higher BMI (33.3 kg/m2 [95% CI: 32.4–34.2] vs 29.7 kg/m2 [95% CI: 29.3–30.1], P < .0001), and were slightly less likely to be Non-Hispanic White (weighted 72.6% [95% CI: 65.2–78.9] vs 79.5% [95% CI: 76.9–81.9], P = .0843). Users were also more likely to be male (weighted 68.8% [95% CI: 62.0–74.9] vs 61.7% [95% CI: 58.8–64.6], P = .0508) and married (weighted 65.9% [95% CI: 59.3–72.0] vs 59.2% [95% CI: 56.1–62.2], P = .0430). Other covariates showed no significant differences (P > .05). All estimates are survey-weighted to account for NHANES complex sampling design.

3.2. Univariate and multivariable associations

In univariate analysis, β-blocker use was associated with lower mortality (crude HR 0.81, 95% CI: 0.68–0.96, P = .0178). Older age (HR 1.07 per year, P < .0001), marital status (HR 1.52 for widowed/divorced vs married, P < .0001), and higher BMI (HR 0.97, P < .0001) were significant predictors. Weighted Kaplan–Meier estimates (Fig. 2), accounting for NHANES survey design using the svykm function in R, indicated an approximate 5-year mortality rate (at ≈75 months) of 28.07% (95% CI: 21.65–33.96) in β-blocker users vs 29.24% (95% CI: 26.21–32.15) in nonusers, yielding an absolute risk reduction (ARR) of 1.17% (95% CI: 0.2–2.1). At 10 years (≈125 months), rates were 45.85% (95% CI: 34.95–54.93) in users vs 46.66% (95% CI: 42.93–50.15) in nonusers (ARR 0.81%, 95% CI: 0.1–1.5). ARR = nonuser rate − user rate; 95% CIs for rates and ARR are based on the Link (1984) transformation method, with ARR CIs derived using the delta method for rate differences (incorporating variance from weighted estimates). These absolute measures show minimal, nonsignificant differences (CIs for ARR overlap zero), consistent with the null findings from Cox and PS models (see Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R723 for full details, including methodological references, available in the online supplement).[23–25]

3.3. Propensity score analyses

PS was estimated via logistic regression including all covariates (indicator for any missing data, age [smoothed], sex, ethnicity, marital status, poverty-income ratio [categorized], education level, smoking status, alcohol use status, body mass index [smoothed]). As shown in Figure 3, the histogram revealed greater frequency in lower PS ranges for nonusers, with partial overlap between groups, supporting the feasibility of matching and adjustment.

PS matching improved balance for most covariates (e.g., PS itself P = .9653, AGE P = .0695), but residual imbalance remained in BMI (e.g., SMD reduced from 0.4525 pre-matching to 0.20 post-matching; P = .0009) and alcohol use (P = .0228); see new Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R723 for pre- and post-matching SMDs for all covariates, available in the online supplement. Refined matching (e.g., nearest-neighbor with caliper = 0.2) substantially reduced imbalances, and the residual effects did not materially affect results, as confirmed by consistent IPTW and stratified analyses. Matched ATE HR was 0.93 (95% CI: 0.70–1.23, P = .6029); ATT HR 1.03 (95% CI: 0.85–1.25, P = .7683); ATC HR 0.91 (95% CI: 0.67–1.24, P = .5667).

IPTW results were consistent: ATE HR 1.02 (robust 95% CI: 0.83–1.24, P = .8786; survey Wald CI similar). Weight distributions were stable (e.g., ATE mean weight ~1.0 for both groups, max = 7.15, no extreme values; see Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R723 for weight summary, available in the online supplement), indicating reliable estimation. As a sensitivity analysis for missing data (primarily BMI, ~12% missing), we performed multiple imputation (m = 5) using missForest, with pooling via Rubin’s rules. Pooled results were consistent with primary analyses (e.g., multivariable HR 1.02, 95% CI: 0.84–1.23, P = .838; PS-adjusted HR 0.98, 95% CI: 0.81–1.19, P = .864), with low variability (HR sd < 0.03), confirming robustness (details in Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R723).

3.4. Stratified analyses

Stratified analyses showed no significant interactions (all P-interaction > .05), with consistent null effects across subgroups (e.g., by age: low HR 1.31 [95% CI: 0.91–1.87], P = .1471; middle HR 0.88 [95% CI: 0.67–1.15], P = .3499; high HR 0.89 [95% CI: 0.65–1.22], P = .4761; see Table 3). Univariate analyses confirmed associations of covariates with mortality (e.g., BMI HR = 0.97 [95% CI: 0.96–0.98], P < .0001), supporting their inclusion in models. Although HRs suggested potential benefits in certain subgroups (e.g., females: HR 0.70 [95% CI: 0.51–0.96], P = .0290; low-education: HR 0.57 [95% CI: 0.39–0.84], P = .0048; low poverty-income ratio: HR 0.69 [95% CI: 0.52–0.92], P = .0120), these findings require confirmation due to nonsignificant interaction P-values >.05; Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R723, low power, and risk of type I error.[26] Subgroup analyses are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and should be interpreted with caution.

3.5. Age-stratified sensitivity analyses

To assess robustness across age groups, we conducted subgroup analyses (Table 4). Crude HRs suggested potential protective effects in older subgroups (e.g., ≥70 years: HR 0.73, 95% CI: 0.58–0.92, P = .0082; ≥55 years: HR 0.77, P = .0054), as illustrated by the Kaplan–Meier survival curves (Fig. 2). However, these attenuated to null after adjustment (e.g., ≥70 years multivariable HR 0.98, P = .8992; PS-adjusted HR 0.91, P = .4616), with consistent null effects across models. Results were also consistent in the restricted 30–80 years range (adjusted HR 1.03, P = .7743). These findings enhance the credibility of the main null results by demonstrating consistency despite varying age compositions, though subgroup analyses are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and should be interpreted with caution due to limited statistical power, potential multiple testing bias, and the absence of significant interactions (all P-interaction > .05).[27]

4. Discussion

In this NHANES analysis of 2308 MI survivors, β-blocker use was not associated with reduced all-cause mortality after comprehensive adjustment for confounders, with HRs near 1.0 across models. The crude protective effect (HR 0.81) likely reflected confounding by factors like younger age and higher BMI in users, which attenuated upon adjustment. These findings align with recent meta-analyses showing diminished long-term benefits of β-blockers in contemporary MI populations, possibly due to improved revascularization and statin use.[28,29] Additionally, indication bias may confound results, as β-blockers are often prescribed to higher-risk patients with more severe MI or comorbidities, potentially masking true effects or leading to apparent null associations in observational data.[30] In contrast, a recent real-world cohort study by Ling et al[14] reported that long-term β-blocker use (>1 year) was associated with reduced all-cause mortality (lower hazard ratios) in MI patients without reduced ejection fraction, highlighting how patient-specific factors like treatment duration and comorbidities may influence outcomes. This discrepancy underscores the role of real-world adherence and subgroup variations in our null findings, warranting further investigation into personalized prescribing.

Age-stratified sensitivity analyses, despite low power in subgroups which limits precision and increases the risk of spurious findings, demonstrated consistent null effects post-adjustment across various thresholds. For instance, while crude HRs suggested stronger protection in older groups (e.g., ≥70 years HR 0.73), adjustment eliminated significance, indicating age-related confounding (e.g., healthier older users). This pattern was uniform, even in the 30 to 80 years restricted range, providing some support for the overall null association, though interpretations should be cautious. Subgroup analyses suggested potential benefits in females, low-education, and low-income groups, consistent with studies indicating greater efficacy in vulnerable populations. However, nonsignificant interactions warrant caution and require confirmation in larger studies.

This study has several strengths, including the utilization of a large, nationally representative NHANES sample for broad generalizability to US MI survivors, the application of multiple PS methods (matching, inverse probability weighting, and adjustment) to robustly handle confounding and emulate randomized conditions, and age-stratified sensitivity analyses to confirm robustness across subgroups. The visualization of PS distribution and its relationship with mortality provides transparency, confirming adequate overlap and the utility of PS for adjustment.[14,22] However, limitations include potential misclassification from self-reported β-blocker use (relying on participant recall rather than verified prescriptions or adherence data), residual confounding from unmeasured variables (e.g., LVEF or medication adherence), residual covariate imbalance post-propensity score matching (e.g., in BMI and alcohol use, as detailed in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R723, though refined matching minimized its impact on results), and assumptions in multiple imputation for missing data (e.g., BMI, assuming missing at random with m = 5, which may introduce minor uncertainty). Low event rates could reduce power, and age subgroups may have limited precision in older strata (e.g., ≥75 years). Future research should incorporate electronic health records for precise dosing and longitudinal adherence, and explore age-specific mechanisms (e.g., β-blocker tolerability in the elderly).[15]

In conclusion, these results suggest no overall association between β-blocker use and reduced all-cause mortality in this real-world MI cohort after confounder adjustment, with consistent findings across age subgroups despite methodological limitations. Clinicians should weigh individual risks, particularly in older patients where benefits may be minimal, supporting personalized therapy approaches rather than routine use. Guidelines may need updating based on contemporary evidence.[2-4]
