Introduction

Carotid free floating thrombus (CFFT) is a rare but clinically significant cause of transient ischaemic attack and ischaemic stroke, characterised by an intraluminal thrombus surrounded by circumferential blood flow and demonstrating mobility within the carotid artery lumen. Although uncommon, CFFT carries a substantial risk of early embolic events, particularly during the first days following diagnosis. Previous studies have reported ischaemic event recurrence rates as high as 19.7% within the first week and up to 27.4% at three months.1,2

The pathophysiology of CFFT is heterogeneous and includes atherosclerotic plaque, carotid dissection, carotid web, and hypercoagulable states.1, 2, 3, 4 Importantly, CFFT may occur in the absence of significant carotid stenosis, complicating management decisions, as standard criteria for carotid intervention do not readily apply.

There is currently no consensus regarding optimal treatment. Reported management strategies include anticoagulation alone, urgent or delayed carotid endarterectomy, and, in selected cases, endovascular approaches.1,3,5, 6, 7, 8 Given the rarity of the condition and the absence of randomised data, treatment decisions are largely individualised and based on thrombus characteristics, clinical presentation, and cerebrovascular anatomy.

This report presents case of symptomatic CFFT without associated carotid stenosis, managed by urgent surgical thrombectomy, and discusses the rationale for this approach considering current evidence.

Report

A 66 year old woman with a history of arterial hypertension presented with a brief episode of right upper limb paraesthesia, which spontaneously resolved within minutes. Neurological examination on admission was normal, consistent with a transient ischaemic attack.

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed a non-occlusive intraluminal thrombus in the proximal segment of the left internal carotid artery (Fig. 1A). The thrombus measured approximately 16–18 mm in length and was surrounded by circumferential contrast opacification, consistent with a free floating thrombus. Duplex ultrasound confirmed a highly mobile, non-adherent thrombus with characteristic oscillating motion, without associated plaque ulceration, dissection, or significant stenosis (Fig. 1B). Neurovascular imaging demonstrated a patent circle of Willis with normal intracranial perfusion. The contralateral carotid artery and both vertebral arteries were patent, without haemodynamically significant disease.

The patient had no previous history of antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy and was not receiving statin treatment at presentation. A comprehensive aetiological work up, including full blood count, coagulation profile (PT and INR, aPTT, fibrinogen), D dimer levels, and thrombophilia screening, revealed no abnormalities. Cardiac evaluation and thoracic CTA excluded proximal embolic sources.

Urgent surgical thrombectomy was selected based on the recent transient ischaemic symptoms, the large and highly mobile thrombus (16–18 mm), and favourable cerebrovascular anatomy with a patent circle of Willis. This decision was further supported by the centre's and surgeon's experience with carotid surgery. Initial medical therapy was felt to offer less immediate protection against early embolic recurrence, while endovascular treatment raised concern about distal embolisation during catheter manipulation. As there was no significant carotid stenosis or underlying plaque, a focused thrombectomy was considered the most appropriate strategy.

The patient underwent carotid thrombectomy under general anaesthesia approximately five hours after vascular assessment, corresponding to 20 hours after symptom onset. Systemic heparinisation (5 000 IU intravenous bolus) and antibiotic prophylaxis were administered. Continuous transcranial Doppler monitoring was used throughout the procedure to assess middle cerebral artery flow velocities and detect micro-embolic signals. No shunt was employed, as pre-operative imaging and intra-operative monitoring indicated adequate collateral cerebral circulation and tolerance to carotid clamping.

A standard longitudinal incision along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle was performed. Following careful dissection and minimal manipulation of the carotid bifurcation, vascular control was obtained proximally on the common carotid artery and distally on the internal and external carotid arteries. A longitudinal arteriotomy was made in the common carotid artery, extending into the proximal internal carotid artery. The free floating thrombus was identified and removed en bloc (Fig. 1C), without evidence of plaque rupture or arterial dissection. Primary arteriotomy closure was then performed using a continuous 6–0 polypropylene suture, with a satisfactory final appearance of the carotid artery after closure (Fig. 1D). There were no intra-operative or post-operative complications. Operating time was 45 minutes, with a carotid clamp time of 12 minutes.

The patient was discharged on rivaroxaban 20 mg daily and high intensity statin therapy (atorvastatin 40 mg daily). Anticoagulation was subsequently de-escalated to aspirin 100 mg daily at one year, after exclusion of embolic and prothrombotic sources. At one year follow up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with sustained carotid patency on duplex ultrasound. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Discussion

CFFT represents a rare but high risk vascular condition, with management decisions primarily driven by thrombus morphology, clinical presentation, and cerebrovascular anatomy rather than by the degree of carotid stenosis alone. This case illustrates several key aspects relevant to therapeutic decision making and helps position the current case within the available literature. CFFT may arise from a heterogeneous range of underlying conditions, including atherosclerotic plaque, carotid dissection, carotid web, and hypercoagulable states, although no definite cause is identified in some patients despite extensive investigation.3,4

Importantly, the absence of significant carotid stenosis does not imply a benign course. Previous series have shown that thrombus mobility and length are important predictors of early embolic recurrence, particularly within the first week after diagnosis.1,2 In a systematic review, Fridman et al. reported ischaemic event recurrence rates of 19.7% at seven days and 27.4% at three months, with thrombus length > 15 mm and marked mobility identified as predictors of early recurrence.1 Similarly, Müller et al. found that patients with mobile thrombi had significantly higher rates of recurrent ischaemic events compared with those with adherent thrombi.2 In symptomatic patients, this early risk period poses a particular challenge when considering conservative treatment alone.

Current management strategies include medical therapy, open surgical treatment, and endovascular intervention, each with specific advantages and limitations. Anticoagulation is often advocated as first line treatment because it may promote thrombus stabilisation and resolution while avoiding procedural risk. Several reports have described successful thrombus resolution with medical therapy alone, particularly in patients with small or less mobile thrombi.3,4 However, recurrent neurological events have also been observed despite therapeutic anticoagulation, especially in patients with elongated or highly mobile thrombi (> 15 mm). In the series by El Harake et al., recurrent ischaemic events occurred in 12% of patients who were initially managed medically.3 Delayed surgery after initial medical therapy may be appropriate in selected cases, particularly when residual stenosis is present, but this approach may not adequately address the immediate embolic risk in all symptomatic patients.

Open surgical treatment offers the advantage of immediate and definitive thrombus removal. Previous reports have shown acceptable safety profiles in experienced centres, particularly when careful patient selection and cerebral monitoring are employed.3, 4, 5 Ferrero et al. reported a series of 16 patients who were treated surgically; there was no peri-operative stroke or death.5 Tolaymat et al. emphasised that surgery may be particularly appropriate in patients with high risk thrombus features, irrespective of degree of stenosis.8 Potential disadvantages include the risks of general anaesthesia, cranial nerve injury, and the need for carotid clamping with its attendant ischaemic risks.

Endovascular approaches, including aspiration thrombectomy, stent retriever thrombectomy, and carotid stenting, have been described in selected cases, particularly in patients deemed high risk for open surgery.6,7 Hou et al. recently reported successful endovascular thrombectomy in a patient with progressive stroke and high thrombus burden.6 Carr et al. described aspiration thrombectomy for a symptomatic common carotid thrombus.7 However, concerns regarding distal embolisation during catheter or device manipulation and the limited availability of robust outcome data currently restrict their broader application. Embolic protection devices and flow reversal systems may theoretically mitigate this risk, but evidence specifically for CFFT remains limited. Endovascular intervention may be most appropriate in patients who are poor surgical candidates due to comorbidities, hostile neck anatomy, or high carotid lesions inaccessible to open surgery.

Post-operative anticoagulation was chosen to mitigate the risk of occult embolic or prothrombotic sources during aetiological evaluation and to provide secondary prevention during the early post-operative period.3 Rivaroxaban was selected for its predictable pharmacokinetic profile, fixed dosing, and rapid onset, which were practical advantages in the post-operative setting while cardio-embolic or thrombophilic aetiologies remained under investigation. Following their exclusion, de-escalation to aspirin monotherapy was deemed appropriate, reflecting an individualised antithrombotic strategy in line with guideline recommendations.9,10

As a single case report, this study does not support general recommendations favouring surgery over medical therapy, and the absence of comparative data and inherent selection bias must be acknowledged. Nevertheless, it highlights that CFFT is a rare but high risk condition requiring prompt recognition and individualised management, and that urgent surgical thrombectomy may represent a safe and effective option in carefully selected symptomatic patients with high risk thrombus features and favourable cerebrovascular anatomy. Medical therapy remains appropriate in lower risk cases or in patients who are unsuitable for surgery, while endovascular approaches may be considered in specific settings.
