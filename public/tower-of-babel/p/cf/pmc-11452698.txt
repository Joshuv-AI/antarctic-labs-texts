A genetic study to identify pathogenic mechanisms and drug targets for benign prostatic hyperplasia: a multi-omics Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Bohan Liu, Xinyi Wang, Zerui Yang, Zhaofa Yin, Cai Tang, Yushi He, Qi Ling, Zhongli Huang, Shijian Feng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) as a common geriatric disease in urology, the incidence and prevalence are rapidly increasing with the aging society, prompting an urgent need for effective prevention and treatment of BPH. However, limited therapeutic efficacy and higher risk of complications result in the treatment of BPH remaining challenging. The unclear pathogenic mechanism also hampers further exploration of therapeutic approaches for BPH. In this study, we used multi-omics methods to integrate genomics, transcriptomics, immunomics, and metabolomics data and identify biomolecules associated with BPH. We performed transcriptomic imputation, summary data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR), joint/conditional analysis, colocalization analysis, and FOCUS to explore high-confidence genes associated with BPH in blood and prostate tissue. Subsequently, three-step SMR was used to identify the DNA methylation sites regulating high-confidence genes to improve the pathogenic pathways of BPH. We also used cis-instruments of druggable genes to conduct SMR analysis to find potential drug targets for BPH. Finally, we used MR analysis to explore the immune pathways and metabolomics related to BPH. Multiple analytical methods identified BTN3A2 (Blood: TWAS Z score = 5.02912, TWAS P = 4.93 × 10–7; Prostate: TWAS Z score = 4.89, TWAS P = 1.01 × 10–6) and C4A (Blood: TWAS Z score = 4.90754, TWAS P = 9.22 × 10–7; Prostate: TWAS Z score = 5.084, TWAS P = 3.70 × 10–7) as high-confidence genes for BPH and identified the cg14345882-BTN3A2-BPH pathogenic pathway. We also used druggable gene data to identify 30 promising therapeutic target genes, including BTN3A2 and C4A. For MR analysis of immune pathways, we identified immune cell surface molecules as well as the inflammatory factor IL-17 (OR = 1.25, 95% CI = 1.09–1.43, PFDR = 0.12, Maximum likelihood) as risk factors for BPH. In addition, we found that disulfide levels of cysteinylglycine (OR = 1.11, 95% CI = 1.05–1.18, P = 5.18 × 10–4, Weighted median), oxidation levels of cysteinylglycine (OR = 1.09, 95% CI = 1.04–1.14, P = 3.87 × 10–4, Weighted median), and sebacate levels (OR = 1.05, 95% CI = 1.02–1.08, P = 3.0 × 10–4, Maximum likelihood) increase the risk of BPH. This multi-omics study explored biomolecules associated with BPH, improved the pathogenic pathways of BPH, and identified promising therapeutic targets. Our results provide evidence for future studies aimed at developing appropriate therapeutic interventions.

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a common disease in urology1. Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) in men are most frequently caused by BPH2. LUTS, which include urination and storage symptoms such as weak urine flow, urinary urgency, urinary frequency, nocturia, and dysuria, have a serious impact on patients’ quality of life and cost the American healthcare system more than $4 billion annually3. Additionally, BPH increases the risk of acute urine retention and urinary tract infections2. The incidence and prevalence of BPH are rapidly increasing as society ages, prompting an urgent need for effective prevention and treatment of BPH4. However, there are still some drawbacks to pharmacological and surgical treatments for BPH, including the lack of efficacy of pharmacological treatments and the poor tolerance of surgical treatments in elderly patients, prompting urologists to seek a more refined treatment5,6. Due to the complexity of pathophysiology, the pathogenic mechanisms of BPH remain poorly understood, the biomolecules associated with BPH remain elusive, and the relative scarcity of molecular data has hampered further exploration of the pathogenic mechanisms of BPH, resulting in the slow development of pharmacological therapeutic research7,8. Therefore, we need to further explore the risk factors associated with BPH.

Despite being a common disease in men, BPH is still believed to be linked to genetic factors. In a cohort of 909 men who underwent prostatectomy for BPH, researchers found that the cohort included male participants with a four-fold increased risk of a first-degree relative developing BPH requiring surgery, compared to a control group9. In addition, the single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) rs103294 was found to be associated with an increased risk of BPH in a Chinese study10. However, to date, the vast majority of genetic studies of prostate disease have focused on prostate cancer rather than BPH11. Genetic factors associated with BPH still need to be further explored. BPH is considered a chronic inflammatory disease, and immune pathways contribute significantly to BPH12. Fluctuating sex hormone levels and disturbed levels of circulating metabolites due to metabolic syndrome are also risk factors for BPH13,14. Understanding the immune pathways and the causal relationship between circulating metabolites and BPH offers hope for the development of new therapeutic strategies. However, the causal relationship between immune pathways and circulating metabolites and BPH remains to be elucidated, as traditional observational studies are limited by inherent shortcomings, including environmental confounders, selection bias, and reverse causality15. Although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are the gold standard for verifying causality, the sheer number of immune pathways and circulating metabolite species makes RCTs challenging to implement in a real-world clinical setting16.

In conclusion, we used a multi-omics Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis and based on public databases, aiming to provide information for further exploration of the pathogenesis and therapeutic targets of prostate hyperplasia while eliminating the bias of traditional observational studies.

Methods

Transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) use expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) and genome-wide association study (GWAS) data to identify susceptibility genes associated with disease risk17. The method clusters multiple genetic variants into a single gene unit and maps directly to candidate genes. As a powerful gene-based approach, TWAS has been successfully applied to decipher the genetic structure of complex traits18. MR is a method that uses genetic variation as instrumental variables (IVs) to explore potential causal relationships between lifetime exposure and outcome19. Properly performed MR analysis can eliminate bias resulting from environmental factors and prevent reverse causality because genetic variations are originated from parents and randomly allocated at conception20. Summary data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) extends and develops the concept of MR. SMR allows for the combination of GWAS data with eQTL, prioritizing potential candidate gene expression-mediated causality identified in GWAS21.

Study design

In this study, we used a multi-omics approach to comprehensively and deeply explore the biomolecules closely associated with BPH. We used large GWAS data on BPH as well as blood and prostate tissue data from GTEx v8. Using these data, we performed TWASs to identify genes associated with BPH. To identify the functions of TWAS genes, we used Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment to functionally annotate these genes. We then employed SMR analysis, FOCUS, and joint/conditional analysis to select high-confidence genes with potential causal relationships with BPH. To improve the pathogenic pathway of BPH, we identified DNA methylation sites that regulate BPH pathogenic genes. Next, we performed SMR analyses using the cis-instrument of the druggable genes and identified drug targets related to BPH. We also performed the phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) to identify potential health effects of drug targets. To verify the relationship between immune pathways and BPH, we performed cell type enrichment analysis on GWAS data of BPH. Cell type enrichment analysis indicated that immune cells were associated with BPH, and subsequent MR analyses of 731 immune cells as well as 91 inflammatory factors further elucidated the role of immune pathways in BPH. Finally, we performed metabolome-wide MR analyses to identify circulating metabolites that influence the development of BPH.

GWAS data and preprocessing for BPH

We used GWAS summary statistics for BPH from FinnGen Biobank R9 to identify risk sites22. The GWAS summary statistics for BPH comprises 30,066 BPH cases and 119,297 controls, all of European ancestry. BPH was diagnosed according to the ICD-10, and the mean age for the disease was 67.93 years. Gene expression weights and reference panels for TWAS

Gene expression weights were based on the training model obtained from GTEx v823. For the TWAS analysis, we chose eQTL from blood and prostate tissues as predictive models, as both tissues are associated with the pathophysiology of BPH. We used the LD reference data for European populations from the 1,000 Genomes Project (version 3) as the reference panel for the TWAS analysis to reduce the effect of LD17.

Transcriptome-wide association studies

To perform TWAS analyses, we combined the genetic effects of BPH (GWAS Z score) with gene weights from GTEx v8 using the Functional Summary-based Interpolation (FUSION) method, which is restricted to autosomes17. Briefly, FUSION is a method used to combine transcriptomic data with GWAS to enhance the interpolation of GWAS summary statistics. First, we used data from the 1,000 Genomes Project as a reference panel to develop SNP-based predictors using transcriptomics and GWAS summary statistics to estimate the heritability of each gene. Subsequently, FUSION selected the best model from multiple prediction models (top1, blup, lasso, enet, and bslmm) by comparing the R2 of the sample, and applied it to the gene weights. Finally, we used FUSION to combine the genetic effects of BPH (GWAS Z score) with gene weights to perform TWAS analyses of BPH by calculating the linear sum of Z score × gene weights for each SNP at the gene locus. To correct for multiple comparisons and perform subsequent analyses, we adjusted the results using a Bonferroni correction (P < 0.05/total number of genes included in the analysis).

Enrichment analysis of TWAS genes

For TWAS genes corrected by Bonferroni, we categorized them according to the GWAS Z score. Genes with the Z score > 0 were considered risk genes, with the potential to increase the risk of BPH. In contrast, genes with the Z score < 0 were considered protective genes for BPH. To explore the functions of these genes, we used GO enrichment analysis to functionally annotate TWAS genes from three aspects: molecular function, cellular components and involved biological processes24. We used the false discovery rate (FDR) to correct for pleiotropy, defining significance by a threshold of PFDR < 0.05. Finally, the “clusterProfiler” R package was used for pathway visualization.

Sensitivity analyses of TWAS

To assess the robustness of the gene-trait associations we identified using TWAS, we performed several sensitivity analyses. First, we performed joint/conditional analyses on TWAS genes17. The joint/conditional analysis aimed to determine how much GWAS signals remained significant after removing the expression weights from TWAS. The analysis is based on the TWAS statistics and a gene correlation matrix to evaluate the joint or conditional model, where the correlation matrix can be estimated via predicting the expression for each TWAS gene and Pearson correlations across genes were computed. The joint/conditional test was run on all TWAS hit regions to assess the independence of these associations within their respective 1 Mb regions. We defined Joint P < 0.05 as conditional significance. The genes that passed the joint/conditional tests represented promising driven genes. In addition, to avoid gene-trait associations being by chance, we performed permutation tests for each gene expression weight locus17. We fixed the maximum number of permutation tests at 100,000 and defined P < 0.05 as permutation significance. It should be noted that due to the highly conserved nature of the permutation test, the true causal genes may be deleted. Finally, we performed the colocalization analysis of TWAS genes using the “coloc” R package, aiming to find evidence of shared causal variation between genes and traits25. Five hypotheses (H0, H1, H2, H3, H4) were used in the colocalization analysis and the posterior probability of each hypothesis was calculated using the Bayesian test. H4 means that the trait and the gene are driven by the same causally variable locus, and therefore we defined PP.H4 > 0.8 as strong evidence of colocalization.

High-confidence genes

We used SMR and FOCUS to identify high-confidence genes associated with BPH. As an extension of the MR, SMR can identify causal associations between genetically determined traits (gene expression, DNA methylation, etc. as exposures) and complex traits of interest (disease phenotypes as outcomes)21. We obtained cis-eQTL data for blood and prostate tissue from the GTEx v8 database and identified genes associated with BPH by SMR analysis23. HEIDI tests were performed and we defined PHEIDI > 0.05 as no significant heterogeneity21. FOCUS is a Bayesian model that allows estimation of the likelihood of a gene’s involvement in causing a disease trait by combining GWAS data with eQTL analyses26. FOCUS determines the posterior inclusion probability (PIP) for each gene, which is the estimated probability that the gene contributes to the disease trait. If the PIP is greater than 0.8, it indicates that there is 80% confidence that the gene contributes to the development of the disease trait. Compared to other methods, FOCUS can identify pathogenic genes more sensitively and ensure the robustness of our study. In this study, we defined genes with Joint P < 0.05, SMR PFDR < 0.05, PIP > 0.8, PP.H4 > 0.8, and PHEIDI > 0.05 as high-confidence genes. High-confidence genes may have a causal effect on BPH.

Identification of DNA methylation sites

We obtained cis- eQTL data for blood and prostate tissue from the GTEx v8 database and cis-DNA methylation QTL (mQTL) data from pooled data from meta-analyses of two cohorts (n = 1,980)23,27. To improve the pathogenic pathway of BPH, we performed a three-step SMR analysis: (1) SNPs as instruments, gene expression as exposure, and BPH as outcome. (2) SNPs as instruments, DNA methylation sites as exposure, and BPH as outcome. (3) SNPs as instruments, DNA methylation sites as exposure, and gene expression as outcome. The third step uses only significant signals from steps 1 and 2. The final target signals were defined as (1) All three steps of SMR were significant after Bonferroni correction (P < 0.05/total number of biomolecules included in the analysis). (2) SNPs were significant (P < 5 × 10–8) in all cis-eQTLs, cis-mQTLs, and GWAS. (3) PHEIDI > 0.05.

Drug target SMR analysis

The Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb) integrates information on druggable genes from publications, databases, and other web sources28. We downloaded the “category data” for all druggable genes in DGIdb. We also obtained a list of druggable genes from the review by Finan et al29. To generate genetic instruments for SMR analysis of druggable genes, we used whole-blood eQTL data from the eQTLGen consortium. This data included significant cis-eQTLs (PFDR < 0.05) for 16,987 genes (n = 31,684, European ancestry). We extracted cis-eQTLs located within 1000 kb on either side of the transcription start site (TSS) of the target genes and closely associated with gene expression (P < 5 × 10–8). Finally, cis-eQTLs and GWAS were integrated using SMR analysis. genes that were significant after SMR PFDR < 0.05 and PHEIDI > 0.05 were considered to be druggable genes related to BPH.

Phenome-wide association studies

We used PheWAS to evaluate BPH drug target genes to determine the possible health effects of drugs targeting these genes. We downloaded data for 1,419 diseases from SAIGE GWAS in the UK Biobank (n = 408,961)30. Using these data, we performed PheWAS for SNPs within 100 kb on either side of the TSS for each target gene and strongly associated with gene expression (P < 5 × 10–8). We report all significant phenotypic associations at a threshold of P < 3.5 × 10–5 (0.05/1,419).

Cell type enrichment analysis

To identify cell types associated with BPH, we used functional mapping and annotation (FUMA) to combine single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data with GWAS summary statistics31. ScRNA-seq was obtained from Mouse Cell Atlas, a transcriptome database containing 400,000 cells and more than 40 mouse organs/tissues32. ScRNA-seq was analyzed by MAGMA gene-property analysis, identifying cell types enriched in BPH-related genes. We focused on the relationship between immune cells and BPH, as BPH is considered a chronic inflammatory disease. A threshold of P < 0.05 was considered significant.

The causal relationship between immune pathways and BPH

To further evaluate the interaction between immune pathways and BPH, we performed two-sample MR of immune cells and inflammatory factors separately. MR is based on three assumptions: 1. There is a strong correlation between SNPs and exposure factors; 2. SNPs are independent of confounders; 3. SNPs can only have an effect on the outcome through exposure factors20. GWAS data for immune cells were obtained from the study by Orru et al. (n = 3,757, European ancestry)33. This study included 731 immune cells and was divided into seven categories: peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets (TBNK), regulatory T cells (Treg), maturation stages of T cells, dendritic cells (DC), B cells, monocytes, and myeloid cells. GWAS data for inflammatory factors were obtained from the study by Zhao et al. (n = 14,824, European ancestry)34. This study included 91 inflammation-related proteins. We used the “TwoSampleMR” R package to perform all MR analyses. To ensure the stability and reliability of the MR analysis results, we chose IVs based on the following criteria: 1. SNPs with statistically significant association with exposure factors were selected as IVs (P < 5 × 10–6); 2. To avoid LD, we chose independent SNPs (r2 < 0.01 and distance > 10,000 kb); 3. We deleted SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01. 4. We searched Phenoscanner and deleted SNPs related to BPH (P < 5 × 10–8). 5. We calculated the F statistic (F) by the formula: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F=\frac{{Beta}^{2}}{{Se}^{2}}$$\end{document}F=Beta2Se235. SNPs with F < 10 were excluded.

Subsequently, we performed two-sample MR analyses for immune cells and inflammatory factors, respectively. For exposure factors with only 1 SNP, we used the Wald ratio for MR analysis36. For exposure factors with more than 1 SNP, inverse variance weighted (IVW) was used as the main statistical method for MR analyses. IVW integrates individual ratio weights through meta-analysis and is reliable when all SNPs are valid37. In addition, we used weighted median, maximum likelihood, weighted mode, and MR Egger methods for complementary analyses. These methods rely on weaker assumptions than IVW, resulting in reduced statistical power38. Strict multiple comparisons were not applicable due to interactions between immune pathways, so we defined PFDR < 0.2 as the threshold of significance. Exposure factors were considered suggestively associated with BPH when IVW P and at least one complementary method P < 0.05 but PFDR > 0.2. Finally, we conducted sensitivity analyses for exposure factors including more than 2 SNPs. We used Cochran’s Q statistic to examine the heterogeneity of IVW and MR Egger. The random effects model IVW was used for MR analyses if there was heterogeneity in the result (P < 0.05). We also used the intercept of the MR Egger regression and the MR Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) global test for pleiotropy analysis39.

Effects of circulating metabolites on BPH

To identify the role of metabolomics in the development of BPH, we performed a two-sample MR analysis of circulating metabolites. GWAS data for circulating metabolites were obtained from the study of Chen et al. (n = 50,000, European ancestry)40. This study contained 1,091 circulating metabolites and was divided into 8 categories: lipid, amino acid, xenobiotics, nucleotide, cofactor and vitamins, carbohydrate, peptide, and energy. Instrument selection, MR methods, and analytical procedures for circulating metabolites were the same as for MR analysis of the immune pathway. Due to the non-independence of the metabolites, strict multiple comparisons were not applicable40. We therefore divided the conventional significance threshold of P < 0.05 by 73 effective numbers of independent metabolites, yielding a more appropriate significance threshold of P < 6.8 × 10–4.

GO analyses were used to explore the biological functions of TWAS genes (Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Table S4). Figure 2C and D show the biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions involved in the TWAS genes in blood and prostate tissue, respectively. We found that TWAS genes in the blood are mainly related to the clearance of apoptotic cells, secretion of androgens, and activation of the immune system. The TWAS genes in prostate tissue are mainly related to metabolic processes and enzyme activity. These gene functions may be related to the development of BPH. We performed joint/conditional analyses, permutation tests, and colocalization analyses to assess the robustness of our results (Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Table S5, and Supplementary Table S6). Of the TWAS genes we identified, the vast majority passed joint/conditional analyses, indicating that our genes were independently associated with BPH (11/17 in blood and 8/14 in prostate tissue). In addition, only 1 TWAS gene in blood failed the permutation test, and 9 TWAS genes in prostate tissue passed. However, since the permutation test is highly conservative, TWAS genes that failed the permutation test are still worthy of attention. Finally, we found that most TWAS genes colocalized with BPH, suggesting that gene expression and BPH share SNPs.

High-confidence genes

We used SMR and FOCUS to further explore high-confidence genes associated with BPH (Supplementary Table S7-Supplementary Table S10). First, we performed SMR analysis of cis-eQTLs in blood and prostate tissue and identified 16 genes in blood and 7 genes in prostate tissue associated with BPH (Fig. 2E and F). Subsequently, we integrated TWAS genes in blood, SMR genes in blood, TWAS genes in prostate tissues, and SMR genes in prostate tissues and identified 2 genes: BTN3A2 and C4A (Fig. 2G). In addition, we performed FOCUS on BTN3A2 and C4A. After a series of rigorous and complex screenings, we finally identified the high-confidence genes in blood: BTN3A2 (Joint P = 4.93 × 10–7, SMR PFDR = 0.001, PP.H4 = 0.979, PIP = 0.995, PHEIDI = 0.102), and in prostate tissue: BTN3A2 (Joint P = 1.01 × 10–6, SMR PFDR = 0.002, PP.H4 = 0.977, PIP = 0.990, PHEIDI = 0.100) and C4A (Joint P = 3.70 × 10–7, SMR PFDR = 0.025, PP.H4 = 0.983, PIP = 0.816, PHEIDI = 0.411) (Fig. 2H).

DNA methylation sites

Our three-step SMR analysis was performed based on high-confidence genes (Supplementary Table S7, Supplementary Table S8, Supplementary Table S11-Supplementary Table S13). This study showed that BTN3A2-associated SNP signals in blood were significant in the data of BPH GWAS, cis-eQTL, and cis-mQTL. The DNA methylation site cg14345882 is located upstream of BTN3A2 and has a negative effect on BTN3A2 expression and BPH risk (Fig. 3A and B). The level of BTN3A2 expression was positively correlated with BPH risk. Interestingly, we found the same results in prostate tissue (Fig. 3C and D). In conclusion, our study identified a potential pathogenic pathway for BPH, that lower cg14345882 levels upregulated BTN3A2 expression, which increased the risk of BPH. Our study did not identify a potential pathogenic pathway associated with C4A, probably because the Bonferroni correction was too stringent. SMR identified druggable genes related to BPH

Using data from the DGIdb database, we identified 10,639 druggable genes (Supplementary Table S14). In addition, we extracted 4,463 druggable genes from the gene “druggable” review by Finan et al. (Supplementary Table S15). Finally, 10,745 unique druggable genes were summarized from the DGIdb database and the review by Finan et al. for further analysis (Supplementary Table S16).

We used whole-blood eQTL data from the eQTLGen consortium and intersected them with druggable genes to obtain eQTLs for druggable genes. we extracted cis-eQTLs that were located in the range of 1,000 kb on either side of the TSS of the target genes and closely related to the gene expression (P < 5 × 10–8). Finally, 662,968 cis-eQTLs were included in this study for SMR analysis. We used these cis-eQTLs as IVs to represent lifetime exposures to the gene product and assessed the impact of these exposures on BPH risk. Through SMR analysis, we identified 30 druggable genes associated with BPH (Fig. 4). Among them, CCNA2, GSTO2, PTGES3, and CCNE1 were defined by Finan et al. as therapeutic targets for small molecules, biotherapeutics, and clinical-stage drug candidates, and have great potential to become target genes for BPH. Although there are currently no drugs that target BTN3A2 and C4A, our study provides strong evidence for their important role in the development of BPH, which is worthy of continued exploration in future studies.Fig. 4Druggable genes related to BPH. PheWAS elucidated the potential health effects of druggable genes

To model the potential health effects of BPH drug target genes, we performed a PheWAS using data from the UK Biobank. We focus on reporting potential side effects of CCNA2, GSTO2, PTGES3, and CCNE1, and the full PheWAS can be found in Supplementary Table S18. CCNA2 is located on chromosome 4, and SNPs in the 100 kb range on either side of its TSS are associated with an increased risk of alcoholism. GSTO2 is located on chromosome 10, and SNPs in the range of 100 kb on either side of the TSS increase the risk of oliguria and anuria. PTGES3 is located on chromosome 12, and SNPs in the range of 100 kb on either side of the TSS are associated with circulatory disorders such as atrial flutter, atrial fibrillation, and hypertension. CCNE1 is located on chromosome 19, and PheWAS did not find phenotypes significantly associated with it. In conclusion, follow-up studies should be aware of the potential side effects that may occur with these gene-targeted drugs.

MR analyses identified the effect of immune pathways on BPH

To identify cell types associated with BPH, we used FUMA to combine scRNA-seq data with GWAS summary statistics. In blood, we found that GWAS for BPH was mainly enriched in macrophages with high expression of Platelet factor 4, B cells with high expression of ribosomal protein S27, and macrophages with high expression of angiotensin-converting enzyme (Supplementary Table S19). In prostate tissue, no cell type was associated with BPH.

To further elucidate the effect of immune pathways on BPH, we performed MR analysis of 731 immune cells and 91 inflammatory factors (Supplementary Table S20 and Supplementary Table S23). According to the selection criteria of IVs, we included 10,909 SNPs for immune cells, and 1,573 SNPs for inflammatory factors (Supplementary Table S21 and Supplementary Table S24). F for all SNPs were all > 10. The results showed all SNPs were effective strong IVs. We found that CD20 on IgD + CD38br (odds ratio (OR) = 1.03, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.07–1.11, PFDR = 0.10, Maximum likelihood) increases the risk of BPH, while HLA DR on CD33dim HLA DR + CD11b- (OR = 0.96, 95% CI = 0.94–0.98, PFDR = 0.17, Maximum likelihood) decreases the risk of BPH (Fig. 5A). Among the inflammatory factors, IL-17 (OR = 1.25, 95% CI = 1.09–1.43, PFDR = 0.12, Maximum likelihood) also increases the risk of BPH (Fig. 5B). MR analyses to identify the effect of metabolomics on BPH

To identify the effects of circulating metabolites on BPH, we performed MR analysis of 1,091 circulating metabolites (Supplementary Table S26). According to the selection criteria of IVs, we included 20,629 SNPs and F for all SNPs were all > 10 (Supplementary Table S27). We found that sebacate (C10-DC) levels (OR = 1.05, 95% CI = 1.02–1.08, P = 3.0 × 10–4, Maximum likelihood), cysteinylglycine disulfide levels (OR = 1.11, 95% CI = 1.05–1.18, P = 5.18 × 10–4, Weighted median) and cysteinylglycine oxidized levels (OR = 1.09, 95% CI = 1.04–1.14, P = 3.87 × 10–4, Weighted median) increase the risk of BPH. In addition, we also found a series of circulating metabolites suggestively related to BPH, including 16-ahydroxy DHEA 3-sulfate, which is the same as in previous observational studies (Fig. 5C). Sensitivity analyses proved the reliability of our results (Supplementary Table S28).

Discussion

In this study, we used large genomic data and a multi-omics approach to explore biomolecular associations with BPH. Through multiple analysis methods, we identified two genes, BTN3A2 and C4A, that are highly related to BPH in blood and prostate tissue. To further explore and improve the pathogenic mechanism of BPH, we explored the DNA methylation sites and identified the cg14345882-BTN3A2-BPH pathogenic pathway. In addition, our study builds on previous studies. Multiple studies have demonstrated that chronic inflammation is a major factor in the occurrence and development of BPH4,41. We used FUMA to verify that BPH-related SNPs are enriched in a variety of immune cells and used MR to prove the role of the immune system in the development of BPH. Our study also identified 30 potential genetic drug targets, including two highly related genes, BTN3A2 and C4A. These targets deserve further investigation into their relationship to BPH, as genomic evidence in drug discovery greatly increases the possibility of successful drug development42. PheWAS enables us to identify potential health effects of targeted drugs. Finally, we explored circulating metabolites that influence BPH. Our study identified many BPH-related traits, from genes to immune pathways to circulating metabolites, laying a solid foundation for BPH research and providing directions for basic and translational research on therapeutic drugs.

Our TWAS identified several genes associated with BPH, and after multiple sensitivity analyses, BTN3A2 and C4A were identified as high-confidence associated genes. BTN3A2 gene is located in the MHC proximal telomeric region (class I) on the sixth chromosome, encoding a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily and expressed in most human immune cell subsets, including T cells, B cells, monocytes, and natural killer cells43–45. Up to now, the relationship between BTN3A2 and BPH still lacks in-depth research. We think that BTN3A2 mainly promotes the occurrence and development of BPH through two pathways. (1) BTN3A2 can mediate the activation of γδ T cells and activate Vγ9Vδ2 T cells through phosphoantigen (PAg)43,46. Through the antigen-presenting cell properties of Vγ9Vδ2 T cells, γδ T cells can stimulate αβ T cell activation and proliferation, promote the release of inflammatory factors including IFN-γ, induce the proliferation of BPH stromal cell lines, and stimulate the growth of BPH epithelial cells47–51. In addition, IFN-γ upregulates the expression of BTN3A2 gene, becoming positive feedback, further promoting the occurrence of cellular immune response and thus promoting the progression of BPH52. (2) Up-regulation of BTN3A2 gene expression will induce the activation of γδ T cells, which can to produce IL-1743,53. In healthy individuals, only a few γδ T cells produce IL-1754. However, in disease environments such as inflammation, these cells accumulate in large numbers, leading to the overexpression of IL-1754. IL-17 overexpression in BPH disrupts tolerance to self-antigens, increases self-immunogenic potential, and leads to chronic immune-mediated tissue destruction1,55. Interestingly, in the MR analysis of the immune pathway, we also found that IL-17 can promote the occurrence and development of BPH, which further verified the reliability of our research results. The T cells in BPH are mainly αβ T cells, and the number of γδ T cells is relatively rare50. Therefore, we believe that BTN3A2 mainly promotes the occurrence and development of BPH through pathway 1, and the role of pathway 2 is relatively limited. In addition, we also identified the cg14345882-BTN3A2-BPH pathogenic pathway by SMR analysis. we found that cg14345882, a DNA methylation site located upstream of BTN3A2, negatively regulated BTN3A2 expression, suggesting that there is a link between cg14345882, BTN3A2 expression and the risk of BPH, that lower cg14345882 levels up-regulated the expression of BTN3A2, which increased the risk of BPH. The discovery of the cg14345882-BTN3A2-BPH pathogenic pathway completes the pathogenic mechanism of BPH, and animal experiments should be performed in the future to further validate this pathogenic pathway.

We found that the C4A gene is closely related to BPH. The C4A gene is located in the MHC class III gene region on chromosome 6, and the C4A protein it encodes is implicated in the formation and clearance of the immune complex and plays a key role in the classical and lectin pathways of the complement system56–58. The complement system, an important component of the innate immune system, is involved in tissue regeneration, angiogenesis, and inflammatory amplification. A previous study found significant deposition of complement proteins in prostate tissues of human BPH patients and significant increases in complement gene expression and complement protein levels in BPH tissues of a rat model59,60. In the early stages of BPH, C4A protein levels are upregulated, activating the classical pathway of the complement system and the lectin pathway. Excessive or uncontrolled activation of the complement system leads to tissue damage and may result in epithelial-mesenchymal transition or tissue remodeling, which promotes prostate proliferation and fibrosis, ultimately leading to the onset and progression of BPH60,61. In conclusion, our study suggests that the C4A gene mediates the development of BPH through hyperactivation of the complement system. However, because the C4A gene is highly polymorphic, with more than 40 different alleles, gene duplications, and “null alleles”, its pathogenic mechanism in BPH still needs to be further evaluated by experimental studies62.

Using SMR analysis, our study identified 30 possible genetic drug targets, looking for new uses for old drugs or exploring new drug targets to provide new directions for therapeutic approaches to BPH. The PTGES3 gene is located on chromosome 12 and is responsible for encoding prostaglandin E2 synthase63. Heat shock protein 90 (Hsp90) is a co-chaperone protein of PTGES3 and plays an essential role in PTGES3 activation64. PTGES3 and Hsp90 are jointly responsible for prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) synthesis. Our study found that overexpression of the PTGES3 gene is associated with an increased risk of BPH. A study showed that PGE2 synthesized by PTGES3 had increased levels in the prostate tissues of patients with BPH65. PGE2 can activate EGFR, prompting it to bind to TGF-α and form dimers, activating the downstream EGFR-ERK1/2 signaling pathway and inducing prostate cell growth and proliferation66,67. Gedunin, a limonin with anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, and anti-viral effects, can inhibit the activity of the Hsp90 protein and induce the degradation of Hsp-dependent proteins68. We believe that gedunin may treat BPH by degrading PTGES3, down-regulating PGE2 levels in prostate tissues of BPH patients, and inhibiting the growth and proliferation of prostate cells. However, our PheWAS found that SNPs near PTGES3 may be associated with circulatory disorders such as atrial flutter, arrhythmias, and hypertension. In conclusion, our study identified that PTGES3 is a potential target for the treatment of BPH by affecting prostate cell growth and proliferation. Gedunin treatment for BPH deserves further investigation and the risk of circulatory disorders caused by the use of geduning should be emphasized. Interestingly, we also found that BTN3A2 and C4A could be drug targets for the treatment of BPH. Although there are currently no drugs that explicitly target BTN3A2 and C4A, their pathogenic mechanism of increasing the risk of BPH by activating the immune system may provide direction for drug development.

Metabolic disorders and immune dysregulation are recognized as important risk factors for BPH, but knowledge of these associations is limited69. We performed MR analysis of the metabolome and immune pathways in BPH and demonstrated the important roles of circulating metabolites, immune cells, and inflammatory factors in BPH. First, our study supports previous observational evidence. The development of BPH is strongly associated with steroid hormone dysregulation, and an observational study found that serum levels of dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) were significantly lower in patients with BPH compared to healthy men70. Yang et al. concluded that the reduction of DHEA has a greater impact on BPH patients and may serve as a disease marker for effective prevention and diagnosis of BPH70. Our findings were identical and further linked this association to 16-ahydroxy DHEA 3-sulfate. In MR analysis of immune pathways, we found that hyperactivation of multiple immune cells may increase the risk of BPH, including multiple B cells, T cell counts, and monocyte counts. This is consistent with previous studies69. IL-17 is thought to be an important player in prostate inflammation, and its levels are negligible in normal prostate but highly increased in BPH tissues71. IL-17 is a local regulator of the immune response to BPH, amplifying prostate tissue damage by maintaining the inflammatory microenvironment and oxidative stress71. Our study found that upregulation of IL-17 levels increases the risk of BPH and is involved in the BTN3A2 gene pathogenic pathway. In addition, our study identified many metabolic and immune biomolecules associated with BPH. Although the mechanism of action of these biomolecules on BPH lacks in-depth study, we believe that changes in the levels of these circulating metabolites, immune cells, and inflammatory factors play an important role in the development of BPH and need to be further explored in future studies. Our study also provides insights into the optimization of RCTs. RCTs are the gold standard for exploring causality16. The metabolome and immune pathways have irreplaceable roles in organismal homeostasis, so it is worthwhile to explore the roles of the metabolome and immune pathways in BPH through RCTs. The results of this study provide theoretical foundation for RCTs. For a lot of circulating metabolites, immune cells, and inflammatory factors, it is difficult to conduct RCTs on each biomolecule. MR studies can help explore new approach of research by identifying causal relationships between biomolecules and BPH. We can also quantify risk factors using the OR value to provide reference variables and outcomes in RCTs.

Our study identifies multiple novel risk factors associated with BPH, providing new targets for BPH diagnosis. Currently, clinical differentiation between BPH and early prostate cancer (PCA) relies on prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels and direct rectal examination (DRE)72. Based on these results, further transrectal ultrasound-guided prostate biopsy (TRUS) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can be performed72. However, PSA has a low specificity, making it difficult to effectively differentiate between benign prostatic hyperplasia and PCA, and studies have shown that 20–67% of patients are over-treated due to PSA misdiagnosis73,74. In individuals with an abnormal PSA or DRE test, a prostate tissue biopsy is subsequently performed to confirm or exclude the diagnosis of PCA. TRUS is a highly invasive test accompanied by symptoms such as bleeding, impotence and pain75. MRI has improved biopsy options to some extent, but is expensive and some false-positive results are still reported76,77. In conclusion, there remains a need for more accurate methods of risk stratifying men presenting with LUTS to prevent overdiagnosis and unnecessary treatment of patients with benign conditions. This would significantly reduce the rate of overdiagnosis of patients and alleviate financial and administrative pressures on health-care providers78. We believe that it is necessary and feasible to construct BPH risk assessment models based on changes in specific genes, circulating metabolites, immune cells, and inflammatory factors. The model should take into account changes in the levels of all risk factors associated with BPH, not just specific risk factors. This model, which uses less invasive liquid biopsies and is based on molecular techniques such as genomics, metabolomics and immunomics, is expected to refine existing diagnostic methods and enable risk stratification and personalized treatment. It has been shown that identifying BPH and early PCA based on changes in the expression levels of DNA methylation sites is more accurate than the PSA examination (area under the curve = 0.99 vs 0.79)79.

Our study also has some limitations. Our TWAS and SMR analyses used only cis-eQTLs as genetic instruments, but trans-eQTLs also regulate gene expression. Moreover, we used eQTL data for drug-target analyses because eQTL data are more comprehensive than existing protein QTL (pQTL) studies, allowing us to detect more drug-targeting genes80. However, most approved drug therapies are protein-targeted81. More comprehensive pQTL data are needed to support further studies. We only explored the health effects of eQTLs within ± 100 kb of the drug-targeted genes’ TSS in diseases. However, the effects of drugs on their targets are quite broad, and many off-target effects cannot be explored by MR. Further exploration through subsequent basic and clinical experiments is needed to gain a more comprehensive understanding. In addition, due to the lack of diverse datasets, the human dataset we used throughout the study consisted almost exclusively of participants of European ancestry. Caution is needed when extrapolating our results to other ethnic groups due to differences in allele frequencies and genetic structure of ancestral complex traits. Finally, based on public databases, we conducted a large-scale multi-omics study that identified 2 high-confidence genes, 30 druggable genes, and a large number of immune cells, inflammatory factors, and circulating metabolites associated with BPH. Our findings provide a solid foundation for future studies. However, we did not perform animal experiments, which will be necessary in the future.

Conclusions

Our study performed multiple analytical methods identified BTN3A2 and C4A as high-confidence genes for BPH and identified the cg14345882-BTN3A2-BPH pathogenic pathway. We also used druggable gene data to identify 30 promising therapeutic target genes, including BTN3A2 and C4A. For MR analysis of immune pathways, we identified immune cell surface molecules as well as the inflammatory factor IL-17 as risk factors for BPH. In addition, we found that disulfide levels, oxidation levels of cysteinylglycine, and sebacate levels increase the risk of BPH. In conclusion, our study lays a solid foundation for future studies on the pathogenic mechanisms of BPH, explores new directions for the therapeutic approach of BPH, and provides a new method for differential diagnosis between BPH and early PCA.
