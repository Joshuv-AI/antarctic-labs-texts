Micro-computed tomography evaluation of aligner fitting on anchorage attachments
Authors: İlter Burak Köse, Eyüp Burak Küçük, Meral Arslan Malkoç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the fit of clear aligners (CAs) manufactured from different materials and thicknesses to anchorage attachments using micro-computed tomography-based (Micro-CT) three-dimensional and two-dimensional analyses.MethodsForty CAs fabricated from different thicknesses and materials (0.50 mm CA-Soft, 0.625 mm CA-Medium, 0.75 mm CA-Hard polyethylene terephthalate glycol [PET-G], and 0.75 mm CA PRO-Hard ABA) were fitted onto a master model with rectangular attachments on the first and second premolars. Micro-CT scans were obtained to assess the air gap between the aligner and attachment. Total air-gap volume was calculated using three-dimensional volumetric analysis, while mean gap width was measured in predefined two-dimensional sections. Adjusted mean differences were interpreted using a practical relevance threshold of 20 µm.ResultsCA PRO-Hard showed the lowest mean three-dimensional air-gap volume, but differences among groups were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Two-dimensional analysis revealed localized differences in aligner–attachment adaptation. The most pronounced and practically relevant differences were detected in the palatal and gingival sections (P < 0.001), where PET-G groups showed greater gap widths than CA PRO-Hard and exceeded the 20 µm threshold. In the sagittomedian section, mean differences also exceeded 20 µm, but confidence intervals indicated cautious interpretation. No statistically significant or practically relevant differences were observed in the transversomedian (P = 0.054), buccal, frontomedian, mesial, or occlusal sections.ConclusionsMicro-CT enabled detailed assessment of CA fit. Although volumetric analysis showed no significant material-related differences, section-based analysis demonstrated localized adaptation differences, particularly in palatal, gingival, and sagittomedian regions. CA PRO-Hard showed superior fit in these areas.Keywords: Clear aligner fitting, Attachment, Gap width, Aligner material

INTRODUCTION

In clear aligner (CA) treatment, several auxiliary elements and interventions, such as attachments, intraoral elastics, and interproximal reduction, are required to facilitate tooth movement and enhance the predictability of treatment.1 Specifically, attachments are used to regulate tooth movement and manage anchorage units.2,3

Orthodontic aligners of varying thicknesses can be used to adjust the force exerted on the teeth, corresponding to the specific stage of treatment.4 Previous studies have demonstrated that the degree of activation and the thickness of the aligner significantly affect the orthodontic force generated by the appliance.5,6

Notably, direct digital three-dimensional (3D) printing methods and thermoforming processes are preferred for manufacturing aligners,7 and thermoforming is widely applied in aligner production.8,9 The thermoforming process thins the aligner material unevenly, altering force generation depending on the local thickness. Previous research also indicates that the thermoforming process reduces the thickness of the aligners compared with the original size of the thermoplastic foil.8 The mechanical behavior of the thermoplastic material used to manufacture CAs, along with the aligner thickness uniformity and geometry, plays a critical role in achieving the desired clinical results in difficult orthodontic movements.10 The thickness homogeneity of the aligner determines the magnitude and distribution of applied forces; thickness variations affect accuracy and adaptation to teeth.11

In CA treatment, the movements of the tooth, as well as anchorage and retention, are contingent upon the gap width between the aligner and the tooth and/or attachment surface. An increased gap width indicates poor fitting of the aligner, whereas a decreased gap width indicates good aligner fitting, supporting treatment objectives and enhancing the predictability of the treatment.12 In addition to the differences in thickness generated during the thermoforming procedure, the gap between the aligner and the tooth changes in certain areas owing to differences in tooth morphology, aligner material, and thickness.13

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) is a non-destructive, high-resolution method used to evaluate the fitting of aligners. This scanning/analysis method enables the acquisition of data concerning the gap between the aligner and the tooth without causing any damage to the aligner material or the resin model.14 The fitting,13 thickness,15 and homogeneity16 of CAs, as well as the impact of the thermoforming procedure17 and production methods,7 have previously been examined by micro-CT. However, to the best of our knowledge, no micro-CT studies have specifically addressed the fitting of CAs with attachments.

This study aimed to assess the adaptation of CAs with varying thicknesses and material compositions to rectangular anchorage attachments using micro-CT scanning and analysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This in vitro study was approved by the Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Hatay Mustafa Kemal University (number 04/2022) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the participant.

An initial sample-size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Franz Faul, University of Kiel, Kiel, Germany) based on the expected effect size for gap width and gap volume measurements. However, during the final statistical planning, the aligner was defined as the independent experimental unit to account for the non-independence of repeated measurements obtained from the same aligner. Consequently, individual point-level, attachment-level, and section-level measurements were treated as repeated observations rather than independent samples. Based on the final experimental design, 10 aligners per group were included in the analysis.

The master model was obtained from a skeletal Class I patient with minimal crowding and printed using a Form 3+ 3D printer (Formlabs, Somerville, MA, USA). The patient had no restorations, missing teeth, periodontal problems, reduced clinical crowns, or coronal surface abnormalities.

A total of 40 CA-branded CAs (Scheu Dental, Iserlohn, Germany) were used, with 10 allocated to each group, using three thicknesses and two different materials (i.e., 0.50 mm, CA-Soft polyethylene terephthalate glycol [PET-G]; 0.625 mm, CA-Medium PET-G; 0.75 mm, CA-Hard PET-G; and 0.75 mm, CA PRO-Hard ABA) (Table 1). One type of standardized attachment was positioned on the first and second premolar teeth of the master model, and the fit of the aligners to these two attachments was evaluated. Therefore, 20 measurements were performed on the attachments in each group to assess the fit of all 10 aligners with the two attachments (Table 1).

A vertical rectangular attachment measuring 3 mm in height, 2 mm in width, and 1 mm in depth was precisely positioned on the convex buccal surface at the midpoint of the upper first and second premolar teeth using Ortho Analyzer 3D software (3Shape A/S, Copenhagen, Denmark). The attachment was oriented mesiodistally and occlusogingivally (Figure 1).

Vertical rectangular attachment designed using Ortho Analyzer 3D software. A, Frontal, B, oblique, and C, profile views of the vertical rectangular attachment.

In this study, all aligners were thermoformed using an Erkopress ci motion device (Erkodent Erich Kopp GmbH, Pfalzgrafenweiler, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions to prevent overheating and ensure consistency during fabrication. The aligners were trimmed 2–3 mm above the cervical gingiva, and care was taken to remove them from the master model without causing any deformation.

A SkyScan 1172 micro-CT system (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium) was used to measure the air-gap volume (mm3) and gap width (μm) between the aligner and the attachment. Each aligner was scanned in a standard position using a standard scanning procedure (100 kV acceleration voltage, 100 µA beam current, 0.5 mm aluminum filter, 13.68 µm voxel size at 2,000 × 2,000-pixel resolution, 320° rotation in 0.4° steps, and average scan time of 55 minutes). Approximately 1,300 cross-sectional images of each sample were captured using an 11-megapixel camera (Hamamatsu, Tokyo, Japan). Images were reconstructed using NRecon software (Bruker) to obtain the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format with standard parameters. After scanning, the sections were combined using the CTVox software (Bruker), and the respective 3D images were obtained (Figure 2). To ensure consistency among the micro-CT data, all aligners were scanned and analyzed using the same protocol and identical grayscale thresholding parameters.

Three-dimensional modeling of the attachment-aligner fitting using CTVox software.

Micro-CT images were analyzed using the CTAn software 1.20.8.0 (Bruker). The attachment was delineated in each cross-section using the region of interest function to separate it from the other elements in three dimensions (Figure 3). Subsequently, the air-gap volume between the attachment and aligner was calculated using the same task list as a standard for each sample (Figure 4).

The region of interest function was used to separate the attachment region from the air gap in all three dimensions (area highlighted in red).

The air gap was separated from the external environment and dental tissues using the binary page tool.

For each attachment, measurements were obtained from multiple predefined points and sections. These point-level measurements were used to calculate section-level and attachment-level summaries. The mean gap width (MGW) of the section was calculated by taking the average of eight measurement points. Since each attachment was evaluated in nine sections, 72 measurements were obtained from each attachment. As two attachments were positioned on each aligner, a total of 144 points were measured per aligner. The final 2D and 3D analyses were performed by a single investigator with extensive experience in micro-CT measurements, who was blinded to all aligners.

Because no universally accepted clinically validated threshold has been established for aligner–attachment MGW differences, a predefined practical relevance threshold of 20 µm was used for cautious interpretation of the section-wise differences. This threshold was selected because it is greater than the micro-CT voxel size used in the present study (13.68 µm), thereby representing a difference above the basic image-resolution level. Accordingly, adjusted mean differences were interpreted not only according to statistical significance but also in relation to this practical relevance threshold.

Statistical analysis

The independent experimental unit was defined as the aligner rather than the individual attachment or point-level measurement. For the 2D MGW analysis, the eight point-level measurements within each anatomical section were first averaged to obtain one section-specific MGW value for each attachment. Because two premolar attachments were measured on each aligner, attachment-level MGW values were then averaged for each aligner and section before inferential statistical analysis. Thus, the final analytical dataset consisted of one MGW value per aligner for each of the nine anatomical sections.

For the 3D air-gap volume analysis, measurements were initially obtained separately for the two premolar attachments of each aligner. To maintain consistency with the revised analytical framework, the aligner was defined as the independent experimental unit. Therefore, the two attachment-level air-gap volume values were averaged to obtain a single 3D air-gap volume value for each aligner before statistical analysis. Consequently, one-way ANOVA was performed using aligner-level observations (n = 10 aligners per group; total n = 40 aligners).

Descriptive statistics were calculated as mean and standard deviation for each group and anatomical section. All MGW values were expressed in micrometers (µm). Normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of residual plots, whereas homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test.

3D air-gap volume was compared among the four aligner groups using one-way ANOVA. Because the group sizes were balanced and equal (n = 10 per group), ANOVA was retained despite minor deviations from normality.

For the 2D MGW analysis, a linear mixed-effects model was used to account for the repeated section-level measurements obtained from the same aligner. Group, Section, and the Group × Section interaction were included as fixed effects, and AlignerID was included as a random intercept. The model structure was as follows:

The primary hypothesis concerned the Group × Section interaction, namely whether the effect of aligner material and nominal thickness on MGW differed according to anatomical section. When a significant interaction was detected, section-wise estimated marginal means were calculated and pairwise comparisons among groups were performed using Tukey-adjusted contrasts.

Post hoc or observed power values were not used for statistical interpretation. Instead, section-wise results were reported using adjusted mean differences, 95% confidence intervals, and Tukey-adjusted P values. To avoid overinterpretation based solely on statistical significance, the magnitude of MGW differences was also interpreted relative to a predefined practical relevance threshold of 20 µm. This threshold was selected because it is greater than the micro-CT voxel size used in the present study (13.68 µm), thereby representing a difference above the basic image-resolution level. This threshold was used as a practical interpretive criterion and not as a clinically validated cutoff value.

A two-tailed significance level of P < 0.05 was adopted. Statistical analyses were performed using R software 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the lme4, lmerTest, emmeans, rstatix, and effectsize packages. Degrees of freedom for the linear mixed-effects models were estimated using the Kenward–Roger approximation, and pairwise comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means with Tukey adjustment.

2D section-based MGW analysis

The linear mixed-effects analysis demonstrated a significant Group × Section interaction (F (24, 288) = 12.12, P < 0.001), indicating that differences among aligner materials varied according to the anatomical section evaluated. Because the omnibus interaction test was significant, section-wise estimated marginal means and Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons were subsequently performed. Section-wise adjusted mean differences were interpreted relative to the predefined practical relevance threshold of 20 µm (Table 3). The palatal and gingival sections showed the strongest support for a practically relevant difference, because the adjusted mean differences between the PET-G groups and CA PRO-Hard exceeded 20 µm and the lower limits of the 95% confidence intervals remained above this threshold (P < 0.001). In the sagittomedian section, the point estimates exceeded 20 µm; however, the confidence intervals crossed the predefined threshold, indicating that practical relevance should be interpreted cautiously. No statistically significant group differences were observed in the transversomedian section (P = 0.054), and all observed mean differences remained below the predefined practical relevance threshold. No statistically or practically relevant differences were observed in the buccal, frontomedian, mesial, or occlusal sections. DISCUSSION

The fitting of aligners with anchorage attachments and teeth is essential for successful orthodontic treatment. Various methods are used to evaluate and quantify the fit of CAs. Aligner fitting can generally be assessed by examining the sections using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).18,19 However, measuring the gap in different regions is necessary to clarify the noncompliant regions, which is difficult to achieve using SEM. A large number of sections are required, making it difficult to rapidly evaluate numerous positions. Notably, the micro-CT method does not cause damage to the samples and/or master model and provides more accurate data than the SEM method, making micro-CT the preferred method for obtaining both 2D and 3D data.20 In the first study conducted using the micro-CT method, Lombardo et al.13 compared six different brands of aligners with the same thickness and concluded that further investigation of different aligner thicknesses and attachment types was needed. To the best of our knowledge, the present in vitro study is the first to measure and compare the fitting of different types of aligners with anchorage attachments using the micro-CT method. The most notable advantages of this method are the absence of special sample preparation processes and the ability to repeatedly scan samples without damage, thereby preserving the structural integrity of aligners during the scanning process. Furthermore, 3D images obtained by micro-CT exhibit the resolution and sensitivity required for comprehensive evaluation.21

In this study, micro-CT-based 3D volumetric evaluation was performed to compare aligners manufactured using different materials and thicknesses. Although CA PRO-Hard demonstrated the lowest mean 3D air-gap volume, the difference was not statistically significant (Table 2). This finding suggests that global volumetric assessments may obscure clinically meaningful localized adaptation differences, indicating that the 3D gap volume alone is insufficient to distinguish aligner performance. The retention of CAs can be influenced by various factors, including the type of attachment, as well as the material and thickness of the aligner.22 However, some researchers have noted that although differences may exist in specific regions of the attachments, the overall fit remains consistent.18 Although the gap volume between the aligner and the tooth has previously been measured in 3D,13,15,17 this study represents the first attempt to measure the gap between the attachment and the aligner.

The section-wise 2D analysis showed that differences in MGW were mainly localized in specific regions rather than being uniformly distributed across the attachment. The most relevant differences were observed in the palatal and gingival sections, where the adjusted mean differences between PET-G and CA PRO-Hard groups exceeded the predefined 20 µm practically relevance threshold. Since the confidence intervals also remained above this predefined threshold, these differences can be considered both statistically and practically meaningful.

These findings may be explained by the anatomical complexity of the palatal and gingival areas of a rectangular attachment, where curvatures, margins, and surface transitions can affect material adaptation during thermoforming.23 Additionally, our findings may indicate that multilayer ABA materials provide superior thermomechanical resistance and improved surface conformity compared with monolayer PET-G, suggesting that the use of more flexible materials in aligner production may enhance the fit between the aligner and the anchorage attachment.13,24-26

In the sagittomedian section, the mean differences also exceeded 20 µm; however, the confidence intervals crossed the predefined threshold. Therefore, the practical relevance of these findings should be interpreted with caution, and they should not be considered as evidence of definitive practical superiority. In contrast, the transversomedian section showed only borderline evidence of a group effect, and the observed differences remained below the predefined practical relevance threshold.

No relevant differences were found in the buccal, frontomedian, mesial, or occlusal sections, indicating comparable adaptation among materials in these areas. Overall, material-related fit differences appeared to be region-specific, with the palatal and gingival sections showing the strongest evidence for differences exceeding the predefined practical relevance threshold, whereas the sagittomedian finding, despite being statistically significant, should be interpreted as only suggestive in practical terms.

Although section-specific variations were observed within material groups, the revised statistical analysis did not support a consistent directional trend across the assessed regions and planes in this study. Nevertheless, the revised statistical analysis did not support a consistent pattern of variation across the assessed sections. Thus, these findings should be considered section-specific differences rather than a uniform spatial trend. In line with Mantovani et al.,18 who reported a non-uniform aligner-attachment adaptation, our results also suggest that fitting may vary between sections and planes. Such differences may be associated with attachment geometry and the thermoforming process, including material flexibility and vacuum-forming effects. In the present study, the CA PRO-Hard group showed significantly better fit than the other groups in both the sagittomedian and gingival sections. This difference may be related to its material composition and mechanical properties, which can affect adaptation during the thermoforming process.

Although 3D-printed aligners are slowly gaining attention,7 thermoforming remains the most widely used method for producing CAs.9 Ihssen et al.27 demonstrated that manual thermoforming is a reliable technique, but generally, aligner materials can be affected by different production processes, including pressure vacuuming, cooling, trimming, and cutting.28 Additionally, even if the material is printed using the same 3D printer and stereolithography file, resin models may not be identical, and CAs can be directly affected by the model’s features.27 Hence, a single resin model was used for all samples in this study. Moreover, studies have indicated that the thermoforming procedure results in a reduction in aligner thickness, particularly in the gingival region, which correlates with diminished tooth movement and decreased predictability of treatment outcomes.13,16,29 Therefore, the material used for aligners is crucial for minimizing the loss of tooth movement and enhancing the overall predictability of orthodontic treatment.

In this study, 2D and 3D measurements were performed, revealing differences in the aligner-attachment fit. This distinction sets this study apart from studies that utilize only 2D19 or 3D17 measurement methods. Although no difference was found in the total gap volume in the 3D measurements, significant differences were observed in the 2D measurements in different planes and sections. Some studies have examined aligner fitting using the micro-CT method.13,15 The 2D analysis involved only one plane and one section for each tooth. In comparison, a total of nine sections, three in each plane, were analyzed in the present study. As a result, attachment was analyzed from various aspects and the factors affecting the fitting and the regions where the fitting decreases are presented in detail. Therefore, this study was enhanced in terms of its analytical rigor and validity.

This study had several limitations. First, we conducted measurements only on the maxillary arch and premolar teeth, and only one type of rectangular attachment was considered. In addition, the measurements were performed in a laboratory environment, and the thermal changes in the oral environment and the effects of saliva were neglected. Moreover, the use of a single master model limited the generalizability and external validity of our findings. Another limitation of the present study is that all micro-CT measurements were performed once by a single investigator. Although this approach may reduce variability related to differences in observer experience and measurement technique, intra-observer and inter-observer reliability could not be statistically assessed. Future in vivo studies evaluating the interactions between saliva, temperature, wear, and occlusal loading would provide additional insights to validate these results under real-world treatment conditions.

CONCLUSIONS

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, micro-CT analysis showed no significant differences in overall 3D air-gap volume among the tested CA materials. In contrast, section-based 2D gap width analysis revealed that fit discrepancies were region-specific. The most pronounced and definitively practically relevant differences were observed in the palatal section, followed by the gingival and sagittomedian sections, where PET-G aligners consistently showed greater gap widths than CA PRO-Hard.

Overall, multilayer ABA-based CA PRO-Hard showed lower MGWs, particularly in the palatal, gingival, and sagittomedian sections, which may represent clinically challenging areas for aligner adaptation around rectangular attachments. The results indicate that aligner material composition may influence attachment fit in specific areas, and that combined 2D and 3D micro-CT evaluation is a valuable approach for comprehensively evaluating aligner–attachment adaptation. Further in vivo studies should be conducted to confirm the clinical relevance of these findings.
