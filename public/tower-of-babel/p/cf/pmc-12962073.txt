Developing and Evaluating an Inpatient Caregiver Support Program: Feasibility, Acceptability, and Perceived Impact
Authors: Joan M Griffin, Lynne M Vitagliano, Angela K Wold, Claire Yee, Charles Van Der Walt, Kairin Whiteside, Beverly B Hansen, Sherry Chesak, Kelli D Strader, Ellen C Meltzer, Amber Drake, Nandita Khera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and perceived impact of a hospital-based Caregiver Support Program (CSP) that supports family care partners (FCPs) of seriously ill hospitalized patients by addressing their unmet needs through emotional support, help navigating the hospital, and referrals to trusted and vetted resources.Patients and MethodsThe evaluation, conducted from October 15, 2021 to January 30, 2024, was designed using a 2-phase interrupted time series. Phase 1 included prelaunch and postlaunch surveys delivered to staff and FCPs on 2 hospital units. CSP procedures were refined based on phase 1 results, and then new procedures were evaluated in phase 2 on a third unit using the same survey procedures. Feasibility was measured by the number of FCPs seen, total visits, contact hours, and time per visit. Changes in staff and FCP acceptability and perceived care quality were assessed before and after the CSP’s implementation.ResultsIn phase 1, 253 FCPs received 282 hours of support, and 100% (8/8) of staff at postlaunch with knowledge about the CSP program strongly believed that it benefited patients and FCPs. In phase 2, 88% (53/61) of FCPs rated the program as very or extremely helpful. Care quality improved over time, but differences from prelaunch to postlaunch were not statistically significant.ConclusionThe CSP is a feasible and acceptable approach to support FCPs that is highly valued by both staff and FCPs. Program expansion may benefit both FCPs and staff by improving care quality, including communication between families, patients, and staff, and access to vetted support resources.

Family care partners (FCPs) of patients with serious illnesses play a vital role in patient safety and well-being.1 Compared with noncare partners, FCPs face higher risks for depression, anxiety, and poorer health2,3 due to the strain of balancing the physical and emotional demands of providing care with family and work responsibilities.3 Hospitalizations can further exacerbate FCPs’ stress because of uncertainty, anxiety, and grief,4, 5, 6 often causing them to neglect their own well-being during a particularly vulnerable time.7 Yet, identifying and assessing FCPs’ needs has not been a priority in most hospital settings.8,9

Health care providers’ paramount concern is addressing patients’ immediate needs, with scarce time to assess and address FCPs’ well-being, especially if support needs are not directly related to patient care or treatment.8,10, 11, 12 The United States National Strategy to Support Family Care13 has underscored the need for new care models to identify and support FCPs, especially during patient hospitalizations.

In 2021, Mayo Clinic Arizona launched the Caregiver Support Program (CSP), a peer support program using social workers and trained volunteers to support FCPs of hospitalized patients. Peer support has been shown to improve patient self-care and care navigation in outpatient settings,14,15 but few studies have evaluated its use with FCPs of adult patients during inpatient care.16,17 Therefore, we developed a quality improvement project that included an evaluation to assess the program’s feasibility and acceptability on FCPs, staff, and volunteers, and explored its potential to improve perceptions of care quality.14,16 Here we describe the program’s development and present findings related to its feasibility and acceptability in a hospital setting. In addition, we explore potential associations between CSP availability and changes in staff confidence in providing compassionate care and burnout, and for FCPs, changes in perceptions of communication quality, care team compassion, and preparedness for caregiving.

Patients and Methods

Design

We used an interrupted time series design to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and outcomes of the CSP. There was no randomization. The evaluation had 2 phases. Phase 1 included data collection on 2 hospital units before (prelaunch) and after (postlaunch) CSP implementation. Based on these results, program and procedural modifications were made. The CSP continued on phase 1 units with the modified procedures. Phase 2 tested the revised CSP on a third unit using the same evaluation design as phase 1.

Setting

This evaluation took place at a large tertiary care hospital from October 2021 to January 2024. In phase 1, the CSP launched on 2 units (30 and 36 beds), and in phase 2, on a third unit (36 beds). Most patients on the selected units were preparing for, receiving, or recovering from stem cell or solid tumor transplants for cancer treatment. These units were chosen because eligibility for transplant requires an FCP to be present, and for patients with stem cell transplants and some solid organ transplants, hospital stays are often 2-6 weeks long, which allowed for repeated CSP visits.18, 19, 20

Participants

The project was reviewed by the health system’s institutional review board, which determined it to be quality improvement, not human subjects research. The evaluation focused on hospital staff and FCPs. Peer volunteer outcomes were reported elsewhere.21 Eligible staff included physicians, nurses, advanced practice providers, social workers, chaplains, and administrative staff on the designated units. All FCPs caring for patients on the designated units were also eligible. If multiple FCPs were involved, the patient-designated primary FCP completed the study questionnaire.

Development of the Caregiver Peer Support Program

The CSP was grounded in theories of social support that posit that informational, instrumental, and emotional support benefits recipients by buffering stress.22 CSP goals included delivering emotional peer support, assisting with hospital navigation and complex health care matters, and connecting FCPs with information on community resources. It focused explicitly on the FCP’s well-being, not the patient or patient care, and was intended to supplement, not replace, formal support from hospital staff.

Development and Planning

The program was codeveloped by a steering committee of the program’s managers, leaders from Care Management, Volunteer Services, Nursing, Office of Experience, Hematology/Oncology, Chaplain Services, and the Kern Center for the Science of Healthcare Delivery, with guidance from the Ken Hamilton Caregiver Center at Northern Westchester Hospital in New York. The steering committee agreed on a set of principles to guide the CSP mission and procedures, including: (1) being FCP focused, (2) available to single or multiple FCPs, (3) provided free of costs on inpatient units at the point of care, and (4) complementary to, rather than competing with, existing care teams’ roles. Before the launch, CSP managers presented proposed procedures to hospital unit leaders, staff, and FCPs for feedback on workflows and potential barriers. During these meetings, unit leaders and staff stressed that the program must focus solely on FCPs with no interference to hospital workflows or patient care; therefore, phase 1 procedures were developed to be inobtrusive and limit interaction with unit staff.

Training for the CSP

Two licensed social workers with extensive medical social work experience led the CSP. Volunteers were initially identified and screened by the Office of Volunteer Services and then interviewed by the CSP program managers, who evaluated the volunteer’s ability to demonstrate listening skills, empathy, compassion, and abide by assigned roles and boundaries. If program managers determined that there was a mutual fit, volunteers were then trained in CSP procedures, expectations, and boundaries. Training also included modules in empathetic listening and responding techniques, including how to ask open-ended questions to initiate engagement (and provide suggested questions).23 Program managers codeveloped training materials with a nurse educator specializing in empathic communication. Materials were based on principles from the LENS (Listen, Engage perspective taking, Notice your reaction, Share back others’ experience) and EPE (Elicit, Provide, Elicit) models,24,25 and volunteers practiced these methods during training. After classroom training, volunteers shadowed program managers on units, observing modeled techniques during visits with FCPs. After each visit, managers and volunteers debriefed about what went well, identified areas for improvement, and addressed questions. Program managers directly observed all new volunteers to ensure fidelity to CSP procedures and provided quality improvement feedback until volunteers were ready to round independently. Volunteers could also round with experienced peers to observe varied styles and approaches to challenging situations. Training lasted an average of 3 months before volunteers met independently with FCPs. Once trained, volunteers worked at least once per week for a 2-hour shift.

CSP Program Procedures

The Figure shows the standard practice and workflow for the CSP. After the CSP launch (and during the evaluation period), CSP managers and peer volunteers rounded each weekday on the designated hospital units. After each visit, the program manager or volunteer completed an encounter form to document relevant details. Peer visits could continue throughout the patient’s length of stay depending on volunteer availability.

Two months before CSP launch, unit staff received emails with a REDCap electronic survey link. The email introduced the program, its purpose, the rationale for evaluating the program’s outcomes, and instructions on how to complete the survey. Reminders were sent 3 times. The same process was repeated for phase 2. Postlaunch surveys were sent 1 year after initiating the CSP in phase 1 and 7 months after initiating in phase 2. All surveys were anonymous and treated as independent samples due to expected attrition in staff over the evaluation period.

FCP Data Collection

For 2 months before CSP launch, study coordinators approached consecutive FCPs on the designated units and asked them to complete an electronic questionnaire about FCP support. Postlaunch, any FCP who engaged with CSP was contacted approximately 1 week after their final visit to complete a questionnaire, either on paper or electronically. Nonrespondents received reminders via email and telephone.

All administrative data were collected postlaunch. Data were collected after each FCP visit to document details, program fidelity, and intervention reach. These data, independent from patient health records, included visit dates, volunteer ID, discussion topics, resources and referrals offered, follow-up needs, and time spent visiting.

Measures

Data constructs, measures, and data sources are presented in Table 2. Feasibility was assessed during phase 1 using administrative data: number of FCPs, contact hours, visits per FCP, time spent, and volunteer attrition. Staff acceptability (phases 1 and 2) was measured using single-item questions and Likert-scaled items from previous research.26,27 Questions assessed staff level of engagement with the CSP, perceived program value to patients and FCPs, interruptions to clinical workflow, and changes in workload. FCP acceptability was measured using a single question: “Did you find meeting with the Caregiver Support Volunteer helpful?” Five options, ranging from extremely helpful to not helpful at all to no help needed, were included.

Care Quality

Staff outcomes included perceived ability to provide compassionate care and avoid burnout. The 14-item adapted Compassion Competence Scale (5-point Likert) measured communication, sensitivity, and insight.28 Burnout and depersonalization were assessed using validated single-item measures.29

FCP outcomes included perceived communication quality, compassion from the care team, and caregiving preparedness. Communication quality was measured using an adaptation of the 12-item CAPACITY scale (4-point Likert, with 4 having the best communication), the 12-item Schwartz Center Compassionate Care Scale (10-point Likert scale, with 10 having the most compassion),30 and the Preparedness for Caregiving Scale (0-4 response scale, with 4 being the best prepared).31 Postlaunch surveys included questions about CSP usefulness.

Demographic data were collected for staff (eg, age, sex, and years in current position) and FCPs (age, sex, and relationship to care recipient).

Analysis Plan

Descriptive statistics summarized phase 1 and 2 samples. Medians and interquartile ranges were used for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used for continuous variables and trend tests for ordinal variables. As an exploratory study, no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P<.05. Analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Feasibility

In phase 1, the CSP program had 487 visits with 253 FCPs, totaling 282 hours of support. CSP visits averaged 20 minutes, but ranged from 2 to 150 minutes. Most visits involved peer support and resource navigation. Volunteer training was staggered throughout the first year, with 3 training sessions of 2-4 volunteers per session. By years end, 10 volunteers were fully trained, with no attrition.

Staff Acceptability

In phase 1, staff knowledge of the CSP increased from 38% (31 of 82) prelaunch to 64% (37 of 59) postlaunch (P<.01). Half of the sample (29 of 59) reported some engagement with CSP. No statistically significant changes were observed in perceptions of the CSP’s benefits to patients and FCPs, knowledge of caregiving resources, ability to address patient needs, or workload impact. However, confidence in meeting FCP needs significantly increased from 66% prelaunch (54 of 82) to 81% postlaunch (46 of 59) (P=.03). After 1 year, staff with the highest CSP exposure (n=8) were statistically more likely than those with only some or no exposure (n=51) to agree that the program benefited patients (100% [8 of 8] vs 57% [29 of 51], P=.04) and FCPs (100% [8 of 8] vs 59% [30 of 51], P=.03), helped staff better meet patient needs (100% [8 of 8] vs 47% [24 of 51], P=.01), and did not undermine other team support efforts (75.0% [6 of 8] vs 37% [19 of 51], P<.01).

FCP Acceptability

In phase 1, 90% (56 of 75) of FCPs reported the CSP as very or extremely helpful. Only 1 respondent (2%) with a CSP visit reported not needing help.

Care Quality

Staff reported no statistically significant change in their ability to provide compassionate patient care but did show a significant increase in confidence to meet FCP needs—from 66% prelaunch (54 of 82) to 81% postlaunch (46 of 59) (P=.03). Staff also reported decreases in weekly feelings of burnout (from 42% [34 of 82] to 24% [14 of 59]; P<.01) and callousness (from 23% [19 of 82] to 5% [3 of 59]; P<.01). FCPs reported no statistically significant differences in communication quality, perceived compassion from the care team, or their preparedness for caregiving.

Quality Improvements Implemented After Phase 1

Based on phase 1 results, 51% (30 of 59) of staff reported no interaction with the CSP. As a key indicator of acceptability, this result led to 2 major changes for phase 2. In prelaunch planning, CSP managers had only briefed unit leaders about the CSP. For phase 2, they expanded prelaunch outreach by meeting with all unit staff during in-service training. In addition, prelaunch planning concerns from unit leaders and advisory committee members about potential disruptions to workflow resulted in designing the CSP to have minimal staff engagement. In phase 2, CSP volunteers began checking in with staff to inquire about FCPs with potential unmet needs (see the Figure).

Evaluation tools were also revised. Rather than assessing global perceptions about care quality and staff interactions, phase 2 assessments focused on the most recent hospital stay and care team. This allowed for differentiation between general care and specific hospital experiences.

Phase 2 Findings

Response Rates

In phase 2, the FCP prelaunch survey’s response rate was 71% (61 of 86) and the postlaunch survey’s response rate was 44% (61 of 138). Staff response rates on the new unit were 39% for both prelaunch (44 of 113) and postlaunch (50 of 127) surveys. Most postlaunch staff respondents were nurses (40 of 50, 80%) or social workers (7 of 50, 14%), with 60% (30 of 50) having 1-5 years of experience in the health system.

Staff Acceptability

After 7 months, 78% of staff (38 of 50) reported interacting with the CSP. There were no statistically significant changes in perceptions of the program’s benefits for patients and FCPs, its impact on meeting patient needs, or workload. However, the proportion of staff who believed that the CSP would interfere with their work significantly decreased from 23% prelaunch (10 of 44) to 12% postlaunch (6 of 50) (P=.03). Similar to phase 1, staff with CSP exposure (38 of 50) were statistically more likely than those with no exposure (12 of 50) to agree that the program benefited patients (97% [37 of 38] vs 50% [6 of 12], P<.01) and FCPs (100% [38 of 38] vs 50% [6 of 12], P<.01), helped staff better meet patient needs (92% [35 of 38] vs 50% [6 of 12], P<.01), and did not undermine other support efforts by care teams (92% [35 of 38] vs 50% [6 of 12], P<.01).

FCP Acceptability

FCP acceptance of the CSP remained high in phase 2, with 88% (53 of 61) reporting that the program was very or extremely helpful. No FCP respondent who used the CSP found it unhelpful or unnecessary.

Care Quality

Two care quality indicators improved from prelaunch to postlaunch. The proportion of Staff agreeing that they could provide FCPs with adequate information and support for their self-care increased from 73% (32 of 44) to 90% (45 of 50) (P=.05), and the proportion of staff able to support FCPs without being affected by personal challenges rose from 68% (30 of 44) to 78% (39 of 50) (P=.04). Other indicators—such as confidence in addressing FCPs’ emotional and informational needs—showed no statistically significant change.

Postlaunch, FCPs reported greater awareness of online family services (mean score: 4.3 vs 3.7, P<.01) and knowledge of hospital staff available to support them (mean score: 4.4 vs 3.8, P<.01). Knowledge of educational resources improved but was not statistically significant (mean score: 4.4 vs 4.0, P=.06). Perceived compassion and preparedness also did not significantly change.

Discussion

This quality improvement evaluation tested the feasibility and acceptability of an inpatient FCP support program and explored its potential to affect staff and FCP perspectives on care quality. Our results add to evidence suggesting that programs supporting FCPs—without being directly tied to patient care or unmet needs—can be both feasible and acceptable.15,32, 33, 34, 35, 36 Although studies have examined the impact of support programs on FCPs of children with chronic illness or disability in group format and delivered in community, clinic, or online settings,37,38 far fewer have examined the impact of support for FCPs of adult or hospitalized patients.39, 40, 41

Our evaluation broadens the evidence base to include FCPs of adult patients in hospital settings and offers insight into potential impact for staff and FCPs. Results show that embedding an FCP in an inpatient setting is feasible. In the first year of the CSP, extensively trained volunteers delivered support in a busy hospital setting, and FCPs were willing to engage with volunteers. Unlike time-intensive CSP behavior change programs with coaches42 or those focused on matching peers to FCPs,15,43 the CSP offered in-the-moment, private and confidential support and navigation with little time commitment, which may have increased participation. Low volunteer attrition may have also contributed to feasibility.25 However, scaling the CSP will likely require institutional commitments, such as staffing, funding, and integrating systematic needs assessments to identify FCPs who require support to carry out the program. Although tools to assess FCP needs during hospitalization and care transitions exist,9,44, 45, 46 they are not widely used in routine care. Standardized measures will be critical for tracking program reach and outcomes and measuring sustainability.

The CSP was also acceptable to FCPs and staff. FCPs reported that the support was very or extremely helpful, and staff reported a statistically significant decrease in concerns about interruptions with their workflow and increases in their ability to support FCPs. Over three-quarters of phase 2 staff reported engaging with the CSP, and those who had the most knowledge about the program strongly endorsed the benefits to patients and FCPs. The large proportion of those who had little engagement or knowledge about the CSP, possibly due to limited exposure (eg, evening or weekend staff), had mostly neutral perceptions. The CSP’s attempts not to interrupt patient-directed care were effective but may have resulted in less visibility to staff of benefits to patients and FCPs. Future research could better target staff exposed to the CSP and use qualitative interviews to deepen understanding about acceptability.

Care Quality

The CSP was not designed to improve staff communication or caregiving preparedness directly but rather to offer an additional support layer. Our evaluation was also not designed for generalizability or statistically powered to rule out other factors that could explain differences in care quality, but rather to explore potential changes that the introduction of a CSP could bring to FCPs and staff. Nonetheless, in phase 2, staff reported greater agreement that they could provide adequate information and support to FCPs, suggesting possible direct or indirect effects. The CSP likely contributed to FCPs’ improved ability to navigate resources—an effect also seen in other peer programs.15 However, it did not change perceptions of compassionate care, possibly due to ceiling effects.47 Similarly, caregiver preparedness may not have changed due to the CSP’s focus on empathetic listening and support rather than skill-building.

Limitations

This evaluation has limitations. First, our sample was relatively homogeneous, so findings may not generalize across diverse populations of hospital staff or FCPs. Developing methods and care models to systematically identify those with unmet needs is an important consideration for future research and the focus of a growing body of research.9,48 Second, our design was appropriate for testing feasibility and acceptability but not for establishing causality for care quality. Future research should aim to test the impact of hospital-based peer support on recipients and staff using hybrid designs that integrate pragmatic trials with implementation science and qualitative research. These designs could be used to test hypotheses, such as whether an extra safety net of support has direct and indirect benefits for FCP health and well-being, and whether that support could reduce staff burnout, and save costs associated with reduced health care utilization for FCPs and care recipients. Third, survey response rates were low, raising the potential for nonresponse bias. FCP49 and staff50 participation is known to be especially challenging populations to engage in surveys.49 It is possible that staff response was lower because this was a quality improvement project and could be higher in a research study with consent. Previous research has shown high unmet needs among FCPs of transplant patients,51, 52, 53 and therefore, it is also possible that in contrast to a population cohort,54 FCP respondents had insufficient time and energy to participate. Lastly, the CSP was implemented at a well-resourced academic center with dedicated staff and a strong volunteer infrastructure. Replicability in different settings may vary. Twenty-two other US academic and community hospitals have developed their own CSPs, with guidance from the Ken Hamilton Caregiver Support Center replication program (https://nwh.northwell.edu/your-visit/ken-hamilton-caregivers-center), and although none has undergone a similar formal evaluation, this informal network has reported that their local needs, expertise, and resources can be accommodated while maintaining the core principle of providing high-quality, in patient FCP support.

Conclusion

This quality improvement project demonstrated that providing peer support to FCPs in a dynamic hospital setting is feasible and acceptable to staff and FCPs. Future research should evaluate how best to implement FCP programs without disrupting workflows while simultaneously raising visibility of the program and should evaluate the impact of CSPs on well-being and knowledge of available resources. CSPs may also offer staff opportunities to refer FCPs to those highly specialized in providing needed support, thus allowing them to compassionately engage with FCPs about their needs without compromising patient care responsibilities.
