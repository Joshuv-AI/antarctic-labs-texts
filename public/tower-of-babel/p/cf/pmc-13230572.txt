Strategies for designing informal learning spaces in libraries based on comprehensive field measurements of light, noise, thermal, and air quality
Authors: Li Wang, Weihong Guo, Xiao Liu, Xinyu Wang, Luca Caneparo, Guibin Zhang, Bao-Jie He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs higher education increasingly emphasizes quality enhancement and innovative development, the importance of learning spaces is being recognized. The swift progress in information technology has also significantly altered learning methodologies, with informal learning becoming increasingly vital. The variety of spatial types and the intricate factors influencing informal learning spaces (ILS) can often result in interference among different elements, thereby undoubtedly escalating the complexity of research. The ILS within libraries has emerged as a research subject due to its exemplariness. To address this challenge, our study employed ultra-wideband (UWB) indoor positioning technology to conduct comprehensive research. This was achieved by gathering and analyzing data related to the indoor physical environment and user seating preferences, among other factors. We then established correlations between different data sets, considering the dimensions of time and space. This study constructed a multi-factor model influencing ILS and calculated the proportions of four decision-making elements using a comprehensive approach that included SPSS analysis and comparative scoring. Among these, physical elements topped the list, accounting for nearly 40% of student preferences. Following that, facilities elements contributed to over 20% of preferences. Ontology elements (macro layout, functional zoning, and form composition) explained over 15% of preferences, and lastly, atmosphere elements (> 10%). The study identified 7 key bottom-level indicators and their respective weights for each ILS type, and proposed improvement methods for them, aiming to offer reference and support for future ILS design.Keywords: Informal learning space (ILS), High-precision positioning technology, Space environment quality, Learning behavior, Optimization and improvementSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In history, universities had emphasized the emergence of traditional teaching methods in formal learning spaces (ILS)1. In the new era, the rapid development of high intelligence, informatization, and big data analysis has greatly changed the learning methods of current students. The traditional teaching method is conducted in the classroom and taught by the teacher, which is single and fixed, and can no longer meet the learning needs of students. Students’ learning and thinking methods are gradually shifting towards diversification2. In addition to individual self-directed learning, team learning and communication learning have become increasingly important. ILS provides greater flexibility, collaboration, participation, and independence for informal learning behavior of students, and it has become a key place for future university student learning3. At the same time, learning theory is also constantly developing. In the early twentieth century, behaviorist learning theory was based on the famous "S-R" linkage reaction (Fig. 1), and in the 1980s, constructivist theory identified "active learning and constructivist learning" as key points. This study will establish a new learning theory model based on two learning theories. Informal learning is happening anytime and anywhere, and it exists in multiple places such as restaurants, libraries, cafes, and dormitories4. ILS can be divided into internal ILS and external ILS, and this main focus is on internal ILS. Related studies have shown that university libraries have become the most popular ILS due to their open learning resources, good learning atmosphere, and complete facilities5. Chinese university libraries are also undergoing a transformation from traditional libraries to learning centers6. How to optimize the ILS of the library to make it more suitable for the needs of all users is the main problem faced by designers and scholars.

S-R theoretical foundation model.

Research has found that the space environment quality of many ILS within the library still needs to be improved7,8. For libraries in southern China, the main problems with their internal ILS are caused by two factors. Firstly, the hot and humid climate makes it crucial to create a comfortable indoor environment; Second, different types of ILS have different requirements for spatial ontology, facility furniture, and environmental atmosphere shaping, which has also led to the poor quality of ILS in many university libraries9,10.

Scholars have mainly conducted research on the quality of ILS at the following three levels. In terms of spatial satisfaction, the specific scores of various spatial design characteristics were mainly obtained through the Likert scale11. In terms of influencing factors, relevant research was mainly obtained through on-site observation and subjective questionnaires12, and the relevant influencing factors were determined from a qualitative analysis. In recent years, quantitative research has mainly developed from a single element, such as the measurement of sound environment, the relationship between visual environment and student behavior, and so on4,13. The above research has promoted the development of ILS. However, the existing research still lacks exploration of the relationship between different types of ILS and various influencing factors, which is limited by the many influencing factors and diverse learning behaviors14. Although there have been studies conducted from a certain perceptual perspective13, the perspective is singular, and there is an urgent need to establish a multi-dimensional overall research framework to fill this gap. If this problem is not solved, it will bring students a bad learning experience, reduce learning efficiency, and more seriously, it will endanger their physical and mental health.

In the early days, spatial behavior data was more often surveyed using methods such as cross-sectional human flow and questionnaire surveys15. Nowadays, the rapid development of indoor positioning systems (IPS) has made it possible to collect real-time positioning information16, which can clearly capture user behavior in real time17,18. Environmental sensors can record and store data in real-time19, and with the use of IPS, they can help establish accurate spatiotemporal relationships between physical environmental data and user behavior data, thus breaking the constraints of current research.

This study selected the Wushan Library of South China University of Technology (SCUT) in Guangzhou, China as a typical case for analysis and research. The study used UWB high-precision IPS to collect data and analyze the relationship between student behavior and physical environment data20. This research are committed to clarifying the relationship between subjective choices, objective behaviors, and indoor environment. The purpose of this study is to identify the key elements that affect different types of ILS in libraries and strategies for improving its space environment quality, providing reference for later designers and researchers.

The research objective of this study was to use high-precision indoor positioning technology to achieve real-time capture of student behavior, location information, and spatial environment throughout the entire process. This method could compensate for the fragmented shortcomings of traditional research methods, analyze the specific correlation between student preferences, behavior, and indoor spatial environment, and quantitatively discover the specific shortcomings of ILS in libraries, and find ways to improve different types of ILS.

This research presented a robust and novel approach to understanding and designing ILS in university libraries. The study’s strength lies in its comprehensive, data-driven methodology, which successfully integrates four objective environmental metrics with user behavior tracking and preference scoring. The resulting multi-factor model and the quantification of student preferences are highly valuable for practitioners.

Literature review

The emergence and transformation of ILS

The term “learning space” initially appeared as “teaching environment” or “teaching space”21–23. During this period, educators Dewey and Lindeman pointed out that "life is education" and "learning needs to be done in problem-solving situations"24,25, which could be seen as the earliest growths of informal learning methods26.

In the 1950s, Knowles officially proposed the concept of “informal learning”27. Different from formal learning (Table 1), it referred to the learning behavior of acquiring skills from daily life or environments, and the acquisition channels included many places such as home, work, entertainment, library, etc.28. Tough proposed in the 1970s that informal learning should be a very common and natural behavior29. The emergence of learning centers in 1994 also greatly stimulated research on ILS. In 2001, The United States released the "White Paper on Learning Infrastructure"30, which pointed out that learning spaces should not just be classrooms, but rather spaces aimed at promoting learning31, just namely ILS. In 2006, the book “Learning Space” was published32, promoting research on ILS in various countries. In 2011, the publication of the Journal of Learning Space sparked more international attention to ILS33.

Research stages and content of ILS

In recent years, research on ILS has mainly been divided into the following stages:

Firstly, the importance and classification of ILS. ILS stimulate learning behavior by enhancing students’ interests and learning motivation34. The research by Walton and Matthews confirmed the important position of library ILS in universities35. Cunningham and Walton focused on the classification of ILS at the University of Loughborough, as well as the learning activities of its students. To better summarize ILS36, Webber proposed a theoretical framework and design principles for the four quadrants of learning space (Fig. 2), which is also the main basis for classifying in this study37.

Four types of ILS * (Taken by Li Wang).*ILS-1 refers to personal and public ILS, ILS-2 refers to open and communicable ILS, ILS-3 refers to collaborative and intimate ILS, ILS-4 refers to personal and intimate ILS.

Secondly, the utilization and satisfaction survey of ILS. Lee and Looker conducted a questionnaire survey on students at Nanyang Technological University and identified the most important factors affecting student satisfaction38. Fouad and Sailer studied the activities of students in ILS to guide design39. Wu et al. compared the satisfaction of ILS between two universities and analyzed the reasons for the differences and the relationship with student behavior14. Wang and Han ranked five learning spaces based on their recovery potential, and the results showed that ILS scored the highest11.

Thirdly, qualitative research on influencing factors. Some scholars believed that ILS were mainly influenced by physical environmental factors and human cultural factors40–42.One study summarized six characteristics that affected the space environment quality of ILS through questionnaires, such as comfort, flexibility, and openness14. Tan et al. used the 5C framework (confidence, culture, recreating, community, and conflict resolution) to investigate how ILS promote interest development43. Lam et al. established the four design principles of ILS, namely technology, spatial design, aesthetics, and comfort44.

The recent research focus has shifted to quantifying environmental perception45–50. Zhang et al. measured the sound environment and examined its correlation with student preferences for various ILS13. The study showed that students who were sensitive to sound tended to choose quiet private spaces rather than active public or private spaces. Chen et al. optimized visual perception in ILS on university campuses4. Research showed that ILS in universities consist of three elements: physical space, facilities, and environment. Lourenço et al. provided suggestions for the composite application of natural light and artificial lighting51. Research showed that improvements in the design and management of circular areas could lead to a significant reduction in the use of artificial light (and energy). Wu et al. explored the relationship between informal learning and spatial openness using “viewing volume” and “viewing area” as quantitative parameters52. The results indicated that the standard deviation of the average viewing volume and viewing area of the three story high space was the largest, and the difference in spatial openness was the largest. The diversity of spatial openness provided flexibility in spatial experience52. Hsu et al. reported the top five key factors, and provided the proportion of each item53. DeFrain and Hong used a comprehensive qualitative and quantitative research method and found that independent learning is currently the main form of informal learning, accounting for 76%54. Li et al. analyzed the ILS of a library using structural equation modeling and identified three major influencing factors and their proportions55. The influence of light was the most severe and its weight was 25.6%55. In Barrett et al.'s study45, physical factors accounted for 49% and were also the most important factor. Just because of the differences in specific research subjects, there are differences in the values of this proportion. Barrett et al. pointed out that current space environment quality measurements mainly focus on four aspects: sound, light, heat, and air quality, but there is no consensus on their relative importance in ILS45. Barrett et al.'s research indicated that light ranked first among the physical factors.

The above research has gradually promoted the development of ILS in universities. However, there are two main shortcomings in existing research. On the one hand, quantitative research currently only starts from a single element51, without considering the mutual interference of multiple factors. On the other hand, there is no distinction made between different types of ILS. So this research are committed to establishing multi factor impact models for different types of ILS and identifying key influencing factors to propose more targeted improvement strategies.

Application of UWB positioning technology

The application of IPS has become very common in people’s daily lives, such as mobile navigation, children’s tracking, and other scenarios56. But few studies have developed a general framework for location-based informal learning to assist designers and researchers working in this field57. The study planned to record informal learning behavior, which could be achieved through wearable location-based products that allowed users to participate more widely in experiments58,59. The wearable instrument could realize remote data storage through the WPAN link with the internet center equipment60. After comparison, the research chose the UWB high-precision IPS (Fig. 3)61. UWB is an emerging technology. Its positioning accuracy error is not more than 10 cm, and the positioning distance can cover up to 300 m, which is more suitable for the positioning of informal learning behaviors inside the library. Due to the attenuation of ultra wide band radiation in media because of wall barriers20, there were certain limitations in experiments on ILS. The first solution of this study was to find a suitable base station location as much as possible to reduce signal attenuation and interference64; Secondly, the number of base stations was increased to minimize the probability of signal distortion65.

Methodology

Research object and scope

Before identifying typical cases, the research conducted field investigations on ten typical university libraries in Guangdong Province, China. The main methods of investigation included observation, photo recording, interviews, etc. Of the ten libraries compared, most only included two or three types of ILS, with relatively incomplete types. After comparative analysis, "SCUT-L" had become a typical case due to its relatively complete ILS types, unique internal space, and convenient measurement. It was located on the campus of SCUT in Tianhe District, Guangzhou, China (Fig. 4), serving a total of 28000 users. "SCUT-L" had four floors and was divided into three major spaces: the South Pavilion, the North Pavilion, and the central section (Fig. 5).

Personal and public ILS (ILS-1)

It was located on the east side of the courtyard on the first floor of South Pavilion (Fig. 6-1), with a reading space and a daily usage of 25–40 people. For the convenience of later analysis, the research divided the spatial module into three major regions. The side closer to the courtyard was the ILS-1A area, and the side farther away from the courtyard was the ILS-1B area. The middle bookshelf reading area was the ILS-1C. The north and east sides of ILS-1 were walls and the south side was connected with other ILS. Students could enter the area through the north entrance or through the south interface.

The plan and their spatial usage.

Open and communicable ILS (ILS-2)

It was located on the north side of the courtyard on the fourth floor of South Pavilion (Fig. 6-2), an information sharing space, with 10–20 daily users. It mainly included the bar area (ILS-2A area), sofa area (ILS-2B area), double table area (ILS-2C area), and multi table area (ILS-2D area). The north side of ILS-2 was the railing of the atrium, with a corridor leading to the North Pavilion. The french window on the south side was equipped with sun shading grille. Students could enter the space from the north, south, and east sides.

Cooperative and intimate ILS (ILS-3)

It was located on the south side of the courtyard on the fourth floor of the South Pavilion (Fig. 6-3) and was part of the Study Academy, with a daily usage of 20–35 people. This module mainly consisted of 10 training rooms and 1 seminar room. This area needed to be reserved in advance before it could be used, and the booking time might vary, such as two or three hours. Different rooms were separated by lightweight partitions. Due to the exposed top pipeline, there was a gap of 85 cm between the partition wall and the ceiling.

The sizes of the three modules were all within the distance range measured by UWB positioning, which exactly met the experimental requirements. After determining these modules, the characteristics of the space ontology, facilities and equipment, and atmosphere creation were recorded for the convenience of subsequent comparison work.

The construction of new learning theory

This study combined behaviorist learning theory with constructivist learning theory to construct a novel S-R model (Fig. 7). After being stimulated by different environmental factors, students made certain responses, chose a learning space that was suitable for their own learning, and then constructed and applied it to the existing environmental factors, generating new cognitive structures. This can be understood as a student coming to the library, when stimulated by various influencing factors and actively choosing a seat, he will always have a certain sense of territory and construct his own learning microenvironment through his own way, thus having a certain sense of belonging and shaping the overall learning atmosphere of the area. The difference between this new S-R model and the original model is that it incorporates the “active acquisition” feature of constructivist learning theory, and its selection will also have a certain impact on the original environment.

Research method

Collection of personnel positioning information

The instrument the research chose was designed based on the UWB high-power module MAX2001-IPEX/CA. The PROANC base station was equipped with a 10DB directional antenna, which improved the signal coverage range of the base station while improving its appearance52. The measured accuracy error was not greater than 10 cm and the spatial granularity of the UWB measurements was 5–10 cm. The positioning algorithm used TOF (Time of Flight)20. The actual measurement method of thermal environment was mainly based on the "Standard for testing methods for thermal environment of building" JGJ/T 34767. This study mainly conducted actual measurements of indoor environments under typical summer weather conditions, where indoor users and main heating equipment were in normal working conditions, and the tested environment was in a state of natural ventilation or mechanical ventilation.

The layout of measuring points referred to the provisions of standard JGJ/T 34767. The height of the measuring point was set to 1.1 m, which corresponds to the height of the human head in a sitting position. Due to the height of the library being 4.2 m, local discomfort caused by vertical temperature differences could be eliminated within the range of human activity height. At the same time, the library had a large volume and adopted a homogenized window design and air conditioning vent design, which could also eliminate the discomfort of local horizontal temperature and airflow. This study referred to the "Evaluation standards for indoor thermal environment in Civil Buildings" GB/T 5078568 and "Ergonomics of the thermal environment—Analytical determination and interpretation of thermal comfort using calculation of the PMV and PPD indicators and local thermal comfort criteria" GB/T 1804969. Library users engaged in light activities while sitting for long periods of time, which met the steady-state heat transfer conditions, and could be measured using the indoor thermal environment predicted mean vote (PMV) index. Then the research conducted initial validity tests on the 20 proposed indicators and four decision-making factors (Table 11). The KMO value for this test was 0.743, greater than 0.6, and it passed the Bartlett sphericity test, indicating the validity of the data. P-value of 0.000 indicated a strong correlation between variables and was suitable for factor analysis. However, there were three main issues with the validity test. Firstly, the commonality of P4 did not meet the requirements; Secondly, the result of P5 deviated from its initial setting; Thirdly, the correspondence between O2 and O7 and the expected dimensions did not match. So, after removing P4 with low commonality, the research randomly selected four factors from 19 indicators for retesting (Table 12). The KMO value for this retest was 0.758, which is greater than 0.6. It passed the Bartlett sphericity test and had a P-value of 0.000. The cumulative variance interpretation rate after rotation was 70.89%, indicating that the four decision-making factors could extract 70.89% of the information from 19 analysis items. This indicated that all 19 indicators had met the expected correspondence and had good structural validity. It should be explained that O7 was set in the opposite direction during questionnaire setting, resulting in negative value. Because it met the set latitude and its absolute value and commonality met the requirements, the indicator variable was retained.

Specific implementation

The choice of experimental time was closely related to the local climate environment and the usage status of the library. Guangzhou is located in a subtropical climate. It is very hot from April to November, with an average temperature of 25 to 32 ℃, and air conditioning needs to be turned on to cool down. The time from December to February is colder and non-air-conditioned, with an average temperature of 9–16 ℃ and a minimum of 3 ℃. Summer vacation of SCUT usually starts on July 15th and ends at the end of August. Winter vacation usually lasts from mid-January to the end of February. Considering that there were very few people studying in the library during the holiday, and the third to fourth floors of the library were closed during the holiday, experiments could not be conducted during these two periods.

Therefore, the experiment was conducted from September 11, 2023 to September 18, 2023, for a total of one week. At that time, it was summer in Guangzhou and the students were in the middle of the semester. The outdoor temperature was 24–35 ℃, and the relative humidity was 77–86%. The operating hours of the library were from 8:30 a.m. to 9:40 p.m., and the research collected data from 9:00 a.m. to 9:00 p.m. The research did some preparation work before the experiment started. Firstly, the research placed the equipment for measuring physical data at the detection point and activated the recording button. Then, the research used a tripod to fix the position of each base station. Finally, the research team opened the test tab and debugged on the laptop terminal to ensure that the entire system could run normally. When the teachers and students first entered this area, they were informed of the types of data to be collected for the experiment and asked to sign an informed consent form. One was a data availability statement, representing that the data could be obtained from our research team when necessary; The other was an ethical statement, stating that this study would not involve the privacy of the researchers and would not expose their facial features. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. This study confirmed that informed consent has been obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardians. Both the ethical approval statement and the accuracy statement are reviewed by the Academic Committee of the School of Architecture at South China University of Technology. All experimental protocols were approved by the School of Architecture and/or its academic committee at South China University of Technology. After the experiment started, the staff at the module entrance distributed tags and questionnaires. When the user left, the staff collected the tags and questionnaires. In the end, 3290 measured data such as temperature, humidity, illumination, and noise were collected in the experiment, and 532 valid questionnaires were collected. More than 300,000 pieces of positioning data had been collected, including time and location dimensions, as well as the number of base stations receiving outgoing signals and their distance from tags. After the experiment, the positioning data, physical environment data, and subjective perception data were classified and organized in a spatiotemporal manner to facilitate subsequent analysis (Fig. 13).

Relationship between preferences and user attributes

It was set that when a tag stayed on a seat for more than 5 min, that seat was selected. In this way, this study obtained the selected location and stay duration for each tag within a day. By overlaying the data from that week, a preference map for user seat selection was obtained (Fig. 14). The darker the color of the seat in the picture, the longer the user stayed.

Seat selection preferences.

The users’ favorite seating area in ILS-1 was ILS-1B, followed by ILS-1A, and finally ILS-1C. The users’ favorite seat in ILS-2 was ILS-2A, followed by ILS-2C, then ILS-2D, and finally ILS-2B. In ILS-3, users preferred rooms that were completely separated from other rooms, followed by neighboring rooms. Through observation, the user orientation of the three modules was related to the lighting direction at that time, and the overall performance was towards the direction of incoming light.

For the convenience of unified analysis, this study divided student behavior into two categories. The behaviors of revising for exams, doing homework, or academic writing were called targeted learning behavior, and leisure, reading, discussing and communicating behaviors were defined as leisure learning behaviors. The results (Fig. 15a) showed that 59% of undergraduate students came to the library for targeted learning, but there was also a considerable portion for leisure (41%). Master’s and doctoral students mainly came to the library for targeted learning, accounting for 89%.

(a) Overall analysis: purpose of visiting the library, (b) Comparative analysis: proportion of behavior quantity.

Comparing the user information within the three modules (Fig. 15b), 47% of ILS-1 users were undergraduate students, mainly studying for exams (25%), while 53% of ILS-1 people had a master’s or doctoral degree, and were mainly writing papers (51%). 73% of ILS-2 were undergraduate students and their main behaviors were leisure (66%), while 27% had a master’s or doctoral degree, and a primary focus on academic exchange (17%). 25% of ILS-3 were undergraduate students, with a focus on leisure (15%), while 75% had a master’s or doctoral degree and their main behaviors were writing (41%). Through comparison, it could be concluded that master’s and doctoral students preferred ILS-1 and ILS-3, while undergraduate students preferred ILS-2.

The master’s and doctoral students still had a high level of learning vitality from 8–9 pm in the evening. During lunch and dinner time, most of the learning behaviors of ILS-1 would decrease, but there was still a certain proportion of students writing papers.

The relationship between the average number of daily behaviors and time.

Discussion and proposal

The correlation between preferences and user attributes

This study had obtained three related results in this section. Firstly, 89% of the learning behaviors of master’s and doctoral students were targeted learning, while leisure learning for undergraduate students accounted for nearly half of the total. In this regard, Zhang et al.'s research is similar to this study, as they also found that the main learning behavior of master’s and doctoral students was academic writing, while younger students had a more flexible learning behavior13. Secondly, academic qualifications influenced students’ choices of different types of ILS. Master’s and doctoral students preferred ILS-1 and ILS-3, while undergraduate students preferred ILS-2. Zhang et al. pointed out that people engaged in academic research were the least efficient in noisy open spaces (ILS-2), so they preferred to learn in quiet environments (ILS-1)13. The lack of necessary facilities and privacy environment in ILS-2 was also the reason why the master’s and doctoral students abandoned this space. These two major factors have been described in the studies of Anggiani6 and Wang12. Wang and Han also believed that academic qualifications could affect students’ choices and perceptions of ILS, but he did not conduct a specific analysis11. Thirdly, this study identified students’ different preferences of three types of ILS (Fig. 27). Light and visual attractiveness were key factors affecting the selection of seats in different regions of ILS-1 and ILS-2. Students liked to sit next to visually pleasing courtyards (ILS-1A1 and ILS-2D) to study, but they would change seats and escape due to unstable lighting, which was also found in Chen et al.'s research4. The main influencing factor of rooms in ILS-3 was noise. Due to the poor sound insulation effect of partitions, students usually do not choose adjacent rooms to sit in. When selecting seats inside a room, lighting became the most crucial factor. Students usually sit facing the window to ensure that the computer screen does not reflect light. Dong et al. pointed out that the relationship between different types of ILS and student behavior perception was complex and required a lot of work to explore74. This is also one of the innovative points of this study.

Selection preferences for seating areas.

The correlation between different learning behaviors and time

Firstly, undergraduate students in ILS-1 mostly studied during the daytime and stopped learning after evening. Master’s and doctoral students still showed high academic vitality from 8–9 pm, and even engaged in academic writing during lunch and dinner time. Wang and Han explained the emergence of this result from the perspective of learning style11. The study of master’s and doctoral degrees belonged to intuitive learners, who preferred a quiet atmosphere that could bring deeper thinking. Therefore, they would study when there were few people, and many PhDs chose to write papers late at night11. For people engaged in leisure learning behaviors, they preferred a lively environment, and a too quiet environment was not conducive. Lotfy et al.5 pointed out that a quiet atmosphere restricted conversation, which is consistent with the results of this study. Research also found that being too quiet could make people drowsy5. So, how many decibels of noise in the environment are more preferred? This issue will be further explored in the next Sect. (5.3).

Secondly, the number of leisure learning behaviors in ILS-2 had surged during lunch and dinner times. Students enjoyed the open communication environment here, where they could have group discussions during meals or prepare for afternoon classes. Anggiani and Heryanto found that the duration of discussions was not very long, at most one hour6, which is consistent with results of this study. ILS-2 also had many resting behaviors, such as taking naps, surfing the internet, and eating. Anggiani and Heryanto found that the main posture for students to rest was sitting6, but the survey showed that lying down also accounted for a large proportion. This indicated that the sofa area design of ILS-2B was comfortable and popular among students. The furniture of ILS-1 and ILS-3 was almost all hard tables and chairs, which could not meet the rest needs of students. Therefore, during lunch breaks, students would return to their dormitories for rest, and a certain proportion of students came to ILS-2B for rest.

Thirdly, academic exchange behavior in ILS-3 had a certain level of vitality at various time periods. This was because the space was booked in advance, but the number of rooms was limited, so some online meetings could only be scheduled at noon or evening. This also indicated the great demand of students for ILS-3. Lotfy et al. pointed out that current libraries lack collaborative learning spaces and suggested adding 24-h open spaces to meet the needs of different groups of people5. Among the ten libraries, this study also found that only one library has a 24-h open space. Wang and Yu pointed out that the overall environment of ILS on campus should be achieved at both the temporal and spatial levels75, which is more conducive to the sustainable development of the campus.

The relationship between physical environment and learning behavior

Light and learning behavior

Experimental tests showed that seats close to the courtyard did not have an advantage due to significant changes in lighting, while seats away from natural light had more stable lighting and were more popular. However, due to being far away from natural light, insufficient illumination often occurred and needed to be supplemented by artificial lighting to solve the problem. The above results indicate that uneven illumination distribution affects student choices, and this is the same in the three types of ILS, that is, regardless of what learning behavior students engage in, stable and gentle illumination is required. The seat of ILS-1A also had glare from the west side, so students had to use umbrellas to study or change seats because they could not tolerate the strong light here. In order to improve this situation, the library had specially added a grille on this side of the wall. However, due to the large spacing between the grilles and their inability to adjust, the lighting here still often causes criticism from users now. Barrett et al.46 argued that natural light was more effective in promoting academic performance than artificial lighting. This conclusion also explains the phenomenon of "learning under umbrella", as students prefer to study under natural light. Another reason for students changing seats is the high temperature accompanied by strong sunlight. Juan and Chen48 believed that light could cause anxiety in students, but this anxiety was less affected than the temperature. Barrett et al.45 believed that light had the greatest impact on learning effect. This study will discuss the specific impact weights of temperature and light on informal learning in the following content (5.5). In response to the current insufficient lighting in the library, an adjustable control system can be applied to solve the problem. Lourenço et al.51 pointed out that an intelligent simulation system should be established based on the difference in indoor lighting, and the use of smart windows with adjustable solar transmittance could settle this issue.

Noise and learning behavior

The noise performance in ILS-1 was that zone A was the most noisy, followed by zone B, and zone C was the quietest. The noise in ILS-2 was ranked from quiet to noisy in areas A, D, C, and B. The noise in ILS-3 was ranked from quiet to noisy as follows: writing mode in zone A, meeting mode in zone A, and discussion mode in zone B (Fig. 28). Compared with the standard values, few places met the regulations, indicating that the overall noise level of this library was relatively high. In ILS-1, zone A was the most noisy due to its proximity to the entrance and its role as a corridor. The B area in ILS-2 was also noisy due to frequent pedestrian traffic. Due to the poor sound insulation effect of the partition of ILS-3, not only people in adjacent rooms but also those studying in other areas were complaining about it.

Noise ranking and student preferences of three types of ILS.

It should be noted that high background noise does not necessarily cause student aversion. Research has shown that for students who study quietly, the sounds of flipping books and typing are both popular sounds because they play a role in shaping the learning atmosphere14. However, the sound of speaking or moving chairs can be annoying for quiet scholars. In ILS-1, although the average noise level in zone B exceeded 45 dB, the most popular area in the sound environment was zone B, not the quietest zone C. This indicates that the research conclusion of Wu et al.14 is consistent with this research. However, for ILS-2, speaking noise was beneficial as it enhances the communication atmosphere. So this study need to recognize that the current regulations in China have certain principles, which mainly start from limiting large noise levels to ensure students’ quiet learning48. However, the limitation of the standard is that it ignores the impact of different sounds on student psychology, and overlooks the preference of different learning behaviors for the sound environment. Zhang et al.13 argued that sound should not be a harmful factor, but a controllable and useful resource, which is consistent with viewpoint. The sound insulation effect of ILS-3 can be improved through various measures, such as using sound-absorbing and noise-reducing materials for the ceiling (NRC greater than 0.6), laying carpets on the floor, and partially using high-definition sound-absorbing panels on the wall.

Thermal environment and learning behavior

Results have shown that the overall indoor temperature of this library was higher than the regulated 27.0 ℃. The evaluation results of PMV and PPD showed that users in the library felt a slight warmth. The library was designed with a green and low-carbon strategy, mainly utilizing passive green technology means. In order to reduce energy consumption, it was stipulated in management that air conditioning could only be turned on when the temperature is above 30.0 ℃. Students in ILS-1A and ILS-2D reported high temperatures during actual use, while students in other areas generally felt more comfortable. From the internal partitions of each ILS (Fig. 29), the temperature near the window was higher than that far away from the window. The temperature in the southward window area (ILS-1A) showed a significant increase in the morning, while the temperature in the westward window area (ILS-2D) mainly increased in the afternoon. ILS-3 was equipped with air conditioning that students could automatically adjust environmental parameters, so the temperature was the most comfortable.

Temperature ranking in different regions.

The overall trend of humidity was highest in the morning, then gradually decreasing, lowest at nightfall, and gradually increasing again at night. Compared with the standard, the maximum humidity met the requirements, while the minimum exceeded the regulations. This indicated that the dehumidification equipment in the library was operating well, as the storage of books required a relatively dry environment. Undoubtedly, among the three types of ILS, the appropriate temperature and humidity are important, so the ILS-3 with adjustable parameters was the most popular. Juan and Chen48 believed that temperature had the greatest impact on learning attention of ILS-1 students and was the most likely to cause student anxiety. Wargocki and Wyon found that when the temperature slightly decreased, students’ language test scores increased76, indicating that a suitable temperature could help improve learning outcomes. Barrett et al.45 suggested that temperature and air quality together affected 49% of learning levels.

Air quality and learning behavior

The air velocity was not only related to thermal comfort, but also closely related to air quality76, so this study compare and discuss them together. Figure 30 shows the comparison between CO2 content and air velocity. The higher the air velocity, the lower the CO2 content, which was most evident in ILS-1 and ILS-2. Among them, the seat near the window had the highest and most fluctuating air velocity, while the seat away from the window had the lowest and most stable air velocity. The air velocity in ILS-3 varied due to automatic adjustment by different users. There was also a certain correlation between the content of CO2 and formaldehyde. Figures 31 and 32 showed that when ventilation was inconvenient, the concentrations of CO2 and CH2O would increase. Lotfy et al.5 found that poor air circulation could prevent students from studying in the library. Wang12 concluded that ventilation in transportation areas is crucial for other ILS around them. Wang and Yu75 pointed out that maintaining good indoor ventilation and planting green plants could improve air quality. The above conclusions are consistent with this research findings. In addition to the use of air conditioning, this library also utilized passive technology in the design to maintain indoor temperature. In summer, both the courtyard and atrium help to lift the wind, accelerating air circulation to a certain extent, taking away heat while also improving air quality.

The relationship between satisfaction evaluation and learning behavior

Figure 31 shows satisfaction evaluation results. The main advantages of ILS-1 were its proximity to the central lobby, good accessibility, and convenience in purchasing snacks. The central lobby was equipped with vending machines and dining areas. The rainy weather in Guangzhou often prevents users from going out to eat, and many students hope to relax by drinking coffee during the learning process. During the renovation, the top pipeline was exposed, which effectively ensured the height of the space and prevented students from feeling oppressed. ILS-1 also had several prominent disadvantages. Firstly, poor ventilation. This also confirms the changes in carbon dioxide content mentioned earlier (Fig. 25). The ILS-1 only had windows on the west side and no draught, especially in the ILS-1B area where ventilation was particularly poor. Secondly, there was a lack of storage facilities, which was a common issue among the three modules. Students hope to have 2–3 days or a week of storage time because books are very heavy and inconvenient to carry. Thirdly, the location of ILS-1 was close to the bathroom and dining area, greatly reducing privacy and cleanliness. Privacy was a key point that students emphasized. Many users really hoped that the library could have a place for individuals to study alone, because when sitting in ILS-1, face-to-face learning would make them feel embarrassed. This has also been validated in the papers of many researchers13,47. Among the ten libraries surveyed, only one (HSD-3) had a personal private learning space (ILS-4), indicating that the previous designers had overlooked this demand. Some areas of ILS-1 can be equipped with partitions of a certain height on the table to meet this needs of students.

The main advantages of ILS-2 were (1) near the elevator for easy finding, (2) suitable spatial scale and relatively open space, and (3) the use of soft sofas brought convenience to casual communication. The main disadvantages of ILS-2 were (1) lack of sufficient power supply, (2) dim lighting and uneven illumination, and (3) Overly open, people walking back and forth were distressing. Lotfy et al.5 pointed out that the furniture of a traditional library is heavy, formal, and immovable, lacking flexibility and diversity, while ILS-2 has provided us with a positive demonstration. The main disadvantages of ILS-3 were (1) limited opening time, (2) poor sound insulation, (3) lack of natural design atmosphere, and (4) insufficient privacy. Wang and Han11 pointed out that greenery in the learning space (such as plants, flowers, natural window landscapes, and green murals) has high restorative properties, which is beneficial for improving the learning mood. ILS-3 can be enhanced through the use of soft wall paintings, which not only absorb certain levels of noise but also allow students to relax.

The weight of indicators

The study determined the proportion of four major decision-making elements, which had consistent rankings among the three ILS, but different proportions (Fig. 32a). Physical factors ranked first and explained about 40% of preference reasons, which fully demonstrated the importance of physical factors. In Barrett et al.'s study45, physical factors accounted for 49% and were also the most important factor. Just because of the differences in specific research subjects, there are differences in the values of this proportion. Barrett et al. pointed out that current space environment quality measurements mainly focus on four aspects: sound, light, heat, and air quality, but there is no consensus on their relative importance in ILS 45. This research providedan explanation for this viewpoint. There were always differences in the ranking of these four physical factors due to the different research objects and the different types of ILS. Figure 32b shows the sorting differences of these four physical factors among the three types of ILS. Barrett et al.'s research indicated that light ranked first45, which is consistent with the performance in ILS-1 and ILS-2 spaces. But in ILS-3, sound ranked first.

Barrett et al. also identified seven key design parameters, which were light, temperature, air quality, owner, flexibility, complexity, and color45. This study also identified seven most critical factors, which varied depending on the type of ILS (Table 18). Compared to Barrett et al.'s research, this classification was more refined, and this study also explored factors such as facility availability, privacy, openness, and opening duration. In terms of weight calculation methods, Barrett et al. pointed out that the application of single factor analysis in multidimensional environments poses certain risks, so they adopted a multi-level analysis approach to establish the model45, which is similar with this research. This study combined the multi factor analysis model with SPSS analysis and comparative scoring method to determine the weights of each indicator.

Conclusion

Main conclusion

This study conducted an in-depth analysis of the typical case of the library "SCUT-L", using a comprehensive research method of UWB positioning technology, physical environment measurement, and survey questionnaires. Several important conclusions have been drawn from this study.

Firstly, the study identified the relationship between choose preference and student attributes and learning behaviors. Master’s and doctoral students preferred to ILS-1 and ILS-3 for targeted learning; Undergraduate students tended to ILS-2 for leisure learning. The relationship between learning behavior and time had been summarized. The targeted learning leaned towards full-time learning, especially with higher vitality at night. The leisure learning tended to at afternoon or break time. The academic exchange had vitality at all times.

Secondly, the study identified the impact of physical environmental factors on user’s perception. Uneven distribution of light, glare, and insufficient illumination were the main problems. Students from different ILS had distinct perceptions of noise. Students of ILS-1 preferred quietness, while users of ILS-2 preferred noise. Most areas exceeded the temperature limit, but some areas received praise for their ability to independently control air conditioning. CO2 content was closely related to the degree of spatial enclosure and air velocity.

Thirdly, the satisfaction rates of ILS had been obtained. Students of ILS-1 emphasized the importance of storage facilities and privacy, ILS-2 users highlighted diversity and flexibility of furniture facilities, and ILS-3 students focused on sound insulation. Research found that the ranking order of decision making elements in different ILS was consistent, but the weights were different. The ranking from high to low based on the percentage of weight was as follows: physical elements, facility and equipment elements, spatial ontology elements, and atmosphere elements. Their weights were 40, 25, 20, and 15%, respectively. The study also obtained seven critical indicators and their weights. In the physical factors of ILS-1 and ILS-2, the key factor was light, while in ILS-3, the key factor was sound.

Lastly, this study combined behaviorist learning theory with constructivist learning theory to establish a new S-R model, which innovated the concept from a theoretical perspective and was well integrated with the environmental perception and behavioral mapping of libraries.

Limitations and research prospects

Although the study has drawn some constructive conclusions, there were also corresponding limitations.

Firstly, due to the complexity and diversity of research factors, this study only measured the ten key physical indicators that characterize the physical environment of ILS, and did not cover all the physical indicators comprehensively. This might affect the final weight value to some extent. According to existing research findings, if more physical environmental factors are taken into account, the proportion of this decision-making elements will further increase. When conducting in-depth research in the future, this research team will try best to measure other factors, in order to achieve more accurate results.

Secondly, in terms of case selection, although the most typical case in Guangzhou was chosen, it only covered three types of ILS. This study did not explore personal private learning spaces (ILS-4), which was a great regret. Personal and private ILS were relatively rare on university campuses in Guangzhou, but some libraries include such learning spaces (ILS-4). With the increasing awareness of self-directed learning, students are increasingly demanding privacy in their personal learning spaces. In the future, more attention will be paid to ILS with ILS-4. After drawing relevant conclusions, This study will conduct comparative analysis with the current three types of ILS.

Thirdly, the research framework and methods can be extended to university libraries in other climate zones. Different climate to some extent affect the design of spaces. In southern China, buildings tend to be more transparent to enhance ventilation, while in northern China, buildings tend to be more enclosed to enhance insulation. Therefore, the number and weight of influencing factors may change to some extent, but the research framework and methodology are fully generalizable.
