Symptom network for psychological distress in college freshmen: a large sample bayesian network analysis
Authors: Shoushi Wang, Zh Yeng Chong, Min Yi, Zhihui Zhang, Wei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCollege students show a high prevalence of psychological distress, particularly during the freshman year. Investigating the network structure of psychological distress symptoms can reveal both comorbid patterns and potential directional relationships among symptoms.MethodsUsing data from a large sample of Chinese university students (N = 14,372), this study estimated a Gaussian graphical model (GGM) and a Bayesian network to explore undirected and directed connections among the nine core dimensions of the Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90).ResultsAnxiety exhibited the highest expected influence (EI) in the GGM, followed by interpersonal sensitivity and depression. In the DAG, depression and anxiety were located upstream in the network, with depression identified as the primary parent node, suggesting that emotional distress may serve as the “trigger” for the entire symptom network. Furthermore, interpersonal sensitivity demonstrated the highest betweenness and occupied a midstream position in the DAG, potentially functioning as a key hub linking other symptoms. Phobic anxiety emerged as a stable downstream node.ConclusionThese findings emphasize the importance of depression, anxiety, and interpersonal sensitivity in the network of psychological distress symptoms and highlight the value of early cognitive-emotional interventions and transdiagnostic strategies targeting symptom interactions.LimitationsThis study did not collect detailed demographic information from participants or include other mental health scales for comparison, which limits the generalizability of the results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04501-w.

Introduction

The university period represents a critical transitional phase between late adolescence and early adulthood. Previous studies have revealed that approximately one-third of university students meet the diagnostic criteria for at least one mental health condition, a prevalence exceeding that observed in the general population [1–3]. Notably, a large-scale meta-analysis on the age of onset of global mental disorders identified 19–20 years as the peak period for the emergence of addictive behaviors, personality disorders, mood disorders, schizophrenia, and primary psychoses [4]. The first year of university presents heightened challenges: for most freshmen, the transition from secondary education involves navigating multiple stressors, including reduced familial support, the formation of new social networks, and adjustment to unfamiliar environments [5, 6]. These challenges may manifest in academic difficulties, interpersonal conflicts, and emotional disturbances such as stress, loneliness, and depression, thereby elevating mental health risks [2, 7, 8]. A pivotal multinational study involving 72,288 university freshmen across 18 countries and regions revealed that 57.4% screened positive for 12-month mental disorders, highlighting the epidemiological significance of psychological distress in this population [9]. Given these considerations, this study focuses on university freshmen to investigate their mental health characteristics through a novel analytical framework.

As defined by the American Psychological Association (APA) Dictionary of Psychology, psychological distress refers to a set of painful mental and physical symptoms that are associated with normal fluctuations of mood in most people [10]. It is recognized as the most prevalent mental health concern among university students [11, 12]. Psychological distress can be understood as a heterogeneous cluster of symptoms, including sadness, stress, low self-esteem, social withdrawal, somatization, obsessive-compulsive tendencies, irritability, and other manifestations [13, 14]. This condition may precipitate numerous adverse outcomes, including academic underperformance, dropout, interpersonal difficulties, and future employment challenges [1, 2, 15, 16]. A meta-analysis has further shown that the presence of a single psychiatric disorder increases the likelihood of developing additional psychiatric disorders, especially for individuals with early-onset conditions before the age of 20 [17]. Therefore, exploring the interactions among different symptoms of psychological distress may not only help identify core targets for early intervention but also shed light on the mechanisms underlying psychiatric comorbidities.

Despite growing research in this area, existing studies on psychological distress among university students exhibit several key limitations. First, prior investigations have typically conceptualized psychological distress as an aggregate construct, often relying on total scores [18–20]. Given the multifaceted nature of psychological distress, such an approach risks overlooking critical symptom-level information. Second, most explanatory models of psychological distress rely on structural equation modeling (SEM), which assumes that the symptom emergence, development, and maintenance of symptoms are influenced by internal and external factors such as gender, personality, or environment [21–23]. However, traditional SEM and latent variable models often fail to capture the direct interactions between symptoms [24]. In response to these limitations, the present study adopts a network approach to investigate psychological distress at the symptom level.

Network theory conceptualizes symptoms not merely as indicators of underlying disorders but as active elements that constitute the disorder itself [24]. According to this framework, mental disorders arise from symptom-to-symptom interactions, and comorbidity results from interconnected symptoms shared across disorders [25]. In psychological networks, nodes represent symptoms and edges denote pairwise associations after controlling for other symptoms. However, the most widely used cross-sectional network model, Gaussian graphical modeling (GGM) [26], generates undirected networks and cannot specify the directionality of associations (i.e., X→Y vs. Y→X). To address this limitation, the current study incorporates Bayesian network analysis to examine conditional independence relationships [27, 28]. Numerous studies have previously employed Bayesian networks to explore psychopathology, including depression, bipolar disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder [29–31]. The directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) estimated through Bayesian network analysis can model admissible causal relationships within observational data, thereby identifying the variables that truly drive the symptom network [28]. However, the complex reciprocal interactions often found among psychological symptoms may not fully satisfy the underlying assumptions of Bayesian networks. A combined application of both GGMs and Bayesian networks is recommended to overcome the individual limitations of each approach [32–35].

To ensure comprehensive coverage of psychological distress symptoms, this study employed the Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90) [36] as the primary assessment instrument. Research has demonstrated its reliability in capturing symptom severity through its nine subscale scores [13]. These nine dimensions show a high degree of congruence with the psychological distress typically encountered by university freshmen. For instance, they encompass depressive symptoms, anxiety, and interpersonal concerns, which have been identified in previous research as the most prevalent issues in this population, as well as somatization symptoms that may be triggered by the academic pressures of transitioning to university life [37]. Additionally, multiple studies have confirmed its alignment with dimensional psychiatric frameworks such as the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) model [38, 39]. To date, several studies have employed network analysis to explore the associations among the dimensions of the SCL-90. For instance, Teng et al. [40] conducted a ten-year longitudinal study of 24,047 college freshmen, estimating annual SCL-90 dimensional networks. Their findings consistently identified anxiety, depression, and interpersonal sensitivity as the most central symptoms within the network across the ten-year period. Similar findings regarding the centrality of these three dimensions have been observed in network analyses across diverse populations, including individuals with suicidal ideation, adolescents, patients with eating disorders, and manufacturing employees [41–44]. However, no existing research has used directed network analysis to examine the potential predictive relationships among these symptoms. Identifying core symptoms, especially the “trigger” symptoms, is crucial for implementing more effective transdiagnostic interventions. Therefore, this study employs network analysis to describe both undirected and directed relationships among psychological distress symptoms in college freshmen, aiming to identify optimal intervention targets and advance the development of precision mental health practices.

This study was conducted to investigate the network structure of psychological distress symptoms in university freshmen using a large dataset. Based on a sample of 14,372 freshmen, we employed the SCL-90 to map the interplay among nine psychological dimensions. Specifically, we combined GGM with Bayesian network analysis to identify the most central symptoms and explore potential directional pathways within the network. By integrating these two complementary approaches, this study aims to provide a more granular understanding of the potential associations among symptoms of psychological distress during the early university stage.

Methods

Participants and procedures

The participants (N = 14,372) in this study were first-year undergraduate students from two universities in southern and northern China, located in Changsha, Hunan (N = 10,831) and Weifang, Shandong (N = 3,541). Of the participants, 56.1% were female (N = 8,069), and 43.8% were male (N = 6,302), with one participant not reporting gender. Data were collected between October and November 2023 and were distributed electronically alongside the routine mental health assessment for freshmen. In the first school, all incoming students participated in the survey. In the second school, a random sample of half of the freshman participated in the study. Power analysis was conducted using the R package powerly [45]. A Gaussian graphical model with nine nodes and an estimated network density of approximately 0.4 was employed. With a statistical power of 0.8 and sensitivity of 0.6, the required minimum sample size was 393 participants. The final sample size far exceeded this requirement, ensuring the robustness and reliability of the findings.

Measures

Participants reported their gender and academic year and completed the Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90) [33] to assess psychological distress. This study used the Chinese version of the SCL-90, translated by Wang [46], which has been widely applied in Chinese contexts [47]. The SCL-90 consists of 90 items divided across nine dimensions : somatization (SOM; 12 items), encompassing cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and respiratory symptoms, muscle aches, back pain, headaches, and other anxiety-related physical manifestations; obsessive-compulsive (O-C; 10 items), assessing intrusive thoughts, compulsions, and broader cognitive-behavioral disturbances; interpersonal sensitivity (I-S; 9 items), evaluating social discomfort, feelings of inferiority, and negative expectations; depression (DEP; 13 items), characterized by depressed mood, anhedonia, hopelessness, and cognitive-somatic symptoms, including suicidality; anxiety (ANX; 10 items), measuring restlessness, tension, hyperarousal, panic attacks, and dissociation; hostility (HOS; 6 items), reflecting irritability, anger outbursts, argumentativeness, and hostile affect; phobic anxiety (PHOB; 7 items), covering agoraphobia, social phobia symptoms, and specific fear responses; paranoid ideation (PAR; 6 items), involving suspiciousness, persecutory ideation, ideas of reference, and grandiosity; and psychoticism (PSY; 10 items), indicating psychosis-like symptoms and schizotypal features. The remaining seven items were excluded from the primary analysis because they do not theoretically form a single dimension and may not represent the same latent construct. This nine-dimension structure is consistent with multiple validation studies and normative data established for Chinese university students [48–50].

Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“not at all”) to 5 (“extremely”). The total score represents the severity of overall psychological distress. The severity of each dimension is calculated as the average score of all items within that dimension. Previous studies have confirmed the reliability of the SCL-90 in university student populations. In the current study, the overall and dimension-specific reliabilities were satisfactory, with detailed results presented in Table 1.

Data analysis

Data were initially cleaned and analyzed for reliability and descriptive statistics using SPSS 25.

Graphical Gaussian Model (GGM)

We used a Graphical Gaussian Model (GGM) to estimate a network of regularized partial correlations among symptom dimensions [26]. The GGM was estimated using the graphical Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) algorithm [51] with the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC), via the R packages glasso [52] and qgraph [53]. This approach balances network sparsity and sensitivity, reducing the likelihood of false-positive edges. Nodes represented SCL-90 symptom dimensions (e.g., depression, anxiety), and edges denoted conditional dependencies controlling for all other nodes. The network was visualized using qgraph, with edge width indicating the strength of partial correlations and edge color representing directionality (positive: blue; negative: red).

Centrality indices were calculated to determine the influence of individual nodes. Using qgraph, three indices were calculated: expected influence (EI), closeness, and betweenness [53]. EI represents the sum of edge weights connected to a node. EI was selected as the primary indicator of node centrality rather than strength centrality because, unlike strength centrality, (which sums the absolute values of all edges), EI takes into account both edge weights and their positive or negative signs. It can more accurately reflect a node’s ability to activate or inhibit activity within the network. Betweenness quantifies the number of shortest paths passing through a node, indicating its bridging role. Closeness represents the average distance from a node to all other nodes, indicating the node’s efficiency in information transmission. Predictability quantifies the extent to which a node is predicted by all its neighboring nodes and was estimated using the R package mgm [54, 55]. In the GGM, predictability was represented as the area of the sector around each node.

Network stability was tested using the R package bootnet [56]. Non-parametric bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) was performed to compute 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the edge weights, thereby assessing their accuracy. Case-dropping bootstrap procedures were used to assess central stability, with the correlation stability coefficient (CS-C) indicating the largest proportion of data that can be excluded without significantly altering centrality estimates. According to Epskamp et al. [26] [56], a CS-C of at least 0.25 is required for reliable centrality interpretation.

Directed acyclic graph (DAG)

Bayesian network analysis employs machine-learning algorithms to infer potential causal directions from cross-sectional data, resulting in directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). In this study, the hill-climbing algorithm in the R package bnlearn [57] was used to estimate the Bayesian network and visualize the DAG. This algorithm is an iterative machine-learning procedure that continuously adds, removes, and reverses connections in the network to optimize the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), a target score for the goodness of fit, ultimately retaining the best-fitting DAG.

To ensure the stability of the DAG, the current study estimated edge frequency and directional consistency across 5,000 bootstrap samples. An edge was included in the final average DAG if it appeared in at least 85% of the bootstrap samples and pointed from symptom X to symptom Y in at least 51% of the samples. Edge thickness in the DAG represents the likelihood of directional stability across bootstrap samples.

In addition, two sets of sensitivity analyses were performed. First, given the large sample size and following the reviewer’s suggestion, we employed the model search (ggmModSelect) [58] method to estimate the GGM in addition to the LASSO regularized method. Second, since some previous SCL-90 network studies combined the seven excluded items into an “Other” dimension [42, 44, 59], we estimated a supplementary 10-node network to examine the impact of these items.

This study is based on data from routine school-based psychological screenings and was not pre-registered prior to the start of the study. The data and code for this study have been publicly released to OSF (https://osf.io/jz73n/?view_only=cc496cc1caf24fac994534e8dd3d4157).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 14,372 first-year Chinese undergraduate university students participated in this study, with 3,541 students from Shandong Province and 10,831 from Hunan Province. Among them, 8,069 (56.1%) were male and 6,303 (43.9%) were female. At the second participating university, a cluster sampling strategy was utilized. A random number generator was used to select approximately half of the university’s departments, and all eligible freshmen within these selected departments were invited to participate in the survey. Descriptive statistics were calculated for the SCL-90 scores. Detailed results are provided in Table 1.

Accuracy and stability of network parameters

Results from 5,000 bootstrap samples indicated that edge estimates in the network were stable (see Figure S1). Centrality stability coefficients were all above the threshold of 0.25, with CS-C values of 0.99 for EI, 0.89 for closeness, and 0.78 for betweenness, indicating robust centrality estimates. Detailed results are presented in Figures S2-S4.

Graphical gaussian model

Figure 1 shows the GGM of the SCL-90 dimensions, and Fig. 2 displays the corresponding centrality indices. Anxiety, depression, and interpersonal sensitivity had the highest EIs, indicating greater importance within the network. Specifically, anxiety had an EI of 1.25, interpersonal sensitivity had an EI of 1.15, and depression had an EI of 1.13, all significantly higher than those of the remaining nodes. Depression and interpersonal sensitivity also exhibited the highest closeness values (closeness = 0.019), suggesting that these symptoms act as efficient hubs for information dissemination. Interpersonal sensitivity also had the highest betweenness (7), indicating its potential role as a central bridge. In terms of predictability, all nodes had predictability values above 0.6, indicating that changes in their neighboring nodes could explain at least 60% of the variation. Similar to the centrality results, depression (0.840), anxiety (0.834), and interpersonal sensitivity (0.833) had the highest predictability. The specific centrality and predictability values for each node are presented in Table 2. Results of the centrality difference tests are presented in Figures S3-S5.

Sensitivity analysis

The GGM results calculated using ggmModSelect were nearly identical to those obtained via the LASSO method, with the only minor exception being a slight increase in the closeness centrality of Interpersonal Sensitivity to 0.021. Detailed results are provided in the Supplementary Material (Table S7; Figures S8–S9).

Additionally, the 10-node network (including the “Other” dimension) also yielded results consistent with our original nine-node network, further confirming the reliability of our findings. Detailed statistics for the 10-node network are presented in Tables S10–S11 and Figures S12–S14.

Discussion

In the present study, GGM and DAG analyses were employed to examine the symptom network of psychological distress among a large sample of 14,372 first-year college students. The results emphasized the central roles of the depression dimension, anxiety dimension, and interpersonal sensitivity dimension within the psychological distress network. Specifically, the depression dimension and anxiety dimension functioned as primary driving nodes, while the interpersonal sensitivity dimension emerged as a central hub for symptom propagation. In addition, the study highlighted strong associations between the somatization dimension and multiple other dimensions, including anxiety, hostility, and paranoid ideation, as well as between obsessive-compulsive symptoms and interpersonal sensitivity. The phobic anxiety dimension was identified as a downstream symptom influenced by multiple psychological domains.

First, the GGM results showed that the depression dimension and anxiety dimension exhibited the strongest expected influence and closeness, indicating their predominant activating roles within the symptom network. This finding is consistent with previous studies that used GGM to investigate the network of psychological distress among university freshmen [40]. In the DAG, the depression dimension functioned as a parent node, directing all other symptoms, which aligns with prior studies that have positioned depression at the hierarchical apex of psychopathological networks [35, 60, 61]. According to Beck’s cognitive model of depression, depressive symptoms may lead individuals to develop a negative interpretation bias, an inclination to interpret situations negatively [62]. Martin-Romero and Sanchez-Lopez [63] found that this negative interpretation bias was present in clinically depressed, subclinically depressed, and remitted samples. This bias may, in turn, contribute to the development of additional psychiatric symptoms [64] and is increasingly regarded as a transdiagnostic feature [65]. While the depression subscale of the SCL-90 employed in this study is not a complete substitute for a formal diagnostic measure of depressive disorder, our results offer preliminary evidence supporting the potential primacy of depressive symptoms within the distress network.

In the DAG, the anxiety dimension occupied a secondary position relative to depression, directing all of the remaining dimensions. Marr et al. [55] [66], in an 18-year longitudinal study, found that generalized anxiety predicted the onset of major depressive disorder in 16.08% of cases, while depression predicted the emergence of anxiety in 20.59% of cases. Similarly, the present study found a strong association between the depression dimension and anxiety dimension. Although the DAG showed a directional link from the depression dimension to the anxiety dimension, the bootstrap analysis revealed that this direction appeared in only 55% of networks, suggesting the possibility of a bidirectional relationship. Previous epidemiological reviews have also noted that anxiety typically precedes the onset of other psychiatric disorders [67], a finding partially supported in the current study.

Second, the interpersonal sensitivity dimension showed the highest betweenness centrality in the GGM, suggesting that it serves as a central hub for information dissemination across the network. In the DAG, the interpersonal sensitivity dimension was situated centrally, bridging higher-order symptoms (e.g., depression, anxiety, and paranoia) with downstream dimensions (e.g., aggression, compulsion, somatization, and phobic anxiety). Interpersonal sensitivity refers to heightened concern about others’ emotions and behaviors [68–69]. According to Cognitive-Affective Processing Systems (CAPS) theory, individuals with higher interpersonal sensitivity may misinterpret and distort others’ actions, contributing to a variety of negative psychological outcomes, including anger, loneliness, and depression [70–72]. Interpersonal sensitivity has been shown to correlate significantly with depression [73, 74], anxiety disorders [75, 76], paranoia [77], borderline personality disorder [78, 79], and the development of psychosis [69, 80]. However, studies have not yet adequately explored the directionality of these associations [81]. The present study offers preliminary insights, suggesting that interpersonal sensitivity may mediate the externalization of internal emotional and cognitive symptoms into somatic and behavioral manifestations.

In addition, the GGM revealed three highly weighted edges: somatization with anxiety, hostility with paranoid ideation, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms with interpersonal sensitivity. Among them, the edge between the somatization dimension and anxiety dimension also showed high strength in the DAG, with the anxiety dimension directing to the somatization dimension in 70% of bootstrap networks. This suggests a relatively stable directional relationship, consistent with prior evidence indicating that anxiety frequently precedes physical illness [67]. Similarly, the DAG showed the paranoid ideation dimension directing to the hostility dimension in 61% of networks, reinforcing previous findings that individuals with paranoid ideation symptoms are more likely to infer or perceive non-existent hostility [82–84]. The connection between the interpersonal sensitivity dimension and obsessive-compulsive symptoms dimension appeared in 56% of bootstrap networks, in line with the results of previous studies [85]. The relatively low directional certainty suggests a strong bidirectional relationship, possibly due to the role of excessive reassurance seeking (ERS) as a shared maintenance factor for both constructs [86]. This implies that safety-seeking behavior in response to perceived social or general threats may generate self-reinforcing symptom loops.

Finally, the DAG results revealed strong directional links from the phobic anxiety dimension to other dimensions, including interpersonal sensitivity, anxiety, hostility, and somatization, each exceeding an 80% probability. This suggests that phobic anxiety symptoms in the SCL-90 may represent a downstream outcome in the symptom network, potentially following the emergence of upstream symptom clusters rather than driving them. The SCL-90 phobic anxiety dimension primarily captures agoraphobic and social phobic symptoms. Existing evidence links social phobia to rejection sensitivity [87–89] and perceived hostility [90], which may explain its associations with interpersonal sensitivity and hostility. Additionally, fears related to public transportation or being alone often stem from concerns about experiencing somatic emergencies (e.g., panic attacks), which may account for the link with somatization [91]. Although studies have suggested that phobias predict the onset of generalized anxiety disorder [92], the reverse predictive relationship remains unexplored and warrants further investigation. In summary, the findings of this study indicate that phobic anxiety symptoms occupy a relatively stable downstream position within the network. This suggests that the emotional exhaustion stemming from other symptom clusters may statistically manifest as a coping style characterized by fear and avoidance [93].

The present study is the first to explore both undirected and directed relationships among symptoms of psychological distress by combining Gaussian graphical modeling and Bayesian networks in a large sample of more than 10,000 participants. At the theoretical level, the findings identified depressive symptoms as a highly central node within the network, showing robust statistical associations with various maladaptive cognitive patterns across multiple domains. This positions depression as a potential upstream indicator within this measurement framework. The interpersonal sensitivity dimension emerged as a central transmission hub, possibly facilitating the transformation of internal emotional and cognitive disturbances into somatic and behavioral symptoms. Phobic anxiety symptoms were consistently positioned at the downstream end of the network, indicating their vulnerability to influence from other symptoms. At the clinical level, interventions should primarily focus on emotional disturbances (depression and anxiety) through evidence-based approaches that address negative cognitive biases, such as cognitive behavioral therapy or mindfulness-based cognitive therapy [92, 94–96]. The high predictability exhibited by all nodes suggests that interventions targeting a single symptom may generalize and improve other symptoms as wll. For college students experiencing emotional difficulties, incorporating interpersonal psychotherapy [97, 98] may help prevent symptom escalation or spread. Existing research has indicated that IPT may prevent the onset of depressive disorders in individuals with subthreshold depression [99]. In the future, the potential of IPT to prevent the worsening of mental health problems among university freshmen could be further examined. Finally, the symptom pairs of somatization-anxiety, hostility-paranoid ideation, and obsessive-compulsive-interpersonal sensitivity may serve as specific targets for novel or integrated intervention strategies.

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, a major limitation of this study is its exclusive reliance on a single self-report instrument, the SCL-90. Although the SCL-90 is a widely used and efficient tool for large-scale screening, relying on one instrument significantly constrains the informativeness and interpretability of the results. Specifically, without the inclusion of complementary scales or structured clinical interviews, we were unable to cross-check different symptom areas or empirically determine the convergent and discriminant validity of the identified constructs. Furthermore, as noted in previous psychometric evaluations of the SCL-90 in Chinese student populations [49], the high correlations among subscales may indicate insufficient discriminant validity, suggesting that the nodes in our network might represent a broad factor of general distress rather than distinct, independent clinical disorders. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as associations between self-reported symptom dimensions rather than confirmed relationships among full psychological constructs such as major depressive disorder or anxiety disorders.

Second, since our data were drawn from annual school-based mental health assessments of students in the same grade, we did not obtain detailed information on participants’ exact ages or socioeconomic status. The sample was limited to college freshmen, excluding individuals from other age groups, educational backgrounds, or cultural contexts, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings. Future research should aim to replicate these findings in more diverse populations.

Third, the cross-sectional design of this study inherently limits our ability to draw definitive causal inferences. Although Bayesian networks can provide directional probability estimates to identify plausible causal relationships in the data, these statistical indications represent only probabilistic preferences rather than temporal causal relationships established through a longitudinal design. Interpretations of these causal relationships must rely on critical assumptions, including the absence of other key driving variables and the absence of circular relationships among symptoms. For example, the probability of a causal relationship between anxiety and depression in our study was only 55%. The lower probabilities represented by the thinner edges in these networks suggest the potential for bidirectional relationships. For thicker edges, we can have greater confidence in our assessment of predictive relationships. Therefore, the directional results obtained in this study should be interpreted with caution. Future work should employ dynamic Bayesian network models to examine the temporal evolution of symptom interactions [28].

Fourth, the reliance on self-report measures introduces potential social desirability bias, which could compromise the validity of responses. Future studies could consider multimodal assessments, including physiological measures (e.g., cortisol levels) or behavioral indicators (e.g., eye-tracking during social tasks). Finally, another limitation is that our analysis was conducted at the subscale level. Although this provides a clear overview of the interactions between psychological dimensions, it may overlook fine-grained dynamics among individual symptoms. Future studies could employ Exploratory Graph Analysis (EGA) [100] or other community detection methods to investigate the symptom network at the item level.

Conclusion

The findings of the present study suggest that depressive and anxiety symptoms exert the strongest influence within the psychological distress network and occupy upstream positions in the directed network, indicating their potential causal primacy. These results imply that emotional problems may contribute to the development of other psychological symptoms and thus represent key targets for early intervention. Furthermore, interpersonal sensitivity emerged as a central bridge symptom, potentially mediating the spread from internalizing to externalizing problems. In contrast, phobic anxiety was situated at the distal end of the network and appeared to be more strongly influenced by other symptoms than to influence them. This study constitutes the first exploratory investigation of symptom-level associations in psychological distress using a large-scale, multicenter sample of Chinese university freshmen and provides meaningful implications for identifying potential intervention targets in future mental health efforts.
