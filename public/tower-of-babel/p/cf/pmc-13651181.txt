Associations of sleep duration, physical activity, and longitudinal changes with the risk of fatal stroke occurrence in middle-aged to older individuals
Authors: Han-wen Hu, Jun-xiao Li, Qing Li, Jin-wei Zhang, Feng-lian Liang, Wei-sen Zhang, Ya-li Jin, Jing Pan, Feng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep and physical activity (PA) are associated with the risk of stroke, but the relationships between longitudinal changes in sleep and PA habits and between their combination index and fatal stroke are unclear.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study on the risks of stroke and sleep and physical activity (PA), 26,672 participants with 951 stroke deaths in the Guangzhou Biobank Cohort Study (GBCS) and 7218 participants with 49 stroke deaths in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were enrolled after mean follow-up times of 15.4 (SD = 3.57) and 6.71 (SD = 3.43) years, respectively.ResultsCompared with stable participants, GBCS participants with sleep durations ≤ 5 and ≥ 9 h were at greater risk for fatal all-stroke (aHR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.10–1.66, P = 0.004; aHR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.16–1.87, P = 0.001) and fatal ischemic stroke (aHR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.14–2.03, P = 0.004; aHR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.18–2.27, P = 0.003); longitudinally increased sleep duration was associated with greater risks for fatal all-stroke (aHR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.04–1.72, P = 0.02) and fatal ischemic stroke (aHR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.08–2.12, P = 0.02), and greater risks for fatal ischemic stroke were presented in moderate–inactive PA (aHR = 3.24, 95% CI: 1.49–7.05, P = 0.003). The NHANES substandard PA had a greater risk for fatal all-stroke (aHR = 2.86, 95% CI: 1.54–5.30, P < 0.001) than did standard PA; a greater risk of fatal all-stroke was associated with short sleep + substandard PA (aHR = 7.11, 95% CI: 2.46–20.54, P < 0.001) and normal sleep + substandard PA (aHR = 2.46, 95% CI: 1.16–5.24, P = 0.02) than with normal sleep + standard PA.ConclusionsAbnormal sleep and longitudinal changes in sleep and substandard PA are associated with an increased risk of fatal stroke occurrence; these findings may be potential indicators of future fatal stroke occurrence in relatively healthy middle-aged to older individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28258-4.Keywords: Sleep, Physical activity, Stroke, Ischaemic, Haemorrhagic

Introduction

A Global Burden of Disease report revealed that approximately 100 million people worldwide in 2019 suffered from stroke (the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability) [1], and approximately 17.04 million people are aged ≥ 40 years and currently suffer from stroke in China, ranking first in the world [2]. Among stroke subtypes, hemorrhagic mortality is greater, and ischemic onset is more common. Among the subtypes of ischemic infarction, cardioembolic stroke has the highest in-hospital mortality, and the short-term prognosis is poor compared with that of other subtypes [3]. Detailed research on stroke and its subtypes is highly valuable for the precise prevention of this disease.

The risk factors for stroke include age, sex, obesity, diabetes, hypertension, physical activity (PA), smoking, etc. [1], and sleep and PA are receiving increasing attention. Some studies have reported that too little and too much sleep are associated with stroke, whereas a long duration of sleep has a strong effect, with a J-shaped relationship [4]. Most results concerning the relationship between too little or too much sleep and stroke are still controversial. Studies have also indicated that a certain level of PA, especially moderate-intensity PA, can prevent stroke [5–10]. However, different studies often have inconsistent grouping criteria for the intensity and energy consumption of different populations in different regions. In a cohort study, a decrease in PA was associated with an increased risk of stroke mortality, but high-intensity PA was associated with a lower risk of stroke mortality among elderly Chinese [11, 12] and the United States population [13].

Sleep and PA may be interdependent, influence each other, and affect health through related pathways. Previous studies have shown that PA can improve health by improving metabolic status, maintaining stable circadian rhythms and healthy sleep patterns, and increasing energy expenditure [14–17]. There was a significant relationship between the average sleep duration of nonstroke patients and stroke risk factors. To some extent, appropriate prevention methods and interventions targeting PA and sleep can reduce the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [18–23], especially coronary heart disease or stroke events [24]. However, there was no statistically significant relationship between PA levels, fatigue and sleep in stroke survivors; in particular, a research gap remains in the associations between longitudinal changes in PA and sleep and their combined indicators and stroke outcomes [25]. The relationships between sleep duration, PA (especially their longitudinal changes), their combined index and stroke risk are not clear in middle-aged to older individuals. Therefore, exploring potential predictors related to sleep duration and physical activity for the incidence and mortality of different stroke subtypes (atherothrombotic, cardioembolic, lacunar, unusual, essential infarct, and intracerebral hemorrhage) among the elderly population would be extremely interesting in the future.

Materials and methods

Data and study participants

This is a prospective analysis of a prognostic hematological score for fatal stroke in the Guangzhou Biobank Cohort Study (GBCS) from 2003–2022 and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2007–2018, and all participants were recruited from a population of permanent residents aged ≥ 50 years. The details of the GBCS have been reported previously [6]: the baseline (from September 2003–February 2008) and 1 st follow-up (from March 2008–December 2012) information included a face-to-face computer-assisted interview by trained nurses on lifestyle, family and personal medical history and assessments of anthropometric data, blood pressure and laboratory tests. Each participant had made an appointment in advance to ensure good health, was able to visit the designated place by himself/herself and was able to sit and rest for at least half an hour before sampling and examination. The NHANES is a representative national population survey of the United States aimed at assessing the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. The NHANES data are collected every two years as a cycle by the National Centre for Health Statistics (NCHS), including collection of demographic, socioeconomic, dietary, and health-related data through trained investigators; medical, dental, and anthropometric examinations at a mobile inspection center; and laboratory tests conducted by trained medical personnel. The NCHS Research Ethics Review Committee approved the survey protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Exposure indicators

Sleep

Sleep refers to daily sleep duration, which is from face-to-face computer-assisted interviews; sleep duration categories are divided into ≤ 5, 5–7, 7, 7–9, and ≥ 9 h in the GBCS [26] and ≤ 5, 5–7, 7, 7–9, and ≥ 9 h in the NHANES. Longitudinal change in sleep time in the GBCSs: The longitudinal change in sleep time refers to a change in this indicator from baseline to the 1 st follow-up in a cohort study (sleep time at the 1 st follow-up—sleep time at baseline) ÷ sleep time at baseline; its groups are as follows: decreased sleep time (< –20%), basically unchanged sleep time (−20%–20%), and increased sleep time (> 20%).

Physical activity

GBCS data: The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [27] was used, which includes information on walking frequency and duration, all vigorous and moderate activities lasting at least 10 min, and the duration of sedentary activities. The weekly reported minutes for each activity are weighted on the basis of the metabolic equivalent of task (MET) energy expenditure [11]. They are classified into three levels: inactive, moderate, and active. Longitudinal changes in PAs: Baseline PAs were divided into inactive, moderate, and active groups, which were fully combined with inactive, moderate, and active PAs, respectively, during the 1 st follow-up. They are divided into nine groups: inactive-inactive, inactive-moderate, inactive-active, moderate-inactive, moderate-moderate, moderate-active, active moderate-inactive, active moderate-moderate, and active moderate-active. In addition, a general classification of longitudinal changes in PA was conducted for nine groups, including increased PA (inactive–moderate, inactive–active, moderate–active), stable PA (inactive–inactive, moderate–moderate, and active), and decreased PA to inactive (moderate–inactive and active–inactive).

NHANES data: PA was measured via the General Practice Assessment Questionnaire (GPAQ). The GPAQ collects weekly PA information at the respondent level and covers several components, including work-related PA, transportation-related PA, and leisure-time PA. These types of activities are further subdivided into vigorous PA/vigorous recreational PA, moderate PA/moderate recreational PA, and transportation PA. The weekly MET is determined via the recommended MET scores from the NHANES, with 8 points for vigorous work activity/vigorous recreational activity and 4 points for moderate work physical activity/moderate recreational PA and traffic PA. The weekly metabolic equivalent of PA was calculated via the MET score, daily duration, and number of days participating each week: total PA MET minutes/week = [(daily duration of vigorous PA × days of vigorous recreational PA per week × 8) + (daily duration of moderate PA × days of moderate PA per week × 4) + (daily duration of traffic-related PA × days of traffic-related PA per week × 4) + (daily duration of vigorous recreational PA × days of vigorous recreational PA per week × 8) + (daily duration of moderate recreational PA × days of moderate recreational PA per week × 4)] [28]. PA categories are divided into standard PA (vigorous PA/vigorous recreational PA and moderate PA) and substandard PA (moderate recreational PA and transportation PA) in the NHANES.

Sleep duration combined with physical activity

GBCS data: The combined indices of sleep and PA are classified as short sleep (< 6 h)-inactive PA, short sleep-moderate PA, short sleep-active PA, normal sleep (6–8 h)-inactive PA, normal sleep-moderate PA, normal sleep-active PA, long sleep (> 8 h)-inactive PA, and long sleep-active PA [19, 29]. NHANES data: The combined indicators of sleep and PA are classified as short sleep (< 6 h)-substandard PA, short sleep-standard PA, normal sleep (6–8 h)-substandard PA, normal sleep-standard PA, long sleep (> 8 h)-substandard PA, and long sleep-standard PA [19, 29].

Study outcomes

Information on the underlying causes of death up to July 30, 2022, was obtained mostly via record linkages with the Guangzhou Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (GZCDC). Owing to the lack of other information on stroke severity, infarct volume, lesion site and infectious complications, fatal stroke was chosen as the primary outcome of this study. The causes of death were coded according to the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases as follows: I60 ~ I69 for stroke; I60.0 ~ I62.9 and I69.0 ~ I69.2 for hemorrhagic stroke; I63.0 ~ I63.9 and I69.3 for ischemic stroke; and the other codes for unclassified stroke. When death certificates were not issued by medical institutions, the causes were verified by the GZCDC as part of their quality assurance programmed by cross-checking past medical history; additionally, five prominent clinicians from the Guangzhou Twelfth People’s Hospital and the Universities of Hong Kong, China and Birmingham, UK, performed a verbal autopsy [30, 31].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as the means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used for categorical variables, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Kruskal‒Wallis test were used for continuous variables. A restrictive cubic spline plot was used to represent the relationship between sleep duration and changes in sleep duration with fatal stroke. In the baseline study, the sleep groups were ≤ 5, 5–7, 7, 7–9, and ≥ 9 h; the PAs were divided into inactive, moderate, and active groups, with the active group being the control group; and the sleep + PA groups were divided into short sleep-inactive PAs, short sleep-moderate PAs, short sleep-active PAs, normal sleep-inactive PAs, normal sleep-moderate PAs, normal sleep-active PAs, long sleep-inactive PAs, long sleep-moderate PAs, and long sleep-active PAs. In the follow-up study, sleep changes were grouped into declines (< –20%), stable (−20%–20%), and increases (> 20%). The changes in PAs were divided into inactive–inactive, inactive–moderate, inactive–active, moderate–inactive, moderate–moderate, moderate–active, active–inactive, active–moderate, and active–active. The other category included total changes that increased (inactive–moderate, inactive–active, and moderate–active), remained essentially unchanged (inactive–inactive, moderate, and active), and decreased to inactive values (moderate–inactive, and active–inactive).

The NHANES analyses are conducted under complex weighting: if the continuous variables satisfy normality, a statistical description is performed using the mean ± standard error, and a comparison is made via a t test or analysis of variance. If the data were not normally distributed, the median and interquartile ranges were used for statistical description, and comparisons were performed via the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables are described statistically as frequencies (unweighted) and rates or composition ratios (weighted) and were compared via the chi square test. Deaths/follow-up person-years represent the lethal stroke mortality rate, with the mortality rate unit being per 100,000 person-years. Sleep duration was divided into groups of ≤ 5, 5–7, 7, 7–9, and ≥ 9 h. PAs were divided into standard and substandard groups. Sleep + PAs were divided into short sleep + substandard PAs, short sleep + standard PAs, normal sleep + substandard PAs, normal sleep-standard PAs, long sleep-standard PAs, and long sleep-standard PAs.

The above analyses used a weighted Cox regression model, with the final results presented as the adjusted risk ratio (HR) and its 95% CI, where sleep and PA are core exposure variables, whereas diabetes status, hypertension status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and body mass index are included in comorbid conditions and lifestyle factors, rather than stratified or matched objects. Model 1 is the original model; Model 2 includes age, sex, and education level; and Model 3 includes age, sex, education, smoking status, diabetes status, hypertension status, BMI, and alcohol consumption. The mediation package in R was used to analyse the mediation effect. All the above statistical analyses were conducted via R 4.1.3 statistical software, and the threshold for statistical significance was P<0.05 with a focus on the point estimates and their corresponding confidence intervals.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 30,430 participants from the GBCS were screened, with 286 participants excluded because of a history of stroke, 382 because of loss of vital status, and 3,086 because of missing values for variables; ultimately, a total of 26,672 subjects at baseline (2003–2008) were included, and 951 stroke (489 ischemic, 289 hemorrhagic, and 173 unclassified) deaths in July 2022 were recorded after a mean follow-up time of 15.34 (SD = 3.57) years. A total of 14,984 (sleep) or 14,965 (PA) subjects without a history of stroke at the 1 st follow-up (from March 2008–December 2012) were included; 363 stroke deaths (192 ischemic, 106 hemorrhagic, 65 unclassified) from sleep and 361 stroke deaths (190 ischemic, 107 hemorrhagic, 64 unclassified) from PA were recorded. Additionally, a total of 59,842 participants (2007–2018) from the NHANES were screened, with the number of excluded participants being 420 because of a stroke history, 10,535 because of an age of < 50 years, 40,737 at baseline and 919 at follow-up (~ December 31 st, 2019) because of missing values for variables; ultimately, a total of 7,218 subjects (2007–2018) were included, and 49 stroke deaths were recorded after a mean follow-up time of 6.71 (SD = 3.43) years (Fig. 1).

Participant screening flowchart for the GBCS (A and B) and the NHANES (C). GBCS: Guangzhou Biobank Cohort Study; NHANES: National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey

The number of participants who slept for 5–7 h was greater, and the proportion of people with a college education or above who slept ≤ 5 h was the greatest. The prevalence rates of diabetes and hypertension were the highest among participants who quit smoking and slept ≥ 9 h. The mortality rate of stroke was highest in i participants who slept ≤ 5 h. The GBCS population with a sleep duration of ≥ 9 h and the NHANES participants with a sleep duration of ≤ 5 h had the highest rate of a BMI ≥ 28 (Table 1).

Sleep duration and longitudinal changes in sleep duration and the risk of fatal stroke

In a restricted cubic spline model, the relationships between sleep duration, sleep duration changes and the risk of fatal all-stroke risk showed V-shaped trends, with node values of approximately 7 h for sleep duration and 0 h for sleep duration changes after adjusting for a series of confounding factors (Fig. 2). Compared with those who slept for 7 h, those who slept for ≤ 5 h had a significantly increased risk of fatal all-stroke (aHR = 1.35; 95% CI: 1.1–1.66; P = 0.004), fatal ischemic stroke (aHR = 1.52; 95% CI: 1.14–2.03; P = 0.004), and fatal unclassified stroke (aHR = 1.77; 95% CI: 1.12–2.81; P = 0.01). Similarly, those with a sleep duration of ≥ 9 h had a significantly increased risk of fatal all-stroke (aHR = 1.47; 95% CI: 1.16–1.87; P = 0.001) and fatal ischemic stroke (aHR = 1.63; 95% CI: 1.18–2.27; P = 0.003) (Fig. 3 A). Compared with longitudinally stable sleep, increased sleep was associated with a significantly greater risk of fatal all-stroke (aHR = 1.34; 95% CI: 1.04–1.72; P = 0.02) and fatal ischemic stroke (aHR = 1.51; 95% CI: 1.08–2.12; P = 0.02); decreased sleep had no similar effect on the risk of fatal all-stroke (aHR = 1.22; 95% CI: 0.92–1.62; P = 0.17) or fatal ischemic stroke (aHR = 1.22; 95% CI: 0.82–1.79; P = 0.33) after adjusting for a series of confounding factors (Fig. 3 B).

Changes in PA and its longitudinal distribution and the risk of fatal stroke

Compared with active PAs, inactive PAs and moderate PAs were not significantly associated with the risk of fatal strokes (Supplemental Fig. 1); a longitudinal decrease in PA was significantly associated with an increased risk of fatal ischemic stroke (aHR = 3.24; 95% CI: 1.49–7.05; P = 0.003) in the GBCS (Fig. 5 A). Mediating effects of sleep duration, physical activity and the risk of fatal stroke

In the GBCS, the indirect effect of PA on fatal all-stroke through sleep was 0.00012 (P = 0.98), and the direct effect was 0.29 (P = 0.22) (Supplementary Fig. 2 A); the indirect effect of sleep on fatal all-stroke through PA was 2.76 (P = 0.53), and the direct effect was −3.79 (P = 0.94) (Supplementary Fig. 2B). In the NHANES, the indirect effect value of PA on the occurrence of fatal all-stroke through sleep was 0.0003 (P = 0.26), and the direct effect value was 0.06 (P = 0.16) (Supplemental Fig. 2 C); the indirect effect value of sleep on fatal all-stroke through PA was −42.3 (P = 0.28), and the direct effect value was −565 (P = 0.10) (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this study, we used the GBCS and the NHANES to investigate the associations of sleep time, physical activity (especially longitudinal changes in sleep time and physical activity), and their combined indices with the risk of fatal stroke in middle-aged and elderly people. We observed that a sleep duration that was too short or too long, a longitudinal increase in sleep duration and a decrease in PA, short sleep-active PA, and long sleep-moderate PA were associated with an increased risk of fatal all-stroke.

Sleep duration and fatal stroke

This study revealed that compared with those with a sleep duration of 7 h, middle-aged and elderly individuals with sleep durations of ≤ 5 and ≥ 9 h in the GBCS but not the NHANES had an increased risk of fatal all-stroke. This difference is similar to that of short sleep durations for all-stroke (≤ 6 h) [32, 33] and ischemic stroke (≤ 5 h) [34] patients. However, no associations were observed between short sleep duration and ischemic stroke [26, 35, 36] or hemorrhagic stroke mortality [26]. Short sleep durations increased not only fatal all-stroke but also ischemic stroke in the GBCS, possibly because of the inclusion of more newly fatal stroke cases. However, a duration of sleep ≤ 6 h may reduce the incidence of ischemic stroke; this is because the study included people of different age groups, the duration of sleep was approximately 5 h, and other factors related to napping, sleep quality, etc., were not included.

A long sleep duration increases the risk of stroke [37, 38], and the association between sleep duration and stroke varies by sex and age [39]. Here, we report that longer sleep duration is more likely to increase the risk of fatal all-stroke and fatal ischemic stroke among middle-aged and elderly people. Similarly, elderly postmenopausal women with long sleep durations have an increased risk of ischemic stroke [40]. However, a sleep duration of ≥ 9 h was not associated with the occurrence of ischemic stroke or hemorrhagic stroke in the NHANES. These differences may be due to the study model and an insufficient sample size.

We also observed that increased longitudinal sleep duration was associated with a significantly increased risk of fatal all-stroke and fatal ischemic stroke in middle-aged and elderly individuals; short sleep also led to an increase in blood pressure. Long or short sleep durations can increase the risk of hypertension, diabetes, and obesity [41–45].

Sleeping too long or too short is associated with oxidative stress and inflammatory effects, which may in turn disrupt vascular homeostasis and circulatory embolism, ultimately leading to the occurrence of cerebrovascular diseases. Maintaining a reasonable sleep duration and stable sleep habits can effectively reduce the risk of fatal stroke occurrence.

Physical activity and fatal stroke

No protective effect of active PA on all fatal strokes was found in the middle-aged and elderly population from the GBCS but rather from the NHANES, which is consistent with previous results of ischemic stroke and hemorrhagic stroke [7] and the incidence rate of stroke [46]. However, only weak- and moderate-intensity PAs have a protective effect on ischemia [47] and stroke [12]. We also investigated longitudinal changes in PA from baseline to the 1 st follow-up, and the results are similar to those in which individuals whose PA decreased from moderate or inactive levels had an increased risk of mortality from stroke [11].

A meta-analysis revealed that regular moderate- to high-intensity aerobic exercise can reduce blood pressure in hypertension patients. PA is conducive to reducing weight and maintaining normal weight, increasing the sensitivity of the body to insulin to increase the amount and activity of glucose transport in muscle and adipose tissue [48]. Regular PA can effectively prevent type 2 diabetes [49]. Aerobic PA and resistance exercise are most effective at improving ectopic fat deposition and physical and metabolic function in obese elderly individuals. PAs can protect against stroke by increasing these risk factors [50].

Insufficient or inadequate physical activity is clearly associated with a high incidence of comorbidities in older individuals, which are clear risk factors for stroke. Therefore, maintaining a reasonable amount of physical activity and stable physical activity habits can effectively reduce the risk of fatal stroke occurrence.

Sleep duration combined with physical activity and fatal stroke

Abnormal sleep and exercise combinations in GBCSs and inadequate exercise and sleep combinations in the NHANES are associated with the risk of fatal stroke, which indicates that there are differences in race, lifestyle habits, and cardiovascular disease risk across populations. Those with short sleep durations plus inactive PA had a greater risk of fatal all-stroke than did those with normal sleep durations plus standard PA in the NHANES. Similar results are shown in the UK Biobank [19]. We also reported that short sleep durations plus active PA were associated with a greater risk of fatal all-stroke and fatal ischemic stroke. Similar findings have also been reported [24]. These findings indicate that irregular sleep time and PA increase the risk of stroke events. Excessive PA may worsen sleep and potentially increase cardiovascular health risks [51, 52], and a stable and less fragmented circadian rhythm is associated with a lower incidence of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. The amplitude, duration, and regularity of sleep–wake and rest-activity patterns may be important targets for reducing cardiovascular risk [53]. However, a small-scale analysis revealed an association between mild PA and fatigue as well as between moderate to vigorous PA and sleep efficiency among stroke survivors, indicating that appropriate prevention methods and interventions targeting PA and sleep can reduce stroke risk [18]. However, PA did not affect fatal all-stroke in the middle-aged or elderly population through sleep, and sleep did not affect the occurrence of fatal all-stroke through the variable of PA in either the GBCS or the NHANES.

In summary, the balance and stability of circadian rhythms (daytime physical activity and night-time sleep duration) can effectively reduce the risk of fatal stroke.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we obtained only death information via a record linkage with the GZCDC and the NHANES, wherein only the death outcome was obviously weakened because of a lack of other clinical outcomes of stroke events. Second, our results are not statistically robust for the confidence intervals because several hazard ratios are very close to the significant unit; for example, smoking and alcohol consumption sources differ between Chinese cohorts and other sources [54, 55], which may be due to the coverage area and occupational participants, and thus, the results for the overall population are not clear. We also did not clearly define the effect of white matter alterations associated with metabolic syndrome in our analyses because a cluster of silent vascular risk factors may influence cerebrovascular pathology among aging participants [56]; these factors need to be given close attention to cohort criteria and verification in future cohort studies. Third, the different information from the GBCS and NHANES, such as representative of the participants in the GBCS and fewer cases recorded in the NHANES, led to analytic diversity and subsequently reduced the reliability of our results. We will only match more suitable different ethnic cohorts in subsequent works. Fourth, a mediation analysis here is ill fitting; the mediation analyses are calculated once the number of cases increases in the following study. Finally, the longitudinal data sources from the starting point and follow-up are lengthy, and other analyses, such as linear mixed models and group-based trajectory models, should be employed in the future.

Conclusion

Abnormal sleep duration and longitudinal changes in sleep duration and physical inactivity are associated with an increased risk of fatal stroke in middle-aged to older individuals; abnormal sleep and substandard PA may be potential indicators of future fatal stroke occurrence in relatively healthy middle-aged to older individuals. Thus, the dynamic balance between sleep duration and physical activity is the survival wisdom of humanity and one of the crucial safeguards for individuals to avoid disease risk and maintain long-lasting vitality.
