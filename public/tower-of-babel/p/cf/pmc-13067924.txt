Design and Implementation of an Innovative Health Equity Curriculum
Authors: Melicia Escobar, Heather M. Bradford, Katie DePalma, Signey Olson, Kathryn Atkin, Jenna Benyounes, Lauren Arrington, Ebony Marcelle, Noelene K. Jeffers, Joel Michael Reynolds, Julia Perry, Christina X. Marea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealth professional students must be trained to understand and address the structural and interpersonal drivers of health care inequities for diverse populations, particularly those who have experienced systematic oppression and discrimination. The medical, nursing, and midwifery care models have often neglected, mistreated, or outright exploited marginalized communities, particularly people who are Black, Indigenous, Latinx, disabled, larger bodied, economically oppressed, and gender diverse. Health professional students will emerge as clinicians within a health care system and society characterized by deep inequities in health care experiences. Students must have the competence to identify and respond to individual, family, and community health needs in the context of inequities. At one nurse‐midwifery and women's health nurse practitioner program, the faculty designed and implemented an innovative health equity curriculum. The curriculum prepares future clinicians to provide socially and clinically appropriate care to all populations; identify structural and systemic barriers to health and wellness; seek to mitigate the harmful effects of bias, discrimination, and inequity in their care; and grow in self‐awareness of their own role in mitigating bias and health inequities. This article describes the development and implementation of the curriculum, associated challenges and opportunities, and our vision for evaluation and expansion.

INTRODUCTION

Health care equity requires the creation of and commitment to conditions that promote optimal health for all people. At a time when the concepts of diversity, equity, and accessibility are being threatened, 1 health care professions programs must remain responsive to evidence‐based professional mandates, uphold their core professional values, and educate students to provide the care patients need to flourish.

The American College of Nurse‐Midwives (ACNM) 2 and the American Association of Colleges of Nursing (AACN) 3 affirm that health care professionals must understand health care equity, be prepared to address health care inequities, and provide patient‐centered and inclusive clinical health care. Both ACNM and the National Association of Nurse Practitioners in Women's Health call for a comprehensive approach to sexual and reproductive health care that addresses intersecting oppressions and is guided by a reproductive justice framework. 4 , 5 Care provided using a trauma‐informed approach—one that recognizes widespread effects of trauma—can counter pervasive “deficit” narratives that may arise in discussions of health care inequities by focusing on patients’ strengths to foster safety, trust, autonomy, and collaboration in the context of social and systemic oppression. 6 To this end, in 2016, nurse‐midwifery (NM) and women's health nurse practitioner (WHNP) faculty designed and implemented an innovative health equity curriculum (“the curriculum” moving forward) in a hybrid online learning environment as an upstream intervention to prepare all students in the program with the necessary frameworks and competencies to meet these professional mandates.

Health equity is grounded in the premise that all people are equally valuable and seeks to recognize and rectify historical and ongoing injustices. 7 Structural determinants of health care, the seen and unseen rules and mental modes that perpetuate hierarchies of power and privilege across race, ethnicity, gender, economic status, body size, ability, and sexual identity, impact health and health care access. 8 Bias and discrimination can occur across intersectional identities, which exacerbates and perpetuates harmful systems that prevent people from seeking or accessing necessary health care. 9 Left unrecognized and unaddressed, structural determinants negatively influence health care quality and safety and result in subtle or overt discrimination. 10 The resulting inequities among patients and communities, specifically among historically and presently oppressed groups, are the result of human behavior and system design; thus, they can be addressed and remedied through the same. Furthermore, when biased care becomes normalized for individual health care professionals and within the system, moral distress begins to erode health care professionals’ empathy and contributes to burnout. 11 Previous studies have described the benefits to health care professionals when they find meaningful connection and joy through high‐quality, culturally responsive, and structurally competent care as a means of mitigating professional stressors and burnout. 12 , 13 Attention to understanding ways to mitigate moral distress contributes to self‐care and self‐preservation and is a professional responsibility in seeking to provide quality health care and improve patient outcomes.

DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION OF THE HEALTH EQUITY CURRICULUM

Three underlying factors were critical to the design and implementation of the curriculum: (1) centering the clinical and educational needs of students, (2) the ability to hire and compensate faculty experts, and (3) support from program leadership.

Centering Student Needs

Centering the clinical and educational needs of students was instrumental in developing and implementing the curriculum. Students of the health care profession often struggle to apply a patient‐centered approach to health care that mitigates inequities within the current health care system. This may be because of a knowledge deficit, a real or perceived lack of time and resources, personally held biases, the existing culture in a given unit, or discomfort or a sense of powerlessness to resist the paradigm, given their vulnerability as students. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Health professional education curricula have taught cultural sensitivity or competency, which focus on the awareness of an “other” and often preclude attention to interpersonal interactions and structural factors that perpetuate inequitable health care environments. 18 Reframing curricula to promote self‐awareness of potential biases, increase understanding of health care equity concepts (ie, structural competency and intersectionality), and apply patient‐centered strategies in the clinical setting is an upstream intervention that addresses health care equity in the health care system and empowers future clinicians. 14 , 19 , 20 , 21

As the second‐largest US NM and WHNP program, with graduate students in 49 states, our faculty has a comprehensive perspective on students’ diverse learning needs. In 2016, faculty designed the curriculum based on the needs identified through course and clinical site evaluations as well as debriefing sessions held both inside and outside the classroom. Although students often found it difficult to convey, they reported unsettling experiences in their clinical rotations, such as instances of racism and bias directed toward their patients from oppressed groups and, sometimes, themselves. Additionally, and perhaps most importantly, although students felt prepared to engage in clinical skills, they felt inadequately prepared to manage these situations.

Hiring Faculty

The first iteration of the curriculum was led primarily by adjunct faculty with lived experience related to the content, who were reflective of their own positionality—or how their identities have been shaped by proximity to or distance from power—and were skilled facilitators capable of navigating complex, and sometimes highly charged, conversations. 22 At the time of the curriculum design, the full‐time faculty lacked these essential elements to develop and distribute the content with accuracy and authenticity; however, there was enthusiastic support to hire adjunct faculty with content expertise. Responding to this hiring need, program leadership reevaluated processes for adjunct hiring, prioritizing curricular needs over historical, inequitable informal network hiring practices. 23 School leadership also reevaluated faculty hiring processes to promote equitable hiring practices and retention, resulting in a more full‐time faculty with robust health care equity expertise. 24 As a whole, the faculty collectively committed to engaging in individual, educational, community, and systemic action steps that seek to address internalized biases and promote health care equity in order to mitigate and disrupt discriminatory conduct within the learning environment.

Support From Leadership

School leadership support for this curricular innovation was strong because of its alignment with both the university and the school of nursing's mission, vision, and values. Beyond verbal affirmation, additional funds and resources were secured to support the design and implementation of the curriculum, including hiring a faculty member with the required expertise, expanding the curriculum, and securing internal grants to further develop the content.

Curriculum Content and Structure

The curriculum aims to (1) develop structural competence and resilience to identify and address the root causes of health care inequities, (2) improve the quality of patient care and improve health care outcomes, and (3) promote joy, meaning, and self‐preservation among our graduates. Brave Spaces

Faculty endeavor to create “brave” (rather than “safe”) spaces where students are encouraged to center the experience of oppressed groups and reflect on their past experiences and understanding of the topic. 26 Brave spaces can foster generative discussion, encourage critical reflection, and enable modeling that prepares students for the clinical setting. Safe spaces are not typically appropriate for diverse groups with varying levels of privilege and marginalization, because they can center presumed fragility and result in protecting those who cause harm from accepting responsibility or being accountable for harm. Students are never asked or encouraged to disclose personal experiences, although faculty support them if they do. Given the significant prevalence of bias, it is assumed that many students will have had personal experiences with one or more forms of bias. Thus, through the use of brave spaces and established community norms (Table 1), faculty seek to limit retraumatization during the discussions and promote shared learning.

Foundational Concepts

Among the introductory sections, the faculty selected topical areas for the sessions based on insights from students and faculty regarding bias in the clinical setting. The program's curriculum begins with a series of sessions during the students’ first on‐campus intensive, which occurs before their first clinical rotation. Each session reinforces foundational concepts of structural competency, intersectionality, and patient‐centeredness (Table 2), which are scaffolded throughout the specialty courses to promote knowledge uptake, critical reflection, and accountable behaviors that can be carried into clinical learning environments and clinical practice. When applicable, intersections between topics are cross‐referenced. Faculty also present foundational terminology related to health care equity to establish a shared vocabulary that can enable robust discussion among students with varying levels of familiarity with the concepts.

Teaching Strategies

The preclinical timing of the health care equity content in the curriculum and the selected teaching strategies were intentionally designed to mitigate moral distress related to being ill‐equipped to respond to inequities and bias, which are associated with a decline in empathy in the clinical setting. 12 In addition to building a shared topical vocabulary, students are building one for naming factors that contribute to moral distress. For example, through facilitated group dialogue, nonhomogeneous groups of students identify key terms and shared definitions. Through guided case studies or role‐play, students apply this new, shared vocabulary to clinical cases in a low stakes learning environment. Through structured reflection, students consider and reframe their personal experiences and beliefs in the context of the new vocabulary introduced in each session. Throughout this learning process, together, students begin to understand that moral distress is structurally driven rather than individually dependent. By clarifying and strengthening the school and professional values that may ground their practice as learners and clinicians, students build resilience to challenges that arise in the clinical setting.

Identification of Topical Sessions

The opening session anchors the series within the broader curriculum, connecting it to our school's Jesuit mission and values as well as the values of our professional associations. It is also structured to build a shared sense of purpose (ie, providing health care and improving outcomes) and community (ie, all students choosing midwifery or women's health and our school) and to establish a reservoir of positive regard and mutuality to sustain potential disagreement. Faculty introduce students to critical reflection as a concept and practice to support their exploration of their own values and their ability to tolerate discomfort both internally and interpersonally. Faculty review the school's values, which are rooted in Jesuit values (Table 3), with particular attention to Cura Personalis (care for the whole person) and Cura Communitas (care for the community). These concepts ground us in a commitment to recognize the intersectional complexity of the individual and a shared responsibility to one another as part of a community of scholars and humans.

Curriculum Implementation

The health equity curriculum was implemented in 2016 as a required component of the NM/WHNP and WHNP programs’ coursework during students’ first on‐campus objective clinical intensive (OCI). The 3‐day OCI format intermixed sessions on clinical hand skills with community‐building among faculty and students. Students were provided with preparatory materials for all OCI sessions (eg, brief readings, audiovisual resources, and tools). At the beginning of the OCI, faculty emphasized that skills promoting an understanding of health care equity and patient‐centered communication are as essential to patient care as clinical hand skills. Sessions were 60 to 90 minutes each and used a variety of teaching strategies (eg, brief lectures, facilitated group discussions, guided dialogue, reflection, role‐play, case studies, and debriefing).

As a standard component of OCI, students were required to submit a brief reflection at the conclusion of each health care equity and hand‐skill session. The reflection could address new concepts learned, identified challenges, or goals for growth and development. Students were encouraged to share the OCI session topics with their clinical preceptors to promote greater awareness of their new knowledge and skill development.

Ongoing, comprehensive evaluations of the sessions were conducted. Students completed an anonymous evaluation at the close of each OCI. OCI faculty engaged in daily debriefing. The OCI coordinator and program leadership also regularly reviewed all evaluations and other informal feedback from students and faculty. Iterative curricular improvements were made as needed in collaboration with faculty experts.

These health care equity concepts are scaffolded throughout the curriculum in subsequent virtual and on‐campus OCIs and didactic courses. In addition to the teaching strategies used in the initial sessions, faculty use assigned readings, clinical debriefing, objective structured clinical experiences, and guided case studies to reinforce the content.

CHALLENGES AND OPPORTUNITIES

Implementing the curriculum had several challenges, including faculty hiring and development, clinical education, and curricular issues, which also presented opportunities for improvement. Addressing these challenges proactively may mitigate harm to students within the curriculum and increase a sense of belonging.

Faculty Hiring and Development

Hiring of adjunct faculty to develop and lead the sessions provided our team with the flexibility to rapidly hire experts with diverse lived experiences. However, heavy reliance on adjunct faculty is itself an equity issue because adjunct positions are typically short term and contract based, lacking the same protections and benefits as full‐time positions. Thus, they are undercompensated, which is common among those from oppressed groups. In addition, midwifery and nursing educators are largely a homogenous group of primarily women who are White and cisgender. 27 Therefore, to sustain and further revise curricula, programs must adopt hiring practices that attract and retain a diverse workforce with both lived and clinical expertise, seek external consultation when needed, and commit to a culture of belonging where all members of the learning community can thrive.

Health equity expertise and self‐awareness among all faculty varied. Critical reflection and ongoing professional development must occur among educators to effectively incorporate these concepts into formal and informal curricula and avoid harming students from oppressed groups. 13 Faculty with limited expertise in the health care equity domains lack confidence in their ability to effectively teach the content and are wary of causing harm either through White fragility, “saying the wrong thing,” or ineffectively managing classroom discussions. An essential element of successful curriculum distribution is assuring that the teaching does not disproportionately fall to faculty members from oppressed groups. Prioritizing and adequately compensating faculty development and mentorship through co‐teaching is a critical component to increasing faculty confidence and competence. A goal is that all faculty have similar levels of competence and confidence to engage in conversations around all topical areas. Achieving this goal is more complex for adjunct faculty with full‐time clinical positions, who are unable to make time for faculty development. Faculty knowledge in the health care equity domain could be bolstered through faculty competencies and course evaluations.

Clinical Education

Students’ ability to retain the content without guaranteed practice opportunities is a challenge. Without regular reinforcement in the didactic and clinical settings, students may lose comfort in applying newly acquired knowledge and skills and revert to social and cultural norms. Clinical learning in settings where foundational health care equity knowledge is absent can also limit a student's ability and confidence to implement the information. Additionally, in confronting their own privilege and unexamined biases, students may inadvertently cause harm during their verbal processing. To address these challenges, faculty aim to organize student affinity groups by lived experiences, which may provide additional mechanisms for student peer support.

In response to preceptors’ requests to advance their own health care equity acumen, faculty obtained funding in 2025 to offer the health care equity sessions to staff and clinicians at 4 Washington, DC–area perinatal practices (3 federally qualified care centers and 1 tertiary care hospital practice). Evaluation of their knowledge uptake and intention to change practice is underway. A programmatic long‐term goal is to offer the sessions at no cost to all clinical preceptors, accompanied by continuing education credits.

Curriculum

The curriculum topical areas are not exhaustive, and faculty have identified the need for additional topics, including respectful care of patients with substance use disorder, immigrants and refugees, and veterans. The curriculum's timing poses a challenge in relation to other courses. Because the curriculum was implemented by faculty within the NM and WHNP programs, students’ first exposure to the content occurs midway through the program, after the core graduate nursing courses (eg, pathophysiology, pharmacology, physical assessment). Ideally, the health care equity concepts should be threaded into earlier courses to promote application to the core curricula and deeper scaffolding. This would also expand the curriculum to other advanced practice nursing programs (ie, family nurse practitioner and acute care adult gerontology). Through ongoing advocacy efforts within the school, traction is building to make this change. 25 In addition, comprehensive course mapping is underway to promote consistent implementation. Since the curriculum's implementation in 2016, revised core competencies for ACNM and AACN reflect many health care equity concepts (Supporting Information: Appendix S1), reaffirming the curriculum's essential contributions to nursing and midwifery pedagogy.

Ultimately, the authors acknowledge the inherent vulnerability of students who encounter bias or discrimination in school or clinical settings. 28 Faculty must be accountable to students when harm occurs during curriculum engagement. Consequently, program leadership developed policies and processes that enable students to come forward anonymously about experiences of bias or discrimination without fear of reprisal, which were later adopted at the school level.

CONCLUSION

Recognizing the need to strategically weave health care equity content throughout their courses, faculty designed and implemented an innovative health equity curriculum to increase students’ knowledge and fundamental skills, promote patient‐centered care, and advance health care equity. The theoretical framework and values‐based approach support students’ ability to critically assess how oppression can negatively influence health care distribution and outcomes, particularly for the most vulnerable patients, and examine these concepts in a brave and supportive learning environment.
