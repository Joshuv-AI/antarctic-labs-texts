Immuno-metabolic diseases and therapeutics: molecular mechanisms via inflammasome signaling
Authors: Joo-Hui Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Inflammatory responses serve as essential defense mechanisms in living organisms, but persistent or excessive activation can contribute to the development of chronic metabolic diseases. A central regulator of such inflammation is the inflammasome, a cytosolic multiprotein complex that senses pathogenic or stress-related signals and triggers the maturation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18). While inflammasome-induced pyroptosis, a form of lytic cell death, can play protective roles in pathogen clearance, excessive or dysregulated activation is more commonly associated with chronic inflammation and tissue damage. Increasing evidence points to the involvement of inflammasomes, especially the NLRP3 inflammasome, in the pathogenesis of immune-metabolic diseases that characterized by the interplay between immune dysfunction and metabolic imbalance, including obesity, diabetes, atherosclerosis, and sarcopenia. In these conditions, aberrant inflammasome activity contributes to insulin resistance, lipid dysregulation, muscle wasting, and vascular injury through sustained cytokine release and immune cell recruitment. Recent studies have advanced our understanding of how inflammasome signaling is integrated into the molecular landscape of metabolic disease, offering new insights into disease mechanisms and highlighting inflammasomes as viable therapeutic targets. This review provides an updated overview of inflammasome biology, defines their role in four representative immune-metabolic diseases, and discusses recent progress in targeting inflammasome pathways as a strategy to mitigate chronic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction.

Introduction

Inflammation initiates an immune response by activating various defense mechanisms [1, 2]. However, dysfunction in the anti-inflammatory system can result in prolonged antigen exposure, disrupting metabolic homeostasis and contributing to the onset of chronic diseases [3, 4]. This impairment reduces the body’s ability to effectively counter external threats and has been linked to the increasing global prevalence of conditions such as obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases [5–8]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of inflammatory signal transduction pathways may facilitate the prevention, management, and treatment of metabolic diseases.

The innate immune system detects a wide range of pathogenic stimuli—including viruses, bacteria, parasites, and toxins—as well as non-infectious stressors such as oxidative stress, ionic imbalances, tissue injury, and trauma [9, 10]. This recognition is mediated by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby initiating and amplifying the immune response [11–15].

PRRs of the innate immune system are classified into five major types based on protein domain homology: toll-like receptors (TLRs), nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptors (NLRs), retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)-like receptors (RLRs), C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), and absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2)-like receptors (ALRs) [16, 17].

Among these, inflammasome protein complexes are assembled primarily by NLRs, ALRs, or pyrins, which activate caspase-1 either directly or through the adaptor protein ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain) [18–20]. During the priming stage, TLR ligands-mediated stimulation triggers the activation of the NF-κB signaling cascade, leading to the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 in the nucleus [21]. In the subsequent activation stage, external stimuli—including microbial toxins, environmental particles, and crystalline substances—induce the assembly of inflammasome complexes such as NLRP3, resulting in caspase-1 activation [22]. Activated caspase-1 then cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their mature forms, initiating their secretion and leading to an inflammatory form of programmed cell death known as pyroptosis [23–26]. This dual signaling—comprising both pyroptotic cell death and cytokine release—amplifies inflammatory responses and contributes to the pathophysiology of metabolic diseases.

Given this context, understanding how excessive cytokine signaling drives the progression of metabolic diseases is critical for developing effective strategies to prevent and treat cytokine-mediated pathogenesis. This review highlights the current understanding of PRRs in the context of metabolic diseases, including diabetes, obesity, atherosclerosis, and sarcopenia.

Mechanism of PRRs activation

Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) are pivotal for the innate immune system, recognizing pathogens and initiating appropriate immune responses [27, 28]. PRRs are classified into five families: TLRs, NLRs, RLRs, CLRs, and ALRs [29, 30].

The post-receptor signaling pathways of TLRs are closely linked to the activation and nuclear translocation of NF-κB (Fig. 1A). This process plays a central role in initiating and amplifying innate immune responses against pathogenic threats by promoting the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and other immune-related genes [31]. Upon ligand recognition, TLRs recruit specific adaptor proteins to their intracellular Toll/Interleukin-1 receptor/Resistance protein (TIR) homology domains, primarily myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88) or TIR domain-containing adaptor-inducing interferon-β (TRIF), depending on the involved TLR subtypes [32–34]. MyD88 is used by most TLRs (except TLR3) and leads to the rapid activation of NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), resulting in the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. In contrast, TRIF is primarily used by TLR3 and TLR4 and activates a distinct signaling pathway [35]. TRIF-dependent signaling leads to the activation of interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and, subsequently, the production of type I interferons (e.g., IFN-β), which are critical for antiviral defense. This pathway involves TNF receptor-associated factor 3, TANK-binding kinase 1, and IκB kinase epsilon (IKKε), which phosphorylate and activate IRF3. Additionally, TRIF can also engage TRAF6 and RIP1, leading to a delayed activation of NF-κB and MAPKs, contributing to inflammatory cytokine production [36]. NLRs such as NLRP1, NLRP3, and NLRC4 are inflammasome sensors that trigger the assembly of caspase-1, leading to the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 by detecting microbial and immune signals [37–39] (Fig. 1A). NLRs consist of three domains: the central NACHT domain, C-terminal LRR, and N-terminal interaction domain, which contains the caspase recruitment domain (CARD) and pyrin domain (PYD) [27, 40]. The NACHT domain facilitates ATPase, essential for oligomerization, and activation of NLRP protein, as well as maintaining stability during the formation of the inflammasome. On the other hand, the N-terminal PYD domain functions as a platform, enabling binding of the PYD domain of ASC protein, ultimately leading to interaction of CARD-CARD and activation of innate immune signaling [41]. Among the NLR family members, NLRP3 is one of the most well-characterized, with its activation mechanism extensively studied due to its involvement in a broad spectrum of inflammatory diseases. These conditions are often mediated by stimuli such as extracellular ATP, pore-forming toxins, heme, particulate matter, and pathogen-associated RNA [42]. Both PAMPs and DAMPs are known to trigger NLRP3 inflammasome activation, which subsequently leads to the activation of downstream signaling components such as caspase-1 [12]. Activated caspase-1 cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD), separating its N- and C-terminal domains, resulting in the formation of pores in the cell membrane [43, 44]. These pores facilitate the secretion of IL-1β, IL-18, and other pro-inflammatory cytokines [45, 46].

CLRs are membrane-bound receptors with short intracellular signaling tails that promote phagocytosis through interactions with Fc receptors on the cell surface [47, 48] (Fig. 1A). Most CLRs contain carbohydrate recognition domains (CRDs), which bind various carbohydrate ligands (e.g., β-glucan) and some non-carbohydrate ligands (e.g., lipids, proteins) in a calcium-dependent manner [49]. Among the best-studied CLRs in infection recognition are Dectin-1, Dectin-2, and Mincle. Dectin-1 binds β-glucans, whereas Dectin-2 is involved in the recognition of cytokines such as TNF and IL-12 [50, 51]. Mincle, which lacks its own intracellular signaling motifs, relies on the Fc receptor common γ chain (FcRγ) to signal and recognizes α-mannose structures [52, 53]. Upon activation, CLRs stimulate the adaptor molecule caspase-associated treatment domain adaptor 9 (CARD9), spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk, combined with CARD9 to transmit signals), and NF-κB, leading to inflammatory responses and cytokine production for host defense [54–56].

RLRs, including RIG-I, melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5), and laboratory of genetics and physiology 2 (LGP2), are cytosolic RNA sensors involved in antiviral innate immunity [57–59] (Fig. 1B). RLRs serve as cytoplasmic receptors for inflammation and innate immunity [60]. RIG-I detects short double-stranded RNAs with 5′-triphosphate ends, while MDA5 recognizes long dsRNAs [58]. Upon binding viral RNA, RIG-I and MDA5 undergo conformational changes, exposing CARD domains that oligomerize and translocate to mitochondria, where they interact with mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) [61]. LGP2, lacking CARDs, acts as a positive regulator of RIG-I and MDA5, requiring RNA binding and ATP hydrolysis for its function [62–64]. MAVS recruits TNF receptor-related factor (TRAF) family proteins (e.g., TRAF2, 3, 5, and 6), promoting K63-linked ubiquitination and activation of kinases such as IKKα, IKKβ, IKKε, and TBK1 [62, 65]. This leads to the activation and nuclear translocation of NF-κB and interferon regulatory factors (IRF3, IRF7), resulting in the production of type I interferons and pro-inflammatory cytokines [63, 64].

ALRs, such as AIM2 and interferon-gamma-inducible 16 (IFI16), are cytoplasmic or nuclear receptors composed of a C-terminal HIN domain that binds double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and an N-terminal PYD involved in protein interactions [66, 67] (Fig. 1C). AIM2 recognizes cytosolic dsDNA—typically 80–300 base pairs—and forms inflammasomes by recruiting ASC and activating caspase-1, which in turn promotes IL-1β and IL-18 secretion [29, 68]. IFI16, primarily localized in the nucleus, signals through a stimulator of interferon genes (STING)-dependent pathways [69, 70]. Unlike NLRs, AIM2 directly binds dsDNA via its HIN domain during infection [19, 71].

In resting conditions, AIM2 remains auto-inhibited by intramolecular interaction between its PYD and HIN domains [72]. Upon sensing pathogenic dsDNA, this inhibition is relieved, enabling oligomerization, ASC recruitment, and inflammasome assembly, ultimately activating caspase-1 and triggering inflammatory cytokine release [73, 74]. The electrostatic interaction between the HIN domain and dsDNA facilitates this process by increasing local AIM2PYD concentration, enhancing downstream signaling [19, 75].

As previously described, inflammasome assembly is central to innate immune responses, facilitating the secretion of key cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 [76, 77]. Understanding the mechanisms underlying inflammasome activation offers valuable insights into therapeutic strategies for metabolic diseases driven by dysregulated inflammation. Role of inflammasome in obesity

Obesity is a chronic, low-grade inflammatory condition associated with an increased risk of diseases such as type 2 diabetes (T2D), cardiovascular disease, and cancers [78–80]. Inflammasomes play a central role in the inflammation linked to obesity [81, 82]. In obese individuals, inflammasomes such as NLRP3, AIM2, NLRP1, and NLRC4 are activated. NLRP3 activation is triggered by various obesity-related signals, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and lysosomal rupture [39, 83, 84]. AIM2 responds to cytosolic double-stranded DNA released from dying adipocytes, which are abundant in obesity [85]. NLRP1 detects bacterial toxins and activates the inflammasome in adipose tissue, leading to the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines [86].

Inflammasome activation is implicated in the pathogenesis of obesity, as it plays a key role in regulating adipose tissue inflammation [87]. Upon activation, these inflammasomes recruit and activate the adaptor protein ASC and the effector protein caspase-1, leading to the processing and secretion of the pro-inflammatory cytokines. These cytokines contribute to the development of insulin resistance, liver steatosis, and endothelial dysfunction, thereby linking inflammasome activation to various chronic diseases, including obesity, T2D and atherosclerosis [88, 89]. Thus, targeting inflammasome sensors in the context of obesity may offer a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating obesity-induced inflammation and its associated comorbidities.

Adiponectin is an adipokine primarily secreted by adipose tissue and plays a crucial role in regulating insulin sensitivity. Its deficiency is associated with acute inflammatory conditions, including chronic obesity [90]. A recent study demonstrated that inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway ameliorates palmitic acid- and high-fat diet (HFD)-induced damage, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target for obesity [91]. In a murine podocyte clone 5 (MPC5) cell model of palmitic acid-induced obesity-related glomerulopathy, treatment with both adiponectin and MCC950 (a selective NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitor) significantly reduced palmitic acid-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and the expression of thioredoxin interacting protein (TXNIP), apoptosis- and inflammation-related proteins, including NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1, IL-1β, IL-18, and phosphorylated NF-κB, compared to the untreated group (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, decreased adiponectin expression and increased NLRP3 expression were observed in an HFD-fed C57BL/6J mouse model. Treatment with globular adiponectin reversed these changes, enhancing adiponectin expression and reducing NLRP3 expression relative to the HFD-fed group. These findings suggest that adiponectin may serve as a promising therapeutic target for obesity and inflammation through inhibition of the ROS/NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway.

Previous studies have demonstrated a reduction in expression levels of Meteorin-like, Glial Cell Differentiation Regulator (METRNL) in the adipose tissues of obese mice [92, 93]. METRNL expression is upregulated in adipose tissue in response to stimuli such as exercise, cold exposure, and muscle contraction. In a treadmill-based exercise intervention study in obese mice, exercise intensity was gradually increased over 8 weeks (from 12 m/min for 20 min/day to 20 m/min for 50 min/day, 5 days/week), which effectively alleviated HFD-induced inflammation and metabolic disturbances. Elevated METRNL levels were shown to improve glucose tolerance and induce the expression of thermogenesis-related genes (Ucp-1, Dio2, Pgc-1a, and Err-a), as well as anti-inflammatory cytokines, in both subcutaneous and epididymal white adipose tissue [94, 95] (Fig. 2B). A recent study investigating the relationship between inflammasomes and obesity found that METRNL partially suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines through activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways [96]. Additionally, levels of transforming growth factor (TGF)-β1 and collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1) were significantly reduced in both subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissues. Moreover, pretreatment with METRNL directly suppressed IL-1β secretion in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammatory bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) [97, 98]. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID)-activated gene-1 (NAG-1), also known as growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF15), is a protein that plays a significant role in obesity-related diseases, including hepatic steatosis [99–101]. The effects of NAG-1/GDF15 overexpression on hepatic steatosis and its underlying mechanisms were investigated in a previous study [71]. The results indicated that NAG-1/GDF15 overexpression prevented the development of hepatic steatosis in HFD-fed mice. This suggests that NAG-1/GDF15 inhibits the release of double-stranded DNA triggered by oxidative stress—a key activator of the AIM2 inflammasome—and downregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines, both of which contribute to the development of hepatic steatosis (Fig. 2C). However, other studies have highlighted a more complex and sometimes opposing role of AIM2 in obesity and insulin resistance [102]. Specifically, AIM2 deficiency was shown to promote inflammation and adipogenesis in white adipose tissue, thereby contributing to obesity and insulin resistance [103]. In AIM2-knockout (Aim2⁻/⁻) mice, the interferon-inducible gene Ifi202b, which encodes protein 202 (p202), was upregulated, along with increased inflammatory signaling in gonadal white adipose tissue (gWAT). Conversely, AIM2 overexpression in a mouse model of obesity was associated with reduced adiposity and improved glucose homeostasis. The seemingly paradoxical roles of AIM2 in metabolic regulation reflect its context-dependent functions within distinct tissue microenvironments and disease stages. These contrasting effects underscore the spatiotemporal complexity of AIM2 signaling and highlight the importance of context-specific targeting in therapeutic interventions for obesity-related diseases.

Obesity is a signific ant risk factor for the development of various cancers [104, 105]. The relationship between obesity and breast cancer incidence has been explored, revealing a correlation between breast cancer and NLRC4 inflammasome activation in mouse models [106]. Specifically, activation of the NLRC4 inflammasome in adipose tissue was shown to stimulate the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which in turn promote breast cancer development in obese mice via increased expression of vascular endothelial growth factor A (Vegfa) and subsequent angiogenesis. Notably, inhibition of NLRC4 inflammasome signaling was found to reduce both the incidence and metastasis of breast cancer in obese mice. These findings establish a mechanistic link between obesity and breast cancer and suggest that targeting the NLRC4 inflammasome may offer a novel therapeutic strategy for the prevention and treatment of breast cancer in obese individuals.

In summary, inflammasomes such as NLRP3 and AIM2 are key mediators linking metabolic stress in obesity to chronic inflammation and tissue dysfunction. By sensing signals like oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and cytosolic DNA, these inflammasomes contribute to insulin resistance, hepatic steatosis, and renal injury. Regulatory factors like adiponectin, METRNL, and NAG-1/GDF15 attenuate this process by suppressing inflammasome activation. Given the tissue-specific and context-dependent roles of inflammasomes, targeted modulation offers a promising therapeutic strategy for obesity-related metabolic diseases.

Role of inflammasome in diabetes

Diabetes arises from a combination of insulin resistance, elevated blood glucose levels, and dysregulated lipid metabolism. Increasing evidence also implicates inflammasomes in the pathogenesis of diabetes and its associated complications [107], with the NLRP3 inflammasome playing a particularly prominent role [108]. Experimental studies have shown that activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome contributes to pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, heightened insulin resistance, increased oxidative stress, and elevated secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators in diabetic mouse models [109, 110]. These findings underscore the critical role of NLRP3 inflammasome activation in the development and progression of diabetes and point to the importance of further research aimed at developing therapeutic strategies to inhibit its activity.

Diabetes can lead to progressive kidney damage due to persistent hyperglycemia [111, 112]. Hyperglycemia directly induces podocyte injury and structural alterations in the renal vasculature, including thickening of the tubular basement membrane, loss of endothelial fenestrations, and expansion of the mesangial matrix. These changes impair glomerular function and disrupt interstitial substance exchange [111, 113]. Among these pathological mechanisms, NLRP3 inflammasome activation has emerged as a key contributor to podocyte damage in diabetic nephropathy [114]. Recent studies have demonstrated that inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome with MCC950 alleviates podocyte injury in diabetic nephropathy [115] (Fig. 3). Diabetic mice treated with MCC950 showed histological improvements in podocyte structure. Podocyte function was further evaluated by assessing the expression of collagen IV, a key extracellular matrix protein, which was significantly upregulated following MCC950 treatment. Mitochondrial dysfunction associated with insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes (T2D) promotes the overproduction of ROS under hyperglycemic conditions. Excess ROS can damage mitochondrial macromolecules, leading to reduced transcription of mitochondrial RNA (mtRNA), impaired protein synthesis, diminished mitochondrial function, and the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the extracellular space [116, 117].

Circulating cell-free mitochondrial DNA (cf-mtDNA) acts as a danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), triggering inflammatory responses through the activation of inflammasomes such as NLRP3, and pro-inflammatory signaling proteins including TNF-α and NF-κB [12, 118]. Notably, recent research has shown that cf-mtDNA also contributes to chronic inflammation via activation of the AIM2 inflammasome in patients with T2D [119]. In this study, plasma samples from T2D patients and healthy controls were analyzed using real-time PCR. Results revealed significantly elevated levels of cf-mtDNA in the T2D group, along with increased expression of AIM2 and inflammatory markers. These findings highlight the role of circulating cf-mtDNA in driving chronic inflammation through AIM2 inflammasome activation in T2D.

Interestingly, the pathological role of NLRP3 inflammasome activation extends to other inflammatory conditions such as acute pancreatitis. Cathepsin B (CTSB), released from lysosomes into the cytoplasm under conditions of cellular stress, exacerbates acute pancreatic inflammation by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and promoting caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis [120]. In a cerulein-induced acute pancreatitis mouse model, pretreatment with the CTSB inhibitor CA-074Me attenuated pancreatic injury and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, indicating the therapeutic potential of targeting CTSB in inflammatory pancreatic diseases. This cross-disease relevance of NLRP3 inflammasome activation highlights its central role in inflammation-related pathologies.

Efforts to suppress autoimmune T cell responses, particularly those involving Th1, Tc1, and Th17 cells within peripheral lymph nodes (PLNs), remain a significant challenge in type 1 diabetes (T1D) research [121, 122]. However, recent findings suggest that NLRP1 may play a critical role in regulating pathogenic Th17 cells. Studies have shown that reduced expression or loss of function of NLRP1 leads to an increase in Th17 cell populations in both human and mouse samples [123]. Nlrp1−/− models exhibited upregulation of genes associated with Th17 differentiation, including RAR-related orphan receptor gamma t (RORγt, Th17-defining transcription factor) and IL-17 A [124, 125]. These results suggest that NLRP1 acts as a negative regulator of Th17 cell differentiation and function, thereby playing an immunomodulatory role in autoimmune diabetes.

Recent studies have underscored the involvement of the NLRC4 inflammasome in the pathogenesis of diabetic nephropathy. Increased NLRC4 expression and activation have been observed in renal tissues—particularly in tubular epithelial cells—in both clinical samples from patients and experimental mouse models of diabetic nephropathy [126]. This upregulation has been closely associated with impaired mitophagy and elevated mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) production. Moreover, in diabetic mouse models of myocardial infarction, mitophagy dysfunction has been shown to activate multiple inflammasomes, including NLRC4, AIM2, and caspase-1, likely mediated by the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) [127]. Although the precise mechanisms linking high glucose to NLRC4 activation remain to be fully elucidated, current data suggest an indirect regulatory pathway involving mitochondrial damage, mitophagic impairment, and increased intracellular mtDNA and mtROS exposure.

Furthermore, a recent study revealed a link between NLRC4 inflammasome activity and the circular RNA circ_0000181 in patients with diabetic nephropathy [128]. This circular RNA was found to interact with miR-667-5p, which suppresses NLRC4 expression. Dysregulation of the circ_0000181/miR-667-5p/NLRC4 axis promoted pyroptosis, a form of programmed cell death, suggesting a novel mechanism contributing to kidney injury in diabetes. These findings expand our understanding of the complex interplay between inflammasome activation, pyroptosis, and the progression of diabetic nephropathy.

In summary, inflammasomes—especially NLRP3, AIM2, NLRP1, and NLRC4—play pivotal and multifaceted roles in the pathogenesis of diabetes and its complications. In T2D, metabolic stress and mitochondrial dysfunction trigger NLRP3 and AIM2 activation, driving chronic inflammation, β-cell dysfunction, and insulin resistance. In diabetic nephropathy, inflammasome-induced pyroptosis contributes to podocyte and tubular injury, with recent findings implicating novel regulators such as mitochondrial DNA and circular RNAs. In T1D, NLRP1 can suppress T cell–driven inflammation. These multifaceted roles underscore the dual nature of inflammasomes as both mediators and modulators of disease, and point to the necessity for context-specific, cell-type–targeted therapeutic approaches in diabetes management.

Role of inflammasome in atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis is a chronic cardiovascular disease characterized by inflammation and lipid accumulation within the vascular wall [129]. It leads to narrowing and stiffening of the arteries, reducing blood flow and contributing to various cardiovascular complications [130]. The pathogenesis of atherosclerosis is multifactorial, involving elements such as low-density lipoprotein (LDL), ROS, and infections by certain DNA and RNA viruses [131]. Among these, inflammation is a central mechanism. Endothelial injury triggers the recruitment of inflammatory cells and platelets to the vascular wall, where cytokines and chemokines released by these immune cells promote further infiltration, lipid deposition, and the formation of atherosclerotic plaques [132, 133].

The NLRP3 inflammasome plays a pivotal role in the development and progression of atherosclerosis [134]. Its activation by oxidized LDL (ox-LDL)-stimulated monocytes contributes to the phenotypic switching of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), which is critical in plaque formation (Fig. 4). This process involves the downregulation of octamer-binding transcription factor 4 (Oct-4) mRNA, essential for maintaining the contractile phenotype of VSMCs [135, 136], and upregulation of Krüppel-like factor 4 (Klf4), a key factor in VSMC plasticity and inflammation [134, 136]. Collectively, these changes promote vascular wall remodeling and inflammation. Forkhead box protein P1 (FOXP1) is a critical regulator of cardiovascular function, modulating vascular homeostasis and immune-inflammatory responses through its effects on monocytes and macrophages [137, 138]. In a recent study using LDL receptor-deficient (Ldlr−/−) mice, endothelial-specific deletion of FOXP1 resulted in heightened NLRP3 inflammasome activation and increased IL-1β secretion, thereby accelerating atherosclerosis progression [139]. In contrast, FOXP1 overexpression attenuated inflammasome activity and reduced atherosclerotic burden. Mechanistic analyses identified FOXP1 as a direct downstream target of the atheroprotective transcription factor KLF2, establishing a KLF2–FOXP1–NLRP3 regulatory axis that limits vascular inflammation and plaque formation [140, 141]. These findings highlight this axis as a promising therapeutic target for atherosclerosis.

Clonal hematopoiesis, a condition involving somatic mutations in hematopoietic stem cells that lead to the expansion of specific leukocyte lineages, has also been linked to increased atherosclerosis risk [142]. Recent evidence suggests that the AIM2 inflammasome mediates this relationship [143]. In Ldlr−/− mice models with induced clonal hematopoiesis, AIM2 activation promoted IL-1β production and accelerated atherosclerotic plaque development. Mice lacking AIM2 (Aim2−/−) exhibited reduced atherosclerotic lesion size, attenuated inflammatory responses, and altered immune cell composition within the plaques.; These results highlight AIM2 as a crucial mediator in the intersection of clonal hematopoiesis and atherosclerosis, offering a promising therapeutic target for cardiovascular disease prevention.

Additionally, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as important regulators of inflammasome-mediated vascular inflammation [144]. NEXN-AS1, a lncRNA, has been shown to exert anti-atherosclerotic effects by enhancing the expression of its target gene NEXN and inhibiting pyroptosis, a form of inflammatory cell death [145]. In human endothelial and VSMCs stimulated with LPS, overexpression of NEXN-AS1 significantly reduced the expression of pyroptosis markers—NLRP3, caspase-1, GSDMD, IL-1β, and IL-18—while suppressing activation of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway. Conversely, silencing NEXN-AS1 reversed these effects, confirming its role as a negative regulator of inflammasome activation. These findings point to the NEXN-AS1/NEXN axis as a novel target for the treatment of atherosclerosis through the suppression of pyroptosis and vascular inflammation.

In summary, inflammasomes—particularly NLRP3 and AIM2—serve as key drivers of the chronic inflammation underlying atherosclerosis. NLRP3 fosters endothelial dysfunction, vascular smooth muscle cell plasticity, and plaque formation, counterbalanced by regulatory axes such as KLF2–FOXP1. AIM2 connects clonal hematopoiesis to atherogenesis by amplifying IL-1β signaling and immune activation within lesions. Together, these findings position inflammasomes as central hubs in atherosclerotic progression and promising targets for precision cardiovascular therapies.

Role of inflammasome in sarcopenia

Sarcopenia is a well-recognized condition characterized by the progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, commonly associated with aging, physical inactivity, nutritional deficiencies, and chronic diseases [146]. In recent years, growing attention has been directed toward the role of inflammation in the development of sarcopenia. In particular, activation of the inflammasome has been shown to exacerbate systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, establishing a significant link between sarcopenia and metabolic diseases [147]. The interplay among muscle loss, chronic inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction is complex and multifactorial, prompting increasing interest in uncovering the underlying molecular mechanisms and identifying novel therapeutic strategies.

Sarcopenia is also closely linked to metabolic diseases, particularly T2D and obesity [148]. The loss of muscle mass and strength disrupts glucose metabolism and promotes insulin resistance, given the essential role of skeletal muscle in glucose uptake and utilization [149–151]. As discussed earlier, chronic inflammation observed in metabolic conditions like diabetes and atherosclerosis can further exacerbate sarcopenic progression.

Recent studies have proposed a direct association between sarcopenia and the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome within skeletal muscle tissue [152]. In particular, NLRP3 activation has been shown to induce morphological and metabolic alterations in myotubes. Experimental models demonstrated that LPS stimulation upregulated key inflammasome-related genes—Nlrp3, Casp1, and Il1b— suggesting an amplified inflammatory response. This activation was associated with reduced myotube diameter and growth, indicating impaired muscle development. Furthermore, inflammasome activation led to decreased protein synthesis and mitochondrial dysfunction, partly driven by mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS), which collectively contribute to muscle atrophy.

Supporting these findings, a clinical study involving elderly patients undergoing hip surgery revealed elevated expression levels of NLRP3, ASC, NIMA-related kinase 7, GSDMD, and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6 in patients with reduced physical independence [153]. Increased caspase-1 activity and macrophage infiltration were also observed, alongside upregulation of markers associated with muscle regeneration (embryonic myosin; MYH3/MYH2), cellular senescence (senescence-associated protein; p16INK4a), and tissue damage (ATP). These results strongly implicate NLRP3-mediated inflammation in the degeneration of muscle tissue and decline of physical function in sarcopenic individuals.

To address this inflammatory component, recent investigations have explored the therapeutic potential of bone morphogenetic protein-7 (BMP-7) in mitigating diabetes-induced pyroptosis and muscle remodeling [154]. BMP-7, a member of the TGF-β superfamily, possesses anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory properties and has been shown to suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production in monocytes [155]. In a diabetic mouse model, BMP-7 treatment effectively attenuated inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis and systemic inflammation. Moreover, BMP-7 improved muscle integrity and prevented maladaptive remodeling by inhibiting transcription of high-mobility group box-1 (HMGB1) and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [156]. These findings suggest that BMP-7 holds promise as a potential therapeutic agent for combating inflammation-driven sarcopenia, particularly in the context of diabetes.

In summary, NLRP3 inflammasome activation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of sarcopenia by driving chronic inflammation, impairing mitochondrial function, and disrupting protein synthesis in skeletal muscle. Elevated inflammasome activity contributes to muscle fiber atrophy, reduced regenerative capacity, and functional decline—especially in aging and metabolically compromised individuals. The intersection of sarcopenia, T2D, and systemic inflammation underscores skeletal muscle as both a target and amplifier of metabolic dysfunction. Emerging therapies such as BMP-7 that attenuate inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis and preserve muscle integrity represent promising strategies for preventing or treating inflammation-driven sarcopenia.

Among the most extensively studied agents is MCC950, a selective small-molecule inhibitor that blocks NLRP3 oligomerization and the recruitment of ASC, thereby preventing caspase-1 activation and subsequent IL-1β and IL-18 release [162–164]. Preclinical models have demonstrated its efficacy in ameliorating insulin resistance, attenuating atherosclerotic plaque formation [165], and preserving skeletal muscle integrity [166]. However, despite its promise, the clinical development of MCC950 was discontinued after Phase 1b trials due to hepatotoxicity concerns [167].

Other NLRP3-specific inhibitors have shown similar mechanistic promise. CY-09 targets the ATPase activity of NLRP3 by binding to its NACHT domain and has demonstrated efficacy in models of gout and metabolic syndrome [168]. Other inhibitors, such as D359-0396, have been found to suppress IL-1β release and pyroptosis by blocking the NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD pathway [169]. Likewise, Tranilast, a known anti-allergic agent, suppresses inflammasome activation and IL-1β release in preclinical models of cardiovascular fibrosis and inflammation [170].

A complementary therapeutic strategy involves modulation of upstream regulatory mechanisms. Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), a nutritional omega-3 fatty acid, reduces NLRP3 activation via GPR120 signaling [171], while Bay 11–7082 exerts dual inhibitory effects on NF-κB and NLRP3 ATPase activity [172]. Furthermore, 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI) has garnered interest for its ability to alkylate critical cysteine residues on NLRP3 and concurrently activate Nrf2 antioxidant signaling, underscoring the interplay between redox homeostasis and inflammasome inhibition [173].

Mitochondrial regulation has also emerged as a key axis of inflammasome control. Agents such as UK5099, a mitochondrial pyruvate carrier inhibitor, attenuate NLRP3 activation indirectly by reducing mtROS production [174]. In parallel, melatonin has been shown to suppress NLRP3 through enhancement of mitophagy and mitochondrial integrity, highlighting the relevance of cellular metabolism in inflammasome signaling [175].

Beyond synthetic compounds, natural products and phytochemicals have shown considerable potential as low-toxicity modulators of inflammasome activity. Curcumin [176–178], rosmarinic acid [179, 180], aucuabin [181], and green propolis extracts [182] inhibit NLRP3 by targeting ROS generation, NF-κB signaling, and ER stress pathways. Notably, melatonin has demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in models of sarcopenia, reducing inflammasome-associated cytokines and preserving muscle function in aged tissue, thereby representing a viable candidate for age-related interventions [183].

Recent interest has also extended to non-NLRP3 inflammasomes, such as AIM2, particularly in DNA-driven inflammatory diseases. 4-sulfonic calixarenes exemplify this broader scope, as they inhibit both NLRP3 and AIM2 inflammasomes by interfering with cytosolic DNA binding, expanding the therapeutic landscape to ischemic stroke, cancer, and autoimmune conditions [184].

Despite these advances, several challenges remain for clinical translation. Inflammasomes are integral to host defense, and their systemic suppression raises concerns about immunosuppression and increased vulnerability to infection and malignancy. Furthermore, many candidate compounds lack sufficient data on bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, and tissue specificity. Off-target effects and the redundancy of inflammasome-related signaling pathways further complicate drug development.

Future research should prioritize the development of inflammasome subtype-specific inhibitors with improved selectivity and safety profiles. The implementation of immune cell–targeted delivery systems and the identification of robust biomarkers for inflammasome activity will be critical for therapeutic precision. Additionally, combinatory regimens incorporating metabolic or immunomodulatory agents may offer synergistic benefits and enhance the efficacy of inflammasome-targeted therapies in complex metabolic diseases.

Concluding remarks

In recent years, remarkable progress has been made in elucidating the intricate relationship between chronic inflammatory diseases and inflammasome signaling. Conditions such as obesity, diabetes, atherosclerosis, and sarcopenia are now widely recognized as states of chronic low-grade inflammation, with compelling evidence pointing to the inflammasome—particularly NLRP3—as a central mediator of this pathological process. When dysregulated, inflammasome activation drives the excessive release of proinflammatory cytokines, contributing to systemic inflammation and, in severe cases, a hyperinflammatory state akin to a “cytokine storm,” as observed in infections like COVID-19 [185].

This review offers a comprehensive synthesis of the current literature linking inflammasome activity to the pathophysiology of major chronic diseases. For instance, NLRP3 inflammasome activation is upregulated in the adipose tissue of individuals with obesity, coinciding with elevated IL-1β and IL-18 levels and systemic metabolic dysfunction. Similarly, in diabetes, aberrant inflammasome activation disrupts insulin signaling and glucose homeostasis, while in atherosclerosis, enhanced inflammasome signaling in endothelial cells contributes to vascular inflammation and plaque development.

Although NLRP3 remains the most extensively characterized inflammasome, emerging data implicate other inflammasome complexes—including AIM2, NLRC4, and NLRP1—in chronic disease pathogenesis. Furthermore, the human genome encodes a diverse array of NLRs, with 22 known members spanning the NLRA (CIITA), NLRB (NAIP), NLRC (e.g., NOD1, NOD2, NLRC3–5, NLRX1), and NLRP (NLRP1–14) families [24, 37, 41]. Yet, the functional roles and disease associations of many of these receptors remain largely undefined, highlighting the urgent need for systematic functional and mechanistic investigations.

Targeting inflammasomes as a therapeutic strategy for chronic inflammatory diseases presents both opportunities and challenges. It demands precise, disease-specific approaches informed by a deep understanding of inflammasome biology, tissue-specific expression, and regulatory networks. Future research must focus on delineating the molecular mechanisms that govern inflammasome activation and identifying actionable therapeutic nodes. This includes the development of highly selective small-molecule inhibitors, metabolic modulators, and immunomodulatory strategies that can effectively suppress pathological inflammasome signaling while preserving host defense.

Moreover, incorporating insights from genomics, cell-type–specific signaling, and inflammasome-associated biomarkers will be essential for advancing personalized therapeutic interventions. As our understanding of inflammasomes continues to evolve, so too will the potential to intercept chronic inflammation at its molecular roots.

In summary, inflammasomes represent a crucial link between immune dysregulation and metabolic pathology. They hold significant promise not only as biomarkers of disease progression but also as actionable targets for the next generation of anti-inflammatory therapies. Continued research in this field is poised to redefine therapeutic paradigms for a wide spectrum of chronic inflammatory diseases.
