Endoscopic submucosal dissection–assisted double-layer running suturing technique for closure of a gastrocutaneous fistula
Authors: Steven Steinway, Joshua Anicetti, Daryl Ramai, Pichamol Jirapinyo, Christopher C Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsGastrocutaneous fistulas are rare and challenging adverse events that can have considerably high morbidity and mortality rates. They result from an abnormal connection between the stomach and the skin. They can be caused by various factors, including infection, trauma, anastomotic leaks, and removal of percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tubes. Endoscopic management of chronic gastrocutaneous fistulas is associated with modest success rates, with reported closure rates of approximately 50%. Effective intervention is necessary to prevent adverse events. We present an endoscopic approach for closing a chronic gastrocutaneous fistula resistant to typical therapy.MethodsAn 85-year-old female patient with a complex history including a previous hiatal hernia repair presented with persistent leakage from a gastrocutaneous fistula after removal of a PEG tube that had been in place for 5 years. Two prior standard endoscopic closure attempts were unsuccessful. We used an advanced endoscopic technique involving mucosal dissection, argon plasma coagulation, and a 2-layer running suture with clip placement.ResultsImmediate endoscopic evaluation after the procedure confirmed complete closure without adverse events. At the 3-month follow-up, clinical assessment demonstrated successful closure with resolution of local symptoms.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates the feasibility of an endoscopic submucosal dissection–assisted, double-layer endoscopic suturing approach for the closure of a chronic gastrocutaneous fistula refractory to conventional endoscopic therapies.

Introduction

Gastrocutaneous fistulas are defined as connections between the stomach and the skin. They are rare adverse events but quite difficult to treat from a surgical and endoscopic standpoint.1 They have various potential causes, including intestinal injury, chronic infections, leakage from an anastomosis, and in some cases, removal of a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube.2 Diagnosis is easily achieved in cases with active leakage and patency of the fistula. However, with advancements in technology, CT with fistulography is commonly used to visualize the tract.3

Gastrocutaneous fistulas have a spontaneous closure rate of 6%, and mortality can reach up to 35% in patients with recent gastric surgery. Thus, optimal treatment is important to prevent adverse events.1 Management can be surgical or endoscopic, each with its own set of advantages and disadvantages. Surgical approaches tend to be reserved for fistulas that are large in size and include open laparotomy with direct dissection of the fistula.4 Endoscopic management includes placement of clips and/or sutures and is typically reserved for smaller fistulas, with reported closure rates of approximately 50% in chronic cases.1

We report a case of an 85-year-old female patient with persistent leakage after PEG tube removal and 2 previous failed closure attempts.

Case presentation

This case is about an 85-year-old female patient with a complex history, including coronary artery disease, Barrett's esophagus, and a prior incarcerated hernia. She presented with persistent leakage from a gastrocutaneous fistula after PEG tube removal (Fig. 1). The PEG tube was initially placed in 2019 after a laparoscopic hiatal hernia repair to anchor the stomach to the abdominal wall. It was then removed in early 2024 after being in place for almost 5 years. After removal, she experienced continuous leakage that worsened with oral intake and movement. She initially complained of skin irritation around the fistula opening.

The patient underwent 2 prior endoscopic closure attempts before referral. Her first endoscopic approach used tract abrasion with a cytology brush, argon plasma coagulation (APC), and running suturing but failed to achieve complete closure. A second attempt using endoscopically guided percutaneous suturing with miniature forceps, as described by Garcia et al,5 was also unsuccessful with recurrent leakage. Given these failures, a third endoscopic approach was pursued. Finally, the patient underwent an approach that involved mucosal dissection, APC, and a double-layer running suture closure with clip reinforcement.

Technique

We adapted a technique commonly used for gastrojejunal anastomotic revision—endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) combined with transoral outlet reduction (ESD-TORe)—to address the fistula closure. Although transoral outlet reduction is typically used to reduce the diameter of the gastrojejunal anastomosis in postbypass patients with weight regain, we applied the framework to this chronic fistula. A key modification was the use of a double-layer linear running suture pattern rather than a traditional purse-string approach to fuse mucosa in 2 layers with intervening submucosal apposition.

ESD was performed by circumferential submucosal injection followed by mucosal incision and dissection to expose healthy submucosa around the fistulous tract, with minor bleeding controlled by soft coagulation (Video 1, available online at www.videogie.org, Fig. 1B and C). Both the inner and outer margins of the dissection zone were ablated using forced APC (70 W, 0.8 L/min) to promote tissue healing (Fig. 1D).

A double-channel endoscope (Fujifilm Medical Systems USA, Lexington, Mass, USA) equipped with an over-the-scope full-thickness endoscopic suturing system (Boston Scientific Corporation, Marlborough, Mass, USA) was then used to place the first running suture, with the objective of achieving submucosa-to-submucosa apposition. Four full-thickness bites were placed using a single continuous suture to create a foundational seal. The bites were made from the 10-o’clock to 2-o’clock positions and 8-o’clock to 4-o’clock positions (Fig. 2A and B). This initial closure achieved submucosal apposition and served as the foundational seal for the fistula.

A second running suture, also using a single continuous suture, was placed to incorporate the surrounding mucosa and reinforce the closure. The bites were made at the 11-o’clock to 1-o’clock, 10-o’clock to 2-o’clock, 8-o’clock to 4-o’clock, and 7-o’clock to 5-o’clock positions (Fig. 2C and D). This was followed by the placement of 4 tissue-closure clips as adjunctive reinforcement (Fig. 2E). This layer incorporated surrounding healthy mucosa to distribute tension and enhance mechanical stability. Finally, 4 tissue-closure clips were deployed across the closure site for additional reinforcement and enhancement of mechanical stability during healing. The closure remained intact under insufflation, with no immediate adverse events. The full procedural steps are demonstrated in Video 1.

Follow-up

Endoscopic inspection after the procedure confirmed closure, and no adverse events were observed immediately. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient reported a significant reduction in leakage and no local pain or skin irritation. Ongoing clinical follow-up at 9 months confirmed durable closure without recurrence or signs of local infection.

Conclusions

This case describes the application of ESD-assisted, double-layer endoscopic suturing for the closure of a complex gastrocutaneous fistula after failed standard endoscopic therapies. By integrating a double-layer running suture into an ESD procedure, closure was achieved in a case resistant to conventional therapies. This article describes a single-case experience and therefore has limitations. The durability of this approach cannot be assessed past the follow-up period, and broader applicability cannot be inferred from a single case. Larger studies are needed to better define the role of this technique for closing chronic refractory gastrocutaneous fistulas. This method could be considered for patients with chronic gastrocutaneous fistulas who have failed conventional endoscopic closure methods.
