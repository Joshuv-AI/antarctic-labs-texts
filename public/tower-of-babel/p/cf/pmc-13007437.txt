A Remarkable Tale: Increased Retinal Fluorescence Following Application of Permanent Marker Ink to Mouse Tails
Authors: Sierra Foshe, Sophia Shi, Brent A. Bell, Sarah Lee, Anjali Shankar, Kimberly Dine, Ying Song, Joseph Vithayathil, Ahmara G. Ross, Joshua L. Dunaief
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeUnusually high retinal fluorescence was observed during in vivo imaging among a few mice in our colony. Their tails had been marked with red Sharpie ink, a common practice for identification. They were observed licking the ink off their tails, suggesting possible oral intake of a fluorescent ink component that might accumulate in the retina. One component of red marker ink is the fluorophore Rhodamine 6G (R6G), suggesting that it might be responsible.MethodsWe tested this hypothesis by tail marking C57Bl/6J mice with one line or four lines of red ink. Retinal fluorescence was monitored in vivo with confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscope (cSLO) imaging at baseline and from 6 hours to 2 weeks after marking. Retinas and brains were sectioned for histology. A second cohort of mice received an oral gavage of R6G at the concentration estimated in red ink, and retinal histology was performed.ResultsTail marking with red ink led to a transient, dose-dependent increase in retinal fluorescence in cSLO images, peaking at 6 hours. Histology revealed that this fluorescence was predominantly in the photoreceptor outer segments. This fluorescence localization was reproduced with oral R6G alone. Fluorescence in the brain was concentrated in the choroid plexus and ventricles.ConclusionsThese results suggest that R6G can cross the gut–blood, blood–retina, and blood–cerebrospinal fluid barriers. This raises important technical considerations for experimental mouse identification and has potential applications for fluorescence imaging and central nervous system drug targeting.

Tail marking is a common laboratory practice1,2 that involves using a permanent marker to draw different numbers of marks along mice's tails for easy identification. Compared to alternatives such as ear punching, tail marking may be preferable because it induces less pain and anxiety.3 Despite the relative procedural safety, we observed that mice often engaged in tail licking behavior after marking, licking both their own and their cage mates’ tails. This raises potential concerns regarding the systemic absorption and ocular effects of permanent marker ink.

Permanent marker inks contain a mixture of solvents, colorants, and binding agents. One of the pigments in red marker ink is Rhodamine 6G (R6G),4 a cationic lipophilic fluorophore. R6G has a variety of research applications, including conjugation to secondary antibodies and as an intracellular mitochondrial label. In ophthalmology, R6G has been evaluated as a potential vital dye for intraocular surgery.5–7 It has also been used as a fluorescent indicator in preclinical studies of retinal therapies—for example, being loaded into nanoparticles or conjugated to liposomes.8–10 This study aimed to determine whether tail marking could, surprisingly, lead to uptake of a fluorescent dye into the retina and to discover the identity of the dye.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Ten- to 16-week-old C57BL/6J mice (strain number 000664) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). They were maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle and provided food and water ad libitum. A mix of males and females was used for each experimental group. All experiments adhered to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Pennsylvania.

Tail Marking

Mice were manually restrained, and their tails were marked with a fine tip permanent marker (Sharpie #30072, assorted colors; Newell Brands, Maryville, TN, USA). Each line was about 1 cm in length, wrapping around the circumference of the tail (Supplementary Fig. S1A). If a tail had multiple lines, they were spaced about 1 cm apart (Supplementary Fig. S1B). For histology experiments, mice were marked with four lines to maximize fluorescence. Assuming an average tail diameter of 0.1 to 0.5 cm and an ink thickness of 0.5 to 2 µm,11 a rough estimate of 0.5 µL ink was applied to a tail with four marks.

Confocal Scanning Laser Ophthalmoscope Imaging

Blue autofluorescent (BAF) and infrared autofluorescent (IRAF) confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscope (cSLO) imaging was performed as described previously.12 Briefly, mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of 95-mg/kg ketamine with 11-mg/kg xylazine. They received eyedrops of 1% tropicamide/2.5% phenylephrine and 0.5% tetracaine hydrochloride for dilation and local anesthesia, respectively. Images were captured with a SPECTRALIS HRA cSLO system (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). The BAF and IRAF channels have excitation peaks at 486 nm and 786 nm and emission ranges of 500 to 700 nm and >800 nm, respectively. The brightness of the cSLO images was quantified with ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA. Images were converted to 8-bit, and mean pixel intensity of the circular field was quantified using the built-in Measure function. Both eyes were averaged together for each mouse, and the values at each time point were normalized to baseline.

R6G Concentration Assay

A stock solution of R6G (83697-1G; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in ethanol was diluted to create a concentration gradient of standards. Ethanol alone was also used to determine the background signal. Ink from red markers was extracted by breaking the plastic marker casing and squeezing the dye from the internal reservoir into an Eppendorf tube. The ink was serially diluted in ethanol until its fluorescence intensity fell within the range of the R6G standards. The fluorescence intensity (excitation, 530 nm; emission, 550 nm) was measured in duplicate for the R6G standards and the diluted marker ink with an Infinite 200 PRO microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf Switzerland). For in vivo use, a 10× stock solution of R6G in ethanol was diluted to its working concentration in PBS. Mice received 50 µL of R6G solution by oral gavage.

Histology

Mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation at 6 hours after tail marking or 10 to 15 minutes after oral gavage. Eyes were enucleated with curved forceps. After decapitation, brains were dissected from the skull and flash-frozen in isopentane on dry ice. Tissue was embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound (Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA, USA) and stored at −80°C.

Tissue was sectioned with a Leica CM3050 cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany) into 10-µm (retina) or 20-µm (brain) sections. Slides were immediately imaged without washing to best detect fluorescence. Fluorescent images of the retina were captured with an Eclipse 80i microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and images of the brain were obtained with a ZEISS Axio Imager M2 (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The excitation peaks for the Cy2 and Cy3 channels are 495 and 554 to 555 nm respectively, and the emission peaks are 515 to 525 and 568 to 570 nm, respectively. After imaging the Cy2 and Cy3 channels, slides were washed with PBS and coverslipped with SouthernBiotech Dapi-Fluoromount-G Clear Mounting Media (OB010020; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to image the nuclei. These steps substantially reduced Cy3 fluorescence (Supplementary Fig. S2). ImageJ was used to adjust image brightness for improved visibility. Images from the same channel within each figure were captured using the same microscope settings and adjusted equally to maintain consistency across groups.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with Prism 10 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA). cSLO image quantification was analyzed by two-way ANOVA with Šidák's multiple comparisons test. The significance threshold was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Tail Marking Induces Fluorescence in Photoreceptor Outer Segments

Mice received one line of black or red marker ink around their tails (n = 6 black, 7 red), and were photographed to monitor the disappearance of the ink over time. Both colors faded gradually from 0 to 15 days, but the red markings were substantially more visible than the black ones at each time point (Fig. 1). Red ink was used for all subsequent tail marking experiments. Next, mice received one line or four lines of red marker ink around their tails (n = 3 per group). BAF and IRAF cSLO images were taken at 6 hours, 1 day, 1 week, and 2 weeks after marking and compared to baseline imaging (Figs. 2A, 2B). The one-line group exhibited a small but non-significant increase in BAF fluorescence at 6 to 24 hours, but retinal fluorescence in the four-line group peaked at 6 hours with an almost fivefold increase (Fig. 2C). Fluorescence returned almost to baseline by 2 weeks. There was no effect of tail marking on IRAF cSLO images (Fig. 2D).

To examine the localization of retinal fluorescence, mice received four lines of red marker ink on their tails, and eyes were harvested for histology 6 hours later. Control animals had some RPE autofluorescence in the Cy2 and Cy3 channels, as expected (Fig. 3). The marked group exhibited increased fluorescence throughout the retina/retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), predominantly in the photoreceptor outer segments (OSs). This signal was best observed in sections prior to washing (Supplementary Fig. S2). Fluorescence being higher in the BAF than IRAF and higher in Cy3 than Cy2 images is consistent with the excitation/emission range of R6G (530 nm/550 nm).

Tail Marking Induces Fluorescence in the Choroid Plexus and Ventricles

Given the specificity of the retinal labeling, we next investigated whether the dye accumulated in other regions of the central nervous system. We examined whether there was cell type- or region-specific fluorescence in the brain 6 hours after tail marking. Histology of longitudinal sections showed fluorescence localized to the choroid plexus, a highly vascularized area responsible for production of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), as well as the lining of the ventricles (Fig. 4). As in the retina, this fluorescence was detected in the Cy3 channel and was absent from control brains.

Oral R6G Reproduces the Retinal Labeling Pattern of Tail Marking

Fluorescence intensity measurements indicated that the concentration of R6G in red marker ink is 6.8 mg/mL (Fig. 5A). Mice were given 50 µL of 6.8 mg/mL R6G (or vehicle control) by oral gavage, and eyes were harvested 10 to 15 minutes later (n = 3 per group). Similar to tail marking, oral R6G induced fluorescent signal in the Cy3 channel localized to the photoreceptor OSs (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

Red ink persists longer on mouse tails compared to black. Although this would make red ink preferable for longer-term experiments, our results reveal both concerns and opportunities. Tail marking with red ink induced significant retinal fluorescence due to the fluorophore R6G accumulating in or around photoreceptor OSs. Although this finding identifies a potential confound for experimentalists, understanding the mechanism behind it could have broader applications.

Retinal fluorescence imaging is a useful tool for experimental and clinical ophthalmology; for example, fluorescein angiography involves the injection of fluorescent dye to examine the integrity of a patient's retinal vasculature. cSLO is used to localize RPE autofluorescence in vivo to monitor RPE degeneration. Immunohistochemistry uses fluorescence intensity as an indicator of protein levels and localization in tissue. Our data demonstrate that tail-marking mice in advance of retinal imaging may confound fluorescence measurements. On the other hand, the localization of R6G to OSs could be useful in the development of imaging techniques focused on photoreceptors.

Within cells, R6G can impair mitochondrial function.13–15 It has been shown to induce toxicity in RPE cells in vitro and retinal ganglion cells in vivo,5,7 underscoring the risk of confounding mouse retinal experiments through the use of tail marking. However, it is important to note that we have not identified a hazard for permanent markers in humans when used as instructed, nor do we provide evidence that accidental exposure or ingestion would damage the retina or vision.

The preference of R6G for photoreceptor OSs is striking. This could be due to its lipophilicity, adhering to the densely stacked membranes of the OS discs. In the brain, marker-induced labeling appears within the choroid plexus and around the ventricles. This may suggest that the dye has crossed into the CSF, although not necessarily the brain parenchyma.

In addition to being cell membrane permeable, R6G can be actively transported through organic cation transporters16,17 and the efflux pump P-glycoprotein.18,19 These transporters are expressed in RPE/choroid,20–22 as well as the choroid plexus,23,24 a potential mechanism to explain how R6G crosses the blood–retina and blood–CSF barriers. These transporters act on a variety of endogenous and exogenous molecules, and R6G could be used as an example substrate for drug screening.25,16 Identifying the specific transporters and cell types that drive R6G accumulation in the retina and CSF could have applications for the development of drugs that must cross these barriers.
