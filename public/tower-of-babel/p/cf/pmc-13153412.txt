MIrROR release 02: Expanded and refined 16S-ITS-23S rRNA operon dataset
Authors: Jisol Lee, Juyong Hong, Donghyeok Seol, Wonseok Lee, Junho Lee, Gyungbu Kim, Seoae Cho, Heebal Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the rapid advancements in genome sequencing technologies, microbial genome data has exponentially increased, making it essential to continuously update dataset for accurate microbial identification and classification. We present the development of Microbial Identification using rRNA Operon Region (MIrROR) release 02, an expanded dataset based on 1,690,470 genomes (1,674,514 bacterial and 15,956 archaeal) sourced from NCBI. The final curated dataset covers 476,579 sequences, 249,907 genomes, and 29,051 species, representing increases of 387.39%, 472.49%, and 206.28% over the previous release. Key updates include the addition of archaeal genomes and taxonomy reclassification based on GTDB R220. Extensive curation was performed, including filtering operon lengths (3,500–7,000 bp), removing duplicate sequences, eliminating sequences with ambiguous nucleotides, and clustering of sequences at 99% identity to remove redundancies. The updated dataset showed improved performance in microbial mock community analyses, supporting its accuracy and reliability. These improvements make MIrROR release 02 a valuable resource for microbial profiling and various microbiological research applications.Subject terms: Genetic databases, Bacterial genetics

Background & Summary

Since the early 2000s, microbial genomics has advanced rapidly with the development of next-generation sequencing technologies. Among these, short-read sequencing of hypervariable regions within the 16S rRNA gene has been widely adopted for profiling bacterial and archaeal communities due to its cost-effectiveness and high throughput1. However, this approach often lacks sufficient resolution to distinguish closely related species, limiting its utility in detailed taxonomic and phylogenetic analyses2,3. More recently, long-read sequencing platforms such as Oxford Nanopore and PacBio have addressed many of the limitations of short-read technologies, including difficulties in assembling repetitive regions and detecting structural variations4. Nevertheless, even full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing based on long-read platforms has limitations in achieving species-level resolution, due to the high sequence conservation of the gene across closely related taxa and intragenomic heterogeneity among multiple 16S rRNA gene copies5.

To overcome these challenges, researchers have increasingly turned to sequencing the full rRNA operon—including the 16S rRNA, internal transcribed spacer (ITS), and 23S rRNA regions—which provides higher species-level resolution and better reflects the genomic context. Carl Woese originally emphasized the use of conserved rRNA genes (16S, 23S, and 5S) based on their universality and molecular clock-like properties6, and modern long-read sequencing has now made it feasible to apply this approach comprehensively.

In response, we constructed the Microbial Identification using rRNA Operon Region (MIrROR) release 01 dataset in 2022, featuring an extended 16S-ITS-23S rRNA operon sequence (approximately 4.3 kb). This dataset, which comprises 97,781 sequences of the 16S-ITS-23S rRNA operon across 43,653 bacterial genomes covering 9,485 species, provides a more robust framework for microbial identification7. The extended sequence length offers more informative positions for capturing fine genetic differences between microbial species, thereby improving phylogenetic resolution and reliably distinguishing species sharing over 99% sequence identity in the 16S rRNA gene, such as Streptococcus mitis group8 or Escherichia coli and Shigella spp.9.

Following the MIrROR release 01, the number of bacterial genomes hosted by National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) quadrupled in just two years, significantly contributing to the expansion of the MIrROR dataset. Additionally, the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) was updated from release 89 to release 22010, playing a crucial role in enhancing the accuracy and reliability of microbial identification. Significant issues related to taxonomy validated by the International Committee on Systematics of Prokaryotes (ICSP) also exist. In 2021, the names of 42 prokaryotic phyla were officially published11, and in 2020, the ‘Lactobacillus’ genus was re-evaluated based on genomic data, resulting in the reclassification into 25 new genera and the proposal to merge the ‘Lactobacillaceae’ and ‘Leuconostocaceae’ families into ‘Lactobacillaceae’12. This evolving landscape of microbial genomics necessitated updates to the MIrROR dataset.

Here, we present the comprehensive restructuring and redevelopment of the MIrROR dataset and web interface, along with a discussion of its potential applications. As of August 10, 2023, a total of 1,674,514 bacterial genomes and 15,956 archaeal genomes were collected from NCBI GenBank, encompassing all microbial genomic data hosted by NCBI13. Among these, 298,267 genomes (295,897 bacterial genomes and 2,370 archaeal genomes) contained 16S-ITS-23S rRNA operon sequences with an operon length ranging from 3,500 to 7,000 bp. In total, 595,964 operon sequences (593,081 from bacteria and 2,883 from archaea) were extracted (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1).

The curation process followed these steps: (i) Reassigning taxonomy using the latest GTDB R220. (ii) Removing genomes that were not assigned to the species level in the reassigned taxonomy. (iii) Removing duplicate sequences within the same genome. (iv) Removing duplicate sequences across different species. (v) Removing sequences with more than five ambiguous nucleotides. (vi) Clustering sequences with 99% or higher identity and removing outlier species sequences within the clusters.

Unlike the previous release, MIrROR release 02 did not remove duplicate sequences within the same species. This approach was taken to avoid entirely excluding genomes when only duplicate sequences remain for a given genome. By retaining these duplicates, MIrROR release 02 more accurately reflects the natural variation and polymorphism that occur within microbial genomes, providing a valuable resource for studies on microbial evolution and environmental adaptation. Additionally, for users who require it, a lightweight release with duplicate sequences removed is also available on the website.

In the previous release, a stringent criterion was applied where any genome containing even a single ambiguous nucleotide was entirely removed. However, this led to the exclusion of important microbial species that were rarely found. To address this issue, MIrROR release 02 relaxed this criterion, removing only sequences with more than five ambiguous nucleotides14. This adjustment helps maintain genome quality while preserving the diversity of species included in the dataset to the greatest extent possible.

Through this curation process, a total of 119,385 sequences and 48,360 genomes were removed from the raw operon sequences. The final dataset contains 476,579 sequences, 249,907 genomes, and 29,051 species. This represents a 4.9-fold increase in the number of sequences, a 5.7-fold increase in the number of genomes, and a 3.1-fold increase in the number of species compared to MIrROR release 01 (Table 1, Supplementary Table 2).

One of the major changes compared to the previous release is the inclusion of archaeal sequences. The number of archaeal sequences was highest in the following order: Halobacteriota, Thermoproteota, Methanobacteriota, and Nanoarchaeota (Fig. 2B). MIrROR release 02 now includes a variety of archaeal species, such as Saccharolobus solfataricus15,16, a model organism crucial for protein and enzyme research, Methanobrevibacter smithii17, known for its role in methane production in the human gut, Methanobrevibacter oralis18, associated with oral diseases such as periodontitis, as well as Thermococcus kodakarensis and Halobacterium salinarum19,20, which are significant for industrial and biotechnological applications. The inclusion of these archaeal species allows MIrROR to provide precise data for species identification in a wide range of environmental samples, making it an important resource for diverse research fields and industries.

The taxonomy reclassification was performed using GTDB R220 (updated on April 24, 2023) (Table 2). This update enhances the accuracy of the dataset by reflecting the most current microbial taxonomy information. Out of 249,907 genomes, 125,706 (50.3%) were taxonomically reclassified in MIrROR release 02. Among them, 47,025 genomes were reassigned to entirely different species, while 78,681 genomes were further subdivided within their original species (Supplementary Table 3). Notably, GCA_019428815.1 and GCA_030697685.1, which were classified as bacteria by NCBI, were reclassified as archaea within the Nanoarchaeota phylum. This misclassification is likely due to the highly reduced, mosaic genomes characteristic of Nanoarchaeota21, highlighting the limitations of traditional 16S rRNA gene-based classification22.

Furthermore, several important species that were not included in the previous release have been newly added to MIrROR release 02. This expansion was made possible by incorporating recently deposited complete genome sequences and addressing significant taxonomic updates that were unavailable or misclassified in the previous dataset (GTDB R89). For example, a total of 18,958 bacterial species have been newly added in this release, including rare species such as Methylopumilus universalis, Polynucleobacter paneuropaeus, and Pseudoalteromonas haloplanktis; clinically significant pathogens such as Helicobacter pylori, Mycobacterium abscessus, Salmonella arizonae, and Campylobacter concheus; and industrially important bacteria such as Apilactobacillus kunkeei, Bacillus paranthracis, and Citrobacter gillenii (including species with updated names). Additionally, Clostridium perfringens and Shigella flexneri, which are associated with gut dysbiosis, have been reclassified as Sarcina perfringens and Escherichia coli, respectively. Pathogens such as Brucella abortus, Burkholderia pseudomallei, and Mycobacteroides abscessus have also been reclassified as Brucella melitensis, Burkholderia mallei, and Mycobacterium abscessus, and included in the dataset. This expansion and diversity allow the MIrROR dataset to make significant contributions not only to species profiling but also to pathogen identification, microbial diversity research, and studies on the relationship between microbes and diseases.

Selecting an appropriate primer set is essential for the simultaneous detection of bacteria and archaea in metagenomic samples. The universal primer set proposed in the previous release of MIrROR (16S-27F: 5′-AGRGTTYGATYHTGGCTCAG-3′ and 23S-2241R: 5′-ACCRCCCCAGTHRAACT-3′)7 covers a broad range of bacterial genomes and can process multiple samples simultaneously when integrated with Nanopore’s barcoding system. However, it has a limitation in its coverage of archaeal genomes. To address this, an in silico amplicon generation analysis was conducted using primer sets designed in previous studies to amplify the full-length rRNA operon (16S–ITS–23S), and assessed their coverage across bacterial and archaeal genomes. The bacterial amplicon generation rate was highest at 98.81% with primer set #5 (341F-2241R), and overall, all primer sets showed similar bacterial amplicon generation rates ranging from 96.54% to 98.81%. On the other hand, the archaeal amplicon generation rate was highest at 75.78% with primer set #4 (519F-2429R) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 4). As a result, to effectively detect both bacteria and archaea, it is recommended to use primer set #4 (519F-2428R: 5′-CAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′ and 5′-CCRAMCTGTCTCACGACG-3′) or #5 (341F-2241R: 5′-CCTACGGGNBGCWSCAG-3′ and 5′-ACCRCCCCAGTHRAACT-3′). These primer sets offer a practical balance between broad taxonomic coverage and compatibility with long-read sequencing workflows7,23,24.

The results of comparative analyses using commercial and simulated mock communities (see Technical Validation) demonstrate that MIrROR release 02 provides improved species-level resolution compared to previous release, underscoring the importance of taxonomic updates and curated dataset for accurate species-level classification. In this analysis, we applied more relaxed alignment thresholds when using 16S rRNA gene-based dataset such as SILVA, compared to operon-based datasets, but still showed a high proportion of ambiguous assignments. Although MIrROR release 02 includes archaeal genomes, its coverage of archaeal diversity remains limited. Expanding the dataset with complete and high-quality genomes from both bacterial and archaeal lineages is an important future task. In parallel, continued research is needed to identify archaeal-compatible primer sets suitable for operon-based long-read metagenomic approaches.

Finally, despite the substantial expansion of MIrROR release 02, it is imperative to acknowledge a biological phenomenon that may introduce potential bias in sequence-based analyses. Brewer et al. reported that unlinked rRNA genes are prevalent across bacteria and archaea, particularly in phyla such as Deinococcota and Planctomycetota25. Our previous investigations on this subject revealed enhanced symbiotic traits in bacterial genomes harboring unlinked rRNA operons, suggesting an evolutionary adaptation toward characteristics associated with organisms exhibiting a low dynamic range26. These observations indicate that the 16S–ITS–23S rRNA operon may not comprehensively capture the diversity of microbial communities. Furthermore, intragenomic variation within this operon region and variability in rRNA gene copy number per genome contribute to additional bias, collectively representing intrinsic limitations of operon-based community profiling. Accordingly, a thorough understanding of these limitations is essential for the accurate interpretation of relative abundance data derived from this approach.

Methods

Genome collection and rRNA operon extraction

As of August 10, 2023, a total of 1,674,514 bacterial genomes and 15,956 archaeal genomes were collected from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly), using ncbi-genome-download (v.0.3.1) (https://github.com/kblin/ncbi-genome-download). Genomes were downloaded in Genbank and FASTA formats. The rRNA locations within these genomes were predicted using Barrnap (v.0.9) (https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap), with a length cutoff set to 0.8. Based on the positions of 16S, 23S, and 5S rRNA genes identified in the generated genomic GFF files, operon sequences were extracted from the FASTA files only when 16S and 23S rRNA genes were linked, while unlinked cases were excluded. Operons with a length between 3,500 and 7,000 bp were retained through filtering, following the criteria established in MIrROR release 017. Subsequently, genomes containing identified operons were reclassified taxonomically using GTDB-Tk (v.2.4.0, GTDB Release 220) (https://ecogenomics.github.io/GTDBTk/). The ‘classify_wf’ workflow was implemented, based on whole-genome sequences. Sequence extraction, filtering, and taxonomic reclassification processes were automated using a custom python script (v.3.10.10, https://github.com/jisoll/MirrorMaker).

Dataset curation

To improve the accuracy and reliability of the rRNA operon dataset, the sequences were filtered and curated. Initial curation was performed using a custom python script (v.3.10.10, https://github.com/jisoll/MirrorMaker) and involved the following steps: (i) removing genomes that were not assigned to the species level in the reclassified taxonomy, (ii) if duplicate sequences were present within a single genome, only the sequence with the smallest genomic coordinate was retained, and the remainder were deleted, (iii) deleting sequences that were duplicates across different species, and (iv) removing sequences containing more than five ambiguous nucleotides. Subsequently, a curation based on 99% identity was performed27, and the initially curated sequences were clustered using MeShClust (v.3.0)28. Clusters containing sequences from two or more species were considered contaminated. The necessity for this rigorous manual curation stems from the likelihood that source genomes in public databases contain flawed sequence data, such as the inclusion of contaminant sequences during assembly or sequences from distantly related species due to low genome quality. These sequences were then mapped against the entire draft dataset using BLAST (v.2.15.0). Contaminated clusters were categorized into four groups based on genus and species counts: (i) a single genus with multiple species, (ii) two genera with two species, (iii) two genera with multiple species, and (iv) three or more genera. Sequence counts were used to identify the major and minor taxa within each cluster, and decision criterion was applied based on the BLAST results to remove contaminants. If the decision criterion was inconclusive, multiple sequence alignment was performed using Clustal-Omega (v.1.2.4)29, followed by phylogenetic analysis using IQ-TREE (v.2.0)30. An all-to-all BLAST analysis was conducted to verify whether genomes from other species appeared in the top 100 hits for each genome. If contaminated sequences were identified within a genome, the associated correct sequences were also excluded from further analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1).

In silico primer binding prediction

To assess the amplification efficiency of each primer set, an in silico primer binding analysis was performed using a custom python pipeline. Primer sequences were first expanded to account for IUPAC nucleotide codes. Each expanded primer was then searched against the curated rRNA operon sequences in the MIrROR release 02. Amplicon regions were extracted only when both forward and reverse primers were detected within the same sequence and met the defined mismatch threshold. The pipeline used for this analysis is publicly available at https://github.com/jisoll/MirrorPrimerChecker.

Technical validation using mock communities

Sequencing of commercial mock community standards

To evaluate the dataset using a mock community, we employed three standards: the ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard (catalog no. D6300), ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community DNA Standard (catalog no. D6305), and ZymoBIOMICS Gut Microbiome Standard (catalog no. D6331). We utilized the manufacturer’s whole-genome data for taxonomic reassignment of each strain.

DNA extraction was performed using zirconia and glass beads (0.1- and 0.5-mm diameter, Bio Spec Products Inc.) with vigorous vortexing for 5 minutes using an Allsheng Multi Tube Vortex Mixer (w/MTV-B01, MTV-B04, MTV-B06) at maximum speed (2,500 rpm)31. Following vortexing, the samples were centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 20 minutes to separate the supernatant. The supernatant was then transferred to a 2-mL tube containing 0.75 volumes of isopropanol, vortexed again, and applied to a DNA-binding column (Bioneer). DNA purity and concentration were quantified using a Synergy HTX multimode reader and Gen5 software (Biotek). A commercial DNA extraction kit was not used.

The bacterial rRNA operon was amplified using primers modified with ONT barcode sequences: 16S-27F-GGTGCTG-ONT barcode sequence, TTAACCTAGRGTTYGATYHTGGCTCAG, and 23S-2241R-GGTGCTG-ONT barcode sequence, TTAACCTACCRCCCCAGTHRAACT32,33. PCR amplification was carried out with over 1 ng of DNA template in a 50 µL reaction volume using BX-Tag polymerase (Bionics). The thermal cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 2 minutes, followed by 30 cycles of 10 seconds at 98 °C, 30 seconds at 50 °C, 150 seconds at 72 °C, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 minutes. The PCR amplicon was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis, cleaned with HM SPRIselect beads at a 0.45x ratio, and quantified using a Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA assay kit (Invitrogen).

Library preparation followed the ONT ligation sequencing kit protocol (SQK-LSK114, Nanopore). Barcoded DNA amplicons were pooled in equal molar amounts (100 to 200 fmol total) and repaired using the NEBNext Ultra II End Repair/dA-Tailing Module (NEB) at 20 °C for 30 minutes, 65 °C for 5 minutes, and 8 °C indefinitely. DNA purification was performed using AMPure XP beads in volumes equal to the end-repaired samples. The end-repaired sample was then ligated with a Nanopore adapter using the NEBNext Quick Ligation Module at room temperature for 10 minutes. DNA purification was again performed using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) after each enzymatic reaction according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The final library was loaded onto a flow cell (FLO-MIN114, R10.4.1) and sequenced for approximately 7.5 hours on a MinION MK1b using MinKNOW software (v.24.02.10). All experimental steps followed the procedures outlined in the Oxford Nanopore Technologies protocol (Ligation sequencing amplicons V14 (SQK-LSK114), ACDE_9163_v114_revL_29Jun2022).

Generation of simulated mock community reads

To assess the classification performance of MIrROR release 02 on archaeal genomes, an in silico mock community dataset was constructed using NanoSim (v.3.2.2)34. The community consisted of eight bacterial and two archaeal species. Genomes were selected from the Unified Human Gastrointestinal Genome (UHGG) catalogue35, specifically from isolated gut-derived genomes with ≥97% completeness and ≤2% contamination. Simulated long-read sequencing data were generated using the pre-trained model metagenome_ERR3152364_Even included in NanoSim (https://github.com/bcgsc/NanoSim). The theoretical abundance of each bacterial species was set to 12%, and each archaeal species to 2%. In silico amplicon sequences were extracted using primersearch (EMBOSS v.6.6.0.0)36 with the primer pair that showed the best overall performance in previous evaluations (Set 4: 519F–2428 R; 5′-CAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′ and 5′-CCRAMCTGTCTCACGACG-3′). The mismatchpercent parameter was set to 30.

Processing and analysis of mock community reads

For the commercial mock community data, raw signal files were first basecalled using Dorado (v.0.5.3) (https://github.com/nanoporetech/dorado) with the --no-trim option, and the resulting BAM files were converted into FASTQ format using samtools (v.1.20)37. Read trimming was then performed using Chopper (v.0.8.0)38 with parameters -q 7, --minlength 3000, and --maxlength 7000. Adapter trimming and demultiplexing were carried out using Porechop (v.0.2.4).For both experimental and simulated mock community reads, taxonomic classification was performed using Minimap2 (v.2.26)39 with the options -x map-ont, -K 500 M, and -c. The --secondary = no option was used to reduce false positives. To evaluate the performance of MIrROR release 02, three publicly available datasets were also included: GROND (gtdb207) (10.5281/zenodo.10889037)40,41, ncbi_202006 (https://bitbucket.org/ykinoshita1984/ncbi_db/downloads/)42, and SILVA 138.2 (https://www.arb-silva.de/) (Table 3)43. Comparative analyses were conducted using the native taxonomic nomenclature provided by each database.

Technical Validation

Evaluation using commercial mock community standards

A comparison of datasets was conducted using the ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standards (D6300), Microbial Community DNA Standard (D6305), and Gut Microbiome Standard (D6331) to evaluate taxonomic classification accuracy.

In the comparison among operon-based datasets (MIrROR release 02, MIrROR release 01, GROND, and ncbi_202006), broadly similar taxonomic profiles were observed across all mock datasets (Fig. 4A–E), except for the species-level classification in D6331 (Fig. 4F). In the D6331 mock community, Prevotella was consistently detected at the genus level across all operon-based datasets, with relative abundances ranging from 10.16% to 11.64%. However, at the species level, Prevotella corporis was detected only in the MIrROR release 02 dataset (10.28–11.64%; theoretical: 4.98%), while all other operon-based datasets identified 0%. Instead, multiple species within the Prevotella genus were assigned to the “others” group, such as P. jejuni, P. fusca, and P. melaninogenica (Supplementary Table 5). It is important to note that the absence of P. corporis sequences in the MIrROR release 01 and GROND datasets is the primary reason for this classification disparity. The successful detection of P. corporis in MIrROR release 02 demonstrates a specific content refinement that supports more accurate species-level profiling of gut microbial communities.

SILVA showed considerably lower classification accuracy, with many reads being assigned to non-specific labels such as uncultured_bacterium, gut_metagenome, or uncultured_marine. This may reflect the limited resolution of 16S rRNA-based approaches in complex microbial communities.

In D6300 and D6305, Bacillus subtilis NRRL B354 was reclassified from Bacillus marinus in MIrROR release 01 (GTDB R89) to Bacillus spizizenii in MIrROR release 02 (GTDB R220) (https://gtdb.ecogenomic.org/genome?gid=GCA_006094475.1). Additionally, Fusobacterium nucleatum 2-1-50 A in D6331 was reclassified as Fusobacterium animalis in MIrROR release 02 (https://gtdb.ecogenomic.org/genome?gid=GCF_028743415.1). In all mock datasets, Lactobacillus fermentum was renamed as Limosilactobacillus fermentum according to the updated taxonomy12.

Validation using an in silico simulated mock community including archaeal species

To further evaluate dataset performance—particularly for archaeal classification—an in silico mock community was constructed using eight bacterial species (Bifidobacterium bifidum, Campylobacter jejuni, Clostridioides difficile, Enterococcus faecium, Escherichia coli, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus, Listeria monocytogenes, and Salmonella enterica) and two archaeal species (Methanobrevibacter smithii and Methanosphaera stadtmanae). Long-read sequences were simulated using NanoSim with the metagenome_ERR3152364_Even training model. The analysis was performed using the same datasets applied to the experimental datasets, utilizing the 519F–2428R primer set for archaea evaluation.

Archaeal species were not detected in MIrROR release 01, as it did not include archaeal genomes in its reference dataset. In contrast, all other datasets evaluated—MIrROR release 02, GROND, ncbi_202006, and SILVA 138.2—were developed or updated after 2020, and include archaeal sequences, thereby enabling the detection of both archaeal species in the simulated community.

At the genus level, operon-based datasets such as MIrROR release 02, GROND, and NCBI_202006 successfully assigned most reads with minimal misclassification. In contrast, SILVA showed a high rate of ambiguous assignments, with an average of 42.39% of reads classified as “others.” At the species level, MIrROR release 02 and GROND again showed the lowest proportion of reads classified as “others” (0.05%), while SILVA exhibited the highest (46.67%) (Supplementary Table 6, Supplementary Fig. 2).

Notably, many of the reads in SILVA were assigned to non-specific or unrelated taxa such as uncultured_bacterium, metagenome, and uncultured_marine, as well as to bacterial species such as Streptococcus agalactiae, Lactobacillus brantae, and Candidatus Arsenophonus. These findings highlight the limitations of 16S rRNA-based datasets in accurately resolving species-level classifications, particularly in the context of complex microbial communities.

Clustering using MeShClust (v.3.0)

meshclust3 -d {input_fasta} -o {output_fasta} -t 0.99 -c 50 -r n -e y

Redundancy filtering BLAST (v.2.15.0)

blastn -db {blast_database} -query {input_fasta} -out {output_xml} -outfmt 5 -num_threads{num_threads}

primersearch -seqall {input.fasta} -infile {primers.txt} -outfile {output.txt} -mismatchpercent 30
