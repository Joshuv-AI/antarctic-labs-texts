Melatonin improves high temperature stress tolerance by physiological and reproductive stabilization in common bean
Authors: S Kruthika, Apoorva Ashu, Amol P Solanke, J V Navodhaya, C Harimadhav, T Basavaraja, Ajay Arora, Parvaze Sofi, Madan Pal, P Ratnakumar, P V Vara Prasad, S Gurumurthy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe common bean is an essential legume crop that faces significant productivity threats from high temperature stress (HTS). It negatively impacts physio-biochemical processes, floral organ development, and fertilization, and degrades seed and nutritional quality, leading to yield losses. To alleviate the impact of HTS on common bean genotypes that are tolerant to water deficit stress (WDS), foliar applications (0 and 300 µM) of melatonin (MT), a low-molecular-weight organic compound that acts as both a plant hormone and an antioxidant, was applied at pre and post flowering stage of the crop. Two experiments were conducted to study the efficacy of MT foliar application on common bean genotypes under HTS (I & II) conditions during the late Rabi (post-rainy) season of 2023-24. Foliar application of MT reduced the canopy temperature by 29.1% under HTS-I (29–32 °C) and 7.9% under HTS-II (32–35 °C) and increased the pollen viability by 25.8% and 45.9% under HTS-I and II, respectively. In addition, MT significantly enhanced seed yield, with increases of 11.2% and 34.4% under HTS-I and HTS-II, respectively. MT also increased the micronutrient content of the seeds under HTS. Overall, these results provide a comprehensive understanding of the protective and tolerance effects of MT against HTS, ensuring yield stability in HTS-prone areas.Keywords: Common bean, Canopy temperatures, High-temperature stress, Melatonin, Pollen vigorSubject terms: Hormones, Climate sciences

Introduction

The common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) is a vital legume for human consumption, highly valued for its rich protein, carbohydrate, and dietary fiber content, as well as essential minerals such as iron (Fe) and zinc (Zn)1. Almost 28.9 Mt of dry beans are produced annually on a global scale, covering an area of 33.1 Mha and representing 50.37% of the total land area used for global pulse production2. India produces 23% of the total global production, accounting for 43% of the world’s cultivated area, which boosts yield by 417 kg ha− 13. Studies have revealed that abiotic stresses like water deficit stress (WDS) and high temperature stress (HTS) disrupt key physiological functions. They damage the photosynthetic machinery, reduce stomatal conductance, transpiration, electron transport rate, and water use efficiency (WUE), leading to a significant drop in common bean yield4. Globally, approximately 73% of common bean production areas are projected to experience water scarcity due to prolonged WDS periods5 along with HTS.

HTS is a significant threat to common bean plants. According to climate model predictions, by 2050, HTS could affect approximately 50% of the land currently used to grow common beans6. The projected increase in HTS highlights the pressing need to understand its physiological effects on reproductive processes, which ultimately contribute to yield. High temperatures of 33/25°C (day/night) disrupt the structure and impair the function of the endoplasmic reticulum in the tapetum, leading to premature degeneration of the tapetum. Early tapetum degeneration is linked to pollen sterility7. Similarly, temperatures exceeding 30/20°C can significantly reduce seed yield in common bean due to flower abscission, the development of parthenocarpic pods (pin pods), lower seed set pod− 1, and decreased seed size8. Exposure to high temperatures of 32/27°C for five days during anthesis also significantly reduced pod and seed numbers in common bean9.

HTS leads to flower, bud, and pod abortion; reduced pollen viability; impaired anther dehiscence; damaged pollen tube formation; and decreased seed filling and seed size, ultimately causing substantial yield reductions10,11. Likewise, HTS reduces seed set by reducing pollen viability, leading to poor fertilization and reduced reproductive success in cereals such as sorghum12.

To mitigate the impact of HTS on crops, an effective strategy, such as the exogenous application of melatonin (MT), is being employed. MT (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine) is a low-molecular-weight organic compound that acts as both a hormone and an antioxidant, which strengthens the tolerance of plants against environmental hardships, including HTS13 and WDS14,15. Studies have also reported that an increase in endogenous MT production or exogenous application enhances stress tolerance in various crops under multiple stress scenarios16,17. Exogenous MT application enhances thermotolerance by improving photosynthesis and antioxidant capacity in crops such as rice (Oryza sativa L;15, tomato (Solanum lycopersicum18, , wheat (Triticum aestivum L.;19, and maize (Zea mays;20. In addition to enhancing pollen viability and germination, MT has been shown to maintain a cooler canopy temperature (CT) under HTS21 and to increase the ability to detoxify reactive oxygen species (ROS) by activating scavenging cascades and activating antioxidant enzymes22.

Recent research has primarily focused on identifying WDS-tolerant common bean genotypes through assessments of physiological, biochemical, pollen vigor, and yield-related traits. Building on these findings, the selected genotypes were subsequently evaluated for their response to the foliar application of MT under HTS conditions. Although the use of exogenous MT for abiotic stress mitigation is gaining attention in various crops, its potential role in enhancing HTS tolerance in common bean remains largely unexplored. Therefore, the present study aimed to (i) evaluate the physiological and biochemical responses of common bean genotypes under HTS and (ii) assess the impact of exogenous MT on CT, pollen viability, pollen germination, and seed nutritional quality. The findings highlight the potential of MT as an effective strategy for enhancing HTS tolerance, offering promising avenues for its integration into agronomic practices to improve crop resilience and productivity under HTS-prone areas.

Materials and methods

Experimental site

The field experiments were conducted on two sowing dates during the Kharif and Rabi seasons of 2023 (Exp. I). and the late Rabi season of 2023-24 (Exp. II), to understand the seasonal temperature variations referred to as HTS-I and HTS-II, at the ICAR-National Institute of Abiotic Stress Management (NIASM) located at Baramati, Pune, India. The field experiments were conducted at the South Research Farm at 18°09’ N latitude and 74°30’ E longitude, with an elevation 550 m above mean sea level (a.m.s.l.).

Soil properties

The soil at the experimental site was inherent murram soil, with a pH of 7.55, an electrical conductivity (EC) of 0.14 dSm− 1, and a very low organic carbon content (0.33%). In the top 15 cm, 67.7 kg ha− 1 of available nitrogen, 1.6 kg ha− 1 of phosphorus, 86.5 kg ha− 1 of potassium, and 7.4 kg ha− 1 of total sulfur were detected. The soil’s field capacity (FC) and permanent wilting point (PWP) are 20.2% and 8.8% (w/w), respectively.

Planting material and experimental details

Experiment I

A set of 17 common bean genotypes (Table S1), including breeding materials and cultivated varieties, were assessed for water stress tolerance. The sowing for the Kharif season was done on June 13, 2023, those for the Rabi season was done on October 13, 2023, and the respective harvests were performed in the first week of September and in the last week of December 2023 (Fig. S1 and S2). A factorial randomized block design was used involving two treatments, well-watered (WW) and water deficit stress (WDS), for three replications. Sowing was performed on a raised bed with three rows 7.5 m long × 45 cm long, and the plant-to-plant distance was 15 cm.

WDS was imposed during the flowering and pod initiation stages by withholding irrigation, specifically by reducing the irrigation volume to half that of WW conditions. In the Kharif, WW plots received 52 L m−2 of water, while WDS plots received 30 L m−². In the Rabi, WW plots were irrigated with 50 L m−2, whereas WDS plots received only 27 L m−2. Agronomic practices such as fertilizer, weeding, and fungicide application were performed as per crop recommendations. The weather parameters during the Kharif and Rabi seasons are presented in Fig. S4.

Experiment II

Four genotypes were selected from the pool of seventeen in Exp. I based on their performance under WDS. These genotypes were sown on two separate dates, November 28, 2023 (HTS-I), and January 2, 2024 (HTS-II), to determine the effect of HTS on common beans (Fig. S3). A 300 µM MT solution was prepared for foliar application. MT (supplied by Sigma-Aldrich Co., 3050 Spruce Street, St. Louis, MO 63103, USA) was accurately weighed (140 mg) to prepare 2 L of the working solution, based on its molecular weight (232.28 g mol⁻¹). Due to the limited solubility of MT in water, it was first dissolved in a minimal volume of absolute ethanol to ensure complete solubilization, followed by dilution with distilled water, as described by23. A control treatment (0 µM) was prepared similarly by adding the same volume of ethanol to 3 L of distilled water without melatonin, to maintain consistency across treatments. Foliar application was carried out at two critical growth stages: pre-flowering (~ 35 days after sowing, DAS) and post-flowering (~ 45 DAS) in both sowing dates. Similar and standard rise bed plots, rows, and plant spaces were maintained as in Exp. 1.

Both HTS-I and HTS-II were naturally occurring stress events. HTS-I coincided with the flowering to pod-filling stage, during which maximum temperatures ranged between 29 °C and 32 °C (from 35 to 55 DAS; January 2 to 12, 2024). HTS-II was observed during the reproductive phase, with peak maximum temperatures rising further to 32–35 °C (from 35 to 55 DAS; February 6 to 26, 2024).

HTS-I showed fluctuating minimum temperatures and a brief rise in maximum temperature, with minor rainfall and moderate humidity, indicating moderate heat stress. In contrast, HTS-II was more severe, with consistently high maximum temperatures (> 34 °C), low rainfall, increased sunshine hours, and reduced humidity, indicating intensified heat stress and higher vapor pressure deficit. Morphophysiological traits

The days to 50% flowering and pod set, days to physiological maturity, plant height (PH), membrane stability index (MSI), relative water content (RWC), canopy temperature (Make: FLIR E63900), normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) (hand-held device make: GreenSeeker®, Trimble, USA) and soil plant analysis development (SPAD) (SPAD-502; make: Konica) were measured in both experiments. The proline24 and micronutrient contents were measured in Experiment II.

Pollen viability and germination

Live images of pollen viability and germination were captured using Leica Application Suite Easy (LAS EZ) software, version 3.4.0 (https://www.leica-microsystems.com) under a Leica DM-3000 LED fluorescence microscope at 40x magnification. Pollen viability was measured by the total ratio of the number of viable stained pollens (with 2% acetocarmine stain) to the total number of pollen grains25. Pollen germination was estimated using the in vitro pollen germination method. The germination medium was prepared by dissolving 20 g of sucrose and 10 mg of boric acid in 100 ml of distilled water. A single droplet of germination medium was then applied to the pollen grains on the slide, which was covered with a cover slip. The prepared slides were incubated for 5 min at 25–28 °C before observation. A pollen grain was regarded as germinated when the length of its pollen tube was equal to or exceeded the diameter of the grain26. The total number of pollen grains and the number of germinated grains with pollen tubes were counted to calculate the pollen germination percentage.

Yield parameters

In both experiments, the number of pods plant− 1, the number of seeds pod− 1, the test weight (100 seeds), the pod length, and the seed yield (kg ha− 1) were measured.

Water productivity (WP)

Water productivity evaluates the efficiency of water use in crop production and is measured as the ratio of crop yield to the volume of irrigated water applied or consumed27.

Water productivity (kg m– 3) = Total seed yield/Volume of water applied.

Micronutrient analysis using ICP-MS

Micronutrients were estimated using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), which involves digesting the seed samples with concentrated nitric acid to break them down into a liquid form. This solution is subsequently introduced into the ICP-MS instrument, where the elements are ionized in a high-temperature plasma, and their concentrations are precisely measured based on their mass-to-charge ratio. This technique allows accurate and sensitive quantification of trace elements and micronutrients such as Zn, Fe, manganese (Mn), and selenium (Se) in seeds.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from the experiments were subjected to statistical analysis using a General R-based analysis platform empowered by statistics (GRAPES) software developed by the Department of Agricultural Statistics, College of Agriculture, Vellayani, Kerala, available online on the website (https://www.kaugrapes.com/). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was also conducted with two-way analysis and the level of significance (LSD, p ≤ 0.05); the data were interpreted according to previous methods28. The yield data obtained from experiment I were subjected to statistical analysis via cluster analysis. The analysis was performed using the R program, version 4.3.0 (https://cran.r-project.org/) with the “ggdendrogram” package.

Results

Experiment I: performance of common bean genotypes under WDS

All 17 genotypes demonstrated superior performance under WW conditions compared to those under WDS in both the Kharif and Rabi seasons. However, the overall performance was marginally better during the Rabi season. The genotype NIAM-20-21 exhibited healthier pollen even under WDS conditions, indicating better reproductive resilience. In contrast, IPBR-98-22 consistently showed poor pollen vigor across both Kharif and Rabi seasons. Pollen germination declined by 25% during the Kharif season and 15.4% during the Rabi season under WDS. Additionally, the total seed yield decreased by 14.5% during the Kharif season and by 13.43% during the Rabi season. The phenological and other yield-related parameters are presented in Tables S2, S3 and S4. Under WW conditions, Varun had a 56% greater WP than IPBR-98-22 in Kharif, while NIAM-20-21 had an 81% greater productivity than IPBR-98-22 in Rabi. Under WDS, Waghe had 53% greater productivity than IPBR-98-22 in Kharif, and Varun had 73% greater productivity than IPBR-98-22 in Rabi.

The dendrogram hierarchical cluster revealed that the NIAM-20-21, Varun, NIAM-236-20, and Waghe genotypes exhibited the best performance under WDS (Fig. 3). NIAM-20-21 consistently performed better on multiple parameters, such as early maturity (Fig. S5), pollen viability, number of pods and total seed yield, under WDS conditions. The mean pollen characteristics and yield performance of all the genotypes are presented in Table 1. These stable WDS-tolerant genotypes were further selected for subsequent evaluation of HTS tolerance through the foliar application of MT.

Experiment II: effect of melatonin in mitigating HTS

This study highlights the positive influence of MT on various physiological and biochemical parameters of common bean genotypes, particularly under both HTS-I and HTS-II conditions.

Phenological traits

Two different sowing dates were used to impose different stress levels (HTS-I and HTS-II) in the following experiment. The foliar application of MT generally reduced the days to flowering, with a 1.72% reduction in HTS-I and a 4.11% reduction in HTS-II. Similarly, MT slightly reduced the number of days to pod set in both conditions. The number of days to physiological maturity was consistently reduced by MT, which promoted earlier maturity by approximately 1.54 days in HTS-I and 2.67 days in HTS-II. Across all the traits, the NIAM-20-21 genotype consistently presented earlier flowering, pod set, and maturity than other genotypes, indicating genotype-specific responses to MT. These findings suggest that MT enhances earlier phenological development, particularly under HTS-II conditions.

Physio-biochemical traits

The foliar application of MT effectively moderated CT, achieving reductions of 4.39% in the HTS-I conditions and 8% in the HTS-II conditions. Similarly, NIAM-20-21 exhibited the lowest CT in response to MT, while Waghe and NIAM-236-20 exhibited elevated temperatures, indicating reduced sensitivity to the cooling effects of MT (Fig. 4a and Fig. S6a). MT significantly enhanced the MSI by 34.07% under HTS-I conditions and 38.5% under HTS-II conditions. Furthermore, among the genotypes, NIAM-20-21 exhibited the highest MSI, indicating superior heat tolerance, while NIAM-236-20 demonstrated the lowest MSI, indicating its susceptibility to HTS. The treatment also improved the RWC across both the HTS-I and HTS-II conditions, with NIAM-20-21 recording the highest RWC values (89.66% for HTS-I, 91.48% for HTS-II). In contrast, NIAM-236-20 had the lowest RWC, suggesting inadequate water retention capacity under stress conditions. Additionally, MT application resulted in a 13.55% increase in PH in both the HTS-I and HTS-II conditions. Notably, NIAM-236-20 was the tallest genotype, reaching 43.58 cm under the MT application, whereas Varun recorded the shortest height at 28.21 cm under control condition (Fig. S6b). MT also facilitated an increase in the NDVI, with a rise of 17.54% in the HTS-I condition and 26.66% in the HTS-II condition. NIAM-20-21 exhibited the highest NDVI values across treatments, indicative of robust biomass accumulation, while Waghe consistently displayed the lowest NDVI values. Moreover, MT significantly elevated the proline content, with increases of 31.64% in HTS-I and 33.74% in HTS-II conditions. Similarly, NIAM-20-21 presented the highest proline concentration, reflecting a strong physiological response to stress, whereas NIAM-236-20 exhibited the lowest proline levels.

Pollen viability and germination

Under the HTS-I condition, the foliar application of MT led to a 25.8% increase in pollen viability, with Varun achieving the highest value (81.0%) and NIAM-236-20 the lowest (66.1%). In the HTS-II, MT resulted in a 45.9% increase in pollen viability, with Varun again exhibiting the highest value (78.2%) and NIAM-236-2 the lowest (66.1%). While in pollen germination, MT increased by 16.9% in the HTS-I conditions and 26.7% in the HTS-II conditions. Varun consistently presented the highest values, while NIAM-236-20 had the lowest recorded values under both conditions (Fig. 4b and c; Fig. S6c and d). Yield and its attributes

According to our two-factor analysis, the NIAM-20-21 genotype consistently demonstrated superior performance in terms of the number of pods plant− 1, the number of seeds pod− 1, the pod length, the test weight, and the seed yield (Fig. 4d; Table 4), exhibiting enhanced performance under MT application compared to the control across both HTS conditions. Notably, some 7-seeded pods were observed in the genotype NIAM-20-21 plants treated with MT, contributing to the increased number of seeds pod− 1 and ultimately leading to a higher total seed yield. Conversely, NIAM-236-20 consistently exhibited the lowest performance under both treatment and HTS conditions, although it exhibited improvement under MT application. A greater number of shriveled seeds was observed in the NIAM-236-20 genotype in the treatments, with a greater percentage of shriveled seeds found in the control treatment (Fig. 5). Regarding pod length, NIAM-236-20 consistently produced the longest pods in both the treatment and HTS conditions, with a notable increase observed under the MT treated plants. Conversely, Waghe consistently produced the shortest pods across both the treatment and HTS conditions, with an increase in pod length observed under the MT treated plants. MT improved test weight by 8.31% in the HTS-I conditions and 9.9% in the HTS-II conditions. Notably, the foliar application of MT resulted in a significant increase in seed yield, boosting it by 11.23% in the HTS-I conditions and by an impressive 34.37% in the HTS-II conditions. NIAM-20-21 produced the highest seed yield under both conditions, whereas NIAM-236-20 had the lowest, likely due to seed shrinkage under HTS.

Micronutrient content of seeds

For all the genotypes, the levels of zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), and selenium (Se) were consistently greater in the MT treatment than in the control during the HTS-II treatment (Fig. 6). Notably, NIAM-236-20 had the highest Zn and Fe contents in both treatments, while NIAM-20-21 had the highest Mn and Se contents. These results suggest that MT treatment effectively enhances the micronutrient content of seeds despite HTS, with NIAM-236-20 being particularly promising for nutritional improvement. These findings indicate that the foliar spray of MT may help mitigate HTS effects, potentially leading to more nutritious seeds under adverse growing conditions.

This improvement is likely due to MT’s role in mitigating high temperature stress and enhancing nutrient uptake, allowing plants to withstand higher temperatures.

Discussion

Experiment I

In experiment I, we found four WDS-tolerant genotypes that exhibited stable performance in both the Kharif and Rabi seasons. Despite slightly lower yields in Kharif compared to Rabi, cultivating common beans in Kharif remains economically viable and can still offer substantial income. Similarly, Gurumurthy et al.29,30 reported that cultivating chickpeas (Cicer arietinum L.) during the off-season offers benefits such as increased yield and income within a shorter growth period, efficient seed production, and the potential for rapid generation advancement or speed breeding31.

Experiment II

Plant-based MT, as a multifaceted molecule, has various functions in plants, including promoting germination, growth, and root architecture; enhancing photosynthesis; and providing tolerance to both biotic and abiotic stresses32–34. It acts as a multifunctional regulator that enhances thermotolerance under HTS by scavenging ROS, enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities, stabilizing cellular membranes, protecting the photosynthetic apparatus, modulating stress-responsive gene expression, interacting with key phytohormones, and maintaining overall metabolic homeostasis13. The exogenous foliar application of 300 µM MT significantly improved the yield under HTS. This improvement is attributed to enhanced physio-biochemical traits, including MSI, RWC, proline content, cooler CT, increased pollen vigor, and improved seed nutrition and yield in the common bean (Fig. 7).

Role of melatonin in alleviating high-temperature stress: Melatonin treatment enhances various physiological and biochemical parameters in plants. It improves membrane stability, leaf water content, chlorophyll content, plant height, pollen viability, and germination. Additionally, melatonin increases the micronutrient content in seeds, particularly iron and zinc, and boosts overall yield and its components. Notably, melatonin maintains cooler canopy temperature compared to the control plants.

Influence of melatonin on regulating pollen vigor during the reproductive stage

High temperatures can induce changes in bud morphology and result in pollen sterility, consequently reducing yield in crops35. Research on utilizing exogenous MT to mitigate the adverse effects of HTS during plant reproductive stages has been limited. In our research, MT significantly improved pollen viability and germination under HTS, with a stronger effect during HTS-II. In a previous study, yield and related traits, such as pollen viability, were used to select superior heat-tolerant genotypes36. Pre-treatment with MT significantly improves pollen viability and germination in heat-stressed tomatoes by reducing ROS accumulation in anthers, enhancing pollen viability by up to 45% and germination by up to 50.5%37. Similarly, applying MT as a foliar spray a day before HTS has been shown to enhance heat tolerance in rice plants during the reproductive stage38. These findings highlight the role of MT in boosting reproductive heat tolerance by preserving pollen viability and germination through enhanced antioxidant defense.

Melatonin-induced physiological adaptations to high-temperature stress

The application of MT positively influenced the PH under HTS. Hu et al.39 reported that in carnation (Dianthus caryophyllus) plants exposed to HTS, MT treatment effectively attenuated the reduction in PH compared to that in untreated controls. Similarly, Ahmad et al.40 reported enhanced PH and increased stem thickness in MT-treated maize plants under WDS conditions. Askari et al.41 demonstrated that exogenous MT application augmented both the PH and leaf area of common bean plants subjected to salt stress, highlighting its broad positive effects on growth metrics. Furthermore, ElSayed et al.42 reported that the exogenous application of MT improved growth in common bean under salinity stress by elevating PH, fresh and dry biomass.

The assessment of CT changes is vital for understanding plant mechanisms that regulate water loss and prevent overheating, subsequently promoting higher WUE43. In this study, MT application contributed to maintaining a cooler CT, thus ameliorating the deleterious impacts of HTS. This improvement is associated with elevated stomatal conductance, which minimizes water loss and enhances photosynthetic efficiency under HTS, as corroborated by findings from Hassan et al.44. Our findings further demonstrated that foliar application of MT significantly preserved leaf RWC, thereby enhancing the resilience of treated plants to HTS compared to the control. Supporting this, a previous study reported that foliar spraying with 100 µmol L–1 MT markedly improved heat tolerance in sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas (L.) Lam.) through increased RWC45. Interestingly, Sher et al.46 observed that a 300 µM MT treatment resulted in the highest RWC (~ 83.70%) in sorghum cultivars under WDS, outperforming other tested concentrations. Furthermore, our results revealed that MT application significantly enhanced MSI, with a more pronounced effect under HTS than under non-stress conditions. Likewise, Chaturvedi et al.47 reported that a concentration of 10 µM MT led to a substantial reduction in MDA and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) levels of 68% and 49%, respectively, thereby improving MSI under high-throughput sequencing in rice. In line with these findings, Zhang et al.48 reported that 20 µM MT increased MSI in ryegrass (Lolium perenne L.) under HTS, highlighting its role in enhancing stress resilience.

Influence of melatonin on enhancing proline content

Our study showed that the foliar application of MT significantly increased proline content under both HTS conditions, likely due to its role in enhancing antioxidant defense and activating stress-responsive pathways for proline biosynthesis. The NIAM-20-21 genotype consistently demonstrated the highest proline accumulation. In addition, MT enhanced proline accumulation in maize roots and sprouts, promoting HTS tolerance20. Similarly, HTS elevated proline levels in common bean plants, enhancing thermotolerance through increased antioxidant activities49. Buttar et al.19 demonstrated the thermotolerance of wheat plants by applying a foliar spray of MT at a concentration of 100 µM on three-day-old plants for seven days; this treatment involved reducing the MDA and H2O2 levels and increasing the photosynthetic pigments, proline, and both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant levels. These findings collectively suggest that MT-induced proline accumulation is a key adaptive mechanism contributing to enhanced thermotolerance across diverse crop species.

Influence of melatonin on enhancing yield under high-temperature stress

HTS during the flowering and seed set stages can adversely affect fertility as well as the quantity and quality of yield in common beans50. In our study, the foliar application of MT significantly enhances yield-related and seed nutritional quality traits across both HTS conditions. These parameters were consistent for the NIAM-20-21 genotype, demonstrating notable HTS tolerance. A similar study performed by Alinia et al.51 reported that priming common bean seeds with 100 µM MT led to the highest seed yield under saline conditions. According to Chang et al.52, MT enhanced yield by modulating antioxidant metabolism, promoting root development, and improving photosynthetic efficiency. Similarly, Manafi et al.53 reported increased numbers of pods plant− 1 and seeds pod− 1 and increased grain yield in soybeans under stress conditions combined with MT treatment.

MT plays a crucial role in regulating the mineral composition of plants and mitigating stress by upregulating essential nutrient levels54. Our results showed that MT increased the contents of micronutrients such as Fe, Zn, Se, and Mn in seeds during HTS-II, similar to the findings of other studies55,56.

MT appears to be more effective under HTS-II than under HTS-I across most physiological and yield parameters. In HTS-II, compared to MT, the control treatment was more severely affected by HTS, resulting in a greater percentage difference between the control and MT treatments. For example, in pollen viability, the mean values in HTS-I were 63% for the control and 80% for MT, reflecting a percentage increase of 26% due to melatonin application. In HTS-II, the control dropped further to 48%, while MT maintained a higher value of 71%, leading to a percentage increase of 46%. The bigger difference between MT and the control under HTS-II is mainly because the control value is much lower, showing that MT offers better protection in harsher conditions. Also, MT is more effective under HTS-II than HTS-I due to greater oxidative stress, membrane disruption, and hormonal imbalance at higher temperatures, which trigger stronger activation of MT. Supporting this, Xu et al.23 reported that exogenous MT more effectively restored photosynthetic efficiency and alleviated oxidative damage at 42 °C than at 35 °C in tomato. Similarly, MT was shown to preserve membrane integrity better and boost antioxidant enzyme activities under severe heat stress (42 °C) in both wheat seedlings and tall fescue19,57. Overall, MT enhances thermotolerance by safeguarding vital physiological functions and reproductive processes under HTS.

Conclusion

Our study identified four genotypes (NIAM-20-21, NIAM-236-20, Varun and Waghe) that demonstrated tolerance to water deficit stress (WDS) based on indicators such as canopy temperature, the membrane stability index, the relative water content, pollen performance, and yield traits. These genotypes could be valuable resources for breeding programs aimed at enhancing WDS tolerance. Furthermore, the exogenous foliar application of melatonin (MT) appeared to effectively ameliorate the adverse effects of high-temperature stress (HTS) while preserving seed yield and nutritional quality. Notably, NIAM-20-21 showed remarkable dual tolerance to both WDS and HTS. Moreover, MT can be applied during HTS to increase seed yield and quality by improving tolerance to HTS in common beans. Additionally, MT can be utilized for off-season cultivation and in HTS-prone areas to sustain productivity effectively.
