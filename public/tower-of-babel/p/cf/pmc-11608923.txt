An evaluation of the efficiency and effectiveness of diagnostic tests for foot and mouth disease: are novel diagnostic tests for FMD more feasible than conventional tests in Southeast Asia?
Authors: WY Tan, MP Ward
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFoot and mouth disease (FMD) remains endemic in many areas of continental Southeast Asia (SEA). It is responsible for substantial economic losses in the smallholder sector and threatens livelihoods. In recent years, novel diagnostic tests have been developed which reportedly detect FMD virus more effectively and efficiently. This critically appraised topic (CAT) aimed to evaluate the feasibility of these diagnostic tests for FMD in SEA compared to conventional tests. Relevant studies that evaluate diagnostic tests are identified and critically assessed, and recommendations are made on suitable potential diagnostic tests for use in the smallholder sector in SEA. A systematic search of electronic databases (CABI: CAB Abstracts, Web of Science Core Collections) was carried out to identify relevant studies that compared novel and conventional diagnostic tests. The search strategy initially identified 12 papers, of which six fulfilled all the inclusion criteria and were selected for this review. Most of the selected studies had limitations in design and comparability, making it difficult to validly compare the effectiveness and efficiency of the relevant diagnostic tests. These limitations include variation in sample characteristics, methodology, measurable outcomes and the different aspects of the diagnostic tests that each study focused on. Most studies concluded that novel diagnostic tests were more effective and efficient than conventional tests: had greater analytical sensitivity and specificity, were more robust, had a wider range of processable sample types and serotypes, could detect various diseases, had faster testing speeds and provided greater value for money. However, strong recommendations on which specific diagnostic test to rely on could not be made, since there was conflicting evidence and multiple confounding factors. Overall, the evidence found did not entirely apply to the target scenario, being SEA smallholder farms. Recommendations for the target scenario were also made based on the study findings.Keywords: cloven‐hoofed animals, diagnostic tests, foot and mouth disease, Southeast Asia

Clinical scenario

Foot and mouth disease (FMD) remains endemic in most of continental Southeast Asia (SEA) with multiple outbreaks. 1 It is caused by the highly contagious FMD virus, a member of the Picornaviridae family. It infects cloven‐hoofed animals, such as pigs, goats, sheep and cattle. It typically causes vesiculo‐erosive lesions on the feet, teats and around the oral cavity. Infected herds have prolonged substandard production, causing revenue losses for farmers. 2 , 3 , 4 The majority of outbreaks in SEA between 2007 and 2017 were due to serotype O (PanAsia strain; 77.9%), followed by A (19.8%). 5 , 6

Previously, FMD outbreaks have caused massive economic losses for smallholder SEA farmers. 5 Estimated financial losses from FMD in Laos from 2011 to 2013 was USD 30,881 per smallholder village farm, and an estimated national loss of up to USD 102 million. 7 In 2010, it was reported that Southern Cambodian smallholder cattle farms infected with FMD had 54%–92% lower economical profits from production losses. 8

Foot and mouth disease presents similarly to other vesicular diseases such as swine vesicular disease (SVD) and vesicular stomatitis. 4 Therefore, suspected FMD cases should be confirmed with diagnostic tests. This review compared multiple diagnostic tests to identify an optimal one for use on smallholder SEA farms. Optimal diagnostic tests are critical for accurate data collection in disease surveillance, and to guide prompt control strategies, which is lacking in some regions of SEA. 9 Given the high disease prevalence in SEA (up to 80% in some regions), 10 an ideal diagnostic test for FMD endemic regions would be highly specific 11 and applicable to field conditions for rapid disease reporting, such as a lateral flow immunochromatographic (LFI) strip test. 12

Objectives

The aim of this critically appraised topic (CAT) was to review the effectiveness and efficiency of novel FMD diagnostic tests used on SEA smallholder farms, compared to conventional ones. Effectiveness is defined by the accuracy of the diagnostic test 13 in detecting FMD antigens or antibodies using parameters such as sensitivity (Se), specificity (Sp) and positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV respectively). 11 Efficiency is defined by the level of costs involved in testing animals, the logistics and skilled staff needed and the range of processable clinical sample types.

Focused clinical question

In cloven hoofed animals on smallholder farms in SEA infected with FMD or at risk of being infected with FMD, does using novel diagnostic tests compared to conventional diagnostic tests result in more efficient and effective diagnosis of FMD in SEA?

Inclusion criteria

Studies were only included if they were relevant to study objectives. Studies had to be published in peer‐reviewed journals and have the full English text available. For inclusion, papers had to include:

Diagnostic validation tests involving detection of either the FMD antigen or antibody present in collected samples. Studies had to evaluate the diagnostic performance and validity of the diagnostic tests against a reference test or compare diagnostic tests within a single study, with outcomes measured by estimates of either sensitivity or specificity (or both) in the studies. According to the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH), valid reference tests would be able to demonstrate either FMD viral antigens, viral nucleic acid or virus‐specific antibodies in clinical samples. Long‐standing reference tests include virus isolation (VI), reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR), enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) and virus neutralisation test (VNT). 14

These tests include virus isolation (VI), liquid‐phase blocking ELISA (LPBE) and singleplex reverse RT‐PCR. 6 , 7 Novel tests are defined as diagnostic methods that are less utilised in SEA, with a particular focus on molecular techniques involving nucleotide sequencing over handheld platforms, as well as multiplex screening for similar vesicular diseases. 4 , 15 Examples of these include reverse transcription loop‐mediated isothermal amplification (RT‐LAMP), lateral flow immunochromatographic test strips (LFI) and multiplex RT‐PCR (mRT‐PCR) assays.

Key results Both multiplex and singleplex RT‐LAMP assays achieved diagnostic sensitivities similar to RT‐qPCR. mRT‐LAMP had similar detection limits to the singleplex RT‐LAMP tests. RT‐qPCR and multiplex RT‐LAMP assays achieved high specificity. RT‐qPCR took over two times longer than mRT‐LAMP to achieve diagnostic results. FMDV‐Ag SAI kit was far more sensitive in detecting FMD in vesicular epithelium emulsion samples than vesicular lesions swab and saliva. Both FMDV‐Ag SAI and RT‐qPCR showed no cross‐sensitivity with non‐FMD vesicular disease viruses. FMDV‐Ag SAI showed higher sensitivity than Svanodip®, especially in detecting serotypes Asia 1 than O, A, C, SAT1 and SAT2. FMDV‐Ag SAI had far higher detection rates in saliva contaminated samples compared to Svanodip®. mRT‐PCR had better analytical sensitivity than ELISA for detecting serotypes O and A. Difference of % positivity results between mRT‐PCR and ELISA in clinical specimens were found to be statistically significant (P < 0.05). mRT‐PCR was able to clearly detect and differentiate all FMD samples as serotype O or A, yielding the expected PCR products, while ELISA was unable to determine almost half of all samples. mRT‐PCR was able to completely detect and amplify all viral RNA genome products simultaneously from a mixture of the four target viruses. mRT‐PCR was the most sensitive in detecting FMDV O1 Manisa and least sensitive in detecting VSV, with higher detection rates when combined with microarray detection. mRT‐PCR showed 100% assay specificity in identifying and serotyping from non‐target vesicular disease viruses and control samples, particularly when used in combination with microarray. Microarrays that used probes generally showed higher specificity. Indirectly labelled amplicons performed better than directly labelled ones, identifying more serotypes and reacting with higher intensity. mRT‐LAMP showed higher detection rates and sensitivity than RT‐qPCR. Both assays were highly specific for the FMD target sequence. RT‐LAMP in all formats showed higher assay concordance than RT‐RPA. rRT‐LAMP and rRT‐PCR had similar analytical sensitivities using the RNA standard, while RT‐RPA was less sensitive. RT‐RPA and RT‐LAMP showed 100% specificity for all assays. Samples that underwent RNA extraction yielded higher analytical sensitivity compared to simple sample preparation methods. RT‐RPA requires samples to undergo RNA extraction for accurate detection of FMD while RT‐LAMP formats only had a minimal decrease in analytical sensitivity when simple sample preparation methods were used. ConclusionmRT‐LAMP was just as effective as real‐time RT‐PCR, but more efficient in terms of a faster diagnosis time, being more cost‐effective and being simpler to use. FMDV‐Ag SAI was more effective than Svanodip®, but less effective than real‐time RT‐PCR. FMDV‐Ag SAI was more efficient than Svanodip® as being more robust, user‐friendly, and valid with a bigger range of samples, but had no significant differences in other aspects. mRT‐PCR was more effective than ELISA. In terms of efficiency, mRT‐PCR was more time‐efficient but less robust than ELISA. mRT‐PCR and microarray combination assay with probes showed similar effectiveness to real‐time RT‐PCR. mRT‐PCR was more efficient than real‐time RT‐PCR in terms of time‐effectiveness and robustness, particularly with microarray probe usage. mRT‐LAMP was more effective than RT‐qPCR. mRT‐LAMP was also more efficient than RT‐qPCR, as it involved less equipment, was more cost‐effective, time‐efficient, and more user‐friendly. RT‐LAMP had similar effectiveness to rRT‐PCR but was more effective than RT‐RPA. Simple sample preparation methods slightly reduced the sensitivity of RT‐LAMP. RT‐LAMP was more efficient than rRT‐PCR and RT‐RPA since it was able to process a larger range of clinical samples, was more robust, and required lesser logistics. Results

Out of the initial 12 papers identified and screened, six publications were found to fulfil all inclusion criteria, all being diagnostic test studies. Novel diagnostic tests had greater analytical sensitivity and specificity, were more robust, processed more sample types and serotypes, were faster and were more budget friendly than conventional ones. Four studies showed that novel and conventional diagnostic tests were similarly effective. However, two studies concluded that the novel test was less effective. Five studies reported that novel tests had greater efficiency with one study reporting otherwise.

Most of the selected studies had significant limitations. Firstly, none of the studies had a calculated sample size. Confidence intervals of the reported estimates of sensitivity and specificity in almost all studies were not discussed. 16 Only two studies replicated their tests comprehensively, 17 , 18 while two studies did not. 19 , 20 One study only replicated some of its tests, 21 while another claimed that the study was replicated without presenting any relevant data. 22 Only two studies involved blinding of assessors. 16 , 21 Randomisation methods were employed in four studies. 17 , 18 , 19 , 21 Only two studies discussed the disease spectrum of the animals which were sampled, with one study documenting disease progression in inoculated samples. 21 One study utilised solely clinically healthy animals which were either uninfected, or latently infected as a carrier. 22 The results were not fully representative of the target population. Not all studies included the relevant O or A serotype samples, and no study used strains of SEA origin. One study did not specify the strain or serotype. 22

Three articles 18 , 20 , 22 included samples from cattle species while another study 21 included cattle, sheep and pig samples. Two articles failed to identify the species of origin. 17 , 19 All studies were conducted under laboratory conditions. For reference tests, five studies used real‐time RT‐PCR while one used indirect ELISA 20 but only two studies justified their choice.

Additionally, studies used a wide range of populations and methodology. The studies had vastly different measurable outcomes (Table 4) for effectiveness and efficiency, failing to account for inter‐laboratory differences. Studies used differing vesicular viruses that present similarly to FMD to determine specificity. 3 All studies utilised vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), all studies except one used swine vesicular disease virus (SVDV) and three out of six studies used vesicular exanthema of swine virus (VESV). Viruses used should be standardised to and can include significant emerging vesicular diseases such as African swine fever (ASF) and bluetongue virus (BTV). In terms of data collection periods, one study 21 carried out a trial over time while the remainder were performed at a single time point.

Discussion

All but two studies concluded that the novel diagnostics, some of which are expanded forms of conventional ones, are superior to conventional ones. 4 However, not all circumstances call for novel diagnostic tests. Some factors to consider include available resources, distance from processing laboratories and outbreak scenarios as opposed to endemic situations.

The selected studies ranged in the strength of evidence. All studies attempted to validate the novel diagnostic test against the conventional one, albeit not always reliably. Therefore, studies with stronger evidence and reliable methodology should contribute greater weight to any conclusions made from this CAT. In terms of sample size, four articles utilised a substantial sample size (n > 100). These sample sizes enabled better target population representation for stronger evidence. Most studies validated the diagnostic test against a reference test, providing a more accurate measure of true positive and negative samples. None of the studies provided 95% confidence intervals (CI), which is recommended by the FDA (U.S. Food and Drug Administration) to quantify precision of reported estimates of sensitivity and specificity of dichotomous diagnostic tests. 9 Data to calculate 95% CI should be made public for further reanalysis. The positive and negative predictive values for each study were calculated using the MSD Manual Clinical calculator (https://www.msdmanuals.com/en‐au/professional/pages‐with‐widgets/clinical‐calculators?mode=list). The PPVs were 100% for three studies, 17 , 19 , 21 whereas the NPVs were 0% for two studies 17 , 21 and 14.35% for one study. 18 There was insufficient information to calculate the values in the remaining studies.

Most studies did not replicate their tests comprehensively. This greatly hindered the observation of data patterns and outliers, thus compromising study integrity and reliability. None of the studies carried out in‐field tests to gauge practical use of the tests in a realistic scenario, 4 which is not always guaranteed in tests found to be valid in laboratory settings. 18

The selected studies had multiple differences and confounding elements across the study designs that complicated fair comparisons of the diagnostic tests. The variable factors included sample characteristics, data collection periods, disease spectrum, viruses involved in specificity testing and discussed aspects of efficiency. Samples that were collected from a limited geographical area provided better internal validity (samples represented the limited target population) but poor external validity. Studies that included samples from various locations globally had results that could be applied to a wider context. 23 However, having a huge variety in sample sources across studies made it difficult to consistently evaluate internal and external validity. In this CAT, internal validity in the context of SEA smallholder farms is considered a more important domain, but none of the study samples originated from SEA. Future studies should include strains and serotypes of FMD typically found in SEA or involve common SEA domestic animal species such as buffaloes and goats to increase applicability to the CAT objectives.

The studies used vastly different outcome measures to determine the ‘effectiveness’ of the diagnostic tests. None of the studies provided a clear definition of ‘efficiency’, further complicating what is already considered a subjective measure when considering the wide range of factors involved.

Ideal diagnostic tests for SEA smallholder farms would be simple to use in the field, cost‐effective and give results rapidly for timely disease reporting. These qualities make farmers more likely to test for FMD without relying solely on vaccination strategies and ensure rapid outbreak detection. 3 , 24 The availability of cost‐effective diagnostic tests will encourage established industry stakeholders to invest in sustainable testing, which is not done extensively enough in endemic SEA regions. 24 While sensitive molecular tests that can provide comprehensive information and serotype samples remain vital for managing outbreaks, this is not needed in all situations.

While no test will be perfect for all situations, the foot and mouth disease virus‐Ag SAI (FMDV‐Ag SAI) test is most suitable for smallholder SEA farmers as it appears to be highly cost‐effective and applicable in the field as a pen‐side diagnostic with no equipment needed. In endemic regions with high disease prevalence, it is likely to have good PPV and lower NPV.

Conclusion

Generally, novel FMD diagnostic tests were found to be more effective and efficient than conventional ones. However, the studies had limited comparability and reliability. Only two databases were included in the search strategy, and not all FMD diagnostic tests were evaluated. Further research could employ longitudinal study designs, carry out in‐field tests for relevant FMD strains and establish standardised testing protocols to minimise confounding factors that complicate comparison. Advanced molecular methods, adaptation to portable testing and multiplex diagnostics prove promising for more accessible disease surveillance on smallholder farmers in Southeast Asia. 3 , 4
