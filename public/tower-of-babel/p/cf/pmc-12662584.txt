Readiness of parents to attend first dental visit within three years of child's age: An analysis applying Anderson's behavior model of health and utilization
Authors: V Sree Keerthi, Sibyl Siluvai, Sagul Ramanuja Mugunthan, KP Indumathi, G Krishnaprakash, Victor Lazar Rakesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:A First Dental Visit (FDV) is significant for the child as it allows the dentist to counsel parents and enables the early detection, prevention, and treatment of dental caries. To investigate the readiness of parents to attend their first dental visit within 3 years of the child’s age using Andersen’s behavior model of health and utilization.MATERIALS AND METHODS:In this cross-sectional questionnaire study, the sample was collected from the parents of children within 3 years of age. Considering that 14.3% is the assumed proportion (P) of willingness to attend the FDV, the sample size was 208. Samples were collected from the outpatient department of pediatric wards and other centers such as vaccination camps, and daycare in Chengalpattu district using a questionnaire based on Anderson’s model. Using logistic regression, the willingness to attend the FDV was obtained.RESULTS:Out of 208 samples, 10 (4.8%) of them had visited the dentist before 3 years of age and 198 (95.19%) were not aware to visit the dentist. 94.7% of parents selected healthy teeth as a reason for not visiting the dentist, and only 22 of them were planning to visit the dentist in the next 3 months.CONCLUSION:The study concluded that rather than waiting for an oral health issue to arise, parental knowledge about FDVs should be improved and encouraged to attend the FDV early.

Introduction

An integral part of overall wellness is dental health. Young children can eat and speak normally when they have good dental health. Primary tooth decay lowers children’s self-esteem, raises the chance of being underweight, and impacts oral health throughout childhood.[1] Therefore, keeping primary teeth healthy is crucial for a child’s general and oral development.[2] The age at which a child who has no known dental issues should see a dentist for the first time has been the subject of a great deal of discussion over the past several years. It is best before the child reaches the age of 12 months, according to several research studies.[3] At or around the age of 1 year, fewer children are discovered to have had a prophylactic dental appointment.[4] It was also found that early dental visits led to less restorative need and lower costs.[5] Early dental neglect or lack of treatment can have an impact on a child’s quality of life, speech, diet, and academic achievement. Early dental environment familiarization aids in lowering a child’s fear of the dentist. It gives parents a chance to talk about any worries or inquiries they may have about their child’s dental health.[6] Infant oral hygiene, feeding techniques, preventative therapy administered at home and in the office, nutritional patterns, oral habits, and dental injury prevention are all instructed to the parents.

Children’s dental health is greatly impacted by their first dental visit (FDV) within a year of birth.[7] The FDV first evaluates the risk of developing dental caries to improve the management of early childhood dental health. Second, the FDV offers dietary recommendations, demonstrates oral hygiene suitable for age, and offers proactive dental habit advice, all of which can motivate parents to give their kids the finest dental care possible.[8] Children who had the FDV within a year of birth not only had an average dental expenditure which was nearly 40% lower than that of children whose FDV was delayed but also had a higher likelihood of using the following preventive interventions.

Within a year of age, the majority of newborns did not have the FDV, despite the positive effects on health and the economy. Only 2.2% of Indian children had their FDV within a year of birth, according to earlier research, and the majority had not seen a dentist until they were between 3 and 6 years old or even older.[9]

First, rather than advising FDV before the age of 1 year, the majority of research concentrated on dental appointments or the first time they were made.[10] Second, the retrospective study design makes it possible to recall the age and reason for the FDV, but it is difficult to collect information regarding the parents’ subjective and psychological states at the time. Third, Since much research concentrated on pediatric dental clinics, which only included children who had dental visits, the circumstances surrounding children who did not attend the FDV were unclear.[11] Therefore, investigating the possible influencing factors surrounding FDV within the 3-year-old age range can be aided by a community-based study using a sample of infants aged 3 years.

Dental care utilization behavior has been widely described by the behavioral model of health service utilization created by Andersen, which may be used to explain FDV utilization behavior. The utilization of health services by an individual is contingent upon need, enabling, and predisposing variables, as per Andersen’s model. Predisposing factors include social structure, health beliefs, and demographics that influence attitudes toward the utilization of healthcare services and are associated with dental visits.[12] Utilization of dental care can be facilitated by enabling factors such as social support, income, travel, and waiting times.[13] Need factors are the degree to which people believe they are ill and are predictive of the use of healthcare services. More research is required to determine the relationship between these variables and the FDV. Current data indicate that the rate of FDV in children under 1 year of age is low, and it may have been far lower during the COVID-19 epidemic.[14]

Given that behavioral willingness serves as a significant behavioral predictor,[15] this study will focus on mothers’ willingness to bring their 3-year-old children to their first dentist appointment. Utilizing Andersen’s model as a guide, this study investigates the need, enabling, and predisposing factors that affect a mother’s readiness to visit the FDV within 3 years of a child’s age.

The purpose of this study is to find the readiness of parents to take their child for a first dental visit before 3 years of age, analyzing the various predisposing, enabling, and need factors of health and utilization and finding the association between the factors involved in the study. By analyzing influencing factors using the Andersen behavioral model, this study provides fresh insights into parents’ desire to take their child for an FDV before the age of 3. In addition to addressing myths around basic dental care and the value of maternal education, it looks at predisposing, enabling, and need-based factors in parental decision-making in a novel way. The findings’ generalizability is improved by the sample’s diversity. This study offers information to direct health policies and actions to enhance early children’s oral healthcare by identifying the factors that encourage and hinder early dental visits.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting: This study was a cross-sectional study, and the type of study is a questionnaire type of study, conducted in the outpatient department of pediatric wards and other centers such as vaccination camps, and daycare in Chengalpattu district, Tamil Nadu, India.

Study participants and sampling: (INCLUSION CRITERIA): Parents of children below 3 years of age group. Parents not willing to attend the study are excluded.

The sample size was determined using an assumed willingness proportion (P) of 14.3% to guarantee that the study took into account a wide variety of sociodemographic factors, using the formula

n = Z^2 P (1 - P)/d^2

The minimum sample required was estimated to be 196. However, the final sample size was changed to 208 to account for nonresponse and potential variability in order to improve representation across various socioeconomic origins, gender distributions, and educational levels. This method strengthened the data generalizability by ensuring that they represented a larger and more varied population.

Data collection tools and technique

Informed consent was collected from each participant as they presented to the medical OPD and other centers. Depending on the patient’s need, it was provided in both Tamil and English. By providing a questionnaire, FDV attendance and willingness to attend the FDV were assessed based on Andersen’s approach as shown in Figure 1; the independent variables in this study were predisposing, enabling, and need factors. According to the study’s objectives, the questionnaire was assessed. The data were collected and recorded in a specially designed format that consisted of four sections.

Ethical clearance: Prior approval to conduct the study clearance was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of the institution (SRMIEC-ST1023-763).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences software (SPSS version 19, USA). The level of P value was set at ≤0.05. Student’s t-test and Chi-square analyses were used to see if there were any significant differences in the demographic and health-related features between mothers who were willing to take their child to FDV and those who were not. Using three logistic regression models that fit Andersen’s model, the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals for the willingness to attend the FDV were obtained. To take into consideration of need, enabling, and predisposing factors, these models were gradually modified.

Results

This study examined need-based, enabling, and predisposing factors that affect healthcare utilization to evaluate parental readiness for an FDV before the age of 3. Out of 208 samples, 10 (4.8%) of them had visited the dentist before 3 years of age and 198 (95.19%) were not aware to visit the dentist. 197 (94.7%) of parents have selected “teeth are healthy” as a reason for not visiting the dentist, and only 22 (10.57%) of them were planning to visit the dentist in the next 3 months. In this study, 103 (49.51%) of the participants are male children and 105 (50.48%) are female children. Maternal education was 54 (25.96%) for middle and high school levels, 88 (42.30%) of them are undergraduates, and 32 (15.38%) are postgraduates.

86 (41.34%) of parents believed that since permanent teeth could replace primary teeth, they did not need to be treated if they were damaged. 148 (71.15%) of parents believed that their child’s teeth had recently erupted and were generally healthy, negating the need for an oral checkup. Many predisposing factors, including different birth orders (P = .192), maternal oral health knowledge levels (P = .032), perceived caries susceptibility (P = .024), and perceived caries severity (P = .000), were statistically quite distinct in the distribution of FDV willingness. Additionally, there were statistically significant variations in willingness among mothers with different levels of family social support (P = .032). Discussion

Our research revealed an association between a mother’s birth order as a risk factor, her desire to bring her child to the FDV as a need factor, and the social support of her family as an enabling factor. When it comes to a child’s first dental appointment, which is regarded as one of the preventive strategies for dental caries, mothers are crucial. Oral health counseling is the first step in providing infants with dental care. According to the American Academy of Paediatric Dentistry, the first appointment should take place no later than 12 months of age, and ideally within 6 months of the first main tooth erupting. Additionally, at this time, parents ought to have counseling, proactive advice on proper dental hygiene practices, and general dietary counseling on oral health in addition to minimizing dangerous behavior including feeding practices and fluoride use.

About 4.8% of the parents in this study took their kids to the dentist before the age of 3. Unlikely, according to an Iowa cohort study, 31% of the kids had visited a dentist by the time they turned 3.[4] 40% of children who reported their FDV were younger than 3 years old in Bulgarian research.[16] The main reason for not visiting the dentist is found to be “teeth are healthy”, which could be a misnomer as many dental issues are not clinically visible or noticeable by parents, such as Baby bottle tooth decay, dental caries, gum, and periodontal disease, a genetic disease which can lead to further complication as the child grows, and they take their child to the dentist only when a dental problem arises.[11] The first dental appointment occurring later in life has also been connected to cultural norms, financial limitations, dental professionals’ reluctance to treat young patients, and pediatricians’ and general dentists’ ignorance of the importance of infant oral health care in developing countries.[17] This is unlike the other similar study in China where the parent’s main reason for not visiting the dentist was COVID-19, which may be the major reason due to the period when the study was conducted.[18] Only 22% of them are planning to visit a dentist in the next 3 months, which shows a lack of awareness to the parents. Just 10.4% of parents were aware that tooth caries can be avoided by using preventative dental techniques including fluoride treatment and pit and fissure sealant, whereas 70% of parents had never heard of these techniques from their dentist.[19]

Birth order also plays an important role in planning to visit a dentist based on the article showing the relation between birth order and adult health. Breastfeeding firstborns are more common than second-born among mothers.[20] An identical pattern was also seen between the order of birth and adolescent dental visits.[21] Birth order may affect how much money is spent on children since many families have little to divide among their present children and because cumulative investments (such as healthcare costs) are lower for later-born children.[22] However, this may not be applicable in this study as only 14.42% have the reason that taking the child to the dentist will cost a lot of money. Nevertheless, no correlation was observed between the components of oral health awareness, perceived susceptibility to caries, reported severity of caries, perceived advantages and disadvantages of dental visits, and the intention to make dental appointments. We found that our results did not match the findings of a qualitative investigation on predisposing factors, which could be explained by the parents’ different educational experiences.

First, social support within the family has a significant role as a stress reducer for parents, which in turn reduces the need for pediatric healthcare.[2324] Second, family social support can help remove obstacles to children receiving dental care, hence enhancing access to dental care. Examples of this kind of support include accompanying mothers to their children’s dental appointments, assisting mothers in making appointment schedules, and offering educational counseling in terms of enabling factors.[25] Parents should be educated based on feeding habits and other preventive modalities and how early childhood caries can affect a child’s oral health. In a study conducted among graduating medical students from Kulasekaram, it is clear that 79.5% of respondents felt reducing the occurrence of ECC does not need counseling on feeding and weaning habits and 63% of those surveyed did not know when to book an infant’s first dental appointment.[26]

The current study’s findings clearly show that most parents still take their kids to the dentist only when necessary for treatment and not as a regular checkup. This is true even though dental treatment is readily available in Chennai, an urban city with a large concentration of corporate dental clinics, private practices, and dental schools.

Limitations and recommendations

The limitations of this study include the distribution of questionnaires in a single area that is not representative of the entire community, as well as the occupation, ethnicity, and household income of the participants, all of which may have an impact on their knowledge and attitudes regarding their first dental visit. Future research is advised in this study.

Public health campaigns can be conducted nationwide or locally using television, newspapers, and so on. Social media outreach through leverage platforms like Facebook and Instagram can be helpful by sharing engaging content such as short videos and testimonials and educating pediatricians and family doctors to encourage discussions about the first dental visit during routine child health checkups; conducting workshops for parents from daycares, preschools, and community centers; creating interactive apps or websites that educate parents about oral health milestones and send reminders for dental visits; organizing free dental checkup camps, seminars, and workshops in local communities, especially in underserved areas; and introducing oral health education as part of school health curriculums for teachers, parents, and children.

Conclusion

The results of this study offer some initial understanding of the factors that influence mother’s propensity to take their child for FDV. The World Health Organisation (WHO)[7] and the International Association of Paediatric Dentistry (IAPD)[27] all advise that a child’s first dental appointment should take place within 6 months of the child’s first tooth eruption or by the time the child is 1 year old. The results of the study, however, indicate that parental awareness and compliance with these recommendations are still below ideal levels. The study’s conclusions suggest that rather than waiting for an oral health issue to arise, parents should be informed about FDVs and encouraged to attend them freely. In the meantime, parents can more easily receive pediatric dental treatment when the family and health system assist.
