Risk factors and predictive model for postoperative pulmonary complications following pancreaticoduodenectomy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xiaohan Kong, Quan Yuan, Heng Wang, Meng Liu, Zheyu Niu, Jun Lu, XU Zhou, Huaqiang Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) can significantly reduce quality of life and may be life-threatening. While predictors of PPCs have been studied in various surgeries, specific data on pancreaticoduodenectomy is limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 338 patients who underwent pancreaticoduodenectomy for biliary, pancreatic, and duodenal tumors at our hospital from 2017 to 2023. We compared demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables between patients with and without PPCs within 30 days post-surgery. Independent risk factors were identified using multivariate logistic regression analysis, and a nomogram was developed to predict PPC risk. Model accuracy was validated with the concordance index (C-index) and calibration curves.ResultsOf the 338 patients, 149 (44.1%) developed PPCs. Significant variables associated with PPCs included age, smoking, alcohol consumption, type and duration of surgery, timing of nasogastric tube removal, tumor size, tumor location, and preoperative albumin levels. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified independent risk factors: type and duration of surgery, tumor location, maximum tumor diameter, and preoperative albumin levels. The nomogram incorporating these factors had a C-index of 0.749. The ROC curve showed an AUC of 0.75, indicating satisfactory predictive performance. Calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and clinical impact curves confirmed the model's validity and reliability.ConclusionType and duration of surgery, tumor location, maximum tumor diameter, and preoperative albumin levels are independent risk factors for PPCs after pancreaticoduodenectomy. We developed a new nomogram to predict PPCs in these patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-025-03681-0.Keywords: Pancreaticoduodenectomy, Postoperative pulmonary complications, Risk factors, Nomogram

Introduction

Pancreaticoduodenectomy is one of the most challenging abdominal surgeries and remains the standard treatment for tumors of the pancreatic head and periampullary region, such as those in the ampulla of Vater, duodenal papilla, distal common bile duct, pancreatic duct opening, and the medial wall of the duodenum. This surgery can be performed as either open pancreaticoduodenectomy (OPD) or laparoscopic pancreaticoduodenectomy (LPD) [1, 2].

In patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy for tumors, postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) significantly affect morbidity and mortality. The oxygen supply to tissues is crucial for surgical wound healing, and any impairment increases hospital morbidity, prolongs hospital stays, and adds extra medical costs [3–5].

A previous observational cohort study indicated that C-reactive protein (CRP) levels are significantly associated with complications following major abdominal surgery [6]. According to earlier research, gender and BMI are risk factors for PPCs after pylorus-preserving pancreaticoduodenectomy [7]. Another study suggested that preoperative albumin levels could predict PPCs after elective laparoscopic gastrectomy [8].

However, there is limited information on the risk factors for various PPCs following pancreaticoduodenectomy. Therefore, we decided to evaluate potential risk factors for PPCs in patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy. Based on perioperative variables, we developed a predictive model for PPCs after pancreaticoduodenectomy.

Considering the user-friendliness and high accuracy of nomograms, we converted the resulting model into a nomogram, which can serve as a clinical tool for anesthesiologists and surgeons in perioperative management. To our knowledge, this is the first nomogram assessing the risk of PPCs in patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy.

Patients

This retrospective, single-center cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital. It included 370 patients diagnosed with biliopancreatic or duodenal tumors who underwent pancreaticoduodenectomy at Shandong Provincial Hospital between May 2017 and November 2023. Patients with benign conditions and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors were excluded because they were part of a separate independent study, and relevant data were not collected for the current analysis. Additionally, patients with incomplete medical records were excluded. The occurrence of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) was documented through a detailed review of patient medical records. Data collection adhered strictly to the protocol approved by the Ethics Committee, ensuring data completeness and accuracy.

Data collection

Demographic data (age, gender, body mass index, etc.), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes), intraoperative tumor parameters (tumor size, location, etc.), detailed surgical types (laparoscopic or open), preoperative conditions (history of abdominal surgery, jaundice reduction procedures, nutritional tube placement), intraoperative variables (surgery duration, blood loss, etc.), postoperative complications, and outcomes were meticulously recorded. Preoperative forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1/FVC) and vital capacity (VC) were measured and recorded.

All pancreaticoduodenectomy procedures, including resection of related organs, anastomosis, and lymphadenectomy, were conducted according to international protocols [9, 10]. All postoperative adverse events were recorded following the previous classification by Dindo et al [11].

Definition of PPCs

Postoperative PPCs are defined as pneumonia, pleural effusion, or pulmonary embolism occurring within 30 days after pancreaticoduodenectomy. Pneumonia is defined based on PPCs’ signs and symptoms (shortness of breath, cough, chest pain, and fever) and confirmed through blood tests and CT scans [12, 13].

Pleural effusion: All types of pleural effusions, regardless of size or clinical symptoms, were included. Diagnosis was based on postoperative imaging findings (chest X-ray or CT scan).Pulmonary embolism: Defined as clinically confirmed cases diagnosed via imaging (CT pulmonary angiography [CTPA] or ventilation/perfusion [V/Q] scan). Incidental findings without symptoms or blood gas abnormalities were excluded.

All patients underwent routine postoperative CT scans to ensure consistent and accurate detection of PPCs.

Preoperative laboratory variables

Preoperative blood samples were collected from all enrolled patients in the morning for analysis of blood cells, biochemistry, and inflammatory cytokines. C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Statistical analysis

In our study, we used SPSS 27.0 for statistical analysis. Data are presented as numbers (percentages) or mean ± standard error (SE). Continuous variables were analyzed using Student's t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, while categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Factors with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate logistic regression analysis to explore their association with PPCs.

We used R version 4.2.2 for model construction and chart drawing. Factors with p<0.05 in univariate analysis that were clinically significant and did not show significant statistical correlation with other variables were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. The discriminatory performance of the predictive model was assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Calibration curves were used to compare the consistency between observed outcomes and predicted results. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to summarize the model's performance as a decision support tool for clinical use.Finally, the final model was converted into a nomogram based on the results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Regression coefficients (β) for each variable from the constructed multivariate logistic regression model were proportionally converted into scores ranging from 0 to 100. The scores corresponding to each variable were summed to obtain a total score, which was aligned with the predicted probability of PPCs for each patient.

Construction and use of the nomogram

We developed a nomogram to predict the risk of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) following pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD). The nomogram is based on a multivariable logistic regression model, which includes variables such as tumor diameter,, duration of surgery, and preoperative albumin levels. The purpose of this nomogram is to provide a simple and easy-to-use tool to assist clinicians in individual risk assessment and clinical decision-making support.

Risk factors associated with PPCs

The incidence of PPCs after pancreaticoduodenectomy was 44.1% (149/338). Among all PPCs, suspected lung infections were the most common complication. A detailed breakdown of complication types and their management approaches is provided in Table S1. Table 1 summarizes the Demographics and the intraoperative tumor parameters of patients with and without PPCs. Compared to patients without PPCs, those with PPCs were older (p = 0.028). In terms of preoperative history, patients with a history of smoking (p = 0.027) and alcohol consumption (p = 0.026) had a higher incidence of PPCs. Considering surgical variables, patients with PPCs had significantly longer surgery durations than those without PPCs (p = 0.017), and the incidence of PPCs was higher in open surgeries compared to laparoscopic surgeries (p = 0.006). Regarding intraoperative tumor parameters, the tumor diameter was significantly larger in the PPCs group than in the non-PPCs group (p<0.001), and the incidence of PPCs was significantly higher in non-pancreatic tumors compared to pancreatic tumors (p<0.001). Additionally, the nasogastric tube removal time was significantly longer in patients with PPCs compared to those without PPCs (p = 0.044). Other patient conditions showed no significant differences between the PPCs and non-PPCs groups (P > 0.05).

Multiple logistic regression analysis

Variables with p<0.05 in univariate analysis, that were clinically significant and did not show significant statistical correlation with other variables, were included in multivariate logistic regression analysis to assess potential risk factors for PPCs. Consequently, surgical type (OR: 2.14, 95% CI: 1.16–3.92, p = 0.014), surgery duration (OR: 1.003, 95% CI: 1.001–1.006, p = 0.013), tumor location (OR: 3.77, 95% CI: 2.13–6.69, p<0.001), tumor diameter (OR: 2.02, 95% CI: 1.51–2.71, p<0.001), and preoperative albumin level (OR: 0.92, 95% CI: 0.86–0.99, p = 0.024) were identified as independent risk factors for PPCs, as shown in Table 3. These findings are visually represented in a forest plot (Fig. 1).

Nomogram construction - clinical predictive model

The five significant risk factors identified through multivariate logistic regression analysis were used to construct a nomogram model to predict the risk of PPCs after pancreaticoduodenectomy, allowing for the calculation of each patient’s predicted risk score (Fig 2). The nomogram shows that tumor diameter has the greatest impact on PPCs, with an increase in tumor diameter correlating with a higher incidence of PPCs. Surgical method, surgery duration, tumor location, and albumin levels also significantly influence the occurrence of PPCs. Laparoscopic surgery, shorter surgery duration, pancreatic tumor location, and higher albumin levels contribute to a reduced risk of PPCs. In clinical applications, the total score for each patient can be calculated based on the scores corresponding to the indicators in the nomogram, predicting the patient's risk of PPCs.

Nomogram for predicting PPCs after pancreaticoduodenectomy

To guide surgical decision-making and evaluate patients who may benefit from enhanced monitoring or rehabilitation, separate preoperative and postoperative models were considered. However, as only one significant preoperative factor (albumin level) was identified, a standalone preoperative model was not constructed. A postoperative model was developed and validated, with detailed results provided in the supplementary material (Fig. S2, S3, S4 and Table S3).

Nomogram model validation

The AUC of the nomogram model was 0.75, and its accuracy was evaluated through calibration curves, ROC curves, and DCA analysis. The cutoff value for the ROC curve was determined using the Youden Index, optimizing the balance between sensitivity and specificity.The calibration curve shows good consistency between predicted and actual values across the range (Fig. 3), with a slope of 0.958 and an intercept of 0.026, both statistically significant (p < 0.05). The high goodness-of-fit (R² = 0.975) further supports the model's strong calibration performance. These results indicate that the nomogram model has excellent predictive value for the risk of PPCs.The ROC curve's AUC of 0.75 suggests high accuracy of the predictive model (Fig. 4). As shown in Fig. 5, the DCA curve (left panel) demonstrates that the red curve has a net benefit across different threshold ranges, further confirming the model's effectiveness in predicting PPC risk. The CIC curve (right panel) illustrates the relationship between the number of patients predicted to be at high risk and the actual number of high-risk PPC patients at different thresholds, further validating the model’s predictive accuracy.

Discussion

Through univariate analysis, we identified age, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, surgery duration, surgical type, tumor diameter, tumor location, preoperative albumin level, and nasogastric tube removal time as risk factors for postoperative PPCs. Further multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that surgery duration, surgical type, tumor diameter, tumor location, and preoperative albumin level were independent risk factors for PPCs.The nomogram showed that tumor diameter had the highest correlation with the occurrence of postoperative pulmonary complications. This could be due to multiple combined factors. Larger tumors make the surgery more complex, prolonging the duration of surgery and anesthesia, increasing intraoperative injury, and complicating postoperative recovery. Larger tumors may compress the gastrointestinal and biliopancreatic ducts, affecting the patient’s digestive system function, leading to poorer overall health and nutritional status, which may weaken the immune system, increase the risk of infections, including pulmonary infections, and hinder postoperative recovery. The increased tumor size may also directly compress the lungs, affecting lung function recovery. These factors collectively make tumor diameter a significant factor influencing the occurrence of postoperative pulmonary complications. This is consistent with previous studies [14–16].

Subsequent C-index, calibration curve, ROC curve, DCA, and CIC analyses of the nomogram further confirmed the model’s effectiveness.

The analysis by Chun Kevin Yang et al. found that prolonged surgery duration is an independent risk factor for PPCs after major abdominal surgery [17]. Another prospective cohort study also indicated that prolonged anesthesia duration is an independent risk factor for pulmonary complications after non-thoracic surgery [18], consistent with our findings. Extended surgery duration correlates with prolonged respiratory system suppression and physical stress, increasing the impact on the diaphragm and thoracoabdominal muscles, thereby reducing postoperative respiratory function and increasing the risk of pulmonary complications.

Antoniou S. A. and Matsuo Y. suggested that laparoscopic surgery has the following advantages: smaller incisions and less trauma, less postoperative pain, faster lung function recovery, reduced intraoperative blood loss and transfusion requirements, less diaphragmatic and thoracic interference, and better diaphragmatic function [19, 20]. These factors reduce the incidence of PPCs. Our study also indicated that laparoscopic pancreaticoduodenectomy significantly lowers the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications compared to open surgery.

Current research lacks studies on the impact of tumor location before pancreaticoduodenectomy on postoperative PPCs. Previous research found that liver tumors in segments 7/8 are independent risk factors for PPCs after laparoscopic liver resection (LLR) [21]. For spinal tumors, the incidence of PPCs is higher in patients with thoracic tumors than in those with lumbar tumors [22]. Our study found that when the tumor is located in the pancreas, the incidence of postoperative PPCs is significantly lower compared to tumors outside the pancreas. This may be because pancreatic tumors are deeper and further from the diaphragm, resulting in less preoperative and intraoperative impact on the diaphragm. Additionally, patients with distal bile duct and duodenal ampullary tumors have a higher incidence of preoperative jaundice and poorer overall health, which may also influence the occurrence of postoperative PPCs. Higher levels of preoperative jaundice can lead to impaired liver function and immunosuppression, increasing the risk of postoperative infections. Poorer overall health can slow postoperative recovery, increasing the incidence of pulmonary complications. Furthermore, bile duct and duodenal ampullary tumors can cause bile stasis and bacterial translocation, increasing the risk of infection and inflammatory responses, all of which can negatively impact postoperative lung function recovery.

A systematic review by the American College of Physicians concluded that serum albumin levels below 30 g/L are significant predictors of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) after non-cardiothoracic surgery [23]. Jeong, B.-H et al. retrospectively analyzed clinical data from 2059 patients and developed a new PPCs estimation scoring system, identifying albumin as an independent risk factor [24]. Other studies also found that preoperative albumin levels are independent risk factors for PPCs after pylorus-preserving pancreaticoduodenectomy and laparoscopic gastrectomy [7, 8], consistent with our findings. We believe that low preoperative albumin levels indicate poor nutritional status, impaired immune function, poor wound healing, and fluid imbalance, making patients more prone to postoperative pulmonary complications.

Postoperative complications, such as pancreatic fistula, bile leakage, and intra-abdominal infection, were significantly more common in patients with PPCs. These complications may contribute to the development of PPCs through mechanisms such as increased systemic inflammation, impaired diaphragmatic function, or prolonged immobilization due to extended recovery times. Effective management of these systemic complications might reduce the risk of PPCs and improve overall patient outcomes.

DCA and CIC indicate that most patients can benefit from using this nomogram to predict PPCs. Currently, there are no other models that can predict PPCs after pancreaticoduodenectomy. Decision curve and impact curve analyses demonstrate that the created nomogram is superior to default strategies. This nomogram can be used in clinical settings to provide individual risk predictions and assist clinicians in making intervention recommendations.

This study successfully developed a simple and reliable predictive model for PPCs following pancreaticoduodenectomy. The nomogram includes modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors. For non-modifiable risk factors (tumor location, tumor size), we emphasize close monitoring and early intervention. For modifiable risk factors (surgical method, surgery duration, preoperative albumin levels), we suggest establishing a multidisciplinary collaborative strategy to improve patients' postoperative pulmonary complications.

By developing and applying a nomogram, we can better predict the risk of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) following pancreaticoduodenectomy using easily assessed perioperative variables. This not only helps clinicians perform individualized risk assessments for patients before surgery, allowing for precise preoperative preparation and perioperative management plans, but also provides crucial support for clinical decision-making. The nomogram can help identify high-risk patients, optimize resource allocation, and ensure these patients receive closer monitoring and care after surgery (such as nebulization therapy, antibiotic therapy, and enhanced recovery pathways) to improve postoperative outcomes. This, in turn, can enhance surgical outcomes and reduce overall medical costs. Additionally, the nomogram can serve as a communication tool with patients and their families, helping them understand the surgical risks and potential prognosis, and enhancing their engagement and cooperation in the postoperative recovery process. Therefore, as the importance of preventing PPCs in major surgeries continues to grow, our study on the nomogram for PPCs following pancreaticoduodenectomy is highly significant.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, as a retrospective study, there may be inherent biases in the collected data. Secondly, although the nomogram demonstrated good predictive performance, its reliability needs to be further confirmed through prospective studies. Additionally, the data collection period spanned from 2017 to 2023, during which advancements in surgical techniques and perioperative care might have influenced the prevalence of PPCs. Another limitation is the lack of recorded timing for postoperative complications, which prevents determining causal relationships between systemic complications, such as POPF, and PPCs. Without temporal data, it remains unclear whether POPF contributes to PPCs or is secondary to them. Prospective studies with detailed temporal data are needed to clarify these interactions.Furthermore, there is the possibility of unknown confounding factors that were not accounted for in the analysis. Lastly, as this study was conducted in a single center, external validation using data from other centers is crucial to confirm the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

We identified the risk factors for pulmonary complications following pancreaticoduodenectomy and developed a new nomogram for predicting PPCs. Using this tool, clinicians can optimize resource allocation and enhance shared decision-making.
