Outcomes associated with an inpatient addiction consult service: Systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Tina E Abraham, Abdul Qadeer, Madeline H Meier, Areeba Fareed, Daniel L Roberts, Khalid Benkhadra, Gretchen A Colbenson, Amy E Kole, Jamie J Coracides, Umesh M Sharma, Samuel R Choulet, Robert W Kirchoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with substance use disorder (SUD) have high rates of hospitalization, readmission, and prolonged lengths of stay.ObjectivesTo conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis of the impact of addiction consult services in the inpatient setting for patients with SUD.MethodsFive databases were systematically searched from their earliest available records through January 2025. Two authors independently screened articles, extracted data, and assessed the quality of each study with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for observational studies and the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized clinical trials. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated for dichotomous outcomes, and mean differences were calculated for continuous outcomes.ResultsThe search retrieved 20 studies that met inclusion criteria. The intervention for all studies was an addiction medicine consult, but the definition of this consult varied. The meta-analysis did not detect statistically significant differences in hospital length of stay, hospital readmission, or emergency department visits at 1 year among patients who received the consult compared with those who did not; however, many of these outcomes were likely underpowered. However, addiction medicine consults significantly improved the odds of inpatient initiation of medication for SUD (OR, 7.30; 95% CI, 3.30-16.13; P<.001) and postdischarge SUD treatment linkage and adherence (OR, 3.31; 95% CI, 2.06-5.32; P<.001).ConclusionInpatient addiction consult services are associated with increased inpatient initiation of medication for SUD treatment and postdischarge treatment linkage and adherence. No clear association was identified between the consult and readmissions, emergency department utilization, or length of stay.Keywords: alcohol use disorder, inpatient addiction medicine consult, opioid use disorder, substance use disorder, substance use treatment

Introduction

In 2024, approximately 48.4 million people in the United States aged 12 years or older had a substance use disorder (SUD), of whom only about 19.3% received substance use treatment. 1 People with SUD have high rates of hospitalization, readmission, and prolonged lengths of stay. 2 Hospitalization, however, may be an ideal time to initiate addiction treatment because it provides an opportunity to coordinate addiction care. 2 An addiction consult service (ACS)—a multidisciplinary team often including a physician, social worker, nurse, and recovery coach—provides specialized treatment for SUDs in the inpatient setting, such as initiation of medication treatment, linkage to outpatient clinics and resources, and patient counseling. 3 ACSs were introduced to expedite the provision of evidence-based addiction treatment to inpatients. 4

As of May 2025, 3 previous reviews on this topic aimed to investigate outcomes associated with inpatient ACSs.5–7 In a systematic review by Bahji et al., 5 some studies showed reduced postdischarge addiction severity and improved initiation of addiction treatment after ACS engagement, although too few high-quality studies were available to reach strong conclusions. A scoping review by Rodger et al. 6 focused on the effects of ACS on pharmacotherapy, health care utilization, and outpatient follow-up. ACS appeared to improve the rates of pharmacotherapy use but had inconsistent effects on health care utilization, and overall follow-up rates were low. The review by Danovitch et al. 7 reported that the majority of studies showed positive outcomes associated with the ACS, including medication initiation, linkage to postdischarge care, and utilization outcomes. Since these literature searches were conducted, several high-quality studies have been published, and an updated review that incorporates these new studies is needed.

None of the previously published reviews on this topic have performed a meta- analysis to quantitatively assess outcomes across studies. The purpose of the current study was to address this gap by conducting a systematic review and meta- analysis of the extant literature on ACS outcomes, incorporating several new studies that have been published since the previous reviews were conducted. Here, we report on the association between ACSs and inpatient initiation of medications for SUD, postdischarge adherence to outpatient SUD treatment, readmissions, subsequent emergency department (ED) visits, and hospital length of stay (LOS).

Methods

Data sources and search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed (1946-June 2023), Embase (1947-January 2025), Scopus (2004-January 2025), Web of Science (1900-January 2025), and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and Controlled Trials (from earliest available records to January 2025). The search strategy used a combination of relevant terms and keywords (Supplemental Table 1). This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023432986), and the research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study selection

We included studies that reported quantitative data on the effects of inpatient ACS for adults (age ≥18 years) with SUDs and also included a control group. Studies were excluded if they lacked a control group; were qualitative; involved outpatient, psychiatric, or ED settings; or included patients younger than 18 years.

Covidence, a web-based systematic review management and collaboration software platform, was used to complete screening at each stage. 8 The abstract of each study identified was assessed for eligibility by 2 independent reviewers . Studies deemed eligible underwent independent full-text review by 2 authors . A third author resolved conflicts at both stages when necessary.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two authors independently extracted the data from each included study. Data extraction included study dates, setting, study design, population characteristics, intervention, and results. A third author (R.W.K.) resolved conflicts.

The quality of the studies was assessed with the Newcastle-Ottawa quality assessment scale for cohort and case-control studies 9 and the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized clinical trials (RCTs). 10 Two authors (T.E.A. and M.H.M.) independently completed the quality assessments, and disagreements were resolved by team consensus .

We assessed the following outcomes: hospital readmission within 30 days, 90 days, 6 to 12 months, and 1 year; ED visits within 30 days, 3 to 6 months, and 1 year; hospital LOS; inpatient treatment of SUDs; and postdischarge SUD treatment linkage and adherence. Studies that discussed expected outcomes but did not allow for pooled analysis were described qualitatively.

Data analysis

The meta-analysis was conducted with RevMan (version 5.4; Copenhagen: The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014). Pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated for dichotomous outcomes by using a random- effects model; mean difference was calculated for continuous outcomes with Hedges g with an inverse variance method. P values <.05 were considered statistically significant. Funnel plots were used to assess publication bias.

Results

Literature search and study characteristics

The search identified 943 articles; 599 studies underwent primary screening, 105 articles underwent secondary screening, and 20 studies were included for data extraction (Figure 1).3,11–29 Four of the 20 studies were RCTs13,19,22,26 and the others were observational studies (Table 1). Studies featured cross-sectional designs and retrospective or prospective cohort studies. Most studies (n=18) were conducted in the US, and the study periods ranged from 1995 to 2024. Of the 20 studies, 15 reported on the outcomes that were the focus of the meta-analysis.3,11–13,15,18–23,25,26,28,29 The remaining 5 studies were not included in the meta-analysis.

Quality assessment

For the 16 nonrandomized studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa scale indicated that 3 were poor quality and 13 were good quality studies (Supplemental Table 2). The 4 RCTs13,19,22,26 were rated across 5 domains with the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool; 2 had a low risk of bias, and 2 had a moderate risk of bias (Supplemental Table 3). The risk of publication bias is shown in the Supplemental Figure; funnel plot asymmetry was not assessed because most of the meta-analyzed outcomes contained fewer than 10 studies, per recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews and Meta- analyses. 30

Primary outcomes

30-Day hospital readmissions

Of the 20 studies, 9 focused on 30-day hospital readmission as an outcome of interest, and 6 allowed for pooled analysis,3,13,15,18,20,28 which indicated non-significantly decreased odds of hospital readmission among the experimental group compared with controls (OR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.56-1.48; P=.71, I2=76%) (Figure 2(a)).

Forest plots for the effect of addiction consult services (ACS) on hospital readmissions. (a) 30-Day readmissions; (b) 90-Day readmissions; (c) 6- to 12-Month readmissions; (d) 1-Year readmissions.

Among the other 3 studies, Wakeman et al. 27 reported that patients who received ACS had a greater decrease in hospitalizations related to substance use at 30 days post discharge than those who received usual care (61% decrease vs 51% decrease; P=.001), and Kast et al. 16 reported similar results (average readmissions, 0.19 [ACS] vs 0.30 [no ACS]; adjusted OR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.56-0.76). Lambert et al. 17 showed that ACS was associated with a reduced risk of readmission within 30 days post discharge (adjusted incidence rate ratio, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.75-0.89).

90-Day hospital readmissions

Among 20 analyzed studies, 4 focused on 90-day hospital readmissions. For the 3 studies that allowed for pooled analysis,18,20,22 the pooled results indicated no effect of ACS use (OR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.21-1.45; P=.23, I2=40%) (Figure 2(b)). In the other study, Kast et al. 16 reported that patients who were seen by an ACS in the hospital were less likely to be readmitted. Within 90 days, these patients had an average of 0.43 readmissions, compared with 0.65 for patients who did not receive a consultation (adjusted OR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.59-0.76).

6- to 12-Month hospital readmissions

Among the 20 studies examined, 4 addressed readmissions within 6 to 12 months after discharge as an outcome of interest and allowed for pooled analysis11,13,18,28. The combined findings from these studies showed no effect of ACS on hospital readmissions within the 6- to 12-month time frame (OR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.68-1.00; P=.05, I2=6%) (Figure 2(c)).

1-Year hospital readmissions

Of the 20 studies, 8 reported on 1-year hospital readmissions as an outcome of interest.3,11,13,18,20,22,28,29 These studies, encompassing 10,089 participants, were included in the meta-analysis. The synthesized findings indicated nonsignificantly decreased odds of hospital readmission for patients receiving ACS compared with the control group (OR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.77-1.13; P=.47, I2=40%) (Figure 2(d)).

30-Day ED utilization

Seven of the 20 studies analyzed addressed ED utilization within 30 days. Five studies had sufficient data for pooled analysis,3,15,20,25,29 which showed no effect of ACS on ED utilization within 30 days after hospital discharge (OR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.84-1.08; P=.46, I2=0%) (Figure 3(a)). In contrast, Wakeman et al. 27 reported a significantly greater decrease in substance-related ED visits for patients seen by ACS than controls (66% decrease vs 53% decrease; P=.002), and Kast et al. 16 reported lower odds of ED visits 30 days post discharge with ACS (adjusted OR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.67- 0.92).

Forest plots for the effect of addiction consult services (ACS) on emergency department (ED) utilization. (a) 30-Day ED visits; (b) 1-Year ED visits.

3- to 6-Month ED utilization

Of the 20 studies, 2 analyzed 3- to 6-month ED utilization, but pooled analysis was not possible. In the study by Gryczynski et al., 13 the intervention group had a lower incidence rate of ED visits than did the treatment as usual (control) group at both the 3-month and 6-month postdischarge time points. Kast et al. 16 reported lower odds of ED visits 90 days post discharge with ACS (adjusted OR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.69-0.89).

1-Year ED utilization

Overall, 5 studies,3,13,20,25,29 with a combined total of 6,190 participants, provided data on ED visits within 1 year post discharge and were included in the meta-analysis. The collective findings showed no significant reduction in the odds of ED visits among patients receiving ACS compared with controls (OR, 0.92; 95% CI,0.79-1.09; P=.33, I2=21%) (Figure 3(b)).

Secondary outcomes

Hospital LOS

Among the 20 studies reviewed, 7 examined LOS during initial hospitalization. Four of these studies, which reported mean (SD) LOS, were analyzed11,19,20,28 and showed no significant effect of ACS on LOS (mean difference, 1.32; 95% CI, −0.67 to 3.32; P=.19, I2=62%) (Figure 4(a)). The 3 other studies were not included in the meta-analysis because they did not report SD, but these showed varying effects of ACS on hospital LOS. Wilson et al. 29 reported an average treatment effect indicating a longer LOS by 2.39 days for the treatment group (P<.001). In the studies of Thompson et al. 24 and Tran et al., 25 patients underwent consultation from the Substance Use Intervention Team (SUIT) service. Thompson et al. 24 reported that hospitalized patients with a SUD who received a SUIT consultation had a shorter LOS than those who did not (5.77 vs 6.54 days; P<.01). Tran et al. 25 reported a notable decrease in the mean LOS for patients with SUD diagnosis after receiving a SUIT consultation, from 6.31 days without SUIT consult to 5.29 days with SUIT consult.

Forest plots for the effect of addiction consult services (ACS) on various measures. (a) Hospital length of stay; (b) Inpatient initiation of medication for substance use disorders; (c) Postdischarge substance use disorder treatment linkage and adherence.

Inpatient initiation of medication for SUDs

Of the 20 studies, 7 identified inpatient initiation of medication for SUDs as an outcome of interest. The pooled results of 5 studies3,15,20,22,23 indicated significantly increased odds of inpatient SUD treatment among the experimental group compared with the control group (OR, 7.30; 95% CI, 3.30-16.13; P<.001, I2=83%) (Figure 4(b)). In the study by Kast et al., 16 ACS was associated with increased initiation of medications for opioid use disorder compared with controls (adjusted OR, 5.07; 95% CI, 4.41-5.82). Lambert et al. 17 reported that ACS was associated with higher rates of medication receipt during admission (medications for opioid use disorder, 84% vs 49% [P<.001]; medications for alcohol use disorder, 33.4% vs 6.0% [P<.001]).

Postdischarge SUD treatment linkage and adherence

Among the 20 studies examined, 10 reported on the effect of ACS on linkage to SUD treatment post discharge and its associated outcomes. For the 9 studies included in the pooled analysis,3,12,13,15,18,19,21,22,26 results indicated significantly increased odds of treatment linkage and adherence among the experimental group compared with the control group (OR, 3.31; 95% CI, 2.06-5.32; P<.001, I2=81%) (Figure 4(c)). In the other paper, Hillman et al. 14 reported that patients who were treated by a specialist alcohol liaison service had significantly higher treatment completion rates than those in the control group (88% vs 40%; P<.001). Overall, 44 of 50 patients in the intervention completed rehabilitation, compared with 31 of 78 in the control group.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis assessed the association between ACS and clinical outcomes in patients with SUD. Ultimately, 20 unique studies met inclusion criteria and assessed varied outcomes of ACS implementation, including hospital readmission at multiple time points, ED visits within various times post discharge, LOS, inpatient initiation of medication for SUD, and postdischarge SUD treatment linkage and adherence. The intervention in all studies was an ACS, but the service was referred to by different names and defined variably across studies. All studies used a control group, but only 4 studies were RCTs.13,19,22,26 Among studies eligible for pooled results, ACS consult was associated with significant increases in inpatient initiation of SUD treatment and postdischarge SUD treatment linkage and adherence.We found reductions in 30-day and 1-year hospital readmissions for patients receiving addiction consultation services, although this was not statistically significant. Similarly, there were no significant effects on 90-day or 6-12-month readmissions, and emergency department utilization was unaffected by ACS at any time point. Although these findings suggest a limited impact on healthcare utilization, the services provided by ACS seem vital in encouraging patients to seek treatment.

Our results provide an update on previous work and augment the extant literature on patient outcomes associated with ACS. Danovitch et al. found that the use of ACSs was correspondingly linked to better clinical and health-system outcomes such as greater initiation of medications to manage SUD, especially opioid use disorder, and better connection to the outpatient addiction treatment after discharge, and a corresponding decrease in the number of hospital readmissions and care continuity. 31 Likewise, French et al. also showed that hospital-based addiction medicine consult services had continually higher rates of in-hospital initiation of MOUD and enhanced post-discharge follow-up treatment initiation, with more inconsistent effects on other outcomes. 32 As noted by Bahji et al., in many cases the inpatient ACS programs focused on the pharmacologic interventions, which were frequently supported by psychotherapy and discharge planning. They concluded that there were improvements in post-discharge engagement, together with lesser levels of addiction in a sub-group of controlled studies, but also observed that comparator groups were missing in the majority of the literature. 33 Moreover, Rostam '-Abadi et al. evaluated the CATCH addiction consult service, an interprofessional program providing comprehensive care and linkage to treatment for patients with SUD, and found a significant reduction in ED visits, with no apparent impact on hospital admissions or mortality. 34 All these findings suggest that ACSs can be particularly effective in preventing acute-care utilization and facilitating the transition into outpatient care, although these effects on the long-term outcomes cannot be established yet. On the contrary, the current review found inconsistencies or insignificant relationships between various outcomes such as hospital readmissions, ED utilization, and LOS towards ACS implementation. Such findings are probably the consequence of patient cohort heterogeneity, ACS design, and follow-up protocols as they hinder the comparability between studies. Incomplete follow-up data in a number of studies also complicated accurate evaluation of readmissions and ED visit. In addition, LOS can be prone to many confounders, including co-occurring medical conditions, social determinants of health, and institutional discharge procedures. Barker et al. 11 also noted that providing intervention for SUDs may increase the LOS in some cases. These complexities present a challenge in establishing the effect of ACS on hospital LOS.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths, including a comprehensive search strategy, robust statistical methods, and rigorous quality assessment. However, several limitations warrant careful consideration. ACS were variably defined across studies, with differences in intervention components (counseling, MOUD initiation, follow-up care), staffing models (physicians, nurses, social workers, peer counselors), target populations (opioid use disorder vs broader substance use disorders), and follow-up protocols (single-hospital vs multi-hospital, varying durations). Most included studies were observational, which increases the risk of bias and limits causal inference, while study designs and outcome measurements differed, including variability in the capture of readmissions and ED visits and in definitions of outcomes (e.g., 30-day vs any readmission). The inclusion of a pilot RCT 22 reduces the ability to draw conclusions regarding effectiveness due to its focus on feasibility. Additionally, interventions based on SBIRT 26 are conceptually different from ACS and show low adjusted effects, which reduces comparability. The Gryczynski study also increases heterogeneity because it represents a patient navigation intervention, NavSTAR, that is different from a typical ACS. 13 Another limitation is that several studies utilized data only from a single hospital, resulting in the readmissions or follow-up care at other hospitals to not have been reported. These sources of heterogeneity affect the comparability of results and limit generalizability. Future studies should prioritize standardized ACS definitions, stronger study designs, and the use of claims-based or cross-system data to more accurately capture utilization outcomes across health systems. One included study, McNeely et al., employed a stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial with randomization at the hospital level rather than the patient level. 19 This design requires adjustment for temporal trends and intracluster correlation, as outcomes for patients within the same hospital are correlated. The McNeely study appropriately used mixed-effects models to account for these design features. When pooling cluster trials with individually randomized trials, readers should recognize that pooled estimates combine different levels of randomization and may reflect both patient-level and hospital-level treatment effects.

Additionally, many of the longer-term utilization outcomes (e.g., 6- to 12-month readmissions, ED visits) were secondary endpoints in the original studies and likely underpowered to detect clinically meaningful differences. The absence of statistically significant effects on these outcomes should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect; rather, these null findings may reflect insufficient statistical power rather than true lack of efficacy. Future trials adequately powered for utilization outcomes are needed to clarify the impact of ACS on longer-term healthcare utilization.

Furthermore, the potential for publication bias, reliance on self-reported measures, and varying quality of included studies pose challenges in synthesizing the evidence. Moreover, the absence of statistically significant effects on utilization outcomes should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect, as many of these were secondary endpoints with insufficient power to detect meaningful differences. In the last, the use of ‘substance use disorder’ as an umbrella term may overgeneralize findings, as several included studies focused primarily on opioid use disorder (e.g., Jakubowski et al., Kast et al., McNeely et al., Nordeck et al.) or alcohol use disorder (e.g., Singh-Tan et al., Hillman et al., Tryggedsson et al.). The effectiveness of ACS may vary by substance type, and future research should examine whether outcomes differ across specific SUDs.

As research on addiction consult services continues to evolve, additional randomized studies have been published since completion of the January 2025 search update. Future systematic reviews incorporating these newly published studies may help further clarify differences across intervention models and strengthen the overall evidence base.

Implications for practice

The findings of this review highlight the importance of implementing ACS in hospital settings to improve the treatment and management of SUDs. ACS can have a vital role in initiating treatment during hospitalization and ensuring continuity of care post discharge. Health care providers and policymakers should consider integrating ACS into routine hospital care to address the growing burden of SUDs with the goal of improving patient outcomes.

Conclusion

Inpatient ACSs can improve outcomes for patients with SUDs. Our study did not detect statistically significant effects on readmission, ED utilization, or hospital LOS; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given that many utilization outcomes were secondary endpoints and likely underpowered to detect clinically meaningful differences. However, the findings from this review and partial meta-analysis highlight the effectiveness of ACS in increasing the treatment of SUDs and improving postdischarge outcomes. Given the high prevalence and low treatment rate of SUDs, our findings suggest that ACS is a promising approach to addressing these issues. ACS provides specialized treatment of SUDs in the inpatient setting and connects patients to community resources, thereby improving access to specialized care.
