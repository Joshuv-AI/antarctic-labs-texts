Focal epidural hematoma occluding the sigmoid sinus evacuated through a single burr hole in a pediatric patient: Illustrative case and review of the literature
Authors: Andreas Theofanopoulos, Andreas Iliadis, Petros Zampakis, Constantine Constantoyannis, Sotirios Raftopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Venous sinus occlusion is a rare complication of head trauma, carrying increased morbidity and mortality. Sinus occlusion is usually a result of intraluminal thrombosis, with rare cases attributed to external pressure from depressed fractures and extensive hematomas.Case Description: We present a 13-year-old female who developed intractable life-threatening intracranial hypertension with signs of impending uncal herniation 48 h after pediatric intensive care unit admission due to head trauma. This was attributed to occlusion of the right dominant sigmoid sinus, caused by a focal traumatic epidural hematoma (EDH) contained entirely within the sigmoid sinus-transverse sinus groove and causing no mass effect otherwise. The extraluminal location of the EDH was ascertained in magnetic resonance imaging. Emergency burr-hole placement over the point of the sigmoid sinus occlusion achieved immediate intracranial pressure (ICP) normalization and reconstitution of sigmoid sinus flow, evidenced by intraoperative Doppler ultrasonography. The patient was reoperated on 24 h later due to re-elevation of ICP for cranial extension of the burr hole to evacuate EDH remnants over the transverse sinus. Afterward, anticoagulation was instituted due to a small thrombus inside the sigmoid sinus, attributable to the preceding sinus occlusion. The patient made a full neurologic recovery. A systematic review of the relevant literature is also presented.Conclusion: Dominant sigmoid sinus occlusion can cause life-threatening intracranial hypertension due to venous stasis. A focal epidural hematoma over the sinus can be safely evacuated with a burr hole over the point of maximal compression, allowing for enough reconstitution of flow within the sinus to alleviate intracranial hypertension.

INTRODUCTION

Cerebral venous sinus occlusion after head trauma is an increasingly recognized condition, with a prevalence of 4% of all traumatic brain injury patients. It is especially frequent after skull fractures overlying the venous sinuses, with an estimated prevalence of up to 26.2% in this subset of patients.[1] The most common cause is intraluminal thrombosis, which is usually managed similarly to nontraumatic cerebral venous sinus thrombosis with therapeutic anticoagulation. External pressure caused by an epidural hematoma is a relatively rare cause of sinus occlusion and necessitates a radically different treatment approach centered on hematoma evacuation for restoration of sinus patency.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 13-year-old female with no medical history was admitted due to an isolated head injury after falling from a height of approximately 3.5 meters (fall from the top of a tank truck). On arrival at the emergency department, the patient was comatose with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 8/15 (E1V2M5), with pupils equal and reacting to light. The patient was intubated and admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit, where an intracranial pressure (ICP) monitor was placed. The patient developed intractable life-threatening intracranial hypertension (up to 45 mmHg) with signs of impending uncal herniation (anisocoria with mydriasis and loss of pupillary light reflex on the right side) 48 h after her admission. Based on an emergency computed tomography (CT) scan demonstrating hyperintensity in the area of the right sigmoid sinus, the ICP rise was attributed to occlusion of the right sigmoid sinus (which was dominant, with the left transverse sinus side being hypoplastic). The sinus occlusion was caused by a focal traumatic epidural hematoma (EDH) with an estimated volume of 2.5 cm3, contained entirely within the sigmoid sinus-transverse sinus groove and causing no mass effect otherwise. The extraluminal location of the EDH was ascertained in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [Figure 1].

Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging demonstrating epidural thrombus (arrows) compressing and occluding the right sigmoid sinus with cessation of intraluminal flow. (a-c) T1 Magnetic Resonance Angiography (T1 MRA) postcontrast axial view, (d) T1 MRA postcontrast coronal view.

The patient was transferred emergently to the operating room where she was positioned in a left lateral park-bench position. A 3 cm retrosigmoid linear incision was performed, the bony landmarks were recognized, and a burr hole was placed over the estimated point of maximal hematoma thickness, assisted by MRI neuronavigation. Epidural hematoma evacuation through the burr hole achieved immediate ICP normalization and reconstitution of patency of the sigmoid sinus with antegrade flow, evidenced by intraoperative Doppler ultrasonography. Postoperative CT/CT venography demonstrates partial reconstitution of right sigmoid sinus patency, with narrowing remaining over the transverse sigmoid junction [Figure 2a-c].

Postoperative computed tomography (CT)-CT venography (CTV) demonstrating partial reconstitution of flow within the right sigmoid sinus (arrows) – (a) CTV axial view, (b) CTV coronal view). (c) 3D-reconstruction demonstrating burr-hole location (arrow). (d) Intraluminal thrombus in the sigmoid-jugular junction (arrow).

The patient underwent a reoperation 24 h later due to re-elevation of ICP (up to 25 mmHg). Cranial extension of the burr hole was performed to evacuate EDH remnants over the transverse-sigmoid junction and transverse sinus. Therapeutic anticoagulation with enoxaparin was instituted 24 h later due to a small thrombus inside the sigmoid sinus, attributable to the preceding sinus occlusion [Figure 2d].

MRI/magnetic resonance venography (MRV) imaging 1 week postoperatively demonstrated adequate sinus patency and recanalization with resolution of intraluminal hematoma [Figure 3]. The patient was extubated 3 days after the second operation without neurologic deficits; she went on to make a full recovery and returned to normal activities.

Systematic literature review

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted through the PubMed database in September 2025, using the following terms: (Epidural hematoma) OR (EDH) AND (venous sinus) AND (occlusion).

RESULTS

The database search yielded a total of 7 studies. After removing articles written in languages other than English, 5 articles remained for abstract screening. Out of these, 3 articles remained for full-text screening and were finally included in the review.[2,3,5]

DISCUSSION

Zoerner et al. report a depressed skull fracture in a 14-year-old patient with a depressed parietal fracture causing compression and thrombosis of the superior sagittal sinus.[5] The patient was successfully treated conservatively, in part due to a favorable clinical status, combined with concern of catastrophic bleeding in the event of attempted fracture elevation. Apart from external compression due to the fracture, intraluminal thrombosis was documented on imaging; however, anticoagulation was not administered and follow-up MRV imaging after 7 months demonstrated sinus recanalization.

Owler and Besser report a 10-year-old patient with a right-sided dominant transverse sinus occlusion due to an overlying EDH, accompanied by a linear occipital fracture after head injury.[3] The patient presented with severe headache and diplopia and was treated by EDH removal via a right occipital craniotomy. Postoperative MRV demonstrated reconstitution of flow through the transverse sinus, although incomplete, and the patient went on to have a complete neurologic recovery.

Li et al. report on 40 patients with cerebral venous sinus occlusion after head trauma.[2] Of those, 9 patients had intraluminal thrombosis, 18 had external sinus compression due to epidural hematoma of depressed fracture, while 13 patients presented with both external compression and intraluminal thrombosis. Furthermore, 34 patients presented with skull fractures (31 linear and 3 depressed ones). In total, 18 patients had an epidural hematoma compressing the sinus (16 cases were associated with the transverse and 2 with the superior sagittal sinus). No data regarding treatment or outcomes were provided.

In our case, complete right sigmoid sinus occlusion due to external compression by a focal EDH caused life-threatening intracranial hypertension, which was probably precipitated by the fact that the contralateral transverse sinus was hypoplastic and inadequate to take over complete venous drainage. In the literature, posttraumatic sinus occlusion is often associated with skull fractures; in our case, however, no fracture was evident either on imaging or intraoperatively. Therefore, a high index of suspicion for sinus occlusion is required in head trauma patients who are deteriorating or show signs of intracranial hypertension out of proportion with imaged lesions. This is because, as evidenced by our case, even relatively benign-appearing EDHs may be enough to cause complete sinus occlusion. Appropriate neuroimaging with high-resolution MRI/MRV images with and without contrast administration facilitates accurate differentiation between extra- and intraluminal thrombus and allows for targeted intervention.

Epidural hematomas overlying dural sinuses are traditionally regarded as high-risk lesions for operative management, due to the potential for catastrophic bleeding from possible sinus lacerations that have been tamponaded by the hematoma.[5] However, in case of intractable intracranial hypertension attributed to venous outflow restriction due to the occluded sinus, aggressive surgical management for removal of the hematoma and the associated sinus compression may be unavoidable. Our initial single burr-hole approach highlights a minimally invasive way to alleviate sinus compression in focally contained EDHs. This approach may be used as a temporizing, life-saving intervention as even incomplete reconstitution of flow in the sinus may be enough to normalize ICP. When symptomatic sinus compression remains or recurs, extension of the burr hole, as in our case, or even craniotomy may be required. The latter may be necessary in cases of sinus laceration to allow for primary repair.

One should be aware of the possibility of intraluminal thrombosis co-existing or following extradural compression. In such a case, after surgical intervention has been undertaken or the risk of hematoma expansion has been ruled out by serial imaging, therapeutic anticoagulation with either unfractionated heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin should be administered. While the rationale is similar to the treatment of spontaneous venous sinus occlusion, there are no unequivocally established guidelines regarding pharmacological agents and duration of treatment. A reasonable choice could be unfractionated heparin, due to rapid reversibility; its titration however requires close monitoring with serial blood draws due to a narrow therapeutic window. In our case, we administered therapeutic-dose enoxaparin, starting 24 h after the second operation for 3 months. Low-molecular-weight heparin seems a reasonable initial treatment choice, due to ease of administration and an acceptable safety profile established in studies for spontaneous venous sinus thrombosis; however, robust data regarding anticoagulation after traumatic venous sinus thrombosis are lacking and randomized studies could be justified to guide treatment decisions.[4]

CONCLUSION

Epidural hematoma is a rare but potentially lethal cause of venous sinus occlusion. Acute occlusion of the dominant transverse/sigmoid sinus when the contralateral one is hypoplastic, as the one in our case, may be poorly tolerated and may necessitate aggressive treatment. Appropriate interpretation of neuroimaging is paramount to determining the exact cause of sinus occlusion and facilitates precise intervention. Single burr-hole placement is a life-saving procedure that may allow sufficient hematoma evacuation for reconstitution of sinus flow and amelioration of intracranial hypertension.
