Clinical evaluation of plasma neopterin as a biomarker of immune activation in allergic rhinitis using a fluorescence-based o-phthaldehyde derivatization method
Authors: Ali Alqahtani, Taha Alqahtani, Adil Alshehri, Humood Al Shmrany, sherif M Elaidy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAllergic rhinitis (AR) is a common chronic inflammatory condition of the nasal mucosa driven by IgE-mediated hypersensitivity, yet reliable biomarkers of systemic immune activity and oxidative stress remain limited despite involvement of Th2 cytokines, eosinophils, and macrophages. Neopterin (NPT) is a macrophage-derived marker of interferon-γ–induced immune activation and oxidative imbalance, but current plasma detection methods are expensive, or lack sufficient sensitivity for routine clinical use. Herein, a validated fluorescence-based method was developed for determination of plasma NPT using o-phthaldehyde derivatization in the presence of 2-mercaptoethanol under mildly alkaline conditions. The reaction formed a stable isoindole derivative with strong fluorescence (λ_ex = 335 nm, λ_em = 455 nm), increasing the quantum yield from 0.24 for native NPT to 0.65 for the NPT–OPA product. The method showed linearity from 1 to 20 ng/mL (r² = 0.9996), with a detection limit of 0.22 ng/mL and a quantification limit of 0.66 ng/mL. Accuracy ranged from 99.0% to 100.8%, and precision remained below 1.0%. No interference appeared from biopterin, folic acid, riboflavin, or xanthopterin, each contributing less than 5% of the NPT signal. Plasma NPT was measured in 26 AR patients and 26 healthy controls. AR patients showed elevated NPT concentrations (2.9 ± 0.7 ng/mL) compared to controls (1.5 ± 0.3 ng/mL, p < 0.001). NPT levels correlated with symptom severity (r = 0.44, p = 0.015) and eosinophil counts (r = 0.41, p = 0.022), indicating an association between immune activation and disease intensity. Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is one of the most prevalent chronic inflammatory conditions of the nasal mucosa, driven by IgE-mediated hypersensitivity to inhaled allergens. Its pathophysiology encompasses disruption of the epithelial barrier, acute mast cell degranulation, and persistent infiltration of eosinophils, basophils, and type 2 helper T (Th2) cells, which collectively sustain chronic inflammation1–3. While traditionally conceptualized as a Th2-dominant disorder mediated by IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, growing evidence underscores the involvement of innate and cellular immune pathways, including dendritic cells, innate lymphoid cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and macrophages. A comprehensive assessment of these overlapping immune axes is essential to improve mechanistic phenotyping of AR and to establish biomarkers capable of reflecting disease activity, therapeutic response, and patient heterogeneity4,5.

Neopterin (NPT), a low-molecular-weight pteridine derivative, is produced by monocytes and macrophages upon stimulation with interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and other activators of cellular immunity6,7. Circulating NPT levels have long been recognized as sensitive markers of immune activation in diverse clinical contexts, including infections, autoimmune disorders, malignancies, and transplant rejection. Beyond its role as a surrogate of IFN-γ-driven immune activity, NPT is mechanistically linked to redox imbalance: its biosynthesis is associated with enhanced generation of reactive oxygen species and modulation of nitric oxide pathways, thereby implicating it in oxidative stress mediated tissue injury. These properties render NPT a promising candidate biomarker for diseases in which immune dysregulation and oxidative stress converge8–10.

The relationship between NPT and AR is complex and remains incompletely defined. Acute allergen exposure, local tissue inflammation, and innate immune activation can transiently raise NPT levels, largely via NK cell– or T cell–driven IFN-γ release and subsequent macrophage activation; because NPT production depends on IFN-γ signaling, the degree of local Th1-type activity will modulate systemic NPT11,12. Reported findings are inconsistent, with decreased, unchanged, or elevated NPT concentrations observed in atopic individuals at baseline and during symptomatic exacerbations, reflecting biological and methodological heterogeneity13–15. Important contributors to this heterogeneity include the sampling compartment (serum, plasma, saliva, or nasal secretions), which determines whether measurements reflect local or systemic macrophage activation; disease phenotype (seasonal versus perennial; intermittent versus persistent), which shapes the timing and magnitude of immune activation; and coexisting inflammatory conditions that can raise NPT independent of nasal disease. Additional factors that may decouple clinical symptoms from measured NPT are the temporal dynamics of NPT production and clearance and differences in assay sensitivity and specificity. Taken together, these considerations imply that single, cross-sectional NPT measurements have limited interpretability in AR, and that resolving the relationship between NPT and disease activity requires attention to sample type, timing relative to allergen exposure, clinical phenotype, and assay methodology.

Different analytical techniques have been reported for NPT determination in biological fluids, each with specific advantages and notable limitations. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) has been used16, but it is time-intensive and requires trained personnel. Synthesized magnetic silica-supported molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP-MS) with UV–vis spectrophotometry17 and the surrogate matrix approach18 have also been applied, yet both rely on cost-intensive reagents and specialized laboratory facilities. Electrochemical methods, including differential pulse voltammetry19 and potentiometric sensors20, offer lower-cost alternatives, although their selectivity is often reduced by structurally similar compounds. Moreover, Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS) has been employed for NPT determination via plasmonic amplification21; however, dependence on expensive nanomaterial substrates and high surface sensitivity limits reproducibility. Recently, several fluorescence-based approaches have focused on developing specialized fluorescent probes and nanomaterial-based sensors to improve sensitive NPT determination. These include advanced ratio metric sensors based on Zn (II)-Nd (III) nanoclusters22, Europium (III) metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)23, and luminescent Zn (II)-Eu (III) complexes24. Other studies have utilized ferric ion-modulated carbon dots25 and various HPLC methods coupled with fluorometric detection as standard approaches for analyzing NPT in biological fluids26–30. Furthermore, refined chromatographic techniques using different stationary phases or post-column photoinduced derivatization have been explored31,32.

The determination of plasma NPT is essential for demonstrating its role as a biomarker of immune activation and oxidative stress in AR. However, existing analytical techniques, are limited by labor-intensive sample preparation, long analysis times, or insufficient selectivity in complex biological matrices. Moreover, the intrinsic fluorescence of NPT is prone to quenching by plasma constituents, which hampers its direct determination. To overcome these challenges, fluorescence spectroscopy, particularly when coupled with derivatization tactics, was chosen due to its high sensitivity, rapidity, and capability for trace-level determination. The present study therefore aims to develop and validate a novel fluorescence-based method for plasma NPT determination using derivatization with o-phthaldehyde (OPA) as a fluorescent probe. The method was rigorously validated in accordance with ICH guidelines assessing linearity, accuracy, precision, and selectivity to confirm analytical reliability. Beyond method development, this study explores the clinical relevance of plasma NPT levels in patients with AR compared to healthy controls, thereby providing insight into its potential role as a biomarker of AR.

Experimental

Materials, reagents, and instruments

Standard NPT (Purity ≥ 98.5%) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). o-phthaladehyde (OPA) was also purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). It was prepared as 0.5% (w/v) by dissolving 50 mg of OPA in 10 mL of HPLC-grade methanol. HPLC-grade solvents, including methanol and ethanol were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (USA). 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME) was obtained from Alpha Chemika (Mumbai, India) and freshly prepared as a 0.01% (v/v) solution in ethanol prior to use.

Standard solutions

Standard stock solutions of NPT (100 µg/mL) were prepared in distilled water, and serial dilutions were performed to obtain final working solutions within the concentration range of 1–20 ng/mL.

Method development

Calibration standards were prepared by transferring accurately aliquots of the NPT stock solution into 10 mL volumetric flasks to yield final concentrations in the range of 1 to 20 ng/mL. Each flask received 1.0 mL of human plasma, which had been pre-treated with 2.0 mL of 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid to precipitate plasma proteins, followed by centrifugation at 8,000 rpm for 4 min at 4 °C. The resulting clear supernatant was collected for analysis. Subsequently, reaction was carried out by adding 1.0 mL of 1% OPA, 0.5 mL of 0.3% 2-ME and 0.5 mL of 0.1 M NaOH, and the mixtures were diluted to volume with distilled water. After 20 min of reaction, fluorescence intensity (FI) was recorded at 455 nm (emission) with excitation at 335 nm. A reagent blank prepared under identical conditions was used for correction, and calibration curves were constructed by plotting FI against NPT concentrations (ng/mL). All described procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

The method was validated in accordance with ICH recommendations by assessing linearity, accuracy, precision, and selectivity. Accuracy and precision of the proposed method were assessed at three NPT concentrations (5, 10, and 15 ng/mL). Accuracy was presented by calculating percentage recovery at each level under both intra-day and inter-day conditions, while precision was presented as repeatability (intra-day) and intermediate precision (inter-day), expressed in terms of relative standard deviation (RSD).

Selectivity was assessed by examining structurally related compounds that might interfere with NPT determination. Biopterin, xanthopterin, folic acid, and riboflavin were tested, each prepared at a final concentration of 15 ng/mL and analyzed under the optimized conditions. FI was recorded at the same excitation (335 nm) and emission (455 nm) wavelengths as used for NPT to evaluate potential spectral overlap.

Clinical study

Study design

The developed method was used in cross-sectional case-control clinical study and applied to quantify plasma NPT levels investigating their clinical relevance in patients with AR compared to healthy, non-atopic controls.

These estimates were utilized in the present calculation to maintain clinical applicability. Based on this, a minimum of 23 subjects was required per group. To compensate for possible exclusions or sample loss (e.g., hemolysis), the sample size was increased by 10%, resulting in 26 participants per group and a total of 52 subjects.

Participants and selection criteria

A total of 52 participants were consecutively recruited from the otorhinolaryngology clinics at Al-Azhar University in Damietta, Egypt. The study population consisted of two equal groups: 26 patients with clinically confirmed AR and 26 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. For the AR group, inclusion required a diagnosis of allergic rhinitis according to the Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma (ARIA) guidelines, confirmed by clinical symptoms (nasal obstruction, rhinorrhea, sneezing, and itching) together with a positive skin prick test or elevated serum IgE to relevant aeroallergens. To confirm that participants were in a sustained phase of allergic inflammation and to minimize the impact of transient fluctuations, inclusion was further restricted to patients demonstrating an absolute eosinophil count (AEC) exceeding 450 cells/µL. This criterion served as an objective marker of consistent systemic immune involvement, aligning with the symptomatic phase during which plasma NPT was quantified. Healthy controls were required to have no history of allergic diseases, negative skin prick test, and normal IgE levels. Eligible participants were between 18 and 55 years old. Exclusion criteria for both groups were strictly defined to eliminate potential confounders of systemic immune activation. These included chronic rhinosinusitis, asthma, concomitant atopic diseases, autoimmune disorders, or acute/chronic systemic inflammatory conditions. To further ensure the stability of pteridine metabolism, participants with cardiovascular disorders, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney or liver disease, or malignancy were excluded. Additionally, any use of corticosteroids or immunosuppressive drugs within the past 3 months, pregnancy, or lactation served as grounds for exclusion to avoid pharmacological or physiological interference with NPT levels.

Plasma sample collection and preparation

Venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected from each participant between 8:00 and 10:00 AM into EDTA-coated tubes after an overnight fast of at least 10 h to minimize dietary effects and circadian variations in plasma metabolites. Immediately after collection, samples were placed on ice and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to separate plasma. This standardized morning collection window was chosen to ensure consistency across study groups and to mitigate the influence of temporal fluctuations on neopterin concentrations. The processed plasma was then immediately subjected to the analytical procedure using the described fluorometric method.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Al-Azhar University, Damietta (Approval No. DFM-IRB 00012367-25-09–060). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to enrollment, written informed consent was obtained from all participants after they were fully informed about the study objectives, procedures, and potential risks. Participant confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained, and individuals were assured of their right to withdraw at any time without any impact on their medical care.

Results and discussion

Mechanistic investigation of the reaction between NPT and OPA

NPT was reacted with OPA in the presence of 2-ME under slightly alkaline conditions, yielding a highly fluorescent isoindole derivative as shown in Fig. 1. The derivatization mechanism is proposed to follow a pathway similar to that reported for primary amine-containing compounds. The amino group of NPT first condenses with one aldehyde moiety of OPA to form a carbinolamine derivative, which undergoes dehydration to produce an imine intermediate. This intermediate rapidly couples with the thiol group of 2-ME, forming an α-alkyl amino benzyl sulfide adduct. Subsequently, an intramolecular nucleophilic attack occurs between the second unreacted carbonyl group of OPA and the nitrogen atom of the imine moiety, followed by a second dehydration step33–35. This sequence of reactions results in the formation of a stable isoindole derivative, which is responsible for the observed strong fluorescence signal.

Schematic of the reaction mechanism between NPT and OPA in the presence of 2-mercaptoethanol, showing formation of the fluorescent isoindole derivative.

The structural confirmation of NPT and its derived isoindole product was supported by 1H NMR spectroscopy. The spectrum of NPT, as shown in Figure S1, exhibited characteristic resonances corresponding to the pteridine ring protons, observed in the downfield region (δ 6.0–8.5 ppm), along with signals attributable to the hydroxylated side chain appearing in the δ 3.5–4.5 ppm region. Following derivatization with OPA, the spectrum of the product, shown in Figure S2, displayed new diagnostic signals in the aromatic region (δ 6.0–10 ppm), corresponding to the isoindole ring system. These changes were accompanied by noticeable downfield shifts of the amino proton signals, confirming the covalent interaction of OPA with the amino group of NPT. The preservation of the aliphatic resonances in the δ 2.5–4.5 ppm range further indicated that the side chain structure remained intact after derivatization.

While NPT possesses intrinsic fluorescence, the derivatization is essential to shift the measurement window away from the high-background ultraviolet region where plasma matrix constituents interfere, thereby significantly improving the signal-to-noise ratio and selectivity for clinical samples.

Fluorescence based quantification of NPT using OPA

NPT was quantified indirectly through its reaction with OPA, which yielded a fluorescent isoindole derivative. The fluorescence features of this derivative were compared to the blank, as shown in Fig. 2. The blank spectrum displayed only weak background signals with no significant excitation or emission bands, whereas the isoindole derivative exhibited a distinct excitation peak at 335 nm and a strong emission maximum at 455 nm. The fluorescence emission spectra recorded at the NPT concentrations range of (1–20 ng/mL) are shown in Fig. 3. A significant increase in FI with increase of NPT concentrations confirmed a clear concentration-dependent fluorescence response.

Method optimization

The experimental parameters governing the reaction between NPT and OPA were varied individually to assign the optimal conditions that produced the highest FI of the reaction product. The OPA, 2-ME, and sodium hydroxide, as well as the reaction time, were systematically adjusted, and their effects on the resulting fluorescence were evaluated.

Concentration and volume of OPA

A range of OPA concentrations (0.2–2% w/v) was evaluated, the findings revealed that FI increased with concentration, reaching a maximum at 1.0% w/v, as shown in Fig. 4a. Above this level, no further rise was observed, indicating that 1% w/v yields the highest FI without quenching effects. The effect of varying the volume of the 1% w/v OPA solution on complex formation with NBT was then examined. Volumes from 0.2 to 2.0 mL were verified, and FI increased proportionally up to 1.0 mL, reflecting efficient interaction within this range. However, as shown in Fig. 4b, further increases beyond 1.0 mL did not improve the signal, indicating saturation of binding sites and stabilization of the complex under these conditions.

(a) Effect of OPA concentration (0.2–2% w/v) on the fluorescence intensity of the NPT–OPA isoindole derivative. (b). Effect of OPA volume (0.2–2.0 mL) on fluorescence intensity, showing maximal signal at 1.0 mL of 1% OPA.

Concentration and volume of 2-ME

The addition of 2-ME is essential for the formation of the isoindole derivative with high fluorescence activity. In the absence of the thiol compound, the reaction between OPA and NPT yields a product with only weak fluorescence. To evaluate its role, the effect of 2-ME concentration was examined over the range of 0.1–0.8% w/v. As shown in Fig. 5a, the FI increased with rising 2-ME concentration, reaching a maximum at 0.3%. Beyond this point, no further enhancement was observed, and the signal remained constant up to 0.8%. Accordingly, 0.3% 2-ME was considered sufficient and was used throughout this study.

(a) Effect of 2-mercaptoethanol concentration (0.1–0.8% w/v) on fluorescence intensity, with maximal response at 0.3%. (b). Effect of 2-mercaptoethanol volume (0.1–1.0 mL) on fluorescence intensity, showing saturation at 0.5 mL.

Moreover, the effect of varying the volume of the 0.3% 2-ME solution on the interaction with NBT was investigated. The volumes ranging from 0.1 to 1.0 mL were tested, and FI increased proportionally up to 0.5 mL, indicating efficient interaction within this range, as shown in Fig. 5b. However, further increases beyond 0.5 mL produced no additional enhancement of the signal, suggesting that the reaction had reached saturation and that 0.5 mL represents the optimal volume for stable complex formation under the studied conditions.

Effect of sodium hydroxide volume

The effect of sodium hydroxide (0.1 M) volume on FI was evaluated by testing volumes in the range of 0.1–1.0 mL. As shown in Fig. 6a, FI increased with increasing sodium hydroxide volume, reaching a maximum at 0.5 mL. This maximum was maintained up to 1.0 mL. Accordingly, 0.5 mL of 0.1 M sodium hydroxide was selected as the optimal volume and was used throughout this study.

(a) Effect of sodium hydroxide (0.1 M) volume (0.1–1.0 mL) on fluorescence intensity, with optimal signal at 0.5 mL. (b). Effect of reaction time (5–40 min) on fluorescence intensity, reaching a maximum at 20 min.

Reaction time

The influence of reaction time on the interaction of NPT with OPA was checked by varying the duration from 5 to 50 min. As shown in Fig. 6b, FI increased with time and reached its maximum at 20 min. The FI was tracked for 120 min in order to assess the stability of the formed isoindole derivative. When kept in the dark for 60 min after derivatization, the signal recovered with a 98.6% stability. After that, there was a noticeable decrease in FI, which at 90 min had dropped to 93.4%. In order to guarantee optimum precision and avoid signal degradation, an acceptable measurement window of 20 to 60 min was defined based on these findings, and all analytical processes in this investigation were finished within this timeframe.

Additionally, potential matrix effects resulting from trichloroacetic acid protein precipitation were quantitatively evaluated. This was done by comparing the slopes of the calibration curves generated in distilled water and in pre-treated plasma (matrix-matched). The matrix factor was calculated as the ratio of the two slopes; a value of 0.96 was obtained, indicating that the plasma matrix and trichloroacetic acid treatment did not significantly influence the NPT fluorescence signal. Regarding potential interference from endogenous plasma amines or amino acids, the accuracy of NPT determination was maintained through the optimization of specific fluorometric parameters (FI was recorded at 455 nm (emission) with excitation at 335 nm) that prioritize the NPT-isoindole signal. Furthermore, the trichloroacetic acid protein precipitation step significantly reduced the concentration of competing endogenous amines. The calculated matrix factor and the consistent use of a reagent blank additionally confirm that the effect of the plasma matrix is negligible, ensuring that the fluorescence intensity is solely attributable to the NPT-isoindole derivative without systematic bias.

Selectivity was additionally evaluated by testing structurally related compounds, including biopterin, folic acid, riboflavin, and xanthopterin, for potential spectral interference. These compounds were selected because of their structural resemblance to NPT either as members of the pteridine family (biopterin and xanthopterin) or due to their heteroaromatic and conjugated ring systems (folic acid and riboflavin) which could lead to overlapping fluorescence signals. Each compound was tested at 15 ng/mL under the same conditions used for NPT analysis. As shown in Fig. 7, none of the tested compounds exhibited significant fluorescence at the selected wavelength, with emission intensities remaining below 5% of those recorded for NPT. These results confirm the high selectivity of the proposed method, ensuring minimal interference from structurally related biomolecules.

Fluorescence emission spectra of NPT and structurally related compounds (biopterin, folic acid, riboflavin, xanthopterin), demonstrating selectivity of the method with < 5% interference.

Clinical application

The proposed method was applied to plasma samples assessing its clinical relevance in AR. As presented in Table 3, the mean plasma NPT concentration in healthy controls was 1.5 ± 0.3 ng/mL, consistent with values reported for physiological baseline conditions (< 2 ng/mL). In contrast, patients with AR exhibited a marked elevation, with mean concentrations reaching 2.9 ± 0.7 ng/mL, a difference that was statistically significant (p = 0.0003).

Further correlation analysis investigated that plasma NPT levels were positively associated with symptom severity scores (r = 0.44, p = 0.015) and circulating eosinophil counts (r = 0.41, p = 0.022). This indicates that NPT may not only serve as a marker of systemic immune activation but also reflect disease intensity in AR. The rise of NPT in this context, despite AR being traditionally Th2-dominant, likely reflects the activation of the innate immune axis and the recruitment of macrophages as a response to chronic mucosal injury. In persistent AR, the interaction between Th2-type inflammation and compensatory Th1-type signals (such as IFN-γ production from NK cells or Th1 cells) creates a mixed inflammatory environment.

Elevated NPT levels are mechanistically consistent with IFN-γ–driven macrophage activation and the generation of reactive oxygen species, both of which are increasingly recognized as contributing to epithelial dysfunction and chronic mucosal inflammation in AR. The high association with clinical and cellular markers (eosinophils) suggests that NPT serves as a systemic integrator of the allergic cascade, rather than a nonspecific inflammatory response.

Taken together, these findings support the clinical utility of NPT as a non-invasive biomarker that integrates immune activation and oxidative stress, offering potential for improved disease monitoring, risk stratification, and evaluation of therapeutic interventions in AR. Although NPT is a sensitive indicator of overall cellular immune activity, strict exclusion criteria were used in this investigation to control its specificity and successfully exclude out confounding systemic inflammatory, viral, or autoimmune diseases. As a result, the measured rise in plasma NPT is regarded as a quantitative indicator of the severity of allergic disease rather than as a stand-alone diagnostic tool for AR. NPT’s function as a particular integrator of the allergic inflammatory burden in these individuals is further supported by the strong association seen between NPT levels and clinically meaningful indicators, particularly symptom severity and eosinophil counts.

Comparative evaluation of the proposed fluorescence-based method versus the previously reported method for NPT determination

The analytical efficiency of the proposed method for NPT determination was compared with previously reported techniques as presented in Table 4. The current method achieved a linear range of 1–20 ng/mL with a LOD of 0.22 ng/mL, indicating higher sensitivity than a magnetic silica-supported molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP-MS)17. Although the MIP-MS based method presented efficient adsorption capacity, its complex fabrication limits its application. In contrast, the current method is simple, requires minimal preparation, and can be readily used in standard clinical laboratories. While a direct comparison using the same samples on an HPLC or ELISA platform was not performed, the clinical NPT levels obtained here are in high agreement with established reference ranges reported in the literature using those techniques. This alignment, supported by rigorous ICH validation, confirms the clinical equivalence and accuracy of the proposed method as a cost-effective alternative for monitoring immune activation.

From a clinical perspective, the current method demonstrated elevated plasma NPT levels in patients with AR compared to healthy controls (2.9 ± 0.7 vs. 1.5 ± 0.3 ng/mL, p < 0.001). This highlights its potential utility for monitoring immune activation and oxidative stress in AR, aspects that were not specifically addressed by the MIP-MS based method. Overall, the proposed method combines high sensitivity, operational simplicity, and direct clinical applicability in AR, whereas the MIP-MS based method contribute primarily through analytical innovation and regulatory validation.

Limitations and future prospectives

Analysis of the developed analytical method highlights both advantages and limitations. The method relies on derivatization with OPA, providing enhanced sensitivity, selectivity, and reliable determination of plasma NPT at Nano scale levels. However, the need for a derivatization step involves additional handling, which may limit its use in high-throughput clinical laboratories compared to simpler platforms. Although the method shows excellent recovery and minimal interference from structurally related compounds, validation was performed in a single laboratory, and external confirmation is required to ensure reproducibility. Regarding clinical application, the study was constrained by a relatively small sample size and a cross-sectional design. To mitigate temporal and circadian variability, all plasma samples were collected under strictly standardized conditions at the same time of day after an overnight fast. While neopterin levels may vary based on the timing of allergen exposure, the significant correlation with circulating eosinophils suggests that the measured concentrations accurately reflect the sustained inflammatory burden of the disease phase. However, a single measurement cannot fully capture the dynamic fluctuations of immune activation. Future studies should therefore involve larger cohorts, longitudinal designs to assess neopterin stability over time, and inter-laboratory comparisons. In addition, further optimization of the derivatization procedure or exploration of alternative fluorogenic reagents may improve the clinical applicability of this method.

Conclusion

The present study establishes a simple and sensitive fluorescence-based method for plasma NPT determination through o-phthaldehyde derivatization. The method demonstrated excellent linearity, accuracy, and precision, with a remarkably low LOD of 0.22 ng/mL, making it suitable for trace-level analysis in clinical samples. Importantly, application to AR patients confirmed elevated plasma NPT levels compared to healthy controls, with significant correlations to symptom severity and eosinophil counts. These findings highlight the potential of NPT not only as a marker of immune activation and oxidative stress but also as a clinically relevant biomarker for disease monitoring in AR.
