Error Awareness in the Volcano Plots of Oxygen Electroreduction to Hydrogen Peroxide
Authors: Ricardo Urrego‐Ortiz, Michell O. Almeida, Federico Calle‐Vallejo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrocatalysis holds the key to the decentralized production of hydrogen peroxide via the two‐electron oxygen reduction reaction (ORR, O2(g)+2H++2e−‐>H2O2(aq)). However, cost‐effective, active, and selective catalysts are still sought after. While density functional theory (DFT) has already led to the discovery of various enhanced catalysts, it has a severe yet often unnoticed drawback: the ill description of O2 and H2O2. Here, we analyze the impact of the errors in those two species on the most widespread activity plots in the literature, namely free‐energy diagrams and Sabatier‐type volcano plots. Uncorrected or partially corrected gas‐phase energies lead to appreciably different activity plots that may provide inaccurate predictions. Indeed, we show for a variety of electrocatalysts that only when the errors in O2 and H2O2 are corrected can DFT mimic the experiments. In sum, this work provides concrete guidelines to avoid a common pitfall of computational models for electrocatalytic hydrogen peroxide production.

Introduction

Decentralized hydrogen peroxide production is an appealing strategy to sanitize and reuse polluted water in communities with inadequate or inexistent infrastructure for wastewater treatment.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] However, a recent study of the U.S. department of energy identified hydrogen peroxide as an energy‐intensive chemical the production of which should be optimized, [5] as the prevailing anthraquinone process demands large amounts of energy and is also waste‐intensive.[ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ] In this context, electrocatalysis offers an appealing alternative, as it can be used for the synthesis of H2O2 by combining O2 with electrons and protons through the so‐called two‐electron oxygen reduction reaction (2e− ORR: O2g+2H++2e-→H2O2aq ).[ 11 , 12 , 13 ]

Nevertheless, the 2e− ORR competes with the four‐electron reduction to water (4e− ORR: O2g+4H++4e-→2H2Ol ) and they share some adsorbed reaction intermediates.[ 14 , 15 ] This competition causes low hydrogen peroxide selectivity at large current densities, and so far pure noble metals and some of their alloys display the best performance in terms of activity and stability. [16] Hence, nonnoble catalysts with enhanced activity and selectivity are crucial to efficiently and affordably produce H2O2 at relevant scales for industry or isolated communities.[ 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ]

Modern approaches to design improved 2e− ORR electrocatalysts by screening are based on the adsorption free energies of either *OOH (ΔGOOH ) or *OH (ΔGOH , a key intermediate of the 4e− ORR). The latter is extensively used because the energetic difference between these two species is relatively constant across materials and facets and amounts to 3.2±0.2 eV,[ 14 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] such that 2e− and 4e− analyses can simultaneously be made.[ 19 , 24 ] These energies are usually obtained by means of the computational hydrogen electrode model (CHE) and density functional theory (DFT) calculations at the level of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA).[ 19 , 25 , 26 ] Nevertheless, the accuracy of DFT at the GGA level fades when estimating the total energies of gaseous compounds,[ 25 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] which might prevent an accurate assessment of the reaction energy and the energies of the electrochemical steps.[ 30 , 31 ] Because water and hydrogen are typically well described by DFT,[ 25 , 29 ] they have conventionally been used in combination with experimental values to provide an estimate of the total energy of O2. Such an approach is suitable for the 4e− ORR and has helped circumvent the oxygen problem for nearly two decades.

However, it has long been assumed that H2O2 is well described by DFT, which is an unnecessary and questionable approximation. In fact, previous works have shown that the DFT gas‐phase errors of H2O2 are substantial for several exchange‐correlation functionals, not only at the GGA level but also at the more advanced meta‐GGA and hybrid levels of theory.[ 32 , 33 ] So far, the known consequences of those errors are twofold: inaccurate predictions of equilibrium potentials for the 2e− ORR, and an incorrect energy landscape of the ideal catalyst, which is a reference to evaluate the performance of real catalysts. [33] However, the implications of those errors for activity plots including real catalysts remain unseen.

In this work, we show how to simultaneously account for the errors of O2 and H2O2 when modelling the 2e− ORR on real catalysts. The errors are estimated using a semiempirical method previously applied to detect the DFT inaccuracies in C‐ and N‐containing molecules[ 27 , 28 , 34 , 35 ] and in the O−O bond in H2O2. [33] We show analytically and graphically the relocation of 2e− ORR activity plots that follows after the inclusion of gas‐phase errors for several DFT functionals, enabling analyses based on actual equations rather than mere correlations or linear fits. Finally, the progressive effects of these errors on the activity predictions for real materials are illustrated by means of free‐energy diagrams and volcano plots for a reaction with more than one ill‐defined molecule, which is, to our knowledge, currently unavailable in the state of the art.

Separate DFT Energies and Reaction Free Energies

The DFT‐calculated free energy of a compound i (GiDFT ) at a temperature T , can be calculated as per Equation 1. (1) GiDFT=EiDFT+ZPEi-TSi+∫0TCpidT

where EDFT is the ground‐state energy, ZPE is the zero‐point energy, TS is the product of temperature and entropy, and ∫0TCpdT is the integral of the heat capacity between 0 K and T. For gaseous compounds, it is customary to use the experimental total entropies while for ORR adsorbates TS is most often discarded.[ 21 , 25 , 36 ] To obtain the free energy of a reaction j (ΔGjDFT ), one simply needs to take the difference between the products and reactants of the aforementioned quantities, each of them multiplied by the respective stoichiometric coefficient.

Gas‐Errors in ORR Modelling

In line with previous works,[ 27 , 34 ] the total error (ϵORRT ) in ΔGORRDFT is defined with respect to its experimental counterpart (ΔGjexp ): ϵORRT=ΔGORRDFT-ΔGORRexp . Besides, ϵORRT is the sum of the errors in the products (ϵH2O2 ) minus the errors in the reactants (ϵO2 and ϵH2 ): ϵORRT=ϵH2O2-ϵO2-ϵH2 . This implies that if DFT correctly accounts for the errors of each species, ϵORRT vanishes, and the experimental value is reproduced. In this order of ideas, and considering that molecular hydrogen is typically well described by various DFT exchange‐correlation functionals (ϵH2≈0 ), [29] a fully corrected DFT energy for the ORR (ΔGORRFC ) is (see section S3: (8) ΔGORRFC=ΔGORRDFT+ϵO2-ϵH2O2

Since the first step of the reaction involves the O2 error, the fully corrected energy equals the OC energy (ΔG1FC=ΔG1OC ). However, as the second step does not involve the O2 error, its OC energy is the same as the uncorrected one (ΔG2OC=ΔG2DFT ).

Importantly, the errors in the free energy of the 2e− ORR are also present in its equilibrium potential (UORR0 ), as: UORR0=-ΔGORR/2 . Likewise, for each free electrochemical step there is a potential that involves the same errors but with opposite signs. All in all, Equations 8–11 show that the 2e− ORR is a suitable yet challenging case study to analyze the impact of gas‐phase errors in heterogenous electrocatalysis, as all of its intermediate steps involve at least one molecular error. Free‐Energy Diagrams

Free‐energy diagrams are arguably the most widely used tool in computational electrocatalysis.[ 21 , 28 , 52 , 53 ] These diagrams condense the free energies of the intermediate steps of electrocatalytic reactions and allow for a rapid appraisal of important quantities such as the onset potential and the reaction energy.

As the adsorption of reactants and the desorption of products happen at least once in electrocatalytic reactions, gas‐phase errors may modify the free‐energy diagrams, as previous works showed for various reactions.[ 27 , 28 , 30 , 33 , 35 , 39 ] Here, we study the effects of gas‐phase errors on the diagrams of Pt(111), Ag(111), Ag(211), Au(111), and Au(211) for the 2e− ORR. For each metal facet we built the diagrams at the equilibrium potential using (i) the uncorrected O2 and H2O2 energies (“DFT as is”); (ii) the corrected O2 energy and the uncorrected H2O2 energy (“OC”); and (iii) the corrected O2 and H2O2 energies (“FC”). More details on the calculated free energies can be found in section S7. The diagrams for Pt(111), Au(211), and Au(111) calculated with PBE are shown in Figure 1, while those for Ag are included in section S9.

Free‐energy diagrams for the 2e− ORR on (a) Pt(111), (b) Au(211), and (c) Au(111) calculated with PBE. When the energies of neither O2 nor H2O2 are corrected (“DFT as is”), the profile is in red. When only the energy of O2 is corrected (“OC”) the orange profile is obtained. The green paths are obtained when both O2 and H2O2 are fully corrected (“FC”). Importantly, as each method includes different corrections, the equilibrium potentials are different in all cases. Specifically, the PBE uncorrected equilibrium potential is 0.57 V, the O2‐corrected equilibrium potential is 0.81 V, and the fully corrected equilibrium potential is 0.68 V.

In the diagrams in Figure 1 the red and orange paths lie above the green one. This indicates that the uncorrected and OC‐corrected adsorption energies of *OOH are most often underestimated. This also occurs for Ag(111) and Ag(211), the diagrams of which are shown in Figures S2 and S3. The underestimation is caused by the gas‐phase errors of O2 and H2O2, as *OOH adsorption involves the O2 energy and the equilibrium potential encompasses both energies. In fact, the difference between the red and orange energies is 0.24 eV for PBE (corresponding to UORROC-UORRDFT , see values in Table 1) and the difference between the orange and green lines is 0.13 eV in all cases (corresponding to UORRFC-UORROC=ϵH2O22 , see definitions in Table 1).

Furthermore, in some cases in Figure 1 the *OOH adsorption energy at the equilibrium potential switches from positive to negative depending on the errors under consideration. For instance, the O2 correction pushes the adsorption energy below zero for Pt(111) (Figure 1a) while the adsorption energy on Au(211) is negative only when both ϵO2 and ϵH2O2 errors are rectified (Figure 1b).

Volcano Plots

A convenient way to visualize the collective effect of gas‐phase errors on the catalytic activity is by means of volcano plots. These massively used plots provide a quantitative assessment of the performance of a catalyst based on the Sabatier principle,[ 14 , 25 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 ] although it is worth noting that various authors have pointed out their limitations and the dangers associated to their indiscriminate use.[ 41 , 58 , 59 ]

The coordinates of the volcano apex establish the maximum attainable catalytic activity and the concomitant optimal value of the descriptor used to capture the trends. In turn, the legs of the volcano represent suboptimal performances and typically correspond to cases in which a key intermediate species is strongly bound (left leg) or weakly bound (right leg).

For the 2e− ORR, the adsorption energy of *OOH is typically used as a descriptor (ΔG1, Equation 3). On the left leg of the volcano, catalysts are limited by the second electrochemical step (Equation 4), whereas materials on the right leg are limited by the first electrochemical step (Equation 3). A common metric for the catalytic activity is the potential of the limiting step (UL ), defined as the potential of the least favorable electrochemical step (see Equation 11), namely, the most endergonic step.[ 19 , 24 , 60 ] Using this definition together with Tables 1 and 2 we built three ORR volcano plots for PBE in Figure 2.

For the three cases, the coordinates of the peaks are shown. The vertical differences between the tops are a function of ϵO2 and ϵH2O2 , as shown in the plot.

The red volcano in Figure 2 is based on the uncorrected results (“DFT as is”, ULDFT vs ΔGOOHDFT ); the orange volcano is built upon the O2‐corrected energies and potentials (ULOC vs ΔGOOHOC ); and the green volcano is based on O2‐ and H2O2‐corrected free energies and potentials (ULFC vs ΔGOOHFC ). It is possible to analytically determine the free energies and potentials at the top of each volcano, hereafter referred to as ΔGapex and Uapex , by equating the potentials of steps 1 and 2.

Notably, the right leg of the volcanoes remains unchanged upon gas‐phase corrections and the three lines overlap. This is because the limiting potential on that side corresponds to ΔGOOH (or ΔG1 ), i. e., the descriptor in the abscissae, such that the error in O2 cancels out. This implies that only when ϵH2O2 is corrected can the top coinciding with the experimental equilibrium potential be obtained. Figure 2 also indicates that not correcting gas‐phase errors or partially doing so results in significant departures from the experimental volcano. Specifically, for PBE in Figure 2 the uncorrected (red) apex is displaced 0.11 eV to the right with respect to the green volcano, while the O2‐corrected (orange) apex is to the left of the green one by 0.13 eV. Moreover, the errors also modify the height of the peaks. In fact, the red top is 0.11 V below the corrected top. Similarly, the orange top is vertically more positive than the green top by 0.11 V. All these shifts attest to an inherent DFT limitation for 2e− ORR modeling: both O2 and H2O2 DFT energies ought to be corrected if one aims to mimic experimental values. In Figure S1, we provide analogous volcano plots for RPBE, for which similar shifting tops are also observed with different magnitudes, as the shifts are functional‐dependent.

A widespread approach to sidestep the O2 error in ΔGOOH consists of using water as reference.[ 21 , 24 , 25 ] This procedure vanishes ϵO2 and produces a corrected descriptor, i. e., ΔGOOHDFT=ΔGOOHFC (see section S6). However, when following this procedure both potentials U1 and U2 must as well be corrected, otherwise shifted volcanoes with uncorrected tops will be obtained. This is particularly important for U2 , as it involves ϵH2O2 . This is illustrated in Figure 3b, which correlates the coordinates of the apex for the volcanoes associated to the functionals in Table 2. In this case, the abscissae of the tops are represented by ΔGOOHopt,FC , obtained using water and proton‐electron pairs as the reference ( ), and the ordinate corresponds to Uapex,DFT , calculated with the uncorrected potentials U1DFT and U2DFT . Notably, although there are no errors in the abscissae, if U1 and U2 are not suitably corrected, the tops of the analyzed functionals are related by a linear trend and none of them falls close to the green diamond, which is the experimental apex reproduced when both quantities are corrected (i. e., ΔGOOHopt,FC vs Uapex,FC ).

It is apparent in Figures 1 and 4 that reshaped free‐energy diagrams translate into volcanoes in which the summit and the actual catalysts have been relocated, yielding three strikingly different scenarios. Specifically, in the uncorrected (red) volcano most active sites are on the right leg, Pt(111) and Ag(211) being closest to the top and Au(111) being the least active. However, after correcting the free energy of O2 (orange volcano), Ag(111) and Au(211) are closer to the top and the left leg now harbors Pt(111), Ag(111), and Ag(211). Finally, in the green volcano four of the seven materials are on the strong‐binding side.

In addition, the overpotential is noticeably modified by the DFT errors, as each correction stage adjusts the limiting potential and/or the equilibrium potential. This is apparent from the shifting of the equilibrium potential lines (horizontal dashed lines in Figure 4) and the redistribution of the metals on the volcano legs. For instance, the uncorrected overpotential of Pt(111) is calculated as 0.57-0.54V=0.03V , suggesting that it is an outstanding catalyst for this reaction (the ideal catalyst has η=0V ). However, after the respective gas‐phase corrections, the final overpotential is 0.34V . This sizable increase in the overpotential changes drastically the catalytic activity predictions for the 2e− ORR on Pt(111) and is in better agreement with experiments. [19]

Furthermore, the experimental activity order at 0.1 mA/cm2 reported by Siahrostami and coworkers is: [19] PtHg>Au≈Ag>Pt. In that work, PtHg4(110) was identified as a promising 2e− ORR catalyst and its adsorption energy of *OOH was around 0.37 eV weaker than that of Pt(111) and close to the top of the volcano, [19] and the pure metal surfaces were represented by their (111) facet. The green volcano with O2 and H2O2 corrections is able to reproduce this activity ordering but those without corrections or partially corrected cannot (see the orange and red volcanoes in Figure 4). Furthermore, Pd2Hg5(001) (denoted as PdHg) has also been shown to be highly active for the 2e− ORR, and binds *OOH 0.02 eV more strongly than PtHg4(110). [61] Again, only the fully corrected (green) PBE volcano in Figure 4 is able to capture this experimental observation. While comparisons between computational predictions for actual catalysts and experiments escape the theoretical focus of this study, the good qualitative agreement between the activity predictions in Figure 4 and the experimental results in refs.[ 19 , 61 ] illustrate the benefits of assessing the gas‐phase errors in 2e− ORR models. All in all, Figures 1, 2, 3, 4 offer a picture of how the gas‐phase errors in oxygen and hydrogen peroxide may distort the computational prediction of catalytic activities and highlight the importance of correction schemes in DFT‐based electrocatalysis studies.

Conclusions

It is challenging to evaluate the impact of gas‐phase errors of a given catalytic reaction when more than one reactant and/or product is not well described by DFT. Here we detailed how the errors in H2O2 and O2 can be accounted for when modelling the 2e− ORR on real catalysts. Gas‐phase errors are shown to alter the calculated free‐energy diagrams and Sabatier‐type volcano plots for the 2e− ORR, resulting in predictions that differ considerably from experiments. It is possible to observe shifts in the apices of the volcanoes and in the predictions for real catalysts. Indeed, materials can change from being weak‐binding to strong‐binding, and (partially) uncorrected schemes may classify them as overly active when they are in fact moderately active or inactive.

Importantly, we note that current 2e− ORR models typically account for the O2 error only by using water and hydrogen as references but leave the energy of hydrogen peroxide untouched. All this hints toward the fact that correcting DFT errors should be increasingly regarded in the electrocatalysis community as a step forward in the direction of more accurate computational models.
