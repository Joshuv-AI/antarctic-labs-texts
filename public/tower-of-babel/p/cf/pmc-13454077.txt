Scan duration for dopamine transporter neuroimaging with[123I]FP-CIT and a high-sensitivity multiple-pinhole SPECT/CT
Authors: Gerda Thomsen, Karine Madsen, Cecilie A Poulsen, Claus Svarer, Lars H Pinborg, Gitte M Knudsen, Robin de Nijs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDopamine transporter (DAT) imaging with [123I]FP-CIT (Ioflupane, DaTscan™) is an established diagnostic tool for diagnosing parkinsonism and usually requires 40 min. of scanning. The three-headed high-sensitivity multiple-pinhole SPECT/CT scanner with dedicated brain multi-pinhole collimators (MPH) has in many respects superior performance and potentially enables a reduced scan duration, which can be a major advantage for patients and increases the number of patient studies that can be performed in one day. The aim of this study is to determine the minimal required scan duration that still returns good image quality and diagnostic accuracy in parkinsonism.MethodsFifty patients suspected of parkinsonism (age range 52–87 years) and 9 healthy controls (age range 57–66 years) underwent imaging with Mediso AnyScan Trio SPECT/CT and MPH collimators for 40 min. three hours after injection of 185 MBq [123I]FP-CIT. The images were reconstructed using four different acquisition lengths: 10, 20, 30, and 40 min., and subsequently quantified with an automatic image-based analysis. Outcomes of the different acquisition lengths were compared by statistical analysis of the SBR-values and by visual assessment by experienced physicians.ResultsCompared to 40 min. acquisitions and both in patients and healthy controls, 10 min acquisitions resulted in a significantly higher specific binding ratio (SBR) (≥ 0.5) in the striatum, putamen and caudate (p ≤ 0.001). SBR did not differ statistically significant between 30- and 40-min. acquisitions. Mean SBR difference between 20- and 40-min. scans with the 40-min. scan as a reference was 0.07 with 95% CI [-0.45, 0.59] for the striatum. Visual inspection of images showed that 20 min. acquisitions resulted in acceptable image quality (≥84% rated as sufficient).ConclusionWe find that the scan time with DAT SPECT imaging on SPECT/CT and MPH collimators can be reduced to 20 min. without compromising image quality and diagnostic outcome. A scan duration of 20 min is beneficial for both patient comfort and the clinical workflow.

Background

Functional imaging of the dopamine transporter (DAT) in the presynaptic neuronal projection areas is commonly used in the clinical work-up of patients with movement disorders, including Parkinson Disease (PD) and atypical parkinsonian disorders. [123I]FP-CIT is an established and commercially available radioligand for cerebral SPECT imaging of DAT [1–5].

DAT imaging is typically performed using general-purpose dual-head SPECT cameras with low-energy high-resolution (LEHR) parallel-hole collimators, as recommended in the guidelines of the European Association of Nuclear Medicine, EANM [6,7] and the Society of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging, SNMMI [8]. The procedure necessitates a scan time of typically 30 to 60 min. and both EANM and SNMMI guidelines recommend, if available, to use a dedicated high sensitivity system for [123I]FP-CIT DAT imaging.

Mediso (Budapest, Hungary) launched in 2020 the new three-headed SPECT camera (AnyScan Trio) with dedicated brain multi-pinhole collimators (MPH). The MPH-collimators have at least 2.4 times better sensitivity in the FOV (~ 250 cps/MBq at the FOV-borders) than conventional systems and in the FOV-center the sensitivity is even an extra factor of ~ 2.5 higher since MPH-collimators do not have a flat sensitivity profile. The higher sensitivity of the AnyScan Trio SPECT-scanner enables a theoretical reduction in scan duration by a factor of 2.4, corresponding to approx. 25 min. This would constitute a significant benefit for both patients and the clinical workflow. Due to both a better sensitivity and resolution [9] we would also expect to be able to visualize more details with DAT-SPECT multi-pinhole scan images than standard brain SPECT scanners [10–12]. The MPH-collimators are superior to common parallel-hole collimators and have the advantage that the scatter effect of the 123I’s high energy photons is less pronounced since there is no septal penetration [13]. A disadvantage of the MPH-collimators is the limited axial field of view size of 15 cm (19 cm with continuous table movement and later reduced to respectively 13 cm and 17 cm) and the complexity of the reconstruction.

The aim of this study was to investigate whether the duration of DAT-scanning with [123I]FP-CIT AnySan Trio SPECT/CT with MPH collimators can be reduced without compromising image quality and diagnostic outcome. More specifically, we hypothesized that in this way, scan duration can be reduced to 20 min.

Methods

Subjects

Nine healthy controls (5 females and 4 males, mean age: 59.0 years, range: 57–66 years) were included to constitute a normal reference database; none of them had any history of neurological or psychiatric disorders and they all had normal blood chemistry, neurological examination and MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination). They all gave informed written consent. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declarations of Helsinki and was approved by the ethical committee of Copenhagen Capital Region (protocol number: H-19082644).

We analyzed images from patients scanned as part of the diagnostic work-up for possible parkinsonian disorder. Images from 50 unselected patients were included retrospectively (24 females and 26 males, mean age: 74.7 years (range: 52–87 years)). Patients were scanned from May 2020 to April 2021.

Tracer injection and imaging procedures

In order to block thyroidal uptake of free radioiodine, all subjects received 200 mg potassium perchloride intravenously 5–10 min. before tracer injection. The SPECT scan was performed 3 h after injection of mean 185 MBq (range 177–205 MBq) [123I]FP-CIT (123I-ioflupane, DaTscan™, GE Healthcare B.V, Eindhoven, the Netherlands). All patients and healthy controls were scanned with a duration of 40 min. (acquired as four scans of 10 min.) [14]. A scan of 40 min. duration was created by summing of the four 10 min. scans and used as a reference for both correlation graphs and difference plots. A three-headed dedicated brain SPECT/CT camera (AnyScan Trio SC, Mediso Medical Imaging Systems, Budapest, Hungary) with second generation general purpose brain MPH-collimators with 30 pinholes each [9,15] was used for DAT-scanning with a 20% width energy window centered at 159 keV. A localizer CT scan was used for positioning the patient in scanner. Further, a low dose CT was performed for attenuation correction. Starting with the projection data (for all the angle positions) of the first scan and by sequentially adding later scans projection data for four different scan durations of 10 (the first scan), 20, 30 and 40 min. were created. Reconstruction of the DAT-SPECT images was performed using the Tera-Tomo Q procedure in the Mediso software. Projection (raw) data was acquired in dynamic or so-called “frame mode”. For each 10 min. scan projection data was acquired with three heads at 24 angles yielding 72 angles in total with a two-dimensional isotropic pixel size of 2.3 mm. For each 10 min. scan every head rotated 120 degrees. The projection data was reconstructed with an iterative ordered subsets expectation algorithm including CT-based attenuation correction and scatter correction with a Monte Carlo algorithm. Settings were 36 iterations and 3 subsets corresponding to 36 updates (other vendors typically the number of iterations in this case as 12) and medium quality for the Monte Carlo scatter correction. Decay correction is performed in the reconstruction process. The resulting isotropic voxel size of the reconstructed images was 1.8 mm. Head motion is restricted by use of a dedicated head holder and wedges of foam.

ROI delineation and SBR calculation

We used the ratio of specific binding in striatal regions to nonspecific binding in a reference region. SBR was calculated for the images acquired three hours after tracer injection as a measure of the DAT availability. ROIs were delineated and SBR was calculated using DATquan [16]. DATquan offers a fast, accurate, and highly reproducible method for semi-automatic ROI delineation using a template-based approach.

Visual interpretation

First, a pilot study was conducted to test intra-reader visual agreement. Fifteen patients and five healthy controls were randomly selected for visual inspection. The pilot test consisted of 20 sets of 4 images (for respectively 10-, 20-, 30- and 40-min. scan duration for each individual patient/healthy control). Visual inspection of the DAT scans was performed independently by two blinded internal raters (GT, LHP) with more than ten years of experience in clinical reading of DAT-SPECT. The readers were asked to score each DAT-SPECT image for scan quality as sufficient (e.g. comma-shape with symmetric well- delineated borders or asymmetric or symmetric dot-shape with homogeneous background activity) or insufficient (e.g. noisy background activity and barbell shape of the striatum).

After the pilot study, 50 patients and nine healthy controls were selected for visual interpretation. One of the patient was excluded from the evaluation because a barbell-shape was present in all four scans and one patient and one healthy control was excluded because of technical problems of the images. Then, the test consisted of 56 cases of (four images for respectively 10-, 20-, 30- and 40-min. scan duration for each individual subject). The DAT-SPECT images were visually evaluated from printout A4 of 12 axial slices using a color scale from 10% to 90% of the maximum counts in the image (same color scale of consistency and level of contrast and background subtraction carefully checked), similar to the display used in everyday clinical routine. Visual interpretation of the DAT scan quality (insufficient or sufficient) was performed by one external rater (KM) with 10 years of experience in clinical reading of DAT-imaging, blinded to the clinical information. The reader was also asked to assess if the images were consistent with a neurodegeneration typical reduction or normal DAT uptake and specifically, if putamen and caudate showed a DAT reduction, and if uptake was asymmetrical.

Statistical analysis

Absence of SBR-difference at different scan durations constituted the null-hypothesis. The significance level was set at 0.05. The 40 min. scan was used as the reference for the t-tests were performed for both patients and control subjects. In addition, two-sided paired t-tests were used as post-hoc analysis to determine any significant difference. The Bonferroni correction was used to adjust the significance level for three comparisons. Data is shown in correlation plots together with the coefficient of determination R2 and regression line. Difference plots similar to Bland-Altman plots, but with the reference value on the horizontal axis, show both variability and bias relative to the 40 min. reference scan.

Clinical diagnosis and SBR-values

All patient data is reviewed for change in the clinical diagnosis using the semi-automatic ROI for calculation of SBR. There was no change from normal to abnormal SBR-values or vice versa from 40 to 30 min. From 40 min. to 20 min. there were two borderline-cases, where the SBR-value altered slightly from an abnormal to a normal value according to the diagnostic threshold value, but it did not change the clinical diagnosis for those two cases.

Visual interpretation

The pilot study showed 100% consensus between the two readers regarding image quality for 10, 30 and 40 min. images, and 95% consensus for the 20 min. scans.

For all patients and controls, the 40 min. images were visually rated as sufficient: Comma-shape with symmetric well-delineated borders or asymmetric or symmetric dot-shape with homogeneous background activity. For 30 min. scans, two images were rated as insufficient due to noisy background activity, but based on the clinical follow-up, a correct diagnosis was still achieved. For 20 min. scans, 16% were rated as insufficient and for 10 min. scan, 61% were rated as insufficient, as shown in Table 3.

Discussion

In this study we find that the scan duration on the Mediso AnyScan can be reduced from 40 to 20 min - but not 10 min - without compromising the image quality significantly. A shorter scan duration allows for an optimized and more efficient use of the scanner with a faster patient throughput and also helps to improve patient comfort during the scan. Patients with parkinsonism and memory impairment may be particularly challenged by lying in a scanner for a longer time and shorter scans decrease the risk for motion artifacts. In all instances, images should always be critically examined for signs of head motion.

The EANM practice guideline/SNMMI procedure standard for dopaminergic imaging in Parkinsonian syndromes (6) recommended minimum count level for this imaging task is a minimum of 1.5 M counts when acquired using parallel hole or fan beam collimators in the clinic. As seen in Table 1, even though the scanner configuration is different regarding collimator and sensitivity profile, 20 min. scans yield approx. 1.4 Mcts, so 20 min. scans (almost) fulfil the minimum of 1.5 Mcts as stated in the guidelines for parallel hole collimators.

In this study we used an in-house automated segmentation tool for semi-quantitative DAT SBR values for the striatum, caudate, and putamen (16) We find a strong correlation between SBRs for 30 vs. 40 min. and 20 vs. 40 min. scan durations as shown in Fig. 1; Table 4. The 20 min. and 30 min. scans show a good correlation with the 40 min. scans, while the differences in SBR are close to zero as shown in the difference plots in Fig. 2. The SBRs from the 10 min. scans have a lower correlation to the 40 min scan and also introduce a possible bias of approximately 0.5 in the SBR-value as can be seen in Fig. 2. SBR-values for the 10 min. scans are also noisier. Although there are statistically significant differences between the 20 min. and 40 min. scans for striatum and caudate (Table 2), the mean difference in SBR is small as 0.07 and 0.10, respectively, as can be seen in Table 5. The agreement between both the 20- and 30-min. scans and the 40 min. scans holds for wide range of SBR-values.

Regarding visual interpretation none of the images at 30- and 40-min. scans were considered too noisy or insufficient for diagnostic outcome, see Table 3; Fig. 3, which shows representative examples for two cases with 10-, 20-,30-, and 40 min. scans. For 20 min. scans 16% were rated as insufficient. A possible reason for the observed variation in DAT-SPECT interpretation is that the reader also has experience with DAT-PET, and thus more accustomed to its higher spatial resolution.

Our results should be seen in the context of similar previous studies. Mathies et al. (17) used the same type of scanner with MPH collimators and a scan time of 30 min and a dose of 182 MBq [123I]FP-CIT and they showed that MPH collimators improve the certainty of the visual interpretation in DAT-SPECT. This was particularly helpful for exclusion of nigrostriatal degeneration, and they recommend 30 min acquisition time for routine clinical use.

Buchert et al. [9] also used an identical scanner with MPH collimators and a triple-head system and report that DAT-SPECT images are reliable with a 12-min scan duration and a dose of about 180 MBq [123I]FP-CIT. Our data do not support this conclusion, although that study included a large number of patients (n = 640). For both the automatic method (for extracting regional values) and the visual inspection we used 1.8 mm thick slices; we chose this slice thickness to maintain a 3-dimensional analysis instead of a (nearly) 2-dimensional analysis with, e.g., 12 mm thick slabs as is used in Buchert et al. [9] for visual inspection. An important difference between this study and the work of Buchert et al. is that the latter utilizes a different method for calculation of SBR, which yield higher values for the coefficient of determination R2 than the values in Table 4. Another difference is that in the work of Buchert et al. neither attenuation nor scatter correction is used. The difference analysis, which includes the difference plots that are similar to the Bland-Altman plots, is more sensitive to possible differences than inspection of scatter plots and the calculation of R2 and it reveals significant differences between the 10- and 40-min. scans. For the striatum, R2-values were 0.79 for the 10 min. scans and above 0.9 for the longer scans indicating good correlation between all scans. However, the difference analysis reveals a possible bias (of approximately + 0.5 SBR) and a significantly larger confidence interval for the 10 min. scans, whereas this is not observed for the 20- and 30-min. scans.

A limitation of this current study is that the acquisition data were not acquired in list mode, accordingly we have only data from 10-, 20-, 30-, 40- min. scans. It is possible that scan durations slightly below 20 min. also are sufficient.

In this study, we focused on the optimal scan duration and not on reducing administered radioactivity. All subjects had a standard administered activity according to the EANM practice guideline/SNMMI procedure standard for dopaminergic imaging in Parkinsonian syndromes [6]. Alternatively, the administered amount of radioactivity can be reduced instead of reducing the acquisition length.

In addition to reducing the scan duration, there are also other advantages of using dedicated MPH collimators. For example, Tecklenburg et al. [15] find in their study using the first generation MPH-collimators that the largest benefit of improved image quality and spatial resolution in DAT-SPECT with MPH collimators is expected for early detection (or exclusion) of PD before the loss of putaminal DAT reaches 50% and PD-characteristic motor symptoms occur.

Conclusion

Our data show that with the SPECT/CT with MPH collimators, the scan duration can be reduced from 40 to 20 min. without compromising image quality and diagnostic certainty. The shorter scan duration is an important advantage for patient comfort and the clinical workflow. Alternatively, the administered amount of radioactivity can be reduced.
