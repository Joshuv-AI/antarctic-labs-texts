Polypropylene Nanoplastic Exposure to Respiratory Epithelial Barrier‐On‐Chip and Interfacial Interactions With Human Serum Albumin
Authors: Omur Sert, Eyup Bilgi, Reyhan Coban, Pelin Saglam‐Metiner, Basar Dogan, Zeynep Imir‐Tekneci, Suleyman Selim Cinaroglu, Ozlem Goksel, Ceyda Oksel Karakus, Ozlem Yesil‐Celiktas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnder the influence of environmental pollution, chronic non‐communicable diseases have increased. The epithelial lining of the airway forms the initial barrier; however, upon exposure, nanoplastics (NPs) may cross this interface and enter systemic circulation. This study aims to elucidate the effects of polypropylene nanoplastics (PP NPs) on the epithelial barrier and the interactions with serum proteins. PP beads were chemically degraded, characterized, and administered to the epithelial barrier‐on‐chip at a dose of 250 μg/mL, followed by continuous perfusion for 3 days. Their colloidal and biological behavior was investigated in cell culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. To explore clinical relevance, the interaction of PP NPs with human serum albumin was analyzed using molecular dynamics simulations, supported by protein profiling of plasma samples from healthy male and female individuals to understand gender‐related differences. PP NPs with an average size of 305 nm were exposed to the epithelial barrier‐on‐chip leading to a decrease in cell viability via apoptotic cell death and transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) value from ~2600 to 2200 Ω·cm2 with altered ZO‐1 and ACE2 expression, as well as an increased proinflammatory response and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. [Correction added on 15 April 2026, after first online publication: The word “suppressed” has been deleted from the abstract.] The size of NPs increased to 364 nm after incubation with cell culture medium, indicating protein corona formation. The simulation revealed a biphasic adsorption pattern. The major differences in blood plasma of male and female donors were observed in the protein bands around 130 and 180 kDa. By integrating experimental and computational approaches, this study advances our understanding of how inhaled NPs may interact with blood proteins upon systemic exposure, with potential implications for human health risk assessment.

Introduction

Plastic waste pollution, particularly in the form of micro‐ and nanoplastics (MNPs), poses a growing threat to global ecosystems and human health. Advances in methods for detecting tiny pieces of plastic have proven what has long been suspected and feared: MNPs do stay in our bodies. The latest research shows that large plastic pieces are broken down into smaller fragments, which are further fragmented into tiny particles, referred to as nanoplastics (NPs). The most common types of NPs are categorized based on the synthetic polymer they are made from, such as polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polypropylene (PP), polystyrene (PS), and polyethylene (PE) [1]. Once NPs enter the body by inhalation, ingestion or contact with skin, the particles could evidently pass into blood, body tissues and fluids, instead of staying in the digestive tract and being excreted [2], where they may induce inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, DNA damage, and disruption of cellular membrane integrity [3]. Given that MNPs are airborne and readily present in outdoor and indoor air environments [4], the respiratory tract is inevitably bound to interact with inhalable MNPs. In recent studies, PP exposure has been linked to a range of adverse biological effects. Woo et al. reported that PP induces pulmonary inflammation, along with the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) due to mitochondrial damage and activation of the NF‐κB signaling pathway [5]. The epithelial barrier creates a formidable defense to control the passage of inhaled NPs to the underlying tissues. However, high concentrations of small‐sized particles may diffuse and enter the cardiovascular system by passing through the epithelial barrier [6]. With the shift toward non‐animal testing [7, 8], physiologically relevant in vitro models are used to investigate the effects of inhaled NPs on the lung epithelium. Trans‐well systems are one of the fundamental techniques for studying exposure [9]. However, epithelial barrier‐on‐chip approaches more effectively replicate the complexity of physiological microenvironments by incorporating controllable flow conditions [10, 11] and mechanical stretching [12]. While exposure conditions can alter the defense mechanisms of the epithelial barrier, circulating NPs may pose health risks to deeper tissues and organs. Using an in vivo mouse model, Huang and coworkers showed that PP NPs can enter the systemic circulation, accumulate within cerebral capillaries, and induce thrombosis, ultimately leading to neurobehavioral impairments [13]. These observations support a sequential scenario in which inhaled PP NPs first challenge the airway epithelial barrier and, once this interface is compromised, may gain access to the bloodstream.

The fate of NPs in physiological systems is affected by various intrinsic physicochemical parameters as well as extrinsic physiological conditions [14]. PP NPs are expected to differ from PS and PE, the two most widely studied reference NP types that dominate the current NPs literature, in their physicochemical properties and related biological behavior. Unlike PS, which contains electron‐rich, conjugated aromatic ring units that promote strong π–π and hydrophobic interactions with biomolecules, PP has a fully aliphatic structure with only methyl side groups (i.e., no polar functional groups or aromatic rings), resulting in much weaker surface interactions [15]. While both PP and PE are aliphatic polyolefins with saturated hydrocarbon backbones, their key structural difference is that PP carries methyl side groups, whereas PE contains only hydrogen atoms attached to the backbone. The methyl side groups in PP influence its crystallinity, oxidative susceptibility, and surface reactivity, which, in return, can alter both colloidal stability and cellular interactions [16, 17]. Considering these structural differences, the biological behavior of PP NPs cannot be reliably predicted from PS or PE models.

Not only intrinsic physicochemical features, but also extrinsic environmental and physiological factors shape the biological behavior of NPs. One key extrinsic parameter that modulates cellular uptake and biodistribution of NPs is their interaction with biomolecules, particularly proteins [18]. The surfaces of NPs are covered by proteins following exposure to biological fluids, forming the so‐called protein corona layer [19, 20]. The competitive adsorption/desorption of proteins to NP surfaces and the resulting protein corona layer are strongly influenced by the presence of different functional groups, surface characteristics, and the variability introduced by aging in natural environments [21, 22]. Upon the entry of foreign substances such as plastics into the body, proteins are typically among the first biological structures encountered [23]. Importantly, small bioactive peptides with therapeutic potential may not only contribute to protein corona formation but also influence nanoparticle uptake and cellular responses [24]. Elucidating the pharmacokinetics of major blood proteins as well as their binding affinity to NPs is essential for understanding the potential physiological disruptions that PP may cause. Significant progress has been made in understanding protein interactions of inorganic NPs such as nanostructured metallics and metal oxides that are commonly used in biomedical applications [25]. However, research on NP‐protein interactions remains relatively limited [18], with the majority focusing on PS or PE NPs and overlooking other major NP groups such as PP NPs, a widely used thermoplastic in packaging materials, medical supplies, clothing fibers and composites [26]. The physical and oxidative degradation of PP leads to the micro‐ and nano‐sized particles, contributing to the widespread presence across multiple human exposure pathways [5, 27].

Although PP is manufactured at extremely high volumes, its recycling rate remains very low. While 9%–10% of all plastics are recycled globally [28], the recycling rate of PP remains below 1% [29], despite annual production volume exceeding 97 million tons worldwide [30]. The low recycling rate of PP is partly due to the limited understanding of its enzymatic and physicochemical degradation pathways. They tend to persist in the environment because they are highly resistant to degradation, often requiring strong initiators such as potassium persulfate produced from highly reactive sulfate radicals [31]. However, chemical stability does not prevent PP from breaking into micro‐ or nanoscale fragments. Many factors, including physical abrasion, UV exposure, temperature fluctuations, and mechanical stress, can gradually reduce larger PP pieces into smaller NPs. Thus, even polymers that degrade slowly at the molecular level may still produce environmentally relevant NPs. Indeed, an increasing body of evidence indicates that people are exposed to PP MNPs through inhalation and other exposure pathways [31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. Considering PP's massive production volume, low recycling rate, and increasing evidence of airborne exposure, understanding its biological interactions is scientifically important and urgently needed.

This study provides the first detailed experimental and computational investigation of the interaction between PP NPs, produced through chemical degradation of macro‐sized PP beads, and albumin, a major protein component of blood plasma. By combining epithelial barrier‐on‐chip experiments, in silico molecular dynamics simulations, and plasma protein analyses, it establishes an integrated framework to elucidate how PP NPs pass through the lung epithelial barrier, enter systemic circulation, and interact with plasma proteins.

Materials and Methods

Briefly, commercial PP microbeads (Sigma‐Aldrich, Product ID: 427888, Mw≈250,000; Mn≈67,000) were chemically degraded to NPs and physicochemically characterized. In parallel, the airway epithelial barrier‐on‐chip platform was fabricated by replica molding of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were performed to examine the diffusion of NPs across the epithelial barrier. Subsequently, CALU‐3 cells were cultured, and NPs were introduced to the epithelial barrier‐on‐chip for 72 h. Toxicity and barrier integrity were assessed. Furthermore, THP‐1, the human monocytic cell line, was employed to elicit the immune response induced by PP NPs and evaluate the endotoxin content of the NPs, using a lower concentration (15 μg/mL) and 1 μg/mL LPS as a positive control. Given that barrier impairment may allow PP NPs to reach the bloodstream, subsequent analyses focused on their interactions with major plasma proteins to model the systemic phase of exposure. Protein corona formation studies were conducted using human blood plasma samples as well as cell culture media (DMEM +10% FBS). Bound proteins were extracted and analyzed both qualitatively and quantitatively via SDS‐PAGE and bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Finally, coarse‐grained molecular dynamics (CGMD) simulations were performed to analyze the molecular protein corona formation kinetics. Detailed materials and methods are available online as Appendix S1.

Results

The chemical degradation of isotactic PP microbeads into micro‐ and nano‐sized particles was systematically studied by varying solvent type, reaction temperature, reaction time, and quenching time using a multilevel categorical design approach (Table S1). Results indicated that the degradation efficiency was influenced by the solvent type and temperature, followed by the reaction time and quenching conditions. Optimal results were achieved with xylene as the solvent, a high‐temperature setting (> 135°C), a reaction time of 30 min, and a quenching duration of 24 h, leading to the formation of PP NPs with the smallest average particle size and consistent morphology. The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis confirmed the retention of the polymer's structural integrity during the degradation process (Figure 1A,B). Characteristic absorption peaks were observed at 2956 cm−1, 2875 cm−1, and 2840 cm−1, corresponding to CH stretching vibrations, while the peak at 2921 cm−1 was attributed to asymmetric CH stretching. Peaks at 1465 cm−1 and 1377 cm−1 were attributed to CH3 asymmetric and symmetric bending, respectively.

Additionally, C—C stretching vibrations were identified at 1160 cm−1 and 840 cm−1. Complete bead degradation was only achieved under high temperatures (≥ 138°C) with xylene, whereas toluene and chloroform only resulted in partial or superficial degradation. The proximity of the Hildebrand solubility parameter of xylene (18.0 MPa1/2) and isotactic PP (14.9 MPa1/2) may have contributed to its high efficiency in facilitating polymer degradation [36, 37]. Additionally, the position of the methyl group in the solvent (ortho, meta or para) plays a crucial role in determining the efficiency of polymer degradation [38]. In our study, a mixture of ortho‐, meta‐, and para‐xylene isomers was used to enhance degradation efficiency. SEM analysis (Figure 1C–F) revealed a polydisperse structure, with particle sizes ranging from 300 to 1000 nm. Importantly, FTIR spectra of PP processed via xylene‐assisted degradation overlapped closely with those of mechanically fragmented PP (including fragmentation in water), confirming that the PP chemical fingerprint remained unchanged and that no detectable solvent‐related signatures were present [39, 40]. In particular, neither aromatic bands attributable to xylene (≈3100–3000 cm−1 region (aromatic C—H stretching) and prominent ring‐related bands around ≈1600–1500 cm−1, along with additional aromatic C—H out‐of‐plane/ring modes typically found in the ≈900–700 cm−1 region) nor the characteristic acetone carbonyl (C=O) band near ~1715 cm−1 were observed, indicating that any residual solvent‐if present‐is below the detection limit of FTIR (Figure S1) [41, 42]. To obtain a uniform particle distribution and enhance the dispersion of hydrophobic NPs in aqueous systems, an additional post‐synthesis homogenization step involving ultra‐turrax treatment in acetone was performed, which facilitated the formation of uniform structures and enabled the transfer of highly hydrophobic PP NPs into water. SEM images confirmed that chloroform‐degraded PP exhibited a sticky texture that remained even after filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane, suggesting incomplete polymer breakdown and chain entanglement. Similarly, toluene‐degraded PP displayed a sticky bulk texture, indicating partial degradation but insufficient fragmentation to disrupt polymer cohesion. Xylene‐degraded PP demonstrated a more favorable degradation profile, yielding sub‐0.22 μm particles with a uniform size (~300 nm), while larger fractions (above 0.22 μm) remained polydisperse, but in a powdered form rather than sticky aggregates. The powdered morphology improved dispersibility and handling, making xylene the most effective solvent for further studies. TGA results of the degraded PP revealed that degradation onset occurred at 304°C (T5), with significant weight loss progressing through 388°C (T10), 422°C (T20), and 455°C (T50), ultimately reaching complete decomposition at approximately 501°C (T100) (Figure 1G). The average particle size and zeta potential of PP NPs were 305 nm and −30.8 mV, respectively (Figure 1H,I).

The next step was to expose these NPs to the epithelial barrier‐on‐chip platform, which was fabricated in our previous study, in a three‐layered microfluidic device consisting of a top air chamber, a porous PET membrane populated with lung epithelial cells, and a bottom flow chamber (Figure 2A) [10]. Simulation studies were carried out to predict the diffusion rate of PP over the experimental period, revealing that PP concentration on the top surface of the membrane remained relatively constant, while a time‐dependent concentration gradient developed toward the bottom surface. The model produced 3D surface maps showing PP accumulation only on the top surface at t = 0, whereas diffusion‐driven transport of PP was apparent at t = 7 (Figure 2B and Table S2). After assembly, PP NPs was administered at a dose of 250 μg/mL to the epithelial barrier‐on‐chip model, and perfused for 3 days at a flow rate of 9 μL/s as previously optimized [10]. The group carried out solely in growth medium without PP NP exposure and growth medium containing only the solvent vehicle without PP NPs were used as negative controls. Based on geometric mass‐to‐particle conversion for 305‐nm PP NPs (ρ = 0.90 g/cm3), this dose corresponds to approximately 1.9 × 1010 particles/mL and a total nanoparticle surface area of ~5.5 × 109 μm2/mL (see Appendix S1 for details), which lies within the 1010–1011 particles/mL range commonly used in in vitro nanoparticle exposure studies [43, 44]. At the end of the 3‐day exposure, the membranes seeded with epithelial cells were harvested and stained with anti‐ZO‐1 and ACE‐2 antibodies (Figure 2C). ZO‐1, which is specific for epithelial cells and known to decrease after NP exposure, resulting in altered intercellular connections, tight junction polarity and barrier integrity [45, 46]. In polarized epithelial cells, multiple tight‐junction proteins (e.g., claudins, occludin) play active roles in cell‐to‐cell binding. This process, initiated particularly in the early stages of binding complex formation via nectin and E‐cadherin, continues with junctional maturation and the binding of the ZO‐1/α‐catenin pair to the cytoskeleton [47]. In this study, the central cytoskeleton protein ZO‐1 was deliberately examined because it spatially organizes all tight‐junction proteins, ensuring epithelial barrier integrity. Since ZO‐1 functions as a whole with multiple tight‐junction proteins, it can already be disrupted if there is a disorder in other tight‐junction proteins. To understand whether barrier damage is truly mediated by PP, a peripheral exposome source, a more holistic approach was one of the focus of this study, and the ZO‐1 marker was sufficient. This marker is already frequently used in the literature to assess barrier integrity within CALU‐3 cells [48, 49]. All findings were compared with those of the untreated control group, which demonstrated intact epithelial barrier integrity in CALU‐3 cells.

ACE‐2, which is expressed on the cell surface of respiratory tract epithelial cells, has also been reported to increase with exposure to environmental factors [50, 51], thus we investigated the mechanistic effects of PP exposure on the epithelial barrier by observing the changes in the expression levels of ACE2. Our previous study also showed that it increased as a result of exposure to the influenza virus [52]. Accordingly, under the control and PP‐free solvent group, ACE‐2 displayed a consistent basal expression pattern along the epithelial monolayer. In contrast, PP NPs exposed CALU‐3 cells exhibited a visibly enhanced ACE‐2 IF signal intensity despite a decrease in cell number and barrier disruption, suggesting that PP exposure is associated with altered ACE‐2 expression and epithelial stress‐associated response at the epithelial barrier. This change occurred concomitantly with tight‐junction alterations, as indicated by ZO‐1 staining, highlighting a coordinated epithelial response to PP exposure (Figure 2C and Figure S2A).

After exposure to PP NPs, TEER was employed to quantitatively measure the integrity of tight junctions [53]. A reduction in TEER values of CALU‐3 cells was observed compared to the non‐exposed controls, decreasing from 2605.81 ± 84.48 to 2199.54 ± 72.95 Ω·cm2 (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2D). Furthermore, there were no significant changes in the solvent group (2566 ± 65.46 Ω·cm2, p > 0.05). The cytotoxic effects of PP exposure were further evaluated by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release. Due to increased cell death resulting from PP exposure, the amount of LDH released from the barrier increased significantly, whereas this difference was not significant in the PP‐free solvent group (control: ~14% ± 2.6%; PP: ~31% ± 1.14%, p < 0.001; solvent: ~15% ± 0.92%, p > 0.05) (Figure 2E). Since barrier damage was the primary focus of the current study, in addition to immunofluorescence and TEER analyses, the secretion levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines TNF‐α and IL‐1β were also examined. Increased levels of these cytokines have been associated with altered organization of tight‐junction proteins and increased epithelial permeability [54, 55, 56]. As part of the pro‐inflammatory response, TNF‐α levels increased to 58.89 ± 3.02 pg/mL compared to 36.56 ± 2.87 pg/mL in the control group (p < 0.01) and no significant inflammatory response was observed in the PP‐free solvent group (39.15 ± 0.98, p > 0.05) (Figure 2F). As for IL‐1β expressions, there was a statistically significant difference between the control (426.34 ± 10.54 pg/mL) and PP NP exposure (615.58 ± 11.77 pg/mL) groups (p < 0.001) and the release profile of the solvent group was similar to that of non‐exposed controls (448.88 ± 2.98, p > 0.05) (Figure 2G). In parallel, TSLP, an epithelial‐derived cytokine and also one of the alarmins, was significantly increased in the PP group to 218.94 ± 10.21 pg/mL compared to control 166.17 ± 4.40 pg/mL (p < 0.001) (Figure 2H). Similarly, levels of IL‐33, an alarmin associated with epithelial stress and barrier injury, were significantly elevated upon PP exposure to 102.64 ± 2.27 pg/mL (control: 38.09 ± 7.35 pg/mL, p < 0.001) (Figure 2I). For both alarmin proteins, no significant release was observed in the PP‐free solvent group (TSLP: 170.52 ± 10.06 pg/mL; IL‐33: 35.14 ± 0.87 pg/mL, p > 0.05).

On the other hand, THP‐1 monocytes and CALU‐3 cells were employed to assess immune responses and determine endotoxin content of both PP NP and PP NP‐free solvent, using a lower particle concentration, along with lipopolysaccharides (LPS) as a positive control. Endotoxins, such as LPS derived from gram‐negative bacteria, are known to trigger the production of proinflammatory cytokines [57]. No significant changes in cell proliferation (p > 0.05) or morphology were observed at the lower concentration of 5 μg/mL, compared to the control group. In contrast, exposure to the inflammatory on‐chip concentration (250 μg/mL) of PP NPs, as well as LPS, induced notable immune cell activation and proliferation in both THP‐1 monocytes and CALU‐3 cells (Figure S3A,B). TNF‐α secretion exhibited a cell type dependent response to PP NP exposure. In THP‐1 cells, TNF‐α levels remained unchanged in the solvent (166.7 ± 2.9 pg/mL, p > 0.05) and low‐dose groups (160.6 ± 7.6 pg/mL, p > 0.05) compared to untreated control group (166.7 ± 2.9 pg/mL), whereas the high‐dose PP induced a significant increase (178.9 ± 3.8 pg/mL, p < 0.05), similar to the strong response elicited by LPS (191.7 ± 5.3 pg/mL, p < 0.0001). In Calu‐3 epithelial cells, TNF‐α secretion was measured as 132.8 ± 8.4 in the control group without any significance in the solvent group (141.2 ± 14.1 pg/mL, p > 0.05). Notably, PP exposure elicited a marked and dose‐dependent increase in TNF‐α release, with both low‐dose (203.1 ± 3.6 pg/mL, p < 0.0001) and high‐dose PP treatments (215.0 ± 3.7 pg/mL, p < 0.0001) significantly exceeding control levels, closely mirroring the strong pro‐inflammatory response observed following LPS stimulation (218.9 ± 2.6 pg/mL, p < 0.0001) (Figure S3C). IL‐1β secretion exhibited a similar pattern in both THP‐1 and CALU‐3 cells. The highest levels of IL‐1β secretion were detected in the LPS‐treated groups (889.7 ± 19.9 pg/mL and 776.1 ± 4.9 pg/mL, p < 0.0001, respectively), while increasing doses of PP NP exposure resulted in a dose‐dependent increase (from 809 ± 12.1 pg/mL, p < 0.001 to 872.7 ± 51.3 pg/mL, p < 0.0001 for THP‐1 cells, from 740.3 ± 13.4 pg/mL, p < 0.0001 to 765.3 ± 8.2 pg/mL, p < 0.0001 for CALU‐3 cells) in IL‐1β secretion. In contrast, no statistically significant difference (p > 0.05) was observed between the solvent (731.2 ± 25.2 pg/mL and 659 ± 19 pg/mL, respectively) and control groups (686.3 ± 21.1 pg/mL and 647.6 ± 7 pg/mL, respectively) (Figure S3D). Notably, pro‐inflammatory cytokine levels in the low‐dose PP NP group remained markedly lower than those induced by high‐dose PP exposure or LPS stimulation, indicating a clear concentration‐dependent inflammatory response. Collectively, these data demonstrate that the observed cytokine induction cannot be attributed to solvent effects or endotoxin contamination. Instead, the results support the conclusion that the PP NPs and PP‐free solvent were endotoxin‐free and that the pronounced pro‐inflammatory responses observed at higher concentrations were primarily driven by PP NP associated cytotoxicity, in agreement with our previous findings [10].

Additionally, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels after PP NP exposure were measured both quantitatively and qualitatively (Figure 2J and Figure S2B), using 2′,7′‐dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH‐DA) dye as a general oxidative stress indicator [58, 59]. In the untreated negative control group, the lowest fluorescence intensity was measured as 17.4 ± 0.4, while the solvent control group without PP NP exhibited a fluorescence intensity of 18.7 ± 1.14 (p > 0.05). In comparison, the PP NP exposure group displayed a markedly increased fluorescence intensity of 48.9 ± 0.85 (p < 0.0001), while the highest fluorescence intensity was observed in the positive control group treated with H2O2, reaching 57.6 ± 0.85 (p < 0.0001). These findings demonstrate that PP NP exposure induces oxidative stress and significantly elevates ROS levels in the epithelial barrier.

To further elucidate the molecular response to PP exposure, the expression levels of genes associated with apoptosis and proliferation were assessed by qRT‐PCR (Figure 2K and Table S3). PP exposure resulted in a marked upregulation of the CASP‐3 gene (6.50‐fold), increase relative to the control, indicating activation of apoptotic signaling [60]. In contrast, the proliferation and cell survival associated markers Ki‐67 and PCNA were strongly downregulated −7.86‐fold and −8.51‐fold, respectively, compared to control conditions [61, 62]. In the PP‐free solvent group, only minor changes in gene expression were observed, with CASP‐3 showing a modest increase (1.12‐fold) and Ki‐67 and PCNA exhibiting slight downregulation (−2.01 and −1.6 fold, respectively), indicating minimal cytotoxic or anti‐proliferative effects of the PP‐free solvent alone. Additional, DAPI nuclear staining also showed that PP NP exposure induced apoptotic cell death in cells (Figure S2C), identified by apoptotic bodies observed in cell nuclei [62, 63]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that endotoxin free PP NP exposure compromised epithelial barrier integrity as evidenced by reduced TEER values and decreased junctional protein expression, accompanied by elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokine and alarmin release, and also impaired epithelial cell viability indicated by increased LDH release and intracellular ROS levels along with transcriptional upregulation of the apoptotic marker CASP‐3, downregulation of the proliferation markers Ki‐67 and PCNA and the presence of apoptotic cellular morphology, compared to untreated control and PP‐free solvent groups.

In parallel, PP NPs were incubated in a cell culture medium containing 10% BSA and SDS‐PAGE analysis was performed to confirm protein corona formation. After incubation, hard corona was separated through washing steps and quantitatively measured with BCA assay (ref). The cell culture medium as the control, along with PP‐treated groups (0.25 mg/mL and 1 mg/mL) yielded total protein concentrations of 4799.95 ± 471 μg/mL, 6582.12 ± 70 μg/mL and 7740.15 ± 102 μg/mL respectively (Figure 3A). Two PP concentrations (0.25 and 1 mg/mL) were used to demonstrate the dose‐dependent increase in hard corona formation and to ensure that differences in protein load reflected nanoparticle concentration rather than experimental variability. To enable quantitative comparison across concentrations, adsorbed protein amounts were additionally normalized to the estimated total PP NP surface area assuming spherical particle geometry (4 πr2). Surface area‐normalized protein adsorption was significantly higher at 0.25 mg/mL PP (118.5 ± 1.6 μg protein/μm2 PP surface area) compared to 1 mg/mL PP (34.8 ± 0.6 μg protein/μm2 PP surface area) (Figure 3B), indicating a non‐linear and surface‐saturation–limited corona formation behavior. Despite higher absolute protein amounts at increased PP concentration, the reduced protein load per unit surface area suggests competitive adsorption and packing constraints at elevated nanoparticle densities. The images of SDS‐PAGE gel, stained with Coomassie blue dye, were in agreement with the BCA assay results (Figure 3C). Band images obtained from SDS‐PAGE analysis were evaluated quantitatively using ImageJ software. Band signal values representing total protein load were measured over specific areas defined for each sample, density values were measured, followed by log transformation and normalization of the data (Figure 3D). In accordance with protein corona formation, PP NPs incubated with cell culture medium reached a mean hydrodynamic size of 364.1 nm and a zeta potential value of −14.5 mV (Figure 3E,F).

Given that our analysis showed PP exposure impaired epithelial barrier integrity and may enable the translocation of NPs into the bloodstream, we further conducted translational analyses by examining the interaction of PP NPs with human serum albumin using an in silico approach, complemented by plasma protein profiling from healthy male and female individuals to explore potential gender‐related differences.

Starting with the in silico approach, we investigated the interaction of PP NPs with human serum albumin (HSA), one of the major components of blood, using molecular dynamics simulations. All‐atom (ribbon diagrams) and coarse‐grained representations of HSA are depicted as colored beads according to the hydrophobicity of amino acids (Figure 4A), which emphasizes the amphipathic characteristics of albumin affecting adsorption.

Insertion of proteins and polymer was carried out under the guidance of hydrophobic region analysis of HSA (Figure 4B) by using the open‐source Expasy server and applying hydrophobicity scores to the topology of the protein [64]. On the polymer side, the MARTINI force field was used for coarse‐graining, ensuring a balance between computational efficiency and physicochemical accuracy [65]. Herein, the chemical structure of PP is shown as a representation of coarse‐grained mapping scheme by colored circles (Figure 4C). Radial density distribution refers to molecular density variation as a function of distance from a reference point [66]. The radial density distribution of protein beads relative to the NP center showed a distinct maximum of 0.00175 beads/nm3 around 75–80 nm, corresponding closely to the approximate radius of 76.10 nm, followed by a steady decline approaching zero beyond 150 nm (Figure 4D). While the analysis of residue‐specific contacts between albumin and the PP surface showed a dominance of hydrophobic residues in the interaction pattern (Figure 4E), structural snapshots and time‐series visualizations (initial → 500 ns) illustrated the adsorption process and associated conformational rearrangements accompanying binding (Figure 4F).

The final step was to incubate PP NPs with human blood plasma from male and female donors, leading to the formation of a detectable hard protein corona, after removal of the soft corona and unbound proteins (Figure 5A). Total protein of the hard corona was quantified by BCA assay. When stratified by sex (F: female, M: male), female control samples (representing plasma proteins remained on the membrane) retained 949.8 ± 242.4 μg·mL−1 (n = 4), whereas PP‐exposed female samples retained 1246.4 ± 222.8 μg·mL−1 (n = 4). Male control samples retained 653.3 ± 115.5 μg·mL−1 (n = 6), whereas PP‐exposed male samples retained 1069.9 ± 91.2 μg·mL−1 (n = 6). Both sexes showed higher retained protein levels after PP exposure (Figure 5A–C). The images of SDS‐PAGE gels revealed prominent bands corresponding to albumin, and the intensities of specific protein bands at approximately 55, 75, 130, and 180 kDa were of particular interest, as they represented the most abundant components. The intensities of protein bands at approximately 55 kDa and 75 kDa were significantly elevated in three donors compared to the control group (Figure 5D,E). Adsorbed proteins on PP NPs from male donors showed significantly higher band intensities at 130 and 180 kDa compared to the control, whereas no detectable bands were observed at these molecular weights in female samples (Figure 5F,G).

Discussion

In this study, chemically degraded PP NPs (average size ~305 nm) impaired airway epithelial barrier integrity in a barrier‐on‐chip model, evidenced by reduced TEER, altered ZO‐1 organization, increased LDH release, elevated intracellular ROS, and increased proinflammatory cytokine/alarmin levels under continuous perfusion for 72 h. PP NPs formed a protein corona in cell culture medium, reflected by increased hydrodynamic size (~364 nm) and a shift in zeta potential. Coarse‐grained molecular dynamics simulations indicated a biphasic adsorption behavior for human serum albumin on PP, and plasma corona profiling suggested sex‐dependent differences in adsorbed protein band patterns.

MNPs are predominantly generated through the progressive fragmentation of bulk plastic materials through both abiotic and biotic degradation pathways [67]. Upon entry into environmental compartments, plastic waste undergoes combined effects of photodegradation, mechanical abrasion, and thermo‐oxidative reactions [68], which facilitate degradation to smaller particles, ultimately transportation to diverse environmental reservoirs, including aquatic systems, terrestrial soils, and the atmosphere [69], making human exposure inevitable. Among NP types, PP exposure requires special attention due to its common use in products that frequently interact with the human body. In this study, several organic solvents were evaluated for their ability to degrade PP, and xylene was identified as the most effective solvent under elevated temperature conditions. However, the polydispersity of the produced particles was high, which could be partly attributed to the challenges associated with dispersing PP in aqueous systems due to its extreme hydrophobicity [70]. To overcome this limitation, an additional post‐synthesis homogenization step in acetone was applied, yielding a more uniform particle size distribution.

The importance of solvent type and reaction temperature in determining the morphology and dispersion characteristics of plastics is well documented in the literature. For example, Tsampanakis and Orbaek White [71] modeled the miscibility of different solvents with plastics and demonstrated that aromatic non‐polar solvents such as xylene, toluene, and benzene are among the most effective solvents for plastic degradation. FTIR spectra exhibited characteristic C‐C stretching bands at 1160 cm−1 and 840 cm−1, consistent with previously reported spectra [72, 73, 74], confirming that no structural modifications or incorporation of functional groups occurred during xylene‐mediated degradation or ethanol quenching. This consistency highlights the stability of PP's backbone under the applied reaction conditions, suggesting that solvent‐mediated degradation predominantly leads to physical fragmentation rather than chemical alteration. The isotactic structure of PP enhances its thermal stability and mechanical strength, making it highly resistant to chemical degradation under mild conditions. As for thermal stability, the TGA values were in close agreement with previous studies. For example, Mat‐Shayuti et al. [75], reported that PP degradation typically begins above 283°C, with complete decomposition occurring around 483°C. Azizi et al. [76] noted that major weight loss in PP occurs within the 340°C–450°C range. Subsequent to characterization, PP NPs were administered to the epithelial barrier‐on‐chip platform. Simulation results demonstrated that PP NP exposure resulted in gradual diffusion into the bottom compartment, while concentrations at the upper surface remained constant. This gradient difference is consistent with diffusion‐dependent transport under perfusion flow and supports the possibility of NP penetration across the epithelial barrier under continuous exposure. Similar behavior has been predicted and observed for different types of NPs in organ‐on‐a‐chip or Transwell systems [77]. In lung and intestinal tissues, where the epithelial barrier is most effective, applications of PS or PE plastics showed penetration over time with limited flux but measurable translocation. Our observation of a time‐dependent gradient, rather than instantaneous equilibrium, highlights the importance of modeling dynamics in NP exposure studies. Consistent with the model, spectrophotometric measurements of chip supernatants confirmed that ~80.04 ± 3.93 μg PP (38.11% of the administered dose) reached the bottom compartment over 72 h.

In the assessment of environmental exposome impacts on human health, in vitro models have become valuable as reliable and controlled systems. Environmental pollutants can disrupt the resilience and mechano‐biological processes of the human body barriers [78]. Such disruptions may ultimately contribute to the onset of toxicity‐associated pharmacokinetic disorders [10, 44, 46, 79].

Previous studies using polystyrene nanoplastics have demonstrated that NP exposure can modulate epithelial barrier integrity and inflammatory signaling in lung epithelial models, although the extent of cytotoxicity and barrier disruption varies depending on particle size, dose, and exposure conditions. These findings provide a contextual framework but cannot be directly extrapolated to PP NPs due to fundamental differences in polymer chemistry and surface properties [44, 80, 81]. In our study, following a 3‐day exposure to PP NPs, CALU‐3 epithelial cells exhibited a marked reduction in barrier integrity, with TEER values decreasing from 2605.81 ± 84.48 to 2199.54 ± 72.95 Ω·cm2, and confirmed disruption of tight junctions through decreased ZO‐1 expression. This was accompanied by a significant increase in LDH release (p < 0.001), indicating elevated cytotoxicity, and a rise in TNF‐α levels (PP: 58.89 ± 3.02 pg/mL; control: 36.56 ± 2.87 pg/mL, p < 0.01), and IL‐1β levels (PP: 615.58 ± 11.77 pg/mL; control: 426.34 ± 10.54 pg/mL, p < 0.001). TNF‐α plays a more significant role in the early response, while IL‐1β remains at lower levels due to the incomplete formation of the inflammasome complex, which is stimulated at later stages [82, 83]. Since epithelial barrier compromise may facilitate the systemic entry of PP NPs, investigating their interactions with circulating plasma proteins was a necessary next step to understand their broader biological fate. The next step was to understand the interaction of PP NPs with plasma proteins as specific protein‐NP interactions may induce conformational changes [84]. The hard protein corona is defined as a stable and tightly bound protein layer formed on the surface of NPs. In contrast to soft protein corona, which is transient, the hard protein corona persists throughout the biological pathway of NPs, directly affecting the fate of NPs by modulating cell interactions, and initiating tissue responses [85]. While certain proteins from the control model solution persisted after washing, the presence of PP NPs resulted in a statistically significant increase in the total protein content (p = 0.0087 for 0.25 mg/mL PP), attributable to the formation of a hard protein corona. Additionally, the relevance of the increase in protein amount caused by corona formation with PP concentration was evident in the 1 mg/mL PP group (p < 0.0001). To further contextualize this increase, the adsorbed protein amounts were normalized to the nanoparticle surface area. Using geometric surface area calculations based on particle size and polymer density, the higher protein load observed at increased PP NP concentrations was consistent with the proportional increase in accessible nanoparticle surface rather than reflecting a qualitative change in protein affinity. Such surface‐area driven scaling is consistent with established nano‐bio interaction principles and supports the interpretability of the applied PP NP concentrations in the context of protein corona formation [86]. The molecular weights of the protein bands were observed to be approximately 65 kDa, corresponding to the predominant presence of albumin [87]. The observed shift in the zeta potential of PP NPs, pre‐ and post‐exposure, suggests the binding of proteins to the NP surface, contributing to electrostatic stabilization [88]. Indeed, one major effect of protein adsorption is the change in physical and chemical features of NPs, particularly in terms of size and charge, which is supported by our data as well. These changes in surface charge of NPs cause different polarization stages of immune cells and dictate cytokine release mechanisms [89]. In order to deepen our understanding with respect to the interaction of PP NPs and human serum albumin on a molecular level, we performed a coarse‐grained molecular dynamics simulation. Based on a 3:1 mapping strategy, each monomer of the polymer has been mapped as one coarse‐grain bead, which preserves the structural features of the polymer while providing improvement in computational power compared to all‐atom simulations. This improvement enabled simulation of realistic sizes by using 48,000 coarse‐grained beads. The resulting distribution pattern confirmed protein corona formation on the NP surface, while the absence of significant density below 50 nm indicated limited penetration. Albumin adsorption onto the PP surface was quantified using a 0.5 nm cut‐off distance to evaluate the kinetics of corona formation. Due to contact analysis, the interaction kinematics can be interpreted as a biphasic behavior. The early stages of simulation showed rapid adsorption, followed by a more gradual stabilization of the rise of contacts (Figure S4A). This behavior suggests a two‐phase adsorption stage: an initial fast recognition of molecules followed by rearrangement of structures to optimize the interaction between protein and polymer structure, as further supported by RMSD analysis (Figure S4B). Given the hydrophobic nature of PP, the interaction pattern of amino acids (of albumin) and polymer has attracted considerable attention, particularly in elucidating the role of hydrophobic interactions [90]. In the residue‐specific contact analysis; approximate interaction numbers of grouped residues exhibited sequentially for hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues, comprising 121 and 66 amino acid beads, respectively. Among the hydrophobic group, phenylalanine (PHE) reached the highest number of contacts, followed by proline (Pro), leucine (LEU), and valine (VAL). Among hydrophilic residues, histidine (HIS), tyrosine (TYR) and lysine (LYS) showed the highest contact numbers. The presence of charged residues at the contact surface suggests the formation of electrostatic interactions as a second and minimal driving force for binding [91]. This interaction profile indicates that the interface between albumin and PP is mainly held together by hydrophobic interactions, while electrostatic effects from positively charged residues contribute to a lesser extent. Together, these results offer a molecular‐level understanding of how protein coronas form on PP NPs, which has significant implications for comprehending their biological identity and behavior in physiological settings.

For clinical translation, we further analyzed the interaction of PP NPs with blood plasma proteins from both male and female healthy individuals to understand gender‐related differences. As expected, an enhanced band intensity was observed at approximately 65 kDa in the PP‐treated samples, corresponding to albumin. Besides the abundant presence of albumin on PP NPs, intensities of specific protein bands at approximately 55 kDa, 75 kDa, 130 kDa, and 180 kDa indicate corona formation with a wide range of proteins. The intensities of all protein bands are higher in plasma from male donors, consistent with the intensity observed in the SDS‐PAGE gel lanes. PP NPs significantly adsorbed the proteins at approximately 55 kDa from the plasma samples obtained from 3 individuals, including 1 female and 2 males. Vitronectin (~54.3 kDa) is a strong candidate for this molecular weight range as an abundant plasma protein [92]. Vitronectin unfolds and forms multilayer stacks due to strong hydrophobic interactions when it is adsorbed on a hydrophobic surface, which may restrict cell adhesion [93]. Similarly, plasma proteins around 75 kDa, sourced from 3 donors, were significantly adsorbed on NPs. Transferrin is an iron‐binding protein together with fibrinogen (beta chain ~55 kDa), which is known to be adsorbed on polymeric NPs. The adsorption of iron‐binding proteins on the NP surface results in a local concentration increase, which may disturb the iron regulation mechanism [94]. Given the significant intensities of protein bands at approximately 55 kDa and 75 kDa, there is a strong likelihood of iron‐binding proteins being adsorbed onto PP NPs. Although heat shock protein (HSP) in the bloodstream is not a marker for asthma, studies have demonstrated a strong relationship between HSP and airway inflammation. The elevated levels of HSP70 had been detected in the serum and nasal fluids of asthma patients; however, molecular mechanisms remained unknown [95]. The conceivable adsorption of HSP70 12B (~75.6 kDa), a member of the HSP family, on PP NPs may lead to the local excess or depletion of concentration that may induce an instability in this mechanism. Upon inhalation, PP NPs interact with the airway epithelium, where they can disrupt epithelial barrier integrity and trigger the release of epithelium‐derived cytokine alarmins (e.g., IL‐33, TSLP, and IL‐25) [96]. These alarmins activate dendritic cells and group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), initiating a type‐2 inflammatory cascade characterized by increased Th2 cytokines (IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13) [97]. The recruitment of eosinophils and activation of Th2 lymphocytes amplify the inflammatory response, promoting airway remodeling and hyper responsiveness. The formation of a protein corona on PP NPs may further modify their biological identity and immune recognition, altering the balance between tolerogenic and pro‐inflammatory signaling at the epithelial interface [98]. Collectively, these findings suggest that NPs‐induced epithelial stress and protein corona formation could contribute to airway inflammation and asthma pathogenesis [99].

Protein bands from male donors at approximately 130 and 180 kDa exhibited significantly higher intensities, whereas bands obtained from female donors did not demonstrate any significance compared to the control. The underlying reason for this may be variations in plasma protein levels that are specific to gender. For instance, a clinical study found that healthy females exhibited significantly lower complement pathway activity and reduced levels of complement components, such as complement factor 3 and properdin, compared to males [100]. Complement component 3 (187.1 kDa) and complement factor H (~139.0 kDa) proteins have regulatory roles in the activation pathways of the complement system. The circulation of complement protein‐coated NPs in the bloodstream and their transfer to various organs may trigger complement system‐mediated diseases due to imbalanced levels. For instance, dysregulation in the secretion mechanisms of both is associated with kidney malfunction [101, 102]. Ceruloplasmin (122 kDa), which may be one of the adsorbed proteins on PP NPs and exhibited on the protein bands of male donors around 130 kDa, is responsible for the transport of copper in the circulation. Coating NPs with ceruloplasmin increased their affinity for cell surface receptors, resulting in enhanced cell attachment to the NPs [103]. Although SDS‐PAGE analysis helps to determine molecular weight ranges of adsorbed proteins, the presence of hundreds of proteins with similar molecular weights makes molecular weight an insufficient indicator, rendering it unfeasible to determine the exact identity of proteins based solely on this measurement. Therefore, a more detailed proteomics analysis is necessary to identify the specific proteins adsorbed on PP NPs to understand the potential impacts of the bio‐nano interphase. Although protein corona formation is highly critical for human health, the majority of existing studies focus on generic plastic particles such as polystyrene, whereas no studies have addressed PP‐specific protein corona formation and its biological consequences so far. Therefore, our results provide a basis for future studies.

In conclusion, PP NPs were produced by a solvent‐mediated chemical degradation process. An additional post‐synthesis homogenization step involving ultra‐turrax treatment in acetone was performed to obtain a uniform particle distribution and enhance the dispersion of hydrophobic NPs in aqueous systems. This approach resulted in particles with an average particle size of 305 nm and a zeta potential value of −30.8 mV. A coarse‐grained molecular dynamics simulation demonstrated a biphasic profile. SDS‐PAGE analysis confirmed the formation of protein corona, leading to an increased hydrodynamic diameter of 364.1 nm and a zeta potential shift to −14.5 mV. Surface‐area normalization was applied only to the concentration‐dependent corona experiments (0.25 vs. 1 mg/mL), not to plasma corona profiling as the latter was performed at a fixed nanoparticle concentration to capture inter‐individual variability. Profiling of plasma samples from healthy male and female individuals provided insights into gender‐related differences. Taken together, these findings provide valuable insights into the possible biological implications of PP NPs.

A major limitation of this study is the absence of direct microscopic visualization confirming PP‐NPs contact with epithelial cells in the microfluidic platform. Although fluorescent labeling was attempted using FITC‐based approaches, stable labeling could not be achieved due to the intrinsic physicochemical properties of PP, and further modification would have altered particle characteristics and biological relevance. Thus, future studies will focus on developing robust new‐generation labeling strategies to enable direct imaging‐based validation of PP NPs localization within organ‐axis microphysiological models. The other limitation of the present study is the absence of direct endotoxin quantification using established assays such as the Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) test or functional endotoxin neutralization controls, polymyxin‐B. Although multiple indirect strategies were employed to minimize and functionally assess potential endotoxin contamination, including solvent‐based processing, comprehensive sterilization, and immune cell‐response screening, these approaches cannot fully exclude the presence of low‐level endotoxin contamination. Direct endotoxin testing therefore remains essential, particularly for studies aiming at regulatory translation or conducted under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions [104]. Future studies will need to incorporate validated endotoxin‐specific assays optimized for nanomaterials, ideally within standardized manufacturing and quality‐control pipelines, to ensure full compliance with translational and regulatory requirements.
