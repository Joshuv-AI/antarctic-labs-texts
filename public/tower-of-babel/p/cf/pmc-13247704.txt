One size does not fit all: Understanding child safety through context, power, and co-creation
Authors: Lisette Farias, Mai-Lis Hellenius, Gisela Nyberg, Susanne Andermo
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

This letter responds to the comment “Rethinking Child Safety in Community-Based Health Programmes: A Critical Response to Parents' Perceptions of Health Coordinators”, based on our published article “If children don't feel safe, they won't come back”: A qualitative exploration of parents' perceptions of health coordinators in a family-based programme in socially disadvantaged communities [1]. As the authors of the correspondence note, our findings highlight children's sense of safety as a prerequisite for participation, particularly in socially disadvantaged neighbourhoods that are often portrayed by authorities as ’problematic’ and segregated from mainstream society. In such areas, family-based programmes play a crucial role in reaching children who have limited access to organised leisure and after-school activities due to factors beyond their families' control. These include structural barriers such as a lack of recreational or sports facilities, economic and transportation costs, and social barriers related to stigma and marginalisation.

We agree that families encounter complex barriers, making safety a multifaceted issue that extends beyond individual psychological or relational aspects. Consequently, understanding how families engage in health-promoting activities requires close attention to the social environments in which these activities take place [2]. As described in our article, families struggle to participate in outdoor activities due to safety concerns, which interact with economic, religious, and cultural barriers to physical activity already documented in previous research. In this sense, safety was positioned as one layer among several overlapping constraints, creating distinct forms of disadvantage that risk being overlooked when programmes are homogenised and generalised as ’one size fits all.’ For this reason, we diverge from the correspondence in one important aspect: we argue that being context-specific is not a limitation but a prerequisite for meaningful adaptability. The urgency of adapting family-based programmes lies precisely in understanding the complexities of the realities each community faces. Such adaptability must extend beyond cultural awareness. It should support cross-sectoral work with relevant stakeholders to address the structural barriers that limit families’ access to safe spaces, rather than focusing mainly on strengthening resilience to these conditions [3].

Another important aspect discussed in our article concerns the risk of failing to train health coordinators to recognise the challenges communities face. We emphasised the importance of promoting safety through enforcement of rules, respect, and fairness. While these elements may appear to focus on interpersonal relations, their application requires a deep understanding of how safety concerns have evolved within a specific community, and which behavioural patterns need to be role-modelled. This is particularly important in responding to families' internalised stigma and distrust. Such work is foundational for understanding how broader forces, such as public policy and media representations, shape children's sense of safety. In this regard, we agree with the correspondence that context-sensitive strategies are essential for long-term sustainability and should be embedded within the training of health coordinators.

Building on the steps proposed by the authors of the correspondence, we offer three additional recommendations informed by an extended analysis of our results and recent health-promoting projects. First, we suggest explicitly incorporating an intersectional approach [4] to better understand how overlapping social positions, such as class, ethnicity, and gender, produce differentiated forms of disadvantage within communities. An intersectional analysis can enhance awareness of the diverse needs health coordinators must address and clarify when and why multilevel stakeholders should be involved. Second, we recommend recruiting health coordinators from the targeted communities to build on local knowledge and lived experience, while also promoting alternative role models that contrast with those offered by gangs or other informal power structures. Third, we propose adopting co-creation approaches rather than limiting programmes to collaboration or participation. Co-creation directly addresses power imbalances by positioning families, children, and community actors as equal partners in programme design and implementation, thereby fostering communication and trust [5]. Co-creation can respond to the correspondence's call for cross-sectoral intervention by coordinating action with community-based organisations, as in our study, primary healthcare and schools, which have shown to provide an important platform for reaching across socio-economic groups [6].

This response underscores that safety is one of several interconnected layers that restrict families' access to physical activity. We emphasise that context-specific training and awareness form the foundation for meaningful safety promotion and for addressing power imbalances. Intersectionality and co-creation offer critical tools for understanding families’ complex challenges and supporting their involvement in decision-making about programme content and activities. Together, these approaches position safety not as a discrete outcome but as a dynamic process co-produced through contextually adapted and sustained community engagement, ultimately supporting long-term sustainability.
