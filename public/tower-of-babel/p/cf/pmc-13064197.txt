Post-progression treatment patterns in systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease-associated interstitial lung disease: insights from a US retrospective, observational study
Authors: Joseph Yang, Katy Sadowski, Akshay Kharat, Ann Chauffe, Tejaswini Kulkarni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePatients with systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease (SARD) are at high risk of developing interstitial lung disease (ILD). We sought to gain insight into the pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments being used by patients with SARD-associated ILD (SARD-ILD) following ILD progression.DesignThis was a retrospective, observational cohort study.SettingOptum Clinformatics Data Mart administrative medical and pharmacy claims database in the USA.ParticipantsPatients with SARD-ILD who had an incident ILD diagnosis and progression between January 2018 and March 2023.Primary and secondary outcome measuresTreatment patterns and healthcare services utiliation were descriptively summarised for baseline and follow-up periods.ResultsWe identified 6431 patients with SARD-ILD and evidence of ILD progression (mean age, 71.2 years; 75.3% female; 68.9% white). The mean (SD) time between the initial SARD-ILD diagnosis and the onset of ILD progression (index date) was 104 (201) days. On average, patients were followed for 936 (467) days.Oral corticosteroids were the most common treatment post-progression (69.5%), followed by non-biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (non-biologic DMARDs) (41.6%), biologic DMARDs (15.5%) and immunomodulators (15.4%). Antifibrotics were received by 3.5% of patients. Supplemental oxygen was the most frequent non-pharmacological treatment (48.9%). For the baseline period, 53.0% and 42.1% of patients used inpatient and emergency department services, respectively. During the follow-up, 69.7% and 62.8%, respectively, used these services.ConclusionThe high use of corticosteroids and limited use of DMARDs and antifibrotics post-progression in this descriptive study implies a significant gap between current practice and optimal management of patients with SARD-ILD.

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a broad term that encompasses over 200 lung disorders characterised by chronic inflammation and fibrosis of the interstitial lung tissue surrounding the alveoli.1 Patients with systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases (SARDs), including systemic sclerosis (SSc), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD), myositis or Sjögren’s disease, often develop ILD.2 These SARD-associated disorders are referred to as SARD-ILD. The prevalence rates for the development of ILD among patients with SARD and SSc can extend above 80%, whereas comparative rates for patients with RA range from 3% to 58%, depending on the definition used and whether clinically significant lung disease is evident.3 Reported prevalence rates for the development of ILD among patients with MCTD, myositis and Sjögren’s are 35–67%, 20–70% and 10–15%, respectively.2

The development of SARD-ILD poses a significant clinical challenge due to the condition’s progressive nature and associated rates of morbidity and mortality.4 In patients with SSc, ILD is the leading cause of death, with pulmonary fibrosis accounting for 35% of SSc-attributable fatalities.5 The 10-year mortality rate for SSc-associated ILD is reported to be as high as 40%.6 Pulmonary complications are reported to be directly responsible for 10–20% of mortality in patients with RA, with 5-year mortality rates among persons diagnosed with RA-associated ILD ranging from 36% to 39%.7 In patients with RA, ILD is the second-leading cause of death, following cardiovascular disease.8

The 2023 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and American College of Chest Physicians (CHEST) guidelines4 for managing SARD-ILD conditionally recommend short-term glucocorticoids, except in SSc, where they strongly advise against their use due to the risk of SSc renal crisis. The antifibrotic therapy, nintedanib, is conditionally recommended as an additional first-line agent for SSc. For patients with SARD-ILD experiencing progression on first-line therapy, the decision to implement therapy with nintedanib or an immunosuppressive agent should depend on the pace of progression, the extent of fibrotic disease and the presence of a usual interstitial pneumonia pattern on computed tomography (CT) scan. For people with SARD-ILD progression despite first ILD treatment, the 2023 guidelines conditionally recommend mycophenolate, rituximab, cyclophosphamide or nintedanib as treatment options. A clinician’s decision to implement antifibrotic therapy should consider a patient’s disease severity and the extent of fibrotic disease or the presence of a usual interstitial pneumonia pattern on high-resolution CT (HRCT) scan.4

Published evidence on the real-world management of progressive SARD-ILD is lacking. Published data are generally limited to the efficacy of individual therapies.913 To address this gap, we investigated treatments prescribed preceding and following SARD-ILD progression, along with patterns of health services utiliszation, providing important insights into both therapeutic decision-making and the broader care needs of affected patients.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective, observational cohort study using Optum’s Integrated Clinformatics Data Mart, a large deidentified administrative medical and pharmacy claims database.14 This nationwide database includes medical and pharmacy claims data on over 84 million members enrolled in commercial or Medicare Advantage health plans. The database encompasses all inpatient, outpatient, pharmacy, emergency department, office visit and other ancillary service utilisation during a member’s enrolment in the insurance plan. The Optum Clinformatics Data Mart database was converted into V.5.4 of the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model15 and V.5.0 30-AUG-24 of the OMOP Standardised Vocabularies16 to facilitate analysis. For this investigation, we evaluated claims from January 2017 to March 2024.

Study population

Our study cohort comprised adult patients with newly diagnosed SARD-ILD demonstrating evidence of ILD progression. Eligibility criteria for the study cohort were applied sequentially as described. First, patients were required to have at least two International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10-CM) codes for the same SARD condition recorded on different dates within a 12-month period. The SARDs of interest were SSc, RA, dermatomyositis or polymyositis, Sjögren’s syndrome, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-associated vasculitis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), or MCTD. Patients were then required to be newly diagnosed with ILD, defined as having at least two ICD-10 codes for fibrosing ILD on different dates within a 12-month period, subsequent to the first recorded SARD diagnosis date. The date of the first fibrosing ILD code was designated as the SARD-ILD diagnosis date, which was required to occur between 1 January 2018 and 31 March 2023, and be the first such diagnosis in a patient’s history. Lastly, patients had to have evidence of ILD progression following their SARD-ILD diagnosis date. The index date was defined as the earliest date of ILD progression following SARD-ILD diagnosis and was required to be on or before 31 March 2023. Each patient contributed only one index date to the analysis and multiple entries per patient were not permitted. All patients were required to be 18 years or older on the index date, have a known gender and have uninterrupted health plan enrolment beginning 12 months prior to the SARD-ILD diagnosis date through to at least 12 months following the index date (i.e., first 12 months of the follow-up period) (figure 1).

To identify patients with evidence of ILD progression after the earliest date of SARD-ILD diagnosis, we applied the previously published claims-based algorithm for fibrotic ILD progression,17 using the ICD-10-CM code for ILD with progressive fibrotic phenotype (J84.170), plus additional codes for pulmonary function tests, chest imaging, lung transplant, oxygen therapy, palliative care and respiratory hospitalisation. Our applied algorithm included a menu of eight potential criteria (online supplemental table 1). A patient needed to satisfy at least one of the criteria to meet our definition for SARD-ILD progression. Some criteria required completing a series of individual tests within a designated time window, such as two or more oxygen titration tests on separate dates within ≤90 calendar days. In these situations, the date of the first observed data element within the allowed time window was assigned as the index date.

The analysis was conducted using R statistical software (V.4.2.3) and open-source tools developed by Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics.18 19

Outcome

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients receiving SARD-ILD related pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments during the baseline and follow-up periods. Pharmacological treatments were assessed at both the drug class and individual drug levels for the full sample and for each respective SARD-ILD aetiology. Drug classes of interest included oral corticosteroids (glucocorticoids), immunomodulators, biologic disease modifying antirheumatic drugs (biologic DMARDs), non-biologic DMARDs, antifibrotics and other ILD-related treatments. A comprehensive list of therapies assessed within each drug class is presented in table 1. Additional measurements included the proportion of patients receiving any pharmacological treatment, inclusive and exclusive of corticosteroids. Non-pharmacological endpoints examined included the use of oxygen supplementation, pulmonary rehabilitation and receipt of lung transplantation. Treatment utilisation was analysed at baseline, for the first 12 months of follow-up and across the entire follow-up period.

Secondary outcomes included the presence of ILD-related clinical symptoms (dyspnoea, cough, fatigue), as identified by ICD-10 diagnosis codes from claims and all-cause healthcare resource utilisation (HCRU) in different healthcare settings (inpatient, outpatient and emergency department visits; prescriptions; and specialist visits) during baseline and follow-up periods. Also of interest was a description of patient demographics (age, sex, race and ethnicity, geographical region, payer type, year of the index date); clinical characteristics, including Quan-Charlson comorbidity index score,20 respiratory-related and other associated comorbidities, and underlying SARD aetiology; and smoking status as measured on the index date. All relevant codes used in this study are listed in online supplemental table 2.

Pharmacological, non-pharmacological and HCRU outcomes were derived from administrative billing records at the point of service. Given clinical symptoms (dyspnoea, cough, fatigue) were identified via ICD10-CM diagnosis codes, these may be subject to under-capture due to variable clinician documentation.

Analysis

The number and percentage of patients prescribed SARD-ILD-related pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments of interest during the baseline and follow-up periods were analysed. The proportion of patients prescribed each medication class of interest during the baseline period was calculated as the total number of patients with at least one medication claim within that drug class at any time during the baseline period divided by the total number of patients in each cohort. Across the follow-up period, the number and percentage of patients with at least one claim for each medication class during the entire observation period and within the first 12 months following the index date was calculated. Similar logic was applied to ascertain the number and percentage of patients receiving ILD-related non-pharmacological treatments during the respective observation periods. For pharmacological agents, the number and percentage of patients prescribed each individual drug within a category was also assessed. The same approach was applied to assess the proportion of patients with one or more visits with each provider specialty and the proportion of patients with coded clinical symptoms. In addition, the frequency of provider specialty visits was calculated on a per patient per month (PPPM) basis across both baseline and follow-up periods.

All-cause HCRU was summarised across baseline and follow-up periods and reported as proportions of patients receiving each type of service and as mean and median PPPM counts.

Patient and public involvement

This was a retrospective, claims-based study using de-identified patient claims data from the Optum Clinformatics Data Mart, a product of Optum Life Sciences (Eden Prairie, Minnesota, USA). Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, analysis or reporting of this study. No individual-level patient contact was made, and therefore no dissemination of results directly to study participants is planned.

Results

Sample composition

A total of 6431 patients with SARD-ILD and evidence of ILD progression were identified (online supplemental figure 1). The mean (SD) time between the initial SARD-ILD diagnosis and the index date was 104 (201) days. On average, patients were followed for 936 (467) days following the index date (table 2). The mean (SD) age of the cohort was 71.2 years (11.1). The study cohort was predominantly female (75.3%) and white (68.9%).

Patients presented with substantial comorbidity burden with a mean (SD) Quan-Charlson Comorbidity Index20 of 4.5 (2.5). Common respiratory comorbidities during the baseline period included chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (44.6%), upper and lower tract respiratory infection (40.8%), pneumonia (36.5%), obstructive sleep apnoea (23.7%) and asthma (23.0%). Non-respiratory comorbidities observed in at least one third of our sample include hypertension (79.1%), gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (47.9%), cardiovascular disease (46.0%), atherosclerosis (45.6%) and type 2 diabetes (33.8%). In addition, 2762 patients (43.0%) presented evidence of either current or prior exposure to nicotine (table 2). A further itemisation of baseline demographics and clinical characteristics by underlying SARD aetiology is found in online supplemental table 3.

The most identified SARD aetiology in this cohort was RA, and it was the sole underlying SARD in 52.6% of the study cohort. It was concomitant with one or more other SARD aetiology in an additional ∼20% of the sample, indicating that nearly three-quarters of patients with progressive ILD had an underlying RA diagnosis. The most frequently observed diagnosis combinations with RA were Sjögren’s syndrome (n=328, 5.1% of the total study cohort), SLE (n=291, 4.5%), MCTD (n=161, 2.5%) and SLE/Sjögren’s (n=102, 1.6%) (online supplemental table 4).

ILD-related respiratory symptoms

Respiratory symptoms associated with ILD were commonly observed. Dyspnoea was reported in 69.7% of patients during the 12-month baseline period and 73.3% of patients during the first 12 months of the follow-up period. Similarly, cough was observed in 45.1% and 42.0%, and fatigue in 18.2% and 22.7%, across the baseline and follow-up periods, respectively. (Figure 2).

Pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatment

During the follow-up period, 83.0% of patients were prescribed any SARD-ILD–related pharmacological treatments of interest. When oral corticosteroids were excluded, that proportion declined to 71.2%. Focusing solely on disease-modifying pharmacological treatments (ie, excluding both oral corticosteroids and other miscellaneous drugs), the proportion further decreased to 55.3%.

Oral corticosteroids were the most frequently dispensed pharmacological treatment throughout the follow-up period, with 57.3% of patients receiving one or more prescriptions within the first 12 months following the index date and 69.5% during the entire follow-up. Non-biologic DMARDs were the second most prevalent class of medications, with 37.5% and 41.6% of patients receiving one or more prescriptions, respectively. For non-biologic DMARDs, the most frequently prescribed agents were hydroxychloroquine (21.9% and 25.2%, respectively) and methotrexate (14.3% and 16.3%, respectively). Immunomodulators were dispensed to 12.9% and 15.4%, respectively, and biologic DMARDs were received by 12.3% and 15.5%, respectively. Antifibrotics were used sparingly, with 2.1% of patients identified as having received one or more prescriptions in the first 12 months of follow-up and 3.5% across the entire follow-up period. Azithromycin was the most prescribed drug among other ILD-related treatments (22.4% and 38.3%, respectively). (table 1, figure 3).

Oxygen supplementation was the most frequently used non-pharmacological treatment, observed in 40.3% of patients during the first 12 months of follow-up and 48.9% of patients across the entire follow-up period. Pulmonary rehabilitation was received by 3.2% and 4.8%, respectively, and fewer than 1% of patients underwent lung transplant at any point during the study period. (Table 2, figure 3).

Healthcare resource utilisation

Over 99% of patients incurred one or more outpatient visits during each observation period, with a mean (SD) of 3.06 (2.34) and 3.18 (2.46) visits PPPM during baseline and full follow-up periods, respectively. A total of 62.8% of patients visited the emergency room across the entire follow-up period, while 42.1% had at least one emergency room claim during the baseline period. The mean (SD) number of emergency room visits PPPM was 0.07 (0.13) during the baseline period and 0.07 (0.12) throughout the follow-up. Inpatient visit frequency averaged 0.12 (0.26) visits PPPM during baseline and 0.16 (0.39) during follow-up. Mean (SD) monthly prescription fills PPPM were 3.89 (3.3) across the baseline period and 4.24 (3.45) across the follow-up period. Across specialties, the frequency of a pulmonologist visit claim was 0.12 (0.30) PPPM at baseline and 0.18 (0.36) PPPM throughout the follow-up (table 3).

Discussion

Over the past decade, definitions of ILD progression in patients with SARD-ILD and their management have evolved. Major definitions, guidelines and clinical trials have contributed to this evolution, with the American Thoracic Society emphasising that progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF) found with ILD is not a diagnosis and the definition of PPF is agnostic to the underlying disease aetiology.21 Real-world data on treatment patterns for progressive SARD-ILD remains limited. This study addresses this gap by providing comprehensive insights into pharmacological and non-pharmacological management and HCRU in this population, informing future clinical practice and research directions. Given the descriptive nature of this analysis, findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, providing a foundation for future research aimed at optimising management strategies in this population.

Nearly 70% of the patients in this study received oral corticosteroids in the post-progression (entire follow-up) period, and an additional 42% received non-biologic DMARDs. Biologic DMARDs and immunomodulators were administered to 15% each, with antifibrotics received by only 3% of patients. Supplemental oxygen was the most frequent non-pharmacological treatment (49%). Inpatient and emergency department services, respectively, were used by approximately two-thirds of patients within this same period of follow-up. These findings underscore the substantial comorbidity and symptom burden in progressive SARD-ILD, particularly dyspnoea and fatigue. Despite the identified symptom burden, a pattern of limited escalation to disease-modifying therapies was observed following progression. However, the underlying reasons for this cannot be determined from claims data and warrant further investigation.

Our results demonstrate a high reliance on oral corticosteroids and lower rates of DMARD and antifibrotic use, emphasising substantial unmet need in the treatment of these patients. For instance, for patients with SSc and ILD, the latest ACR guidelines strongly caution against glucocorticoids as a first-line ILD treatment, yet 33% of the patients with SSc-ILD in this study (which was before the guideline was released) had one or more claims for a corticosteroid during the first 12 months after the index date. Adverse effects of glucocorticoids are well established, including increased risk for infection, weight gain, increased bone turnover/bone density loss, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, skin thinning and cataract formation.22

While non-biologic and biologic DMARDs were used, overall use of disease-modifying therapies remained limited, observed in just over half of the cohort. Potential contributors to these patterns include clinician familiarity with corticosteroids, their rapid and broad immunosuppressive effects, and the perception of immediate benefit in severe cases. These findings may also reflect clinical uncertainty regarding optimal sequencing or combination strategies for progressive SARD-ILD, particularly due to the paucity of randomised control trial data across the diverse SARD subtypes. The respective nuances among individual SARD aetiologies further complicate the picture for a practising clinician. We do not believe the low utilisation rates are due to missing data; our data repository consists of a closed-claim source, representing all claims processed by a patient’s insurer, regardless of practice setting.

Our literature search revealed no other comprehensive study on treatment patterns for patients with progressive SARD-ILD, with most of the published literature being limited in scope. Identified studies focused on the effectiveness of individual agents and collectively demonstrated the diversity of pharmacological approaches. For example, Moreno-Torres et al demonstrated improved outcomes with cyclophosphamide in inflammatory myopathy-associated ILD.9 Similarly, Yomono and Kuwana reported better outcomes with early intervention using such DMARDs as cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, methotrexate or tocilizumab in SSc-ILD.10 Mena-Vázquez et al found that lung function remained stable in patients with RA-ILD treated with DMARDs.13 Additionally, Yusof et al observed disease stability or improvement in patients with RA-ILD treated with rituximab.12 These investigations, however, do not capture the broader, real-world patterns of medication utilisation or the complexities of clinical decision-making in progressive SARD-ILD. Our study addresses this critical gap by providing a comprehensive assessment of treatment patterns and HCRU across a large, contemporary cohort, offering new insights into how these patients are managed in everyday clinical practice.

The therapeutic landscape continues to evolve. The use of cyclophosphamide and mycophenolate mofetil in patients with SSc-ILD is supported by Scleroderma Lung Study I and II trials in 2006 and 2016, respectively,23 24 though these are not Food and Drug Administration (FDA) indications. Further, FDA approvals of nintedanib for SSc-ILD in 2019 and for progressive fibrosing ILDs in 2020,25 along with tocilizumab for SSc-ILD in 2021,26 have expanded the clinician’s arsenal of potential treatments, yet their uptake remains modest. Collectively, these trends highlight the dynamic and complex nature of SARD-ILD management and underscore the urgent need for more effective and innovative therapeutic options to address persistent unmet clinical needs.

Only 2.1% of the present cohort had one or more prescription claims for an antifibrotic medication during the first 12 months post-index, and only 3.5% presented with an antifibrotic claim throughout the follow-up. The low rate of antifibrotic utilisation may be multifactorial, related to diagnostic uncertainty or perceived lack of benefit in comparison to concerns for adverse events, especially in patients with inflammatory patterns, and prohibitive costs. These potential barriers warrant targeted investigation, as early introduction of antifibrotics alongside immunosuppressive therapy may offer synergistic benefit in select patients. This apparent gap between these real-world patterns and evolving evidence-based recommendations implies the need for strategies to optimise disease management in this population.

Oxygen supplementation was the most common non-pharmacological intervention. Likely, related to this finding, was the frequency of dyspnoea symptoms, which were recorded for 69.7% of patients during the baseline period and 73.3% of patients during the 12-month follow-up (figure 2). Oxygen supplementation is a proxy for decreased quality of life in patients with ILD, based on both the restrictions on ambulation and the patient’s functional respiratory capacity.27 28 These findings reflect the substantial functional burden of disease progression in this population. Furthermore, this study cohort had considerable HCRU both during baseline and throughout the follow-up period, with frequent outpatient and inpatient visits. Focusing efforts on provider education and optimised disease-modifying therapeutic strategies may mitigate this significant healthcare resource use.

To better align real-world care patterns with emerging evidence and recommended clinical guidelines, ongoing clinician education is essential, particularly regarding early diagnosis and prompt initiation of appropriate therapy. Qualitative and quantitative research into barriers to the initiation of DMARDs and other disease-modifying therapies could elucidate challenges to access and inform tailored interventions.

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, as with all administrative claims studies, unmeasured confounding is an inherent limitation, as key variables influencing treatment selection are unavailable in claims. However, this study was designed as a descriptive analysis of real-world treatment patterns rather than a causal inference study. As such, unmeasured confounding does not affect the validity of the primary descriptive findings. The large, nationally representative sample further strengthens the generalisability of the observed patterns to routine clinical practice.

The patient identification process involved identifying patients with newly diagnosed fibrosing ILD after their SARD diagnosis, with subsequent evidence of progression, using an algorithm reliant on diagnosis and procedure-based codes. The presence of a diagnosis code does not necessarily indicate the presence of disease; however, to limit ILD misclassification or misidentification, a confirmatory diagnosis was required. Some treatments contained within our progression algorithm may not be specific to lung disease; hence, a change in treatment attributable to ILD progression could potentially be for management of the SARD itself, as opposed to lung progression, resulting in potential misclassification.

Claims data do not expressly capture disease severity, reasons for specific treatment choices or key clinical variables relevant to ILD management, including pulmonary function test results and HRCT patterns; and there is potential for incomplete data or coding errors. As such, confounding by indication, contraindication and disease severity cannot be excluded as alternative explanations for the observed treatment patterns and should be considered when interpreting these findings.

In addition, our study was limited to patients diagnosed with fibrosing ILD following SARD diagnosis, not those who develop ILD before the SARD onset, so our findings may not be generalised to all individuals with SARD-ILD. Our observed patient counts with RA are proportionally on the high side; this may be due to the challenge of distinguishing between SARD subtypes based solely on administrative data, as overlapping clinical features can influence diagnostic coding patterns.

The study results could be subject to survival bias, as patients must continuously enrol in their health plan starting 12 months before first identified SARD-ILD diagnosis through at least 12 months after the index date to be included in the study (and thus, survived during this period). However, previous studies have estimated that the 5-year survival rates for patients with SARD and ILD range from 73.3% to 84.7%.29 Thus, it is anticipated that there is limited survival bias following the index date.

Lastly, data collection for the present investigation predated the latest ACR guideline publication.4 The observed gap between the observed real-world practice pattern and evidence-based recommendations may, in part, reflect the timing of guideline publication relative to patient progression in our cohort, as many patients experienced progression prior to the release of the 2023 ACR guidelines. Future study may reveal utilisation changes that more closely reflect the present guideline recommendations.

Conclusions

This study provides real-world insights into the treatment patterns of patients with SARD-ILD before and after their ILD progression. The high utilisation of corticosteroids and limited use of disease-modifying pharmacotherapies post-progression in this study underscore the unmet needs of these patients. The publication of the 2023 guidelines may narrow this gap and hopefully improve patient care for those with SARD-ILD. The real-world evidence presented in this study implies opportunities for care improvement, including enhanced clinician education; system-level interventions; and research on early, guideline-based management to improve patient and population health outcomes, and greater utilisation of innovative therapies.
