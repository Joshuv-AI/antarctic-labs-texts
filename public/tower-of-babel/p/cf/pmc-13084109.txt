Immunotherapy challenges surgical dogma in placental site trophoblastic tumor
Authors: J Michael Straughn, Teresa KL Boitano
Published: 2026

Placental site trophoblastic tumor (PSTT) occupies a difficult space within gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD). PSTT is rare, biologically distinct, and often chemoresistant. As a result, it has not benefited from the same treatment advances that have made other gestational trophoblastic neoplasms highly curable. Consequently, hysterectomy remains the standard of care for stage I disease even in young patients for whom fertility loss carries significant consequences.

The recent report by Bolze and colleagues challenges that paradigm. A 26-year-old patient with stage I PSTT seeking fertility preservation achieved rapid biochemical remission and radiologic complete response after a single 200 mg dose of the immunotherapy, pembrolizumab. Despite early treatment discontinuation due to immune-related toxicity, remission has been sustained for three years (Bolze et al., 2026). For a disease long managed surgically, this outcome is clinically remarkable.

The biological rationale for immunotherapy is compelling. Trophoblastic tumors arise from placental tissue, an immune-privileged environment shaped by maternal–fetal tolerance (Menzies, 2025). Overexpression of PD-L1 is common in GTD and particularly prominent in PSTT; this facilitates immune escape through checkpoint signaling. PD-1 inhibition may restore cytotoxic immune activity, offering a mechanism distinct from and potentially superior to cytotoxic chemotherapy in select cases (Zong et al., 2019).

This case represents only the second report of fertility-sparing pembrolizumab monotherapy in PSTT. In the first case, immunotherapy achieved complete remission followed by a successful term pregnancy (Polnaszek et al., 2021). In both cases, responses were rapid, durable, and achieved with limited treatment exposure. This observation raises important questions about treatment intensity and duration. These reports challenge long-held assumptions that PSTT is inherently resistant to systemic therapy and that definitive surgery is unavoidable.

PSTT primarily affects women of reproductive age. When cure requires hysterectomy, oncologic success may come at significant personal cost. Immunotherapy introduces the possibility of preserving both survival and fertility, reframing treatment goals and expanding shared decision-making. Checkpoint inhibition may also avoid some of the significant toxicity that can be associated with chemotherapy. Unlike multi-agent chemotherapy, immunotherapy avoids cumulative bone marrow suppression, neuropathy, and alopecia. Immune-related adverse events can be significant, but the toxicity profile is fundamentally different and often can be more acceptable to the patient.

Several critical questions remain unanswered at this time. Optimal patient selection, predictive biomarkers, treatment duration, and the need for consolidation therapy are undefined. Long-term oncologic safety and reproductive outcomes also require further study. Progress in rare cancers is often slow and limited by small numbers and established practice patterns. Immunotherapy disrupts this dogma by targeting biology rather than precedent. PSTT, once viewed as a surgical disease by necessity, may now represent an area for fertility-preserving precision oncology.

Bolze et al. present more than an exceptional case. If confirmed prospectively, immune checkpoint inhibition may evolve from anecdote to standard of care, reshaping management of one of gynecologic oncology’s rarest tumors. A single infusion leading to durable remission is not merely a clinical milestone but a signal that the treatment paradigm for PSTT may be ready to change.
