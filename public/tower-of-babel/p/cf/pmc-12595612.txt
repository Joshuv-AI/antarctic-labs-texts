Food safety practices among mothers with infants aged 6–12 months in Sekyere South municipal, Ghana
Authors: Richard Mensah, Yvonne Agyeman-Duah, Obed Sarpong Adusei, Richard Nketia, Vivian Darkoah Domfeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnsuring safe food practices among mothers is essential to protect the health of infants and young children, who are particularly vulnerable to foodborne illnesses. In low- and middle-income countries, unsafe household food handling, poor storage, and limited caregiver knowledge significantly contribute to diarrhoea diseases, malnutrition, and preventable childhood deaths. This study assessed food preparation, handling, and storage practices among mothers and examined the influence of various sociodemographic factors on these practices.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 571 mothers in the Sekyere South Municipal via a structured questionnaire. Mothers were recruited through a convenience sampling technique. Data were coded and entered into Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21.0 for analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarize the data. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using chi-square tests. Variables that showed statistical significance at p < 0.05 in bivariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of appropriate food handling practices. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsMost respondents practiced safe food preparation methods (91.1%), 43.1% demonstrated good food handling practices, and 56.6% practiced safe food storage. Significant sociodemographic predictors of food safety practices were age, marital status, occupation, monthly income, and educational level. Higher education consistently emerged as the strongest predictor across all three domains. Mothers with tertiary education had significantly greater odds of good practices in food preparation (AOR = 15.739, 95% CI: 10.86–15.39), handling (AOR = 15.390, 95% CI: 10.81–15.59), and storage (AOR = 15.779, 95% CI: 2.21–15.77). Occupation and income also showed significant associations, with self-employed and higher-income mothers being more likely to exhibit safe behaviors (AOR = 4.921, 95% CI: 2.04–11.88).ConclusionThis study revealed that while food preparation practices are generally good among mothers, food handling and storage practices require improvement. Key sociodemographic factors such as education, income, and occupation significantly influence food safety behavior. Improving mothers’ food handling and storage practices is essential to reducing the burden of foodborne illnesses among children, thereby contributing to better child survival and overall community health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25138-1.

Introduction

Food safety has emerged as a significant international public health concern because of its direct link to morbidity, mortality, and economic productivity [1]. Globally, unsafe food is a major source of foodborne illnesses, which contribute to a considerable number of deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [2, 3]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), 31 foodborne hazards resulted in approximately 600 million cases of illness and 420,000 deaths, including 125,000 deaths among children under five years of age. These illnesses have also led to the loss of more than 33 million healthy life years [4]. Recent epidemiological studies suggest that food may play a more significant role than water in the transmission of diarrhea-related diseases [5]. Children, particularly those under five years, bear a disproportionate burden of foodborne illness in low- and middle-income countries, accounting for approximately 40% of cases in Africa [5]. Infants and young children are particularly vulnerable to the effects of foodborne pathogens because of their immature immune systems and developing gastrointestinal tracts. Inappropriate food handling, storage, and preparation practices during the weaning period can lead to diarrhea, malnutrition, and even death [6]. A study in Ethiopia revealed that 27% of children aged 6–12 months experienced diarrhea within two weeks of initiating complementary feeding [7]. These health outcomes not only affect immediate child survival but also contribute to long-term cognitive and physical development issues [8].

Mothers and caregivers are often the primary food handlers in households and therefore serve as a line of defense against food contamination. Research shows that 10–20% of foodborne illnesses are directly linked to unsafe practices by food handlers [9, 10]. Home environments account for more than half of all foodborne illnesses, primarily due to limited food safety knowledge and poor hygiene during preparation and storage [11, 12]. The WHO defines a food handler as any individual who directly handles food or utensils that come into contact with food [13]. In domestic settings, mothers, by preparing meals and feeding their children, assume a critical role in ensuring food safety. As such, their practices, knowledge, and awareness are essential in preventing infections in infants and young children [14].

In Ghana, foodborne illnesses remain a significant public health issue. According to the Food and Drugs Authority (FDA), staphylococcal food poisoning causes an estimated 185,000 cases of foodborne illness annually, whereas diarrhea and dehydration account for approximately 84,000 deaths each year, including approximately 25% of deaths among children under five [15]. Despite increasing urbanization and education, unsafe food practices persist, particularly in rural and peri-urban communities. While studies have examined food safety among street vendors, institutional food handlers, and food services in urban Ghana [13, 16–18], limited attention has been given to domestic food practices [19], especially among mothers during the complementary feeding period. Moreover, there is a noticeable research gap in the understanding of household-level food safety practices in regions such as the Sekyere South municipality in the Ashanti Region.

Although various studies have explored foodborne diseases and food safety practices in Ghana, no specific study has focused on mothers with infants aged 6–12 months in Sekyere South. This is of great concern, given the critical developmental window and the potential for lifelong consequences of early foodborne infections. This study, therefore, sought to address this knowledge gap by assessing food preparation, handling, and storage practices among mothers in Sekyere South municipality. It also aimed to identify the sociodemographic factors that influence these practices.

Methods

Study area

The Sekyere South municipality (previously Afigya Sekyere District) is one of the 43 metropolitan, municipal, and district assemblies (MMDAs) in the Ashanti Region, and the capital city is Agona. It is one of Ghana’s 261 MMDAs. Along the Kumasi‒Mampong trunk route, Sekyere South municipality is 37 km from Kumasi in the northern part of the Ashanti Region. A total of 416.8 square kilometers, or approximately 1.7% of the region’s total area of 24,389 square kilometers, constitutes the municipality’s entire land area. The area is bound by longitudes 10 40’W and 10 25’W and latitudes 60 50’N and 70 10’N as shown in Fig. 1. A facility-based study was performed, which included SDA Hospital Asamang, Agona Government Hospital, SDA Hospital Wiamoase, Jamasi Health Centre, Kona Health Centre, and Boanim Health Centre.

Study design

The type of design for the study was a descriptive quantitative survey.

Study population

The population of this study consisted of all mothers with infants aged 6–12 months who were receiving postnatal care at child welfare clinic sessions at all the selected health facilities in Sekyere South municipal.

Sampling technique

The purposive sampling technique was used to select three health centers and three hospitals from a total of five hospitals in the municipality using a simple random sampling technique. A convenience sampling technique was used to select the study respondents. Health centers were purposively selected due to high attendance of mothers with infants, while hospitals were randomly chosen from a sampling frame to ensure equal selection chances and reduce bias. Both hospitals and health centers were included to capture a broader range of maternal food safety practices across primary and referral levels of healthcare.

Sampling procedure

Kish’s (1965) approach for known study populations was used to choose an appropriate sample size that adequately reflects the total research population [21].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included mothers or caregivers who had a child within the age range of 6–12 months, had lived in the district for at least six months prior to the study, and were directly responsible for purchasing foodstuffs and preparing food for their babies. Eligible participants were also required to have been feeding their child for at least six months from birth. Mothers or caregivers who declined to provide informed consent or those whose children were admitted to the hospital due to illness at the time of the study were excluded.

Tool for data collection

A structured questionnaire served as the primary tool for gathering information. The questionnaire development was guided by validated questionnaires used in similar studies [14, 19]. Appropriate modifications were made to the questionnaire to fit the study respondents. The questionnaire included a total of 23 items grouped into four key categories: respondents’ demographic information, food handling habits of mothers, methods of food storage, and food preparation behaviors among mothers. The tool was created and administered in English, and each respondent typically took an average of 25 min to complete it. To ensure content clarity and relevance, nutrition experts reviewed the instrument for face validity. The content validity of the instruments was ensured by giving them to supervisors for review to ensure that the items truly measured what they were intended to measure. The internal consistency and reliability of the pretest data were calculated and reported via the Kuder‒Richardson 20 Test (KR-20). The study revealed overall internal consistency reliability, with a Kuder‒Richardson-20 test value of 0.85. This statistical method evaluates whether all the items on a scale are aligned and effectively measures the same underlying concept. The WHO Five Keys to Safer Foods tool (WHO, 2006) was adapted to measure the food safety practices of the respondents. On the WHO’s Five Keys to Safer Food Awareness Test, cumulative scores falling below 70% of the allowed answers were deemed “poor food safety practices,” and cumulative scores above 70% were deemed “good safety practices” [24]. Good food handling was defined as mothers’ adherence to safe food handling behaviors such as checking the expiry date of canned food before purchasing, reading the nutritional facts on packaged foods, washing hands before handling food, checking if a canned food is dented, among others. Appropriate food storage was defined as keeping food in safe conditions to prevent contamination and spoilage. This included using clean covered containers, refrigerating perishable foods where possible, reheating leftover foods thoroughly, and avoiding prolonged storage of cooked food at room temperature. Appropriate food preparation was defined as preparing food in a manner that ensures safety and nutritional adequacy for the child. This included washing hands before preparing food, washing hands before feeding the child, ensuring utensils are clean, covering hair when preparing food, among others.

Data processing and analysis

Data entry was performed using SPSS software version 21.0, where the data were cleaned and analyzed. Descriptive statistics were computed, and the results for each variable were presented as frequencies and percentages. To analyze the factors associated with food safety practices among mothers, a logistic regression analysis was performed. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with 95% confidence intervals reported for all estimates.

Ethical statement

This study received approval from the University of Cape Coast Institutional Review Board (IRB) under approval number (UCCIRB/CHAS/2022/126) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Sekyere South Health Directorate granted permission to carry out the study in the district. Informed consent was sought from the study respondents to make free decisions about their involvement.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the respondents

The mean age of the respondents was 25 ± 4 years. Additionally, 219 (38.4%) of the respondents were aged 24–29 years. More than half of the respondents (321; 56.2%) were married, and 315 (55.2%) were self-employed. In addition, 244 (42.7%) of the respondents earned less than GH₵ 500.00 as their monthly income, with 216 (37.8%) having SHS/vocational income as their highest level of education. Almost half, 269 (47.1%) lived in a house containing approximately 4–6 people (Table 1).

Food preparation, handling, and storage practices among respondents

The majority of the respondents (n = 520, 91.1%) demonstrated good food preparation practices, with only 51 exhibiting poor practices. Sociodemographic correlates of food storage practices among postnatal mothers

Moreover, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the impact of various sociodemographic factors on the likelihood of practicing good food storage among mothers. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs), confidence intervals (CIs), and significance levels (p values) are reported for each predictor. Age was not a statistically significant predictor of food storage practices (p = 0.291). Although mothers aged 30–34 years had the highest adjusted odds (AOR = 2.922) of practising good food storage compared with the reference group (18–23), the wide confidence intervals and nonsignificant p values indicate weak predictive power. Marital status also had no significant effect (p = 0.423). However, divorced/separated mothers had slightly greater odds (AOR = 1.233) of good food storage behavior than single mothers did (reference group). Occupation was a strong and significant predictor of food storage practices (p < 0.001), with self-employed mothers being 4.9 times more likely to adopt safe storage methods than their unemployed peers were (AOR = 4.921, 95% CI: 0.345–0.742). Civil servants also had elevated odds (AOR = 1.737), whereas privately employed mothers had moderately higher odds (AOR = 1.083). Monthly income had a statistically significant effect (p = 0.023). Participants earning more than GH₵ 1,000 were more likely to practice proper food storage (AOR = 1.874) than those earning less than GH₵ 500. Educational level was a strong and significant predictor (p = 0.002). Compared with those with no formal education, those with tertiary education were more likely (AOR = 15.779) to adopt good food storage practices. All other education levels (JHS, SHS/vocational, primary) also presented elevated odds. Household size showed no significant relationship with storage practices (p = 0.780), indicating that the number of people living in the household did not significantly affect behavior. (Table 3).

Sociodemographic correlates of food handling practices among mothers

Table 4 presents the associations between various sociodemographic variables and food handling practices among mothers. Among the total respondents, 43.1% (n = 246) reported good food handling practices, whereas 56.9% (n = 325) reported poor practices. Age was significantly associated with food handling behavior (χ² = 64.129, p < 0.001, Cramer’s V = 0.187). Younger mothers (18–29 years) were more likely to have poor practices than older mothers were. Marital status showed a strong and significant relationship with food-handling practices (p < 0.001). Occupation had a statistically significant association with food handling (χ² = 66.939, p = 0.008, Cramér’s V = 0.215). Sociodemographic correlates of food Preparation practices among mothers

The analysis of food preparation practices among mothers revealed that several sociodemographic variables were significantly associated with the quality of practices. Among the total respondents, 91.1% reported good food preparation practices, whereas 8.9% reported poor practices. Age was significantly associated with food preparation behavior (χ² = 43.879, p < 0.001), with a moderate effect size (Cramér’s V = 0.277), indicating that mothers aged 24–34 years were more likely to have good practices than were those in other age groups. Marital status also showed a statistically significant association (χ² = 8.396, p = 0.015, Cramér’s V = 0.121), with married women being more likely to engage in good practices than single or divorced/separated women. Occupation was another significant factor (χ² = 37.479, p < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.256), with self-employed and civil servant mothers being more likely to prepare food safely than unemployed mothers were. In addition, monthly income was significantly related to food preparation practices (χ² = 9.998, p = 0.007), where mothers earning higher incomes demonstrated better practices. Educational level was also a key determinant (χ² = 151.862, p = 0.021, Cramér’s V = 0.165); mothers with secondary or tertiary education were more likely to practice safe food preparation than were those with no formal education. Sociodemographic correlates of food preparation practices among mothers

A multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the sociodemographic factors that significantly predict food preparation practices among the respondents. The results revealed several significant predictors after adjusting for confounding variables. Age was a significant predictor (p < 0.001). Compared with the reference group (18–23 years), participants aged 30–34 years (AOR = 1.671) and above 34 years (AOR = 2.364) were more likely to engage in good food preparation practices, indicating that older mothers tend to follow better food preparation behaviors. Marital status also showed a statistically significant association (p = 0.001). Compared with single respondents, those who were married (AOR = 1.274) or divorced/separated (AOR = 1.750) had higher odds of good food preparation practices. Occupation emerged as a strong predictor (p = 0.008). Compared with unemployed respondents, those who were self-employed (AOR = 7.807) or civil servants (AOR = 3.320) had significantly greater odds of engaging in proper food preparation practices. Monthly income was significantly associated with food preparation practices (p = 0.013). Those earning GH₵ 500–GH₵ 1000 and > GH₵ 1,000 had higher odds (AOR = 1.419 and AOR = 2.614, respectively) of good practices than those earning less than GH₵ 500, highlighting the role of financial capacity in enabling access to better food hygiene and resources. Educational level was the strongest predictor in the model (p < 0.001). Compared with participants with no formal education, those with primary, JHS, SHS/vocational, and tertiary education had dramatically greater odds of good food preparation practices (AORs ranging from 10.861 to 15.739). In contrast, household size (p = 0.781) was not significantly associated with food preparation practices (see Table 7).

Discussions

Food preparation, handling, and storage practices among respondents

The findings of this study provide valuable insights into the food safety practices of the respondents, specifically in the areas of food preparation, handling, and storage. The majority of the respondents (91.1%) adhered to recommended food preparation practices. This suggests that educational efforts or cultural norms related to food preparation may already be well established and effective within this population. This result is consistent with findings in Jordan [25] where the majority of respondents adhered to proper food preparation protocols, such as handwashing and the use of clean utensils. Similarly, a study in Malaysia [26] reported that food preparation was the most common component of food safety, particularly among mothers and caregivers, likely because of the cultural emphasis on cleanliness during meal preparation. However, food handling practices presented a concerning trend, with more than half of the participants (56.9%), which aligns with studies in Myanmar and Nigeria [27, 28], which reported similar gaps in handling practices, particularly involving raw and cooked food separation, personal hygiene, and improper use of gloves. This indicates a significant gap in knowledge or application of safe food handling techniques, which could contribute to foodborne illnesses. The lower adherence in this area may be due to inadequate awareness of contamination risks, lack of proper training, or misconceptions regarding hygiene procedures during food handling. Food storage practices showed a relatively more balanced distribution, with 56.6% of participants reporting good practices. While this reflects a modest level of adherence, the 43.4% who reported poor storage practices still represent a considerable proportion of the population at risk. Improper storage, particularly of perishable foods, can lead to microbial growth and spoilage, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions to improve refrigeration habits and food labeling practices. This outcome is comparable to findings in a study [29], that reported moderate food storage behaviors, such as covering food and maintaining refrigeration. However, the results contrast with those in [30], where significantly lower adherence to safe storage practices, particularly in low-resource settings, was observed. The differences may be attributed to contextual factors, such as electricity availability, the affordability of refrigerators, and variations in public health education across study sites.

Sociodemographic correlates of food storage practices among mothers

The bivariate multinomial logistic analysis identified factors associated with respondents’ food handling, preparation, and storage practices. Education level emerged as one of the strongest predictors of good food storage practices in both bivariate and multivariate analyses. Mothers with higher education, particularly at the SHS/vocational and tertiary levels, were significantly more likely to adopt proper storage techniques (p = 0.002; AOR for tertiary education = 15.779). These findings align with the results in India and Ghana [29, 31]. Educated individuals are more likely to be exposed to food safety information and have better access to resources that facilitate safe practices. Lower levels of safe storage practices among populations with limited education in low-resource settings [32] suggest that disparities in educational attainment contribute to differences in food storage behavior. Occupation was also significantly associated with food storage practices. Compared with unemployed mothers, self-employed and civil servant mothers demonstrated significantly better practices (p < 0.001; AOR = 4.921 for self-employed mothers). This finding supports findings that income-generating activities empower individuals to adopt safer food handling and storage behaviors, likely due to increased access to refrigeration and storage supplies [30, 33]. Conversely, some researchers reported no significant association between employment status and food safety practices [34], possibly because of the use of a more homogenous low-income sample, which could mask occupational differences. Monthly income also showed a statistically significant relationship with food storage practices (p = 0.023). Respondents earning above GH ₵ 1,000 were more likely to engage in safe storage practices (AOR = 1.874), likely because higher income enables better access to storage equipment such as refrigerators, food containers, and better-quality food. This result is consistent with findings that economic stability enhances food safety behavior [14]. However, other studies reported weak or no associations between income and food storage, possibly due to contextual differences in food access, storage technology, or urban‒rural disparities [35]. Interestingly, age, marital status, and household size did not show statistically significant associations with food storage practices in this study, which supported that these variables had limited predictive value once education and income were controlled [14]. However, this contrasts with findings in another study that reported that older age and marital status were positively associated with food safety compliance, possibly due to more cooking experience and greater household responsibilities [36]. Differences in study location, cultural expectations, and sample composition may explain these contrasting results.

Sociodemographic correlates of food handling practices among mothers

Age was significantly associated with food handling practices, both in bivariate and multivariate analyses. Mothers in the older age groups (24–29 and above 34 years) were more likely to exhibit good food handling behaviors than their younger counterparts (18–23 years). This finding aligns with work conducted in South East Asia [37] and Bangladesh [38], which reported that older individuals tend to have better food handling practices due to increased life experience and caregiving responsibility. Conversely, some studies did not find a significant relationship between age and food handling, possibly because of different cultural norms or homogeneity in the sample age distribution [39]. Marital status also showed a strong and significant association with food handling behaviors. Compared with single mothers, married and divorced/separated mothers had better food handling practices. This result is consistent with the findings from a study that reported that married women often take on greater responsibility for household food preparation, leading to better awareness and hygiene practices [37]. However, another study reported no significant effect of marital status, potentially because of the influence of shared domestic roles in certain communities, which may dilute the impact of marital status on food handling behavior [40]. Occupation was another important factor, with unemployed and self-employed mothers showing poorer practices than those with more formal employment, such as civil servants. These findings are consistent with a study in Saudi Arabia, which highlighted that employment in the formal sector is associated with exposure to food safety training and structured routines that support hygienic behavior [14]. In contrast, self-employed or unemployed individuals may face time constraints, informal working conditions, or limited access to food safety information. Monthly income showed a statistically significant relationship with food handling practices, as mothers with higher income (above GH₵ 1000) were more likely to demonstrate good practices. These findings support findings from a study in India, which noted that higher income levels increase access to food storage facilities, cleaning materials, and safer food options [41]. However, in settings with subsidized or communal food programs, income may have less of an impact, which could explain why a weaker association was found in a different socioeconomic context [42]. Educational level emerged as the most influential factor in predicting good food handling practices. Household size did not have a statistically significant relationship with food handling practices. This is consistent with findings from a study conducted among mothers in Saudi Arabia [14], which indicated that family size does not necessarily predict food safety behavior, as mothers often adapt their practices regardless of the number of dependents.

Sociodemographic correlates of food Preparation practices among mothers

Several sociodemographic factors, including age, marital status, occupation, income, and education level, were significantly associated with food preparation practices in both the bivariate and multivariate analyses, whereas household size and number of young children were not significantly associated. The finding that older mothers (particularly those aged 30–34 years and above 34 years) were more likely to practice safe food preparation aligns with the findings of previous studies [43, 44], which reported that increased age often correlates with better food safety behaviors due to accumulated experience and responsibility. Younger mothers may lack practical knowledge or may prioritize convenience over safety, particularly when juggling multiple responsibilities. However, no significant relationship was found between age and food safety behavior in a similar Nigerian context, possibly because of a narrower age range in the sampled population or different educational backgrounds across age groups [14]. Marital status was also a significant predictor, with married and divorced/separated mothers showing higher odds of good food preparation behavior than single mothers. Similar findings were reported by [43], who reported that marital status often reflects stability in household structure, which may positively influence meal planning, food hygiene, and caregiving responsibilities. Significant link between marital status and food hygiene behaviors was not found in socioeconomically disadvantaged groups in the UK and Victoria, Australia [45], possibly because of cultural differences in family roles or shared responsibilities in food preparation, regardless of marital status. The study also confirmed that occupation significantly influenced food preparation practices. Mothers who were self-employed or civil servants had greater odds of engaging in safe food preparation than unemployed mothers did. This result aligns with research by [46, 47], which suggested that occupational engagement, particularly in structured or skilled sectors, often results in greater access to health information and food safety awareness. Differences in occupational roles, however, may lead to different outcomes; for instance, some self-employed mothers may work under time constraints or informal environments that limit safe food practices. Similarly, monthly income was found to be a significant predictor of food preparation behavior. Mothers earning higher incomes (especially those above the GH₵ 1000) were significantly more likely to practice safe food preparation. This is consistent with the findings that financial capability improves access to clean cooking environments, food-grade utensils, and proper storage facilities [46]. Conversely, another study [14] reported no significant association between income and food hygiene behavior, suggesting that in some contexts, noneconomic factors such as cultural beliefs or food safety education may play a greater role. Among all the variables assessed, education level was the strongest and most consistent predictor of good food preparation practices. Mothers with secondary and tertiary education had dramatically higher odds (AORs as high as 15.739) of safe practices than did those with no formal education. This is strongly supported by a broad body of literature which consistently emphasizes that education improves health literacy, critical thinking, and the ability to interpret food safety instructions [44, 46, 47]. Differences between studies that report weaker effects of education may be due to low overall education levels or poor integration of food safety into educational curricula. On the other hand, household size was not significantly associated with food preparation practices. This finding mirrors results that household size alone does not predict food safety unless it is accompanied by other influencing factors, such as caregiver workload or available support systems [47].

Conclusion

This study assessed food preparation, handling, and storage practices among mothers and identified key sociodemographic factors influencing these behaviors. While the majority of respondents demonstrated good food preparation practices, significant gaps were observed in food handling and storage behaviors. The multivariate analysis revealed that educational level, monthly income, and occupation were the most consistent and significant predictors across all three food safety domains. In particular, mothers with higher education and income levels, as well as those in formal employment, were more likely to practice safe food handling and storage. Conversely, age and marital status showed moderate associations, and variables such as household size were generally not significant predictors, except in specific cases related to storage. These findings emphasize the importance of socioeconomic and educational empowerment in promoting safe food practices among mothers caring for young children.

Limitations of the study

The use of a cross-sectional design limits causal inference between sociodemographic factors and food safety practices. The study focused only on mothers with children aged 6–12 months in Sekyere South District, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations or regions in Ghana. Additionally, the study did not include direct observational assessments of food preparation, handling, or storage practices, which could have validated self-reported responses.
