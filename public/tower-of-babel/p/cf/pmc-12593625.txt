Dental Pulp Stem Cell–Derived Multicellular Microtissue Spheres: A Synergistic Strategy for Dental Pulp Regeneration
Authors: Wei Jian, Fang Ma, Ying Xian, Xingyan Shi, Hairong Ma, Shenglan Ma, Ling Qin, Rui Zhang, Hualin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveRestoring pulp tissue to its natural state is the ultimate goal of pulp regeneration, during which vascularization and neuralization of regenerated pulp are key factors. Hence, this study aimed to construct multicellular microtissue spheres derived from dental pulp stem cells to promote the vascularization and neuralization of regenerated dental pulp tissue.MethodsFirst, we induced dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) to differentiate into endothelial-like cells and neural-like cells. Then, 7 types of multicellular microtissue spheres were constructed from DPSCs, endothelial-like cells and neural-like cells, and their gross morphology, tissue morphology, and endothelial cell marker and neuronal marker expression were evaluated. Additionally, the microtissue spheres were placed in rabbit molars, which were implanted subcutaneously into mice to assess dental pulp tissue regeneration.ResultsInternally dense microtissue spheres with a stable morphology were successfully constructed via the low adhesion method. The spheres containing endothelial-like cells displayed enhanced CD31-positive expression, and those with neural-like cells showed elevated expression of the neuronal marker Nestin. Cell activity was maintained in all 7 spheres, and compared with those derived from DPSCs only, the cell survival rates in the spheres constructed from endothelial-like and neural-like cells together or separately combined with DPSCs were greater. Neovascularized pulp-like tissue with neuronal marker-positive cells appeared in the root canals of each group, indicating that these spheres promoted pulp regeneration.ConclusionsThe results of this study indicate that DPSC-derived multicellular microtissue spheres facilitated neovascularization and expressed neuronal markers in newly formed pulp-like tissue, providing an experimental and theoretical basis for their application in pulp regeneration.

Introduction

The incidence of endodontic diseases is very high in oral health clinics, and restoring the normal structure and function of endodontic tissue is difficult. To date, the main clinical treatment method for endodontic diseases is root canal therapy. However, after root canal treatment, the pulp and its physiological defense reflex and nerve conduction functions are permanently lost, increasing the risk of fracturing the remaining tooth tissue.1 However, the emergence and development of tissue engineering technology has made dental pulp regeneration possible.

Regenerated pulp tissue should ideally have the following characteristics: (1) revascularization; (2) similarity to the natural pulp tissue structure, which contains a certain density of cells and extracellular matrix (ECM); (3) attachment of odontoblasts to the surface of existing dentin and the formation of new dentin; and (4) nerve growth.2 Among them, the revascularization of and nerve growth in regenerated pulp are important directions for pulp tissue engineering research but are also the main factors hindering the large-scale clinical application of this technology. However, there are few reports that have studied both of these factors.

Vascularization and neurogenesis are both very important for pulp regeneration and the maintenance of pulp homeostasis.2 The vasculature in pulp tissue supplies nutrients and oxygen, transports metabolic waste products, and regulates inflammation.3,4 The timely and rapid reconstruction of dental pulp blood vessels is critical for dental pulp regeneration, and the existence of many blood vessel networks in tissues is necessary for cell metabolism and survival. Nerve fibers sense external stimuli, assist in angiogenesis, regulate inflammation, and maintain pulp homeostasis and pulp defense mechanisms.4,5 Therefore, it is necessary to construct a suitable microenvironment that promotes angiogenesis and neurogenesis during dental pulp regeneration.

Studies have confirmed that the direct implantation of DPSCs into the dental pulp cavity decreases the activity of the implant due to a lack of blood and nutrients because of slow angiogenesis.3 At present, there are 2 main strategies for dental pulp vascularization: graft prevascularization and in situ angiogenesis.3 Although research on the prevascularization of grafts has made some progress,6 regenerated pulp vascularization remains difficult because of certain obstacles, such as the difficulty of scaffold implantation, fragility of the implant and the immune response.7, 8, 9

Therefore, scaffold-free technologies have been developed. Scaffold-free technology is based on the tendency of cells to form aggregates or 3-dimensional (3D) microspheres.10 Scaffold-free technology can not only successfully overcome the defects associated with scaffolds, such as failure to simulate the natural ECM and lack of cell‒cell interactions, but also provide a better microenvironment for stem cells in vivo, that is, increasing the stemness of stem cells, the survival rate of transplanted cells and the promotion of angiogenesis.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Concerns such as immune rejection and the implantation difficulty of exogenous scaffolds are also avoided.10,16

The simplest and fastest method of vascular regeneration involves the use of endothelial cells.17 However, endothelial cells are difficult to obtain, primarily because the main sources of endothelial cells are the umbilical vein and adipose tissue; in addition, and the inability to carry out long-term expansion and passage of endothelial cells in vitro is another major factor hindering their large-scale application. Neuronal cells lack the ability to regenerate, so self-recovery after nerve injury is difficult; thus, neurogenesis in regenerated dental pulp is another issue that hinders dental pulp regeneration.18 DPSCs have multidirectional differentiation potential and can be induced to form endothelial cells and neurons or neural-like cells19,20; moreover, DPSCs also have the potential for long-term preservation and expansion.21, 22, 23 Therefore, obtaining endothelial-like cells and neural-like cells through the induction and differentiation of DPSCs is a better strategy.

Therefore, with a focus on the revascularization and neurogenic differentiation of regenerative dental pulp, in this study, the ideas and methods of constructing 3D cellular microtissue spheres for dental pulp regeneration were preliminarily discussed. To this aim, 3D microtissue spheres were constructed from DPSCs and endothelial-like cells and neural-like cells resulting from DPSC differentiation to form exogenous dental pulp grafts. To date, there are few reports on the use of DPSCs to obtain endothelial-like cells and neural-like cells and to construct cellular microtissue spheres with these 3 types of cells.

In this study, DPSCs were first isolated and cultured and then induced to differentiate into endothelial-like and neural-like cells. Then, the DPSCs and endothelial-like and neural-like cells were used to construct spherical 3D microtissues (i.e., cell microtissue spheres) via low adhesion culture. The biological properties of the microtissue spheres and their effects on ectopic pulp regeneration were evaluated to provide an experimental and theoretical basis for pulp regeneration therapy involving microtissue spheres.

Materials and methods

Isolation and culture of rabbit primary DPSCs

The rabbits were anesthetized by 2% lidocaine hydrochloride through the ear side vein. After the maxillofacial region was disinfected with iodophor, the rabbit molars were extracted, rinsed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS, Gibco) containing 2% double antibiotic solution (Solarbio), and placed in precooled Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco) containing double antibiotic solution for 20 min. Then, the pulp tissues were extracted from the molars with a barbed broach and immersed in a 6-well plate filled with DMEM containing 3% double antibiotic solution. The pulp tissues were minced, treated with 1 mL of 3 g/L type I collagenase (Solarbio), mixed well and digested in a 37°C water bath. Digestion was terminated after 1 h, the mixture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. Then, 2–3 mL of DMEM supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS, Front) was added to resuspend the cells, the mixture was transferred into a 25 mL culture bottle, and the cells were cultured in an incubator (Thermo, USA). The culture medium was changed every 3 days, and the cells were observed and photographed under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Endothelial differentiation

Cell induction

Third generation DPSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5×105 per well. When the cells reached 90% confluence, the original medium was aspirated, and endothelial cell induction medium (DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% double antibiotic solution + 10 μg/L basic fibroblast growth factor (Peprotech) + 0.1 mg/L dexamethasone + 50 μg/L vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF, PrimeGene) + 2 g/L bovine serum albumin) was added. The medium was changed every 3 days, and after 14 days, the morphology of the induced cells was observed and photographed under an inverted microscope.

Matrigel tube formation

The Matrigel matrix glue, pipette, pipette tips, and 96-well plate were precooled at 4°C. After placing the 96-well plate on ice, 50 μL of Matrigel was slowly added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37°C for 30 min. After the plate was removed from the incubator, DPSCs or endothelial-like cells were added to the plates at a density of 4×104 cells/well and cultured in the incubator. After 4 h, tube formation was observed and photographed under an inverted microscope. AngioTool software was used to calculate the percent vascular area in the 2 groups.

Then, 0.5 mL of lysis buffer was added to the collected cells from each group for total ribonucleic acid (RNA) extraction using an RNA extraction kit (Novizan). After determining the purity and concentration of the RNA samples, they were stored at -80°C. The RNA was reverse transcribed to obtain complementary deoxyribonucleic acid (cDNA), which was stored at -20°C. Neural differentiation

Cell induction

Third generation DPSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at 5×105 cells per well. When the cells reached 90% confluence, the original medium was aspirated, and nerve cell induction medium (Neurobasal®-A medium (Gibco) + 10% FBS + 1% double antibiotic solution + 40 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor + 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor + 1× B27 (Gibco)) was added. The medium was changed every 3 days, and after 14 days, the morphology of the induced cells was observed and photographed under an inverted microscope.

Giemsa staining

The differentiation of DPSCs into neural cells was detected by Giemsa staining after 14 days of induction. The cells were washed with PBS 3 times, and then Giemsa dye (Solarbio) was added to the 6-well plate. After 30 min, the dye was removed, and the samples were rinsed slowly and gently with running water. After excess dye was no longer observed under the microscope and the cells had been dyed blue–purple, the cells were observed under an inverted microscope, and photographs were taken.

Histological analysis

The cell microtissue spheres obtained from the above 7 groups of cells (n=6) were cultured for 3 days, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sliced, and subjected to histological staining.

Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome staining

Briefly, samples from each group were embedded in paraffin. Then, serial 5-μm-thick sections were generated, 4 of which were subjected to staining with HE, and another 4 were subjected to Masson’s staining to observe the formation of the collagen fibers.

Immunofluorescence staining for platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (CD31)

The tissue sections were dewaxed, placed in citric acid antigen repair solution, boiled under high pressure, and heated in a 100°C oven for 45 min. After the samples were allowed to cool naturally, they were washed with PBS 3 times, blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, washed with PBS 3 more times, and incubated with the primary antibody and then the secondary antibody. Finally, the samples were treated with an anti-fluorescence quencher and observed and photographed under a microscope. The average fluorescence intensity of CD31 was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemical staining for Nestin (ABC kit method)

Dewaxed tissue sections were subjected to antigen repair for 20 min and then washed with PBS twice for 3 min each. After the addition of 3% hydrogen peroxide, the samples were incubated for 20 min at 37°C before being washed twice with PBS. Next, the samples were incubated with goat serum for 30 min, the serum was discarded, and the primary anti-Nestin antibody was added without rinsing the samples. The samples were incubated at 4°C overnight with the primary antibody, washed twice with PBS, and incubated with the secondary antibody for 30 min. After incubation with the secondary antibody, the samples were washed twice with PBS, and incubated with a prepared mixture (composed of liquids A and B (10 µL each) and PBS (1 mL)) for 30 min, washed twice with PBS, and incubated with color developing solution (liquid A:liquid B at a ratio of 1:50). The color developing solution was discarded when the samples appeared brown, after which the samples were rinsed with running water 3 times and stained with hematoxylin for 40 s. Then, the samples were dried, fixed, sealed and observed under a microscope. ImageJ software was used to analyze the percentage of Nestin-positive areas.

Live‒dead cell staining

After 3 days of culture, the cell microtissue spheres were collected, washed with PBS twice and incubated with propidium iodide solution at room temperature for 10 min. Then, the microtissue spheres were gently washed with PBS and incubated with calcein solution at room temperature for 45 min. After the calcein solution was removed, the cell microtissue spheres were washed gently with PBS and observed via laser confocal microscopy (Zeiss, Germany). The number of dead cells (red fluorescence) was quantified using ImageJ software.

Subcutaneous implantation of the microtissue spheres into nude mice

New Zealand rabbits (aged 4–6 weeks, weighing 0.8–1.0 kg) and BALB/c nude mice (aged 8 weeks) were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Ningxia Medical University. All animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines and were performed in accordance with the UK Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act, 1986, and the associated guidelines. The experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Ningxia Medical University (approval number: 2022-027).

After removing the molars of New Zealand rabbits, molars with similarly sized pulp cavities were selected and finely trimmed using a high-speed dental handpiece. The pulp was removed from the open apical hole with a barbed broach, after which the molars were washed twice with PBS containing double antibiotic solution, soaked in 70% alcohol for 20 min, washed twice with PBS again, and set aside. The 7 types of cell microtissue spheres were cultured at 37°C for 3 days, and then the microtissue spheres and medium were slowly removed from the 96-well plates with a pipette gun and placed on a sterile weighing spoon. Microtissue spheres from each group were implanted into the open apical holes of the empty rabbit molar pulp cavities. In the control group, calcium hydroxide paste was injected into the open apical holes fill the pulp cavities.

Nude mice were anesthetized via inhalation of 1% isoflurane and sterilized, longitudinal incisions were made on 1 side of the midline of the back of each mouse, and a subcutaneous pouch formed after blunt separation. The molars filled with cell microtissue spheres were implanted into the subcutaneous pouches (1 sample per nude mouse, n=6), and the wounds were drawn and sutured. The nude mice were euthanized via inhalation of an overdose of isoflurane 8 weeks after the operation. The raised portion of the implant site on the back of each mouse was observed, and blunt separation was performed after a longitudinal incision was made to remove the tooth samples.

Postoperative analysis

General observations

The relationships among the subcutaneous tissue, tooth root and root conditions were noted.

HE and Masson’s trichrome staining

The root samples from each group were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified with ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA), and then stained with HE and Masson’s trichrome.

Microvascular count

Three HE-stained sections were selected from each group for the following analysis. First, the vascular dense area was selected under a microscope at 100× magnification, and then, 3 fields were selected in each section at 200× magnification to count the number of microvessels excluding the bleeding and fibrotic areas. Thus, 9 values were obtained from each group from which the mean value was calculated.

Immunohistochemical staining

Samples from each group were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified with EDTA, and subjected to immunohistochemical staining. The primary antibodies used were anti-CD31, anti-Nestin and anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). Immunohistochemical staining was performed according to the method described above.

Statistical analysis

Statistical Package for Social Science 26.0 software (SPSS 26.0, USA) was used for statistical analyses of the data, and all the data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). The data were tested for homogeneity of variance and a normal distribution. If the data satisfied the requirements of a normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the groups. If the requirements were not met, K independent sample nonparametric tests and 2 independent sample nonparametric tests were used. P < .05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Endothelial differentiation

Cell morphology

Figure 1C shows a photomicrograph of endothelial-like cells after 14 days of differentiation from DPSCs. These endothelial-like cells were spindle shaped or polygonal, more closely connected and arranged like paving stones.

Matrigel tube formation

Fig. 1, Fig. 1 show microscopy images of DPSC and endothelial-like cell tubule formation in Matrigel. Both the DPSCs and endothelial-like cells formed interconnected vascular-like structures, and the endothelial-like cells formed significantly more vascular-like tubules than the DPSCs did. Figure 1F shows a histogram of the percentages of vessel area among the DPSCs and endothelial-like cells, which were 18.97 ± 2.88% and 77.1 ± 1.30%, respectively, indicating that endothelial-like cells exhibited strong angiogenic capacity after 14 days of differentiation.

qRT‒PCR

Figure 1G shows a histogram of the relative mRNA expression levels of VEGF, an angiogenic marker gene, in DPSCs and endothelial-like cells. The relative expression of VEGF in endothelial-like cells was significantly greater than that in DPSCs, indicating that DPSCs were induced to differentiate into endothelial-like cells and these endothelial-like cells have strong angiogenic ability.

Neural differentiation

Giemsa staining

As shown in Figure 1H, the induced neural cells had a typical neuron-like morphology with obvious elongated neurites, indicating that DPSCs could be successfully induced to differentiate into neuron-like cells.

qRT‒PCR

Figure 1I shows the relative mRNA expression levels of NCAM in DPSCs and neuron-like cells. After 14 days of differentiation, the relative expression of NCAM in the neuron-like cells was significantly greater than that in DPSCs, indicating that the neuron-like cells had a strong neurogenic capacity.

Discussion

The 3D cell microtissue spheres formed due to the aggregation of stem cells and other types of cells that led to the construction of spherical cell aggregates via 3D culture methods without the use of exogenous scaffolds, providing a microenvironment similar to the ECM to simulate tissue formation and regeneration.10 Some researchers have explored the mechanism by which cell spheres form via this method, and it is believed to be affected by the adhesion and differentiation of various cells; moreover, cadherin and integrin are closely related to the mechanism of microtissue sphere formation.24 The formation of cell microtissue spheres can be divided into the following steps. First, single cells in the suspension slowly aggregate. Subsequently, cellular aggregation and adhesion are promoted through the mutual combination of cadherins in the peripheral cells. In addition, actin and β-catenin complexes can increase cell stemness and cohesiveness by strengthening the contact between adjacent cells, eventually yielding cell microtissue spheres with strong adhesion.24,25

The microtissue spheres derived from DPSCs in this study were cultured in low adhesion 96-well plates. After 4 h, the cells gradually clustered toward the center and formed round or oval shapes and the sphere diameter gradually increased, and after 24 h, the sphere diameter decreased slowly but then tended to be stable and no longer changed. The microtissue spheres appeared as white spheres and could be removed from the low adhesion 96-well plate with a pipette gun. No loosening or deformation was observed during the absorption process or after release. Masson’s trichrome staining revealed many blue collagen fibers between the cells in the microtissue spheres, which may explain why the microtissue spheres were tough and did not loosen or show deformation.

The results of CD31 immunofluorescence staining revealed that microtissue spheres with endothelial-like cells had angiogenic potential, those with neural-like cells had the potential for neurogenesis, and incorporating both types of cells promoted angiogenesis and neurogenesis in the cell microtissue spheres in vitro. In addition, the dead cells in each group appeared mainly in the center of the spheres, which may be related to the sphere diameter. Some researchers have reported that the optimal distance for the effective dispersion of nutrients and oxygen, which are required for cell growth, is 150–200 μm.26,27 The diameter of the microspheres constructed in this study was 400–600 μm; thus, the cells in the center may have died due to insufficient oxygen supply. However, compared with microtissue spheres derived from DPSCs only, those with different proportions of neural-like cells and/or endothelial-like cells combined with DPSCs exhibited fewer dead cells. The greater number of dead cells in Group 7 may be because the nutrients needed by the cells are completely provided by the culture medium during in vitro culture. The culture medium of Group 7 was composed of complete medium only and growth factors and some nutrients were absent, which led to more dead cells in the Group 7 cell microtissue spheres.

Pulp tissue is a loose connective tissue rich in nerves and blood vessels, and the success of pulp tissue regeneration depends on the regeneration of nerves and blood vessels.2,28 In this study, except for those in the control group, blue collagen-like structures were observed in the apex of the roots of the rabbit teeth after ectopic transplantation, confirming that the regenerated tissues were rich in collagen fibers. In addition, except for the control group, the regenerated pulp tissue contained many tubular capillaries, red blood cells were visible in the lumen, and CD31 was positively expressed. These observations indicate that all 7 types of microtissue spheres had the ability to form blood vessels, and furthermore, the ability of the spheres in the third and fourth groups to form blood vessels was significantly greater than that of the spheres in the seventh group. Moreover, Nestin- and GFAP-positive cells were detected in the newly formed tissue, suggesting neuronal differentiation, but definitive innervation remains to be confirmed. These findings suggest that the 7 types of cell microtissue spheres have potential for pulp regeneration. However, quantitative analysis showed that there was no significant difference in endothelial cell marker (CD31) and neuronal marker (Nestin and GFAP) expression in the regenerated tissues from the 7 experimental groups, which may be due to the short duration of the implantation.

Masson’s trichrome staining revealed local necrosis at the distal ends of the grafts in Groups 5, 6 and 7 but not the other groups, which may have been caused by insufficient oxygen and nutrient exchange. Studies have shown that when cells leave the in vitro culture environment and are transplanted into the body, the blood vessels and tissue fluid of the host provide oxygen and nutrients, which depends mainly on diffusion. However, diffusion of the host tissue fluid and blood vessels is limited to approximately 100 μm.29 If the graft is large, it will face the risk of necrosis and apoptosis due to hypoxia and a lack of nutrients. However, grafts that are prevascularized in vitro exhibit substantial reductions in such risks. However, in this study, the length of the graft was approximately 8–10 mm, making host blood vessel and tissue fluid diffusion relatively difficult; thus, some areas of the graft may not have received sufficient oxygen or nutrients. In addition, no endothelial-like cells were added to the fifth, sixth, or seventh groups, indicating that significant vascularization would be limited in these groups in vitro. Although focal necrosis was present in Groups 5, 6 and 7, odontoblast-like cells were observed in the middle and apex of the root, indicating that the regenerated pulp-like tissue in these groups retained dentinogenic potential.30 Immunohistochemical staining further revealed focal CD31-positive vascular-like structures and early neuronal marker expression within the pulp tissue. Future experiments will explore whether necrotic foci form in these 3 groups by increasing the sample size and performing orthotopic transplantation experiments.

In the present study, we adopted a scaffold-free microtissue sphere composed exclusively of DPSCs, endothelial-like cells and neural-like cells. While scaffold-based strategies provide 3D templates that mimic the ECM, deliver growth factors, and mechanically constrain root canal space, their clinical translation faces persistent hurdles: residual scaffold degradation products may elicit chronic inflammation; mismatch of the elastic modulus with native pulp can suppress neuro-vascular ingrowth; and premature scaffold compression in narrow canals jeopardizes cell survival.3 Conversely, scaffold-free microtissue spheres circumvent these limitations by leveraging autonomous cell–cell and cell–ECM interactions, yielding tissues in which biochemical and mechanical cues evolve endogenously. Indeed, spheres self-produce a collagen-rich dental pulp/dentine-like tissue that not only integrates with host dentin walls31 but also facilitates micro-vascular anastomosis after implantation.32 Dissanayaka et al.’s systematic review reported that scaffold-free strategies conferred advantages over scaffold-based approaches with respect to vascular density, neural regeneration, and overall tissue maturity.3

Nonetheless, scaffold-free constructs are not without shortcomings. Their initial relatively low mechanical robustness rendered them prone to shear damage during surgical manipulation, especially in full-length human roots.33 Moreover, microspheres with a diameter larger than 200 μm or a densely packed spheroid core can impede oxygen diffusion, probably leading to central necrosis and apoptosis unless rapid vascularization is achieved.26,27 Recent evidence therefore supports a synergistic paradigm in which scaffold-free microtissue spheroids are transiently encased within a precisely engineered, photo-degradable hydrogel sleeve that provides mechanical shielding during implantation and then softens on demand to release the spheroids into the canal space.34 Such hybrid constructs retain the biological fidelity of scaffold-free units while borrowing the short-term mechanical advantages of scaffolds, which may have great potential in regenerative endodontics.

Although this study demonstrated ectopic pulp-like tissue formation accompanied by neovascularization and neuronal marker expression at 8 weeks post-implantation, the long-term stability, maturation, and functional integration of these regenerated tissues remain unknown. Previous studies have shown that regenerated tissues within root canals may undergo progressive calcification, fibrosis, or even necrosis beyond 6–12 months, leading to compromised pulp vitality and root development35,36; therefore, the 8-week observation period in our study is insufficient to evaluate late-stage events. Furthermore, although the subcutaneous ectopic model serves as an initial verification, it lacks the distinctive oral microenvironment—encompassing masticatory mechanical stress, ionic release from dentin, thermal cycling, and the resident commensal microbiota—any of which can alter stem cell fate and affect the long-term equilibrium between mineralization and vascular remodeling.37,38 To address these limitations, our future work will extend the in vivo observation window to 6–12 months using orthotopic models to monitor tissue maturation, neurovascular function (e.g., sensory testing and vascular perfusion imaging), and adverse events (e.g., calcification and inflammation). Moreover, whether regenerated nerves integrate with host trigeminal ganglia and whether neovessels exhibit physiological responsiveness will be further assessed.

Conclusions

In this study, DPSCs were induced to differentiate into endothelial-like cells and neural-like cells. Cell microtissue spheres derived from DPSCs were successfully constructed via the low adhesion method. The microtissue spheres were dense, and their morphology was stable. The microtissue spheres containing endothelial-like cells exhibited enhanced endothelial cell marker expression, and those with neural-like cells showed increased neuronal marker expression. In addition, cell activity was maintained in the microtissue spheres from all 7 experimental groups, and compared with spheres composed of DPSCs only, microtissue spheres containing different proportions of endothelial-like cells and neural-like cells with or without DPSCs presented higher cell survival rates in vivo. After ectopic transplantation of the rabbit molars into mice, the regenerated pulp-like tissue filled the root canals in all groups, accompanied by evident neovascularization and neuronal marker expression. These cell microtissue spheres demonstrated the potential for use as grafts for pulp regeneration therapy.
