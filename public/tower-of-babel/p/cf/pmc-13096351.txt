Multiple programmed cell death patterns predict the prognosis and therapeutic sensitivity in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Rong Tang, Zhaoyue Lu, Zhimou Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProgrammed cell death (PCD) plays a critical regulatory role in various human malignancies including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC); however, existing PCD-related prognostic models remain limited. This study aims to integrate multiple forms of PCD to identify more effective biomarkers for improved prognostic assessment and therapeutic response prediction in HNSCC.MethodsPCD-related genes were collected from public databases and relevant literature. Expression matrices were split into training and validation cohorts at a predetermined ratio. An autoencoder was used to extract low-dimensional features, which were then employed together with the XGBoost algorithm to construct a prognostic model. The model was validated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and ROC curves. Differential gene expression analysis, immune infiltration profiling, and drug sensitivity analysis were also performed.ResultsA novel multimodal programmed cell death (MPD) model was developed for HNSCC, enabling effective patient stratification into risk groups with distinct survival outcomes. The risk score was identified as an independent prognostic factor. Biologically, the low-risk group exhibited an immune-active phenotype, characterized by increased expression of immune checkpoints such as PD-1 and CTLA-4, providing a molecular basis for their potential susceptibility to immunotherapy. The model also identified differential drug sensitivity profiles, informing the design of tailored therapeutic strategies based on a patient’s MPD risk group.ConclusionThe MPD model helps distinguish prognosis in HNSCC patients, evaluate the benefits of immunotherapy, and supports the design of appropriate treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04827-2.

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) is currently the sixth most common malignant tumor in the world, mainly including neck tumors (such as Thyroid tumors), otolaryngological tumors (such as laryngeal cancer, oropharyngeal cancer), and oral and maxillofacial tumors (such as tongue cancer and oral cancer) [1]. In 2020, more than 1 million cases of HNC were diagnosed globally, with more than 500,000 new deaths [2–4]. In recent years, the morbidity of HNC has shown an increasing trend, which is closely related to smoking, alcohol consumption, and HPV infection [5]. The vast majority of HNC patients have squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), accounting for approximately 90%. Despite significant advances in comprehensive sequential treatment for HNSCC, the 5-year survival rate for HNSCC patients remains low [6, 7]. Data indicate that 90% of cancer patients die from metastasis. Cervical Lymph Node Metastasis is the most common in HNSCC [8]. The traditional HNSCC staging divides patients into HPV-negative and HPV-positive, but this approach cannot accurately classify patient stages or guide prognosis. The Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) staging system compensates for this limitation [6]. The TNM staging system helps guide clinical treatment and predict patient prognosis; however, this anatomical staging system still fails to fully reflect the complexity and heterogeneity of individual patient tumors [9]. Therefore, identifying effective and sensitive biomarkers for better diagnosis, treatment response, and prognosis is urgently needed.

PCD is an active cell death mechanism precisely regulated by genetic programs, commonly observed during the development of organisms [10, 11]. In recent years, multiple types of cell death have been identified, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, autophagy, necroptosis, cuproptosis, disulfidptosis, parthanatos, entotic cell death, netotic cell death, lysosome-dependent cell death, alkaliptosis, oxeiptosis, and zinc-dependent cell death [12]. The above-mentioned cell death mechanisms play a critical role not only in maintaining homeostasis, regulating inflammatory responses, and immune balance but also in tumor progression [10, 11, 13]. Tumor cells often exhibit anti-apoptotic characteristics, allowing them to evade normal cell death mechanisms and continue proliferating [14]. In recent years, breakthroughs in high-throughput sequencing and deep machine learning algorithms have driven the development of precision oncology. Among these, multi-gene predictive models based on PCD have attracted significant attention due to their important roles in prognosis assessment and treatment response prediction. However, most of these models have been developed based on a single form of PCD [15]. Therefore, understanding the roles of multiple types of PCD in tumors is crucial for identifying more effective predictive biomarkers.

To develop a better prognostic model and provide more effective treatment strategies, we must gain a deeper understanding of these cell death patterns. The characterization of different cell death patterns may offer new insights into the underlying mechanisms of HNSCC initiation and progression. Describing how various cell death patterns alter the tumor microenvironment, affect the response to immunotherapy, and influence the prognosis of patients with HNSCC can provide new advances in improving the efficacy of immunotherapy and prognosis in HNSCC. During the occurrence and progression of malignant tumors, PCD is undoubtedly a powerful weapon in suppressing the survival and proliferation of tumor cells [16]. This study collected each PCD-related gene as a modality, used the modality genes of 14 types of programmed cell death to screen prognosis-related features through autoencoders, thereby constructing a neural network prognostic model and selecting core feature genes related to prognosis. This model can serve as the basis for personalized treatment strategies and is expected to become a guiding model for clinical diagnosis and prognosis of HNSCC.

Methods and materials

Data source and preprocessing

We obtained them by searching various databases using the keyword PCD and reviewing previous literature. These included databases such as the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), GeneCards, and relevant publications [12, 17–19]. After acquisition, the gene lists for each PCD type were subjected to duplication to ensure the uniqueness of each gene. A total of 1,777 genes related to 14 types of PCD were collected, including 580 genes of apoptosis, 54 of pyroptosis, 292 of ferroptosis, 368 of autophagy, 150 of necroptosis, 21 of cuproptosis, 24 of disulfidptosis, 9 of parthanatos, 15 of entotic cell death, 8 of netotic cell death, 220 of lysosome-dependent cell death, 7 of alkaliptosis, 5 of oxeiptosis, and 24 of zinc-dependent cell death (Table S1).

In our study, we utilized R version 4.3.3 with the “TCGAbiolinks” package (v.2.37.1) for data acquisition. Transcriptome data comprising 510 tumor samples were obtained via the GDC query function, while clinical data for 528 samples were sourced from the TCGA portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-HNSC). Following the removal of duplicates, missing values, and outliers, we integrated the transcriptomic data with clinical annotations. The resulting cohort of 510 HNSCC patients was subsequently randomly divided into a training cohort (n = 362) and a validation cohort (n = 148) at a 7:3 ratio using the “caret“(v.7.0–1) package.

Autoencoder

Autoencoder is a type of Neural network used for unsupervised learning, capable of learning effective data representation in an unsupervised manner [20]. An autoencoder comprises an encoder (f) and a decoder (g), which map the input X to a lower-dimensional representation Z and then back to X, respectively. The autoencoder formula is:

where X is the input data, f is the encoder function, g is the decoder function, and X′ is the reconstructed output.

By minimizing the Reconstruction error, the model captures all high-dimensional features from the data [21]. In this study, we utilize the autoencoder to transform each modality of data into a low-dimensional representation. Each modality has an autoencoder with two hidden layers set to 32 and 4, respectively, meaning that each modality ultimately yields four lowest-dimensional features. The validation ratio is set to 0.2, the number of training epochs is set to 150, and the learning rate is set to 0.001. To mitigate overfitting, a dropout rate of 0.3 was applied, and an early stopping strategy was additionally utilized. In total, we obtained 14*4 low-dimensional modal features.

XGBoost construction of survival analysis model

Based on the low-dimensional modal feature expression data of the Training cohort, univariate Cox analysis was performed using the R package “survival” (v.3.5-8) to screen for feature gene sets significantly associated with prognosis.

The machine learning model, especially XGBoost, has attracted attention due to its more effective capability in capturing complex nonlinear relationships compared to traditional models [22]. Therefore, using the obtained low-dimensional modal features data and prognostically significant gene sets, we developed an XGBoost model through the “xgboost” (v.1.7.11.1) package in R to predict patients’ prognostic risk. In this model, the risk score was derived as the aggregated output of the decision tree ensemble, which was trained to minimize the Cox partial log-likelihood. This score is a linear combination of the trees predictions and provides a quantitative estimate of a patient’s relative risk, with higher values indicating poorer prognosis. To construct the XGBoost model, we used the predefined 70% cohort for training and the remaining 30% cohort for validation. To rigorously mitigate overfitting and optimize model performance, we implemented a 5-fold cross-validation (CV) strategy on the training cohort. During the model parameter tuning process, we optimized the hyperparameters through random search with 100 iterations. The final XGBoost survival model was configured with the following parameters: We choose “survival: cox” to model survival data using the Cox proportional hazards framework; eval_metric = “cox-nloglik” to optimize the negative partial log-likelihood; learning rate = 0.05 to prevent overfitting; max_depth = 3 to control tree complexity and enhance interpretability; and nrounds = 100 (number of boosting iterations). The training data were prepared using right-censored survival information, where the label lower bound was set to the observed time and the upper bound was set to infinity for censored observations. After identifying the optimal parameter set through validation set performance, we made predictions on the test set, calculated and output the feature importance, and obtained a visualized decision tree of the XGBoost model [23].

Validation of the constructed MPD model

Based on the median value of the risk score, we divided the samples into high and low risk groups and performed Kaplan–Meier analyses separately for the training set and test set using the R packages “survival” and “survminer” (v.0.5.0). Subsequently, the two datasets were merged for validation. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated using the R package “timeROC” v.0.4. The independent post-analysis forest plot and risk curve were generated using custom R functions, and the heatmap was created using the R package “pheatmap” (v.1.0.13).

Differential analysis and tumor microenvironment (TME) assessment

Differential gene expression analysis was performed on the obtained RNA-seq data using the R package “DESeq2” (v.1.42.1). Differentially expressed genes were identified based on the thresholds of padj < 0.05 and logFC > 1, and the results were visualized using a volcano plot. The “tidyestimate” v.1.1.1 package in R language was used to evaluate the stromal and immunization cells within tumor tissues, to determine the immunization score, stromal score, estimate score, and tumor purity of HNSCC patients, which were presented in the form of a heatmap [24]. The volcano plot was generated using the “tidyverse” v.2.0.0 and “dplyr” (v.1.1.4) packages in R, and the heatmap was created using the “ComplexHeatmap” (v.2.18.0) package.

Evaluation of the model in predicting immunotherapy function

Immune escape plays an important role in tumorigenesis, which is closely related to the aberrant expression of immune checkpoint inhibitor genes in tumors [25]. To better assess the effectiveness of the predictive model and provide new directions for immunotherapy in patients from high and low risk groups, we used the “limma” (v.3.58.1) and “ggpubr” (v.0.6.1) packages in R language to perform differential gene analysis between some representative immune checkpoint inhibitor genes and the expression matrix collected [26, 27].

In addition, since the Immunophenoscore (IPS) is a superior predictor of antibody responses to cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and programmed cell death protein 1 (anti-PD-1) [28], Therefore, we further evaluated the model’s ability to predict immunotherapy response by comparing the differences in IPS between high and low risk score groups. In this analysis, the IPS of HNSCC patients was obtained from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (https://tcia.at/home).

Drug sensitivity prediction and differential analysis

Through the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (www.cancerRxgene.org), we obtained drug response data and sensitivity-related genomic markers to predict drug sensitivity in samples with high and low risk scores. Drug sensitivity prediction was performed using the R package “oncoPredict” (v.1.2) [29], which employs ridge regression models trained on the GDSC2 dataset to estimate the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for each tumor sample. During the prediction process, as implemented in the “calcPhenotype” function, we removed low-variance genes from the raw data (the 20% of genes with the least expression variation were eliminated by setting the “removeLowVaryingGenes = 0.2” parameter) and performed batch correction between the GDSC cell line data and our TCGA-HNSCC transcriptomic data using the EB algorithm of ComBat (by setting batchCorrect = ‘eb’), a standard method for mitigating non-biological technical variations across different dataset [30]. In addition, we used the R package “limma” to conduct differential analysis of the predicted IC50 values between risk groups, setting the P-value filtering threshold at 0.001. As a result, 12 drugs showed significant differences in treatment between the high and low risk groups, which were visualized using a box plot. The box plot was generated using the R package “ggpubr”.

Construction of MPD model using multimodal data

As illustrated in Fig. 1A, an autoencoder model was employed to extract relevant features from 14 PCD-related gene sets, with the training loss progression of the gene sets detailed in Supplementary Figure S1. Table 1 shows the clinical characteristics of patients with HNSCC in the different cohorts. The four most profound features were selected from each gene set and subsequently analyzed using univariate Cox regression to identify a subset of survival-associated features (Fig. 1B).

Using the selected prognosis-associated features, we constructed an MPD model in the training set. The decision-making process of the model was interpreted through visualization of the XGBoost decision tree (Fig. 1C), where all splitting nodes demonstrated gain values exceeding 3, indicating that these splits provided substantial improvement to the model’s performance. The mean Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method was employed to evaluate the importance of each candidate predictor [23, 31]. The top 7 most important features are shown in Fig. 1D. Among them, alkaliptosis_3—the third autoencoder-derived feature gene set for alkaliptosis—demonstrated the highest importance, with a score above 0.2, establishing it as the model’s most influential predictive feature.

Evaluation and validation of the MPD model

In the training cohort, patients with higher risk scores were associated with increased patient mortality and decreased survival time (Fig. 2A). Correspondingly, the Kaplan-Meier curve showed that patients in the high-risk group had significantly shorter overall survival (OS) compared to those in the low-risk group (survdiff.P < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). The model’s efficacy was evaluated using the ROC curve, and the 1-year, 2-year, and 3-year AUC value were 0.810, 0.868, and 0.903, respectively (Fig. 2C). Moreover, similar results were observed in both the validation cohort and the entire TCGA cohort, where patient mortality increased with rising risk score (Fig. 2D, G). There was a significant difference in OS between the high and low risk groups (Fig. 2E, H), and the AUC values for 1-year, 2-year, and 3-year OS all demonstrated good performance (Fig. 2F, I).

Utilizing data from the entire TCGA cohort, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses of clinicopathological variables (age, sex, tumor grade, and tumor stage) as well as overall survival demonstrated that the model’s risk score could serve as an independent prognostic predictor for patients (Fig. 3A, B). Furthermore, the multi-indicator ROC curve revealed that the risk score achieved an AUC value of 0.750 in the clinical ROC curve (Fig. 3C), which was significantly higher than those of other clinical features. This suggests that the MPD model may provide improved predictive capability compared to these clinical indicators alone.

Differentially expressed genes screening and TME analysis

To untangle the differential gene expression profiles and tumor microenvironment characteristics between high- and low-risk patient groups, we performed comparative analyses on Illumina RNA-seq data from 510 HNSCC cases in TCGA. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the low- and high-risk groups in total TCGA-HNSC cohort were visualized via volcano plot (Fig. 3D), revealing significantly elevated expression of KRT36, DRGX, and TDRG1 in the low-risk group, whereas SCGB2A2, MUC6, and FABP4 were predominantly expressed in the high-risk group. Tumor heterogeneity analysis demonstrated that the low-risk group exhibited significantly higher stromal score, immune score, and estimate score compared to the high-risk group, whereas the latter displayed elevated tumor purity. Taken together, these observations suggest that tumors in the low-risk group may be characterized by more pronounced immune cell infiltration relative to the high-risk group (Fig. 3E).

As shown in Fig. 1D, our model evaluated the importance of each feature and visualized the top seven important features. To further explore the underlying molecular mechanisms, we visualized the top three important Feature gene sets, namely Alkaliptosis_3, Cuproptosis_1, and Parthanatos_4, and highlighted a series of feature genes, including IKBKB, CDKN2A, and OGG1 (Fig. 3F-H).

The MPD model associates with putative biomarkers of immunotherapy response

Based on previous analyses indicating significant differences in immune cell distribution patterns between high- and low-risk patients, we further examined the association between the model and the expression of ICI-related biomarkers. In the entire TCGA cohort, the high-risk group was associated with low expression of PDCD1 (P = 0.0029, Fig. 4A), CTLA4 (P = 0.00067, Fig. 4B), TIGIT (P = 3.8e − 05, Fig. 4C), and IDO1 (P = 0.013, Fig. 4D). The scatter plot indicated that the expression of these ICI-related marker genes decreased with increasing risk scores (Fig. 4E–H), which was consistent with the results shown in the box plot.

The immunophenotype score was used to evaluate ICI treatment response [32]. Given that the differential genes of ICI suggested an immune-activated state, we further validated its functional impact through IPS. Based on the expression of CTLA-4 and PD-1, patients with HNSCC in the entire TCGA cohort were classified into four types. In the CTLA-4 − PD-1 − and CTLA-4 + PD-1 + groups, the IPS of low-risk patients was significantly higher than that of high-risk patients (Fig. 4I–L). Higher IPS is positively correlated with better responses to anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 therapies [28]. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 3E, the low-risk group exhibited higher immune cell infiltration, leading to upregulation of immune checkpoint expression in response to “hot tumor” characteristics, which may be associated with a better response to immunotherapy [33, 34]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the low-risk group, as defined by our MPD model, exhibits molecular and immunologic characteristics that are commonly associated with favorable responses to immunotherapy in other cancers. However, this predictive value requires validation in future studies with clinical cohorts treated with ICIs.

Drug sensitivity analysis

To explore potential therapeutic implications of the MPD model, we utilized data from the GDSC database to compare high- and low-risk groups and identify candidate compounds with differential predicted drug sensitivity. Through drug sensitivity differential analysis, 12 significantly sensitive drugs were screened, and four of them were visualized: Alpelisib (P = 0.00017, Fig. 4M), Nelarabine (P = 0.00047, Fig. 4N), Sinularin (P = 0.00098, Fig. 4O), and Ulixertinib (P = 6.3e − 05, Fig. 4P). Among these compounds, the first three drugs were predicted to be more effective in low-risk patients, whereas the fourth drug showed higher predicted sensitivity in the high-risk group.

Discussion

HNSCC is a highly aggressive malignancy with heterogeneous prognosis [35], underscoring the urgent need to identify more sensitive biomarkers for prognostic prediction and treatment guidance. We collected 14 gene sets related to PCD modalities. The multimodal features selected through a neural network model demonstrated robust prognostic stratification and biological relevance in HNSCC patients, owing to the diversity and salience of the modalities. Furthermore, differential gene expression analysis between high- and low-risk groups, as stratified by the MPD model, provided insights for clinical diagnosis and targeted therapy [36]. Immune checkpoint analysis and drug sensitivity profiling offered new perspectives for exploring drug resistance mechanisms and developing novel therapeutics [37].

Recently, there has been growing interest in constructing more effective prognostic models to improve diagnosis, treatment, and outcome prediction for HNSCC patients. However, most existing prognostic models focus on a single dimension, such as M2 macrophage-related genes [38] or E2F expression profiles [39]. While these models have contributed to HNSCC management, they exhibit limitations in feature richness and sensitivity. The MPD model addresses these shortcomings. Moreover, given the critical role of PCD as a second messenger in HNSCC development and the fact that different PCD types follow distinct regulatory pathways, this makes it unlikely for tumor cells to evade all forms of cell death simultaneously [40]. Therefore, our study presents a potentially innovative and feasible approach.

Furthermore, although penalized Cox regression approaches such as LASSO-Cox are widely used for prognostic modeling, their linear assumptions may limit the ability to capture complex feature interactions. In contrast, the AE-XGBoost framework enables flexible nonlinear modeling by integrating neural network–based feature representation with tree-based ensembles. Therefore, the AE-XGBoost framework may offer advantages in capturing complex nonlinear feature interactions compared with traditional linear survival models. To validate this, we constructed a traditional LASSO-Cox regression model using the the same set of PCD-related genes (Fig. S2). The results confirmed that the predictive performance of the MPD-XGBoost model was significantly superior to that of the LASSO-Cox regression model and demonstrated greater stability.

Among the differentially expressed genes we identified, several with significant alterations may elucidate molecular mechanisms underlying tumor progression and facilitate the development of novel therapies. The downregulated gene KRT36 belongs to the type I “acidic” keratin gene family, located on chromosome 17q21.2, and is highly expressed in epithelial cells, hair, nails, and tongue tissue [41, 42]. KRT36 is involved in epidermal cell differentiation and is a type I hair keratin whose role in the oral cavity remains unclear. Evidence suggests that KRT36 plays a role in tumor development, with its expression progressively decreasing as the tumor advances [42, 43]. DRGX (dorsal root ganglion homeobox) is a paired homeodomain transcription factor, also known as PRRXL1, and serves as a key regulator in the development and maintenance of vertebrate dorsal root ganglia. It plays an essential role in sensory neuron specification during embryogenesis [44]. Some studies indicate that post-developmental downregulation of DRGX contributes to neuropathic pain via transcriptional regulation of pain-related genes in primary sensory neurons [45]. TDRG1 (testis development-related gene 1) plays a pivotal role in the progression of several malignancies [46]. It encodes a 100-amino-acid protein (TDRG1 protein) whose expression is elevated in testicular seminomas compared to normal testicular tissue. We further demonstrated that TDRG1 promotes invasion and proliferation in seminoma by activating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, indicating its oncogenic role in seminoma [47–49]. Notably, TDRG1 expression is downregulated in HNSCC patients, which may be attributable to tissue specificity or further suppression during HNSCC progression. The upregulated gene SCGB2A2, also known as mammaglobin A, is primarily expressed in mucosal tissues and encodes a 93-amino-acid protein with a predicted molecular weight of 10.5 kDa [50–52]. Initially found to be overexpressed in breast cancer, its expression was thought to be restricted to normal and malignant breast tissue [51]. However, recent studies have shown that it is also expressed in extra-mammary tissues [53, 54]. MUC6, or mucin 6, is produced by pyloric gland cells in the gastric antrum and mucous neck cells in the gastric submucosa and constitutes a major component of the gastric mucus barrier. MUC6 expressions have been observed in gastric and cancer cells and play an important role in cancer progression [55, 56]. Aberrant mucin expression can lead to loss of epithelial polarity and promote epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby enhancing cell motility and invasion. This is a critical step in tumorigenesis [57–59]. FABP4, fatty acid-binding protein 4, is a known oncogenic factor that plays a key role in shaping the tumor microenvironment and promoting tumor-supportive interactions among adipocytes, macrophages, and endothelial cells. FABP4 is involved in regulating cancer cell proliferation and quiescence [60, 61], and evidence supports its significance in the progression and metastasis of various cancer types, including ovarian cancer [62] and oral squamous cell carcinoma [63]. Additionally, feature importance ranking within our multimodal model highlighted several genes with biological relevance. IKBKB, inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa B kinase subunit beta, plays a central role in nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway activation. Dysregulation of NF-κB signaling is associated with various human diseases, including inborn errors of immunity (IEI), autoimmune diseases, and malignancies. Thus, aberrant IKBKB activity can profoundly impact cellular homeostasis and disease pathophysiology [64–66]. CDKN2A encodes the p16 and p14 tumor suppressor proteins. Inactivating variants in CDKN2A are the most common cause of familial melanoma, accounting for at least one-third of all cases [67, 68]. Our study suggests it is equally important in HNSCC. OGG1, 8-oxoguanine glycosylase 1, catalyzes the DNA base excision repair (BER) pathway for repairing oxidative DNA damage. Although OGG1 has been studied in many cancers, its prognostic significance and functional role in HNSCC remain incompletely understood [69, 70]. Identifying these genes is critical for precision medicine in HNSCC and may provide key clues for developing new targeted drugs. The biological functions of the majority of these genes were consistent with previous reports, which supports the reliability of our results. Importantly, the identification of these biologically relevant genes also highlights the translational potential of the proposed MPD model. Several key genes, including IKBKB and CDKN2A, are amenable to experimental validation and may serve as clinically relevant biomarkers. In future studies, their expression patterns could be validated in independent HNSCC cohorts using immunohistochemistry or quantitative PCR in tumor tissues, enabling assessment of their association with clinicopathological features and patient outcomes. Moreover, functional assays such as gene knockdown or overexpression in HNSCC cell lines, as well as in vivo models, could help clarify their mechanistic roles in tumor progression and therapeutic response. Such experimental validation would provide a critical bridge between this computational prognostic model and its potential clinical application.

Recently, cancer immunotherapy has provided new opportunities to inhibit tumor progression, recurrence, and metastasis. However, immunotherapy shows limited efficacy in immunologically “cold” tumors, including many HNSCC cases [71], which are characterized by insufficient immune cell infiltration and low expression of immune checkpoints, resulting in poor response to immune activation strategies. For example, the objective response rate to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy in unselected HNSCC patients is only 13–14% [72–74], underscoring the substantial influence of the immune microenvironment on treatment response. Therefore, assessing the immune status of the tumor prior to formulating treatment strategies is particularly important. In contrast to cold tumors, hot tumors are characterized by abundant immune cell infiltration, particularly cytotoxic T cells, and high expression of multiple immune checkpoint molecules [71]. Expression levels of these factors can serve as important biomarkers for predicting immunotherapy response and guiding clinical precision medicine. Accordingly, we utilized our multimodal neural network prognostic model to investigate potential immunotherapy benefits across different risk groups. As expected, expression levels of PDCD1, CTLA4, TIGIT, and IDO1 were significantly lower in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group, consistent with the IPS results. Patients in the low-risk group exhibited an immune-active phenotype, which consists of a tumor microenvironment more likely to respond to immunotherapy.

To improve clinical outcomes and guide treatment strategies for HNSCC patients, we screened for drugs with potential sensitivity in HNSCC. Alpelisib is an orally bioavailable small-molecule α-specific PI3K inhibitor that selectively inhibits p110α with approximately 50-fold greater potency than other isoforms [75]. The combination of alpelisib and fulvestrant exhibits synergistic antitumor activity [76, 77]. While alpelisib is primarily used in breast cancer treatment [78], a limited number of studies have explored its efficacy in HNSCC [79]. Nelarabine is an arabinosylguanine (ara-G) prodrug that is converted to ara-G and subsequently phosphorylated to ara-G triphosphate (ara-GTP). Accumulation of ara-GTP ultimately induces apoptosis [80]. It is approved for relapsed/refractory T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma (T-ALL/LBL) [81]. Some reports suggest that nelarabine may control HNSCC by inhibiting Lymphovascular invasion (LOI) [82], though its feasibility and mechanism of action require further investigation. Sinularin is a bioactive compound extracted from the soft coral Sinularia flexibilis and exhibits antitumor activity in various human cancer cell types [83]. Studies indicate that sinularin is effective in immunosuppressive therapy for HNSCC patients with low expression of arylsulfatase I (ARSI), but sensitivity decreases when ARSI is highly expressed [84]. These three drugs showed greater potential benefit for low-risk patients identified by our model. To guide treatment for high-risk patients, we also identified ulixertinib. Ulixertinib (BVD-523) is an ERK1/2 kinase inhibitor reported to exhibit potent preclinical activity in BRAF- and RAS-mutated cell lines [85, 86]. However, its therapeutic effect and mechanism in HNSCC remain unclear.

Furthermore, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the MPD model was developed and internally validated exclusively within the TCGA-HNSCC cohort using a predefined training–validation split. Although the model demonstrated robust prognostic performance within this framework, its generalizability to other patient populations has not yet been confirmed. Validation using entirely independent external datasets, such as GEO cohorts, or prospective clinical cohorts will be essential to further assess the stability and clinical applicability of the model. Second, as this study was based solely on public transcriptomic data and computational analyses, the biological functions of key genes and the predicted drug sensitivities were not experimentally validated. Future studies incorporating molecular validation and functional experiments, as well as clinical cohort studies, will be necessary to strengthen the translational relevance of our findings. Nevertheless, the present study provides a systematic and feasible framework for integrating multiple programmed cell death modalities in prognostic modeling for HNSCC.

In summary, we established the MPD model that incorporates 14 PCD modalities to predict outcomes in HNSCC patients. We identified differentially expressed genes, evaluated immune cell infiltration, and screened relevant targeted drugs. This model can be used to classify HNSCC patient subtypes, assess potential benefits of immunotherapy, and provide a conceptual framework for future studies exploring risk stratification and therapeutic relevance in HNSCC.
