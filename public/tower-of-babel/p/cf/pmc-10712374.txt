New Directions for Health Literacy Research
Authors: Barry D. Weiss, Mary Ann Abrams, Elizabeth D. Mansfield, Kristine Sørensen
Published: 2023

A simple search of PubMed for the term “health literacy” shows a steep increase in publications over the past decade. However, a scan of those publications reveals the vast majority are about the same four issues: (1) a significant proportion of the population, including those with specific health conditions, has limited health literacy; (2) limited health literacy is a risk factor for negative health outcomes; (3) information about health conditions, both handouts and online websites, is often too difficult for people to read and understand; and (4) creating screening instruments to assess health literacy of various population subgroups. This finding is unchanged from a 2015 review of health literacy articles (Weiss, 2015).

Comments on the Institute for Health Literacy Advancement's Healthy Literacy Discussion List have pointed out the need for health literacy research to move in new directions, such as making health systems more health literacy friendly (Institute for Healthcare Advancement, n.d.). It has been noted, for example, that the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality promotes development of health literate health care organizations and describes 10 attributes of such organizations (Brach et al., 2012). Healthy People 2030 provides a formal definition of organizational health literacy which, when combined with its revised definition of personal health literacy, provides a comprehensive definition of health literacy (US Department of Health and Human Services, n.d.). Furthermore, Healthy People 2030 states that attaining health literacy is necessary to achieve health equity (US Department of Health and Human Services, n.d.). The Council of Europe (2023) views health literacy as an ethical concern that is linked to human rights and at a global level, the World Health Organization (2016) calls for development of health literacy approaches to promote health in its 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development.

Such large-scale societal improvements of health literacy will require a systematic approach and innovative strategies to develop health literacy capacity at all levels (Sørensen et al., 2021). This indicates the need to advance the field of health literacy in new directions.

In response, we conducted a workshop at the 2022 Health Literacy Annual Research Conference titled “Making Your Health Literacy Research Count” to raise awareness and garner insights about changing the status quo—to include, for instance, organizational health literacy and social and economic drivers of health literacy. Attendees included health promotion professionals, clinicians, administrators, health literacy researchers, and communication experts, 94% of whom were from the United States (Table 1).

Below we highlight our perspectives as workshop panelists by incorporating recommendations and ideas from workshop attendees about what should be prioritized in ongoing health literacy research. These perspectives fall into several themes.

Theme 1: Research Related to Health Systems

There are four key issues within this theme, related to how research could be better focused on changing health care systems.

Going Beyond the Clinical Health Care Team

Attendees emphasized, and the authors agree, that we need research demonstrating how health literacy aligns with most, if not all, of health care organizations' missions and values—including safety, quality, equity, and patient-centered care—as these are fundamental dimensions of health system leadership. This will require broadening the current focus of health literacy research and education beyond clinical members of the health care team to also include and address issues of concern to those in health administration, executive leadership, boards of directors, and financial officers.

Evaluating Return on Investment

Similarly, the authors and workshop attendees all pointed out the need for research that will awaken health system leadership to the importance of health literacy by, in the words of one workshop participant, “connecting health literacy to dollars.” That is, research needs to demonstrate the financial implications of how improving health literacy can improve health outcomes. If research demonstrates that addressing health literacy can influence lengths of stay, readmission rates, return visits to emergency departments, safety events, and patient experience and satisfaction, it may convince senior leaders that becoming health literate organizations will provide a meaningful return on investment. That is because improvements in those aforementioned factors, and others, are incentivized financially through the Affordable Care Act and by public and private payers. Well-done research will identify both the direct and indirect financial implications of limited health literacy on health care organizations, including costs related to waste, inefficiency, no-shows, and what has long been labeled as non-compliance or non-adherence, which allocates blame to patients rather than acknowledging a failure of the health system to communicate clearly and ensure understanding.

Identifying the Most Effective Interventions

There is also a need to study what kinds of interventions will positively affect such outcomes—strongly enough to garner the attention of health system leaders. The authors agree with the many workshop participants who suggested various interventions that could be studied.

For example, is there a demonstrable benefit to employing and involving health education specialists in the routine care of patients? How about patient navigators? Does training physicians, nurses, and other health professionals (including health professions students and trainees) on how to communicate using plain language and Teach Back affect outcomes of interest? Would there be benefit if health systems routinely provided patients with engaging and health literacy-informed educational videos about common health problems, or apps for patients to save questions for their providers in advance of clinic visits? Would outcomes with financial implications be improved if the system changed the care environment so that patients felt more comfortable asking questions and raising concerns?

Identifying the Most Efficient Interventions

Once we know which interventions are most effective for creating health literate organizations, we also need research to determine how to implement them in practical, simple, and reliable ways. This entails quality improvement methodology, systems processes, and technology, including the electronic health record. Health literacy practices are less likely to be embraced by health systems if they are not scalable and cannot be easily, systematically, and sustainably implemented on a day-to-day clinical level.

Theme 2: Research on Health Literacy Training

If health systems are to be responsive to health literacy issues, all members of the health care team need to be educated, not just about the importance of health literacy, but about how they—in whatever role they serve—can communicate more effectively with patients and their families.

Thus, we need research on how to effectively shift some communication support tasks from physicians and nurses to other members of the health care team, like health educators, patient navigators, pharmacists, community and public health workers, and those who answer phone calls, design websites and patient portals, or create signage for hospitals and clinics. And, once we find out what works well, there needs to be methods and forums for health systems to share and disseminate effective educational approaches with others, a role that professional associations and organizations can support.

Theme 3: Changes in Health Literacy Research Funding

The authors also agree with the many workshop participants who pointed out that successful health literacy researchers tend to be those who repeatedly secure grants for new and ongoing projects that are often focused on specific topics, rather than projects dealing with larger health system issues. And, those researchers are generally affiliated with academic settings.

Funding agencies should broaden their scope to support research by organizations that represent patients and communities. We need to learn from patients, families, and caregivers about what they—rather than academic health literacy researchers—feel are the health system changes that would help them better navigate the health system and manage their personal health needs.

Consideration should also be given to supporting research on the implementation and effectiveness of health literacy training, supported by practical tools and resources, and on innovative approaches like learning collaboratives and research networks. If we want health literacy research to go in new directions, funding agencies need to support projects going in those directions, rather than funding the same types of projects repeatedly.

Theme 4: Improving Evidence-Based Health Literacy Policy Development

Apart from health care research, there is also a need for research involving other relevant sectors. For example, we need to study whether adding health literacy to all levels of education curricula could improve population health literacy over time. Moreover, since the risk for health literacy disparities is higher among certain racial and ethnic groups, there is need for studies on how health literacy issues might vary, and need to be addressed differently, in various cultures and cultural groups to address structural inequity.

For systemic change to happen, it is important to improve evidence-based health literacy policy development by facilitating a better match between conducted research and policy demands. Research could also focus on evaluating policy initiatives such as national action plans on health literacy.

Summary

All the above ideas offer exciting new directions for health literacy research. However, those suggestions also point out that it is not so straightforward to make health systems more responsive to health literacy issues. Health literacy-focused research must demonstrate meaningful mission-related and financial benefits for health systems—extending beyond improving health outcomes. And, there is the challenge of getting that information into the hands of those who need to know it and can make use of it. Ultimately this information must be effectively communicated to health system leaders.

We also need to recognize that health literacy is not a panacea for all that ails health care. Rather, it is one vital domain that cuts across all dimensions of health (Institute of Medicine, 2003); it is necessary but not sufficient. In fact, health literacy can be incorporated into many facets of health besides health systems and their clinical and hospital settings. As just one example, it can be incorporated into national-level food/nutrition labeling policies (Mansfield et al., 2020).

Thus, no single health literacy intervention will magically transform health care and population health. In safety and quality parlance, there could be a health literacy bundle containing key health literacy components—plain language, Teach Back, a welcoming care environment, culturally and linguistically appropriate services, additional support for those needing extra help—that, when reliably implemented, strongly contributes to achieving health organizations' missions, improving outcomes, and striving toward health equity.

Helping funders of health literacy research (e.g., government agencies, foundations, institutes, health systems) recognize and understand the need to change the direction of health literacy research is an approach many have yet to consider. Attention should be given to setting new priorities for funding health literacy research; broadening participation, including those with expertise in business, finance, administration, and process improvement; reaching out to and supporting investigators beyond traditional academic centers; and unique approaches to collaborative learning in real-world settings. Taken together, these could, perhaps, be effective ways to truly advance the field in these important new directions.
