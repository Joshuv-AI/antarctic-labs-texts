Gabapentin Extended Release Tablets in Healthy Subjects Under Fed Conditions: A Randomized, Open-Label, Two-Treatment, Two-Period, Two-Sequence, Single Dose, Crossover, Comparative Bioavailability Study
Authors: Sudershan Kumar, Arshad Khuroo, Sanjay Jagannath Gurule, Sachin Mehra, Hari Krishan Tiwari, Y. Zala, Venkata Rama Rao Kamala, Madhu Kandukuri, Shruti Dharmadhikari, Prashant Devkare, Chintan Khandhedia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGabapentin is effective for treating post-herpetic neuralgia and neuropathic pain by stabilizing nerve activity through blocking calcium channels and reducing neurotransmitter release. Gabapentin is available in immediate-release (IR) and extended-release (ER) formulations. A comparative bioavailability study was conducted between Gabapentin ER 600 mg tablets once-daily (OD) (Gabantin® GRS) [Test (T)] (manufactured by Sun Pharmaceuticals Industries Limited), and Gabapentin Tablets 600 mg OD (Gralise®) [Reference (R)] (distributed by Almatica Pharma LLC) in healthy human male adults under fed conditions.MethodsIn this open-label, balanced, randomized, crossover study each subject received a 600 mg single dose of either T or R in Period 1, followed by crossover treatment in Period 2, with a washout period of 12 days in-between. Pharmacokinetic parameters, including Cmax, AUC0-t, and AUC0₋∞, were assessed. Safety was monitored through treatment-emergent adverse events (AEs).ResultsAll 24 enrolled subjects completed the study. The test formulation demonstrated comparable pharmacokinetic profile to the reference product, meeting the criteria for bioequivalence within acceptable limits (0.80–1.25). The percentage ratio for T vs R product was 0.9171 (90% confidence interval [CI] 0.826–1.0183) for AUC0-t, 0.9191 (90%CI 0.8279–1.0203) for AUC0-∞ and 0.9135 (90%CI 0.8324–1.0026) for Cmax. Plasma concentration-time profiles were similar. One AE of fever was reported after administration of T; no serious adverse event was reported.ConclusionsGabantin® GRS 600 Tablet ER OD of Sun Pharma had a similar pharmacokinetic profile and was bioequivalent to Gralise® in healthy subjects under fed conditions with good safety and tolerability profiles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40268-026-00547-8.

Introduction

Neuropathic pain (NP), as defined by the International Association for the Study of Pain arises from a lesion or disease affecting the somatosensory nervous system [1]. It manifests as spontaneous or stimulus-triggered sensations, including burning, tingling, aching, or electric shock-like pain, often significantly affecting quality of life [2, 3]. NP affects approximately 3% to 17% of the global population [4], with Indian prevalence rates ranging from 7% to 8% in adults [5]. However, available data remains heterogeneous, with wide-ranging frequency estimates of 5 to 2400 cases per 10,000 people [6]. Further, NP is often associated with postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), diabetic neuropathy, leprosy, amputation-related pain, peripheral nerve injury, and stroke [5].

NP is characterized by pathological alterations within the nervous system, including ectopic activity in damaged or adjacent nerves, hyperexcitability of dorsal root ganglia neurons, maladaptive changes in central pathways, and processes of peripheral and central sensitization mediated by diverse molecular mechanisms [7]. NP is known to be difficult-to-treat effectively, with only a subset of patients achieving substantial relief from any single therapeutic option [8]. The management of NP remains highly challenging owing to its complex and multifaceted pathophysiology, the absence of objective diagnostic criteria and resistance to conventional analgesic therapies [2, 9]. Conventional analgesics, particularly non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, are generally ineffective as they do not address the central mechanisms underlying NP [2, 8]. Similarly, opioid medications are avoided owing to concerns about adverse effects, dependence, and potential for misuse [2].

Given these limitations, the therapeutic approach to NP increasingly relies on agents that modulate neural activity rather than traditional analgesic pathways [8]. These include gabapentinoids, such as gabapentin and pregabalin, along with tricyclic antidepressants, and selective serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors [5, 7, 10, 11]. Clinical practice guidelines from the Canadian Pain Society, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, the Neuropathic Pain Special Interest Group, and expert panels in India recommend these drug classes as first-line treatment options for NP management [5, 10, 12, 13]. Additionally, the European Federation of Neurological Societies endorses using gabapentin specifically for PHN and diabetic neuropathy [11].

Gabapentin [1-(aminomethyl) cyclohexane acetic acid], is an anticonvulsant with established efficacy in alleviating NP and seizures [2, 14, 15]. Although structurally similar to gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), a key inhibitory neurotransmitter in the mammalian central nervous system, [15] it does not exert its effects by interacting with GABAA or GABAB receptors [16]. Instead, it targets the α2-δ subunit of voltage-dependent calcium channels, influencing calcium influx and reducing the release of excitatory neurotransmitters [16]. This process diminishes the activation of α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid and glutamate receptors, ultimately diminishing abnormal neuronal hyperexcitability and pain signal transmission, thus providing therapeutic benefit in NP [14, 16].

Gabapentin was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1993 for anticonvulsant use and generically available since 2004 [12, 17]. Although initially introduced as a muscle relaxant and anti-spasmodic, it has been proven to be effective for treating various types of NP, including PHN [12, 17].

There are currently three main formulations of gabapentin used in clinical practice for NP: IR, conventional ER, and gastroretentive [16–18]. IR requires thrice-daily dosing of 600 mg owing to its short half-life of 5–7 h, with plasma peaks 2–3 h post-administration and rapid stomach clearance [16, 18]. To improve convenience and reduce fluctuations in drug levels, ER formulation was developed and approved by the FDA in 2011 [19]. It provides a steady release of the drug over 10 h, allowing for a twice-daily dose (600 mg in the morning and 1200 mg in the evening) [16, 18, 20]. In 2006, India’s Central Drugs Standard Control Organization approved gabapentin ER formulation for NP and as adjunctive therapy for partial seizures in adults [21].

However, both IR and conventional ER formulations are limited by gabapentin’s nonlinear, saturable absorption, which is mediated by a transporter located in the upper GI tract. This results in a short absorption window [22]. As such, conventional sustained-release systems may fail to deliver gabapentin effectively, as the dosage form often transits past the absorption window before full drug release, leading to reduced bioavailability [22]. To address these limitations, innovative gastroretentive drug delivery systems have been developed to retain the drug in the stomach for over 8 h [23–25]. This system consists of a biphasic release profile with an initial rapid release followed by sustained release, thereby optimizing absorption and reducing peak-related adverse effects [25].

Gabantin® GRS ER Tablet utilizes Sun Pharma's patented Gastro Retentive Innovative Device (GRIDTM) technology (Fig. 1) (18). Gabapentin is present in both IR outer layer and ER core of the GRIDTM system. It also contains specialized coatings which enable floatation on gastric contents upon ingestion with food [23, 24]. Activated by gastrointestinal fluids, these coatings cause the dosage form to swell about 5 to 6 times its initial volume, which may help maintaining therapeutic plasma concentrations for a prolonged period. This innovative system may allow for consistent plasma levels and could potentially improve bioavailability, thus allowing for once-daily dosing [23, 24]. Reduced dosing may improve patient compliance [23]. Despite these advances, there is a paucity of comparative pharmacokinetic evidence evaluating the bioavailability of gastric-retentive Gabapentin. This study aimed to assess and compare the bioavailability of Gabapentin ER 600 mg Tablets (Gabantin® GRS) [Test (T)] manufactured by Sun Pharmaceuticals Industries Limited, in comparison to Gabapentin Tablets 600 mg product (Gralise®) [Reference (R)] distributed by Almatica Pharma LLC, in healthy human male adults under fed condition.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was an open-label, balanced, randomized, two-treatment, two-period, two-sequence, single dose, crossover comparative bioavailability study. Study design flowchart. ER extended release, Treatment T test—Gabantin® GRS (Gabapentin ER) Tablets 600 mg of Sun Pharmaceuticals Industries Limited, Treatment R reference—Gralise® (Gabapentin) tablets 600 mg of Almatica Pharma LLC

The study was conducted in compliance with the principles enunciated in the International Conference on Harmonization of Good Clinical Practices, Declaration of Helsinki, and applicable rules and regulations of New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019. The research protocol received approval from the Jamia Hamdard Institutional Ethics Committee on 8 October 2021. Informed consent was obtained from all the subjects prior to the commencement of the study. Although this was an open-label study, laboratory analysts did not have access to the randomization scheme.

Study Participants

Clinically healthy adult human males aged between 18 and 50 years, with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 18.50 kg/m2 and < 30.00 kg/m2, a hemoglobin level ≥ 12.0 g/dL, and those who provided informed consent were eligible. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the Supplementary file.

Study Methodology

Screening

Subjects underwent demographic assessments, a thorough medical history was recorded, and physical examination was performed within 28 days before inclusion in the study. Subject screening procedure included recording demographic data (height, weight, BMI), vital signs, medical and medication history, physical examination, laboratory tests, and other tests (as detailed in the table provided in the Supplementary file). The subjects were to follow a non-vegetarian diet, and were required to have clinically acceptable results for all screening parameters and laboratory investigations, confirming their eligibility for Gabapentin ER tablets 600 mg.

Drug Administration During the Treatment Period

In this two-period crossover study, each subject received a single oral dose of one tablet of either the T or R product, with a 12-day washout period between the two treatment periods. The dosage was set at a single oral dose of 600 mg (one tablet) per period, based on the established safety profile of the drug in healthy subjects (27). Each subject was randomly assigned to one of the two sequences in a balanced manner. Randomization was generated using SAS® system for Windows, release 9.4 or above (SAS Institute Inc., USA). Subjects were admitted to and housed in the Clinical Pharmacology Unit at least 48 h before dose administration and were discharged approximately 36 h after receiving either the T or R product during each period of the study. To ensure consistent dosing conditions, all subjects were admitted to the study center and were provided with a standard meal (dinner) after admission. Meals were served at the following intervals before dose administration: breakfast, lunch, snacks, dinner, breakfast, and lunch at approximately −36, −31, −27, −23, −11, and −8 h, respectively. Subjects fasted for at least 6.5 h before the start of a high-fat, high-calorie dinner, and the dose was administered 30 min after the dinner (a nonvegetarian meal comprising 800–1000 Kcal). Following dosing, subjects were provided breakfast, lunch, snacks, and dinner at approximately 13, 17, 21, and 25-h post-dose, respectively. Water intake was restricted from 1-h pre-dose to 1-h post-dose, except for 240 mL of drinking water given at the time of dose administration. Subjects remained in a sitting or ambulatory posture for the first 2 h after dosing. This structured approach ensured that each dosing period was conducted under controlled and comparable conditions.

Blood Sample Processing and Storage

A total of 46, 2-mL blood samples were collected from each subject. The samples were collected at pre-dose (in duplicate) and at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 7.5, 8, 8.5, 9, 9.5, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, 20, 24, 30, and 36 h post-dose during each period of the study. These blood samples were collected in tri-potassium salt of ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (K3EDTA) vacutainers. Following collection, the blood samples were centrifuged at 3300 RPM for 15 min under refrigeration to separate the plasma.

Throughout the process, a light-protected environment was maintained from the collection of blood samples to the storage of plasma samples. All Aliquot 1 and Aliquot 2 plasma samples were stored in two different deep freezers at temperatures below −15 °C within 2 h of blood collection.

The analytical method for the estimation of Gabapentin in human K3EDTA plasma, using Gabapentin D6 as an internal standard, was developed and validated using Ultra High-Performance Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometric Method (API 4000, AB Sciex) at the Department of Clinical Pharmacology and Pharmacokinetics, Sun Pharmaceutical Industries Ltd., Gurugram 122015, Haryana, India. The sample preparation process was performed using the Solid Phase Extraction technique. The analytical range for Gabapentin in plasma during subject sample analysis was 10.2–8032.5 ng/mL. Method validation was performed in accordance with the FDA Guidance on Bioanalytical Method Validation. The method was evaluated for selectivity, specificity, sensitivity, linearity of response, carryover in human plasma, precision and accuracy (including batch-wise evaluation in human plasma), dilution integrity (assessed as part of precision and accuracy), precision and accuracy in hemolyzed and lipemic matrices, recovery, stability, reinjection reproducibility, matrix effects, ruggedness, and extended precision and accuracy runs. Long-term stability of gabapentin in human plasma was established for 52 days, which adequately covered the maximum storage duration of 33 days for study samples.

Pharmacokinetic Analysis

Pharmacokinetic parameters for Gabapentin were estimated in plasma, including both primary and secondary measurements. Primary pharmacokinetic parameters included the area under the concentration-time curve from time zero to the last quantifiable concentration (AUC0–t), the area under the concentration-time curve extrapolated to infinity (AUC0₋∞), and maximum concentration (Cmax). Secondary pharmacokinetic parameters included the percentage of the AUC extrapolated beyond the last quantifiable concentration (AUC%Extrap), time to reach maximum concentration (Tmax), elimination rate constant (Kel), and half-life (T1/2). The analyses were conducted using the GLM SAS® procedure(s) Version 9.4.

Safety Analysis Criteria

Safety was evaluated through the measurement of vital signs, physical examination, and monitoring of adverse events reported during the study. Laboratory parameters, including biochemistry, urinalysis, and hematology, were reassessed at the end of the study, approximately 36 hours post-dose of Period II, for subjects who had received at least one dose of the investigational products.

Statistical Analysis

Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)

The log-transformed pharmacokinetic parameters (Cmax, AUC0–t, and AUC0₋∞) were analyzed using Type III sum of squares. The main effects included sequence, period, formulations, and subjects nested within sequence. The sequence effect was tested at a 10% level of significance, while formulation and period effects were tested at a 5% level of significance from the ANOVA model. Least-squares means (LSM), the difference between the adjusted formulation means, and the standard error associated with the difference was calculated. These analyses were conducted using the GLM procedure in SAS® Version 9.4.

90% Confidence Intervals (CI) and Ratio Analysis

The 90% CI of the Geometric Least Square Mean Ratio (GMR) of Cmax and AUC0–t for the T and R products of Gabapentin was required to be between 0.80 and 1.25 (inclusive). The ratio (T/R) of means was calculated using the LSM for log-transformed pharmacokinetic parameters (Cmax, AUC0–t, and AUC0₋∞) and expressed as a ratio of the LSM of the R formulation.

Sample Size Justification

Considering a mean ratio range between 0.95 and 1.05 and Intra-subject CV of ~ 19% (based on unpublished data on file), 18 subjects yield a power of 80% to show comparative bioequivalence [26]. Considering possible dropouts and withdrawals, 24 subjects are considered for this study.

Safety Results

The T and R products were well tolerated by the study subjects. One AE (fever) was reported by the subject after administration of the T product. No subjects experienced AEs after the administration of the R product. The AE reported was moderate in severity, non-serious in nature, and unlikely to be related to the study drug. The subject was treated with oral paracetamol and recovered without sequelae. No serious adverse events were reported in the study, and no AEs led to the discontinuation of the study drug. Further, no significant changes were observed in the hematological, biochemical parameters, vital signs, and clinical examinations of all subjects during the study period.

Discussion

The present study assessed the bioavailability of Gabapentin 600 mg ER tablets from Sun Pharmaceutical Industries Limited (T) and Almatica Pharma LLC (R) in healthy, adult, human male subjects under fed conditions. Including only healthy subjects minimized potential risks and ensured that the pharmacokinetic results were not confounded by underlying health conditions, aligning with ethical standards for early-phase trials focused on establishing bioavailability. Also, this study included a small proportion of smokers; however, smoking was not permitted during the study period. Therefore, smoking was not considered a factor contributing to variability in the pharmacokinetic or bioequivalence assessment.

Gabapentin is absorbed via a saturable transporter in the upper GI tract, limiting its bioavailability and making conventional Gabapentin formulations (IR and ER) less effective, requiring two to three daily doses. For NP, the typical clinical maintenance dose for Gabapentin IR (G-IR) formulation is 1800 mg/ day, administered in three divided doses [10]. This dosing schedule aligns with the drug’s elimination half-life of 5–7 h, necessitating frequent dosing to maintain therapeutic levels doses [28]. To address frequent (thrice a day; TID) dosing challenges, a new gastric-retentive gabapentin ER (G-ER) formulation has been developed [17, 22] that aims to achieve a relative bioavailability similar to that of G-IR administered TID while maintaining the same total daily dose [28].

Gabapentin’s pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties, including its saturable absorption, short half-life necessitating frequent dosing of IR forms, and the higher incidence of AEs related to rapid peak plasma concentrations necessitates the use of this advanced ER delivery system [28]. This recently developed gastric-retentive gabapentin formulation is designed for OD dosing that releases the drug more slowly than an IR formulation, which may help prevent the saturation of the active absorption mechanism [17, 22]. Additionally, the gastric-retentive system is intended to help in controlled drug release and keeping the drug in the stomach for longer time, which may allow for consistent plasma levels and a prolonged plateau compared with IR formulation [17, 29]. This approach may enhance gabapentin's bioavailability or may help achieve similar drug exposure while reducing the frequency of dosing [17, 29]. Our study assessed the pharmacokinetics and safety of a single 600 mg oral dose of G-ER, which is lower than the single-escalating doses used in a previous study (2400–6000 mg G-ER) [30].

In the present study, the test formulation (T) exhibited a slower rate of increase, reaching a peak concentration of approximately 4229.28ng/mL at 8.25 h. This was consistent with the results of a previous study that compared pharmacokinetics after a single oral dose of 600 mg of G-IR and 600 mg of G-ER [28]. The plasma concentration-time profiles showed distinct differences between the two formulations [28]. The median Tmax for G-ER was 5.0 h (ranging from 2.5 to 12 h), whereas for the G-IR formulation, it was 4.0 h (ranging from 2.5 to 5 h) [28]. The mean Cmax for the ER ranged from 2970 to 3130 ng/mL, while for the G-IR, it was 4720 ng/mL [28]. These findings highlight the benefits of G-ER in offering a more controlled and sustained release of the drug [28]. In another study, a single peak was observed at 7 h post-administration of G-ER (1800 mg) OD, followed by a steady decline in concentration [17].

The observed mean T1/2 values for the T and R formulations in our study were consistent with the known elimination half-life of gabapentin. Therefore, the present findings do not indicate alteration in the intrinsic elimination of gabapentin. In extended-release and gastro-retentive formulations, continued drug release and absorption may influence the terminal portion of the plasma concentration versus time curve, such that the estimated terminal half-life may represent an apparent half-life rather than solely reflecting elimination [28, 31]. Although the semi-logarithmic profile showed sustained plasma concentrations over several hours, formal assessment of flip-flop kinetics was not performed in this study; therefore, no conclusion regarding flip-flop kinetics can be made.

Moreover, in a fasting state, the Tmax after administering G-ER (single-dose) was 3.5 h, which is nearly the same as that observed with the IR formulation (4.0 h) [28]. However, when taken with food, Tmax increased to 6 h after a meal containing 30% of calories from fat, 6.2 h after a meal with 40% of calories from fat, and extended to 9 h after a meal containing 50% of calories from fat [28]. This prolonged Tmax of single-dose G-ER suggests that although food generally has a minimal impact, higher fat intake may increase gastric retention of the G-ER tablet, extending its absorption duration in the small intestine, while dietary proteins can significantly enhance the rate and extent of gabapentin absorption [28, 32]. Notably, a study found that gastro-retentive ER (1800 mg OD) formulations when administered with a high-fat meal can increase the Cmax of gabapentin by 33–84%, and AUC by 33–118%, resulting in more consistent and higher drug exposure [17]. Consequently, fed conditions were employed in the present study along with gastro-retentive drug systems to reflect the enhanced absorption of gabapentin under typical dietary circumstances. This approach aligns with the recommended clinical practice of administering the gastric-retentive ER formulation with a meal, which prolongs gastric retention time owing to a slower emptying of stomach contents in proportion to the fat content, thus increasing the absorption window [28].

A washout period of 12 days was implemented to minimize potential carryover effects between the two treatment periods. The sequence effect was found to be nonsignificant for the pharmacokinetic parameters AUC0–t, AUC0–∞, and Cmax, confirming the absence of any carryover effect.

This ensured that any potential residual effects from the previous treatment did not influence the subsequent period, thereby validating the suitability of the washout duration and supporting the reliability of the pharmacokinetic comparisons made between the two formulations. This approach adheres to best practices for crossover studies, as endorsed by previous literature in their comparative study on the bioequivalence of two different gabapentin formulations [32, 33].

The primary parameters in this investigation were the AUC0-t, AUC0-∞, and Cmax of gabapentin to evaluate potential bioequivalence between the two formulations T and R. Standard bioequivalence recommendations state that the T/R geometric means ratio's 90% CI must lie between 0.80 and 1.25 for AUC and Cmax to be considered bioequivalent [34, 35]. The criteria used to evaluate the bioequivalence of generic products ensure that the performance of the T product in vivo does not significantly differ from that of the R product [32]. As previously demonstrated in a study comparing the bioequivalence of two gabapentin 300 mg OD formulations, the 90% CI for pharmacokinetic parameters was within the 80.00–125.00% range at 96.64–124.94% for Cmax, 97.60-124.43% for AUC0-t, and 97.90-124.00% for AUC0₋∞ [36]. Similarly, Mak et al. found that the 90% CIs for the pharmacokinetic parameters of gabapentin 300 mg (OD) in healthy volunteers were also within the 80.00–125.00% acceptance range when comparing the 300 mg gabapentin capsule of T with the R product [26]. The findings of current study demonstrated that geometric mean ratios (90% CI) for gabapentin's AUC0-t, AUC0-∞, and Cmax were, 0.9171 (0.8260–1.0183), 0.9191 (0.8279–1.0203), and 0.9135 (0.8324–1.0026), respectively. The observed statistical power in the current study exceeded 90%, indicating that the sample size was adequate and the results are robust and reliable. The 90% CIs for the gabapentin T/R ratios AUC0-t, AUC0-∞, and Cmax fell within the acceptable bioequivalence range. This indicates that a single oral dose of Gabantin® GRS 600 Tablet ER (T) is bioequivalent to Gralise® (Gabapentin) Tablets 600 mg (R) in subjects under fed conditions and hence can be used interchangeably.

Safety evaluations indicated that both the T and R products were well-tolerated by the study participants, with no SAEs reported thus reinforcing the suitability of the T product for clinical use.

The limitations of the study conducted at single-center design on male subjects, which may restrict the generalizability of the results and limit the ability to capture variability across more diverse populations. Although the findings provide valuable insights into the pharmacokinetic profile and safety of Gabantin® GRS 600 mg ER tablets, further confirmation through larger, multi-center studies involving more diverse populations will be important to strengthen the external validity and applicability of these findings.

Conclusions

The single-dose Gabantin® GRS 600 Tablet ER developed using patented GRIDTM technology of Sun Pharma was bioequivalent to Gralise® in healthy Asian male adults under fed conditions. Both formulations were well tolerated, with no serious adverse events reported, supporting the overall safety of the test formulation. The gastric-retentive extended-release formulation may offer pharmacokinetic advantages and improved therapeutic outcomes associated with prolonged gastric retention and sustained drug release. However, comparative adherence and efficacy studies are required to support that Gabapentin ER Tablet could help overcome the limitations of current products and support effective management for patients requiring once-daily gabapentin therapy.
