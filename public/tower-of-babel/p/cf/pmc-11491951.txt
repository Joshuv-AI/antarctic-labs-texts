Protocol for isolating CD163+ Kupffer cells from human liver resections
Authors: Armando Andres Roca Suarez, Marie-Laure Plissonnier, Maud Michelet, Anaëlle Dubois, Sarah Heintz, Maria Saez-Palma, Marion Delphin, Isabelle Bordes, Jennifer Molle, Audrey Diederichs, Mélanie Rodà, Emmanuel Combe, Léa Mougené, Guillaume Giraud, Xavier Grand, Michel Rivoire, Massimo Levrero, Barbara Testoni, Fabien Zoulim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe liver microenvironment contains a wide variety of monocyte and macrophage populations. Here, we present a protocol for the specific isolation of liver-resident macrophages, known as Kupffer cells (KCs), from human liver resections. We describe steps for dissociating human liver tissues, separating non-parenchymal cells into fractions by a 2-phase iodixanol gradient, and positive selection of KCs based on the expression of CD163. We then provide instructions for validating the procedure by immunofluorescence to detect CD163.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Roca Suarez, Plissonnier, et al.1

Before you begin

The use of single-cell technologies has provided in recent years the opportunity to appreciate the wide variety of cell types present in the human liver microenvironment.2,3,4 This heterogeneity has been particularly explored in the hepatic myeloid compartment, which in addition to the embryonically-derived liver-resident Kupffer cells (KCs), presents a whole spectrum of monocytes and monocyte-derived macrophages.5 KCs can be differentiated from other hepatic cell populations by the expression of surface protein markers such as CD163, V-set and immunoglobulin domain containing 4 (VSIG4) and folate receptor beta (FOLR2).3 In addition to their central role in immune defense, KCs are characterized by the expression of genes involved in iron and cholesterol homeostasis.5

This STAR protocol presents a method for the specific isolation of human KCs based on the expression of CD163 (Figure 1). The protocol provides detailed information related to the validation of the isolation procedure by immunofluorescence (IF) (Figure 2) and the conditions for KC culture. Step-by-step method details

Enzymatic and mechanical dissociation of the liver

Timing: 45 min

Timing: 45 min

Although this protocol focuses on the isolation of KCs (Figures 1A and 1B), primary human hepatocytes (PHH), hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) can be isolated in parallel and cultured as previously described.6,7 Thus, the first step of the method is common to all these cell types and consists on the dissociation of liver tissues in order to separate hepatocytes from the non-parenchymal cell (NPC) fraction.Note: As an example, this protocol allows to obtain 2–15 × 108 hepatocytes, a number which is dependent on several factors. These are described in the limitations section below.CRITICAL: Process the liver tissue as soon as possible (<1 h after resection). A longer interval between the surgical procedure and dissociation can lead to a decrease in cell viability.1.Tissue wash with PBS in order to eliminate blood:a.Place the liver resection on a metal tray and identify exposed vessels that could allow to perfuse a large portion of the liver tissue.b.Using a 20-mL syringe filled with PBS (pre-warmed at 37°C), perfuse the liver and select 2–3 vessels that once perfused make the tissue increase in volume.Note: Perform this step slowly in order to avoid leakage. If too much resistance is encountered, examine alternative vessels to perfuse instead.c.Eliminate as much blood as possible by performing several PBS washes. Stop once the flushed PBS is clear.d.Discard flushed blood/PBS from the metal tray.2.Tissue wash with liver dissociation buffer 1:a.Place the metal tray containing the liver resection on a pre-warmed plate previously heated to 37°C.b.Set the pump to 30 mL/min and place the tubes in a flask containing liver dissociation buffer 1 (500 mL). Seal the container with parafilm in order to limit potential contaminations.Note: It is useful to keep a second 500-mL bottle of liver dissociation buffer 1 in the water bath at 54°C, in case more than 500 mL are required.c.Place a pipette tip (without filter) at the end of each tube and start the pump in order to eliminate air bubbles from the tubes.d.Place the tubes in previously selected vessels and perform washing of the tissue. Although the water bath is set to 54°C, the buffer will decrease in temperature as it passes through the tubes and be close to 37°C once in the tissue (Figure 1C).Note: Optimal temperature of the water bath should be determined and adapted by considering the length of the pump tubes. The goal is to obtain a buffer temperature of 37°C as it enters the tissue, which will favor the collagenase action in the following step.3.Enzymatic dissociation of the liver:a.Decrease the pump speed to 20 mL/min and place the tubes inside a flask containing collagenase solution in order to perfuse the liver tissue.b.Verify the liver consistency every few minutes. Collagenase digestion takes ≈10 min.c.Stop perfusion with the collagenase stop solution once the liver tissue has a soft and elastic consistency, as compared to its condition before perfusion.Note: It is important to carefully monitor the time of collagenase digestion. Longer times can result in cell damage and short ones in inefficient tissue dissociation.4.Mechanical dissociation of the liver:a.Place the liver in a metallic bowl and cut it open with a scalpel.b.Pour collagenase stop solution (≈100 mL) and mechanically dissociate the tissue using cell scrapers.c.Pass the dissociated tissues through a funnel with a nylon mesh and collect the filtered cell suspension in a glass bottle (Figure 1D).5.Separation of hepatocytes from the NPC fraction:a.Distribute the filtered cell suspension into 50-mL tubes, fill as many as required and centrifuge at 130 g for 1 min at 20°C (acceleration 6, deceleration 2).b.Recover the supernatant containing the NPC fraction.c.Further process or discard the hepatocyte pellet (Figure 1E).6

Expected outcomes

This protocol allows to isolate 10–50 × 106 KCs from one surgical liver resection. The large majority of recovered cells are CD163+/CD68+ (Figure 2B). Cells isolated with this method can be used for a wide variety of functional studies. As an example, we have previously employed this protocol for characterizing the response of KCs to the selective TLR8 agonist SLGN.1 In this context, we have shown that SLGN treatment (150 nM, 24 h) is able to trigger KC plasticity and promote the activation of antiviral programs over those associated with the KC identity. This can be observed by the upregulation of genes that characterize monocyte populations (e.g., EREG, S100A12), the downregulation of KC markers (e.g., FOLR2, VSIG4) and KC-specific transcription factors (e.g., SPIC, ID3) (Figure 3A). Moreover, these transcriptional changes are accompanied by the production of a variety of cytokines that include tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-6 and interferon (IFN)-γ (Figure 3B).

Limitations

The number and viability of isolated KCs is dependent on several factors, including size of the liver tissue, underlying patient clinical history, time between surgical resection and start of the dissociation procedure, and efficiency of the collagenase perfusion step. To illustrate this situation, our laboratory processes liver resections that weight between 70 to 250 g. However, it has been our experience that smaller samples are often less challenging to perfuse and dissociate, which results in higher cell yields at the end of the procedure, as previously reported.10

As described in the sections above, this protocol based on the positive selection of CD163+ cells is designed for the specific isolation of KCs. If readers are interested in capturing the monocyte/macrophage diversity present in the human liver, there are protocols based on negative selection that can be employed with this aim.11
