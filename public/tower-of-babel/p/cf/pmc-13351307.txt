Timing of Carotid Blow‐Out After Locoregional Recurrence in Head and Neck Cancer: A Single‐Center Retrospective Study
Authors: Fırat Tevetoğlu, Sinem Kara Peker, Chinara Aliyeva, Sevda Yener, Haydar Murat Yener
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCarotid blow‐out syndrome (CBS) is a life‐threatening complication of head and neck cancer, often occurring after prior treatment and locoregional recurrence. Data on the timing of carotid rupture after local tissue compromise remain limited. We aimed to present our institutional experience with recurrence‐associated type III CBS, focusing on timing patterns and associated local factors.MethodsIn this retrospective single‐center study, medical records of head and neck cancer patients who developed type III CBS between 2016 and 2025 were reviewed. Among 11 CBS cases, eight occurred in association with locoregional recurrence and constituted the study cohort. Locoregional recurrence was defined by clinical and radiologic evidence of recurrent disease and was histologically confirmed when feasible. All patients presented with acute carotid rupture and underwent emergent open carotid artery ligation. Demographic, tumor‐related, treatment‐related, local predisposing factors, rupture site, and relevant time intervals were analyzed.ResultsEight patients with recurrence‐associated type III CBS were included. The mean time to locoregional recurrence after primary treatment was 8.3 ± 5.1 months (range, 6–19). Tumor infiltration was present in all patients; three had mucocutaneous fistula, five had wound infection, and six had soft‐tissue necrosis at the rupture site. The mean interval between detection of recurrence or soft‐tissue exposure and carotid rupture was 5.4 ± 3.1 months (range, 2–12). Rupture appeared to occur earlier among patients with mucocutaneous fistula and/or necrosis; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the small subgroup size. Major neurologic morbidity occurred in two patients, and seven patients died during follow‐up due to disease progression.ConclusionIn recurrent head and neck cancer with local tissue compromise, carotid rupture may occur within a short interval, particularly in the presence of necrosis or mucocutaneous fistula. These hypothesis‐generating findings highlight the need for close surveillance, timely multidisciplinary planning, and early goals‐of‐care discussions.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Carotid blow‐out syndrome (CBS) is a rare but life‐threatening complication in patients treated for head and neck cancer [1, 2]. The reported incidence ranges from approximately 2%–5% among surgically treated patients and may result from tumor invasion, radiation‐induced vessel wall damage, or progressive soft tissue compromise leading to exposure and weakening of the carotid artery [3, 4, 5, 6]. Clinically, CBS often presents with massive hemorrhage from the oral cavity, pharynx, or an open cervical wound and is associated with mortality rates up to 76% and substantial neurologic morbidity [7, 8].

CBS is commonly classified into three clinical stages: type I, or threatened CBS, defined as carotid exposure due to soft tissue breakdown without active hemorrhage; type II, or impending CBS, characterized by sentinel bleeding; and type III, or ruptured CBS, defined as active carotid hemorrhage [1, 9]. Several predisposing conditions have been described, including prior radiotherapy, radical neck dissection, mucocutaneous fistula, wound infection, flap necrosis, locoregional tumor recurrence, and tumor encasement of the carotid artery [4, 10, 11, 12].

Preventive strategies remain crucial in high‐risk patients. Reconstructive techniques, including myocutaneous flap coverage, may reduce carotid exposure and help prevent mucocutaneous fistula formation [9]. In many centers, endovascular techniques such as embolization and stent‐graft placement have become the preferred approach due to lower immediate complication rates. Nevertheless, open surgical ligation remains necessary in selected patients, particularly those presenting with active hemorrhage and infected or open cervical wounds.

Despite advances in management, CBS continues to represent a devastating event, particularly in patients with uncontrolled or recurrent disease. Most existing studies primarily address treatment strategies and outcomes; however, data regarding the timing of carotid rupture in the setting of locoregional recurrence and progressive local tissue compromise remain limited.

In particular, the interval between the development of local tissue exposure—such as necrosis, infection, or mucocutaneous fistula formation—and the occurrence of carotid rupture has not been clearly characterized. We hypothesized that, in recurrent head and neck cancer, the first few months following the development of mucocutaneous fistula, necrosis, or infection may represent a critical risk window for carotid rupture. Therefore, we present our single‐center experience with type III CBS following locoregional recurrence in head and neck cancer, with emphasis on timing and associated local factors.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Approval

The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and all procedures were performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent for treatment had been obtained from all patients or their caregivers prior to surgical intervention.

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary referral center and included previously treated patients with head and neck cancer who subsequently developed CBS. Medical records between January 2016 and December 2025 were systematically reviewed.

During the study period, 398 patients were treated for head and neck malignancies at our institution. Among these patients, 11 cases of CBS were identified. Of these, eight patients developed CBS in the setting of locoregional tumor recurrence and constituted the study cohort. Locoregional recurrence was defined as recurrent disease occurring at the primary tumor site and/or regional neck after completion of initial oncologic treatment. The diagnosis was based on clinical examination and radiologic assessment, including contrast‐enhanced computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and/or positron emission tomography/computed tomography when available, and was confirmed histopathologically by biopsy when feasible. Three cases were excluded from the present analysis to maintain a homogeneous population: two patients developed CBS secondary to radiation‐induced tissue injury without evidence of tumor recurrence, and one patient experienced early postoperative CBS following surgery for a paranasal sinus malignancy. Consequently, the final analysis was performed on eight patients with recurrence‐associated CBS.

Among the excluded patients, two had nasopharyngeal carcinoma and developed carotid blow‐out without evidence of tumor recurrence following prior radiotherapy, suggesting a radiation‐induced vascular injury. Both of these patients were managed with endovascular treatment; one patient died during follow‐up, whereas the other remains alive and is currently receiving treatment for skull base osteomyelitis without evidence of recurrent malignancy. The third excluded patient had a paranasal sinus tumor with invasion of the cavernous sinus and died within the first postoperative week following surgery due to early carotid rupture.

For the patients included in the study cohort, all had a documented history of head and neck cancer treated either with primary surgical resection followed by postoperative adjuvant therapy or with definitive radiotherapy/chemoradiotherapy followed by salvage surgery. These patients subsequently presented with active hemorrhage due to CBS and were managed by emergent open surgical ligation of the carotid artery. Open ligation was selected because patients presented with type III CBS requiring immediate hemorrhage control in the setting of hemodynamic instability and/or extensive local infection, necrosis, or open cervical wounds that made timely or definitive endovascular management unsuitable.

Data Collection

Demographic and clinical variables were obtained through detailed review of electronic medical records and operative reports. The collected data included patient age, sex, primary tumor site, nodal stage at the time of initial treatment, primary treatment modality, type of neck dissection, and history of radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy. Radiation dose information was also reviewed. For patients treated with definitive RT/CRT, the standard institutional protocol consisted of 70 Gy in 33 fractions to the primary tumor and clinically positive lymph nodes, 60 Gy to intermediate‐risk regions, and 54 Gy to the elective neck. For patients who underwent primary surgery followed by adjuvant radiotherapy, the standard adjuvant protocol consisted of 60 Gy in 30 fractions to the primary tumor bed and 54 Gy to the elective neck regions. Locoregional recurrence was defined as recurrent disease at the primary site and/or regional neck after completion of initial oncologic treatment, based on clinical examination and radiologic assessment, and confirmed histologically when biopsy was feasible. In addition, local predisposing factors potentially contributing to vascular exposure and rupture were recorded, including mucocutaneous fistula formation, wound infection, and soft tissue necrosis at the bleeding site. The anatomical origin of the rupture was also documented and categorized as involvement of the common carotid artery, internal carotid artery, or external carotid artery.

Staging of the primary tumor and nodal disease at the time of initial treatment was performed according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Cancer Staging Manual. CBS was classified according to the widely accepted system described by Chaloupka et al. [1] and Powitzky et al. [9], which categorizes CBS into three clinical stages: type I (threatened CBS), defined as exposure of the carotid artery due to soft tissue breakdown without active hemorrhage; type II (impending CBS), characterized by sentinel bleeding episodes; and type III (ruptured CBS), defined as active carotid hemorrhage. All patients included in the present study were classified as type III CBS, representing acute carotid rupture requiring emergent intervention.

Two clinically relevant temporal variables were analyzed to characterize the disease course preceding CBS. The first was time to recurrence, defined as the interval between completion of the primary oncologic treatment and detection of locoregional tumor recurrence. The second variable was time to carotid blow‐out, defined as the interval between the clinical detection of recurrence or exposure of cervical soft tissues (such as necrosis or mucocutaneous fistula formation) and the occurrence of carotid rupture.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were performed to summarize the clinical characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and range, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Due to the limited sample size and the descriptive nature of the study, no inferential statistical comparisons were performed. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS statistical software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

During the study period, a total of 11 patients with CBS were identified in our institution. Among these cases, eight patients (72.7%) developed CBS in the setting of locoregional tumor recurrence, whereas two patients (18.2%) developed CBS secondary to radiotherapy‐related tissue injury without evidence of tumor recurrence, and one patient (9.1%) experienced early postoperative CBS following surgery for a paranasal sinus malignancy invading the cavernous sinus. All patients in the study cohort presented with type III CBS and were managed by open carotid artery ligation (Figure 1). Considering the primary tumor subsites among all CBS cases (n = 11), the most frequent origins were the nasopharynx in two patients (18.2%), oral cavity in three patients (27.3%), oropharynx–hypopharynx in three patients (27.3%), larynx in two patients (18.2%), and paranasal sinus in one patient (9.1%).

For the purpose of the present study, only the eight patients with recurrence‐associated CBS were included in the subsequent analyses. All of these patients presented with type III CBS, characterized by acute carotid rupture with active hemorrhage requiring emergent surgical intervention. All patients had locoregionally recurrent head and neck cancer that was considered unresectable at the time of CBS presentation.

With regard to nodal status at the time of initial treatment, one patient (12.5%) had N1 disease, three patients (37.5%) had N2 disease, and four patients (50%) had N3 disease. Regarding primary treatment modality, three patients (37.5%) had received chemoradiotherapy as primary curative treatment, whereas five patients (62.5%) had undergone surgical resection followed by postoperative adjuvant therapy (1 CRT, 4 RT alone) (Table 1). Among those who had primary surgery, radical neck dissection was performed in three patients (60%), while functional neck dissection was performed in two patients (40%).

Tumor infiltration at the site of carotid rupture was observed in all patients. In addition, several local predisposing conditions potentially contributing to vascular exposure were identified. Mucocutaneous fistula was present in three patients, wound infection in five patients, and soft tissue necrosis in six patients at the bleeding site.

The most frequent anatomical origin of rupture was the common carotid artery near the carotid bifurcation, observed in five patients (62.5%). Rupture originating from the internal carotid artery occurred in two patients (25%), while one patient (12.5%) experienced rupture from the main trunk of the external carotid artery just above the bifurcation.

Among the eight patients with recurrence‐associated CBS, the time to locoregional recurrence following primary treatment ranged from 6 to 19 months, with a mean interval of 8.25 ± 5.11 months. The interval between detection of recurrence or soft tissue exposure and the occurrence of carotid rupture ranged from 2 to 12 months, with a mean duration of 5.37 ± 3.11 months. Notably, carotid rupture appeared to occur earlier among the six patients with mucocutaneous fistula formation and/or tissue necrosis (2–4 months, with a mean interval of 4.0 ± 1.2 months); however, this observation should be interpreted cautiously because of the very small subgroup size.

Following carotid artery ligation, two patients (25%) developed major neurologic morbidity manifested as hemiplegia. During postoperative follow‐up, seven patients (87.5%) died due to progression of recurrent head and neck cancer, with a mean time to death of 7.71 ± 6.15 months (range: 1–17 months). None of the deaths in the analyzed cohort were directly attributable to carotid blow‐out itself. Among the three excluded cases, one patient with nasopharyngeal carcinoma died following CBS related to radiation‐induced vascular injury, and one patient with a paranasal sinus malignancy died within the first postoperative week due to early carotid rupture.

Discussion

In the present study, we analyzed CBS occurring in the setting of locoregional recurrence of head and neck cancer. Our findings suggest that carotid rupture in these patients frequently develops after progressive local tissue compromise, particularly in the presence of necrosis or mucocutaneous fistula formation.

CBS, first described by Borsany in 1962, remains one of the most devastating complications encountered in patients with head and neck cancer [13]. Although relatively uncommon, CBS is associated with substantial mortality and neurologic morbidity, reported to reach approximately 40% and 60%, respectively [1, 14]. Several predisposing factors have been identified, including prior radiotherapy, radical neck dissection, mucocutaneous fistula formation, wound infection, flap necrosis, and locoregional tumor recurrence [10, 11]. Tumor encasement and direct invasion of the carotid artery may further contribute to weakening of the vessel wall and eventual rupture [4, 12].

The overall incidence of CBS in patients with head and neck cancer has been reported to range between approximately 2.5% and 4.5% [2, 4, 7]. In the present study, CBS was identified in 11 of 398 patients treated for head and neck malignancies during the study period, corresponding to an incidence of approximately 2.8%, which is consistent with the rates reported in the literature. Among the patients included in the final analysis, rupture most commonly originated from the common carotid artery near the bifurcation, accounting for the majority of cases. This finding is in agreement with previous reports indicating that the common carotid artery represents the most frequent site of rupture, often exceeding 60% of cases [4, 11, 15, 16]. However, some studies have reported the internal carotid artery as the most common site of bleeding [1, 2], highlighting the variability in the anatomical origin of CBS depending on tumor location, prior treatments, and the extent of local tissue destruction.

CBS has been classified into three clinical stages according to the system proposed by Chaloupka et al. [1] and later adopted by Powitzky et al. [9]. Type III CBS has been reported as the most frequent presentation, accounting for more than 60% of cases, followed by type II and type I, respectively [9, 11, 17]. In the present series, all patients presented with type III CBS, reflecting the acute and catastrophic nature of carotid rupture in patients with recurrent head and neck malignancies. Previous studies have reported different angiographic or intraoperative findings associated with CBS. Chaloupka et al. described pseudoaneurysm formation as the most common pathological finding in patients treated with endovascular techniques, followed by tumor encasement [1]. However, other studies have demonstrated that contrast extravasation may be the predominant finding in patients presenting with acute rupture, particularly in type III CBS [15]. Consistent with these observations, all patients in our cohort presented with acute rupture, and the predominant intraoperative finding was active arterial bleeding rather than pseudoaneurysm formation.

Previous radiotherapy is widely regarded as one of the most important predisposing factors for CBS, increasing the risk of rupture by up to seven‐fold and even up to fourteen‐fold [5, 11, 18]. Radiation‐induced vascular injury may weaken the carotid artery wall through fibrosis of the adventitia and damage to the vasa vasorum, thereby increasing the risk of rupture [18]. Consistent with these mechanisms, previous studies have reported a markedly higher incidence of CBS among irradiated patients, with rates ranging from approximately 4.5% to 21.1%, compared with 0%–2.4% in non‐irradiated cohorts [4].

Several studies have reported a high prevalence of prior irradiation among patients developing CBS, often accompanied by additional local factors such as tumor recurrence, tissue necrosis, or mucocutaneous fistula formation [2, 15]. In a large review by Powitzky et al., previous radiotherapy was identified as the most common shared feature, followed by nodal metastasis, prior neck dissection, tissue necrosis, and mucocutaneous fistula formation [9]. Neck dissection, particularly radical neck dissection involving stripping of the carotid artery, has also been reported to significantly increase the risk of CBS [11]. In our series, all patients had previously received radiotherapy as part of their oncologic treatment before the development of locoregional recurrence and CBS. Four patients underwent primary surgical treatment followed by adjuvant RT; one patient underwent primary surgical treatment followed by adjuvant CRT; and three patients received primary CRT followed by salvage surgery. In addition to tumor infiltration observed in all patients, several local factors potentially facilitating carotid exposure were present, including mucocutaneous fistula in three patients, wound infection in five patients, and soft tissue necrosis in six patients. These findings support the concept that carotid rupture in recurrent disease often occurs in the setting of multiple converging local risk factors.

The distribution of primary tumor sites among patients developing CBS varies across different studies. In a large review, the most common primary sites associated with CBS were reported to be the larynx (23%), followed by the hypopharynx (22%) and nasopharynx (21%) [11]. Similar distributions have been reported in other series evaluating patients with head and neck cancer complicated by carotid rupture [15]. In the present study, the distribution of primary tumor sites differed slightly from those reported in the literature. Considering all CBS cases identified during the study period (n = 11), the most frequent primary sites were the oral cavity (27.3%) and oropharynx–hypopharynx (27.3%), followed by the larynx (18.2%) and nasopharynx (18.2%), while one case originated from a paranasal sinus malignancy (9.1%). This variability in tumor distribution may reflect differences in institutional patient populations as well as variations in treatment strategies and referral patterns across centers.

Surgical ligation has traditionally been considered the treatment of choice for CBS [16, 19]. However, following the introduction of detachable balloon occlusion for the management of impending CBS by Osguthorpe and Hungerford in 1984, treatment strategies have gradually shifted toward endovascular approaches [20]. Over the past 15 years, more than 90% of reported CBS cases have been managed using endovascular techniques such as embolization or stent‐graft placement, whereas surgical ligation has been used in fewer than 10% of cases [11]. Several limitations associated with surgical ligation have contributed to this shift in treatment preference. These include hemodynamic instability complicating perioperative management, hypotension exacerbated by general anesthesia leading to cerebral ischemia, consumption coagulopathy caused by massive blood loss resulting in re‐hemorrhage, and technically challenging surgical dissection due to previous operations or radiotherapy [1]. Nevertheless, neurologic complications have been reported with both treatment modalities.

Regardless of the treatment modality, angiography remains the gold standard for the diagnosis of CBS [21]. In addition to confirming the source of bleeding, angiographic evaluation allows assessment of the contralateral carotid circulation and performance of balloon occlusion testing, which may guide therapeutic decision‐making [10, 22]. However, in patients presenting with massive hemorrhage and hemodynamic instability, angiographic evaluation may not always be feasible prior to emergent intervention. Although endovascular techniques such as embolization or stent‐graft placement are currently considered the preferred treatment for CBS in many centers, the clinical circumstances in our cohort limited the applicability of these approaches. All patients in the present series presented with acute type III CBS characterized by massive hemorrhage requiring immediate control of bleeding. In some cases, severe hemodynamic instability made transfer to the angiography suite impractical. In other cases, even when angiographic evaluation could be performed, the presence of extensive soft‐tissue necrosis, infection, or open cervical wounds made endovascular stent‐graft placement unsuitable because of the high risk of graft contamination and subsequent infection. Under these conditions, emergent open carotid ligation represented the most feasible and rapidly available life‐saving intervention.

The time interval between the initial cancer diagnosis and the development of CBS has been reported to vary considerably in the literature. A mean interval of approximately 2.7 years from the initial diagnosis to the occurrence of CBS has been reported, with rupture most commonly originating from the proximal segment of the carotid bifurcation [11]. In patients undergoing re‐irradiation, the median time to CBS has been reported to be approximately 7.5 months from the initiation of the second course of radiotherapy, although longer intervals of up to 4–5 years have also been described [7]. In our patient population, the time to recurrence following primary treatment ranged from 6 to 19 months, with a mean of 8.3 ± 5.1 months. The interval between the detection of recurrence or soft tissue exposure and the occurrence of CBS ranged from 2 to 12 months, with a mean duration of 5.4 ± 3.1 months. Carotid rupture appeared to occur earlier among the six patients who had mucocutaneous fistula and/or tissue necrosis, although no statistical comparison was possible because of the limited subgroup size. A notable observation in our series was the relatively short interval between local tissue compromise and carotid rupture (within 2 to 4 months, with a mean interval of 4.0 ± 1.2 months). These findings suggest, in a hypothesis‐generating manner, that progressive tissue breakdown in recurrent disease may represent a critical stage during which the carotid artery becomes particularly vulnerable.

From a clinical perspective, these observations emphasize the importance of careful surveillance in patients with advanced locoregional recurrence accompanied by tissue necrosis, infection, or mucocutaneous fistula formation. In such situations, early vascular imaging or multidisciplinary planning involving head and neck surgeons, interventional radiologists, radiation oncologists, medical oncologists, anesthesiologists, and palliative care teams may be warranted in order to anticipate and manage potential carotid complications, establish an airway strategy, and clarify goals of care before catastrophic hemorrhage occurs whenever feasible.

Given the poor overall prognosis of patients with unresectable recurrent head and neck cancer, the indication for aggressive interventions such as open carotid ligation should be carefully considered. In selected patients with advanced disease and poor performance status, palliative management strategies may be appropriate. For this reason, early goals‐of‐care discussions and palliative care involvement should be considered in high‐risk patients before the development of catastrophic hemorrhage. However, in many clinical settings, including our institutional practice, the absence of widely implemented do‐not‐resuscitate (DNR) policies or formal end‐of‐life directives may limit the applicability of purely palliative approaches in the setting of catastrophic hemorrhage, and life‐saving interventions are often undertaken in emergency situations.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the number of patients included in the analysis was relatively small, which reflects the rarity of CBS but limits the ability to perform more detailed statistical analyses. Second, the retrospective design of the study may introduce potential selection and information bias. The onset of necrosis or mucocutaneous fistula formation was based on clinical documentation in medical records and may not precisely reflect the actual onset of tissue breakdown. Therefore, the calculated interval between tissue exposure and carotid rupture may be influenced by variability in follow‐up intervals and documentation. Third, treatment strategies for CBS may vary across institutions depending on available expertise and resources, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted primarily as hypothesis‐generating observations. Nevertheless, despite these limitations, our study provides clinically relevant observations regarding the timing and clinical context of carotid rupture in patients with recurrent head and neck malignancies. In particular, the identification of a relatively short interval between soft tissue exposure and carotid blow‐out may help clinicians recognize patients at increased risk and consider closer monitoring, preventive interventions, and early goals‐of‐care planning during this critical period.

Conclusion

CBS is a rare but devastating complication in patients with head and neck cancer, particularly in those with a history of radiotherapy and radical neck dissection. It represents a life‐threatening emergency in which angiography plays a central role both in diagnosis and treatment planning, although surgical ligation may still be required in selected cases. Given the high mortality and neurologic morbidity associated with CBS, prevention, early recognition, airway planning, and early goals‐of‐care discussions are of critical importance. In this retrospective cohort, our findings suggest in a hypothesis‐generating manner that patients with locoregional recurrence accompanied by soft tissue exposure, necrosis, infection, or mucocutaneous fistula formation may experience carotid rupture within the first few months after local tissue compromise, highlighting the need for close clinical surveillance during this stage.
