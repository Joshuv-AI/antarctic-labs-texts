Regional distribution characteristics of eyelid Demodex in patients with squamous blepharitis
Authors: Huiming Wen, Xiulan Li, Hongjuan Wang, Jingru Wang, Ling Zhang, Xiaoping Hong, Yingying Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the regional distribution characteristics of different Demodex species in eyelash follicles of patients with squamous blepharitis, and to explore the correlation between Demodex distribution and ocular surface parameters.MethodsPatients with squamous blepharitis who visited the ophthalmology outpatient clinic from December 2024 to May 2025 were enrolled in this cross-sectional study. Patients’ information, Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) scores, and meibomian gland (MG) dropout were recorded. Two eyelashes were collected from the inner row (conjunctival side) and outer row (cutaneous side) at the central part of each eyelid, respectively. Demodex folliculorum (DF) and Demodex brevis (DB) were identified according to their typical morphological features under a biological microscope. Ova, larvae, protonymphs and deutonymphs of DF and DB were collectively designated as pre-adult-stage (PAS) mites. Data from the patients’ right eyes were selected for statistical analysis to explore the distribution characteristics of Demodex mites and analyze the correlations between Demodex infestation and OSDI scores as well as MG dropout. SPSS 22.0 software was used for all statistical analyses of the data.ResultsA total of 100 patients with squamous blepharitis were enrolled in this study. The overall Demodex detection rate was 92% (92/100) in this study. DF had the highest detection rate (92%) with an average count of 2.78 ± 2.46 mites per eyelid, followed by PAS (detection rate: 42%, average count: 0.8 ± 1.63 mites per eyelid), and DB had the lowest detection rate (27%) with an average count of 0.26 ± 0.64 mites per eyelid (all P < 0.05). The counts of DF and PAS in the upper eyelids (3.72 and 1.34, respectively) were significantly higher than those in the lower eyelids (1.84 and 0.26, respectively) (both P < 0.001). The counts of DF, DB and PAS in the inner row (1.66, 0.2 and 0.67, respectively) were significantly higher than those in the outer row (1.13, 0.06 and 0.13, respectively) (P = 0.001, P = 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively). Both MG dropout score and OSDI score were positively correlated with Demodex count (all P < 0.05). The MG dropout scores in the DB-positive group (upper eyelid: 2.15 ± 0.80, lower eyelid: 1.55 ± 1.00) were significantly higher than those in the DB-negative group (upper eyelid: 1.48 ± 0.76, lower eyelid: 0.99 ± 0.46) (P = 0.005 and P = 0.007, respectively).ConclusionEyelid Demodex in patients with squamous blepharitis exhibits distinct regional distribution characteristics, with the inner row of upper eyelashes being the primary infestation site. Introduction

Demodex mites, particularly Demodex folliculorum (DF) and Demodex brevis (DB), are the most common ectoparasites that colonize human hair follicles and sebaceous glands. Accumulating clinical and basic studies have demonstrated a close and causal association between Demodex infestation and a series of ocular surface diseases, including blepharitis, meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) and dry eye disease [1–6].

However, the detailed regional distribution pattern of Demodex mites in eyelashes of patients with squamous blepharitis remains incompletely understood. Biernat et al. selected 5 eyelashes from each eyelid for Demodex detection, focusing on eyelash root cylindrical dandruff, scaling and crusting but ignoring the sampling position of eyelashes [7]. Barnett et al. and Lee et al. collected eyelashes from the medial and lateral half of each lid, without investigating the proximity of eyelashes to the conjunctiva [5, 8]. Ding et al. selected 3 eyelashes from the medial, middle and lateral parts of each eyelid to quantify Demodex infestation and observed eyelash root morphology, but also failed to clarify the distribution difference between the conjunctival side and cutaneous side of eyelashes [9]. Furthermore, the interrelationships between different Demodex species (adults and pre-adult stages) in the eyelid and their respective correlations with ocular surface parameters have not been systematically reported. As far as we know, these existing studies have insufficiently explored the distribution patterns of Demodex mites in eyelashes at different layers of the eyelid and lack a systematic analysis of the correlations between ocular surface parameters and Demodex distribution. This neglect of relevant research has, to a certain extent, compromised the accuracy of microscopic examination and the targeting of clinical diagnosis and treatment. Delayed diagnosis and inadequate treatment may lead to persistent and aggravated symptoms, increasing patients’ ocular discomfort and the consumption of medical resources. Therefore, clarifying the distribution patterns of Demodex mites is of great clinical significance for the diagnosis and treatment of related ocular diseases.

The correlation between Demodex mites and ocular surface parameters has been preliminarily explored in several studies. Yu et al. proposed that Demodex infestation leads to uneven atrophy of meibomian glands in MGD patients [10]. Pan et al. found a significant positive correlation between Demodex count and OSDI scores in MGD patients [11]. However, key scientific questions remain unanswered, including which Demodex species exerts the most significant impact on the ocular surface and whether DB or PAS mites have a profound effect on meibomian gland function. These knowledge gaps hinder ophthalmologists’ in-depth understanding of Demodex-related ocular surface diseases and impede the development of precise treatment regimens.

Therefore, this study aimed to analyze the infestation status of different Demodex (DF, DB and PAS) in the eyelids of patients with squamous blepharitis, investigate the regional distribution characteristics of Demodex mites in the inner/outer rows and upper/lower eyelids, and explore the correlation between different Demodex species and ocular surface parameters (OSDI score, meibomian gland dropout). The findings are expected to provide a theoretical and practical basis for the research of Demodex-related ocular diseases and the improvement of the success rate of Demodex detection via eyelash microscopy.

Materials and methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University from December 2024 to May 2025. Patients with squamous blepharitis who visited the ophthalmology outpatient clinic during this 6-month period were enrolled in the study. All patients were fully informed of the examination contents, potential risks and study purposes, and signed a written informed consent form. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (ethical approval number: to be supplemented).

Inclusion criteria

(1) Patients with chronic ocular symptoms lasting more than 1 month, including ocular pruritus, foreign body sensation, burning sensation and recurrent eyelash loss; (2) Ocular signs including scaling at the eyelash roots, eyelid margin hyperemia and mild trichiasis.

Exclusion criteria

(1) A history of ocular surgery within the previous 3 weeks; (2) Pregnant or lactating women, and uncooperative children; (3) Patients clinically diagnosed with active fungal, bacterial or viral keratoconjunctivitis; (4) Patients with acute dacryocystitis, acute angle-closure glaucoma attack or other acute ocular diseases; (5) Patients with a history of anti-Demodex treatment for the eyes, or those who used topical glucocorticoids, antibiotics or other drugs that may affect Demodex distribution in the eyes within the previous 3 months; (6) Use of eye cosmetics (mascara, eyeliner, eyeshadow, etc.) within 24 h before examination; (7) Patients with severe systemic diseases, including autoimmune diseases and malignant tumors; (8) Any other conditions deemed unsuitable for participation by the researchers.

Data collection and ocular examinations

Eligible patients were screened by two experienced ophthalmologists based on chief complaints, clinical symptoms and eyelid margin signs. Basic patient information including name, gender, age, chief complaint, past medical history, personal history and OSDI score was recorded in a unified case report form. All subjects underwent a series of standardized ocular examinations, including eyelash microscopy, meibomian gland function analysis and non-contact infrared meibography. All examinations were performed in a constant temperature and humidity clinic with a room temperature of 18–25 °C and relative humidity of 45%–65%.

The OSDI score was used to evaluate the severity of ocular surface symptoms, which includes 12 questions related to ocular discomfort, visual function and daily activities. The total OSDI score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating more severe ocular surface symptoms.

Meibomian gland (MG) dropout was assessed using the Oculus Keratograph 5 M Ocular Surface Analyzer (Oculus GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). The grading standard for MG dropout severity was referenced from the study of Arita et al.: Grade 0, no meibomian gland loss; Grade 1, area loss less than one-third of the total meibomian gland area; Grade 2, area loss between one-third and two-thirds of the total meibomian gland area; Grade 3, area loss more than two-thirds of the total meibomian gland area [12]. Two experienced ophthalmologists who were blinded to the patient’s clinical information independently completed the meibomian gland dropout scoring.

Eyelash microscopy and demodex counting method

Demodex microscopic examination was performed by an independent ophthalmologist who was completely blinded to the patients’ clinical information and examination results. The eyelid margin and eyelash morphology were first examined under a 16× slit-lamp microscope (Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan; Model: SL-D7). Referring to the standardized eyelash microscopy method of Gao et al., eyelashes with attached scales and secretions were selected as the research samples under the slit-lamp microscope [13].

Two eyelashes were collected from the inner row (the row closest to the palpebral conjunctiva, red arrows in Fig. 1) and outer row (the row closest to the facial skin, black arrows in Fig. 1) at the central part of each eyelid (upper and lower), with a total of 8 eyelashes collected per eye (4 from the upper eyelid and 4 from the lower eyelid) and 16 eyelashes from both eyes. The central part of eyelid was defined as the middle 1/3 segment obtained by equally dividing the eyelid into three parts from the medial canthus to the lateral canthus. All collected eyelashes were immediately placed in a glass slide containing cedar wood oil, covered with a coverslip, and observed under a biological microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan; Model: IX53) at 10 × 10 magnification.

The two species of Demodex mites were identified based on their typical morphological features and motility under a biological microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan; Model: IX53) [14]. For statistical convenience, adult DF mites were designated as DF (Fig. 2A), adult DB mites as DB (Fig. 2B), and all developmental stages including ova, larvae, protonymphs and deutonymphs of DF and DB were collectively designated as pre-adult-stage (PAS) mites (Fig. 2C). The presence of one or more Demodex mites (DF, DB or PAS) in either eyelid was defined as Demodex-positive for that species.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, USA). GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA) was used for statistical plotting and chart generation.

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used for normality testing of measurement data. Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), and the independent-samples t-test or paired t-test was used for comparison between groups. Non-normally distributed measurement data used Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test for comparison between groups. Count data used the chi-square test for comparison between groups.

Spearman correlation analysis was used to explore the correlation between Demodex count and ocular surface parameters (OSDI score, meibomian gland dropout score). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

A total of 100 subjects with squamous blepharitis were included in this study, with a mean age of 47.35 ± 18.12 years (range: 10–90 years), including 51 males (51%) and 49 females (49%). The mean count of DF, DB and PAS per eyelid was 2.78 ± 2.46, 0.26 ± 0.64 and 0.8 ± 1.63 mites, respectively.

Differences in detection of different demodex species

In the right eyes of the 100 patients with squamous blepharitis, the mean counts of DF, DB and PAS were 5.56 ± 3.92, 0.51 ± 0.99 and 1.6 ± 2.63, respectively. The count comparison showed a significant order of DF > PAS > DB, with statistically significant differences between each group (P < 0.001 and P = 0.003).The detection rates of DF, DB and PAS in the right eye were 92% (92/100), 27% (27/100) and 42% (42/100), respectively, also following the significant order of DF > PAS > DB (P < 0.001 and P = 0.026).

Differences in demodex detection in different ocular regions

Upper eyelid vs. lower eyelid

The mean count of Demodex mites in the upper eyelid was 5.29 ± 4.11, with a detection rate of 92%; the mean count in the lower eyelid was 2.38 ± 2.64, with a detection rate of 76%. Both the count and detection rate of Demodex mites in the upper eyelid were significantly higher than those in the lower eyelid (P = 0.002 and P < 0.001, respectively).

For different Demodex species, the counts of DF (3.72 ± 2.51) and PAS (1.34 ± 2.02) in the upper eyelid were significantly higher than those in the lower eyelid (1.84 ± 2.01 and 0.26 ± 0.82, respectively) (both P < 0.001). However, there was no statistically significant difference in DB count between the upper and lower eyelids (0.23 ± 0.65 vs. 0.28 ± 0.64, P = 0.239).

Inner row vs. outer row of eyelashes

The mean count of Demodex mites in the inner row of eyelashes was significantly higher than that in the outer row in both upper and lower eyelids (upper eyelid: 3.46 ± 3.09 vs. 1.83 ± 1.99, P < 0.001; lower eyelid: 1.58 ± 1.78 vs. 0.8 ± 1.08, P < 0.001).

In terms of detection rate, only the inner row of the lower eyelid had a significantly higher Demodex detection rate than the outer row (70% vs. 46%, P = 0.001), with no significant difference in the upper eyelid (76% vs. 70%, P = 0.339).

For all three Demodex groups, the counts of DF, DB and PAS in the inner row of eyelashes (1.66 ± 1.64, 0.2 ± 0.51 and 0.67 ± 1.33, respectively) were significantly higher than those in the outer row (1.13 ± 1.39, 0.06 ± 0.28 and 0.13 ± 0.44, respectively) (P = 0.001, P = 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 3).

Correlations among DF, DB and PAS

Further subgroup analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between DB infestation and PAS infestation in squamous blepharitis patients.

The PAS count in the DB-positive group (upper eyelid: 3.54 ± 2.70, lower eyelid: 0.50 ± 0.83) was significantly higher than that in the DB-negative group (upper eyelid: 1.01 ± 1.68, lower eyelid: 0.20 ± 0.82) (P < 0.001 and P = 0.018, respectively).In addition, the DB count in the PAS-positive group (upper eyelid: 0.50 ± 0.93, lower eyelid: 0.57 ± 0.76) was also significantly higher than that in the PAS-negative group (upper eyelid: 0.05 ± 0.22, lower eyelid: 0.23 ± 0.61) (P = 0.002 and P = 0.023, respectively, Table 1).

Correlation analysis between demodex count and ocular surface parameters

Spearman correlation analysis showed that the meibomian gland dropout score was significantly positively correlated with the total Demodex count, DF count, DB count and PAS count (all P < 0.001).

The mean OSDI score of all subjects was 32.52 ± 9.6, and the OSDI score was also significantly positively correlated with the total Demodex count, DF count, DB count and PAS count (all P < 0.05). (Supplementary Table 1)

Subgroup comparison showed the MG dropout scores in the DB-positive group (upper eyelid: 2.15 ± 0.80, lower eyelid: 1.55 ± 1.00) were significantly higher than those in the DB-negative group (upper eyelid: 1.48 ± 0.76, lower eyelid: 0.99 ± 0.46) (P = 0.005 and P = 0.007, respectively, Table 2). Discussion

This study systematically investigated the regional distribution characteristics of different Demodex (DF, DB, PAS) in the eyelids of patients with squamous blepharitis and their correlations with ocular surface parameters, and obtained three key findings: first, the overall Demodex detection rate was as high as 92% in squamous blepharitis patients, with DF having the highest detection rate and count; second, Demodex mites exhibit distinct regional distribution characteristics, with significantly higher infestation in the upper eyelid than the lower eyelid and in the inner row than the outer row of eyelashes; third, Demodex count is significantly positively correlated with OSDI score and MG dropout score, and DB is an important risk factor for MG dropout.

The overall Demodex detection rate of 92% in this study is higher than the previously reported range of 62.4%~75.5% in blepharitis patients [5, 7, 9]. This significant difference is mainly due to two reasons: first, this study adopted strict case inclusion criteria, only enrolling patients with typical clinical symptoms and signs of squamous blepharitis, which ensured the homogeneity of the study population; second, this study optimized the sampling method, collecting eyelashes from both the inner row (conjunctival side) and outer row (cutaneous side) of the eyelid, which made up for the deficiency of previous studies that ignored the distribution differences of eyelashes in different positions, thus reducing missed diagnoses caused by inappropriate sampling sites and improving the detection rate of Demodex mites. This finding further verifies the close and causal association between Demodex infestation and squamous blepharitis, and provides a theoretical basis for Demodex detection as a routine examination for squamous blepharitis.

In the present study, the detection rate (92%) and count (2.78 ± 2.46 per lid) of DF were significantly higher than those of DB and PAS, which was consistent with previous findings [15]. The primary reason is that eyelash microscopy was adopted in this study. DF mainly colonizes the eyelash follicles, where male and female adults mate at the follicle ostium, with a peak emergence time of 10:00–18:00, making it more easily detected in eyelash samples. In contrast, DB primarily resides deeply in meibomian glands and sebaceous glands, with a peak emergence time of 22:00–02:00 [16], leading to a lower detection rate and count in eyelash microscopy. PAS collectively refers to eggs, larvae, protonymphs, and nymphs of DF and DB, and its distribution is highly correlated with that of adult mites; thus, its count falls between those of DF and DB.

The regional distribution characteristics of Demodex mites (upper eyelid > lower eyelid, inner row > outer row) found in this study are closely related to the living habits of Demodex mites and the anatomical structure of the human eye. For the difference between upper and lower eyelids, literature reports that the upper eyelid has more eyelashes (90–160 per eyelid) and more rows (5–6 rows) than the lower eyelid (75–80 per eyelid, 3–4 rows) [17]. The denser eyelashes can form a more stable and humid microenvironment, and provide more abundant sebum and keratin nutrients, which are highly consistent with the survival and reproduction needs of Demodex mites, thus making the upper eyelid a more suitable habitat for DF and PAS. In contrast, DB mainly inhabits meibomian glands and sebaceous glands rather than eyelash follicles, and the DB detected in eyelashes may be the mites that escaped from the meibomian glands to the eyelash surface, thus there is no significant difference in DB count between the upper and lower eyelids.

For the difference between the inner and outer rows of eyelashes, the key influencing factor is the humidity of the microenvironment. In vitro experiments have shown that Demodex mites adapt to a high-humidity environment (96% relative humidity), and the inner row of eyelashes is close to the palpebral conjunctiva, where the humidity is significantly higher than that of the outer row (cutaneous side) due to the tear film and conjunctival secretions [14]. This high-humidity microenvironment is more conducive to the habitation and reproduction of Demodex mites, thus resulting in significantly higher infestation in the inner row. This finding has important clinical practical significance—it suggests that clinical ophthalmologists should prioritize the collection of eyelashes from the inner row of the upper eyelid for Demodex microscopy, which can significantly improve the detection rate and reduce missed diagnoses, and provide a standardized sampling method for clinical Demodex detection.

Another important finding of this study is the significant positive correlation between DB infestation and PAS infestation, especially in the upper eyelid (R²=0.195, P < 0.001). Combined with the life cycle of Demodex mites and the anatomical characteristics of the eyelid, we speculate that the core reason for this correlation is that the upper eyelid has more rows and a larger number of eyelashes, and some eyelashes are located deeper and closer to the meibomian glands. DB mainly inhabits the acinar and ductal system of meibomian glands, and PAS (eggs, larvae, protonymphs and nymphs) as the pre-adult developmental stages of DF and DB can develop around the meibomian glands and in the adjacent eyelash follicles [18]. Therefore, both DB and PAS can be detected in the deep eyelash samples close to the meibomian glands, forming a synergistic infestation phenomenon. This finding fills the research gap in the interrelationship between different Demodex species, and suggests that ophthalmologists should pay more attention to the detection of PAS when DB is found in clinical practice, so as to avoid missed diagnosis of pre-adult-stage mites.

This study confirmed that Demodex count is significantly positively correlated with OSDI score and MG dropout score, and DB is an important risk factor for MG dropout in squamous blepharitis patients. This result is consistent with the research conclusion of previous study, which reported that the MG dropout rate in MGD patients was significantly higher in the upper eyelid than in the lower eyelid and was positively correlated with DB count [19]. Demodex mites can damage the ocular surface and meibomian gland function through three main pathways: first, mechanical stimulation—DF crawls and scrapes in the eyelash follicles, causing epithelial hyperplasia and hyperkeratosis, while DB blocks the meibomian gland orifices and ducts, leading to meibum excretion disorder; second, immune-inflammatory response—the metabolic products and post-mortem lytic products of Demodex mites act as foreign antigens, inducing local immune-inflammatory responses and releasing a variety of inflammatory factors, which aggravate eyelid margin inflammation and meibomian gland damage; third, microecological imbalance—Demodex infestation breaks the normal flora balance of the eyelid margin, promotes the overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens, and further aggravates ocular surface inflammation, forming a vicious circle of “mite infestation—inflammatory response—meibomian gland damage—aggravated ocular surface symptoms” [2].

Notably, although DB had the lowest detection rate and count in this study, it still exerted a significant impact on MG dropout and OSDI score. The main reason is that DB mainly inhabits the interior of meibomian glands, which can directly phagocytose meibum, destroy the acinar structure of meibomian glands and cause irreversible damage to meibomian gland function [19, 20]. In addition, the deep habitation location of DB makes it difficult for local acaricidal drugs to reach the lesion site and completely eliminate the mites, thus resulting in stronger and more persistent pathogenicity. This finding suggests that clinical practice should attach great importance to the diagnosis and treatment of DB infestation, and develop targeted treatment regimens for DB, such as increasing the concentration of acaricidal drugs or extending the treatment course, so as to improve the clinical treatment effect.

Study limitations

This study has certain limitations that need to be acknowledged. First, this study is a single-center cross-sectional study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Multi-center, large-sample and prospective cohort studies are needed in the future to further verify the conclusions. Future studies may also expand the sample size to further explore the relationship between different larval and pre-adult stages of Demodex and ocular surface parameters. Second, this study mainly used the eyelash microscopy method to detect Demodex mites, and failed to directly observe the infestation status of DB in meibomian glands due to equipment limitations. In vivo laser scanning confocal microscopy can be combined in future studies to directly observe the number and distribution of DB in meibomian glands, so as to obtain more accurate research results. Third, this study only included data from the right eye of patients and did not conduct a comparative analysis of binocular data; future studies can collect binocular data to explore the symmetry of Demodex infestation in both eyes.

Conclusion

In summary, this cross-sectional study demonstrated that Demodex mites in the eyelids of patients with squamous blepharitis exhibit distinct regional distribution characteristics, with the inner row of upper eyelashes being the primary infestation site. The total Demodex count, as well as the counts of DF, DB and PAS, are all significantly positively correlated with OSDI score and MG dropout score. DB is an important independent risk factor for MG dropout in squamous blepharitis patients. These findings provide a theoretical and practical basis for the clinical detection and targeted treatment of Demodex-related squamous blepharitis, and suggest that prioritizing the collection of eyelashes from the inner row of the upper eyelid can significantly improve the detection rate of Demodex mites.
