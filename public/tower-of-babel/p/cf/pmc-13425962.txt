Effectiveness of 33 °C targeted temperature management in patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest after resuscitation: a retrospective study
Authors: Zhenyu Shan, Ziqi Zhong, Jingfei Yu, Guanghao Yan, Xingsheng Wang, Rui Shao, Chenchen Hang, Fei Shao, Le An, Ziren Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundControversy exists regarding the selection of the target temperature for patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). This study aims to identify the subgroups of patients who can benefit from targeted temperature management (TTM) at 33 °C.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 282 OHCA patients, who were categorized based on the administration of 33 °C TTM and early mortality within 24 h of ICU admission. The median value was used to determine the cutoff for continuous variables, and patients were further divided into subgroups according to different variables; the outcomes were survival status and neurological function at discharge. Difference analysis, multivariate logistic regression (MLR), and propensity score matching (PSM) were applied to analyze the efficacy of different TTM regimens in each subgroup.ResultsSubgroup analysis showed that 33 °C TTM was associated with better survival only in patients without bystander CPR (OR [95%CI]: 3.41 [1.13, 10.31], P = 0.029), whereas this association disappeared after MLR and PSM adjustment. Among patients with an APACHE II score < 30, the Non-33 °C TTM group presented a more favorable neurological prognosis (χ²: OR [95%CI]: 0.34 [0.17, 0.70], P = 0.003; MLR: 0.29 [0.08, 0.96], P = 0.046). 33 °C TTM stable group was related to superior survival only in the APACHE II > 30 subgroup in MLR analysis (OR [95%CI]: 3.47 [1.09, 12.32], P = 0.042). After MLR and PSM adjustment, favorable neurological outcomes remained significantly higher in the Non-33 °C TTM stable group among patients with cardiac etiology (MLR: OR [95%CI]: 0.25 [0.06, 0.94], P = 0.046; PSM: 0.08 [0.01, 0.51], P = 0.017) and those with APACHE II < 30 (MLR: OR [95%CI]: 0.27 [0.07, 0.88], P = 0.034; PSM: 0.19 [0.04, 0.80], P = 0.029).Conclusions33 °C and Non-33 °C TTM strategies were associated with clinical outcomes in specific subgroups of OHCA patients, and further studies are warranted to clarify this association.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03950-5.Keywords: Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, Targeted temperature management, Subgroup, Severity

Introduction

The global annual incidence of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) ranges from 30 to 97 cases per 100,000 people, with only 2.8% to 18.2% of these patients achieving a favorable neurological prognosis [1]; in China, fewer than 1% of OHCA patients have good neurological function at discharge [2]. Post-cardiac arrest syndrome (PCAS) is a major factor affecting the mortality and morbidity of these patients, among which brain injury accounts for approximately 68% of deaths in OHCA patients [3]. This critical situation underscores the urgent need to optimize cerebral protection strategies following PCAS.

Targeted temperature management (TTM) is a key therapeutic strategy for mitigating post-OHCA brain injury [4]; however, the publication of multiple high-quality, multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [5, 6] has raised questions regarding the selection of specific target temperatures. Specifically, the TTM1 trial [5] demonstrated no significant difference in 180-day neurological prognosis between comatose OHCA survivors with 33 °C TTM and those with 36 °C, while the TTM2 trial [6] showed that 33 °C TTM did not result in a lower 6-month mortality rate compared to target normothermia. Consequently, the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) in 2025 recommended actively preventing fever with a target temperature of ≤ 37.5 °C for 36 to 72 h [7].

The HYPERION trial [8] demonstrated that among comatose patients with cardiac arrest (CA) due to non-shockable rhythms, 33 °C TTM resulted in a higher proportion of patients achieving a favorable neurological prognosis at 90 days compared to target normothermia. This RCT suggested that 33 °C TTM may benefit specific patient subgroups, which has also been confirmed by multiple large-scale retrospective studies [9–11]. Additionally, based on multiple large retrospective studies, Kikutani et al. proposed that the efficacy of TTM is influenced by the severity of illness [12]; specifically, 33 °C TTM benefitted patients with PCAS of moderate severity, as indicated by the revised post-Cardiac Arrest Syndrome for induced Therapeutic hypothermia (rCAST) score [13, 14], while 36℃ TTM benefitted patients with the Pittsburgh Cardiac Arrest Category (PCAC) [15] of grade 2 and 3 [16]. Despite these insights, current guidelines still state that it remains uncertain whether subgroups of cardiac arrest patients can benefit from targeted hypothermia therapy with a temperature range of 32 °C to 34 °C [17].

Therefore, through a retrospective analysis, this study aimed to determine the correlation between 33 °C TTM and prognosis in OHCA patients across different clinical characteristic subgroups, and to analyze the efficacy of 33 °C TTM in PCAS of varying severity, thereby providing a basis for the clinical application of TTM.

Methods

Study population

This study retrospectively analyzed 401 consecutive patients with CA admitted to the emergency intensive care unit (EICU) between August 1, 2016, and February 1, 2025, with the following exclusion criteria: in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA), traumatic brain injury (including subarachnoid hemorrhage, cerebral hemorrhage, etc.), pregnant patients, age < 18 years, implementation of extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR), no return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), and lack of pre-hospital data; according to these criteria, 282 patients with OHCA were enrolled in the study (Fig. 1). Post-CA treatment and care were guided by the American Heart Association guidelines, implemented in line with the treatment recommendations of the ILCOR and practical clinical considerations, and updated following revisions to the guidelines and treatment recommendations. For patients undergoing TTM, a feedback-controlled surface cooling device (Arctic Sun® 5000, BD, USA) was used to maintain the target temperature (33–36 °C), while active measures were taken to prevent fever (body temperature ≤ 37.5 °C) in patients not receiving TTM. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chaoyang Hospital (No: 2024-Science-78-1) and was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines and regulations.

Data collection and definitions

Data was collected retrospectively using the “Utstein Style“ [18]. The data included the following variables: age, gender, etiology of CA, witnessed status, bystander CPR, initial rhythm, time from CA to ROSC, vital signs, initial laboratory tests, as well as APACHE II Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score at admission. The etiology was considered cardiac unless it was clearly known or highly likely to be caused by trauma, drowning, drug overdose, asphyxia, or any other non-cardiac causes. Patients were grouped according to whether 33 °C TTM was initiated. The criterion for initiating 33 °C TTM was that patients underwent immediate initiation of 33 °C TTM upon admission to the EICU and achieved the target temperature. Based on this criterion, patients were divided into the 33 °C TTM group (n = 176) and the Non-33 °C TTM group (n = 106). In addition, considering the impact of early hemodynamic instability in patients with OHCA on the study results, we excluded patients who died during hypothermia treatment and within 24 h of ICU admission. Patients were then reclassified into the 33 °C TTM stable group (n = 165) and the Non-33 °C TTM stable group (n = 164) (Fig. 1).

Outcomes

The outcomes were survival status and neurological function at discharge, which were evaluated using the Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) scale (CPC 1: good cerebral performance; CPC 2: moderate cerebral disability; CPC 3: severe cerebral disability; CPC 4: coma or vegetative state; CPC 5: death/brain death). CPC 1–2 indicated a favorable neurological prognosis, while CPC 3–5 indicated a poor neurological prognosis.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages (%). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, with P > 0.05 considered indicative of approximate normality. Normally distributed data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were summarized as the median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test if normally distributed, or the Mann-Whitney U test otherwise.

In this study, participants were stratified into subgroups based on variable types. Categorical variables were used to directly define subgroup categories, while continuous variables were dichotomized by their median values for subgroup division.

In analyzing the correlation between TTM and prognosis, we conducted multivariate logistic regression (MLR) analyses for different subgroups, with adjusted variables including age, gender, bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), etiology, initial rhythm, pupillary light reflex (PLR), time from CA to ROSC (TROSC), mean arterial pressure (MAP), hemoglobin (HGB), and lactate. Additionally, this study used propensity score matching (PSM) to control for inter-group confounding bias; specifically, an MLR model was employed to calculate the propensity score for each patient, with the same set of covariates included and adjusted according to different subgroups. The nearest neighbor matching method was adopted with a matching ratio of 1:1, and a caliper of 0.25 standard deviations of the propensity score was set to limit the difference in propensity scores between matched pairs and reduce bias caused by over-matching. After matching, the balance of these covariates was evaluated by comparing the inter-group standardized mean difference (SMD), and the matched samples were finally included for subsequent statistical analysis, with an SMD < 0.15 considered acceptable in this study. Table S1 presents the SMD values of each covariate before and after matching. The results showed that most SMD values were less than 0.15 after matching, indicating satisfactory between-group balance. After matching, the enrolled samples were treated as an independent cohort and analyzed using MLR to further adjust for potential residual confounding effects.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1) and RStudio (version 2015.05.01); all tests were two-tailed, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 282 patients were enrolled in this study (Fig. 1). The median age was 64 (50-73.75) years old, with 62.8% being male. Bystander CPR was performed in 70.9% of the patients, and 55.0% had a cardiac etiology. The median TROSC was 26 (15-49.75) mins. The median APACHE Ⅱ score was 27 (24–32), and the SOFA score was 11 (8–14). 176 patients (62.4%) received 33 °C TTM. Overall, 131 patients (46.5%) survived, and 58 patients (20.6%) had a favorable neurological outcome at discharge. After excluding 32 patients who died during TTM and within 24 h after ICU admission, 126 patients (50.4%) ultimately survived to hospital discharge (Tables 1 and 2).

Survivors and those with CPC 1–2 were younger, shorter TROSC, higher HGB, lower lactate, lower APACHE Ⅱ, and lower SOFA, with higher rates of bystander CPR, cardiac etiology, shockable rhythm, and sensitive PLR (Table 1, all P < 0.05). Patients who initiated 33 °C TTM were younger, with a higher proportion of males, fewer with sensitive PLR, longer TROSC, and higher HGB (Table 2, all P < 0.05). No significant differences in prognosis were observed between the 33 °C TTM group and the Non-33 °C TTM group (survival: 82 [46.6%] vs. 49 [46.2%], P = 0.958; CPC 1–2: 31 [17.6%] vs. 27 [25.5%], P = 0.114). After excluding 32 patients, the Non-33 °C TTM stable group had a significantly higher proportion of patients with a favorable neurological outcome (CPC 1–2: 31 [18.8%] vs. 27 [31.8%], P = 0.021).

33℃ TTM group vs. Non-33℃ TTM group

In the non-bystander CPR subgroup, patients who received 33 °C TTM showed a higher survival rate compared with those who did not initiate 33 °C TTM (OR [95%CI]: 3.41 [1.13, 10.31], P = 0.029) (Fig. 2 and Table S2). However, this association disappeared after MLR and PSM (Fig. 2 and Table S2, all P > 0.05). When the CPC was used as the outcome measure, patients in the Non-33 °C TTM group had a significantly higher probability of CPC 1–2 in the following subgroups: age ≥ 65 years (OR [95%CI]: 0.30 [0.10, 0.88], P = 0.027), cardiac etiology (0.35 [0.17, 0.71], P = 0.003), MAP ≥ 86 mmHg (0.28 [0.13, 0.62], P = 0.001), lactate < 8.6 mmol/L (0.40 [0.20, 0.83], P = 0.013), and APACHE II < 30 (0.34 [0.17, 0.70], P = 0.003) (Fig. 2 and Table S2). In the MLR model, only the subgroup with an APACHE II score < 30 showed a higher probability of achieving CPC 1–2 outcomes in the Non-33 °C TTM group (OR [95%CI]: 0.29 [0.08, 0.96], P = 0.046) (Fig. 2 and Table S2). However, no significant association was observed after PSM (all P > 0.05) (Fig. 2 and Table S2).

33℃ TTM stable group vs. Non-33℃ TTM stable group

After excluding patients who died during hypothermia and within 24 h of ICU admission, univariate analysis showed no significant correlation between 33 °C TTM and survival status, and this finding remained consistent across all subgroups. In the MLR model, however, within the subgroup with an APACHE II score > 30, the 33 °C TTM stable group exhibited a better survival outcome at hospital discharge compared with the non-33 °C TTM stable group (OR [95%CI]: 3.47 [1.09, 12.32], P = 0.042). No significant association was observed after PSM (OR [95%CI]: 2.67 [0.07, 10.92], P = 0.152) (Fig. 3 and Table S3). When the CPC score was used as the outcome endpoint, the Non-33 °C TTM stable group had a significantly higher proportion of patients with CPC 1–2 in the following subgroups: age > 65 years (OR [95%CI]: 0.25 [0.08, 0.76], P = 0.012), female (0.37 [1.07, 4.96], P = 0.031), bystander CPR (0.51 [0.27, 0.98], P = 0.043), cardiac etiology (0.24 [0.11, 0.51], P < 0.001), shockable rhythm (0.36 [0.15, 0.89], P = 0.024), sensitive PLR (0.38 [0.15, 0.98], P = 0.043), TROSC < 27 min (0.45 [0.21, 0.93], P = 0.031), MAP ≥ 86 mmHg (0.21 [0.11, 0.54], P < 0.001), HGB ≥ 122 g/L (0.40 [0.17, 0.92], P = 0.029), lactate < 8.6 mmol/L (0.37 [0.18, 0.76], P = 0.006), APACHE II score < 30 (0.30 [0.14, 0.62], P < 0.001), and SOFA score < 12 (0.42 [0.21, 0.88], P = 0.020) (Fig. 3 and Table S3). However, in the MLR analysis, this association remained significant only in the subgroups of patients with cardiac etiology and an APACHE II score < 30 (cardiac etiology: OR [95%CI]: 0.25 [0.06, 0.94], P = 0.046; APACHE II < 30: 0.27 [0.07, 0.88], P = 0.034). Similar findings were observed in the PSM analysis (cardiac etiology: OR [95%CI]: 0.08 [0.01, 0.51], P = 0.017; APACHE II < 30: 0.19 [0.04, 0.80], P = 0.029) (Fig. 3 and Table S3).

Discussion

In this retrospective exploratory study, we observed that 33 °C TTM was correlated with the prognosis of patients with OHCA. Within subgroups of patients with cardiac etiology and those with an APACHE II score < 30, the Non-33 °C TTM stable group exhibited a more favorable neurological prognosis.

Cerebral injury following CA has the highest incidence among PCAS manifestations, and it is a critical factor influencing patients’ survival rate and neurological prognosis [3]. Its mechanisms include impaired oxygen and substrate delivery, calcium homeostasis imbalance, oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and other related processes caused by ischemia and reperfusion [19]. TTM can alleviate cerebral injury by reducing cerebral metabolism and decreasing neuro-excitotoxicity [4], which has been widely confirmed in both cell experiments and animal studies [20, 21]. However, in clinical practice, the selection of target temperature has long been controversial [7], and current clinical RCTs mainly focus on temperatures of 33 °C, 36 °C, or strategies to avoid fever [5, 6, 8].

In our study, patients were grouped according to whether they received 33 °C TTM, and the outcomes of different groups were compared. Unlike other studies, we excluded patients who died during hypothermia treatment and within 24 h after admission to the EICU. This exclusion was intended to reduce the potential impact of early hemodynamic instability on the study outcomes and to emphasize the importance of TTM implementation. Recently, some retrospective studies have indicated that hypothermic TTM may benefit patients in specific subgroups [9–11, 22] or with specific severities of PCAS [13, 16, 23]. A retrospective study by Wallmüller et al. found that 32–34 °C TTM benefited OHCA patients aged < 65 years [11]. In contrast to this finding, chi-square analysis in our study showed that patients aged ≥ 65 years who received non-33℃ TTM had better discharge neurological outcomes (OR [95%CI]: 0.30 [0.10, 0.88], P = 0.027). The HYPERION trial [8] and other retrospective studies [24, 25] have demonstrated that 32–34 °C TTM can improve neurological prognosis and survival rate in patients with non-shockable rhythms; however, our study did not show this relationship, which may be attributed to the small sample size. In addition, in this study, patients with shockable rhythms who received non-33℃ TTM had a higher neurological prognosis (OR [95%CI]: 0.36 [0.15, 0.89], P = 0.024); however, no significant difference was observed in the multivariable analysis. An analysis of the Japanese Association for Acute Medicine Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest (JAAM-OHCA) registry revealed that TTM at 32–34 °C improved a favorable neurological prognosis at 30 days in patients with severe hyperlactatemia (≥ 12 mmol/L) [10]. In our study, after grouping patients based on the lactate threshold determined by the median value, patients with lactate < 8.6 mmol/L who received 33℃ TTM had better neurological prognosis. Regarding subgroup analysis by resuscitation time, mild hypothermia (32–34 °C) showed a significant effect in patients with a “no-flow” duration exceeding 2 min [26], and PCAS patients with a resuscitation interval ≤ 30 min [22] might benefit from TTM at < 34 °C. However, a post-hoc analysis of the TTM1 trial by Kjaergaard et al. [27] indicated that, regardless of TROSC, 33 °C TTM neither reduced the risk of death nor improved neurological prognosis, and this relationship was also not observed in our study’s subgroup. In addition, two other retrospective studies [9, 28], which grouped patients based on computed tomography (CT) images and electroencephalography (EEG) findings, respectively, showed that patients without hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy on CT and those with moderate encephalopathy on EEG could benefit from 33 °C TTM. Furthermore, our study found that patients with cardiac etiology who received Non-33℃ TTM had better neurological prognosis at discharge (MLR: 0.25 [0.06, 0.94], P = 0.046; PSM: 0.08 [0.01, 0.51], P = 0.017). All these results highlight the importance of individualized TTM treatment.

Currently, the primary explanation for such heterogeneity is that the severity of PCAS influences the efficacy of TTM, as described by Kikutani [12], Mark [29], and others [23, 30, 31], who noted that patients with moderately severe PCAS are more likely to benefit from hypothermic TTM. Regarding the assessment of severity, various predictive models have been developed in previous studies, but no standard protocol has been established to date [7]. Nishikimi et al. [32] developed the rCAST score based on the JAAM-OHCA registry; using this score, PCAS patients were categorized into the following risk groups: low severity (≤ 5.5 score), moderate severity (6–14 score), and high severity (≥ 14.5 score). The results showed that 33–34 °C TTM was significantly associated with a higher rate of favorable neurological prognosis in the moderate severity group, but no significant association was observed in the low or high severity groups [13]. A retrospective study from South Korea externally validated the role of this score in guiding 33–36 °C TTM [33], while another study based on the JAAM-OHCA registry confirmed the guiding value of the rCAST score in pediatric patients [34]. Meanwhile, the r-CAST OHCA trial has been initiated, which aims to compare the efficacy of hypothermia versus normothermia in improving the 30-day neurological prognosis of patients with moderate PCAS following OHCA (Trial registration number: jRCT1062220035) [35]. The PCAC classification identifies four distinct grades of disease severity based on the initial neurological and cardiopulmonary dysfunction after ROSC [15]. Based on this classification, Callaway et al. [16] found that among patients with the most severe condition but without severe cerebral edema or malignant EEG findings, 33 °C TTM was associated with higher survival rates than 36 °C TTM; in patients with mild to moderate severity, 36 °C TTM was linked to higher survival rates. Despite these studies, guidelines have not yet identified the specific subset of patients who can benefit from 32 to 34 °C TTM [7, 17]. In the present study, two commonly used scores for assessing the severity of critically ill patients–APACHE II score [36] and SOFA score [37]--were adopted. For patients with an APACHE II score ≥ 30, the initiation of 33 °C TTM was associated with better neurological prognosis (MLR: OR [95%CI]: 0.29 [0.08, 0.96], P = 0.046); for patients with an APACHE II score < 30, those in the Non-33 °C TTM stable group had a higher rate of favorable neurological outcomes at discharge (MLR: 0.27 [0.07, 0.88], P = 0.034; PSM: 0.19 [0.04, 0.80], P = 0.029). However, the SOFA score did not show this correlation in the multivariable analysis. All subgroup analyses in this study were performed for exploratory purposes only. These findings are primarily intended to generate relevant research hypotheses, rather than serving as definitive and confirmatory conclusions. To date, no other studies have confirmed these aforementioned results.

Limitations

Through retrospective analysis, this study found that patients in different subgroups and with different severities may affect the efficacy of TTM; however, there are still some limitations, so we emphasize that these observational data should be used cautiously to guide clinical practice. First, the analysis of this study was based on small-scale, single-center retrospective data, which may lead to selection bias and information bias, and limit the generalizability of the conclusions. Furthermore, due to the retrospective study design adopted, the nursing measures after cardiac arrest were not standardized. Large-scale prospective studies will be needed in the future. Second, the outcome indicators of this study were survival status and CPC score at discharge, focusing on short-term prognosis; however, to more accurately evaluate the outcomes of PCAS, it is preferable to set a longer-term endpoint. Third, this study excluded patients with traumatic brain injury (n = 51) and those who received ECPR (n = 40); the prognosis and hemodynamics of these two groups of patients are significantly different from those of other patients [38], so further studies are required to determine the efficacy of TTM in these patients. Fourth, this study used the APACHE II and SOFA scores to determine severity; in the future, it is necessary to identify predictive models with better performance to assess the correlation between TTM efficacy and PCAS severity. Fifth, baseline characteristics of different groups in this study showed that significant intergroup differences were observed between the 33 °C TTM group and the Non-33 °C TTM group, as well as between the corresponding stable subgroups, in terms of age, gender, PLR, TROSC, MAP, HGB, and other baseline indicators (all P < 0.05). Meanwhile, critically ill patients who died during hypothermia treatment or within 24 h after ICU admission were excluded from this study, which inevitably introduced certain patient selection bias. Therefore, the associations observed between TTM strategies and neurological prognosis in subgroup analyses are more likely to reflect discrepancies in baseline clinical stability, enrollment selection bias, and heterogeneity of early clinical disease trajectories after cardiac arrest, rather than the independent therapeutic effect directly attributable to 33 °C TTM itself. Finally, this study only focused on the temperature of TTM, while patient prognosis may be affected by the time required to reach the target temperature during the induction phase, the duration of maintenance, and the rate of rewarming [39]. It should be noted that the Non-33 °C TTM group in this study included patients receiving 36 °C TTM and fever prevention strategies. This group involved various temperature regulation regimens rather than a single unified intervention, thus presenting inherent within-group heterogeneity.

Conclusions

Our study indicates that the prognostic correlation of 33 °C TTM after OHCA is related to patient clinical characteristics. Such discrepancies may be attributed to differences in baseline disease severity. Further investigations are therefore warranted to establish individualized TTM strategies for OHCA patients.
