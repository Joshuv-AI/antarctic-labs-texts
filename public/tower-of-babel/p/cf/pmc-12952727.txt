Real-world safety and tolerability of intravenous edaravone in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
Authors: Agessandro Abrahao, Polina Da Silva, Malgorzata Ciepielewska, Lorne Zinman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis retrospective cohort study describes real-world safety and tolerability outcomes in United States-based edaravone-treated patients with ALS.Patients & methodsAmerita Specialty Infusion Services provided IV edaravone to patients with ALS treated with their first dose between 25 September 2017-30 September 2022. Mean ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R) scores and forced vital capacity (FVC) %–predicted measures were recorded within ± 100 days from care initiation to approximate baseline values.ResultsIncluded patients (n = 243) received/were still receiving IV edaravone/edaravone oral suspension as of 30 September 2022. At initiation, 66.7% were male, mean age ± SD was 61.2 ± 11.2 years, and 61.3% were covered by government insurance. In patients with provider–recorded ALSFRS-R (n = 115) and FVC (n = 84) %–predicted measures within ± 100 days from care initiation, mean ± SD values were 35.1 ± 8.9 and 72.3% ± 21.7%, respectively. Mean ± SD therapy duration was 13.5 ± 11.4 months. Discontinuation reasons included death/hospice (n = 82), patient’s choice (n = 38), doctor’s choice (n = 31), insurance (n = 18), and other (n = 12). Reasons for IV edaravone discontinuation and IV edaravone administration access device were not associated.ConclusionsTreatment discontinuation was primarily related to ALS disease progression/death, rather than safety or tolerability. This study representative of real-world patients with ALS suggests that edaravone showed consistent safety and tolerability profiles with previous studies.KEYWORDS: Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, edaravone, real-world evidence, safety, tolerability

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neuromuscular disease characterized by loss of upper and lower motor neurons and progressive paralysis [1]. There is no cure for ALS and most patients die within 3 to 5 years of symptom onset, usually of respiratory failure [1,2]. The United States (US) National ALS Registry identified the age-adjusted standardized incidence rates per 100,000 population to be 1.6 to 1.75 in 2014–2016 [3]. In 2017, the prevalence of ALS was 5.5 to 9.9 per 100,000 US population in an analysis that used both established and novel methodologies [4].

At the time of these analyses, only 2 active pharmaceutical agents were approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and available for use by patients with ALS: riluzole and edaravone (intravenous [IV] edaravone and edaravone oral suspension) [5,6]. Although edaravone’s mechanism of action in ALS has not yet been clarified, it has been established that it is a free radical scavenger and antioxidant [5,7,8], which may be beneficial in patients with ALS as neuronal death has been associated with free radicals and oxidative stress. IV edaravone and edaravone oral suspension were approved by the US FDA for the treatment of ALS in May 2017 and May 2022, respectively [5]. IV edaravone became available to patients in August 2017, and edaravone oral suspension in June 2022. FDA approval of IV edaravone was based on a pivotal, phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (MCI186–19; Study 19) in which IV edaravone was shown to slow the loss of functional decline on the ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R) by 33% (p = 0.0013) compared with placebo at 24 weeks [9]. Post hoc analyses of Study 19 that included a 24-week open-label extension period to investigate longer-term safety and efficacy of IV edaravone suggested that patients continue to benefit from IV edaravone through 48 weeks, with no new or cumulative safety concerns [10–13]. Additional details of the 24-week double-blind period and 24-week open-label active treatment period have been described previously (Clinicaltrials.gov: NCT01492686) [9,10]. Briefly, the primary efficacy analysis consisted of IV edaravone–treated (n = 68) and placebo (n = 66) groups, with a slower ALSFRS-R score decline in the IV edaravone vs placebo group (−5.01 vs − 7.50) over 24 weeks [9]. Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were reported in 58 (84%) IV edaravone–treated patients and 57 (84%) placebo patients, with no deaths occurring during the 24-week double-blind period.

In addition to these phase 3 clinical trials, real-world, postmarketing pharmacovigilance data for IV edaravone have been published at 1 and 3 years post launch [14,15]. Genge et al. did not identify any safety signals beyond those already found during the IV edaravone clinical trial program. Furthermore, many of the adverse events in this postmarketing report were consistent with those expected during ALS disease progression [15].

Prior to the FDA approval of edaravone oral suspension, phase 1 pharmacokinetic (PK) and bioequivalence studies showed that 105 mg of edaravone oral suspension displayed a similar PK profile to the FDA-approved 60-mg IV edaravone dose, with the oral formulation demonstrating an equivalent area under the plasma concentration-time curve (AUC) to the IV formulation [16,17]. Subsequently, a global, multicenter, open-label, phase 3 trial called Study MT-1186-A01 was initiated and examined the safety and tolerability of edaravone oral suspension in patients with ALS over 48 weeks [18]. Patients (Safety Analysis Population, n = 185; completed 48 weeks, n = 139) received 105 mg of edaravone oral suspension in treatment cycles mimicking those of FDA-approved IV edaravone. Over 48 weeks, TEAEs and serious TEAEs were all generally consistent with the disease state. As these safety results were generally consistent with the IV edaravone safety profile and identified no new safety concerns, they support the use of edaravone oral suspension in patients with ALS.

IV edaravone is administered by infusion at clinic sites, infusion centers, or at home [5,14]. As one of the few drug therapies FDA approved for the treatment of ALS in the US, interest is focused on real-world treatment experience with edaravone. To date, information on the real-world safety and tolerability in patients treated with edaravone in the US is limited. The objective of this study was to describe real-world safety and tolerability outcomes in patients with ALS who were treated with IV edaravone since the time of its commercial availability in August 2017, as collected by a provider of home and alternate-site infusions.

Patients & methods

Data source

A retrospective cohort study was conducted and included patients with ALS who received infusion services primarily in the home through Amerita Specialty Infusion Services. Amerita is a provider of home infusion, ambulatory infusion suites, and specialty pharmacy services in the US, and it has collected data on patients with ALS treated with IV edaravone (Radicava® IV, Mitsubishi Tanabe Pharma America [MTPA]) since August 2017, the time of its commercial availability.

Study population

Included in this analysis were patients with ALS who were treated with their first IV edaravone dose between 25 September 2017, and 30 September 2022. Some patients (n = 21) in this analysis switched from IV edaravone to Radicava ORS® (edaravone) oral suspension (MTPA) after it became commercially available in June 2022 and were included as continuing with edaravone treatment. Although the FDA approved the combination of sodium phenylbutyrate-taurursodiol on 29 September 2022, for the treatment of patients with ALS [19], it was not yet available for use by the patients analyzed in this study.

Ethics approval

A delegated review of this study was conducted and the protocol received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Board (REB) of Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre (Project Identification Number: 6795). The REB determined that an Informed Consent Form was not required for this study, as consent was either not applicable or was otherwise addressed in accordance with Articles 3.7 and/or 3.12 and/or 5.5 of the Tri-Council Policy Statement 2.

Patient demographic and clinical characteristics

Variables collected and described in this analysis include patient demographics, ALSFRS-R scores, forced vital capacity (FVC) %–predicted measures, and measures of IV edaravone safety and tolerability. FVC %–predicted (FVC as a percentage of predicted) is a pulmonary function test that compares how far a patient’s actual FVC, or the total volume that can be forcefully expired from a maximum inspiratory effort, differs from what is expected in a comparable healthy individual of the same age, sex, height, and other factors. It is calculated as (measured FVC/predicted FVC) x 100, with a normal range usually between 80% and 120%. Patient data, including ALSFRS-R and FVC %–predicted measures collected within ± 100 days from care initiation to obtain a close approximation of baseline values, were recorded by healthcare providers.

Statistical analyses

Demographic and clinical variables were assessed descriptively, using counts and percentages for categorical variables and measures of central tendency (mean/median/SD) for continuous variables.

Results

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 243 patients with ALS who had received or were still receiving IV edaravone, as well as patients who switched from IV edaravone to edaravone oral suspension as of 30 September 2022, were included in the analysis (Figure 1). Amerita Infusion Service Centers were located throughout the US, and since most centers were in the southern/western US, there was a higher concentration of patients from those areas. Patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and insurance coverage are reported for patients with ALS based on the year of their first IV edaravone infusion, which was between 25 September 2017, and 30 September 2022 (Table 1). The number of patients included steadily increased through 2021, with general consistency in demographics for the years reported. Similarly, ALSFRS-R scores and FVC %–predicted measures generally also steadily increased through 2021. Patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and insurance coverage were also split into 2 groups based on whether they continued or discontinued edaravone treatment as of 30 September 2022 (Table 2). All patients initially started on IV edaravone treatment. Edaravone discontinuation meant that patients had discontinued all routes of edaravone therapy, including IV edaravone and edaravone oral suspension. Edaravone continuation included patients who continued either on IV edaravone or edaravone oral suspension through 30 September 2022. There were 21 patients who switched from IV edaravone to edaravone oral suspension and were included in the continued edaravone treatment group.

At the start of treatment, 66.7% of the patients were male, the mean age ± SD was 61.2 ± 11.2 years, and 61.3% were covered by government insurance plans, including Medicare, Medicaid, Veterans, or Tricare. Mean ALSFRS-R scores and FVC %–predicted measures recorded within ± 100 days from care initiation were higher for the cohort of patients who continued edaravone treatment vs patients who discontinued edaravone treatment, as of 30 September 2022 (Table 2). For the patients in the total population who had healthcare provider–recorded ALSFRS-R (n = 115/243 [47.3%]) and FVC %–predicted (n = 84/243 [34.6%]) measures assessed ± 100 days from care initiation, mean ± SD scores were 35.1 ± 8.9 and 72.3% ± 21.7%, respectively. Mean ALSFRS-R scores and FVC %–predicted measures were lower for patients who discontinued edaravone vs those who continued edaravone.

Of the 84 patients who had FVC %–predicted measures recorded within ± 100 days from care initiation, 45.2% (n = 38) had an FVC %–predicted score > 80% with a mean ± SD of 14.5 ± 8.7 months on therapy and 54.8% (n = 46) had an FVC %–predicted score < 80% with a mean ± SD of 11.8 ± 9.6 months on therapy.

The length of time from ALS diagnosis to the start of edaravone therapy was a mean ± SD of 10.6 ± 15.0 months for patients for whom data were available (n = 176) (Table 3). The mean interval from ALS diagnosis to the first IV edaravone infusion shortened over time, from over 1.5 years in 2017 and 2018 to less than 6 months in 2022. The cohort of patients who continued with edaravone treatment (n = 44) had a mean of 2.5 additional months from ALS diagnosis to care initiation vs patients who discontinued edaravone treatment (n = 132), as of 30 September 2022.

The duration of therapy for the total patient population (n = 243) was a mean ± SD of 13.5 ± 11.4 months. At the end of the study period, 181/243 (74.5%) patients had discontinued treatment (Table 4). The most frequent reason for discontinuation was death or hospice for 82 (45.3%) patients, followed by patient’s choice for 38 (21.0%) patients, doctor’s choice for 31 (17.1%) patients, issues related to insurance for 18 (10.0%) patients, and other for 12 (6.6%) patients. For patients who discontinued edaravone (n = 181), the mean ± SD treatment duration was 10.8 ± 9.0 months, and 65.7% of patients who discontinued had received edaravone treatment for ≥6 months. The number of patients who discontinued was comparable for patients who had been on edaravone therapy for < 6 months (n = 62), 6 to 12 months (n = 57), or > 12 months (n = 62). The 62 patients who continued edaravone therapy did so for a mean ± SD of 21.2 ± 14.0 months as of 30 September 2022. Within the subgroup of patients who discontinued edaravone treatment due to patient’s choice (n = 38), most patients discontinued due to disease progression (16/38, 42.1%) primarily within the first 12 months (29/38, 76.3%) (Supplementary Table S1). For patients who discontinued due to patient’s choice, 5 patients cited adverse reaction as the reason for discontinuation. Within the subgroup of patients who discontinued edaravone treatment due to doctor’s choice (n = 31), most patients discontinued due to disease progression (18/31, 58.1%), primarily within the first 12 months (20/31, 64.5%) (Supplementary Table S2), but 5 of these patients cited adverse reaction as the reason.

Information on the IV edaravone access device used was available for 170 patients who discontinued therapy and 57 patients who continued therapy (Figure 2). There was no association between the reason for edaravone discontinuation and the type of access device used for IV edaravone administration. An implanted port was most commonly used for IV edaravone administration both in patients who continued (n = 45, 79.0%) and discontinued (n = 118, 69.4%) edaravone therapy. Following the use of implanted ports, a peripherally inserted central catheter and peripheral IV were nearly equally used within each cohort of patients who continued or discontinued IV edaravone administration, while midline and central venous catheters were rarely used.

Discussion

ALS is a rare and clinically heterogeneous disease, and real-world data on the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with ALS may provide a more comprehensive description of this population than randomized controlled trials (RCTs) alone. Furthermore, as edaravone is one of the few FDA-approved active pharmaceutical agents for patients with ALS [5], with bioequivalence for AUC established between IV edaravone and edaravone oral suspension [16,17], interest is focused on how it is being utilized in the US. In particular, since all of the registrational clinical trials for IV edaravone were conducted in Japan, the real-world safety and tolerability of edaravone treatment in patients with ALS in the US contributes to the existing data from RCTs and real-world studies. As Amerita Specialty Infusion Services is a provider of home infusion, ambulatory infusion suites, and specialty pharmacy services in the US, it is well positioned to provide real-world data from patients receiving IV edaravone infusions at home. A previous report showed that 43% of patients receiving IV edaravone received administrations of the drug at home [14], highlighting the importance of home infusions for this drug. With the FDA approval and commercial availability of edaravone oral suspension for patients with ALS during the approximately 4 final months of this study, 21 patients (8.6%) switched from IV edaravone to edaravone oral suspension. It is likely that if this study reported even more recent data, a higher percentage of patients would have reported switching to edaravone oral suspension due to the greater accessibility of oral therapy and presumed preference for oral vs IV delivery.

The patients in this population had characteristics that were consistent with the overall population of patients with ALS in the US [4]. For example, the most prevalent age group in this population was aged 60 to 69 years and included a higher percentage of males vs females. When compared to participants in the pivotal phase 3 IV edaravone trial [9], this real-world sample had lower mean ALSFRS-R scores, 35.1 vs 41.9, indicating greater disability at baseline in this cohort. A mean FVC %–predicted score of 72.3% was also lower in this study compared to the pivotal trial where all participants had an FVC %–predicted score of at least 80%.

Our data support the safety and tolerability profile of edaravone treatment, as the mean treatment duration was just short of 1 year for patients who discontinued treatment and was between 1.5 and 2 years for patients who continued treatment. Of the patients who discontinued, most did so due to death or hospice (45.3%). Although edaravone has been shown to slow physical functional decline in clinical trials relative to placebo, there is currently no cure for ALS, and as a result, disease progression is expected. In line with this, disease progression was the most common reason patients halted edaravone therapy within the patient’s choice or doctor’s choice discontinuation categories. Within the 21% and 17.1% of patients who discontinued due to patient’s choice and doctor’s choice, respectively, only 5 patients from each category discontinued due to adverse reaction. Reported adverse reactions in this cohort were generally consistent with previous postmarketing pharmacovigilance reports from 1 and 3 years after IV edaravone became available to patients in the US [14,15], and also coincide with those expected with ALS disease progression.

Additionally, an ongoing, postmarketing, observational surveillance study called SUNRISE Japan is evaluating the real-world safety and efficacy of IV edaravone–treated patients with ALS over a 5-year period [20]. The study reported the incidence of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) up to 1 year after the initiation of IV edaravone treatment in approximately 800 Japanese patients with ALS [21]. They found that the most frequently reported ADR was abnormal hepatic function (4.4%), which was commonly reported for patients receiving concomitant riluzole and may have been associated with its known hepatic impact. Although the study is not placebo-controlled, the analysis of a large number of patients with ALS will contribute to data regarding natural disease course and long-term outcomes.

In the current study, IV edaravone discontinuation was not associated with the type of access device used by patients. Between approximately 70% and 79% of patients used an implanted port for IV edaravone administration, which was similar to previous pharmacovigilance reports noting that the majority of patients (59%) used implanted ports [14]. Maintaining long-term IV edaravone use via peripheral IV access can be challenging for nurses and patients. The implanted port appears to be the preferred real-world access given its longer durability and better safety and maintenance profile compared to peripheral access. Discontinuation due to a line infection was reported in 1 patient.

There were several limitations to this study. First, this study lacked a control group or comparator arm. Additionally, this study was limited to patients with ALS who received treatment through infusion services, specifically through a single, large, national infusion provider; therefore, the results may not be generalizable to all patients with ALS. Due to the availability of data as collected through Amerita Specialty Infusion Services, information on concurrent ALS therapies (of which only riluzole was FDA-approved at the time of the study) and details regarding the frequency, severity, or resolution of adverse events were not available. Further, the study relied on electronic health record data and self-reported data, which may be subject to coding and entry error and bias. Finally, patient ALSFRS-R and FVC %–predicted measures were obtained from patient charts, and in the case of multiple scores, the score closest to care initiation was recorded. Only those scores recorded within ±100 days from care initiation were reported in this study to best approximate baseline values.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this descriptive, retrospective cohort study representative of real-world patients with ALS suggests that treatment with IV edaravone showed consistent safety and tolerability profiles with those previously reported, for patients with ALS in the US who received infusions through Amerita Specialty Infusion Services, over a mean treatment duration of 13 months. Reasons for treatment discontinuation were typically related to ALS disease progression, death, or hospice disposition and not primarily due to safety or tolerability, as < 5% of patients total discontinued due to adverse reactions. The long-term, real-world safety and tolerability of IV edaravone shown here expands the safety evidence generated by RCTs and provides insight on the long-term safety of edaravone oral suspension, due to the established bioequivalence for AUC of the IV edaravone and edaravone oral suspension formulations. Future studies may apply more complex models, such as time-to-event or regression analyses, to further elucidate predictors of treatment discontinuation or adverse events. It is hoped that the information from this study will be useful to clinicians who prescribe IV edaravone and edaravone oral suspension for patients with ALS.
