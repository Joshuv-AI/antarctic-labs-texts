Epigenetic regulation of vertebrate limb development: from field specification to tissue morphogenesis
Authors: Claudia Monserrat Vallejo-De Lira, Claudio Iván Galván-Hernández, Jesús Chimal-Monroy, Jessica Cristina Marin-Llera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVertebrate limb development provides an excellent model for understanding how epigenetic mechanisms coordinate the integration of positional information and temporal signals to generate complex three-dimensional structures. An increasing body of evidence shows that chromatin-based mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and higher-order chromatin organization, define the competence of progenitor cells to respond to morphogenetic signals with spatial and temporal precision during limb development. This review explores how epigenetic mechanisms orchestrate and regulate the major phases of limb formation: from field specification and bud induction to morphogenesis, lineage differentiation, and programmed cell death. Here, we examine how chromatin remodeling, histone and DNA modifications, and enhancer – promoter interactions functionally converge with developmental signaling pathways to control gene expression programs that govern timing, positional identity, and cell fate decisions. Furthermore, this review highlights recent advances that link epigenetic landscapes with morphogenetic outcomes and discusses how comparative epigenomic approaches are reshaping our understanding of evolutionary diversification in limb morphology.KEYWORDS: Limb development, epigenetics, DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, limb morphogenesis, cell fate specification, developmental transitions

Introduction

Vertebrate limb development is a paradigmatic example of how complex anatomical structures arise through the coordinated integration of genetic programs, developmental signaling pathways, and epigenetic regulatory mechanisms. The initial specification of the limb field, which leads to the emergence of three distinct axes and several specialized tissues, requires precise coordination of gene expression modules across both temporal and spatial dimensions. While the signaling pathways and transcriptional networks responsible for limb patterning and morphogenesis have been extensively characterized, the epigenetic mechanisms that regulate, stabilize, and integrate these processes remain comparatively less understood. Nevertheless, significant advances in recent years have begun to elucidate how these mechanisms operate during limb development.

Epigenetic mechanisms establish transcriptional states, stabilize cellular identities, and coordinate developmental transitions during limb morphogenesis, thereby refining spatial and temporal gene expression programs throughout development. To this end, this review provides a comprehensive overview of the epigenetic mechanisms that regulate embryonic limb development across key stages, with emphasis on DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and higher-order chromatin organization. Rather than offering an exhaustive list of epigenetic factors during limb development, this review contextualizes findings within the framework of embryonic patterning and cell fate specification. Our objective is to establish a conceptual and mechanistic framework for understanding how epigenetic regulation integrates positional information, temporal competence, morphogen responsiveness, and cell fate decisions to shape the complexity and robustness of vertebrate limb formation. Given the complexity of limb development, and because not all developmental processes can be examined within the same cellular, anatomical, or experimental context, we explicitly indicate the model system used whenever relevant, including chick, mouse, zebrafish, and human disease-associated contexts.

Fundamentals of epigenetic regulation

Epigenetics refers to the study of heritable modifications in gene function that occur without altering the DNA sequence. In developmental contexts, many epigenetic mechanisms act dynamically and reversibly to modulate transcriptional competence without necessarily implying long-term transgenerational inheritance. In this review, we use the term epigenetic regulation broadly to refer to chromatin-based and RNA-mediated mechanisms that influence gene expression potential, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, enhancer accessibility, higher-order genome organization, and non-coding RNAs. These mechanisms constitute an additional layer of information that enables a single genotype to generate diverse phenotypes in response to developmental programs or environmental stimuli. The introduction of the epigenotype concept and of the epigenetic landscape model provided a conceptual framework to explain how developmental trajectories emerge from interactions between genes and cellular states [1]. The distinction between ‘genetic systems,’ which store hereditary information, and ‘epigenetic systems,’ which regulate which genetic potentials are expressed in specific cellular contexts, highlights the idea that developmental outcomes depend not only on inherited genetic information but also on mechanisms that determine when, where, and in which cellular contexts specific developmental programs are activated or repressed [2]. These epigenetic mechanisms function through interconnected molecular processes that regulate chromatin organization across multiple structural levels, from local nucleosome positioning to higher-order genome architecture. The epigenome therefore comprises multiple regulatory systems, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, higher-order chromatin organization, and non-coding RNAs, all of which influence transcriptional accessibility and gene expression. Chromatin folding into loops and domains enables physical interactions between distal regulatory elements and their target genes, contributing to precise spatiotemporal transcriptional control. These structures are stabilized by architectural proteins such as CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) and cohesin complexes, which facilitate enhancer – promoter communication and organize chromatin into topologically constrained domains. Thus, chromatin accessibility and long-range genomic interactions jointly define transcriptional competence (Figure 1A).

Key mechanisms of chromatin organization and epigenetic regulation of gene expression. (A) Chromatin dynamically transitions among closed, permissive, and accessible conformations, thereby regulating the assembly of transcriptional machinery. Higher-order chromatin organization into DNA loops and domains enables long-range interactions between distal regulatory elements and their target genes and is stabilized by architectural proteins such as CTCF and cohesin complexes. (B) Core epigenetic mechanisms that regulate chromatin structure and gene expression include DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs. DNA methylation is established by DNMTs and can be reversed through TET-mediated oxidation-dependent demethylation pathways. Histone modifications are deposited, removed, and interpreted by writer, eraser, and reader proteins, respectively, generating chromatin environments that can be permissive or repressive to transcription. Non-coding RNAs further modulate gene expression by influencing transcriptional regulation, chromatin-associated complexes, and post-transcriptional processes. (C) ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes regulate nucleosome positioning, composition, and stability, thereby modifying chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity.

Epigenetic modifications are deposited and maintained by writer enzymes, including DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), histone acetyltransferases (HATs), and histone methyltransferases (HMTs), whereas eraser enzymes, such as histone deacetylases (HDACs), histone demethylases (HDMs), and ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes, remove these modifications (Figure 1B) [3–5]. Reader proteins interpret these chromatin marks and recruit additional regulatory complexes that reinforce active or repressive transcriptional states. Non-coding RNAs, including microRNAs (miRNAs), small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), further contribute to gene regulation by guiding chromatin-modifying complexes to specific genomic regions or by targeting messenger RNAs for degradation (Figure 1B) [6].

DNA methylation involves the covalent addition of a methyl group to the 5’ position of cytosine residues and occurs predominantly at CpG dinucleotides through the activity of DNMT enzymes. DNMT3A and DNMT3B mediate de novo methylation, whereas DNMT1 maintains methylation patterns during cell division [7]. Methylated DNA generally represses transcription by interfering with transcription factor binding or recruiting methyl-CpG-binding domain (MBD) proteins, which recruit corepressor complexes such as Sin3A and Mi-2/NuRD [8]. A second level of regulation involves post-translational histone modifications, including acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination, often conceptualized as the histone code [4]. Histone acetylation, catalyzed by HATs, generally promotes transcriptionally active chromatin states, whereas HDAC-mediated deacetylation promotes chromatin compaction [9,10]. Histone methylation exerts context-dependent effects depending on the modified residue and methylation state. For example, H3K4me3 is associated with active promoters, whereas H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 are repressive marks associated with heterochromatin and gene silencing [11,12]. ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes further regulate chromatin accessibility by repositioning or restructuring nucleosomes (Figure 1C). SWI/SNF complexes are generally associated with transcriptionally accessible euchromatin, whereas ISWI and Mi-2/NuRD complexes contribute to repressive chromatin organization [13–15].

Thus, epigenetic regulation operates through dynamic and reversible chromatin states that integrate genomic organization, transcriptional accessibility, and regulatory timing. Together, these mechanisms create a regulatory context through which developing cells acquire the competence to activate, maintain, or repress gene expression programs in response to spatial and temporal cues during vertebrate limb formation.

Epigenetic regulation of limb development

Morphogenesis, the process by which developing tissues acquire shape and spatial organization, is a fundamental aspect of embryonic development. During limb development, coordinated processes such as cellular differentiation, proliferation, migration, and programmed cell death transform initially homogeneous cell populations into complex and functional structures. These events require cells to interpret positional cues within specific molecular and tissue contexts, linking gene regulation with pattern formation, intercellular signaling, and and tissue-level interactions that collectively shape developmental outcomes [16].

In the developing limb, signaling networks that pattern the three developmental axes are functionally coupled to regulatory landscapes defined by transcription factor networks, chromatin accessibility states, enhancer usage, and higher-order chromatin organization. Through this coupling, morphogenetic signals including Wingless/Int-1 (WNT) signaling, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling, and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling are interpreted according to the transcriptional competence of each progenitor population, allowing cells in distinct limb regions to acquire specific identities despite sharing the same genome. Genome-wide chromatin mapping studies have further expanded the understanding of how epigenetic states regulate region-specific gene expression during limb patterning. For example, in mouse embryonic fore- and hindlimbs, integrated profiling of H3K27ac, H3K27me3, and gene expression identified limb-specific chromatin signatures associated with active developmental enhancers, illustrating how chromatin state profiling can reveal regulatory elements linked to regional identity during limb development [17]. These analyses demonstrate that chromatin profiling can identify regulatory elements associated with regional identity during limb development, providing a framework to understand how enhancer activity and chromatin accessibility shape the cellular response to morphogenetic signals within the developing limb.

Limb field specification and limb initiation

Limb field specification depends on the establishment of spatially restricted developmental competence within the lateral plate mesoderm (LPM), a process coordinated by axial patterning cues, signaling pathways, and epigenetic regulation. Positional information along the rostrocaudal axis, primarily conferred by Hox genes, defines the presumptive limb territories [18]. During this process, the LPM is regionalized into anterior and posterior domains, which acquire forelimb or hindlimb potential, respectively. In this phase, retinoic acid (RA) signaling plays a central role by restricting the cardiac field and establishing a FGF8-free permissive zone for forelimb induction [19]. In mouse Raldh2-null embryos (Aldh1a2), the absence of RA leads to the anterior expansion of Fgf8 expression and the failure to activate T-box transcription factor 5 (TBX5), indicating that RA functions by repressing Fgf8 expression in the trunk region to establish a permissive zone for limb initiation [20]. Within the posterior LPM, collinear expression of Hox genes establishes positional identity along the anteroposterior axis and directs the selective activation of TBX5 in the forelimb field and TBX4 and paired-like homeodomain 1 (PITX1) in the hindlimb field. These limb-associated transcription factors participate in regulatory networks that control Wnt2b expression in the forelimb field, Wnt8c expression in the hindlimb field, and FGF10 activation in the limb-forming mesenchyme. These interactions help establish the mesenchymal–ectodermal FGF feedback loop required for limb bud outgrowth. Additional regulators from paraxial tissues and signals from the intermediate mesoderm further refine the limb field [19]. Together, these pathways delimit the limb-forming territory within the LPM and establish the positional information required for subsequent limb bud induction.

Limb field specification is accompanied by the progressive acquisition of permissive chromatin states that coordinate axial patterning signals with limb-specific transcriptional programs (Figure 2A). At the molecular level, studies in chick embryos showed that the transcription factor Cut-like homeobox 2 (CUX2) promotes the expression of Hoxb3, Hoxb5, and retinaldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (Raldh2) in the LPM, thereby integrating Hox-dependent positional information with RA signaling to refine the limb-forming territory. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analyses in this model demonstrated that CUX2 directly binds enhancers associated with Hoxb genes and Raldh2, whereas repression of posterior genes such as heart and neural crest derivatives expressed 2 (Hand2) and Sonic hedgehog (Shh) further sharpens the anterior boundary of the limb field ([21]; Figure 2A). Studies in chick and mouse embryos further revealed that the spatial collinearity of Hox gene expression is accompanied by dynamic changes in chromatin organization within Hox clusters. At the mouse Hoxd locus, the regulatory landscape is partitioned into the telomeric domain (T-DOM), associated with proximal limb gene expression, and the centromeric domain (C-DOM), linked to distal limb identity. Activation of proximal Hoxd genes correlates with chromatin decondensation within T-DOM, suggesting that localized chromatin remodeling is sufficient to promote transcriptional activation during early limb specification [22]. These findings indicate that early limb developmental competence depends not only on transcription factor activity, but also on the progressive establishment of accessible chromatin domains that enable region-specific gene expression.

(A) During limb field establishment, CUX2 contributes to the activation of hoxb genes and Raldh2 by binding associated enhancers, thereby linking axial patterning information with retinoic acid (RA)-dependent regulation in the lateral plate mesoderm (LPM). RA signaling promotes a permissive chromatin environment associated with increased H3K27ac and reduced H3K27me3 at developmental target loci, supporting early limb developmental programs. Limb-type identity is further stabilized by differential chromatin organization at the Pitx1 locus: in hindlimbs, Pitx1 and its enhancer pen occupy an H3K27ac-enriched active chromatin compartment that enables enhancer – promoter communication, whereas in forelimbs these elements are separated by H3K27me3-enriched repressive chromatin domains, preventing Pitx1 activation. (B) During limb initiation, Lhx2 is regulated by limb-specific cis-regulatory modules (CRMs) enriched in H3K27ac and bound by β-catenin-associated, ETS, and TCF/LEF-dependent regulatory inputs, linking FGF and WNT signaling to enhancer-mediated transcriptional control. In the apical ectodermal ridge (AER), FGF8 promotes ΔNp63α stabilization and transcriptional activity through a c-Abl/p300-dependent acetylation pathway. Acetylated ΔNp63α activates Dlx5 and Dlx6, which are required for AER integrity and proper digit formation.

Comparative ATAC-seq and transcriptional profiling studies have demonstrated species-specific chromatin dynamics during limb field specification. In mouse embryos, chromatin accessibility strongly correlates with transcriptional activation, whereas chick embryos exhibit a temporal uncoupling between chromatin opening and gene expression, suggesting differences in enhancer usage or repressor activity between species. Conserved enhancers associated with Hand2 and (GLI family zinc finger 3) Gli3 display accessible chromatin conformations during limb induction in both models, highlighting the conserved regulatory role of these loci during early limb patterning [23].

Within this regulatory framework, RA signaling further contributes to limb field specification by regulating developmental target genes through chromatin-associated mechanisms. By integrating RNA-seq and ChIP-seq analyses of H3K27ac and H3K27me3 profiles in RA-deficient (Aldh1a2− /−) mouse embryos, 93 genes associated with RA-dependent chromatin modifications near conserved retinoic acid response elements (RAREs) were identified [24]. These included established RA targets as well as developmental regulators such as Nuclear receptor subfamily 2, group F, member 1 (Nr2f1), Nuclear receptor subfamily 2, group F, member 2 (Nr2f2), Myeloid ecotropic viral integration site 1 (Meis1), and Myeloid ecotropic viral integration site 2 (Meis2). RA-dependent transcriptional activation was associated with a gain of H3K27ac or a reduction of H3K27me3 at target loci, whereas RA-dependent transcriptional repression was associated with reduced H3K27ac or increased H3K27me3. These findings indicate that RA signaling establishes permissive or repressive chromatin environments that coordinate axial patterning with early limb developmental programs (Figure 2A). The chromatin landscape during early limb development is progressively organized by enhancer–promoter interactions and higher-order chromatin folding, which coordinate long-range regulatory contacts required for precise developmental gene expression. For example, in mouse limb buds, HAND2 and GLI3 regulate tissue- and stage-specific activation of Shh and Hoxd-associated enhancers during limb patterning [25,26]. In parallel, studies in mouse limb buds showed that dynamic chromatin looping within the Hoxd cluster contributes to the progressive transition between proximal and distal transcriptional programs, a process associated with chromatin accessibility and H3K27ac enrichment [27]. In mouse limb buds, the RNA-binding protein heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein K (hnRNPK) contributes to this regulatory architecture by interacting with CTCF-containing complexes, helping to maintain chromatin looping and the proper expression of developmental genes including Fgf8, Fgf10, and Shh [28]. A similar principle operates at the Pitx1 locus in mouse limb development, where chromatin topology and histone modifications define hindlimb identity independently of the enhancer sequence. In hindlimbs, Pitx1 and the Pen enhancer occupy the same H3K27ac-enriched active chromatin compartment, facilitating enhancer–promoter interactions and transcriptional activation. In contrast, forelimbs display repressive chromatin domains marked by H3K27me3 that spatially separate these elements and prevent Pitx1 activation. Structural alterations of this three-dimensional chromatin organization induce ectopic Pitx1 expression and partial hindlimb-like transformations in the forelimb, resembling phenotypes associated with Liebenberg syndrome in humans ([29]; Figure 2A).

Limb identity is established by the differential expression of Tbx5 in the forelimb field and Tbx4/Pitx1 in the hindlimb field, while limb bud outgrowth is driven by activation of the mesenchymal–ectodermal FGF signaling loop involving Fgf10 and Fgf8 [30–32]. Several epigenetic mechanisms contribute to the stabilization and transcriptional activity of these limb-specific regulatory programs; for example, in zebrafish, acetylation of TBX5 by lysine acetyltransferases KAT2A/B enhances its nuclear retention and transactivation capacity, promoting the expression of downstream targets such as Fgf10 and Bmp4. Disruption of this modification impairs limb and heart development in zebrafish, demonstrating the functional relevance of post-translational acetylation during early limb outgrowth [33]. Similarly, epigenetic regulation contributes to the maintenance and signaling activity of the apical ectodermal ridge (AER). In mouse limb ectoderm, the p63 isoform ΔNp63α is stabilized and transcriptionally activated by FGF8 through a pathway involving c-Abl and the histone acetyltransferase p300. Acetylation of ΔNp63α is required for Distal-less homeobox 5 and 6 (Dlx5 and Dlx6) expression, essential genes for AER integrity and digit formation, whereas mutations affecting this modification are associated with Split-Hand/Foot Malformation characterized by the absence or hypoplasia of central digits ([34]; Figure 2B). Additional isoform-specific regulation by ΔNp63γ and TAp63γ has been shown to further modulate Fgf8, Fgf4, and Jag2 expression during AER growth [35] (Figure 2B). In parallel, chromatin profiling in mouse embryonic forelimbs and hindlimbs revealed that differential enrichment of H3K27ac and H3K27me3 contributes to the limb-specific regulation of Tbx4 and Tbx5 expression during limb outgrowth [17].

Transcriptional regulation during limb initiation also depends on cis-regulatory modules (CRMs) that integrate developmental signaling pathways with chromatin-mediated gene control. During early limb outgrowth, LIM homeobox 2 (Lhx2) contributes to the coordination of signaling between the AER and the zone of polarizing activity (ZPA). Lhx2 expression is regulated by limb-specific CRMs, initially identified in chick embryos and functionally tested in transgenic mice. These CRMs are characterized by H3K27ac enrichment and p300 occupancy, consistent with active enhancer states, and respond to FGF and WNT signaling through E26 transformation-specific (ETS) transcription factors and the T-cell factor/Lymphoid enhancer-binding factor transcription factor binding sites (TCF/LEF). Modulation of these regulatory elements by β-catenin further links canonical WNT signaling to enhancer-mediated control of limb initiation ([36]; Figure 2B). Pharmacological perturbation studies demonstrate that epigenetic regulation is required during early limb development; for example, in chick limb buds, inhibition of DNA methylation with 5-azacytidine reduces mesenchymal proliferation and results in hypoplastic limb phenotypes, suggesting that DNA methylation is necessary to sustain early limb growth [37]. Likewise, in mouse limbs, inhibition of class I and III HDACs disrupts limb bud morphology, induces apoptosis, and reduces histone H4K12 acetylation, highlighting the importance of histone deacetylation in maintaining progenitor cell states and regulating limb patterning processes [38].

Together, these studies demonstrate that epigenetic mechanisms actively contribute to limb field specification and early limb bud induction by establishing permissive chromatin landscapes, modulating enhancer activity, and shaping higher-order chromatin interactions that translate positional information and signaling inputs into coordinated transcriptional programs (Figure 2).

Epigenetic regulation of limb axis patterning

Tetrapod limbs exhibit a conserved proximodistal anatomical organization consisting of the stylopod, zeugopod, and autopod. The establishment of these anatomical structures depends on interconnected gene regulatory networks that integrate positional information with signaling-dependent transcriptional programs during limb patterning [39].

The earliest morphological evidence of limb development is the appearance of the limb bud [40]. During limb outgrowth, specialized signaling centers coordinate its patterning along the proximodistal (PD), anteroposterior (AP), and dorsoventral (DV) axes. The apical ectodermal ridge (AER) regulates PD outgrowth through FGF signaling, particularly FGF8 [41–44], whereas the ZPA establishes AP polarity through SHH signaling [45] and DV identity is defined by the reciprocal signaling interactions between dorsal WNT7A signaling and ventral Engrailed-1 (EN1) activity [46,47].

Importantly, the activity of these signaling centers depends on dynamic epigenetic mechanisms that stabilize positional information and coordinate region-specific transcriptional responses during limb morphogenesis. From this perspective, the establishment of the PD, AP, and DV axes can be understood as a process in which epigenetic regulation helps translate morphogenetic signals into stable, region-specific transcriptional programs.

Proximodistal patterning and limb outgrowth

Limb outgrowth and patterning along the PD axis depend on the coordinated integration of morphogen signaling, temporal regulation, and epigenetic control mechanisms that guide the progressive specification of limb segments (Figure 3A). During early limb development, reciprocal interactions between proximally derived RA signaling and FGF signaling from the AER establish positional information along the PD axis, contributing to the differential specification of the stylopod, zeugopod, and autopod. Experimental analyses in mouse and chick embryos have demonstrated that the balance between RA and FGF signaling generates two positional thresholds that regulate the sequential activation of limb-patterning genes, including distal Hoxa13 expression ([48]; Figure 3A). Importantly, Hoxa13 expression occurs only after progenitor cells reach a defined temporal state associated with chromatin remodeling, indicating that PD specification depends not only on morphogen gradients but also on epigenetic timing mechanisms. In these models, pharmacological inhibition of HDAC activity with Trichostatin A (TSA) induced premature Hoxa13 activation and resulted in the truncation of the forearm, demonstrating that histone acetylation functions as an epigenetic timer controlling the transition from zeugopod to autopod identity [48]. These findings indicate that chromatin modifications integrate temporal and positional information to coordinate sequential limb patterning along the PD axis (Figure 3A).

(B) Anteroposterior patterning is regulated by spatially distinct chromatin states at the Shh locus and its distal enhancer, the zone of polarizing activity regulatory sequence (ZRS). In non-expressing territories, repressive inputs, including HDAC2-dependent deacetylation, limit ectopic Shh activation. In the distal mesenchyme, H3K4me1 marks a poised enhancer state, whereas in the posterior mesenchyme, GABPα-dependent recruitment of p300 promotes H3K27ac enrichment and Shh activation. Positional identity is further reinforced by PRC2/EZH2-dependent H3K27me3, which restricts posterior gene expression in the anterior limb bud. (C) Dorsoventral identity is controlled by the integration of WNT7A, EN1, and LMX1/LMX1B-dependent regulatory programs. Dorsal Lmx1/Lmx1b expression is maintained through cis-regulatory modules, including LARM-associated enhancers, which interact with the Lmx1b promoter and display dorsoventral chromatin asymmetry. Coordination between signaling pathways and chromatin architecture further contributes to distal limb patterning and sustained limb outgrowth. The transcription factor LHX2 provides one example of this integration by linking FGF, WNT, and SHH signaling during distal limb development. Chromatin analyses and in vivo enhancer assays in chick embryos and transgenic mice identified two limb-specific cis-regulatory modules, LADLRM and LASARM, within the Lhx2 locus. These modules physically interact with the Lhx2 promoter within a shared topologically associating domain (TAD). These enhancers are active in the distal mesoderm adjacent to the AER and ZPA and contain ETS and TCF/LEF binding motifs responsive to FGF and WNT signaling. Functional analyses in these systems demonstrated that both pathways directly regulate enhancer activity, establishing a mechanistic link between morphogen signaling and enhancer-mediated transcriptional control [36].

Higher-order chromatin organization also contributes to limb development by maintaining transcriptional programs required for mesenchymal survival, cellular homeostasis, and tissue growth. Although CTCF binds extensively across the Hoxd cluster, conditional deletion of Ctcf in mouse limb mesenchyme revealed that CTCF is not strictly required for insulating early and late Hox regulatory domains during limb development [49]. Instead, mouse limb buds deficient in Ctcf exhibited extensive mesenchymal apoptosis, severe truncation, and a near-complete loss of skeletal elements, while the general spatiotemporal organization of Hoxd expression remained largely preserved. At the molecular level, loss of CTCF led to strong downregulation of genes involved in mitochondrial function and oxidative stress regulation, as well as of Shh, Grem1, and Jag1, which are central to distal limb outgrowth [49]. These findings indicate that CTCF contributes to limb development primarily by maintaining transcriptional programs required for cell survival, metabolic homeostasis, and tissue growth, rather than by directly organizing long-range enhancer–promoter insulation at gene loci involved in patterning.

Together, these findings indicate that PD patterning depends on epigenetic timing mechanisms that determine when limb progenitors acquire the competence to activate distal transcriptional programs. These studies also show how enhancer accessibility and chromatin topology contribute to the integration of signaling networks required for distal limb growth and patterning (Figure 3A).

Anteroposterior patterning of the limb

Patterning along the AP axis depends on the coordination of morphogen signaling, transcription factor networks, and epigenetic mechanisms that stabilize positional identity within the developing limb bud. In this context, the ZPA, located in the posterior mesenchyme, serves as the primary signaling center that controls AP polarity through SHH signaling (Figure 3B). The antagonistic activities of HAND2 and the repressor form of GLI3 establish posterior versus anterior identities and regulate the spatial restriction of Shh expression. Additional transcription factors, including HOX, PBX, and ETS proteins, further contribute to AP patterning and autopod development ([50]; Figure 3B). SHH functions as a morphogen that contributes to the specification of digit number and identity, although digit 1 is relatively independent of SHH signaling. SHH also regulates Hox gene expression involved in autopod patterning [50].

Epigenetic regulation contributes to AP patterning at multiple levels, including chromatin acetylation dynamics associated with morphogen signaling. In developing chick limbs, inhibition of HDACs with TSA altered the expression of key patterning genes, with downregulation of Fgf8 and Shh and upregulation of Bmp4 [20]. Histone hyperacetylation delayed cellular differentiation and maintained cells in an undifferentiated state, supporting the idea that HDAC activity couples chromatin acetylation to the transcriptional networks that control limb cell morphogenesis. These findings indicate that chromatin acetylation cooperates with morphogen gradients to coordinate temporal progression and positional specification during AP patterning. At the molecular level, Shh expression in the ZPA is controlled by a distal enhancer known as the zone of polarizing activity regulatory sequence (ZRS), which is necessary and sufficient for the proper spatial expression and maintenance of Shh in the limb bud ([51,52]; Figure 3B). Chromosome conformation capture carbon-copy (5C) analyses in mouse limb buds and other embryonic tissues demonstrated that the Shh promoter and ZRS reside within the same TAD and are embedded in a compact chromatin architecture in both expressing and non-expressing tissues. Although these loci remain in close spatial proximity throughout development, stable enhancer–promoter interactions become highly enriched specifically during active Shh transcription in the posterior limb bud mesenchyme and declines after Shh transcription ceases at E14.5, indicating a temporally restricted enhancer–promoter interaction [52]. Thus, transcriptional activation depends not only on genomic proximity but also on developmentally regulated chromatin interactions. The activity of the ZRS enhancer is further controlled through dynamic chromatin states. In mouse limb buds, the ZRS adopts inactive, poised, or active epigenetic configurations depending on tissue context. The poised state, characterized by H3K4me1 enrichment, is established and maintained by FGF signaling from the AER, priming the enhancer throughout the distal mesenchyme (Figure 3B). In this model, inhibition of FGF signaling abolishes H3K4me1 enrichment at the ZRS. In contrast, the active enhancer state is associated with H3K27ac enrichment mediated by GABPα-dependent recruitment of the histone acetyltransferase p300 ([53]; Figure 3B). Conversely, the transcriptional repressor ETV4, also induced by FGF signaling, recruits HDAC2 to maintain deacetylation and inhibit ectopic Shh expression outside the ZPA [53]. These studies demonstrate that enhancer activation during AP patterning depends on a finely balanced chromatin landscape that integrates activating and repressive epigenetic inputs.

ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes provide an additional layer of AP polarity regulation. In mouse limbs, the SWI/SNF complex, through its core subunit Srg3/mBaf155, is required for proper AP patterning and digit formation [54]. Loss of Srg3 disrupts both posterior activation and anterior repression of SHH/GLI3 target genes, resulting in ectopic anterior activation of Gli1 and Ptch1, together with reduced posterior responsiveness to SHH signaling. Mechanistically, Srg3-containing SWI/SNF complexes interact with GLI2 and GLI3 and modulate chromatin accessibility and H3K27me3 deposition at GLI-responsive loci (Figure 3B). Together, these findings indicate that chromatin remodeling by SWI/SNF complexes contributes to AP patterning by balancing permissive and repressive chromatin states across the limb bud [54]. Histone methylation mediated by Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) provides a further mechanism of of epigenetic stabilization during AP patterning. The catalytic subunit EZH2, responsible for H3K27 trimethylation, restricts posterior gene expression and preserves anterior identity during limb specification [55]. Loss of Ezh2 reduces H3K27me3 levels and induces ectopic anterior expression of posterior markers such as Hand2 and Hoxd11/13, while anterior genes, including Gli3, Alx4, and Tbx5, become downregulated, resulting in posteriorized limb identity. At later developmental stages, EZH2 also maintains distal limb elongation and sustains late Hox gene expression. Increased H3K9me3 deposition at Hox loci in EZH2-deficient mouse limbs correlates with premature transcriptional silencing, while transplantation experiments demonstrated that distal mutant cells retain positional competence, indicating that EZH2 contributes to the transition from specification to stable determination [55].

Complementary studies further demonstrated that PRC2-dependent chromatin repression functions independently of GLI3-mediated transcriptional control to maintain anterior limb identity [56]. Analyses of conditional Ezh2 deletion, Gli3-null mutants, and double-mutant mouse models revealed that both EZH2 and GLI3 repress SHH signaling in the anterior limb bud, thereby preventing ectopic activation of posterior genes such as Hand2 and Hoxd13. The severe anterior truncations observed in double mutants, together with the epistatic relationship between EZH2 relative to GLI3, indicate that PRC2-dependent chromatin repression provides an additional mechanism reinforcing positional identities established by morphogen gradients [56].

At the level of higher-order genome organization, chromatin topology also contributes to the robustness of AP patterning. In mouse embryonic limb tissues, the Shh–ZRS regulatory landscape forms a preconfigured chromatin domain stabilized by CTCF and cohesin, as demonstrated by integrated chromatin conformation, epigenomic, transcriptomic, and CRISPR/Cas9 perturbation analyses [57]. This preconfigured domain maintains constitutive spatial proximity between the Shh promoter and the ZRS enhancer, facilitating rapid enhancer–promoter communication upon transcriptional activation. Disruption of Lmbr1, which hosts the ZRS enhancer, or deletion of CTCF-binding motifs weakened these chromatin interactions and reduced Shh expression, leading to limb defects and severe digit malformations when combined with hypomorphic enhancer alleles [57]. In this context, higher-order chromatin organization functions as an epigenetic scaffold that confers transcriptional robustness and stabilizes spatial Shh expression in the ZPA, even in the presence of compromised enhancer activity.

The functional range of developmental enhancers adds another layer of complexity to the regulation of AP patterning. Beyond the spatial proximity provided by TAD organization, recent evidence indicates that specific cis-regulatory features can influence the genomic range over which limb enhancers act across the genome. In mouse limb enhancer-replacement and knock-in experiments, a cis-acting range extender (REX) element that expands the regulatory range of short- and mid-range limb enhancers was identified. Conserved [C/T]AATTA homeodomain motifs, enriched in long-range limb enhancers such as the Shh ZRS, are required for long-distance enhancer activity. Mutation of these motifs preserved short-range enhancer function but impaired long-range activity, leading to severe limb reduction [58].

Comparative epigenomic analyses have extended these principles across species, showing that conserved chromatin landscapes underlie homologous limb structures despite species-specific morphological adaptations. Using ChIP-seq for H3K27ac, H3K4me1, and H3K4me3 together with ATAC-seq, more than 126,000 cis-regulatory elements (CREs) were identified in mouse forelimbs and 145,000 in pig forelimbs [59]. Most experimentally validated limb enhancers were strongly enriched for H3K27ac and H3K4me1, supporting the predictive value of these epigenetic marks for enhancer activity. Although many regulatory landscapes were conserved between species, divergence was particularly evident in enhancer-like and insulator-like regions associated with AP polarity and digit reduction in pigs, suggesting that evolutionary changes in chromatin organization contribute to limb morphological diversification [59].

Together, these studies indicate that AP patterning of the limb relies on enhancer priming, long-distance enhancer competence, chromatin remodeling, and Polycomb-mediated repression to spatially restrict SHH-dependent transcriptional programs (Figure 3B).

Dorsoventral patterning of the limb

Patterning along the DV axis establishes dorsal and ventral limb identities required for the correct organization of ectodermal derivatives, connective tissues, and musculoskeletal compartments (Figure 3C). Classical embryological studies demonstrated that DV identity is established before morphological polarization of the limb bud through extrinsic signals from adjacent embryonic tissues. Chick–quail chimera and barrier insertion experiments showed that somites provide dorsalizing signals, whereas the lateral somatopleure contributes ventralizing cues. Consistent with this, manipulations that increase somitic influence generate bidorsal limbs, supporting the role of these tissues in establishing the initial DV axis [60]. Studies in chick and mouse embryos demonstrated that DV polarity depends on reciprocal interactions among dorsal ectodermal WNT7A signaling, ventral ectodermal EN1 activity downstream of BMP signaling, and Lmx1/Lmx1b-dependent dorsal mesenchymal programs ([46,47,61–63]; Figure 3C). Similar to PD and AP patterning, DV specification requires spatially restricted transcriptional programs that progressively stabilize during limb morphogenesis.

Within this developmental framework, WNT7A signaling from the dorsal ectoderm is considered to promote dorsal mesenchymal identity through the induction of Lmx1/Lmx1b expression, whereas Engrailed-1 (EN1), expressed in the ventral ectoderm, restricts Wnt7a expression to the dorsal region and contributes to ventral ectodermal identity [62,63]. However, in early mouse limb buds, Lmx1b is expressed throughout most of the limb mesenchyme. The dorsal-restricted Lmx1b expression pattern subsequently emerges through downregulation in presumptive ventral cells rather than through dorsal induction, whereas WNT7A maintains Lmx1b expression in dorsal territories [64]. In chick limb buds, ectopic expression of En1 in the dorsal ectoderm represses endogenous Wnt7a expression, reduces Lmx1 expression in the underlying mesenchyme, alters AER-associated Fgf8 and Fgf4 expression, and decreases Shh expression in the ZPA, indicating that DV patterning is functionally integrated with the signaling centers that coordinate limb outgrowth and AP patterning [63].

Although the WNT7A–EN1–LMX1B module provides the core developmental framework for DV identity, dorsal and ventral specification does not depend solely on individual transcription factors acting in isolation. Instead, DV morphogenesis emerges from the integration of epithelial–mesenchymal signaling, mesenchymal transcriptional competence, and cis-regulatory control of gene expression. In mouse limb buds, LIM-homeodomain transcription factors function cooperatively across multiple developmental axes. Simultaneous loss of Lhx2 and Lhx9 disrupts AP and PD patterning and impairs limb growth, whereas broader reduction of LMX1B, LHX2, and LHX9 activity through deletion of the shared cofactor LDB1 produces more severe phenotypes, suggesting functional overlap among LIM-homeodomain factors during limb morphogenesis [65].

Additional support for complex cis-regulatory control of LMX genes comes from transgenic enhancer analyses in zebrafish, which identified multiple conserved non-coding elements associated with enhancer-like chromatin signatures around human LMX1A and LMX1B loci [66]. Although not specifically focused on limb development, these findings support the broader principle that LMX gene expression can be embedded within complex regulatory landscapes composed of multiple tissue-specific cis-regulatory elements.

More direct evidence for enhancer-associated regulation during DV morphogenesis comes from genome-wide analysis of Lmx1b binding in mouse limbs at embryonic day 12.5. In this model, ChIP-seq experiments identified numerous Lmx1b-bound regions that overlap chromatin signatures of putative cis-regulatory modules near genes involved in extracellular matrix organization, skeletal development, vascularization, axonal guidance, and cell proliferation. Functional validation of selected elements, including regulatory regions associated with Gdf5 and Lmx1b itself, linked Lmx1b occupancy to enhancer activity during limb development [67]. These observations suggest that dorsalization involves the coordinated regulation of multiple morphogenetic programs through enhancer-associated regulatory landscapes rather than through isolated downstream targets.

Lmx1b expression is further reinforced within the dorsal compartment through two associated cis-regulatory modules, LARM1 and LARM2, located upstream of the Lmx1b locus (Figure 3C). In embryonic day 12.5 mouse limbs, ChIP-seq and Capture-C analyses identified these regions as limb-associated enhancers physically interacting with the Lmx1b promoter, supporting the existence of an autoregulatory enhancer–promoter loop. Functional analyses in homozygous ΔLARM1/2 mutant mice revealed severe defects in dorsal limb identity, including ventralized distal limbs, biventral skeletal structures, duplicated flexor tendons, absence of the patella, and loss of dorsal structures such as nails and dorsal hair, indicating that these enhancers are required to maintain dorsal limb identity. Additional experiments further showed that LARM activity depends on conserved Lmx1b-binding sites that spatially restrict enhancer activation to dorsal mesodermal territories. Chromatin profiling also revealed dorsoventral asymmetry across the LARM region, with active enhancer-associated marks enriched in dorsal cells and repressive H3K27me3 signatures enriched in ventral territories, supporting the idea that maintenance of DV identity depends on spatially restricted chromatin states and enhancer accessibility [68].

Mouse genetic studies further indicate that WNT-dependent DV signaling operates through a broader ligand–cofactor module. Using conventional and conditional alleles of Wnt7a, Wnt7b, Gpr124, and Reck, Wang et al. demonstrated that combined reductions in WNT ligand and cofactor activity disrupt distal limb growth and alter dorsal limb identity. Mutant embryos displayed reduced distal Lmx1b expression together with distal skeletal abnormalities and ectopic nail-like structures, supporting the idea that mammalian DV patterning depends on an integrated WNT7A/WNT7B–FRIZZLED–LRP5/LRP6–GPR124–RECK signaling module rather than on WNT7A alone [69].

Compared with PD and AP patterning, the direct epigenetic regulation of DV identity remains less extensively characterized. Nevertheless, current evidence indicates that DV morphogenesis depends on regulatory landscapes in which enhancer activity, cis-regulatory modules, chromatin states, and signaling responsiveness cooperate to define dorsal and ventral cellular competence (Figure 3C).

Future studies integrating single-cell chromatin accessibility, spatial transcriptomics, and targeted enhancer perturbation will be required to determine how dorsal and ventral limb territories acquire, maintain, and translate positional identity into tissue-specific morphologies during limb development.

Epigenetic regulation of skeletal lineage differentiation

The transformation of limb bud mesenchyme into the skeletal elements of the limb requires the progressive specification and differentiation of multiple cellular lineages that give rise to cartilage, bone, and synovial joints. During early limb development, mesenchymal progenitors remain competent to respond to local signaling cues that determine whether cells maintain an undifferentiated state or initiate lineage-specific differentiation programs [40]. As development progresses, these progenitor populations undergo sequential transitions, including condensation, chondrogenic commitment, hypertrophic maturation, ossification, and joint formation, ultimately generating the structural organization of the vertebrate limb.

These developmental transitions emerge from the coordinated interaction among morphogen signaling, transcriptional networks, and dynamic epigenetic regulation, which together progressively restrict cellular potential, stabilize lineage-specific transcriptional programs, and couple positional information with tissue differentiation (Figure 4A). Recent studies have begun to define the chromatin-associated regulators that mediate this coupling in limb skeletal progenitors. CXXC finger protein 1 (CFP1), a chromatin-associated regulator linked to H3K4 methylation, has been identified as an essential mediator of BMP signaling and progenitor cell differentiation during limb development. In mouse forelimb mesenchymal progenitors, loss of CFP1 maintains an immature molecular signature characterized by elevated FGF and SHH signaling and reduced BMP pathway activity, thereby impairing the onset of chondrogenesis [70].

Chondrogenic lineage specification and chondrocyte differentiation

In all vertebrates, appendicular skeletal elements originate from cartilaginous templates that subsequently undergo endochondral ossification. This process begins when mesenchymal cells condense and commit to the chondrogenic lineage through the expression of the key transcription factor SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9) together with its cofactors SOX5 and SOX6 [71]. These condensations give rise to cartilage templates that prefigure the future skeletal elements. In this context, SOX9 is indispensable for chondrogenic specification and differentiation, as mouse genetic studies have shown that its absence in undifferentiated mesenchymal cells results in a complete failure of skeletal formation, whereas Sox9 disruption in cartilage-committed progenitors leads to severe chondrodysplasia [71,72]. Throughout chondrocyte differentiation, SOX9 regulates the expression of cartilage-associated genes such as Col2a1 and Aggrecan (Acan), while simultaneously preventing premature endochondral ossification [73,74]. However, the activation and maintenance of the chondrogenic program also depend on epigenetic mechanisms that influence whether limb mesenchymal progenitors remain undifferentiated or become committed to the skeletal lineage. These mechanisms progressively stabilize lineage-specific cell identities through dynamic changes in chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation, and histone modifications.

During early limb development, progenitor cells located in the peripheral limb mesenchyme beneath the ectoderm remain undifferentiated under the influence of ectodermal signaling, whereas cells in the central core progressively acquire chondrogenic competence [40]. This transition is tightly controlled by epigenetic regulation of Sox9 accessibility and expression. In chick limb bud mesenchymal cells, WNT signaling promotes stable Sox9 repression by increasing H3K27me3 and DNA methylation at its regulatory regions, leading to irreversible silencing in cells underneath the ectoderm [75]. In the same model, FGF signaling instead preserves the chondrogenic potential of limb progenitors by preventing DNMT3A recruitment and reversing WNT-induced methylation at the Sox9 promoter, maintaining the locus in a transcriptionally permissive state [75]. Thus, the balance between WNT and FGF signaling establishes whether Sox9 repression becomes stable or remains reversible, linking morphogen signaling with epigenetic control of lineage competence. PRMT5, an arginine methyltransferase, maintains the undifferentiated state of mouse limb progenitors and protects them from BMP4-induced cell death, thereby sustaining the expression of Sox9 and other pro-chondrogenic genes during early lineage specification [76]. Once chondrogenic commitment is established, mesenchymal condensations progressively differentiate into cartilage primordia with defined size, shape, and spatial organization, prefiguring the future skeletal elements of the limb. During this process, SOX9 functions within a broader epigenetic regulatory network that coordinates chromatin remodeling and transcriptional activation of cartilage-specific genes. Several SOX9-associated co-regulators have been identified across chondrogenic cell models and skeletal tissues, including ARID5A, ARID5B, P54NRB/NONO, WWP2, PGC1-α, SMAD3, and ZNF219. These factors contribute to the epigenetic control of chondrogenic differentiation by modulating chromatin accessibility and histone modifications at target loci [77–83]. Among these regulators, ARID5B promotes chondrogenesis in mouse skeletal progenitors through direct interaction with SOX9 and recruitment of the histone demethylase PHF2 to chondrogenic promoters, including Col2a1, thereby removing the repressive histone mark H3K9me2 [80]. These findings demonstrate that SOX9-dependent chondrogenesis relies on coordinated chromatin remodeling mechanisms rather than solely on transcription factor binding.

Epigenetic regulation of the chondrogenic program also involves enhancer activation and higher-order chromatin interactions. Two chondrocyte-specific Sox9 enhancers, E308 and E160, located 308 kb and 160 kb upstream of the transcription start site, display conserved chromatin accessibility and active histone acetylation signatures across vertebrates [84]. Comparative epigenomic and functional analyses using mouse fibroblasts and genetically modified mice revealed strong enhancer–promoter interactions at these loci. These studies further demonstrated that both enhancers act synergistically to regulate Sox9 expression through Pitx1-dependent activation [84].

DNA methylation further contributes to the progression of early chondrogenesis by regulating genes involved in mesenchymal condensation, cell adhesion, and apoptosis. Pharmacological inhibition of DNA methylation with 5-azacytidine disrupts cartilage formation and decreases cartilage-specific gene expression in chicken limb bud micromass cultures by interfering with cell aggregation [85]. In contrast, the same treatment stimulates cartilage-associated gene expression in differentiated chicken chondrocytes, revealing stage-dependent effects of DNA methylation during cartilage development [86]. Similarly, overexpression of Dnmt3b in chicken limb mesodermal micromass cultures promotes Sox9 expression while repressing the tendon-associated transcription factor Scleraxis (Scx). These effects correlate with differential CpG methylation states, with Sox9 downregulation associated with promoter hypermethylation and Scx activation associated with promoter hypomethylation [87]. DNA methylation also accompanies the differentiation from cartilage primordia to surrounding skeletal-associated tissues. During early cartilage differentiation, Tbx18 expression overlaps with Sox9 in mouse limb buds and later becomes strongly associated with Col2a1-positive chondrocytes between E13.5 and E18.5, while remaining low in RUNX2-positive osteoblastic progenitors [88]. The Tbx18 promoter is hypermethylated in TBX18-negative cells but unmethylated in TBX18-positive populations, indicating that dynamic DNA methylation regulates its lineage-specific expression. Functionally, lineage-specific epigenetic regulation of Tbx18 appears to contribute to the differentiation of adjacent mesenchymal populations into perichondrial or periosteal tissues through context-dependent interactions with SOX9 and RUNX2 [88]. Overall, these observations illustrate how epigenetic regulation contributes to the diversification of neighboring skeletal-associated lineages.

Together, these findings indicate that chondrogenic differentiation involves a progressive epigenetic transition in which initially undifferentiated mesenchymal progenitors acquire stable SOX9-centered regulatory landscapes. By coordinating chromatin remodeling, enhancer activity, DNA methylation, and lineage-specific regulatory programs, these mechanisms favor cartilage identity while limiting alternative mesenchymal fates and supporting the diversification of adjacent skeletal-associated tissues (Figure 4A).

Endochondral ossification and hypertrophic maturation

The sequential maturation of cartilage templates during endochondral ossification establishes distinct chondrocyte populations organized into resting, proliferative, and hypertrophic zones [89]. Chondrocytes in the resting zone remain relatively quiescent and display a rounded morphology, whereas proliferative chondrocytes become flattened and rapidly divide to support longitudinal skeletal growth. Toward the center of the developing skeletal element, post-mitotic chondrocytes progressively enlarge and initiate hypertrophic differentiation, accompanied by increased expression of Indian Hedgehog (IHH) and a concomitant reduction in Sox9 expression [89]. The progression from proliferative to hypertrophic chondrocytes is accompanied by chromatin remodeling and changes in accessibility, which helps reinforce stage-specific gene expression programs during endochondral ossification.

Recent enhancer profiling studies further support this view by showing that early to prehypertrophic chondrocytes are characterized by stage- and region-specific cis-regulatory landscapes. Using fetal chondrocytes isolated from mouse embryos carrying a Col2a1 fluorescent regulatory sensor, Darbellay et al. identified chondrocyte-specific genes and putative enhancers active during limb and axial skeleton development. Most identified enhancers showed pan-chondrogenic activity, whereas smaller subsets were selectively associated with limb or trunk chondrocytes, indicating that skeletal maturation is accompanied by both shared and regionally specialized enhancer programs [90].

Within this regulatory context, several transcription factors, including myocyte-specific enhancer factor 2C (Mef2c), Forkhead box A (Foxa) proteins, and members of the Runt-related transcription factor family such as Runx2 and Runx3, participate in the activation of hypertrophic gene expression programs [91,92]. RUNX2 can directly promote Ihh transcription by interacting with its promoter [93], whereas the balance between Sox9 and Runx2 expression is essential for proper endochondral bone development [94]. In parallel, GLI2 and FOXC1 act downstream of IHH signaling to regulate genes associated with hypertrophy, including Pthlh, Ptch1, Col10a1, and Gli1 [95,96].

DNA methylation also contributes to the progression of hypertrophic maturation and skeletal mineralization by modulating the expression of stage-specific genes. In genetically modified mouse models, Dnmt3b ablation in chondrocytes suppresses hypertrophic maturation, resulting in reduced skeletal size and delayed bone mineralization, primarily through the downregulation of BMP signaling pathways [97]. Accordingly, DNMT3B is required for normal chondrocyte maturation and proper limb development. Additional evidence from the human fetal femur indicates that DNA methylation changes dynamically during endochondral development, as methylation levels inversely correlate with the expression of Nos2 and Col9a1, whereas progressive demethylation of the osteocalcin (Bglap) promoter accompanies the transition from fetal to adult skeletal tissues [98]. Temporal regulation of skeletal maturation is further illustrated by Tbx18-deficient mice, which exhibit severe vertebral ossification defects, whereas Tbx18 overexpression reduces skeletal length, decreases chondrocyte proliferation, and delays ossification [99], highlighting the importance of temporally regulated transcriptional and epigenetic states during skeletal maturation.

Beyond DNA methylation, histone acetylation and deacetylation constitute an additional regulatory layer that modulates chromatin accessibility during chondrocyte differentiation and hypertrophic progression. Several HAT-associated coactivators positively regulate chondrogenesis by cooperating with SOX9-containing transcriptional complexes. In chondrogenic cell models, SMAD3 enhances SOX9-dependent transcription by promoting the association of CBP/p300 with SOX9 at the Col2a1 enhancer, thereby increasing Col2a1 expression during primary chondrogenesis [78]. In a related study, p300 was further shown to cooperate with SOX9 on chromatinized DNA templates, potentiating SOX9-dependent transcription through histone hyperacetylation; consistent with this, HDAC inhibition with TSA increased Sox9-regulated cartilage matrix gene expression and histone acetylation around the Col2a1 enhancer in chondrocytes [100]. Tat-interactive protein-60 (Tip60), a member of the MYST family of HAT enzymes, regulates Sox9 expression and participates in chromatin remodeling together with SOX5 [101]. In contrast, HDAC1 and HDAC2 repress Col2a1 and Acan expression [102].

Among histone deacetylases, HDAC4 plays a particularly important role in restricting premature hypertrophic differentiation, as shown by mouse genetic studies. Hdac4 is expressed in prehypertrophic chondrocytes and inhibits RUNX2 activity, thereby preventing premature ossification [103]. HDAC4 and HDAC3 also interact with RUNX2, MEF2, and the corepressor ZFP521 to regulate hypertrophic progression [104], while HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3, and HDAC7 modulate Mmp13 expression during matrix remodeling [105]. Genetic evidence further supports the epigenetic role of HDAC4 in skeletal maturation: ectopic Hdac4 overexpression inhibits hypertrophy and ossification, producing a phenotype that resembles Runx2 loss of function [103,106], whereas Hdac4-null mice display premature hypertrophy and accelerated bone formation similar to that observed with constitutive Runx2 expression [103]. These findings position histone deacetylation as a critical checkpoint controlling the transition from proliferative to hypertrophic chondrocytes.

Histone methylation also contributes to the stabilization of hypertrophic and osteogenic transcriptional states during endochondral ossification. One major repressive mechanism is H3K27me3 deposition catalyzed by EZH2, the catalytic subunit of PRC2. In osteogenic differentiation models, EZH2 plays essential roles during mesenchymal lineage commitment and osteoblast differentiation [107]. Although conditional deletion of Ezh2 in osteoblast precursors does not disrupt gross skeletal patterning, it significantly reduces bone volume due to impaired osteoprogenitor proliferation in young mice [108], indicating that EZH2 supports expansion of osteogenic progenitors during early bone formation. Another histone methyltransferase, SETDB1, an H3K9-specific histone methyltransferase, transiently accumulates in prehypertrophic chondrocytes and regulates growth plate maturation. Conditional Setdb1 deletion accelerates hypertrophic differentiation, reduces IHH protein levels, and impairs long bone growth and trabecular bone formation [109]. Mechanistically, SETDB1 likely cooperates with RUNX2 and HDAC4 to repress RUNX2 target genes through H3K9 methylation-dependent chromatin repression.

Together, these findings indicate that epigenetic regulation functions as a maturation checkpoint during endochondral ossification, controlling when chondrocytes exit the proliferative state, initiate hypertrophic differentiation, and acquire osteogenic competence. By coordinating DNA methylation, histone acetylation and deacetylation, and repressive histone methylation with RUNX2-, MEF2C-, and IHH-dependent programs, these mechanisms help prevent premature hypertrophy while enabling the sequential progression toward skeletal mineralization (Figure 4A).

Synovial joint formation

Chondrocytes within developing skeletal elements undergo divergent differentiation programs that lead either to hypertrophic maturation or synovial joint formation. The earliest morphological indication of joint development is the formation of the interzone, a specialized mesenchymal structure composed of three cell layers, including two densely compacted layers interconnected through connexin 43-mediated cell communication [110]. Joint induction occurs at presumptive articulation sites within the branched cartilage condensations, where cells progressively transition from a rounded to a flattened morphology and activate the expression of interzone-associated genes [111–113].

The initiation of interzone marker expression coincides with the downregulation of Col2a1 and Sox9, indicating that suppression of the chondrogenic differentiation program is required for joint specification. As development progresses, cavitation occurs within the center of the interzone. Whether cavity formation results from apoptosis of central interzone cells or from hyaluronan-mediated extracellular matrix remodeling remains unresolved [114–117]. Cells within the interzone and adjacent regions progressively differentiate into articular cartilage and other synovial joint components [118,119], while chondrocyte hypertrophy is locally suppressed to preserve the articular phenotype [110,120].

Although evidence for epigenetic regulation of synovial joint formation remains more limited than for chondrogenesis or hypertrophic maturation, cis-regulatory and epigenomic studies are beginning to define how joint-associated transcriptional programs are spatially organized. The Gdf5 locus, one of the earliest markers of joint formation, provides a central example of joint-associated cis-regulatory control. In mouse transgenic enhancer assays, modular regulatory elements distributed across the Gdf5 locus were shown to control expression in distinct synovial joints, including proximal and distal limb joints [121]. These findings indicate that joint identity is partly encoded by spatially restricted enhancer modules rather than by a single uniform joint regulatory element. This modular enhancer logic is further supported by functional analysis of a Gdf5-associated regulatory region during elbow development, where Sox, Fox, and Lmx1b binding sites differentially modulate enhancer activity, suggesting that joint-associated expression depends on combinatorial cis-regulatory inputs that integrate chondrogenic, dorsoventral, and joint-specific information [122].

Direct epigenomic evidence for joint-associated regulatory landscapes has been provided by enhancer mapping during early interphalangeal joint formation in embryonic chick limbs. A stage-specific atlas of candidate enhancers in the interzone and adjacent phalangeal tissue was generated using ChIP-seq for H3K27ac and H3K4me1 to identify active and primed regulatory elements associated with early synovial joint formation. Integration of these enhancer maps with previously defined transcriptomic profiles linked interzone-associated enhancers to joint-forming genes such as Gdf5, Bmp2, and Dact2, whereas phalangeal enhancers were preferentially linked to cartilage and extracellular matrix-related genes such as Matn1, Hapln1, and Snai1. These findings indicate that the separation between joint-forming and chondrogenic territories is accompanied by tissue-specific enhancer landscapes [123]. The maintenance of regulatory competence in articular cartilage also involves chromatin accessibility at zone-specific loci. In mouse superficial zone articular chondrocytes, chromatin accessibility analyses combined with ChIP assays demonstrated that CREB5 occupies promoter-proximal open chromatin regions of Prg4, a key marker of superficial zone articular cartilage. Additional experiments further showed that these regulatory elements cooperate with a distal enhancer to mediate TGF-β-responsive expression of Prg4/lubricin, indicating that maintenance of the superficial articular cartilage phenotype depends on accessible cis-regulatory elements that confer competence for zone-specific transcriptional activation [124].

DNA methylation provides an additional regulatory layer during articular cartilage differentiation and maintenance. In mouse articular cartilage and chondrocyte models, DNMT3B regulates postnatal cartilage homeostasis by modulating metabolic pathways, including mitochondrial activity and cellular respiration. Loss of Dnmt3b compromised cartilage homeostasis, whereas Dnmt3b gain of function exerted chondroprotective effects in mouse articular chondrocytes in vitro and in vivo [125]. Additional studies using genetically modified mouse models have shown that DNMT3B also regulates chondrocyte differentiation, hypertrophic maturation, and skeletal mineralization through mechanisms involving BMP signaling and tissue remodeling [97,126].

Epigenetic silencing of hypertrophic genes appears to be particularly important for maintaining the phenotype of articular chondrocytes. Unlike hypertrophic chondrocytes, articular chondrocytes typically do not express Col10a1 and therefore do not undergo terminal maturation. In human articular cartilage, the Col10a1 promoter is highly methylated, whereas the transcription start site of Col2a1 remains unmethylated, indicating that selective DNA methylation contributes to repression of hypertrophic differentiation while preserving cartilage-specific gene expression [127]. This gene-specific view has recently been expanded by methylome profiling of human fetal knee cartilage. In 72 samples collected between 7 and 21 post-conception weeks, McDonnell et al. quantified DNA methylation at approximately 700,000 CpG sites and identified extensive developmental remodeling, including more than 8,200 differentially methylated regions. These data suggest that stabilization of the articular chondrocyte phenotype involves coordinated DNA methylation landscapes rather than isolated regulation of individual hypertrophic or cartilage-specific genes [128]. Recent human multi-omic analyses have begun to define the regulatory architecture of embryonic joint formation at higher cellular and spatial resolution. Integration of single-nucleus transcriptomic and chromatin accessibility profiling with spatial transcriptomics identified spatially organized progenitor zones and cell-type-specific regulatory networks during human bone and joint formation, providing a reference for investigating how interzone-derived and joint-associated cell states are established during early synovial joint development [129].

Synovial joint formation and articular cartilage maturation involve the progressive separation of joint-forming and chondrogenic territories through cis-regulatory, chromatin accessibility, and DNA methylation-dependent mechanisms (Figure 4A). Although functional epigenetic evidence in vivo remains limited, current data support an association between tissue-specific regulatory landscapes and the establishment of interzone identity, articular cartilage stability, and suppression of hypertrophic maturation.

Taken together, these studies indicate that skeletal element formation depends on the integration of morphogen signaling, lineage-specific transcriptional programs, and chromatin-based regulation. Across chondrogenesis, joint formation, and endochondral ossification, epigenetic mechanisms stabilize lineage-specific gene expression programs and modulate the competence of mesenchymal progenitors to adopt distinct skeletal fates. This balance between differentiation and tissue remodeling is particularly evident in the interdigital mesenchyme, where epigenetically regulated programmed cell death contributes to sculpting the final morphology of the limb. The following section examines how chromatin dynamics, DNA methylation, and histone modifications, and chromatin remodeling participate in this process.

Epigenetic regulation of programmed cell death and tissue remodeling

Programmed cell death (PCD), together with cell proliferation and differentiation, is an essential process for sculpting the embryonic limb. Classical studies in avian and mammalian embryos identified several regions of massive cell death. These domains occur in distinct spatial and developmental contexts, including early cell death areas within the limb bud and later interdigital regression associated with digit separation. Among these, the interdigital necrotic zones (INZs) contribute to digit separation. The anterior and posterior necrotic zones (ANZs and PNZs) are associated with digit reduction in avian species, and the opaque patch (OP) separates the skeletal elements of the zeugopod [130]. Beyond these well-recognized areas, PCD also participates in sculpting joint interzones [131], the refinement of muscle bellies and myotendinous junctions [132], and the controlled regression of the AER [133]. Although these processes share common signaling modules, they differ in tissue context, developmental timing, and cellular competence, making it important to distinguish limb bud-associated PCD from interdigital tissue regression.

The molecular control of PCD during limb morphogenesis depends on the interplay among major signaling pathways that act in a context-dependent manner across these distinct cell death domains. BMP signaling has been considered a primary inducer of PCD because it can directly trigger cell death in undifferentiated mesenchyme [134,135], its action inhibits AER-derived FGF8 signaling, which maintains survival of the mesenchyme beneath the AER. Thus, BMP signaling promotes PCD in part by antagonizing the survival-promoting activity of FGF8 in the mesenchyme underlying the AER [44,134,136,137]. WNT/β-catenin signaling maintains the undifferentiated state and participates in the molecular cascade that induces Fgf8 expression. In contrast, its antagonist Dickkopf-1 (DKK1) is upregulated in dying regions and is required for PCD induction [134,138]. In parallel, RA promotes Bmp expression, and BMP signaling subsequently inhibits FGF8 signaling, thereby promoting apoptosis in the interdigital region [136,137,139,140]. The establishment of specific PCD domains, such as the ANZ, depends on negative feedback loops within the FGF-BMP-WNT network [141]. Collectively, these signaling networks establish the spatial and temporal framework within which epigenetic mechanisms regulate cellular competence for survival, differentiation, and tissue regression during limb morphogenesis.

Most mechanistic epigenetic evidence for PCD during limb development comes from studies of the interdigital mesenchyme, particularly in chicken and mouse models. Apoptosis has historically been considered the main mechanism of PCD in the limb. Recent evidence demonstrates that interdigital PCD operates within a complex epigenetic landscape that defines the cellular competence to respond to morphogenetic cues. In this context, the interdigital mesenchyme has provided a particularly informative model for understanding the epigenetic control of PCD.

Developmental signals establish epigenetic domains that render interdigital chromatin intrinsically fragile and predispose interdigital cells to DNA damage, thereby promoting PCD through apoptosis or senescence [142]. The role of DNA methylation in PCD depends on developmental stage and is intricately linked to differentiation programs. While DNA methylation promotes apoptosis in the interdigital tissue, it is also required for the initial condensation events that precede chondrogenesis. In the chicken limb, the methyltransferase DNMT3B promotes PCD by hypermethylating the Sox9 promoter, thereby repressing chondrogenesis, and by hypomethylating the promoters of Bak1, a pro-apoptotic gene, and p21, a pro-senescence gene. These methylation changes define a pro-death epigenetic landscape in the interdigital mesenchyme, supporting the view of interdigital cell death is not a classical ‘suicide’ program, but as a collapse of undifferentiated progenitors with fragile chromatin when exposed to differentiation and apoptotic cues such as BMP signaling [87,143].

In chicken autopods, DNA methylation dynamics are crucial for establishing the divergence between chondrogenic differentiation and programmed cell death [85,87]. In this model, altering DNA methylation with 5‑azacytidine influences the balance between chondrogenesis and apoptosis, leading to ectopic digit formation and changes in the methylation status of pro-chondrogenic gene promoters, including Sox9. DNA methylation during digit development prevents mesodermal cells from undergoing apoptosis and promotes their recruitment toward the cartilage lineage. By contrast, in the interdigital tissue, increased DNA methylation promotes apoptosis and represses ectopic chondrogenesis. Sustained hypomethylation in the interdigital region activates pro-chondrogenic genes and suppresses Raldh2, leading to ectopic digit formation [85]. These observations suggest that DNA methylation dynamics may act as a developmental switch governing the balance among survival, chondrogenic differentiation, and apoptosis, thereby establishing the boundary between chondrogenic and interdigital territories during digit formation (Figure 4B). Consistent with these findings, Uhrf1, Uhrf2, and Dnmt3b are specifically expressed in the interdigital region before tissue remodeling. Functional experiments in chick limbs demonstrated that UHRFs modulate DNA methylation and the expression of key fate determinants such as Sox9 and Bak1, thereby inhibiting chondrogenesis and promoting apoptosis and senescence in skeletal progenitors [144].

Building on classical genetic models, evidence has revealed that the cell death commitment is not solely transcriptional but is also epigenetically encoded through chromatin-associated regulatory mechanisms. In mouse models, AKIRIN2, a nuclear protein that links transcription factors with chromatin remodeling complexes, is required in the limb ectoderm for downregulating Fgf8. When AKIRIN2 is deleted in the ectoderm, Fgf8 downregulation fails, apoptosis is reduced, and mesenchymal proliferation increases, resulting in soft-tissue syndactyly. This finding identifies an epigenetic regulator as a crucial non-autonomous mediator connecting extracellular signals to the transcriptional reprogramming required for interdigital regression [145]. Epigenetic regulation of PCD converges with lineage-specific transcriptional networks that execute the apoptotic program downstream of morphogenetic signaling. In the chick limb, the heterodimer formed by MAFB and cJUN contributes to apoptosis in the interdigital mesenchyme and ectoderm by directly activating p63 and p73, which mediate cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis, illustrating how transcriptional effectors downstream of BMP signaling contribute to the execution of PCD [146]. Moreover, genetic evidence in mouse models has demonstrated that Msx1 and Msx2 genes act as key apoptotic effectors downstream of the SHH/GLI3 pathway. In the absence of Shh, dysregulation of Msx expression promotes excessive apoptosis in the distal limb. In contrast, under normal developmental conditions, MSX, together with the repressor form of GLI3, mediates anterior apoptosis to prevent preaxial polydactyly, thereby positioning these genes within a core genetic regulatory circuit that controls digit number [147].

The execution of this epigenetically regulated cell fate is fine-tuned at the post-transcriptional level by microRNAs (miRNAs). Comprehensive profiling of the interdigital mesenchyme in the chick embryo revealed a dynamic miRNA signature during the stages of massive PCD. Notably, upregulated miRNAs such as the miR-181 family, miR-451, and miR-21 have been associated with promoting apoptosis, senescence, and inflammation, whereas anti-apoptotic miRNAs such as miR-92 and miR-222 are downregulated. This miRNA landscape provides an additional post-transcriptional regulatory layer, targeting components of the apoptotic pathway, TGF-β signaling, and extracellular matrix remodeling, thereby contributing to the cellular response to the cell-death-permissive epigenetic landscape [148].

Histone modifications further refine this epigenetic control of cell death. In chick interdigital cells, a distinct chromatin signature is characterized by high levels of H3K9ac and H4ac, together with the presence of the histone variant H3.3. The physiological relevance of HDAC activity was demonstrated in this model, where TSA treatment induced massive cell death, senescence, and DNA damage in the interdigit, effects that were partially rescued by NOGGIN and FGF2. These findings suggest that HDAC inhibition triggers physiological PCD pathways [149]. This mechanistic connection between HDAC inhibition, hyperacetylation, and apoptosis has also been observed in mammalian models. In mouse limbs during mid-organogenesis, the HDAC inhibitor valproic acid (VPA) induced hyperacetylation of p53 at Lys379, leading to transcriptional activation of pro-apoptotic p53 targets and markers of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway, ultimately resulting in limb malformations [38]. Similarly, the teratogen 2-methoxyacetic acid (2-MAA) inhibits HDACs in mouse limb buds in vitro, causing hyperacetylation of H3K9 and H4K12, and p53, followed by cell-cycle arrest and caspase-3-dependent apoptosis [150].

Together, these findings indicate that interdigital regression is not simply a terminal degenerative process, but an actively regulated morphogenetic event guided by epigenetic mechanisms that define cellular susceptibility to apoptosis or senescence. In the interdigital mesenchyme, DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, and post-transcriptional control establish a permissive regulatory state that links morphogen signaling with tissue remodeling, ensuring that PCD and limb morphogenesis proceed with spatial and temporal precision (Figure 4).

Emerging concepts and future directions in limb epigenetics

One of the major challenges in studying limb development is integrating the multiple molecular and cellular processes that underlie morphogenesis into a coherent mechanistic framework. The complexity of regulatory landscapes, in which chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, DNA methylation, and enhancer activity dynamically interact, frequently obscures causal relationships between gene regulation and morphological outcomes (Figure 5A). Addressing this issue requires linking high-resolution epigenomic data with classical developmental models and functional analyses in vivo.

Spatiotemporal integration of epigenetic regulation during limb development. (A) limb development is presented as a continuous and stage-dependent process in which sequential morphogenetic events, including limb field specification, limb induction and initiation, axis patterning, skeletal lineage differentiation, and programmed cell death, emerge from the dynamic integration of multiple epigenetic layers over time. DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin accessibility, higher-order chromatin organization, gene transcription, and protein activity operate in a coordinated and combinatorial manner, generating temporally regulated chromatin environments that guide gene expression programs involved in pattern formation, cell fate determination, tissue remodeling, and morphogenesis. (B) epigenetic modifications and transcriptional regulation form a reciprocal regulatory network in which chromatin states influence gene expression, while transcriptional activity, signaling inputs, and cellular context feed back to reshape the epigenetic landscape. This bidirectional regulation helps explain how transient developmental signals can be translated into stable or progressively restricted cell states during limb morphogenesis.

One approach is to explore how epigenetic regulatory mechanisms shape transcriptional competence within the organizing centers that control patterning along the three embryonic axes of the limb bud. Such analyses could reveal how transcriptional competence in limb progenitor populations is modulated by core developmental signaling pathways. These mechanisms ultimately contribute to the establishment of positional identity and segmental boundaries during morphogenesis. An alternative and complementary strategy involves aligning epigenetic transitions with key developmental stages, including limb initiation, early specification, skeletal lineage differentiation, and digit formation (Figure 5A). This stage-focused perspective may help clarify how transient chromatin states encode developmental timing and influence the sequential emergence of morphological features.

The comparative analysis of conserved limb gene regulatory networks (GRNs) across diverse species provides an additional opportunity to deepen our understanding of how morphological diversification during tetrapod evolution arises from coordinated changes in gene regulatory activity, chromatin organization, and enhancer function. Although the basic anatomical architecture of the limb is conserved, the size, shape, and number of skeletal elements vary across species, leading to diverse appendicular structures, from fins to wings and arms. Cross-species epigenomic comparisons therefore have the potential to reveal how modifications in chromatin architecture and enhancer activity contribute to evolutionary phenotypic variation and to the regulatory mechanisms underlying congenital malformations. In this context, limb development represents a uniquely powerful model for understanding spatial and temporal epigenetic regulation, as it offers a system in which the relationships between gene expression and morphology are well documented and the cellular and molecular processes underlying pattern formation have been extensively characterized. However, this system also presents specific challenges, as different experimental approaches often target distinct embryonic stages, complicating the integration of results. At the same time, modern multi-omics technologies generate large-scale datasets that reveal novel transcriptional variants, enhancers, and chromatin features, but these datasets still require integrative analytical approaches together with rigorous in vivo validation to establish their functional relevance during limb morphogenesis. Human multi-omic skeletal atlases further illustrate how combined transcriptomic, chromatin accessibility, and spatial datasets can link chromatin states to tissue-level organization in vivo, providing a comparative framework for relating mechanisms inferred from chick and mouse models to human developmental trajectories [129].

Across developmental stages, the reviewed evidence reveals a set of recurrent principles through which epigenetic regulation coordinates developmental timing, positional information, lineage specification, tissue remodeling, and morphogenetic outcomes during limb formation (Table 1). These principles include chromatin competence, enhancer priming, higher-order genome organization, DNA methylation-dependent fate restriction, histone modification dynamics, chromatin remodeling, and post-transcriptional regulation. Together, they suggest that epigenetic regulation does not merely accompany limb development but actively shapes how spatial and temporal information is interpreted, stabilized, and translated into cell fate decisions and tissue-level morphogenesis (Figure 5B). Thus, understanding how transient chromatin states encode developmental timing and positional information remains one of the central challenges in developmental epigenetics. Although derived from limb regeneration rather than embryonic limb development, recent work in axolotl provides a relevant conceptual parallel for understanding positional memory, where genome-wide chromatin profiling of mature and regenerating connective tissue cells revealed segment-specific H3K27me3 patterns at homeobox gene loci, suggesting that chromatin states can encode positional memory beyond embryonic development [151].

Future studies should address how enhancer–promoter interactions establish positional memory during progenitor differentiation, whether local chromatin remodeling events predict morphogenetic competence, lineage commitment, or apoptosis, and how three-dimensional chromatin organization contributes to limb diversification across species. Addressing these questions will require integrating single-cell epigenomics, spatial transcriptomics, and advanced imaging approaches to map gene regulatory dynamics across developmental time and space. Conceptually, these processes can be viewed as a continuous and integrated epigenetic landscape in which multiple regulatory layers converge to guide gene expression programs and cell fate decisions throughout development. The limb, traditionally considered a paradigm in developmental genetics, remains an invaluable model for understanding the epigenetic principles that govern morphogenesis and patterning (Figure 5; Table 1).

Conclusion

Vertebrate limb development provides a powerful experimental model for investigating how signaling pathways, transcriptional networks, and epigenetic regulation are integrated to coordinate morphogenesis across space and time. Although the genetic and molecular basis of limb patterning has been extensively characterized, the contribution of chromatin-based regulation to limb morphogenesis remains comparatively less understood, particularly in vivo. Current evidence indicates that chromatin accessibility, enhancer activity, histone modifications, DNA methylation, and higher-order genome organization contribute to the progressive restriction of developmental potential, the stabilization of lineage-specific transcriptional programs, and the temporal control of gene expression during limb formation.

Importantly, epigenetic regulation does not operate independently of classical morphogenetic pathways, but rather modulates how progenitor cells interpret positional cues, acquire competence, and transition toward specific developmental fates. In this context, changes in chromatin organization and cis-regulatory landscapes may help explain how conserved developmental programs generate evolutionary variation in limb morphology.

Future studies integrating single-cell epigenomics, spatial transcriptomics, chromatin conformation analyses, advanced imaging, and functional in vivo approaches will be essential to establish causal relationships between chromatin dynamics and morphogenesis. By combining classical developmental biology with emerging epigenomic technologies, the vertebrate limb will continue to serve as a uniquely informative system for understanding how genetic and epigenetic regulatory mechanisms interact to generate robust and reproducible developmental outcomes.
