Vibrational and Structural Properties of Aqueous H2SO4 and Na2SO4 Systems from Ambient to Supercritical Conditions: A Comparative Study between GGA(-D3) and r2SCAN Functionals
Authors: Rajorshi Chattopadhyay, Sandro Jahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aqueous fluids containing sulfur-bearing species, such as sulfates and bisulfates, are important in several domains such as aqueous geochemistry, atmospheric chemistry, and medical sciences. For modeling their structural and thermodynamic properties, aside from empirical interatomic potentials, models developed using large data sets and machine learning have become extremely popular in recent years. Often, these data sets are produced by simulations using density-functional theory (DFT), and their quality depends on the approximations used, in particular the chosen exchange and correlation (XC) functional. Here, we determine the structural and vibrational properties of aqueous H2SO4 and Na2SO4 solutions from ambient to supercritical conditions from ab initio molecular dynamics using different XC functionals: BLYP, BLYP-D3, revPBE-D3 and r2SCAN. The latter three produce similar molecular and hydration shell structures of aqueous fluids from ambient to supercritical conditions. r2SCAN predicts vibrational frequencies closest to experimental data, but at significantly higher computational cost. Densities and self-diffusion coefficients at ambient conditions are largely consistent with experimental data except for the BLYP functional where larger deviations are observed. Overall, r2SCAN performs best, however, at largely increased computational cost, whereas both dispersion-corrected (D3) functionals offer a good balance between chemical accuracy and computational efficiency. Our results can serve as a guide for selecting the most suitable DFT method in large-scale data-driven projects for studying aqueous fluids from ambient to supercritical conditions.

Introduction

Aqueous sulfate-bearing solutions are important in several fields of chemistry , and geochemistry. , It plays a very important role in determining mineral dissolution, precipitation as well as crystal growth in ambient to supercritical geo-fluids. , Fluid inclusion experimental studies show that inorganic ligands like sulfate and bisulfate play an important role in the mobilization, transport and precipitation of strategically important elements. Sulfate in seawater acts as an important oxidizing agent and influences the redox budget of the Earth. Records of sulfur and oxygen isotopes in seawater are used in paleo-environment detection like sedimentary, riverine or microbial systems. Molecular-scale simulations provide a framework for interpreting bulk fluid properties such as speciation and vibrational spectra from atomic–scale interactions. In recent years, tremendous progress in computational resources coupled with massively scalable algorithms has made data-driven interaction potentials based on machine learning (ML) models extremely popular for studying aqueous fluids. , Despite lacking a physically motivated functional form, these models show transferability by using large data sets to obtain an accurate representation of the potential energy surface. The predictive capabilities of these ML-driven interaction potentials strongly depend on the quality of the underlying data set used to train them. Training data sets should include all thermodynamic states relevant to the intended application of the potential.

Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations based on density-functional theory (DFT) are often used to generate such data sets of aqueous fluids due to the reasonable trade-off between accuracy and computational cost. The predicted fluid properties are critically dependent on the choice of the exchange and correlation (XC) functional and on the particular physical property being investigated. It is well-known now that although generalized gradient approximation (GGA) functionals such as PBE provide a reasonable description of short-range interactions, they suffer from delocalization or self-interaction errors, resulting in inaccuracies in medium- and long-range dispersion interactions. This leads to a low density, slower self-diffusion, and overstructuring of pure water at ambient temperature and pressure. − The shortcomings in the description of dispersion and weak interactions can be counteracted by the addition of dispersion corrections, e.g. using the DFT-D3 method of Grimme et al. (GGA-D3), or by adding some percentage of exact exchange that results in improvements in the overall structure and density of pure water. However, the inclusion of dispersion corrections can worsen the structural properties of aqueous solutions, e.g., the agreement between the simulated and experimental Na–O distances.

Fluid properties obtained from AIMD simulations are also dependent on the temperature (T) and pressure (P) conditions of the simulations. For example, at elevated temperature and pressure, Fowler and Sherman report excellent agreement between GGA-based calculations and experimental bulk densities of NaCl solutions. While more sophisticated XC functionals incur higher computational costs, they do not necessarily yield improved accuracy due to varying degrees of error cancellation. For example, the strongly constrained and appropriately normed (SCAN) functional (which is a meta-GGA functional) gives better estimates of the infrared spectrum of liquid water and also predicts more accurate ion-oxygen distances in aqueous solutions − under ambient conditions compared to GGA functionals. However, it predicts overstructured water compared to experiments and the revPBE-D3 functional. Using more sophisticated functionals such as hybrid-GGA may give better predictions but are so expensive that even with state-of-the-art hardware they are restricted to short simulations with very few atoms. A systematic study of the predictive capacities of different XC functionals for the system being studied under a wide range of T/P conditions is necessary before large-scale data sets are generated with them.

Although some previous studies have used DFT to study the vibrational properties of hydrated anions in the gas phase , and sulfur-bearing anions in aqueous solutions, − a comprehensive understanding of the effects of different XC functionals on the calculated physical properties from ambient to supercritical conditions is lacking in the existing literature. In this article, we examine the performance of different XC functionals, BLYP, , BLYP-D3 (Grimme’s dispersion corrections), revPBE-D3 and r2SCAN (version of the SCAN functional with enhanced numerical efficiency), in calculating structural and vibrational properties of aqueous H2SO4 and Na2SO4 solutions across a wide range of temperatures. We also provide pressure estimates and self-diffusion coefficients at the studied conditions and compare them between the different XC functionals and to available experimental data.

Computational Methods

AIMD simulations of 1.12 mol/kg aqueous sulfuric acid solutions are performed in 3D periodically replicated simulation boxes. The concentration and temperatures of the simulations are chosen to match the experimental conditions of Schmidt and Seward thereby allowing direct comparison with the results of our simulations. Two different simulation boxes are created: box #1 containing 100 H2O, 2 H2SO4 molecules and box #2 containing 50 H2O, 1 H2SO4 molecule. The edge lengths of the simulation boxes are adjusted so that the density ρ ∼ 1.0 g/cm3. Subsequently, the simulations are carried out along this isochore to match the experimental conditions of Schmidt and Seward. Simulations of Na2SO4 solution are also performed under isochoric conditions with boxes prepared by replacing H2SO4 molecules with Na2SO4 in boxes #1 and #2 resulting in densities of 1.05–1.11 g/cm3. The DZVP basis sets along with the Goedecker–Teter–Hutter pseudopotentials are used for all species. A plane wave cutoff of 1000 Ry is used for simulations with the GGA­(-D3) functionals, whereas 1200 Ry is used for simulations with the r2SCAN functional. In order to calculate stress tensors accurately, a convergence cutoff of 10–7 Hartree is used for the self-consistent field calculations. The time step for the simulations is set to 0.5 fs. We run production AIMD simulations in the NVT ensemble that is at constant number of particles (N), volume (V) and temperature (T) using the Nosé-Hoover thermostat with a time-constant of 100 fs for at least 30–50 ps after 4 ps of initial equilibration. The simulations are performed at ambient conditions, 473 and 673 K for each XC functional along the respective isochores.

Several DFT-based studies of liquid water at ambient conditions have used slightly higher temperatures ranging from 320 K–360 K. , This helps to alleviate the sluggish dynamics leading to nonergodic behavior at lower temperatures while having negligible effects on neighbor distances, coordination numbers and vibrational spectra. The target temperatures for ambient conditions are set to 320 K for the GGA­(-D3) functionals and 330 K for the r2SCAN functional, consistent with previous studies of pure water with the respective XC functionals. ,, Boxes #1 and #3 are used for the simulations with the GGA­(-D3) functionals, whereas boxes #2 and #4 are used for simulations with the r2SCAN functional to compensate for the higher computational expense at all studied temperatures. All simulations are performed with the Gaussian plane wave methodology using the CP2K simulation package.

Structural properties, vibrational properties and stress tensors are derived from the production trajectories. We provide a detailed description of the mathematical formulations of vibrational spectra below, whereas those of the pair correlation functions, H-bond survival probability, self-diffusion coefficients and the calculation of isotropic pressure from the stress tensor are provided in the Supporting Information.

Angular brackets indicate an average over all initial times t′. F(ω) of eq describes the complete molecular vibrational density of states (VDOS) that includes translational, rotational and long-range acoustic contributions, making band assignment difficult. Therefore, the spectroscopically distinct contributions must be isolated from the complete VDOS. This can be achieved using mode projection analysis.

The mode projection analysis is based on the observation that high frequency modes of molecular species such as SO4 2– remain normal mode-like in aqueous solution. A speciation change, e.g., the formation of HSO4 –, results in a distinct change of the vibrational mode frequencies. To uncover these high frequency modes in different environments, the total VDOS is distributed among various quasi-normal modes (QNMs). The QNMs of a group of atoms are calculated by taking the FT of the autocorrelation function of velocities projected onto respective QNM directions, v QNM i . The latter replace vi⃗ in eq .

Projection of atomic motions onto QNMs isolates the spectroscopically distinct contributions because the quasi-local vibrations behave like the molecular normal-modes based on symmetry. For example, the four Raman-active normal modes of SO4 2– tetrahedra having T d symmetry can be approximated with four QNMs with degeneracies: symmetric stretch (υ1(a 1)), asymmetric stretch (υ3(f 2)), symmetric bend (υ2(e)), asymmetric bend (υ4(f 2)) where a 1, e, f 2 denote totally symmetric, doubly degenerate and triply degenerate modes respectively. In the first step of mode decomposition, for each molecular dynamics frame the atoms of individual sub units are grouped together (in our case the S and O atoms of SO4 2– or HSO4 –). Velocities of individual S atoms are then subtracted from velocities of each of the four oxygen atoms of SO4 2– or HSO4 – to eliminate the low-frequency translation modes. In the third step, velocities of individual atoms are decomposed along QNMs strictly based on symmetry. Finally, FT of VACF of the projected velocities gives the frequency of the QNMs. Here, we use the analysis code developed by Spiekermann et al. that has been successfully used, e.g., to assign frequencies to different molecular vibrations in SiO4-bearing melts and glasses , and more recently for SO4-bearing aqueous fluids.

In order to assess convergence of the calculated properties with respect to simulation time, the production trajectories are divided into two chunks, and the structural, vibrational and transport observables are calculated from each of them. The variabilities in the pair correlation functions and mean square displacements are shown in Figures S13–S28 and Figures S30–S37 respectively, in the Supporting Information, whereas that in the vibrational spectra is shown as error bars in the respective plots presented in the next section. To check for possible finite size effects, pair correlation functions and frequencies of the different vibrational modes are recalculated with the BLYP functional using the smaller simulation box (with the same dimensions as the box used for simulations with the r2SCAN functional). The results shown in Figure S29 and Table S6 indicate negligible effects of the simulation box size on the calculated properties.

Structural and Vibrational Properties

For the description of the structural and vibrational properties, we use the following abbreviations for the different types of atoms in the remaining text: Ssulfur, Owoxygen atom of a water molecule, Oboxygen atom bonded to a sulfur atom, Hbhydrogen atom bonded to Ob, Hother hydrogen atoms. Distance based criteria are used to classify species as sulfate, bisulfate or sulfuric acid in each simulation snapshot. Oxygen atoms at distances to S shorter than the sum of the covalent radii of S and O atoms (∼1.8 Å) are considered bonded to S (Ob). Four bonded O atoms per S atom are observed at all studied conditions for both systems. In the next step, each H atom is assigned to be bonded to its nearest oxygen atom allowing classification of species as sulfate, bisulfate or sulfuric acid. Another algorithm to obtain this classification is to search for H atoms within a preset distance cutoff of ∼1.3 Å around each Ob atom. Both approaches give very similar speciation data. An example of the comparison between the two algorithms is shown in Table S2.

Sulfuric acid follows a two-step dissociation process in aqueous solutions 3 H2SO4⇌HSO4−+H+ 4 HSO4−⇌SO42−+H+

While the first dissociation reaction is almost complete, equilibrium is established between sulfate and bisulfate at ambient conditions. The XC functionals predict negligible amounts of undissociated acid at ambient conditions (numerical data is shown in Table S1), consistent with previous experimental and computational work, in which undissociated acid was only observed at concentrations higher than 80 wt %. − Bisulfate remains the most abundant species at all temperatures studied for all XC functionals, in agreement with experimental studies with the same sulfuric acid concentration. The degree of dissociation of the bisulfate ion at room temperature obtained from our simulations is ∼5%, which is significantly lower than ∼30–35% predicted by Raman studies. However, the fraction of SO4 2– decreases rapidly with temperature and already at 100 °C it is almost disappeared in the Raman spectra of aqueous H2SO4 solutions. Simulations with the smaller simulation box #2 and the BLYP functional confirm the small fraction of dissociated bisulfate ions (∼4%) at ambient temperature.

As finite size effects seem to have a small effect on the degree of dissociation, the rather large discrepancy between simulations and real systems may arise from the absence of nuclear quantum effects (NQEs) in AIMD simulations, particularly for the light H atoms. , Although NQEs are known to have a rather small impact on Ow–Ow pair correlation functions and self-diffusion coefficients in pure water, they do influence the length of O–H bonds and the strength of hydrogen bonds by allowing the hydrogen atom to be shared (delocalized) between the oxygen atoms of hydrogen-bonded pairs of water molecules. Assessing the role of NQEs in determining hydrogen bond structure is challenging because it depends on the chemical system, thermodynamic conditions and involves two opposing effects that act concurrently. , The use of slightly higher temperatures at ambient conditions results in better estimates of the degrees of dissociation. However, as shown in Pestana et al., this acts as an ad-hoc estimator of NQEs , and may severely diminish the agreement of these ab initio models with experiments.

Inherent approximations to the XC functionals can also affect the degree of dissociation. Disentangling the two effects would require simulations treating the nuclei as quantum particles, which is beyond the scope of this study. Since atomic mobilities, particularly with the BLYP functional, are very low at ambient conditions (discussed later), simulations on the picosecond time scale may not adequately sample the configurational space leading to systematic deviation of calculated degree of dissociation from experimentally determined values.

In Figure , we present the S–Ow pair correlation functions, g SOw(r), obtained with the different functionals. The functionals predict S–Ow neighboring distances of ∼3.7 Å at ambient conditions and ∼3.8 Å at elevated temperatures. This result is in good agreement with the distance calculated with PBE0 hybrid GGA functional. At ambient conditions, the BLYP functional shows an overstructured hydration shell indicated by the high second maximum and low second minimum (Figure , left). This usually happens due to the self-interaction or delocalization error in BLYP functional as discussed earlier. Inclusion of Grimme’s dispersion corrections or using the r2SCAN functional alleviates the overstructuring of the hydration shell at ambient conditions, as shown in Figure (left). At higher temperatures, g SOw(r) of all different functionals show good qualitative agreement with each other. Similar agreement among all the functionals is also seen for the aqueous Na2SO4 system (Figure S6). The assessment of g OwOw(r) (Figures S7 and S8) reveals similar qualitative and quantitative trends for both systems. The BLYP functional yields an overstructured hydration shell at both ambient conditions and 473 K, as evidenced by its smaller size (∼3.2 Å) compared to the other three XC functionals. Notably, the two GGA-D3 functionals also yield discrepant hydration shell sizes at ambient conditions indicated by the different first minima of the corresponding g OwOw(r) functions. BLYP-D3 and r2SCAN functionals predict minima at ∼3.5–3.6 Å compared to ∼3.9–4.0 Å with the revPBE-D3 functional. However, these discrepancies are resolved at higher temperatures, leading to a good agreement of g OwOw(r) between GGA-D3 and r2SCAN for both systems. All the functionals predict Ow–Ow nearest neighbor distances of ∼2.8 Å consistent with experimental data.

Further insights into the structure of the coordination shell are provided by gObH(r) shown in Figure . The first peak corresponds to the H atom that is chemically bonded to the Ob atom of the bisulfate ion. The Ob-H bond length is ∼1.02 Å for all XC functionals at all studied temperatures. This value agrees well with previous simulations with the BLYP functional and is slightly higher than predictions at the Hartree–Fock (HF) level in the bulk phase. The second peak corresponds to the H atoms that are hydrogen bonded to the bisulfate ion. This peak is consistently observed at ∼1.8–1.9 Å with all XC functionals and is slightly smaller than the distance calculated at the HF level. However, as the temperature increases, this peak becomes broader and more diffused.

The nearest neighbor distances are consistently predicted at ∼1.75–1.80 Å at ambient conditions and ∼1.80 Å at higher temperatures, within the range of experimental data. Complexation of the sulfate ion by the Na+ cation can be determined from g SNa(r) shown in Figure S9. At ambient conditions, the BLYP functional predicts an S–Na coordination number of ∼2.5 whereas the other three functionals yield values of around 1.5–2.0. As shown in Figure S16, g SNa(r) from the BLYP functional is fairly well-converged in the time scale of the simulation. Nevertheless, the BLYP functional is known to underestimate water diffusion, which may severely limit configurational sampling in a 50 ps simulation. Incomplete configurational sampling at ambient temperature may also be an issue for simulations with the other XC functionals, which would result in large uncertainties of the S–Na coordination number. At 473 K all functionals show coordination numbers around one. At 673 K, BLYP and r2SCAN functionals predict ∼1 Na+ in the coordination shell, whereas BLYP-D3 and r2SCAN exhibit values > 1.5, which is consistent with a tendency of ion pair formation at high temperatures.

The strength of the H-bonds between HSO4 – ions and water molecules in aqueous H2SO4 solution can be qualitatively measured from their survival probabilities, as shown in Figure . Good qualitative and quantitative agreement of H-bond strength obtained with BLYP-D3, revPBE-D3 and r2SCAN functionals indicate that they yield similar hydration-shell structures across the temperature range. High survival probability at ambient conditions with the BLYP functional indicates strong and directional H-bonds due to delocalization of the proton, as compared to the other three functionals. Since the bisulfate ion is dominant in aqueous H2SO4 solutions at all studied temperatures, the dissociated H+ ions exist as hydronium (H3O+) ions. The lifetimes of these ions are approximately 0.5 ps for all XC functionals. These ions form H-bonds with water molecules or the bisulfate ion. The distribution of O–HO bond angles and the corresponding O–O distances are shown in Figure . The H-bond angle distribution for the hydronium–water interactions displays a single peak at all temperatures, while hydronium–bisulfate interactions exhibit a bimodal distribution with peaks around 170°and 100°. All functionals predict average Ow–HO angles of approximately 170° at ambient conditions, which decreases with increasing temperature. Regarding the Ow–Ow distances, all functionals yield broadly similar distributions peaked at ∼2.45 Å. In contrast, the Ob–Ow distance distributions are considerably wider, spanning approximately 2.4–3.5 Å. This broader distribution is reflected in the corresponding angle distribution, which exhibits two distinct peaks: the peak near 170° associated with shorter Ob–Ow distances and another near 100° corresponding to larger separations. Distributions of S–H3O+ minimum distances are shown in Figures S38–S41. S–H3O+ distances depend moderately on both the XC functional and temperature. At ambient conditions, r2SCAN predicts the largest separation (6.86 Å), whereas the other functionals yield values in the range 5.5–6.0 Å. With increasing temperature, the predicted distances generally decrease to values close to or slightly above the approximate S–O hydration-shell width of ∼5 Å. At 673 K, all functionals predict minimum distances between 5.4 and 6.1 Å, suggesting that hydronium ions preferentially reside near the outer boundary of the sulfur hydration shell.

In general, r2SCAN predicts higher wavenumbers of the υ4, υ1 and υ3 modes of HSO4 – compared to the GGA­(-D3) functionals. Among the GGA functionals, the following trends are observedυ4, BLYP ≈ υ4, BLYP‑D3 ≈ υ4, revPBE‑D3, υ1, BLYP ≈ υ1, BLYP‑D3 < υ1, revPBE‑D3 and υ3, BLYP ≈ υ3, BLYP‑D3 < υ3, revPBE‑D3. For the SO4 2– ion in aqueous Na2SO4 system, the υ1 and υ3 modes exhibit similar trends (Figure S10). The r2SCAN functional yields the highest wavenumbers among all functionals whereas revPBE-D3 yields the highest wavenumbers among the GGA­(-D3) functionals. Apart from the υ3 mode of HSO4 – ion at elevated temperatures calculated with revPBE-D3 and r2SCAN functionals, all functionals predict lower frequencies compared to the experimental data in both systems. The underestimation of the υ4 mode is ∼100 wavenumbers with the GGA­(-D3) functionals, and ∼80 wavenumbers with the r2SCAN functional. For the υ1 vibrational mode of both ions, BLYP and BLYP-D3 yield frequencies approximately 100 cm–1 below experiments, revPBE-D3 shows a smaller deviation of about 60 cm–1 whereas r2SCAN shows an even smaller systematic shift of around 40 cm–1. In case of the υ3 mode of HSO4 – ion, frequency underestimations with BLYP and BLYP-D3 are approximately 100 cm–1 at ambient conditions and about 25 cm–1 at elevated temperatures. The revPBE-D3 (r2SCAN) functional yields frequencies that are about 50 cm–1 (15 cm–1) lower than experiments at ambient conditions while at elevated temperatures predicted values lie about 15 cm–1 (35 cm–1) above the experimental values. Data obtained at the HF level of theory for bulk phase show rather large positive deviations from experiments (see Figure ). Notably, scaled vibration frequencies obtained at the HF level can exhibit improved agreement with experimental data. However, for the purpose of this study such scaling is not desirable as development of data-driven models relies on (unscaled) energies and forces from the DFT simulations.

Qualitative assessment of the vibrational spectra reveals that all the functionals reproduce the systematic redshift of the υ4 mode with increasing temperature, in agreement with experimental data. This mode of the bisulfate ion is of particular interest because the frequent exchange of the H atom between the solute and solvent makes it a probe for solute–solvent interactions. Increase in temperature weakens the S–ObHb bond as evidenced by the increase in corresponding bond length with temperature (Figure ). Consequently, the weakened S–ObHb bond leads to a redshift of the associated vibrational mode. This negative correlation between the S–ObHb bond length and wavenumber of the υ4 mode explains why HF predicts higher frequencies than DFT.

S–ObHb bond lengths obtained from the different functionals as a function of temperature (values obtained with the BLYP functional are almost equal to those obtained with the BLYP-D3 functional at all temperatures and to those obtained with the revPBE-D3 functional at 473 and 673 K). The error bars represent fluctuations in bond lengths due to thermal vibrations.

None of the functionals considered in this study reproduces the temperature-induced blueshift of the υ1 mode of HSO4 – ion, as seen in experiments. A possible reason for the constant υ1 mode may be the constant S–Ob bond lengths in the HSO4 – ion (1.48–1.49 Å) predicted by all functionals across all temperatures. Shorter S–Ob bond lengths (∼1.45 Å from HF vs 1.48–1.49 Å from DFT) at ambient conditions lead to data from HF simulations being higher than DFT. This correlation between bond lengths and wavenumbers is also seen in the case of the SO4 2– ion in aqueous Na2SO4 solution (Figures S10 and S11).

Conclusions

At ambient conditions, GGA­(-D3) and r2SCAN functionals yield discrepant g SOw(r) and g OwOw(r) for the aqueous H2SO4 and Na2SO4 systems. Excessive structural ordering in BLYP simulations results in too low self-diffusion coefficients of water at ambient conditions. This is largely mitigated by the inclusion of dispersion corrections. At higher temperatures, all the functionals predict similar pair correlation functions and similar hydration shell structures for both systems. The addition of semiempirical dispersion corrections produces no significant effects on the pair correlation functions at elevated temperatures. All functionals predict a low degree of dissociation of the bisulfate ion at ambient conditions. This can be attributed to the lack of nuclear quantum effects in our simulations, incomplete configurational sampling and/or shortcomings of the XC functionals. Although the XC functionals show similar qualitative temperature-induced shifts in the vibrational spectra, systematic quantitative deviations of the frequencies with respect to experimental data are noted for both systems. The smallest differences to experimental data are observed for the r2SCAN functional. Overstructuring results in too low self-diffusion coefficients for the BLYP functional at low temperatures, whereas the GGA-D3 and r2SCAN functionals yield results largely consistent with each other and with experimental data. Similarly, pressures obtained from simulations with the BLYP functional at ambient temperature differ by more than 10 kbar from experimental reference data, whereas those of the other three functionals agree within 3 kbar.

The choice of XC functional to be used for data set generation should depend on the research question and the available computational resources. As shown in Table , the computational expenses of simulations with the GGA­(-D3) functionals are ∼4–5 times lower than those with the r2SCAN functional. Both GGA-D3 functionals offer a good balance between chemical accuracy and computational efficiency, with molecular and hydration shell structures very similar to r2SCAN at ambient as well as elevated temperatures for both systems. The dispersion corrections have negligible effects on the structural observables at elevated temperatures and do not increase the computational expense. Even if GGA functionals such as BLYP (without dispersion correction) may be sufficient if only high temperature structural properties are to be investigated, considering dispersion corrections improves the range of applicability. If more accurate vibrational frequencies or densities are needed close to ambient conditions, r2SCAN should be the XC functional of choice. These factors should be considered for the efficient use of computational resources in large-scale data-driven projects.
