Implementation of Integrated Behavioural Health in Outpatient Cardiology Care for Patients with Injection Drug Use–Associated Infective Endocarditis
Authors: Elizabeth Charron, Bijay Rimal, Brandt Wiskur, Feross Karrit, Frieda Freeman-Cooper, Nelson Iván Agudelo Higuita, Michael Brand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe described the feasibility of implementing integrated behavioural health (IBH) care in an outpatient cardiology clinic for patients with injection drug use–associated infective endocarditis (IDU-IE). From 2021-2024, a total of 141 patients with IDU-IE were evaluated in the clinic. Universal behavioural health screening was completed for 78 patients (55.3%). Of those screened, 23 (29.5%) received a behavioural health assessment and consented to receive IBH care. Among them, 21 (91.3%) received at least one psychiatric consultation, and all 23 (100%) received a brief behavioural health intervention. IBH care for patients with IDU-IE was feasible to implement in an outpatient cardiology clinic.Keywords: infective endocarditis, injection drug use, integrated behavioural health, collaborative care model, multidisciplinary care

Globally, the incidence of infective endocarditis (IE) has sharply increased in the past 3 decades.1 Injection drug use (IDU) is a major driver of the growing number of IE cases in North America.2 In the US, the proportion of IE cases attributed to IDU increased from 15.3% in 2010 to 29.1% in 2015.3 Patients with IDU-associated IE (IDU-IE) have a high risk of death, with 1-year mortality rates ranging from 16% to 26%, and a long-term postoperative mortality rate exceeding 50%.4 Poor long-term survival persists despite valve surgery, which suggests that short-term surgical success fails to produce durable benefit without concurrent addiction treatment and social-care interventions.5

Consequently, effective management of IDU-IE requires coordinated care from cardiology, infectious diseases, cardiac surgery, addiction medicine, case management, and harm-reduction services working across inpatient and outpatient settings.6 A recent meta-analysis reported a 39% lower risk of death among IDU-IE patients managed with multidisciplinary care, compared with usual care.7 Nonetheless, existing multidisciplinary programs have been primarily hospital-based, focused on perioperative management and infection control.8, 9, 10 Incorporating these team approaches into outpatient settings is essential to reduce reinfection and hospital readmission rates and improve long-term outcomes among individuals with IDU-IE.

The collaborative care model (CoCM) is an evidence-based, team-oriented framework for integrating behavioural health into medical settings.11 This approach involves a treating clinician, a behavioural healthcare manager, and a psychiatric consultant who collaborate through a shared care plan to manage behavioural health conditions when usual care is insufficient.12 Although the CoCM has been tested extensively in primary care11 and adapted for certain specialty areas, such as oncology and neurology,13,14 it has not been implemented previously in cardiology or applied to IDU-IE. This study therefore aimed to describe the feasibility of implementing integrated behavioural health (IBH) care based on the CoCM within an outpatient cardiology clinic. As an exploratory secondary objective, we assessed mortality among patients with IDU-IE who did vs did not receive IBH care.

Methods

Setting

This study was conducted in the US at the University of Oklahoma (OU) Health Heart Valve Clinic, an outpatient program that evaluates and manages complex cardiac and valvular conditions, including IDU-IE. The clinic serves patients both within and outside the OU Health system across Oklahoma without a referral requirement. Care is delivered by a multidisciplinary team comprising cardiologists, cardiothoracic surgeons, and nurses specializing in valve disease.

Implementation

Before June 2021, the OU Health Heart Valve Clinic infrequently assessed patients with IDU-IE for mental health or substance use disorders; psychiatric consultation was uncommon, and the multidisciplinary team lacked social work or psychiatric personnel to provide behavioural health screening and intervention. In addition, primary care was not integrated into patient management. Mortality among clinic patients with IDU-IE averaged 32%. To address these gaps, the clinic implemented IBH care based on the CoCM in June 2021. A clinical social worker and a psychiatrist were added to the team. Nurses conducted universal screening for depression, anxiety, and alcohol use disorder using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9),15 the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) test,16 and the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT),17 respectively. Patients who screened positive completed a behavioural health assessment with the social worker and were invited to consent to receive IBH care. The CoCM team, comprising the treating clinician, social worker, and psychiatrist, collaboratively developed individualized care plans and treatment goals with each patient. The clinical social worker provided ongoing case management and brief evidence-based interventions, including psychoeducation, family engagement, brief cognitive-behavioural therapy, and crisis management. The psychiatrist managed psychiatric and addiction pharmacotherapy and guided care-plan adjustments through monthly consultations. The CoCM team regularly communicated with primary care providers to coordinate patient care and follow-up.

Data and measures

Clinical staff involved in IBH care implementation entered data into a secure database to monitor program delivery. Measures used to assess the feasibility of delivering IBH care included the proportion of patients who completed universal behavioural health screening, the proportion who underwent a behavioural health assessment, and the proportion who received psychiatric consultation and brief behavioural health interventions. These measures were made during the implementation period from June 2021 to December 2024.

Patient characteristics and mortality were identified through retrospective chart review. In collaboration with the OU Health Clinical Research Informatics team, we extracted data on patients with IDU-IE who were evaluated in the OU Heart Valve Clinic during the study period. IDU-IE cases were identified from provider documentation within clinical notes, and patients were categorized by receipt of IBH care. Extracted variables included the following: age in years; sex (male, female); race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, other races and ethnicities); marital status (never married, other [married, divorced, separated, or widowed]); and comorbidities included in the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), a validated measure of mortality risk that assigns weighted scores to 17 conditions associated with 1-year mortality (Supplemental Table S1).18 We calculated a total CCI score for each patient and categorized comorbidities as severe (≥ 5) or less than severe (< 5). Mortality was defined as all-cause mortality occurring within 12 months after the first encounter with the patient for IDU-IE.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to analyze feasibility measures, sociodemographic variables, comorbidities, and mortality. We compared characteristics and mortality between patients who received IBH care and those who did not, using χ2 tests or Fisher exact tests (for cell counts ≤ 5) for categorical variables and t tests for continuous variables. To explore mortality, we applied propensity-score matching using 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching without replacement and a caliper of 0.2 to adjust for differences in baseline characteristics between the IBH and non-IBH groups.19 We evaluated balance between groups using standardized mean differences, with values < 0.1 indicating acceptable balance (Table 1).20 We estimated the risk ratio (RR) for mortality using matched data with robust variance estimators to obtain 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Analyses were conducted in SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). This study was reviewed and approved by the OU Institutional Review Board.

Results

Between June 2021 and December 2024, a total of 141 patients with IDU-IE were evaluated in the OU Heart Valve Clinic. Universal behavioural health screening was completed for 78 patients (55.3%). Of those screened, 23 (29.5%) received a behavioural health assessment and consented for IBH care. Among the 23 patients who entered IBH care, 21 (91.3%) received at least one psychiatric consultation, and all 23 (100%) received a brief behavioural health intervention.

Patients who received IBH care (n = 23) were younger than those who did not (mean [standard deviation] age, 50.9 [12.9] vs 57.6 [15.9] years), and they were less likely to have severe comorbidities (CCI ≥ 5; 30.4% vs 54.2%; Table 1). Sex and race and ethnicity distributions were similar between the groups. In the unmatched analysis, mortality was 13.0% (3 of 23) among patients who received IBH care and 15.3% (18 of 118) among those who did not. After propensity-score matching, receipt of IBH care was directionally associated with a 25% lower risk of mortality, compared with no receipt of IBH care; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Table 2; RR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.21-2.66).

Discussion

IBH care for patients with IDU-IE was feasible to implement in an outpatient cardiology clinic. To our knowledge, this study is the first to apply the CoCM to the care of patients with IDU-IE and to describe its real-world implementation in an outpatient cardiology clinic.

We identified several important lessons from our implementation. Universal behavioural health screening proved the most challenging component and was impeded by 2 transitions in medical leadership, multiple staff turnover events, and a change in corporate ownership that disrupted clinic operations. Busy schedules and competing clinical demands further limited consistent screening. We addressed this challenge by embedding screening into routine nursing intake and assigning a staff member to monitor screening completion rates. Patient tracking and data collection were complicated by the use of 2 separate electronic health record systems during the implementation period. Despite these operational challenges, the program was low-cost, requiring only the addition of a clinical social worker.

IBH care was associated directionally with a lower mortality risk, compared with the absence of IBH care. This finding is consistent with prior evidence indicating that multidisciplinary care reduces mortality among patients with IDU-IE.7 Although the observed results were clinically meaningful, they were not statistically significant, due to the limited sample size and the small number of deaths. The direction and magnitude of estimates suggest a possible survival benefit from integrating behavioural health into cardiology care and support the need for further investigation in larger studies. Another limitation is that the study was conducted at a single academic medical centre, which may limit its generalizability. In addition, the potential exists for residual confounding in exploratory analyses, given that we did not have data on clinical factors that influence mortality, such as the nature and severity of the condition.

In conclusion, our experience indicates that IBH care is feasible to implement in an outpatient cardiology setting, to support management of patients with IDU-IE. This approach aligns with current practice guidelines endorsing a multidisciplinary team approach for IDU-IE management.21 Our findings warrant continued evaluation of IBH care integration in cardiology settings, to improve patient care and assess the potential of IBH care to reduce mortality among patients with IDU-IE.
