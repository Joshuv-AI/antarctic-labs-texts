Hypercoagulability and vascular proinflammatory activation promote cardiac-cerebral fibrinogenesis in a rodent model of Chagas disease
Authors: Subhadip Choudhuri, Nisha Jain Garg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCardiomyopathy and stroke are major complications of Chagas disease (CD). We investigated if hypercoagulability, endothelial cell (EC) dysfunction, and blood stasis cause cardiac-cerebral fibrinogenesis in CD. C57BL/6 mice infected with Trypanosoma cruzi (Tc) were evaluated at acute (AT), indeterminate (IT), and chronic (CT) stages of CD. Mice were given anti-parasite, bicistronic immunogens (BCV or BCVR) to determine the role of Tc in fibrinogenesis. We monitored (1) platelet and coagulation cascade activation; (2) cardiac-cerebral blood vessels injury; (3) proinflammatory/prothrombotic phenotypes of microglia, macrophages, and vascular cells; and (4) fibrinogenesis. After a quiescent acute phase, plasma levels of coagulation and other factors of platelet activation/aggregation, hypercoagulability, and clotting capacity were increased in IT–CT mice. Proinflammatory and prothrombotic cytokines/chemokines in cardiac-cerebral blood vessels were also significantly increased in IT–CT mice. Loss of cardiac EC and a marked increase in their proinflammatory/cell-adhesion response were not compensated for by a pro-angiogenic/wound-healing response in cardiac-cerebral tissues of infected mice. Microglial proinflammatory activation preceded the vascular inflammation suggesting a feedback cycle of EC activation in brain of infected mice. Treatment with BCV/BCVR controlled the parasite persistence and hypercoagulability/vascular inflammation in infected mice. Importantly, BCV/BCVR treatment abolished the fibrin clots that otherwise were pronounced in heart and brain of IT–CD mice. We conclude that acute Tc infection triggered a subdued hemodynamic disorder, and the persistence of low-grade parasites contributed to a cardiovascular/cerebrovascular proinflammatory and prothrombotic response and fibrin deposition in mice. BCV/BCVR offer potential immunotherapies for reducing the recurrent clot formation and risk of stroke in CD.

Introduction

Trypanosoma cruzi (Tc) is the causative agent of Chagas disease (CD). It is estimated that 8–10 million individuals are infected with T. cruzi, and >75 million people are exposed to risk of infection in endemic countries in the American continent [1,2]. In general, infected individuals develop the acute stage (AT) characterized by widespread parasite dissemination and replication, to which the host responds with a robust innate and adaptive immune response. Nonetheless, low-grade parasites and associated inflammation continue in the indeterminate stage (IT) that lasts for decades. Eventually, CD patients develop the chronic disease stage (CT), presenting a wide range of echocardiographic abnormalities due to myocardial damage [3]. Liver steatosis and neurocognitive impairments also manifest in CD [4,5]; yet only cardiomyopathy and heart failure are managed clinically [6].

Hemostasis refers to a delicate balance between procoagulant and anticoagulant factors for maintaining the blood flow within the vascular compartments. In the event of vascular injury, endothelial cells (EC) produce von Willebrand factor that signals adhesion and aggregation of platelets and plug formation. Consequently, proteolytic cleavage and activation of coagulation factors of intrinsic (F8, F9, F11, and F12), extrinsic (F3 and F7), and common (F5 and F10) pathways lead to prothrombin/fibrinogen (F2/F1) activation and fibrin (F1a) deposition for strengthening the initial clot [7]. Disturbances in procoagulant/anticoagulant factors can cause bleeding or prothrombotic disorders. In chronic CD, blood flow stasis, which can occur due to cardiomyopathy, atrial arrhythmia, and left ventricle (LV) dysfunction, is recognized as a key contributor to blood clot formation [8]. Yet, Tc and inflammation may also create an environment conducive to hypercoagulability and thrombus formation. A recent review of literature summarized that an increase in vascular congestion and plasma levels of P-selectin and prothrombin fragment (F1 + 2) enhanced the risk of systemic, cardiac, and brain thromboembolic events in CD patients in the IT stage when cardiomyopathy is not detectable [9,10]. Prevalence of transient ischemic stroke was higher in infected individuals than in the general population of Brazil [11], and cerebral infarction was directly linked with >50% deaths of CD patients [12]. It can thus be projected that disturbances of the coagulation cascade are likely the first clinically relevant sign of CD.

Drivers of hypercoagulability that sustain thromboembolic events in CD are not known, though previous reports from others and us provide some clues. One, Tc infection of platelets and EC provides primary cellular input for the activation of coagulation factors at the site of vascular injury [13]. Secondly, Tc invasion induced iCa2+ flux, mitochondrial superoxide production, and oxidant/antioxidant imbalance in the murine and human myocardium [14]. Consequently, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), e.g. oxidized mtDNA of Tc and host, were increased in the circulation of the infected host, and these DAMPs signaled cyclic GMP-AMP synthase and nuclear factor kappa B (NFκB) dependent proinflammatory activation of macrophages [15]. Considering that secreted inflammatory molecules can initiate coagulopathy [16], these studies indicate that Tc invasion and resultant DAMPs may provoke the blood coagulation cascade in CD.

Building on the above literature, we propose that the ability of Tc to cause intravascular thrombosis is a distinct hematologic property that can affect multiple organ systems. Hypercoagulability, vascular endothelial dysfunction, and changes in blood stasis are the key regulatory elements of Virchow’s triad that drive hemostatic disorder and thrombosis [17]. We hypothesized that low-grade parasites and the inflammatory process that persists in infected individuals contribute to thrombosis initiation, progression, and recurrence through coagulation cascade interactions with immune and endothelial cells. To test this, we used a murine model of Tc infection mimicking the three phases of human CD. Briefly, C57BL/6 mice challenged with Tc (SylvioX10 strain) develop the AT stage of parasite replication during 21–45-days post-infection (pi). Tc infection is controlled, but not eliminated by the immune system, and mice enter the IT stage of low-grade tissue parasites and inflammatory/oxidative stress that can last up to 6-months. The CT stage of cardiomyopathy and LV dysfunction is detectable during 6–9-months pi [18]. To develop anti-parasite immunogens, mouse codon-optimized sequences for Tc antigens (TcG2 and TcG4) were cloned into a nanoplasmid without (BCV) and with (BCVR) RIG-I agonist that is known to improve adaptive immunity [19]. TcG2 and TcG4 antigens are small in size, conserved and expressed in clinically relevant parasite isolates of all lineages (I–VI), and recognized by type 1 CD8+T cells (essential for killing intracellular parasites) in multiple infected hosts [20,21]. Mice were infected with Tc and euthanized at three stages of CD. Mice given a bicistronic immunogen before challenge infection were included to determine the role of Tc in CD fibrinogenesis. We utilized cutting-edge biochemical, molecular, and multiplex immunofluorescence assays to test how Tc infection satisfies the three arms of Virchow’s triad and increases the risk of intravascular thrombosis with relevance to cardiac and neurological manifestations in CD.

Methods

All animal experiments were conducted by following the NIH guidelines for housing and care of laboratory animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Welfare Office (PHS assurance: D16-00202/A3314-01, Protocol: 08-05-029). Detailed methods and major resources are described in Supplementary file 1.

Experimental model

Tc trypomastigotes (SylvioX10 strain) were propagated in C2C12 myoblasts. Trypomastigotes were released from infected cells at 5–7 days post-incubation and collected by centrifugation of the cell supernatant at 4000xg for 8 min. C57BL/6 female mice (n = 5–6/group, 8-weeks-old, Jackson Laboratory) were challenged with Tc (10,000-trypomastigotes/mouse, intraperitoneal). For some studies, mice were immunized with two doses (10 μg each, 21-day interval, intramuscular) of BCV or BCVR that express the bicistronic TcG2/TcG4 antigen with or without RIG-I agonist, respectively, and then challenged with Tc. Controls were mock-infected/mock-treated. Infected (and matched controls) were euthanized at 21-, 90-, and 150-days pi, corresponding to AT, IT, and CT stages of CD progression, respectively. Mice treated with BCV/BCVR before challenges were euthanized at 150-days pi to monitor BCV/BCVR efficacy in preventing key prothrombotic events in chronic CD.

Coagulation factors

Commercially available kits were used according to the instructions provided by the manufacturers to measure plasma levels of platelet activation markers and coagulation factors by ELISA. Activities of the coagulation factors (F9a, thrombin, and plasmin) were determined by fluorometric and colorimetric assays. Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), and international normalized ratio (INR) were assessed using commercial kits, and clot formation time was recorded using a stopwatch.

Multiplex immunofluorescence assay

Formalin-fixed 5-micron tissue sections were deparaffinized and microwaved for antigen retrieval. Tissue slides were subjected to multiplex immunofluorescence staining using the Opal 4-Color Manual IHC kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. Slides were imaged by using an Olympus IX51 fluorescence microscope, and overlay images were analyzed using QuPath software. Each tissue-section was analyzed in 9-microscopic fields, and average percentages of different cell populations were calculated based on total DAPI-positive cells (n = 5 mice/group, 2 slides/tissue/mouse).

Parasite burden

Total DNA (12.5 ng) was isolated from brain and heart tissues of infected and mock-infected groups and used as a template for qPCR amplification of Tc18SrDNA and murine Gapdh sequences, and relative parasite burden was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Luminex 21-plex assay

Blood vessels were isolated from brain and heart tissues of mice, and soluble lysates were analyzed using a custom-designed mouse Luminex 21-plex Panel (R&D Systems).

Histology

Paraffin-embedded, 5-micron tissue sections were stained with Martius Scarlet Blue (MSB). Slides were imaged as above, and fibrin (red) distribution was scored as 1–4, representing 1–10%, 11–25%, 25–50%, and >50% of the scanned area, respectively.

Data analysis

All experiments were conducted twice, using 5–6-mice/group (at least duplicate analysis/sample). Datasets were managed in Excel and analyzed using Prism v.10.4.2 (GraphPad) software. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test was performed to check the normal distribution of data. Statistical significance was analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test or Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s post-hoc test. Data are presented as mean value ± standard error mean (SEM), and significance was accepted at a p-value of <0.05.

Results

Hemostatic disorder at different stages of CD progression

Hemostasis is a complex process involving vasoconstriction, platelets, and coagulation factors, which assist stable blood clot formation at the vascular injury site and then degradation of extra clots after vessel healing. We examined if (and when) the hemostatic process is dysregulated during Tc infection and disease progression (Figure 1, Table S1). Plasma levels of P-selectin (mediates rolling/adhesion of platelets at injury site), platelet factor 4 (PF4, promotes plug formation, neutralizes anti-coagulant effects of heparin), and glycoprotein VI (GPVI, binds collagen to support platelet aggregates) that together indicate platelets activation were increased by 1.78–1.97-fold, 1.27–1.68-fold, and 1.29–2.05-fold, respectively, in IT and CT (vs. AT, control) groups (Figure 1(a–c)). Coagulation factors of intrinsic (F12, F9/F9a) and extrinsic (F3) pathway, as well as thrombin (F2/F2a), fibrinogen (F1), and F13/F13B that strengthen and stabilize the platelet-initiated clot were also measured (Figure 1(d–l)). Except for thrombin (F2: 2.92–4.25-fold, F2a activity: 0.54–0.72-fold), which was increased in AT–IT groups, all other markers of the coagulation cascade were increased by 0.48–5.15-fold in IT–CT groups as compared to control mice. Anti-thrombin anticoagulant (inactivates some clotting factors) was increased by 1.5–2.83-fold in IT–CT (vs. control) mice (Figure 1(m)). Pro-coagulant events are neutralized by the fibrinolytic system, which involves plasminogen cleavage to plasmin to degrade excess fibrin. Several enzymes, e.g. tissue and urokinase plasminogen activators (tPA/uPA), plasminogen activator inhibitor, and α2-antiplasmin, regulate plasmin activity. Plasma levels of plasminogen, plasmin activity, and D-dimer (fibrin breakdown product) were increased by 3.67–5.38-fold, 0.96–1.86-fold, and 20.26–30.51-fold, respectively, in IT–CT (vs AT, control) groups (Figure 1(n–p)).

APTT and PT determine the intrinsic and extrinsic activation of coagulation cascade, respectively. APTT was decreased by 26–30% in IT–CT groups, and PT was decreased by 25% in CT mice (vs. controls) (Figure 1(q–r)). INR (normal range: 0.8–1.4, bleeding risk: >1.4, clotting risk: <0.8) monitors extrinsic/common pathways, and it was significantly decreased in CT group (Figure 1(s)).

Together, these results suggest that platelet’s activation and aggregation prompted an increase in the levels and activities of coagulation factors of intrinsic, extrinsic, and common pathways that enhanced the fibrin formation and stabilization in progressive CD. Despite an increase in anti-thrombin and plasmin, the risk of coagulopathy and thrombosis was increased in IT–CT stages of CD. Considering that the coagulation cascade was not activated in AT mice, and Tc-dependent extrinsic events (i.e. F3 and PT) were increased in the IT stage, it is likely that Tc-induced DAMPs (e.g. pro-inflammatory molecules) provided stimulus for the activation of primary and secondary hemostatic disorders in CD. A noticeable decline in PT, APTT, and INR in CT (vs. AT or IT) group supported shortened clotting time and increased clotting capacity in chronic CD.

Endothelial inflammation and injury in myocardium of CD mice

Cardiomyopathy and LV dysfunction contribute to blood stasis and consequent clot formation in chronic CD [8]. To determine if Tc-induced inflammation promotes thrombosis in early stages of CD when myocardial structural and functional changes were undetectable, we examined the frequencies of vascular endothelial (EC, CD31+) and smooth muscle (SMC, TAGLN+) cells expressing CCL2 (inflammatory activation), VCAM1 (leukocyte recruitment/adhesion), Ki67 (proliferation), and VEGF (wound-healing/angiogenesis) in infected mice (Figure 2, S1–S2, Table S2). While CD31+EC were decreased by 16–28%, their inflammatory activation (CCL2+, VCAM1+) was increased by 3.01–15.98-fold in infected (CT > IT > AT) groups (Figure 2(a–j), S1(a–j)). Proliferating EC and SMC exhibiting wound-healing phenotype (Ki67+, VEGF+) were moderately increased by 0.44–1.14-fold in IT–CT (vs. AT, control) groups (Figure 2(k–t), S1(k–t), S2(a–o)). These results suggest that the AT stage is mostly silent, endothelial inflammation persisted in the myocardium during IT–CT stages, and the resultant EC loss was not countered by the wound-healing response. No changes in TAGLN+SMC in IT–CT (vs. control) groups (Figure 2(a–e), S2 (a–e)) provided further evidence that angiogenesis (neo-vessel formation) was unlikely to occur during progressive CD. Microglial and endothelial activation in brain of CD mice

Circulating and tissue parasites were reported in cerebrospinal fluid and brain of infected individuals [22]. We determined if Tc induces neuroinflammation to directly promote thrombosis in the brain. Multiplex fluorescence staining showed that IBA1+CD68+ microglial macrophages were increased by 1.10–1.31-fold in brain of infected (CT > IT > AT vs. control) mice (Figure 3(a–e), Table S3). Frequencies of interleukin 6 (IL6) producing IBA1+ and IBA1+CD68+ microglial cells were increased by 1.45–1.55-fold and 0.71–1.31-fold, respectively, in brain of IT–CT (vs. control) groups (Figure 3(f–o)). Non-significant microglial activation was noted in the AT group, and non-microglial immune cells exhibiting an inflammatory phenotype (CD68+IL6+) were increased in brain of CT mice only (Figure 3(p–t)). These results suggest that Tc-induced stimuli compromised the blood-brain barrier (BBB), and signaled microglial proliferation and proinflammatory activation during IT–CT stages of CD. Microglial IL6 release activates EC [23] that may augment immune cell migration and thrombo-inflammation [24]. We therefore evaluated the brain frequencies of EC and SMC exhibiting proliferating, pro-resolving, or inflammatory phenotypes at different CD stages (Figure 4, S3–S4, and Table S3). Frequencies of CD31+EC and TAGLN+SMC remained unchanged in brain of the enrolled groups (Figure 4(a–e), S4(a–e)). EC and SMC expressing VCAM1 (leukocyte recruitment) and/or uPA (axonal growth/synaptic repair) were increased by 0.99–1.99-fold and up to 1.51-fold, respectively, in brain of infected (CT > IT > AT) mice (Figure 4(f–o), S3(a–j), S4(f–o)). Further, proinflammatory CD31+EC producing CCL2/MCP1 and IL6 were increased by 0.85–1.13-fold in CT mice (Figure 4(p–t), S3(k–t)). Together, these results suggest that microglial proliferation and proinflammatory activation preceded the cerebral vascular inflammation during IT–CT stages of CD. Microglial stimuli initially activated the pro-resolving/repair response for preventing EC/SMC injury and promoting vascular integrity; however, proinflammatory EC ensued in brain of infected mice. IL6 is a critical cytokine that augments vascular inflammation and coagulation via regulating tissue factor (F3) release by microglial/macrophage cells [25]. We surmise that microglial IL6 (and potentially F3) sustained a feedback cycle of EC activation that led to neuroinflammation of microglial-EC origin in IT–CT stages of CD.

Anti-parasite immunogens reduce vascular inflammation and hemostatic disorder in CD

Benznidazole and nifurtimox are highly effective against acute Tc infection in children [26], but these drugs are not recommended for infected adults because of limited efficacy and adverse effects [27]. BCV and BCVR immunotherapies were developed to enhance anti-parasite protective immunity (unpublished data). We determined if treatment with BCV/BCVR before challenge infection reduced the vascular inflammation and prothrombotic state in CD. Tc-specific qPCR showed a very high level of parasite burden in the heart tissue of AT mice, and it persisted in IT–CT stages (Figure S5(a)). In contrast, greater parasite burden in the brain was noted in IT–CT stages than in AT mice (Figure S5(b)). Cardiac and brain parasite burden was reduced by 54–84% and 97–98.5%, respectively, in BCV- and BCVR-treated (vs. untreated) CT groups. These results suggested that (a) Tc primarily replicated in the heart (and other) muscle tissues, (b) Tc-induced proinflammatory mediators likely disrupted the BBB to allow parasite migration to brain during IT–CT stages, and (c) BCV/BCVR controlled the cardiac-cerebral parasite burden in adult mice.

Next, cytokines, chemokines, and cell proliferation markers were evaluated in cardiac and brain blood vessels by a 21-plex immunoassay (Figure 5, S6, and Tables S4–S5). ANGPT2, IL17, IL33, TNFα, CCL7, and CXCL10 were undetectable in cardiac and brain vasculature of enrolled groups. Proinflammatory cytokines (IFNγ and IL1β) and chemokines (CCL2, CCL3, CCL5, and CCL12) were increased by 0.6–3.98-fold and 3.43–24.01-fold, respectively, in cardiac blood vessels, and 0.45–4.21-fold and 0.49–13.69-fold-fold, respectively, in brain blood vessels of infected (vs. control) mice (Figure 5(a–f), S6(a–f)). Prothrombotic (IL6 and intracellular adhesion molecule 1) and pro-angiogenic (VEGF) response was increased by 5.37–6.92-fold and 1.21-fold, respectively, in cardiac and brain blood vessels of infected (vs. control) groups (Figure 5(g–i), S6(g–i)). In general, vascular proinflammatory/prothrombotic response was more pronounced in the CT group, though an increase in CCL2, CCL5, and CCL12 was also noted in the AT–IT groups. Biomarkers of immune resolution, including IL2, IL4, IL10, and uPAR, were unchanged, and CXCL12 (chemokine ligand) was increased by 2.40-fold in cardiac blood vessels of infected mice (Figure 5(j–o)). IL10 and uPAR were increased by 1.52–2.65-fold in brain blood vessels of CT (vs. control) mice (Figure S6(j–o)). When treated with BCV or BCVR before challenge, vascular intensities of proinflammatory cytokines/chemokines and prothrombotic/proangiogenic molecules were decreased by 31.5–82.9% and 54.3–87%, respectively, in chronically infected mice (Figure 5, S6). These results provided further evidence that a vascular proinflammatory/prothrombotic response develops and persists during the IT–CT stages of CD, and it is not counterbalanced by an increase in anti-inflammatory/pro-resolution cytokines and growth factors. Minimal activation of the heart and brain vasculature in AT mice indicated that acute parasite invasion was not the prime cause for vascular injury and dysfunction. Instead, parasite persistence instigated the proinflammatory response in the heart and brain vasculature. Consequently, BCV/BCVR-dependent control of tissue parasites was aligned with the reduction of vascular inflammation and prothrombotic state in CD mice. We determined if BCV/BCVR also provided a relief from chronic hemostatic disorder in CD (Figure 6, Table S1). Biomarkers of platelets activation (15.9–77.2%, Figure 6(a,b)), coagulation factors of intrinsic (29.2–72.6%), extrinsic (23.5–48.5%) and common (8.6–48.5%) pathway (Figure 6(c–k)), and plasminogen/plasmin activity (44.1–75.2%, Figure 6(m,n)) were significantly decreased in BCV- and BCVR-treated/infected (vs. CT) mice. At a functional level, BCV/BCVR improved the APTT (35.6%) and PT (27.7%) that resulted in normalizing of the INR ratio, all of which were compromised in CT mice (Figure 6(p–r)). Together, these results (Figures 5 and 6) suggest that management of vascular inflammation offered a potential approach for restoring the hemodynamic balance in chronic CD. BCVR was more effective than BCV in normalizing a greater number of hypercoagulability and clot degradation markers by lowering the risk of blood vessel injury. Fibrinogenesis in different stages of CD with or without BCV/BCVR

Fibrinogen and thrombin were increased in the plasma of IT–CT groups. Extravasation of fibrin(ogen) and (pro)thrombin with disruption of vascular integrity that might occur due to proinflammatory/prothrombotic endothelial activation can further the stable fibrin clot formation in tissues [28]. We employed MSB staining to probe the distribution of fibrin clots in brain and heart tissues of mice with different CD stages. Overall, myocardial fibrin score was increased by 1.13–1.94-fold in all stages of infection (Figure 7(a,b)), while brain fibrin score was increased by 4.54-fold in the CT group (Figure 7(c,d)). BCV-treated/infected mice exhibited 37% decline in cardiac fibrin, and both BCV- and BCVR-treated/infected (vs. CT) groups exhibited a 50–75% reduction in brain fibrin deposition. These results suggested that fibrin deposition continued throughout the course of infection and disease progression in the heart. Cerebrovascular dysfunction, along with fibrin deposition in the brain, occurred once the disease progressed to the chronic stage. Discussion

In this study, we have systematically examined the synergistic effects of hypercoagulability-vascular endothelial dysfunction-proinflammatory activation in alignment with Virchow’s triad theory to fill the research gap in understanding thrombosis mechanisms across different CD stages in a murine model. We identified that persistent low-level Tc infection – rather than merely blood stasis associated with cardiomyopathy – is a key driver of cardiac and cerebral fibrinogenesis. Our newly designed bicistronic immunogens (BCV/BCVR) significantly alleviated the vascular proinflammatory responses and hypercoagulability by controlling parasite burden. BCV/BCVR provides a novel immunotherapy for preventing CD-related thrombotic complications with important clinical translational significance.

In the context of infectious diseases, platelets serve as first responders that bind pathogens to prevent their dissemination and stimulate diverse host responses to facilitate pathogen clearance by the immune system and enable tissue repair [29]. Platelets also play a key role in thrombotic diseases via multiple interactions with vascular cells, inflammatory components, and coagulation cascade [30]. Platelets shape such complex processes by engaging a broad range of platelet receptors and releasing soluble mediators. Some studies indicate that Tc trans-sialidase-mediated cleavage of sialic acid from platelet surface induced thrombocytopenia and delayed anti-parasite immune response [31]. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) carrying DAMPs of the host and parasite origin (e.g. oxidized mitochondrial and kinetoplast DNA) were generated by endothelial and other cells exposed to Tc infection [32]. These Tc-induced EVs elicited proinflammatory activation of innate immune cells [15]. Tc synthesizes and sheds C18:1-lysophosphatidylcholine that binds platelet-activating factor (PAF)-specific receptor and signaled platelets activation [33]. P-selectin, PF4, and GPVI platelet factors mediating platelets’ adhesion, aggregation, and plug formation were elevated in the circulation of infected mice at IT–CT (vs. AT) stages when parasite burden is at its minimal level. Further, coagulation factors of intrinsic (F12/F9), extrinsic (F3), and common pathway (F13/F13B, fibrinogen) were increased in IT–CT groups of mice. P-selectin, F7, prothrombin, fibrinogen, and plasmin-anti-plasmin complex were also disturbed in CD patients during the IT–CT stage [34–36]. Only thrombin, which, besides its role in clot formation, also induces the acute-phase response to infection via amplifying inflammation [37], was maximally elevated in AT mice. We surmise that an early increase in thrombo-inflammation during acute infection signals a feedback cycle of platelet activation and thrombosis by engaging the blood coagulation cascade in IT–CT stages. Increased levels of P-selectin, PF4, and GPVI strongly point to active involvement of both collagen (via collagen/GPIb-IX/GPVI) and ADP (via ADP-P2Y1-P2Y12) dependent signaling cascades in platelet-mediated thrombus formation during CD progression. Collagen serves as the primary subendothelial initiator of platelet adhesion, and ADP serves as a secondary mediator for the stable, aggregate clot formation [38]. Consequently, thrombo-inflammation in the microvasculature would increase the risk of target organ dysfunction in the murine model of CD.

Thrombosis is recognized as a potential risk factor for the development of cerebral microinfarctions and ischemic stroke in CD [39]. Still, current literature has not addressed the time-course of Tc infection-induced thrombophilia in CD and whether cardiac involvement is essential to promote thrombus formation. In general, cardioembolic events related to apical aneurysm, mural thrombus, and atrial arrhythmias are proposed to be the most prevalent etiology of stroke in CD patients [12]. Others noted pulmonary thromboembolism due to right-sided cardiac thrombosis and associated blood stasis is the major cause of death of CD patients [40]. Yet, incidences of cardiac thrombus primarily occur in CD cases of heart failure, and cases of sudden death were more prone to thromboembolic phenomenon in systemic circulation [41]. Findings of high levels of plasminogen, plasmin, D-dimer, and fibrinogen in the circulation of IT–CT mice suggest that the fibrinolytic system, which removes fibrin threads and blood clots and thereby regulates thrombotic events, was activated in CD. Still, fibrinolysis was not sufficient to prevent fibrin deposition in brain and heart of IT–CT mice. Shortened APTT and PT, as we have noted in CT mice, may further increase the risk of thromboembolic events with increased clotting capacity due to heightened levels of procoagulants in chronic CD [42]. Elevated endogenous thrombin potential and prothrombin fragment (1 + 2), indicating increased thrombin activity, and increased levels of fibrinogen and D-dimer were noted in the circulation of CD patients [35,43]. These findings allow us to surmise that peripheral and tissue clots and fibrin formation and deposition accrue long before the chronic cardiomyopathy stage develops during the spectrum of CD.

A delicate functional balance between platelets and EC promotes and/or prevents clot formation in blood vessels. Briefly, quiescent EC prevent redundant platelets and coagulation cascade activation by secreting anti-platelet factors. Under various activating stimuli, EC undergo phenotypic changes that promote platelet adhesion, upregulate the expression of chemokines, adhesion, and inflammatory molecules, and suppress endothelial anti-thrombotic function. Thus, EC activation promotes hemostatic and immune cell interactions, leading to a proinflammatory/prothrombotic condition in the vascular endothelial bed [44]. However, only a few studies have examined the EC-platelet signaling by Tc, and it is not entirely clear if EC-platelet-mediated proinflammatory/prothrombotic circumstances occur and contribute to the risk of cardiac-cerebral thrombotic events during CD development. Human umbilical vein EC infected with Tc exhibited increased expression of NFκB and VCAM1 [45]. Others proposed that Tc binds P- and E-selectins on EC to extravasate through the vascular endothelium and the extracellular matrix, and then home into deeper tissues [13]. With these insights, we examined the activation pattern and role of EC in cardiac and cerebral thrombotic events in CD. In myocardium, frequency of CD31+EC was decreased, yet their proinflammatory (CCL2+), prothrombotic (VCAM1+), and angiogenic (VEGF+) responses were increased in IT–CT stages of CD. Remarkable increase in proinflammatory/prothrombotic activation of cardiac blood vessels was also noted in IT–CT mice. Endothelial CCL2 and VCAM1 crosstalk can foster recruitment and adhesion of immune cells and inflammation at the vascular injury site [46]. Likewise, VEGF promotes thrombosis through platelet activation and adhesion, and by inducing the expression of tissue factor [47]. In line with these reports, intense proinflammatory activation and inflammation-driven fibrosis were noted in the myocardium of CT mice [15,48]. These observations and our findings of myocardial fibrin deposition in IT and CT stages indicate that persistence of low-grade Tc maintains a feedback loop of proinflammatory activation of EC and vascular pathology in CD myocardium. Such debilitating EC activation was associated with poor anti-thrombotic EC function in CD mice, which together are predicted to increase the risk of prothrombotic state and thrombi formation in the cardiovascular system [49]. No increase in VEGF expression by SMC and no changes in the SMC quantity in myocardial blood vessels of infected mice affirm that angiogenesis and neo-vessel formation are unlikely to occur in CD myocardium.

Approximately 40% of CD patients develop stroke and brain atrophy independently of cardiac involvement [50]. It was posited (but not experimentally tested) that Tc-driven inflammation triggers EC damage, platelet activation, and thrombosis, thereby contributing to cerebrovascular pathology and stroke [51]. We have noted (1) increased frequencies of CD31+EC exhibiting proinflammatory (CCL2+IL6+), prothrombotic (VCAM1+), and fibrinolytic (uPA+) phenotypes; (2) inflammatory activation of microglia (IBA1+IL6+) and macrophages (IBA1+CD68+IL6+); and (3) proinflammatory/prothrombotic activation of blood vessels in brain tissue of mice in IT and CT stages of CD. Microglial and endothelial cells play pivotal roles in immune surveillance, and immune cell migration and activation to regulate the neuroinflammation and ischemia in brain [24,52]. Cerebrovascular EC also maintains stasis-free blood flow within the brain’s intricate network of blood vessels through a variety of mechanisms that regulate vascular tone, prevent platelet activation and aggregation, and regulate the coagulation cascade. We propose that after Tc infection, activation of the coagulation cascade and innate immune cells is required to inhibit parasite dissemination and preserve vascular integrity through clot formation in microcirculation. However, the resultant thrombo-inflammatory mediators cross the BBB and signal a phenotypic switch of cerebral endothelial and microglial cells towards proinflammatory type. Consequently, micro-thrombotic complications develop, as was noted in the brain tissue of IT–CT mice. Our results provide impetus for further studies verifying the role of endothelial and microglial cells in the risk of stroke in human CD patients.

Several observations provide a clue to why fibrinogenesis was detected in the heart at all stages of CD, while fibrin deposition in brain was primarily noticed in chronic CD. A high degree of Tc invasion and replication occurred in the heart at all stages of infection and disease progression. Myocardial tissue and blood vessels, being the prime site of Tc infection, experience irregularities in the cardiac cycle and structure and blood flow [53]. At the molecular level, such alterations promote endothelial dysfunction and inflammatory activation, favoring fibrin deposition. Indeed, proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules were increased in the cardiac blood vessels at all stages of CD progression. In contrast, BBB integrity upholds cerebral hemostasis by preserving the uniform blood flow and higher shear stress [54], and prohibits entry of parasites and large plasma proteins, e.g. fibrinogen, to avert inflammation and fibrin formation in the brain. In alignment, low-grade parasites alongside endothelial and microglial inflammatory responses were noted in the brain and brain vasculature during IT–CT (but not AT) stages of CD. Thus, we surmise that Tc-induced endothelial inflammation drives hemodynamic disorders and clot formation in the heart throughout all stages of Tc infection, while brain becomes susceptible to these events and microglial inflammation leading to fibrinogenesis in chronic CD.

In conditions discussed above, treatments targeting endothelial innate immune response and immunothrombosis could be of great significance for reducing fibrinogenesis and ischemic stroke and morbidity in CD. Indeed, several drugs, including anticoagulants, anti-thrombin, and anti-platelet therapies, are being studied for their effects on sepsis-related immunothrombosis in clinical trials [55]. Oral anticoagulants are also prescribed to infected patients experiencing atrial fibrillation or apical aneurysm; however, these have limited efficacy in reducing the cardiac deterioration and/or risk of systemic or cerebral embolism in CD patients [56]. We tested the hypothesis that early control of Tc infection is required to prevent the underlying cause(s) for prothrombotic events and fibrinogenesis that increase the susceptibility for stroke in CD. Indeed, pre-treatment with anti-parasite BCV and BCVR immunogens resulted in a significant reduction in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular proinflammatory and prothrombotic response and none-to-minimal clots/fibrin deposition in heart and brain tissue of CD mice. BCV/BCVR also reduced the hypercoagulability state in infected mice. BCVR (than BCV) exhibited superior efficacy in arresting the parasite persistence through splenic expansion of mature effector and effector/effector memory T cells exhibiting increased production of cytolytic molecules for parasite killing and displaying protective function to limit tissue damage in immunized/infected mice (unpublished data). We surmise that control of cardiac and brain prothrombotic events and fibrinogenesis for the prevention of stroke is an achievable goal with the use of bicistronic BCV/BCVR immunogens.

In summary, we have shown that Tc infection is largely silent, and systemic occurrence of platelet activation and hypercoagulable state, and proinflammatory activation of cardiac and cerebral blood vessel endothelium occur in indeterminate-to-chronic CD. Our findings underscore the importance of platelets and vascular EC function in regulating the hemodynamic balance to maintain normal blood stasis in physiological and pathological conditions. In CD, persistence of low-grade parasites (and resultant DAMPs) promotes the risk of brain and heart thrombotic events by influencing the proinflammatory and prothrombotic activation of vascular EC and systemic coagulation cascade. BCV and BCVR are simple in design and delivery and offer low-cost safe alternatives to prevent and control CD morbidities.

Limitation of the study

In the present study, male mice were excluded because males secrete growth hormone in a pulsatile fashion, which can impact liver gene expression of coagulation factors and affect susceptibility to thrombosis [57]. Female mice that release growth hormone (GH) in a consistent fashion were used for all experiments to minimize the hormonal effects on Tc infection-induced prothrombotic state. However, results obtained from female mice cannot be extrapolated to male mice. Further studies are required to address the gender-specific mechanism of GH secretion and its role in regulating the prothrombotic state development in the male murine model of CD. The effectiveness of BCV and BCVR in controlling the hypercoagulability, vascular inflammation, and thrombosis was tested in the chronic disease stage only. Considering that these immunogens work via control of parasite burden, we hypothesize that BCV/BCVR arrested the stimuli and consequent thrombosis and fibrinogenesis at all stages of CD; however, this remains to be verified. Benefits of BCV/BCVR in non-primate models and seropositive/infected hosts for controlling CD-associated vascular pathology also remain to be investigated.
