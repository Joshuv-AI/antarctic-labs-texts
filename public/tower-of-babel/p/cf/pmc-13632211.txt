Spotlight on challenges and novel methods in highly multiplexed tissue imaging-based spatial proteomics
Authors: Linyu Yang, Zhenyu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTissue microenvironment heterogeneity, including cellular composition, functional states, and intercellular interactions, shapes complex biological processes and disease mechanisms. Recent progress in highly multiplexed tissue imaging (HMTI) has enabled single-cell-resolved spatial proteomics, supporting detailed interrogation of dynamic tissue organization across contexts such as cancer, embryonic development, and neuroscience. However, the rapidly expanding HMTI landscape also creates practical challenges in platform selection, computational analysis, and cross-study reproducibility.Main bodyHMTI platforms leverage antibody labeling strategies, including DNA oligonucleotide-tagged, fluorophore-tagged, and metal-tagged reagents, to capture highly multiplexed molecular readouts (often tens to > 100 markers) within the same specimen. These approaches generate rich spatially indexed datasets that can reveal cell neighborhoods, spatial programs, and interaction patterns. In this review, we provide a comprehensive comparison of representative HMTI platforms and highlight major analytical bottlenecks in HMTI data analysis, including preprocessing and normalization, denoising, spillover correction, cell segmentation, cell-type annotation, and tissue microarchitecture analysis, with particular emphasis on method limitations, scalability, and performance under challenging conditions. In addition, we discuss tissue- and disease-specific parameter selection, pixel-level analysis, integration with spatial multi-omics, emerging three-dimensional spatial proteomics, and current barriers to clinical translation.ConclusionsHMTI-driven spatial proteomics provides a powerful route to quantify tissue microenvironment heterogeneity at single-cell resolution. Future progress will depend on improved standardized and scalable computational pipelines, robust benchmarking datasets, and more reliable multimodal and three-dimensional analytical frameworks. These advances are expected to enhance reproducibility and accelerate the translation of HMTI toward clinically actionable applications in precision medicine.Keywords: Highly multiplexed tissue imaging, Computational methods, Deep learning

Introduction

Tissue microenvironment heterogeneity encompasses the cellular composition, diverse cellular functions, and interactions among individual cells. Recent advances in spatial proteomics enable in-depth analyses at single-cell resolution to examine microenvironmental changes during embryonic development, tumor evolution, infection, neuroscience, etc. [1–4] (Fig. 1). Spatial proteomic technologies can be divided into untargeted approaches and multiplexed antibody-based methods, as recently reviewed by Andreas et al. [5]. In this review, we focus on highly multiplexed tissue imaging (HMTI) technologies that use the DNA oligonucleotide-tagged, fluorophore-tagged or metal-tagged antibodies to generate 40- to >100-plex imaging data from a single sample, including co-detection by indexing (CODEX, now named PhenoCycler-Fusion) [6], immunostaining with signal amplification by exchange reaction [7], cyclic multiplexed in situ tagging [8], multiplexed immunofluorescence (mIF), tissue-based cyclic immunofluorescence (t-CyCIF) [9], imaging mass cytometry (IMC) [10], and multiplexed ion beam imaging by time of flight (MIBI-TOF) [11]. These HMTI technologies enable the classification of large numbers of cells while preserving both intracellular and extracellular features.

In recent years, HMTI technologies have been widely applied to cancers, including lung cancer [12, 13], breast cancer [14], melanoma [15], colorectal cancer [16], etc. These studies highlight the potential of HMTI in deciphering tumor microenvironment (TME), understanding anti- and pro-tumoral mechanisms, stratifying patient subgroups and predicting prognosis or treatment response. HMTI also provides new insights into pathogenic and genetic mechanisms in endocrine disorders [17], autoimmune [18] or infectious diseases [19, 20]. Although recent reviews have primarily focused on cancer-oriented spatial analysis [21], precision-oncology implementation [22], or computational biomarker discovery [23], this review aims to provide a broader and more practice-oriented synthesis of HMTI by integrating platform comparison with computational workflows, multimodal spatial integration, tissue-specific analytical considerations, and translational perspectives. We emphasize computational methods that shape how raw multiplexed images are processed, corrected, segmented, annotated, spatially modeled, and validated.

To achieve these goals, many comprehensive analysis tools have been developed to address the challenges of data analysis and interpretation in HMTI. Here, we outline the steps and challenges involved in the analysis pipeline for conducting a multiplexed imaging-based spatial proteomic study, including data preprocessing, cell segmentation, cell type identification, tissue microarchitecture detection, colocalization and differential analysis. We also discuss how tissue architecture, disease context, imaging quality, and parameter selection influence the performance and interpretability of these computational methods. We further summarize recent advances in computational frameworks, deep learning techniques for pixel-level utilization, integration of spatial multi-omics and three-dimensional (3D) spatial proteomics.

Comparison of HMTI methods

HMTI technologies can be broadly categorized by their imaging principles into fluorescence-based, mass spectrometry-based, and enzymatic amplification-based methods (Table 1). Among them, CODEX, IMC, and MIBI-TOF are the most widely applied platforms in current spatial proteomics research [22]. Fluorescence approaches such as multiplexed immunohistochemistry (mIHC), mIF, and t-CyCIF, MACSima and chemically cleavable fluorescent antibody (CFA)-based approaches rely on multiple rounds of staining and imaging, whereas CODEX, IMC, and MIBI-TOF achieve multiplex detection within a single antibody-binding step. In mIHC, commercial platforms such as Akoya Phenoptics commonly employ tyramide signal amplification (TSA), which enables covalent signal deposition followed by microwave-based antibody stripping for iterative staining. CODEX employs DNA-barcoded antibodies that are visualized in sequential imaging cycles through oligonucleotide hybridization and washing, enabling visualization of dozens to hundreds of proteins at single-cell resolution [16]. For antibody removal, t-CyCIF uses light-mediated fluorophore oxidation in a high pH hydrogen peroxide solution (fluorophore bleaching), while MACSima employs gentle photobleaching of fluorescent labels on recombinant antibodies (REAfinity), or the release of antibodies (REAlease) or their labels (REAdye_lease) [24]. In CFA methods, Mondal et al. tethered antibodies to distinct fluorophores via a cleavable linker, which can be removed by chemical cleavage [25]. In contrast, IMC and MIBI-TOF label antibodies with unique metal isotopes, and a focused laser or ion beam releases these metals for detection by time-of-flight mass spectrometry, generating highly multiplexed protein maps free from spectral overlap [10, 11]. Compared with IMC/MIBI-TOF, CODEX therefore offers the advantage of preserving tissue integrity after imaging, allowing subsequent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and facilitating seamless integration with digital pathology workflows [26]. Fluorescence-based systems typically achieve ~0.2 μm resolution, whereas IMC and MIBI-TOF are limited to ~ 1 μm due to ablation spot size. However, repeated cycles of fluorescence removal may lead to tissue swelling, distortion, and displacement, which can result in unexpected errors. In terms of multiplexing capacity, mIF/mIHC detect ~6 proteins, while t-CyCIF, CODEX, and IMC/MIBI-TOF can quantify 40–100+ markers. More recently, optimized TSA-based workflows now reach over 20 markers [4, 27].

In addition to multiplexing capacity and spatial resolution, practical platform selection should also consider throughput, tissue compatibility/preservation, and scalability. Fluorescence-based cyclic platforms are generally more scalable for whole slide imaging. The CODEX has been reported to support whole slide imaging, with 25 min per cycle for a 1.5 × 1.5 cm area, and processing of 40 samples at 20-plex within one week. By contrast, IMC and MIBI-TOF are more commonly used for millimeter-scale areas. The IMC platform has a reported acquisition speed of approximately 60 min/mm2 (400 μm2 /s) at 1-μm resolution. The durations of these time-limiting steps were summarized by Bollhagen et al. [22]. Notably, although these methods are generally applicable to formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) or frozen tissue sections, MACSima can also be used for fixed adherent cells and suspension cells. Overall, fluorescence methods offer superior optical resolution, whereas mass spectrometry imaging eliminates spectral crosstalk and expands multiplexing capacity; yet all require careful optimization of staining cycles, antibody panels, and imaging parameters to preserve signal fidelity and tissue morphology in high-dimensional spatial proteomics.

Recent studies have started to integrate post-translational modifications (PTMs) and protein-protein interactions (PPIs) into HMTI workflows. For example, proteoform imaging mass spectrometry (PiMS) [28] and nanospray desorption electrospray ionization (nano-DESI) [29] enable proteoform and PTM mapping with high spatial resolution. Meanwhile, proximity ligation assays (PLA) [30] and multiplex in situ PLA [31, 32] provide spatial insights into PPIs and functional states of proteins. HMTI therefore advances understanding of protein expression and provides a powerful tool for investigating the spatial dynamics of PTMs and PPIs, opening new avenues for exploring cellular function and dysfunction in tissues with high spatial resolution and molecular depth.

Challenges and novel Methods in data analysis

Data preprocessing

One of the most common challenges in image processing is noise reduction. Non-specific antibody staining and spillover effects during signal collection can contribute to significant background noise. Both formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded and frozen tissue samples can be used for protein quantification using HMTI technology. For FFPE samples, which are the most common archived specimens in tissue banks, variations in fixation time, sample size, post-fixation dehydration, and storage conditions can introduce inconsistencies between samples (Fig. 2A). Notably, Nietner et al. reported that the choice of fixative alters tissue morphology, while Werner et al. showed that insufficient fixation frequently diminishes antigenicity [33, 34]. When properly preserved, FFPE specimens can maintain staining quality even after more than 20 years of storage [12]. For frozen specimens, particular care is required during thawing, as ice-crystal formation and melting can disrupt tissue architecture.

The performance of antibody staining and the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in HMTI detection can vary significantly, leading to unavoidable batch effects between samples [34–36]. Rigorous antibody qualification is essential for multiplexed imaging. Each antibody should be validated in single-plex format on tissue-matched positive and negative controls, titrated for optimal SNR, and compared with conventional IHC/IF or other orthogonal references when possible [37]. Validation should ideally be repeated in relevant tissue contexts or multi-organ tissues, followed by full-panel testing to assess cross-reactivity, spectral interference, and cycle-dependent epitope loss in multiplexed workflows. In addition to experimental procedures, the technologies themselves can also introduce noise. Even in the absence of background noise, thermal noise and sensor drift can compromise the accuracy of imaging data [38]. HMTI technologies may have insufficient imaging resolution to clearly distinguish subcellular structures, resulting in image blurring or loss of critical information, especially when detecting low-expression or difficult-to-visualize targets. In multiplex imaging, spectral overlap and crosstalk between channels, especially in flow cytometry, can lead to signal interference [39]. One of the persistent challenges in IMC is the low image quality caused by noise sources, including hot pixels and shot noise [40] (Fig. 2B).

To address these background issues, several signal-amplification methods have been developed. For example, Liao et al. used TSA in combination with CFA or cleavable fluorescent streptavidin-based labelling to enable highly sensitive detection of low-abundance proteins [41]. These methods significantly enhance the SNR. In parallel, computational denoising methods used in image analysis can also address this problem, including Gaussian, mean/percentile, non-local means, bilateral, total-variation, wavelet, and anisotropic-diffusion filters [42, 43]. Hunter et al. used MCD Viewer software (Fluidigm, USA) to manually select a threshold based on the cellularity of target protein staining, which helps filter out background and small speckle-like signals [44]. However, this method is highly dependent on the researcher’s biological knowledge, lacks repeatability, and is time-consuming for large sample sets. Ijsselsteijn et al. [45] performed inter-sample normalization using a semi-automatic background noise removal method, in which protein-positive and background regions were manually labeled in ilastik [46] to generate a training set, followed by training a random-forest model that is applied to all patches. Protein intensity is converted into a binary mask, and cellular protein expression is quantified by the number of positive pixels within the cell. This semi-automatic annotation method is not suitable for large sample sizes due to the substantial labeling workload. While this method effectively removes background noise for proteins with low expression levels, it may also result in the loss of quantitative information for certain functional proteins between samples [45]. Eling et al. provide a Shiny-based graphical interface in the R package cytomapper to set gates based on the expression of up to 24 markers, helping to assess the appropriateness of threshold selection [47]. Lu et al. introduced IMC-Denoise [40], an automated denoising pipeline that enhances IMC image quality by correcting hot pixels using differential intensity map-based restoration and shot noise through deep learning based filtering. Sequeira et al. present PENGUIN (percentile normalization GUI image denoising), an efficient and scalable pipeline for denoising multiplexed proteomics data, using scaling, thresholding, and percentile-based filtering techniques [48].

Due to the overlap in the emission and excitation spectra of fluorescent dyes, signals in flow cytometry can appear in adjacent channels alongside the main channel. For fluorescence-based methods, advanced computational unmixing algorithms can be used to mathematically separate overlapping signals and extract individual marker information from the mixed spectra (Fig. 2C). The spillover effect is nearly linearly correlated with the initial signal and can thus be corrected through compensation. Although the spillover effect in IMC is relatively small, Chevrier et al. confirmed that some spillover still occurs [39]. In some cases, even small quantitative differences can lead to differing conclusions. For example, certain cell markers used to identify cell types are expressed in moderate amounts in other cells, meaning spillover effects can cause quantitative errors and lead to incorrect cell type identification. Spillover effects can arise from adjacent channels or from isotopes of the same metal element in this method. Chevrier et al. generated a spillover effect correction matrix for mass spectrometry by staining a single magnetic bead, integrated it into the CATALYST R-package, and applied it to IMC detection [39]. Xiao et al. performed inter-sample normalization of marker proteins of this cell type through a priori cell type occurrence ratio [49]. CyLinter is a quality control tool for highly multiplexed tissue images that accelerates human visual review through automation and smart thresholding, significantly improving efficiency and accuracy, but its reliance on human oversight and the current limitations of its deep learning model for artifact detection pose challenges for large datasets and full automation [50]. Cell segmentation

Cell segmentation is a crucial step for processing biological images obtained from microscopes, H&E slides, IHC, and other sources, which separates the nucleus, cytoplasm, and background regions [51–59]. Recently, deep learning based novel methods have made substantial progress in this area [60]. The ilastik software is an intuitive interactive tool that makes machine-learning-based image analysis accessible to users with limited computational experience. It provides integrated workflows for object classification, tracking, image segmentation, and related tasks. The internal random-forest classifier can be interactively modified by giving it sparse training annotations that are tailored to the user’s objectives. Once the model is trained, it can be quickly applied to large batches of data without further user annotation [46]. DeepCell is a convolutional neural network (CNN) based framework for cytoplasmic segmentation of mammalian cells in phase-contrast and fluorescence microscopy images, with robust performance even on relatively small training datasets [61]. Mesmer, a pretrained segmentation model implemented in the DeepCell framework, has been widely used in spatial proteomics and multiplex immunofluorescence workflows [62]. It uses nuclear and membrane/cytoplasmic marker channels as two-channel inputs to predict cell interiors, boundaries, and background regions. Nevertheless, Mesmer performs more reliably for cells with regular morphologies, such as round or elliptical cells (Fig. 2D), and may be less accurate for highly polymorphic, elongated, densely packed, or poorly membrane-demarcated cells. DeepCell Kiosk can process extremely large datasets dynamically using deep learning workflows in the cloud. For example, it can identify cell nuclei in approximately 1,061 one-megapixel images in about 5.5 hours, at a cloud-computing cost approximately $250 [63]. CellSeg is a pre-trained, Mask R-CNN (CNN) based tool for nuclear segmentation and pixel quantification. It extracts the nuclear channel and normalizes brightness, segments the image in tiles and stitches them back together, and outputs per-cell masks and summary tables. CellSeg software has achieved high performance in identifying the boundaries of immune cells in cancer research [64]. Using deep-learning methods, Cellpose 1.0 provides a cell-segmentation approach that does not require additional model retraining, new training sets, or parameter adjustments [65]. Cellpose 2.0 improves cell boundary segmentation by leveraging datasets such as TissueNet and LiveCell and providing a set of base models that can be selected to meet diverse annotation requirements across different imaging scenarios [66]. Updating the model with a small amount of newly annotated data is another crucial feature. Because the additional annotations are typically focused on challenging structures, the model can better generalize as new annotations are incorporated. Owing to this efficient annotation strategy, only 100–200 manually annotated regions were required to develop a new model. Moreover, Cellpose 3.0 introduces one-click learned image restoration, which transforms diverse or noisy microscopy images into versions that can be optimally segmented by a generalist model, thereby improving robustness across datasets. This function is available via graphical user interface (GUI), command line interface (CLI), and application programming interface (API), making the software easier to use [67]. TrackMate 7 integrates a variety of cell-boundary segmentation algorithms within a user-friendly interface and provides scripting capabilities [68]. When both nuclear and membrane markers are available, cell boundaries can be delineated with markedly higher accuracy, particularly when using Cellpose 3.0 or Mesmer (Table 3).

The accuracy of cell segmentation is strongly influenced by tissue origin, disease type and imaging characteristics. In HMTI, differences in cellular density, necrosis, stromal composition, and signal-to-noise characteristics may substantially affect segmentation and downstream spatial analysis performance. However, the lack of ground-truth datasets remains a major barrier to method development, benchmarking, and cross-study comparability. Establishing robust benchmarking standards will require large reference cohorts spanning diverse tissues, disease states, imaging platforms, and standardized preprocessing workflows. In addition, orthogonal validation strategies, including same-section or serial-section measurements, expert-annotated tissue regions and biologically well-defined structures, should be incorporated to evaluate segmentation, annotation, and cross-modal alignment accuracy. To address these challenges, several community-driven benchmarking initiatives and large-scale annotated datasets have emerged in recent years. Notably, the 2018 Data Science Bowl established one of the earliest large-scale benchmarking frameworks for nuclei segmentation by providing 37,333 manually annotated nuclei across 841 two-dimensional images collected from more than 30 laboratories under diverse imaging conditions [69]. Similarly, TissueNet developed by Greenwald et al. contains approximately 1.3 million cell-boundary annotations generated using a multi-stage cyclic annotation strategy [62], while LiveCell provides more than 1.6 million manually annotated cells across multiple cell morphologies and imaging conditions with rigorous quality-control procedures [70]. These resources have substantially accelerated algorithm development and enabled more systematic evaluation of segmentation methods. Nevertheless, important limitations remain. Most existing benchmark datasets are dominated by two-dimensional cultured-cell imaging and may not adequately capture the structural complexity, cellular heterogeneity, staining variability, or imaging artifacts commonly observed in HMTI datasets derived from clinical tissues. Therefore, although many deep learning based methods are available for spatial proteomics, we recommend evaluating several segmentation algorithms on one’s own dataset and choosing the one that demonstrates the best performance [71].

Cell type identification

In spatial proteomics, cell type identification is a crucial step for downstream analysis, and its precision determines the reliability of all subsequent results. Because only a relatively small number of targeted proteins can be measured, spatial proteomics, in contrast to single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), is unable to distinguish cell types with the same precision [72–76]. Researchers should design antibody panels that meet the desired resolution for cell subpopulations under investigation while also accounting for tissue- and disease-specific expression patterns and cellular composition (Fig. 2E).

Cell type identification can be performed by protein gating, in which expression thresholds are defined for each marker, or by using unsupervised clustering in conjunction with established canonical cell-type indicators [15, 47, 77, 78]. These two methods can be used to validate one another, and the software single-cell identification from multiplexed images (SIMPLI) integrates both strategies for cell-type identification [79]. According to the CODEX development team, the unsupervised clustering approach is more resilient to background noise; they also suggested that z-value normalization be carried out prior to clustering by grouping all cells together and clustering on normalized expression values [80]. For tumor identification, the tumor region can be marked on an H&E-stained adjacent slice, or a pathologist can delineate the tumor area based on epithelial cell protein marker staining [14]. Murphy et al. used a large set of IHC staining images to train artificial intelligence algorithms to identify cell types [81]. There are also algorithms that identify cell types using spatial network information. STELLAR is trained on a single-cell spatial network in a reference dataset [82]. All cells in this network must be annotated with cell types. After training, the model can identify cell types in another network. This method has two primary components: first, an adaptive boundary model is used to identify cell types in the known dataset by examining pairwise spatial relationships between cells; second, the trained model is applied to find new cell types in the test dataset [82]. Cell type identification with spatial information (CELESTA) integrates both spatial information and protein expression data for cell-type annotation [83]. Initially, it identifies anchor cells, which express only one marker protein and therefore can be assigned a distinct cell type. For non-anchor cells, it builds a neighborhood network using spatial information, represents the cells as nodes in an undirected graph, and assumes that the overall distribution of cell types satisfies a Markov property. CELESTA then uses an expectation-maximization algorithm to iteratively update cell-type probabilities. If a predefined confidence threshold is met across multiple rounds, the cell type with the highest probability is assigned to the non-anchor cell. Cells that still cannot be confidently assigned after multiple iterations are retained for further rounds until convergence [83]. Annotator and spatial pattern finder (AnnoSpat) is an advanced computational tool that leverages neural networks and point-process algorithms to efficiently annotate cell types and quantify cellular arrangements in spatial proteomics data, providing rapid and accurate results; however, its dependence on K-means-based semi-supervised clustering models may limit its flexibility and adaptability to certain datasets or rare cell types (Table 3) [84].

Single-cell identification depends on cell segmentation. Kirchgaessner et al. introduced a machine-learning-based approach to impute missing single-cell protein abundances in multiplex tissue imaging, utilizing models such as regularized linear regression, gradient-boosted trees, and deep autoencoders. By incorporating spatial context, these models achieved mean absolute errors between 0.05 and 0.3 on a [0,1] scale, closely approximating ground-truth values. However, a protein’s signal at the cell-boundary region can originate from multiple surrounding cells, and this “spillover effect” can result in inaccurate protein expression quantification. This spillover may lead researchers to conclude that nearby cells express the protein at medium to high levels in specific locations with very high signal intensity. This phenomenon is especially noticeable in regions such as tumor areas, spleen, and lymph nodes that have higher cell density. Analysis at the tissue-domain level can help resolve this issue (discussed in the next part). Meanwhile, we have observed that in some studies, certain T cells and B cells cannot be completely separated. This may be explained by the formation of tight lymphocyte aggregates. To address this, Bai et al. developed reinforcement dynamic spillover elimination (REDSEA), an algorithm that explicitly considers protein quantification in the cell-boundary region [85]. In REDSEA, the expression of protein M is reassigned from cell A to neighboring cells that do not express protein M if cell A expresses protein P and proteins P and M are not expected to be present in the same cell type [85]. Although this algorithm can largely solve the problem of double-positive cells such as CD3 and CD20, it still cannot fully correct protein expression quantification for markers that are not mutually exclusive, and can significantly increase the proportion of double-negative cells in the data [85]. Therefore, when annotating cell types, analysts need to conduct customized analysis based on the sample characteristics, staining features, and biological knowledge of their own datasets. This step forms the basis for subsequent analysis, and inaccurate cell type annotation will directly lead to unconvincing results [80]. The choice of cell-type identification method largely depends on the specific task and data characteristics. For example, when only a few samples and a limited number of markers are available, simple threshold-based classification may suffice. When handling very large datasets, unsupervised clustering methods are often the most practical choice. However, if a well-annotated dataset under the same disease condition is available, model training and transfer learning can be used to achieve more accurate cell-type annotation. Although various methods are available, a major challenge lies in the absence of a reliable ground truth for evaluating the accuracy of cell-type identification. We therefore recommend that researchers apply and compare multiple algorithms and critically cross-validate the results using prior biological knowledge—such as cell morphology, lineage-specific marker co-expression or exclusivity, spatial proximity, and expected cellular proportions, which serve as key references for validating and refining cell-type identification in spatial proteomics.

Tissue microarchitecture detection

Schurch et al. proposed the concept of cell neighborhoods (CNs): unsupervised clustering is performed on the composition of cells adjacent to each index cell, and a cell neighborhood is established based on the different composition ratios [16]. When combined with biological knowledge, different cell neighborhoods can be interpreted as distinct substructures of the TME, such as tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS), blood vessel region, cancer nests, tumor-stromal boundaries, immune-cell accumulation areas, etc. In many studies, researchers have used this algorithm to characterize biologically meaningful cell neighborhoods. Sorin et al. characterized 10 cell neighborhoods in lung adenocarcinoma, representing the tumor boundary, immune accumulation area 1, immune accumulation area 2, a lymphoid immune-cell accumulation area, the tumor center, a macrophage accumulation area, and a granulocyte accumulation area, a B cell accumulation area, and a vascular microecological area [12]. They found that B cell-rich neighborhoods are protective factors for patients with lung adenocarcinoma [12]. The results of this algorithm depend on (a) the setting of the number of cell neighbors (b) the setting of the k value in the cell neighborhood k-means clustering process. Based on this method, Sheng et al. used Phenograph to perform cell neighborhood clustering. By iterating across different numbers of cell neighbors, Sorin et al. selected the parameter setting that showed the strongest association with clinical prognosis. They discovered that patient prognosis was more strongly associated with B cell rich neighborhoods containing CD4+T cells [16].

Similar to the process of identifying CNs, Jackson et al. created a cell-interaction community based on the cellular spatial network using the Louvain algorithm [86]. This algorithm uses the composition of cell types within each community as a feature vector when clustering the communities [86]. Using this algorithm, the team divided the structure of the TME into vascular areas, interstitial resting areas, interstitial activation areas, TLS areas, immunosuppressive areas [14]. Hoch et al. developed an algorithm to identify specific cell-aggregation areas (defined as patches) and the neighborhoods of these aggregation areas (defined as milieus) [15]. By setting a threshold for the number of cells in a patch and a distance threshold to create a milieu, this algorithm defines a particular region with biological relevance and can be useful in tumor immunity research. These algorithms are implemented in the R package “imcRtools”. Using this algorithm, they identified accumulation areas of different chemokines in melanoma samples and analyzed differences in immune-cell accumulation across these chemokine-rich regions. They found that CCL19, CCL22, CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL13 could recruit T cells and B cells. This evidence validates the important role of chemokines in tumor immunology. At the same time, they characterized B cell-rich milieus and found CXCL13 aggregation in these milieus, which continued to recruit additional T cells and promote antitumor immunity. Compared with cell neighborhoods, these algorithms construct microenvironments with stronger spatial correlations. By leveraging cellular morphology, expression, and cell proximity, Unsupervised Tissue Architecture discovery with Graphs (UTAG) can identify tissue-architecture domains [87]. For example, UTAG successfully distinguished normal structures in human lung, including airway, alveolar space, vessel areas, and submucosal glands. During the progression of COVID-19 infection, compartmentalization of vessels in the alveolar space underwent numerical loss. All these algorithms can provide a deeper understanding of tissue microstructure. However, it is often difficult to assign clear biological functions to each tissue microstructure (Fig. 2F). In addition, the biological interpretation of tissue microarchitectures may vary considerably across diseases and tissue types, further emphasizing the need for context-aware analysis and validation. Researchers should therefore exercise caution when drawing conclusions.

Colocalization analysis

Colocalization analysis comprises two main approaches: pixel-based colocalization analysis and cell- or object-based colocalization analysis. Pixel-based methods compute correlation coefficients, such as Pearson’s, Manders’, Costes’, Van Steensel’s, or Li’s coefficients, by comparing intensity values on a pixel-by-pixel basis across channels [88]. In contrast, cell-based colocalization analysis identifies discrete objects and statistically evaluates the spatial relationships between them, providing a more robust quantification of colocalization at the single-cell level. We summarized recently developed methods for colocalization analysis in Table 4. SpotitPy is a semi-automatic 2D object-based colocalization tool that segments cell boundaries according to selected labels, performs noise reduction, and quantifies protein colocalization and expression [89]. SpotitPy reports the absolute counts of colocalized particles (per image/per cell) rather than intensity-correlation coefficients [90]. Permutation tests are a common approach implemented in cell-based colocalization methods, including histology topography cytometry analysis toolbox (histoCAT) [91], Giotto Suite [89], and Squidpy [92]. By comparing the observed number of colocalization events with a null distribution generated through random permutation of object positions, the statistical significance of the observed colocalization frequency can be evaluated. In histoCAT, directionality is captured by computing pairwise neighbor frequencies for each cell type within a defined distance and performing two one-tailed permutation tests to assess whether cell type A is significantly enriched around, or avoids, cell type B. Squidpy (v1.8) offers distance-binned co-occurrence curves and interaction matrices. Many studies have calculated cell colocalization using permutation-based methods [19, 87]. The main differences among these studies lie in how the spatial connectivity network is constructed: one approach builds adjacency graphs based on object centroids or boundaries (e.g., Delaunay/Voronoi/k-NN), whereas the other quantifies colocalization using the nearest distance between object centroids or edges. The performance and interpretation of colocalization analysis are also highly dependent on tissue structure and disease context. For example, densely packed tissues such as lymphoid organs or highly proliferative tumors may exhibit substantially higher baseline interaction frequencies than sparse stromal tissues, thereby influencing permutation-based significance estimates and neighborhood inference. Differential analysis typically involves basic statistical comparisons between groups, such as t-tests or rank-sum tests, using cell proportions or cell-neighborhood proportions derived from group labels [16, 20, 93]. Some researchers also use linear regression and Fisher’s exact test to evaluate whether cell types are significantly enriched in specific groups [14, 86].

Many additional questions can be addressed via the analysis of cell interactions. For example, analysis of tumor heterogeneity from spatial omics measurements (ATHENA) quantifies tumor heterogeneity by computing cell-cell interactions and constructing cell-interaction networks [94]. MISTy software is a machine-learning-based approach for exploring cell-cell interactions. Unlike other methods, MISTy uses multiview random-forest models to predict each marker from intracellular and neighborhood marker expression and interprets view-specific variable importance as spatial dependencies between markers. Moreover, it is not limited to fixed, predefined anchor cells, specific cell type agglomerations, or a single form of data representation, but instead allows flexibility in building models to interpret spatial data. It does not require annotation of cell types, states, or any other characteristics of the spatial unit [95]. Based on cell colocalization analysis, Bouchard et al. developed colocatome analysis, a quantitative framework that transforms raw cell-position data into a statistically validated, normalized map of cell type proximities, enabling quantitative comparison of spatial organization across biological or clinical contexts [96]. Using this approach, the authors showed that erlotinib treatment profoundly reshapes the spatial organization of tumor-stroma assembloids, reflecting drug-induced remodeling of the tumor microenvironment.

Computational framework

To improve the reproducibility of spatial proteomics data analysis, several researchers have built frameworks for the analysis process. Franken et al. summarized a protocol for processing CODEX imaging data in QuPath and R, including cell segmentation, cell phenotyping, intercellular distance calculation and visualization [97]. MCMICRO is a framework specifically designed to standardize IMC data-processing workflows. This framework integrates raw-data correction, standardization, quality control, cell segmentation, cell-signal intensity quantification, as well as downstream cell type identification and cell neighborhood analysis. There are a number of established software modules under development, some of which are derived from algorithms written in different languages. Through this framework, researchers can conduct IMC data analysis more easily and reproducibly [98]. The framework can also be used to analyze H&E, IHC, IF, mIHC, mIF, and other imaging experiments. Windhager et al. described the process of IMC data analysis, but the application process requires users to have a solid foundation in programming and data analysis [99].

Squidpy is a Python-based spatial multi-omics analysis framework that facilitates research by providing tools for generating histological maps and spatial interaction networks. The software integrates multiple established algorithms and utilities, such as Cellpose for cell segmentation, and provides a reimplementation of the CellPhoneDB ligand-receptor inference algorithm to analyze cell-cell communication within a spatial context. Thus, Squidpy may be particularly useful for teams already working within the Python ecosystem or for studies focused on downstream spatial analysis [92]. Similarly, Giotto Suite supports both spatial proteomics and spatial transcriptomics analyses. As an R-based framework, it is well suited for R-oriented teams seeking a rapid and user-friendly setup. It also provides a flexible environment for spatial visualization, spatial statistics, and multi-scale spatial omics analysis, making it useful for studies involving heterogeneous spatial technologies or multiple spatial resolutions. In this sense, MCMICRO, Squidpy, and Giotto Suite should be viewed as complementary rather than competing frameworks. MCMICRO is most appropriate for reproducible image-to-cell processing of multiplexed imaging datasets; Squidpy is suitable for Python-based downstream spatial omics analysis and integration with the single-cell ecosystem; and Giotto Suite is useful for R-based spatial visualization, spatial statistics, and multi-scale or cross-platform spatial omics analysis. Therefore, the optimal framework should be selected according to the structure of the input data, the expertise of the research team, the required level of workflow automation, and the biological questions being addressed.

Context-dependent method selection and optimization

As discussed above, the performance of spatial proteomics pipelines can vary substantially across tissue types, disease contexts, imaging conditions, and analytical objectives. Therefore, we emphasize that both methodological choices and parameter settings should be optimized according to the specific biological and technical context rather than applied uniformly across studies. For example, segmentation models trained on distinct datasets often exhibit considerable performance differences when applied to tissues of different origins or pathological states (Fig. 3A). Models trained or fine-tuned on disease-specific datasets typically achieve better performance in their target settings. For instance, the NuCLS dataset, which contains over 220,000 annotated breast cancer nuclei, has facilitated high-accuracy segmentation of diverse nuclear phenotypes in H&E-stained breast cancer images [100]. Similarly, the PUMA dataset comprises 155 primary and 155 metastatic melanoma regions of interest with detailed nuclei and tissue annotations [101], and Lal et al. constructed a liver cancer-specific dataset with expert nuclear labels [102]. When existing segmentation models perform poorly, developing or adopting disease-specific annotated datasets may therefore be necessary to retrain or fine-tune models for improved performance.

Context-dependent optimization is also critical for cell-type identification (Fig. 3B). Prior knowledge of organ-specific cellular composition, marker expression, and disease-associated phenotypic changes is essential for accurate annotation. In the lung, for example, alveolar macrophages are commonly characterized by CD68, CD206, and MARCO expression, whereas interstitial macrophages often express CD68 and MHC-II; CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells may be identified by markers such as CD103 and CD69 [103]. In the liver, Kupffer cells represent tissue-resident macrophages and can be distinguished from infiltrating monocyte-derived macrophages by high expression of markers such as CLEC4F, TIMD4, CD68, and F4/80. These examples illustrate that antibody panel design, gating thresholds, and classification strategies should be adapted to tissue- and disease-specific expression patterns rather than relying solely on universal marker definitions.

Method and parameter optimization is equally important for tissue microarchitecture and neighborhood analyses. When constructing CNs, the choice of k should reflect tissue density, cellular organization, and prior pathological or physiological knowledge about the expected scale of cell-cell interactions, rather than relying on a universal parameter across tissues. Studies of lung adenocarcinoma and colorectal cancer have commonly used k = 10 [12, 16], whereas analyses of small cell lung cancer and other dense tissues have employed k = 20 [13]. For tissues that have not been characterized previously, a practical strategy is to begin with morphology-informed priors, including average cell diameter, local cellular density, and the anticipated interaction radius suggested by tissue structure and disease state. In parallel, parameter robustness can be evaluated using a sensitivity-analysis strategy, analogous to strategies commonly applied in single-cell clustering, by repeating CN identification across a range of plausible k values and assessing whether major neighborhood assignments and enrichment patterns remain stable.

Likewise, distance thresholds for colocalization analysis should be adapted to tissue-specific cellular density and biological context. For example, a distance threshold of approximately 12 μm has been used in normal lung tissue, whereas thresholds of 15–20 μm may be more appropriate under infectious or inflammatory conditions, where cells are more tightly packed (Fig. 3C) [87]. In previously uncharacterized tissues, it may also be useful to start from a parameter-free Delaunay triangulation graph as a baseline, and then compare its implied neighborhood structure with those obtained using commonly used k values or distance thresholds. This strategy can help determine whether the resulting spatial patterns are robust and biologically consistent with the hypothesis under study. Overall, variability in tissue architecture, cellular density, and disease-associated heterogeneity, imaging quality, and study objectives necessitates careful tailoring of segmentation strategies, cell-type annotation methods, spatial graph construction, and parameter settings for each organ or disease scenario. Conclusions that remain consistent across reasonable methodological choices and parameter ranges should be interpreted as more biologically robust, whereas findings that are highly sensitive to a single parameter setting require cautious interpretation and further validation.

Pixel-level utilization

Although segmentation is a fundamental step in HMTI, recent studies have moved beyond traditional cell-boundary-based approaches to derive biomarkers directly from raw imaging data [12, 104–108]. Object-level analysis provides strong biological interpretability because it enables quantification of cell types, cellular states, cell proportions, and cell-cell interactions. In contrast, pixel-level analysis does not require explicit cell-boundary delineation and can preserve continuous spatial signals, local protein-expression gradients, and tissue-domain information. It may therefore be particularly useful when accurate segmentation is difficult or when the primary objective is to quantify regional immune infiltration, tissue-level biomarker expression, or spatial protein distribution. In quantitative immunofluorescence (QIF) experiments, automated quantitative analysis (AQUA) has been used to perform quantitative analysis directly at the pixel level. Martinez-Morilla et al. applied this method to IMC data without explicitly using cell boundaries and found that AQUA produced quantitative results that were highly consistent with QIF measurements in the same IMC dataset. Importantly, they found that a higher proportion of CD8-, CD4-, and CD3-positive pixels in melanoma samples from patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), as well as high expression of B2M and MHC class I molecules, were associated with favourable patient prognosis [105]. Sorin et al. trained a ResNet model using ImageNet-derived features to predict recurrence in lung adenocarcinoma directly from tumor regions, achieving an AUC of 0.94 in an independent validation cohort, illustrating that histological texture patterns encode clinically meaningful prognostic signals that may be overlooked by manual annotation [12].

Nevertheless, purely pixel-level approaches often have limited biological interpretability, whereas hybrid approaches can bridge this gap by integrating pixel-level robustness with object- or region-level information to provide more interpretable spatial metrics. For example, the MIRIAM platform integrates machine-learning-based pixel classification to define cellular components and introduces a membrane-reconstruction module to address incomplete segmentation, thereby linking pixel-level signal patterns with object-level cellular features and region-level tissue organization [106]. Likewise, Ternes et al.’s multi-encoder variational autoencoder (ME-VAE) learns latent image representations that disentangle morphological noise from biologically relevant variation, illustrating how image-level features can be transformed into interpretable cellular phenotypes and outperforming conventional feature-based or single-encoder VAEs in identifying subtle cellular phenotypes [107]. Moreover, immune phenotypes of patients can be inferred by measuring levels of immune infiltration in the TME through pixel-level quantification of immune-related proteins expression, which can be further interpreted in combination with cellular composition or tissue-domain information [108]. Collectively, future spatial proteomics pipelines should not view segmentation as a mandatory step, but rather as one analytical option among several. Pixel-level, object-level, and hybrid strategies should be selected or combined according to data quality, tissue architecture, segmentation reliability, and the biological question of interest.

Integration of other spatial technologies

Spatial proteomics provides an important framework for validating and extending discoveries from spatial transcriptomics and other spatial omics. One major strategy is to integrate datasets generated from adjacent or serial tissue sections at the computational level. For example, Takano et al. used Visium spatial transcriptomics to show that lung adenocarcinoma progression is accompanied by spatially restricted tumor-state transitions and microenvironmental remodeling [109]. Using matched CODEX imaging on consecutive sections, they confirmed the presence of exhausted T cell-rich regions, CXCL13/CXCR5-associated immune niches, and boundary-localized immunosuppressive cells at the interface between well-differentiated and more invasive tumor areas [109]. However, integrating spatial proteomics with transcriptomics and other omics remains challenging at both the experimental and computational levels. Different modalities are often acquired from adjacent sections, resulting in substantial coordinate-registration challenges caused by tissue deformation, partial field-of-view mismatch, and differences in spatial resolution. In addition, cross-modal integration must address batch effects, modality-specific noise, and differences in molecular coverage and dynamic range. Several computational methods have therefore been developed to improve spatial alignment and data fusion across modalities. SpatialEx leverages histology as a universal anchor to integrate molecular readouts across serial sections and explicitly demonstrates transcriptomics-proteomics diagonal integration [110]. Multi-Omics Imaging Integration Toolset (MIIT) performs image-guided non-rigid registration and cross-resolution fusion for spatial multi-omics data acquired from serial sections [111]. Lou et al. introduced a vector-guided graph learning method that aligns multi-slice spatial multi-omics data by learning a shared embedding that incorporates spatial directionality and slice-aware integration [112]. To accommodate resolution mismatches across modalities, some studies have performed multimodal integration at the regional rather than single-cell level after spatial alignment. For instance, Ravi et al. characterized glioblastoma using spatially resolved transcriptomics, metabolomics, and proteomics at different resolutions and inferred that tumor clones from different evolutionary lineages exhibit distinct metabolic characteristics [113]. Methods explicitly addressing batch effects across modalities in spatial proteogenomics remain limited. A representative example is spaMultiVAE, which models paired spatial RNA-protein data and uses a conditional autoencoder with batch covariates to learn batch-free latent representations while accounting for protein-specific background noise [114]. More recent work, such as PRESENT, further extends this concept to multi-sample spatial RNA-ADT integration through batch-adversarial learning [115]. Overall, computational integration of spatial proteomics with other omics offers a powerful way to resolve tissue architecture and microenvironmental interactions, but remains limited by challenges in coordinate registration, resolution mismatch, cross-modality batch effects, and robust data fusion.

Another emerging strategy is to reduce the need for computational registration by simultaneously detecting proteins, RNA, DNA, or other molecular features on the same tissue section. These same-section multi-omics approaches can better preserve spatial correspondence across modalities and reduce errors caused by serial-section deformation or imperfect alignment. Jiang et al. developed the Protein and Nucleic Acid in situ Imaging (PANINI) method, which can simultaneously quantify DNA, RNA, and proteins expression in tissues and can be combined with MIBI [20]. Using PANINI, the authors simultaneously labeled more than 30 proteins together with simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) RNA and DNA in a rhesus monkey model. They found that regions within approximately 20–30 μm of infected cells showed increased expression of IL10, FOXO1, and HLA-DR, accompanied by increases in NK cells, Treg cells, granulocytes, and CD8+ T cells. Among these signals, IL10 was mainly produced by induced B cells and was associated with further polarization of macrophages toward an M2-like state [20]. Based on IMC technology, Hoch et al. combined metal-tagged antibody detection with RNAscope, enabling simultaneous measurement of RNA and protein abundance by IMC [15]. This approach showed that CXCL13 and CCL4 were mainly produced by CD8+ T cells, whereas CCL18 was mainly derived from macrophages. CXCL13/CCL4-secreting CD8+ T cells exhibited high LAG-3 expression, consistent with prolonged activation; notably, in B cell rich regions, CXCL13 was exclusively produced by T cells and predominantly by CD8+ T cells in areas of B cell aggregation. Commercial platforms, such as COMETTM, also enable the same-section spatial detection of RNA and protein using RNAscopeTM HiPlex Pro combined with sequential immunofluorescence [116]. Although these same-section approaches reduce registration errors and facilitate direct cross-modal interpretation, their broader application still depends on careful optimization of assay compatibility, signal separation, normalization, and cross-modal feature integration.

3D spatial proteomics

3D spatial proteomics is more informative than two-dimensional (2D) proteomics for capturing histological microstructure and studing the role of microstructure in the TME. In pancreatic cancer, some researchers have used 3D immunostaining methods to characterize the vascular structure in tumors. Compared with 2D detection methods, tumor invasion of venous vessels can be detected more readily in 3D detection [117, 118]. Bronsert et al. used 3D methods to identify tumor boundary areas in pancreatic cancer, colorectal cancer, lung cancer, and breast cancer, and found that local invasion rarely occurred as single cells and instead occurred mainly through clustered cell migration [119]. Zanotelli et al. developed an experimental method that enables spatial proteomic examination of up to 240 spheroid microtissues to quantify the contribution of environmental, neighborhood, and intracellular states to marker variation in single cells [120]. They discovered that cell-intrinsic and environmental factors contribute to marker variability in a hierarchical manner, which has general implications for the systematic study of single-cell phenotypic variability [120]. Kuett et al. conducted IMC staining on 2-micron-thick serial sections to study three-dimensional spatial proteomics and found that 3D-IMC can capture vascular structure more clearly, and the distance between cells and blood vessels is often closer than the measurement value of 2D-IMC [121]. Compared with areas invaded by a single blood vessel, tumor cells in areas invaded by multiple lymphatic vessels/blood vessels highly express the HER2 [121]. Remarkably, 3D imaging reveals that a single immune cell can engage in multiple simultaneous interactions—for example, a PD-1+CD8+ T cell extending filopodia toward a tumor cell while contacting a PD-L1+ dendritic cell and a CD4+ T helper cell at the same time [122]. Such concurrent activating and inhibitory exchanges, which are difficult to capture in 2D sections, illustrate how 3D spatial proteomics can uncover complex and multiplexed communication networks within the TME.

Despite its clear advantages over 2D imaging, 3D spatial proteomics still faces several important technical limitations and unique computational challenges that go beyond conventional 2D imaging analysis. First, accurate volumetric segmentation remains difficult because cells must be delineated across multiple z-planes or serial sections, where nuclear and membrane signals may be discontinuous, distorted, or partially missing. Segmentation errors can therefore propagate into downstream 3D cell-type annotation, neighborhood analysis, and cell-cell interaction inference. Second, anisotropic resolution is a major limitation: lateral resolution is often higher than axial resolution, which can bias the reconstruction of cell shape, cell volume, and intercellular distances. Third, registration of serial sections is technically challenging because sectioning, staining, imaging, and tissue clearing can introduce deformation, shrinkage, expansion, or partial tissue loss. These artifacts complicate the accurate alignment of cells, vessels, stromal structures, and molecular signals across depth. Fourth, computational scalability is a major bottleneck. 3D spatial proteomics generates extremely large image volumes, requiring efficient data storage, visualization, denoising, segmentation, graph construction, and quantitative analysis pipelines. Finally, spatial statistics developed for 2D data cannot always be directly transferred to 3D datasets, because distance thresholds, neighborhood definitions, and cell-cell interaction models need to account for true volumetric geometry rather than planar projections.

Recent 3D CyCIF studies show that thicker sections can suffer from axial signal attenuation while also generating extremely large datasets [122]. Antibody penetration in thick tissues also remains difficult to control uniformly, because staining performance depends not only on diffusion but also on tissue-dependent accessibility and binding kinetics [123]. In addition, tissue clearing may introduce fluorescence quenching as well as tissue shrinkage or expansion, which can affect geometric accuracy and quantitative interpretation [124]. These experimental constraints are compounded by computational challenges, as accurate 3D segmentation, registration, and multimodal integration (described in the previous section) remain difficult and are not yet fully standardized. Together, these issues currently limit the quantitative robustness, reproducibility, and scalability of 3D spatial proteomics.

Challenges and outlook

The future of HMTI in spatial proteomics holds considerable promise for advancing our understanding of complex biological systems and disease mechanisms. However, realizing this promise will require targeted advances in data acquisition, standardization, analysis, and translation into clinical practice. A major immediate need is the development of robust pipelines to integrate spatial proteomics with transcriptomics, metabolomics, and imaging data at single-cell resolution. Current approaches remain fragmented, often lacking standardized coordinate systems and batch correction frameworks. Establishing interoperable data formats and common reference atlases, similar to those in scRNA-seq consortia, will be essential to ensure reproducibility and cross-platform comparability. Researchers worldwide are actively advancing these areas, and ongoing advances may address many of these challenges over the next three to five years. In particular, advances in deep learning algorithms and cloud computing will further automate data analysis workflows, improving the scalability of HMTI methods. This will enable real-time processing of large, high-dimensional datasets, reducing the dependency on manual review and enhancing reproducibility across laboratories. As cyberinfrastructure improves, the integration of HMTI technologies with cloud platforms will facilitate collaborative research at unprecedented scales. However, several critical challenges remain unresolved and lack mature solutions (Fig. 4A), including (i) the lack of ground-truth datasets for benchmarking segmentation and cell-type annotation, (ii) the absence of standardized quality control metrics for HMTI, and (iii) the limited imaging throughput and technical challenges that constrain accurate 3D reconstruction in 3D spatial proteomics. Compared with GeoMx DSP, which can profile up to 1,200 proteins, most HMTI platforms provide much greater spatial resolution, but at the cost of substantially lower proteomic depth. Future innovations in spectral resolution, sensor technologies, and automated sample preparation technologies may help overcome current limitations related to background noise, resolution, and spectral overlap. These improvements will enable higher-precision imaging, leading to better spatial mapping of protein expression and cellular features.

In addition, although spatial HMTI has substantially deepened our understanding of the underlying mechanisms of diseases such as tumors and infections, the clinical implementation of HMTI remains in its infancy [12, 125–129]. Major barriers include variability in staining protocols, lack of standardized analytical pipelines and benchmark datasets, computational and scalability challenges for high-dimensional data, and insufficient multi-center validation of proposed spatial biomarkers (Fig. 4B). Although FDA-approved highly multiplexed spatial proteomics-based diagnostics are not yet widely established, existing tissue-based companion diagnostics provide useful translational precedents. For example, PD-L1 IHC 22C3 pharmDx illustrates how spatially contextual protein biomarkers can be clinically implemented through standardized staining, predefined scoring rules, validated thresholds, and quality-control procedures. Similar principles will be essential for future HMTI-derived spatial biomarkers before they can be translated into routine clinical practice. In particular, progress will require advances in three areas: regulatory readiness, including assay standardization, quality control metrics, reproducibility across platforms and sites, and locked-down analysis pipelines; clinical validation, including large-scale multi-center and prospective studies to establish the robustness, generalizability, and clinical utility of spatial biomarkers; and workflow integration, including compatibility with routine pathology practice, turnaround time, reporting standards, and integration with digital pathology, radiology, and laboratory information systems. In the short term, efforts should focus on standardizing pre-analytical and staining procedures, defining quantitative thresholds for spatial biomarkers, and establishing benchmark datasets and external quality assessment frameworks. Open-access repositories and collaborative platforms are also crucial for promoting data sharing, enabling researchers worldwide to contribute to large-scale tissue atlases [130]. At the same time, ethical issues related to patient privacy, informed consent, data governance, and responsible data sharing must be carefully addressed to support the clinical translation of HMTI.

Another major barrier to the clinical adoption of HMTI is cost. These technologies remain expensive, often require specialized instrumentation, and place substantial demands on data processing and personnel training. To reduce per-sample costs, studies often use tissue microarrays (TMAs), but this approach may miss spatial heterogeneity and introduce sampling bias, particularly in biologically heterogeneous lesions, limiting its value for routine clinical diagnosis. In this context, a particularly promising direction is the use of artificial intelligence (AI) to infer HMTI-like spatial proteomic information directly from routine H&E images. Recent studies have shown that deep learning can computationally generate multiplexed protein maps from standard histopathology, as illustrated by HistoPlexer [131], ROSIE [132], and HEX [133], which progressively extend this concept from proof-of-principle protein multiplex generation to larger-scale, clinically relevant virtual spatial proteomics. Such approaches may substantially reduce the cost, technical complexity, and tissue consumption associated with experimental HMTI, while broadening access to spatially resolved molecular information in routine pathology settings. Looking ahead, integration of these models with real-time image analytics and privacy-preserving federated learning may further facilitate rapid quality assessment.

Conclusion

For researchers new to HMTI, a practical strategy may be to start with TSA-based mIHC when only 6–8 markers are required. When larger marker panels are needed, fluorescence-based technologies may be a more suitable option because they enable higher-plex imaging with validated antibody panels available and still allows H&E staining on the same tissue after multiplex imaging, which can help new users cross-check spatial findings against familiar histopathological features. For data analysis, low-code tools such as QuPath can serve as a useful starting point. With extensions such as StarDist or CellPose, QuPath supports cell segmentation and quantitative feature extraction, and its supervised object classifiers can be trained with limited manual annotation for basic cell phenotyping. More advanced spatial analyses, including intercellular distance and neighborhood analysis, can then be performed through QuPath-compatible extensions or downstream tools as needed.

In conclusion, continued development and integration of HMTI technologies with advanced computational tools are likely to accelerate progress in spatial biology and precision medicine, improving how complex biological systems and disease progression are studied. As these technologies become more accessible and standardized, they may become increasingly important in both basic and clinical research, providing profound insights into cellular heterogeneity, tissue architecture, and therapeutic development.
