Buried-interface homogenization by asymmetric polymeric self-assembled layers powers efficient, durable flexible perovskite photovoltaics
Authors: Qingping Tang, Hao Liu, Jike Ding, Zuolin Zhang, Wenjie Lin, Zhenzhen Liu, Zesheng Deng, Tao Chen, Guoqing Li, Jiaxing Song, Zhiheng Wang, Zaifang Li, Cong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flexible perovskite solar cells and modules are limited by buried-interface nonuniformity and the thickness-sensitive, aggregation-prone nature of conventional small-molecule self-assembled monolayers, which create local recombination hot spots and crack-initiated degradation during large-area coating and bending. Here we introduce asymmetric polymeric self-assembled layers based on poly(4-(3-phenyl-9H-carbazol-9-yl)butyl phosphate) (Poly-3Ph-4PACz), a polycarbazole phosphonic acid featuring rigid phenyl-carbazole units and multiple anchoring sites. The polymeric architecture yields a dense, highly wetting and electrically homogeneous hole-selective contact with enhanced interfacial dipole and strong perovskite binding, enabling uniform crystallization, reduced non-radiative losses and effective stress buffering. Flexible devices reach 26.13% champion power conversion efficiency, while 57.6 cm2 flexible modules deliver 22.22%. Encapsulated modules retain 97.23% after 3000 bending cycles and show damp-heat resilience with T95 > 1000 h at 85 °C/85% RH. Beyond flexibility, Poly-3Ph-4PACz enables 27.18% rigid inverted perovskite solar cells (certified mean steady-state 27.12%) and a certified 32.95% perovskite/Si tandem efficiency. These results establish polymeric self-assembly as a scalable route to high-efficiency, durable flexible photovoltaics.

Introduction

Flexible photovoltaics are emerging as a cornerstone technology for lightweight, shape-adaptive and rollable photovoltaics, enabling power sources for wearables, portable electronics and curved building surfaces1–4. Yet, despite the rapid progress of perovskite photovoltaics on rigid glass, the performance and reliability of flexible devices still lag behind5–7. This gap is not merely a matter of incremental efficiency; it reflects fundamental bottlenecks in scalable processing and in the mechanical-electrical integrity of the buried interfaces under repeated deformation, thermal fluctuations, and electrical stress4,5,7,8. A central challenge is that flexible stacks impose stringent processing constraints and amplify interfacial weaknesses. Low-temperature fabrication on polymer substrates, together with the intrinsically rougher and more defect-prone flexible transparent electrodes, often leads to incomplete wetting, nonuniform nucleation and locally heterogeneous perovskite growth9–11. These local inhomogeneities evolve into electrical “hot spots” and mechanically vulnerable regions, where microcracks, delamination and accelerated degradation are preferentially initiated during bending and long-term operation12–15. Consequently, flexible PSCs frequently exhibit the coupled failure modes of reduced power conversion efficiency (PCE), inferior operational stability and poor bend endurance-precisely the properties required for practical flexible photovoltaics.

The buried hole-selective interface, typically formed by self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) or ultrathin hole transport interlayers on conductive substrates, is widely recognized as a decisive junction governing nucleation, interfacial recombination and mechanical adhesion16–18. Small-molecule carbazole-phosphonic acid SAMs, such as [4-(3,6-dimethyl-9H-carbazol-9-yl)butyl] phosphonic acid (Me-4PACz), have enabled high efficiencies exceeding 27% in inverted PSCs19–21, but their monomeric nature also introduces intrinsic vulnerabilities during large-area and flexible processing, including steric-driven molecular aggregation22,23, incomplete surface coverage on rough electrodes24–26, and a pronounced sensitivity to local thickness/packing variations27,28. In practice, these effects manifest as scattered surface potential landscapes and irregular interfacial electronic properties, indicative of phase segregation and nonuniform SAMs growth on ITO for conventional PACz-type layers. Such nonuniform buried interfaces not only penalize device performance but also provide preferred pathways for defect formation and stress concentration under bending.

Recent studies have advanced SAMs/interface design to mitigate these issues, including molecular asymmetry to suppress aggregation and enhance homogeneous coverage, co-self-assembled strategies to fill voids and improve hydrophilicity/uniform electric-field distribution, and linker engineering to strengthen binding and stabilize the interface5,6,26. These approaches collectively underscore an emerging consensus, that is, for flexible PSCs, interfacial layers must be simultaneously highly wetting and process-robust for scalable coating, electronically homogeneous to suppress local recombination and parasitic injection, and mechanically resilient to buffer deformation-induced stress1,2. However, achieving these attributes within a single monolayer-scale material remains difficult, particularly when targeting thickness insensitivity and interface robustness required by large-area manufacturing.

An instructive paradigm is polymeric hole-transporter design, where polymeric architectures can combine strong substrate anchoring with viscoelastic stress dissipation and improved film-forming tolerance5,27–31. In ambient blade-coated PSCs, polymeric hole transporters have been shown to strengthen chemical bonding at the electrode interface and suppress interfacial non-radiative losses, offering a pathway toward operationally robust devices under harsh electrical conditions28. In situ crosslinked phosphonic-acid polymeric SAMs (poly-V3PACz) convert easily desorbed molecular monolayers into a densely packed, highly conductive, wettable, and anti-solvent-resistant hole-selective interface, thereby mitigating perovskite film/interfacial defects and enabling high-efficiency, long-term stable inverted PSCs30. Extending these concepts to flexible photovoltaics suggests that a polymeric, SAM-like interlayer could simultaneously solve the long-standing trade-off between molecular-level selectivity and polymer-level mechanical/process robustness.

Herein, we report poly(4-(3-phenyl-9H-carbazol-9-yl)butyl phosphate) (poly-3Ph-4PACz), an asymmetric short-chain polymer SAM synthesized via synergistic polymerization of a carbazole phosphonic acid incorporating a rigid phenyl-carbazole unit. This design unifies high hole mobility, robust interfacial anchoring, and efficient stress buffering within one material platform. Poly-3Ph-4PACz exhibits a substantially enhanced interfacial dipole and strengthened binding to the perovskite buried surface, enabling stronger interfacial band modulation. Critically for scalable manufacturing, poly-3Ph-4PACz improves substrate wettability and supports more uniform, smoother interlayers, which promote uniform precursor spreading and high-quality film formation. Beyond average properties, poly-3Ph-4PACz directly addresses the local heterogeneity problem that undermines flexible performance. These features collectively render poly-3Ph-4PACz highly thickness-tolerant, interface-robust, and manufacturing-relevant traits that are difficult to realize with monomeric 3Ph-4PACz-type SAMs.

Leveraging these interfacial advantages, poly-3Ph-4PACz enables high-performance and mechanically robust devices across rigid, flexible, and module form factors, with an efficiency 26.13%. Importantly, 57.6 cm2 scalable flexible modules deliver a high PCE of 22.22%, highlighting the process robustness required for practical manufacturing. Device stability and bend endurance are simultaneously strengthened, with the flexible devices retaining 97.23% of initial efficiency after 3000 bending cycles, and exhibiting damp-heat resilience with T95 > 1000 h at 85 °C/85% RH. Mechanistically, the high-uniformity polymeric interlayer suppresses the formation of local heterogeneities at the buried interface, thereby mitigating parasitic injection/recombination pathways and delaying crack-initiated degradation, an effect that is essential for flexible photovoltaics. Notably, we further demonstrate the compatibility of poly-3Ph-4PACz in rigid PSCs and tandem architectures, reaching a certified mean steady-state PCE of 27.12%, and achieving a certified 32.95% perovskite/Si tandem efficiency, underscoring the broader relevance of this molecular platform for next-generation high-efficiency photovoltaics.

Results

Synthesis and structural characterization of poly-3Ph-4PACz

The synthesis of poly-3Ph-4PACz was achieved through a multi-step reaction (Fig. 1a). Precisely, 3,6-dibromocarbazole was N-alkylated with 1,4-dibromobutane in aqueous KOH using TBAB (C16H36BrN) as a phase-transfer catalyst (60 °C, 12 h) to afford N-(4-bromobutyl)-3,6-dibromocarbazole. The terminal bromide was converted to the corresponding diethyl phosphonate by a Michaelis–Arbuzov reaction with triethyl phosphite (P(OEt)3) (150 °C, 12 h). The dibromo phosphonate monomer was polymerized via Pd(PPh3)4-catalyzed Suzuki coupling with an aryl boronic acid comonomer in 1,4-dioxane (C4H8O2) under N2, using 50 wt% K2CO3 as base (70 °C, 12 h), affording the phosphonate-protected poly-3Ph-4PACz precursor. Finally, the diethyl phosphonate groups were de-esterified with trimethylsilyl bromide ((CH3)3SiBr) in dioxane under N2 at room temperature for 12 h, followed by hydrolysis to yield poly-3Ph-4PACz (phosphonic acid form). The polymer was purified by repeated precipitation in methanol and vacuum-dried. This synthetic route utilizes low-cost starting materials and mild reaction conditions, facilitating future large-scale production. The unique asymmetric structure of poly-3Ph-4PACz was comprehensively confirmed by spectroscopic techniques. Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra (Fig. S1) revealed a significant red shift in poly-3Ph-4PACz films compared to the 3Ph-4PACz monomer, directly evidencing the formation of extended conjugated structures along the polymer backbone after polymerization, indicating effective conjugation between the benzene rings and the carbazole skeleton. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization-time of flight (MALDI-TOF, Fig. S2) analysis primarily showed signals corresponding to trimeric species, which is attributed to the inherent laser-induced fragmentation of the bulky side groups during the severe ionization process. To accurately determine the molecular weight of the synthesized material, gel permeation chromatography (GPC, Fig. S3) was performed, revealing a number-average molecular weight (Mn) of 2545 g/mol and a narrow polydispersity index of 1.036. Given the monomer mass of 435 g/mol, this indicates an average degree of polymerization of ~6. These results collectively validate the successful synthesis of short-chain poly-3Ph-4PACz, featuring the anticipated asymmetric phenyl-carbazole rigid units and a polycarbazole backbone. Crucially, compared to monomeric molecules, this unique short-chain architecture provides multiple anchoring sites per molecule, enabling robust multi-dentate interfacial binding. Enhanced dipole moment and interfacial binding by polymerization

To elucidate the impact of polymerization on the molecular structure and its subsequent influence on interfacial charge dynamics, electrostatic potential (ESP) calculations were performed to evaluate the molecular dipole moment. As shown in Fig. 1b, the calculated molecular dipole moment for the 3Ph-4PACz monomer was 3.60 D. Notably, upon polymerization to form poly-3Ph-4PACz, its molecular dipole moment significantly increased to 6.44 D. This substantial increase in dipole moment suggests that when poly-3Ph-4PACz is employed as a hole transporting layer (HTL), it can induce a more robust dipole orientation at the interface. This enhanced dipole orientation effectively strengthens interfacial charge separation and transport performance23,34.

We then systematically evaluated the adsorption characteristics of the 3Ph-4PACz and poly-3Ph-4PACz on the perovskite surface using density functional theory (DFT) calculations. The calculated binding energy of 3Ph-4PACz with perovskite was determined to be −1.45 eV. In contrast, the binding energy of poly-3Ph-4PACz was significantly enhanced to −1.77 eV (Fig. 1c). This substantial increase clearly demonstrates that the polymeric backbone dramatically strengthens the interfacial interaction of HTLs with the buried perovskite. Fundamentally, this enhanced interaction is driven by the thermodynamic advantage of multidentate coordination, a mechanism analogous to the chelate effect. Unlike discrete 3Ph-4PACz monomers that undergo substantial translational and rotational entropy loss upon individual adsorption, the polymeric backbone facilitates synergistic coordination. Once an initial phosphonic acid group anchors to the substrate, the subsequent binding of adjacent groups along the chain incurs a minimal additional entropy penalty. Furthermore, the extended conjugated backbone of Poly-3Ph-4PACz, in conjunction with its multiple phosphonic acid moieties, enables a strong synergistic interaction with the [PbI6]4− octahedra of the perovskite lattice. Specifically, the oxygen atoms of the phosphonic acid groups establish strong coordination bonds with the undercoordinated Pb2⁺ defects, while the extended π system of the backbone provides stronger π–π stacking and maximizes the contact area. This structurally optimized molecular stacking and multidentate coordination collectively establish a more robust and energetically favorable interface.

The robust anchoring provided by poly-3Ph-4PACz was further corroborated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). It is revealed that the poly-3Ph-4PACz/perovskite samples exhibited a distinct shift of the Pb 4f peak to higher binding energies (0.13 eV), compared to that of 3Ph-4PACz/perovskite films (Fig. S4). This observation indicates a modified chemical environment at the interface, which we attribute to the effective passivation of poly-3Ph-4PACz on uncoordinated Pb2+ defects35. The superior defect passivation capability contributes to reduced interfacial recombination. Collectively, the strongly negative binding energy and the stable molecular-level contact established by poly-3Ph-4PACz provide a solid foundation for the enhanced mechanical stability and suppressed interfacial degradation observed in the corresponding devices.

Improved interfacial wettability and surface coverage

Atomic force microscope (AFM) images (Fig. S5) confirmed significantly improved film quality for poly-3Ph-4PACz compared to the 3Ph-4PACz monomer on ITO. The surface roughness (RMS) of the poly-3Ph-4PACz film was reduced from 1.88 to 1.56 nm, indicating a smoother and more uniform morphology36. Additionally, we have observed that spin-coated perovskite films exhibit incomplete coverage when deposited on 3Ph-4PACz, whereas perovskite films applied on poly-3Ph-4PACz are fully and uniformly covered (Fig. S6). Subsequently, the effect of poly-3Ph-4PACz on surface wettability was investigated, via water contact angle (WCA) (Fig. 1d). The ITO/poly-3Ph-4PACz substrate exhibited a WCA of 56.0°, demonstrating superior wettability compared to the ITO/3Ph-4PACz substrate (WCA: 63.9°). We also detected changes in the contact angle of DMF solution. From this, we can see that the DMF contact angle of poly-3Ph-4PACz is relatively 6.4°, lower than that of 22.2° for the 3Ph-4PACz samples. This wettability is vital for large-area depositing processes, facilitating uniform spreading of perovskite precursor solutions and creating favorable conditions for high-quality perovskite film deposition. This can be attributed to the strong covalent bonding capability of the tridentate phosphonic acid groups in poly-3Ph-4PACz and the flexibility of its polymer chains, enabling superior filling of nanoscale depressions on the ITO substrate surface and achieving ultra-high coverage37,38.

Regulation of perovskite morphology and film formation

As a key linking layer material between ITO and perovskite at the buried interface, poly-3Ph-4PACz is expected to regulate perovskite crystallization kinetics, suppress lattice stress, and passivate interfacial defects, thereby being conductive to improve the overall quality of perovskite films. Top surface scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 2a, b) revealed that the control perovskite films grown on 3Ph-4PACz substrates exhibited discontinuous grain distribution with prominent grain boundary defects. In contrast, the poly-3Ph-4PACz-based perovskite films exhibited significantly improved morphology with minimal voids and a continuous film. Cross-sectional SEM images (Fig. 2c, d) revealed that the perovskite film on poly-3Ph-4PACz is denser, more continuous, and tightly bonded at the interface without defects, unlike the control sample, which exhibited voids and nonuniform grain boundaries. Crystallization control in perovskite films

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. 2e) revealed that the (100) peak intensity of the perovskite film on the poly-3Ph-4PACz substrate was higher than on 3Ph-4PACz, indicating highly oriented crystal growth along the (100) plane. Additionally, the sharp and unshifted diffraction peaks confirm the high crystallinity and phase purity of the perovskite film. This highly oriented and high-quality growth is attributed to the benzene ring structural units in poly-3Ph-4PACz, which form a more ordered molecular arrangement through π–π stacking interactions. This ordered interfacial template effectively regulates the perovskite nucleation process, inducing preferential growth along the (100) crystal plane, consequently enhancing crystal quality.

Suppressed non-radiative recombination and defect density

We then measured steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra (Fig. 2f) and PL mapping (Fig. 2g, h) of the perovskite films. Steady-state PL spectra reveal a higher PL intensity for perovskite films on poly-3Ph-4PACz compared to that based on 3Ph-4PACz39,40. PL mapping further confirmed markedly enhanced intensity and uniformity at the perovskite film. To quantify the reduced defect density, we performed time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements (Fig. 2i) on the perovskite films on the bare glass substrate. TRPL measurements indicate that perovskite films prepared on poly-3Ph-4PACz exhibited carrier lifetimes of τ1 = 0.384 μs/τ2 = 1.543 μs, significantly longer than those of τ1 = 0.116 μs/τ2 = 0.838 μs for the 3Ph-4PACz-based samples. These higher PL intensity and longer carrier lifetimes directly illustrate lower non-radiative recombination rates and more effective defect passivation, crucial for enhancing device open-circuit voltage (VOC) and fill factor (FF).

Homogenized surface potential and energy level alignment

Then, we evaluated the effect of these polymeric SAMs on the surface electrical properties of the substrate. Through Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) analysis, surface potential statistics (Fig. 3a–c) revealed that the average contact potential difference (CPD) for ITO/NiOx/poly-3Ph-4PACz samples exhibited a distinct shift compared to that of the ITO/NiOx/3Ph-4PACz counterpart. The full width at half maximum of the CPD statistics decreased substantially from 15.7 mV for 3Ph-4PACz to 7.4 mV for poly-3Ph-4PACz. This provides compelling evidence that the polymerization strategy and tridentate phosphonic acid groups of poly-3Ph-4PACz achieve more uniform and denser interfacial coverage, overcoming the poor uniformity of conventional SAMs on large-area rough substrates41. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy was conducted to investigate the band alignment at the buried interface. From the secondary electron cutoff edge and valence band region (Fig. 3d–f), the Fermi levels (EF) of 3Ph-4PACz and poly-3Ph-4PACz films were determined to be −4.55 and −4.78 eV, respectively. Their highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) levels were calculated as −5.23 and −5.30 eV, respectively. With the Cs0.1MA0.05FA0.85PbI3 perovskite valence band at −5.39 eV, the deeper HOMO of poly-3Ph-4PACz enables superior energy alignment with the perovskite valence band (Fig. 3g–i). This optimized alignment reduces the energy barrier for hole extraction, enhancing efficiency and minimizing interfacial recombination losses, which is crucial for increasing the VOC of the PSCs.

Enhanced and uniform charge transport properties

Furthermore, the expanded conjugated system of benzene rings in poly-3Ph-4PACz and electron delocalization on the polymer backbone significantly promote intra- and inter-chain charge transport. The enhanced conductivity and superior hole transport homogeneity of poly-3Ph-4PACz were unequivocally demonstrated by conductive atomic force microscopy (c-AFM). The c-AFM images (Fig. 3j–l) visually revealed a more homogeneous and significantly increased current distribution for the poly-3Ph-4PACz film. Quantitative analysis corroborated this observation, that is, the average surface current was dramatically enhanced more than twofold from ~42 nA of 3Ph-4PACz to approximately 87 nA of poly-3Ph-4PACz. More importantly, line-scan profiles extracted from the scanned surfaces revealed a stark contrast in current uniformity. The current for 3Ph-4PACz fluctuated drastically within a wide range of 40–500 nA, indicating inconsistent conductive pathways. In contrast, the current for poly-3Ph-4PACz exhibited exceptional uniformity, with fluctuations confined to within ±10 nA. This remarkably narrow distribution underscores the formation of a continuous and uniform hole-transport network, which is critical for minimizing localized recombination losses. Strategically more matching energy level of the hole transport layer, this unique attribute of high and homogeneous conductivity facilitates efficient hole extraction across the entire interface. This unique attribute provides a theoretical basis for improving hole extraction efficiency and reducing VOC losses42,43.

Device architecture and photovoltaic performance of flexible PSCs

We fabricated the inverted flexible devices with the architecture of PET/ITO/NiOx/SAMs/perovskite/PDI2/C60/BCP/Ag (small active area) and PET/ITO/NiOx/SAMs/perovskite/C60/ALD-SnOx/Ag (modules) to evaluate the effect of polymeric hole-transport layer on photovoltaic performance (Fig. 4a). Perovskite films were crystallized in air using a vacuum flash-evaporation process. Typical 1.53 eV Cs0.1MA0.05FA0.85PbI3 absorber formulations were employed for film and device fabrication. The control 3Ph-4PACz-based flexible PSCs only exhibited a PCE of 24.46% (a JSC of 26.08 mA cm−2, a VOC of 1.133 V, and an FF of 82.76%) in RS mode and 24.31% (a JSC of 26.10 mA cm−2, a VOC of 1.132 V, and an FF of 82.32%) in FS mode. In contrast, the poly-3Ph-4PACz-based PSCs exhibited an improved champion PCE of 26.13% (a JSC of 26.19 mA cm−2, a VOC of 1.181 V, and an FF of 84.43%) in RS mode and 26.01% (a JSC of 26.17 mA cm−2, a VOC of 1.181 V, and an FF of 84.13%) in FS mode (Figs. 4b and S7). To probe the origin of the performance gains, we examined charge transport in poly-3Ph-4PACz-based devices using complementary electrical analyses. Single-hole devices were first measured by space-charge-limited current to quantify defect density based on hole-transport layer. From the trap-filled limit voltage (VTFL) and Nt = 2ε₀εrVTFL/qL2, the control 3Ph-4PACz device shows Nt = 6.44 × 1015 cm−3 (VTFL = 0.89 V), whereas poly-3Ph-4PACz reduces Nt to 4.34 × 1015 cm−3 (VTFL = 0.60 V) (Fig. S8), consistent with suppressed bulk trapping. Dark J–V measurements support this trend, with lower reverse saturation current and dark current in poly-3Ph-4PACz devices (Fig. S9), indicating reduced non-radiative recombination under bias27. Mott–Schottky analysis reveals a higher built-in potential (Vbi) for poly-3Ph-4PACz devices (Fig. S10), consistent with improved carrier separation and transport. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) further shows comparable series resistance (Rs) but markedly increased recombination resistance (Rrec) for poly-3Ph-4PACz (Fig. S11), indicating more efficient extraction and weaker recombination. Light-intensity-dependent VOC (Fig. S12) analyzed using qVOC = Eg-nkT ln(I0/I) yields a reduced ideality factor n for poly-3Ph-4PACz, consistent with suppressed trap-assisted recombination34. Finally, transient photovoltage/photocurrent measurements (TPV/TPC) show a longer recombination lifetime (0.34 μs vs. 0.29 μs) and faster transport (0.26 ns vs. 0.39 ns) for poly-3Ph-4PACz (Figs. Scalable module performance and thickness tolerance

We then assessed the suitability of poly-3Ph-4PACz for large-area devices and scalable processing. Usually, large-area fabrication requires robust film formation while maintaining low sensitivity to variations in solution concentration. By systematically modulating the solution concentration of 3Ph-4PACz or poly-3Ph-4PACz from 0.5 to 5 mg mL−1, it is revealed that the poly-3Ph-4PACz exhibits lower sensitivity to concentration, always maintaining a device efficiency exceeding 25% even with variations from 0.5 to 5 mg mL−1 (Figs. S15 and S16). In comparison, devices based on 3Ph-4PACz exhibited a sharp decline in performance at concentrations exceeding 5 mg mL−1. These results clearly illustrate the thickness-insensitive characteristics of poly-3Ph-4PACz, substantially expanding its processing window for large-area coating techniques. This attribute effectively addresses the pronounced thickness sensitivity commonly associated with conventional SAMs, marking a critical advancement toward scalable device fabrication. On a large-area 57.6 cm2 flexible module (12 sections connected in series), we achieved a breakthrough efficiency of 22.22% (VOC = 13.14 V, FF = 77.74%, JSC = 2.17 mA cm−2) in RS mode and 21.99% (VOC = 13.06 V, FF = 77.46%, JSC = 2.17 mA cm−2) in FS mode, higher than that of 20.08% (VOC = 13.04 V, FF = 71.80%, JSC = 2.14 mA cm−2) in RS mode and 19.69% (VOC = 13.03 V, FF = 70.42%, JSC = 2.14 mA cm−2) in FS mode (Fig. 4c). This result fully demonstrates the immense potential of poly-3Ph-4PACz for large-area flexible PSC module applications. Thus, poly-3Ph-4PACz demonstrates promising performance in flexible modules.

Enhanced operational stability and mechanical durability

Beyond boosting device efficiency, poly-3Ph-4PACz markedly enhances the long-term stability of flexible PSCs, particularly during humid heat aging tests at 85 °C/85% RH (Fig. 4d–f). Poly-3Ph-4PACz- based flexible PSCs could exhibit good stability, maintaining 96.4% of initial efficiency after 1000 h aging (T95 > 1000 h). In contrast, the control 3Ph-4PACz-based flexible PSCs device exhibited obvious efficiency degradation within 400 h under identical conditions. To elucidate the degradation pathways of perovskite modules, we conducted electroluminescence mapping (Fig. 4e, f) on intact devices both before and after damp-heat aging, as it provides insights into device performance under operational conditions free from lateral carrier diffusion artifacts and thus offers a more accurate assessment of film quality and recombination losses46,47. Devices with 3Ph-4PACz display widespread dark regions due to localized degradation, corresponding to an efficiency drop to 72.7% of the initial value. This degradation can be primarily related to the thermal instability of the small-molecule SAMs under 85 °C stress. Specifically, the non-cross-linked 3Ph-4PACz molecules tend to undergo thermal aggregation or partial desorption, which compromises the integrity, creates shunt paths, and facilitates ion migration across the interface. In contrast, those with poly-3Ph-4PACz exhibited highly uniform emission with only slight dimming and a high retained efficiency of 96.4%, underscoring their robust structural integrity.

Maximum power point (MPP) tracking stability testing (Fig. S17) was also carried out under simulated AM 1.5G illumination for 550 h. The large-area poly-3Ph-4PACz module could maintain 94.44% of its initial efficiency throughout the test, whereas the 3Ph-4PACz device exhibited 48.67% efficiency degradation after 300 h. Furthermore, to evaluate the long-term thermal robustness of the polymeric SAMs, we conducted additional stability tests at 65 °C in ambient air. As shown in Fig. S18, the poly-3Ph-4PACz-based devices retained over 99.19% of their initial PCE after 220 h, while the 3Ph-4PACz-based control devices dropped significantly to 65.84% within the same period. This superior thermal stability is primarily attributed to the suppressed molecular migration and enhanced interfacial anchoring of the polymerized SAMs compared to conventional small-molecule SAMs, highlighting the great potential of poly-3Ph-4PACz for durable and high-performance perovskite photovoltaics.

The mechanical durability is assessed through bending tests on a large-area 57.6 cm2 all-flexible module with POE encapsulation (Fig. 4g), which demonstrates that the encapsulated module maintains 97.23% of its original efficiency after undergoing 3000 flex cycles at a flex angle of 80°. This result underscores the enhancement in mechanical robustness afforded by the poly-3Ph-4PACz treatment. This demonstrates that the viscoelastic poly-3Ph-4PACz interface effectively dissipates mechanical stress during repeated bending, thereby preserving the structural integrity of the perovskite film and ensuring excellent mechanical durability. To better understand the origin of the improved bending durability, we further investigated the residual stress and mechanical properties of the flexible perovskite films by GIXRD (Fig. 4h–k) and nanoindentation (Fig. S19). GIXRD measurements were first carried out at different tilt angles, ψ. Compared with the 3Ph-4PACz-based film, the poly-3Ph-4PACz-modified film exhibits a much smaller ψ-dependent shift of the selected perovskite diffraction peak, indicating reduced internal stress evolution. Linear fitting of the 2θ-sin2ψ plots gives slopes of −0.07309 and −0.04042 for the films based on 3Ph-4PACz and poly-3Ph-4PACz, respectively, corresponding to residual stresses of 17.34 and 9.59 MPa, respectively. The smaller slope and lower residual stress suggest that poly-3Ph-4PACz effectively alleviates stress accumulation in the flexible perovskite film. We then performed nanoindentation measurements to directly probe the mechanical properties. The Young’s modulus values of 3Ph-4PACz and poly-3Ph-4PACz are 5.73 and 4.60 GPa, respectively, indicating that the poly-3Ph-4PACz interface is mechanically more compliant. After perovskite deposition, the Young’s modulus further decreases from 26.83 GPa for the 3Ph-4PACz-based film to 18.08 GPa for the poly-3Ph-4PACz-based film. This reduced modulus indicates improved mechanical compliance of the perovskite film, which is beneficial for accommodating deformation and mitigating stress concentration during repeated bending. Together, these results provide direct mechanical evidence that poly-3Ph-4PACz reduces residual stress and improves the mechanical compliance of the flexible perovskite film, thereby contributing to its enhanced mechanical durability.

Flexible PSCs demonstrate broad potential in various self-powered IoT applications due to their high energy capture efficiency under low-light indoor conditions and mechanical flexibility. These applications encompass devices powered by indoor LED lighting and naturally diffused light. We also leveraged this poly-3Ph-4PACz-based platform to fabricate several flexible, photovoltaic-powered demonstration prototypes (Fig. S20). Flexible PSCs can be integrated into nightlights to enable day-night charging cycles and illumination, enhancing smart home convenience. They can also power smart sensors and security devices like thermometers, hygrometers, and fingerprint locks, freeing them from traditional power constraints. Furthermore, their flexibility makes them an ideal power source for flexible electronics and wearable technologies, such as Bluetooth keyboards, light-emitting labels, and beacons, effectively promoting device miniaturization and mobility.

High-efficiency rigid perovskite solar cells

Poly-3Ph-4PACz delivers benefits beyond flexible devices, showing excellent compatibility with rigid architectures and perovskite/Si tandem cells as well. As shown from the J–V curve in Fig. 5a, the poly-3Ph-4PACz-based inverted PSC (Glass/ITO/NiOx/SAMs/perovskite/PDI2/C60/BCP/Ag) achieves an efficiency of 27.18%, with a VOC of 1.188 V, a JSC of 26.25 mA cm−2, and an FF of 87.15%. In comparison, the 3Ph-4PACz-based device exhibited an efficiency of 25.22%, with a VOC of 1.164 V, JSC of 25.84 mA cm−2, and FF of 83.83% (Fig. S21). The poly-3Ph-4PACz device demonstrated significant improvements in both VOC and FF, which directly benefited from the optimized energy level alignment and efficient interface defect passivation of poly-3Ph-4PACz. To validate the JSC values, external quantum efficiency (EQE) measurements (Fig. 5b) were conducted. The calculated JSC values from the EQE spectra (JSC = 25.4 mA cm−2) closely match the JSC measured from the J–V curve. Further, certified by a third-party certification party, the poly-3Ph-4PACz-based inverted PSC demonstrates a mean steady-state PCE of 27.12% with the 300-s tracking, as shown in Fig. S22 (Supporting Information). Performance of wide-bandgap perovskite solar cells

We further validated poly-3Ph-4PACz for 1.68 eV wide-bandgap (WBG) perovskites and tandem solar cells (Fig. 5f, g). As illustrated in Fig. 5f, the control 3Ph-4PACz-based 1.68 eV WBG PSCs only exhibited a PCE of 22.88% (a JSC of 21.89 mA cm−2, a VOC of 1.262 V, and an FF of 82.84%) in RS mode and 22.38% (a JSC of 21.81 mA cm−2, a VOC of 1.259 V, and an FF of 81.52%) in FS mode. In contrast, the poly-3Ph-4PACz-based 1.68 eV WBG PSCs exhibited an improved champion PCE of 24.08% (a JSC of 21.89 mA cm−2, a VOC of 1.289 V, and an FF of 85.05%) in RS mode and 23.86% (a JSC of 21.97 mA cm−2, a VOC of 1.287 V, and an FF of 84.34%) in FS mode. These results demonstrate the universality of our developed buried-interface passivation method. The EQE curves (Fig. S23) of the device with a 1.68 eV WBG perovskite based on control and poly-3Ph-4PACz were 20.82 and 21.07 mA cm−2, respectively, which match the JSC values from J–V curves.

High-performance perovskite/Si tandem solar cells

We then integrated the poly-3Ph-4PACz-based 1.68 eV perovskite sub-cells into a 1.001 cm2 perovskite/Si tandem solar cell, and achieved a certified PCE of 32.95% (National Center of Inspection on Solar Photovoltaic Product Quality, Fig. S24). Figure 5g shows the J–V curves of the best-performing tandem solar cell from lab testing, exhibiting a high PCE value of 32.95% (with a VOC of 1.980 V, a JSC of 20.91 mA cm−2, and an FF of 79.66%). Integrated JSC values of 20.91 and 20.89 mA cm−2, derived from the EQE spectra (Fig. 5h) for the top and bottom sub-cells, respectively, matched well with the JSC determined from the J–V sweep. Thus, poly-3Ph-4PACz established low-loss, hole-selective contacts in perovskite/Si tandem solar cells, demonstrating wide applicability.

Discussion

By shifting from fragile monomeric SAMs to an asymmetric polymeric self-assembled layer, this work breaks the long-standing trade-off between interfacial selectivity and manufacturability in perovskite photovoltaics: the poly-3Ph-4PACz platform is simultaneously wetting, thickness-tolerant, and mechanically stress-buffering while suppressing buried-interface heterogeneity that triggers local recombination and crack-initiated failure. Practically, it delivers 26.13% flexible devices and 22.22% efficiency on a 57.6 cm2 flexible module, maintains 97.23% of initial efficiency after 3000 bending cycles, and achieves T95 > 1000 h under 85 °C/85% RH, alongside a widened processing window for scalable coating. Beyond flexible form factors, the same buried-interface strategy translates to certified 27.12% rigid inverted cells and a certified 32.95% perovskite/Si tandem, underscoring its generality for next-generation high-efficiency photovoltaics. Finally, the demonstrated low-light energy harvesting and prototype devices point to near-term deployment in self-powered IoT and wearable electronics, where robust interfaces are essential under real deformation and harsh environments.

Methods

Materials

Anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.8%) and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%) were purchased from Aladdin. Lead iodide (PbI2), cesium iodide (CsI), lead bromide (PbBr2) and methylamine hydrochloride (MAI) were purchased from Advanced Election Technology Co., Ltd. Methylammonium bromide (MABr), formamidine iodide (FAI) were purchased from Greatcell Solar Materials. NiOx nanoparticles, C60, and PET was obtained from Jiaxing Daze Photoenergy Co., Ltd. Ag was purchased from Beijing Zhongjin Yan New Materials Technology Co., Ltd.

Preparation of perovskite precursor solutions

1.53 eV-Cs0.1MA0.05FA0.85PbI3

The perovskite precursor solution (Cs0.1MA0.05FA0.85PbI3, 1.5 M) was prepared by mixing PbI2 (691.5 mg), FAI (219.3 mg), CsI (38.97 mg), and MAI (11.92 mg) in a mixed anhydrous solvent of DMF/DMSO (4/1, V/V) and was stirred for 4 h.

1.68 eV-Cs0.05MA0.15FA0.8PbI2.25Br0.75

The perovskite precursor solution (Cs0.05MA0.15FA0.8PbI2.25Br0.75, 1.8 M) was prepared by mixing PbI2 (552.7 mg), FAI (247.68 mg), CsI (23.388 mg), MAI (42.9 mg), MACl (6.4 mg) and PbBr2 (253.7 mg) in a mixed anhydrous solvent of DMF/DMSO (4/1, V/V) and was stirred for 4 h.

Device fabrication

Small-size solar cell fabrication

Sequentially wash the 1.5 × 1.5 cm2 Glass/ITO substrate in an ultrasonic bath with detergent, deionized water, absolute ethanol, acetone and isopropanol for 20 min, then dry the substrate with N2 and clean it with Plasma treatment for 3 min and cool to room temperature. Then, it was spin-coated with NiOx solution (10 mg mL−1 H2O) at 2000 rpm for 30 s, and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. substrate was spin-coated with poly-3Ph-4PACz solution (1 mg mL−1 DMSO) at 3000 rpm for 30 s, and annealed at 100 °C for 5 min. The perovskite layer is deposited onto the poly-3Ph-4PACz substrates by spin-coating. First, 100 μL perovskite precursor is spread on the substrate and spin at 1000 rpm for 10 s, and 5000 rpm for 30 s (both 2000 rpm s−1 ramp). The wet perovskite film was then transferred to the flash chamber, where it was vacuumed to 10 Pa and held for 45 s. The as-prepared perovskite films were subsequently annealed on a hot plate at 105 °C for 20 min. Then, the PDI2 solution (0.5 mg mL−1, in isopropanol) was spin-coated on the perovskite layer as a passivation layer at 4000 rpm for 20 s and annealed at 70 °C for 2 min. Finally, 25 nm C60 and 5 nm BCP were deposited by thermal evaporation. Afterward, 100 nm Ag was sequentially thermally evaporated under a pressure of about 10−5 torr to complete the device. For standard laboratory J–V measurements, the active area of the devices was 0.0535 cm2. The champion device was sent for independent certification. The certified active area was determined by a metal aperture mask with an area of 0.0524 cm2.

Perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells fabrication

The silicon heterojunction (HJT) was developed from a 200 µm thick silicon cell fabricated on the production line. Intrinsic, N-type, and P-type hydrogenated amorphous silicon layers were deposited on both sides of the wafer utilizing plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition technology. One hundred fifty nanometers of ITO and 800 nm Ag were sputtered on the backside of the wafer through a shadow mask of 1.2 × 1.2 cm2. An IZO recombination junction approximate 10 nm was sputtered on the front side by the same shadow mask on the backside surface. The wafers were then laser-cut into 2.5 × 2.5 cm2 substrates for integration into tandem devices. The silicon bottom cell was treated with plasma treatment to remove organic residues. Then, it was spin-coated with NiOx solution (10 mg mL−1 H2O) at 2000 rpm for 30 s, and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. substrate was spin-coated with poly-3Ph-4PACz solution (1 mg mL−1 DMSO) at 3000 rpm for 30 s, and annealed at 100 °C for 5 min. 1.68 eV perovskite films (1.8 M) were then deposited onto the prepared substrates. Then, the PDI2 solution (0.5 mg mL−1, in isopropanol) was spin-coated on the perovskite layer as a passivation layer at 4000 rpm for 20 s and annealed at 70 °C for 2 min. For the electron-transporting layer, 1 nm LiF and 25 nm C60 were thermally evaporated at a deposition rate of 0.1 Å s–1, followed by atomic layer deposition (ALD) of SnOx. The SnOx thin films were deposited via ALD using a commercial ALD system (Jiaxingkemin Electronic Equipment Technology Co., Ltd) with tetrakis(dimethylamino) tin (IV) (TDMA-Sn, 99.99%) and deionized water as the tin precursor and oxidant, respectively. During the process, the TDMA-Sn precursor was heated to 65 °C to maintain sufficient vapor pressure, while the H2O canister was kept at room temperature. High-purity N2 (99.999%) was employed as both the carrier and purging gas with a flow rate of 20 sccm, and the reaction chamber was maintained at a base pressure of approximately 0.2 Torr. The deposition was carried out at a substrate temperature of 100 °C using a typical ALD cycle consisting of a 0.1 s TDMA-Sn pulse, a 10 s N2 purge, a 0.05 s H2O pulse, and a final 10 s N2 purge. The growth per cycle was determined to be approximately 1 Å/cycle via spectroscopic ellipsometry, and a total of 100 cycles were performed to achieve a target thickness of ~10 nm. Subsequently, 40 nm of IZO was deposited via magnetron sputtering. Using a precision shadow mask, 800 nm Ag fingers were thermally evaporated. Finally, a 100 nm MgF2 antireflection layer was deposited to complete the fabrication of the tandem device.

Flexible module fabrication

For the device with a size of 10 × 10 cm2, compared with the preparation parameters of small-area solar cells, BCP is changed to 10 nm of SnOx, and all spinning steps are changed to blade-coating. NiOx and poly-3Ph-4PACz were blade-coated on the substrate at 20 mm s−1 with a 150 µm coating gap. The perovskite layers were prepared by blade-coating the Cs0.1MA0.05FA0.85PbI3 perovskite ink (1.0 M) at 10 mm s−1 with a 150 µm coating gap. One hundred microliters [1, 2] 100 μL solution was used for each coating. Modules are connected in series through P1, P2, and P3 lines. The lines were patterned using a laser scribing system with a wavelength of 532 nm, a frequency of 50 KHz, a power of 7.2 W, and a laser processing speed of 100 mm s−1. P1, P2, and P3 are streaked before cleaning the ITO substrate, and before and after evaporating the electrode. The widths of P1, P2, and P3 are 20, 80, and 40 μm, respectively. The inactive P1–P2–P3 interconnection area or dead area width Wd and the active area width Wa of our modules are 0.19 and 5.81 mm, respectively. This geometric fill factor (GFF), or ratio of the active area to aperture area, is given by GFF = Wa/(Wa + Wd) × 100%. Thus, the GFF of our modules is 96.8%.

Encapsulation procedure

The flexible perovskite modules were encapsulated using a structure consisting of a PET/PSC device/POE/Barrier PET. First, conductive copper tapes were attached to the cathode and anode for electrical extraction. A sheet of low-temperature cross-linkable polyolefin elastomer (POE) film (8 cm × 8 cm) was placed over the active area. To ensure superior moisture resistance, a butyl rubber edge sealant (1 cm width) was applied around the POE film. A nonconductive, transparent PET film was then placed on top as the front sheet. The stacked assembly was placed into a vacuum laminator. The process began with a 100 s evacuation step at 100 °C to remove air. Subsequently, a three-stage lamination pressure profile was applied to ensure uniform bonding without damaging the flexible layers. Stage 1: at −70 kPa for 20 s; stage 2: at −50 kPa for 20 s; stage 3: at −30 kPa for 180 s. Finally, the encapsulated module was removed and allowed to cool to room temperature naturally.

First-principles calculations

The DFT calculations of adsorption energy were conducted utilizing the Vienna ab initio simulation package. Modeling of the FAPbI3 surface involved the use of a slab comprising five atomic layers; a minimum vacuum region of 30 Å was implemented perpendicular to the surface plane (z-direction) to eliminate artificial interactions arising from periodic boundary conditions. The binding energy of the passivator with the FAPbI3 or NiOx surface was defined as the total energy of the system with A adsorbed on the surface of catalysts (E*A) minus the energies of the bare surface (E*) and the free reactant A (EA). This is given by the equation: Eₐ = E*A − E* − EA. Molecular models of repeating units and simulated polymers were optimized using DFT. Considering computational cost and accuracy, a molecular model with two repeating units was employed to simulate polymer chains. The ground-state geometric configurations of the molecules were optimized in the Gaussian 09 program using the B3LYP/6-311G(d,p) functional and basis set, and dipole moments were calculated. ESP and localized orbital localization function (LOL) plots were generated from the optimized wavefunction files.

Device characterization

UV–visible absorption spectra of the films were measured with a Shimadzu UV-1900 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Japan). AFM measurements, KPFM and C-AFM were conducted using an Asylum Research MFP-3D system (Oxford Instruments). Field-emission SEM (FE-SEM, JEOL 7610F, Japan) was employed to examine the surface morphology of the films and the cross-sectional view of the devices at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV. XRD patterns were recorded on a Rigaku SmartLab X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation, Japan) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5405 Å) with a measurement power of 2 kW and a scan rate of 8° min−1. Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra were acquired using an FLS1000 fluorescence spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments, UK). Mott–Schottky measurements were performed using an AMETEK VersSTAT 3F electrochemical workstation (AMETEK, USA) at a fixed frequency of 1 kHz, with an applied bias voltage ranging from 0 to 1.2 V. EIS measurements were carried out in the dark over a frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 1,000,000 Hz, using the same instrument employed for the Mott–Schottky measurements. Transient photocurrent (TPC) and transient photovoltage (TPV) measurements were tested using a PD-RS system (ENLITECH, Taiwan). Long-term stability tests were conducted under ambient conditions of 35 ± 5% RH and a temperature of 25 ± 5 °C. The J–V characteristics were measured using a Keithley 2400 source meter under simulated AM 1.5G solar irradiation (100 mW cm−2) provided by a xenon lamp-based solar simulator (7ISO503A, SOFN INSTRUMENTS). The measurements were performed through either forward scanning from −0.02 to 1.20 V or reverse scanning from 1.20 to −0.02 V. All J–V characterizations for the solar cells were carried out under ambient atmospheric conditions. The EQE spectra were recorded using an EQE measurement system (EnliTech, Taiwan) to validate the integrated short-circuit current density, with all measurements conducted at room temperature under ambient conditions using unencapsulated solar cells. LBIC mapping measurements were performed employing an LSD4 system (ENLITECH, Taiwan). The operational stability of the encapsulated devices was evaluated using a commercial multichannel testing system (Wuhan 91PVKSolar) under continuous MPP tracking at 65 °C. The illumination source consisted of a white-light LED array calibrated to 100 mW cm−2 through matching the short-circuit current with that measured under standard AM 1.5G illumination. For damp-heat stability testing, encapsulated devices were maintained in an environmental chamber at 85 °C/85% relative humidity, with periodic PCE measurements taken after cooling to ambient temperature.

Bending stability characterization

The large-area devices (10 cm × 10 cm) were mounted on a testing stage under controlled environmental conditions (25 °C, 40% RH). For the bending fatigue test, the devices were repeatedly flexed between a flat state and a bent state with a fixed radius of 14 mm. Each complete bending cycle took approximately 3 s. To evaluate their mechanical durability, the photovoltaic performance of the modules was recorded after specific numbers of bending cycles.
