Medical facility preferences among older patients with diabetes: A discrete choice experiment in a Japanese primary care setting
Authors: Rie Tanaka, Gerelmaa Gunsmaa, Shinobu Motohashi, Katsuya Yamazaki, Masao Ichikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo support aging patients living with diabetes, understanding their medical facility preferences is relevant, considering their healthcare needs and mobility status.AimsWe conducted a discrete choice experiment among adult patients with diabetes in a Japanese primary care setting.MethodsA binary logit model was used to estimate the relative importance of medical facility attributes based on age group, driving status, and presence of companions during medical visits.ResultsOf 1,027 participants with the median age of 70 years (interquartile range: 60, 75), 29% were aged ≥ 75 years, 65% were men, 21% developed diabetic complications, and 51% had multimorbidity. Generally, the participants preferred clinics over hospitals, face-to-face consultations over online consultations, and shorter travel and waiting times. Older patients were more flexible with travel and waiting times than younger patients. Although online consultations were less preferred overall, older patients who do not drive were relatively receptive to online consultations, and those who were accompanied by someone during medical visits showed a preference for large hospitals.DiscussionPatient preferences vary by age and mobility status and may influence medical facility choices. Online consultations and having a companion during medical visits would be helpful for older patients with diabetes and mobility challenges.ConclusionsThe discrete choice experiment revealed the medical facility preferences among older patients with mobility challenges. Our findings suggest new avenues for older patients with diabetes to access optimal care.Keywords: Aging, Diabetes, Patient preferences, Health services accessibility, Discrete choice experiment

Introduction

The number of older adults with diabetes is increasing worldwide [1]. In Japan, one in five adults aged 70 years or older have diabetes and live longer than before [2, 3]. As diabetic complications and comorbidity become more common with aging, lifelong care for these conditions should be made accessible for older patients [4, 5].

Although people in Japan can select any medical facility according to their preferences, older patients with diabetes need to be careful in choosing medical facilities due to their complex health conditions [6]. Indeed, approximately half of the older patients develop diabetic complications along with one or more other chronic conditions [5]. Previous studies have shown that patients with chronic conditions prioritize geographical convenience when selecting medical facilities [6, 7]. Other studies suggest that the patient preferences for medical facilities rely on patient-provider relationships [8–10]. Moreover, among older adults with mobility challenges, such as whether they can drive a car or whether they are accompanied by someone during medical visits, might influence their choice of medical facilities [6, 11, 12]. These may hinder older patients with diabetes from accessing optimal care.

To assist aging patients with the best possible care, we need to understand their preferences for medical facilities, considering their healthcare needs and mobility challenges. This study aimed to examine the attributes of medical facilities valued by older patients with diabetes according to age group, driving status, and presence of companions during medical visits.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study used a discrete choice experiment to investigate the preferences for medical facilities among older patients with diabetes. We conducted the cross-sectional study at four diabetes clinics in the southern region of Ibaraki prefecture, located 60 km away from Tokyo. The prefecture covers a land area of 6,000 km2 and has a population of 2.82 million, including 454,000 residents aged ≥ 75 years (16%). There was one university hospital offering advanced care, 173 general and emergency care hospitals, and 1,775 primary care clinics in the prefecture at the commencement of the study.

Participants

The study included outpatients aged ≥ 18 years who regularly visited diabetes care clinics. The patients were recruited during their clinic visits from October to December 2023. Based on previous studies, assuming at least a 25% difference between patient preferences for attributes of medical facilities, in a two-tailed test with α = 0.05 and β = 0.2, indicated that 132 participants were required for this study. Besides, taking that 15% of the outpatients were aged ≥ 75 years and that the response rate in the recent census by post was 30%, we intended to distribute 1,500 questionnaires consecutively to eligible participants.

Data collection

Participants were given an anonymous self-administered questionnaire when they visited the clinic and were requested to fill out the questionnaire and return it by post with their consent. In the questionnaire, we asked the participants about their age, sex, living arrangements, employment status, the proportion of copayment for medical expenditure, long-term care or disability certification, presence of companions during medical visits, the duration of diabetes, glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level, current diabetes treatment, the presence of diabetic complications or multimorbidity, whether they have a family physician, and the mode of transportation to the medical facilities.

Discrete choice experiment

Discrete choice experiment is a stated preference method used to measure individual preferences in choice simulations [13]. This method has been utilized to assess patient preferences for care and support across a range of health conditions [10, 11, 14]. In this study, we employed a binary choice model to measure the preferences of patients with diabetes for the attributes of medical facilities.

To prepare the experiment, we undertook a preliminary study involving community-dwelling older patients to explore their preferred attributes of medical facilities, ensuring that we captured the older patients’ insights for patient-centered care. The following four attributes were identified: type of medical facility, travel time to medical facility, patient-provider relationship, and waiting time for consultation. Other potential attributes, such as cost, continuity of care, and multidisciplinary services, were not suggested in the preliminary study possibly because these attributes should not differ between medical facilities due to universal health coverage and standardized diabetes care in accordance with clinical practice guidelines for diabetes [4]. Among the four attributes, we considered that the patient-provider relationship to be a multifaceted attribute. Therefore, we let a consultation style to represent it. We then established the level of each attribute as follows: type of medical facilities (primary care clinic, middle-sized hospital providing emergency care with a capacity of 200 beds, or large-sized hospital providing advanced care with a capacity of more than 400 beds), travel time to medical facilities (30 min, 60 min, or 120 min), consultation style (face-to-face, or online), and waiting time for consultation (30 min, 60 min, or 120 min). Finally, we used LMA design to generate choice sets from an orthogonal main-effect array, resulting in 18 choice sets (each set containing four attributes of medical facilities with different attribute levels). These sets were randomly divided into three blocks, with each block containing six choice sets. The study participants were consecutively assigned to one of the three blocks and asked to respond “Yes” or “No” to each of the six choice sets based on their preferences (i.e., whether they prefer the presented medical facilities) (Fig. 1).

Procedure of the discrete choice experiment

Analysis

First, we described the characteristics of participants aged < 75 years and those aged ≥ 75 years. We then conducted an analysis using a binary logit model to estimate the coefficients of each attribute level with the 95% confidence interval by age group. The coefficient indicates the relative importance of each attribute level in the attributes of medical facilities (e.g., face-to-face consultation relative to online consultation). Additionally, we conducted the analysis specifically among those aged ≥ 75 years separately for those who drive a car to the medical facility and those who use alternative transportation, and for those who are accompanied by someone during their medical visits and those who are not, assuming that their driving status and the presence of companions potentially influence their preferences for medical facilities. Finally, we conducted sensitivity analysis excluding the participants who answered “No” to all choice sets. Model fitting was assessed using adjusted ρ2, with values between 0.2 and 0.4 indicating a good model fit [13]. Analyses were performed using the R Studio application and R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Questionnaires were handed to 1,462 patients at the clinics, of whom 1,047 returned the questionnaires. However, 6 declined to respond or did not provide answers to any questions, 3 were free from diabetes, and 11 did not disclose their age. Therefore, 1,027 of the 1,462 patients (70%) were included in the analyses.

Of these participants with the median age of 70 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 60, 75), 65% were men, 14% were living alone, 52% were employed, 44% paid 20% or less of medical expenditures as a copayment, 3% were certified for long-term care or disability, and 13% were accompanied by someone during medical visits. The median duration of diabetes was 15 years (IQR: 10, 23), the median HbA1c was 7.1% (IQR: 6.8, 7.5), 89% used antidiabetic agents, 21% developed diabetic complications, 51% had multimorbidity, and 63% had a family physician. When they regularly visit the diabetes clinic, 81% of the participants drove a car by themselves. The median time of travel to the current clinic was 25 min (IQR: 15, 40), and the median waiting time before consultation was 30 min (IQR: 20, 40).

Table 1 shows the characteristics of 733 participants aged < 75 years and 294 participants aged ≥ 75 years. Compared with the younger participants, older participants were more likely to be unemployed, incur lower medical expenditures, be certified for long-term care or disabilities, and be accompanied by someone during their medical visits. In terms of clinical conditions, older patients had a longer duration of diabetes with more diabetic complications and multimorbidity. They were also more likely to have a family physician and be driven to the clinic by others or use public transportation. Discussion

Participants generally preferred primary care clinics over hospitals, face-to-face consultations over online consultations, and shorter travel and waiting times. However, the magnitude of their preference varied by age group, driving status, and the presence of companions during medical visits.

Compared with younger patients, older patients preferred large-sized hospitals over middle-sized hospitals while they placed less value on travel and waiting times. Acceptance of time-consuming choice situations vary by patient characteristics and healthcare needs [7, 10–12, 14]. Older patients may be more flexible with travel and waiting times, as they probably have more free time than younger people. In our study, 81% of the participants aged ≥ 75 years were no longer employed, whereas 65% of those aged < 75 years were still employed, which might have resulted in the different preferences between the two groups. Additionally, older patients had more chronic conditions than younger patients, requiring different clinical departments to manage multiple health conditions [4, 5, 15]. Therefore, older patients may have preferred large hospitals with more clinical departments to middle-sized hospitals, even if longer travel and waiting times were required [6, 7]. Moreover, the availability of specialists in large hospitals may contribute to this preference [9–11].

Online consultations were less preferred among older patients, but those who do not drive were relatively more receptive to online consultations compared with those who drive. Since there will be a growing number of older patients who discontinue driving, facing difficulties accessing medical facilities, online consultations might complement face-to-face consultations. However, online consultations remain uncommon and are only conditionally available across Japan [16]. The acceptability of online consultations may depend on age-friendly digital infrastructure and reliable patient-provider relationships [8–10].

Older patients who were accompanied by someone during their medical visits were more likely to prefer large-sized hospitals than those without a companion. This might be due to potential differences between those with and without a companion, such as different medical conditions and healthcare needs. However, these two groups had similar levels of diabetic complications (26% vs. 28%) and multimorbidity (71% vs. 60%). Therefore, regardless of their healthcare needs, older patients who were accompanied may have preferred large-sized hospitals despite complex layouts, tedious administrative processes, and communication challenges arising from advanced and specialized care in such hospitals [17, 18].

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in its use of a discrete choice experiment to simulate realistic choice situations when selecting medical facilities for older patients facing mobility challenges. Although older adults generally have nuanced preferences due to their unique healthcare needs and social challenges [1, 4, 5, 15, 17], this study captured their relative preferences for medical facility attributes. The study findings may contribute to providing viable options for improving access to optimal care among older patients with mobility challenges.

This study had context-specific limitations. First, the study participants were exclusively recruited from primary care clinics. Although many participants had been living with diabetes for an extended period and had comorbidities, our findings may not be broadly applicable to more severe cases. Second, this study was conducted in a region where private cars were the predominant mode of transportation. Preferences among patients living in areas with convenient public transportation may differ. Third, the Japanese healthcare context, such as free access and the lack of age-friendly digital infrastructure, may have influenced patient preferences. Despite these limitations, our findings contribute to the understanding of medical facility preferences among older patients with diabetes and highlight how mobility challenges may shape their choices.

Conclusions

The present study revealed that older patients with diabetes preferred clinics over hospitals, face-to-face consultations over online consultations, and shorter travel and waiting times. Although online consultations were less preferred overall, they might complement face-to-face consultations for older patients with mobility challenges. Seeking advanced care in large-sized hospitals may be feasible if they are accompanied by a companion. These findings suggest new avenues for older patients living with diabetes to access the best possible care.
