The mediating effect of TyG-related indicators between long-term exposure to particulate matter and cardiovascular disease: evidence from a national longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Jiamin Xu, Tongle Yin, Mengshan Pan, Li Qin, Lu Zhang, Xiaoyan Wang, Weijun Zheng, Cuiqing Liu, Rucheng Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAmbient particulate matter (PM) exposure is recognized as a risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD). However, the extent to which PM exposure is associated with CVD via triglyceride glucose (TyG)-related indicators remains unknown. This study examines the relationship between long-term PM exposure and CVD events, further assessing whether TyG-related indicators mediate this association.MethodsThis cohort study involved 7,532 individuals aged at least 45 years who were not diagnosed with CVD in 2011 from the China Longitudinal Study of Health and Retirement (CHARLS) and were followed up for the occurrence of CVD until 2020. The annual PM concentration data at the city level, with aerodynamic diameters ≤ 1 μm (PM1), ≤ 2.5 μm (PM2.5), and ≤ 10 μm (PM10), were obtained from the ChinaHighAirPollutants (CHAP). The average concentration of PM in the 3 years before the baseline survey in 2011 was defined as the long-term exposure level of the individual. The relationship between PM exposure and CVD incidence was examined via Cox proportional hazards models, with a focus on probing the role of TyG-related indicators through mediation analysis.ResultsA total of 1,865 individuals with CVD were diagnosed over the span of a 7.4-year follow-up period. The 3-year average concentrations before baseline were 31.29 µg/m³ for PM1, 56.03 µg/m³ for PM2.5, and 95.73 µg/m³ for PM10. In fully adjusted model, the Cox proportional hazards models revealed that an increase of 10 µg/m³ in the PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 exposure concentrations corresponded to elevated CVD risk, with HRs (95% CI) of 1.135 (1.078–1.195), 1.092 (1.062–1.123), and 1.075 (1.059–1.090), respectively. Mediation analyses further suggested that the correlation between PM exposure and CVD could be partly mediated via TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR, with mediation proportions varying from 5.54 to 15.30%.ConclusionA significant correlation was observed between long-term PM exposure and increased CVD risk, with TyG-related indicators, such as TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR, partially mediating this relationship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-024-02305-8.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) persists as the preeminent cause of morbidity and death worldwide, presenting a significant public health challenge that impacts individuals and society [1]. The study on Global Burden of Disease found that around 4.58 million deaths worldwide in 2019 were caused by CVD, representing more than 40% of all fatalities [2]. With the effects of an aging population and persistent metabolic-related risks, China is increasingly burdened by CVD as a developing country. The China Cardiovascular Health and Disease Report 2022 revealed that CVD still ranks first in terms of disease burden in China, exerting substantial economic strain on the country [3]. Therefore, focusing on risk factors for CVD is critical to mitigate the disease burden.

Recently, an increasing number of studies have associated ambient particulate matter (PM) exposure, including PM with an aerodynamic diameter ≤ 1 μm (PM1), ≤ 2.5 μm (PM2.5) and ≤ 10 μm (PM10), with a heightened risk of CVD [4, 5]. Research on related mechanisms of action has indicated that PM can impact cardiovascular health through pathways related to inflammation, oxidative stress, and malfunction of the cells lining blood vessels [6, 7]. Epidemiological studies have consistently linked PM exposure to elevated CVD morbidity [8, 9], with a meta-analysis illustrating a notable 13.0% elevation in CVD morbidity for every 10 µg/m³ rise in PM2.5 concentration [10]. Nevertheless, most existing research has focused primarily on examining the impact of PM2.5 concentration on CVD, whereas less is known about the effects of PM1 and PM10 [11, 12].

Insulin resistance (IR) is another critical determinant of the occurrence of CVD [13]. The hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, often known as the HEC, is widely regarded as the definitive approach for IR evaluation. However, its widespread clinical applicability is restricted by its invasive nature, technical intricacies, and high cost implications [14]. Recently, triglyceride glucose (TyG)-related indicators, particularly the TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), TyG-waist circumference (TyG-WC), and TyG-waist-height ratio (TyG-WHtR), have been considered reliable alternative indicators for evaluating IR [15, 16]. Moreover, recent findings have identified these indicators as independent predictors of CVD events, contributing to the onset of CVD by enhancing oxidative stress, increasing inflammation, affecting blood pressure and coagulation, among other mechanisms, ultimately disrupting the vascular endothelia and accelerating atherosclerosis [17, 18].

Furthermore, increasing evidence indicates that ambient PM exposure can increase the risk of developing IR [19, 20]. A recent extensive meta-analysis, primarily focusing on longitudinal research designs, has revealed a notable link between IR and PM exposure [21]. This correlation is most likely the result of pulmonary oxidative stress caused by inhaling ambient PM, which leads to inflammation in tissues and organs, ultimately disrupting glucose metabolism [22].

Therefore, PM exposure may damage vascular endothelial cells by increasing TyG-related indicators, ultimately increase the CVD risk. However, research investigating the potential impact of TyG-related indicators on the connection between PM and CVD is lacking. Thus, this study utilized extensive national data from China examine the connection between long-term PM exposure and CVD, assessing whether TyG-related indicators mediate this association.

Methods

Data and study participants

The study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), which is a nationally representative longitudinal study that specifically targets individuals in China who are at least 45 years old. Further details regarding the CHARLS can be found in other available resources [23]. The baseline survey, which began in 2011, includes 28 provinces, 150 counties, 450 villages, and approximately 17,000 people from 10,000 households. The survey utilized a random sampling approach based on multi-stage stratified probability proportional method, which involved four stages: county, village, household and individual. The Probability Proportional to Size (PPS) method was utilized at both the county and village levels. Biennial follow-up surveys were conducted in 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), 2018 (Wave 4), and finally in 2020 (Wave 5) for data collection. Standardized questionnaires were employed to gather sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health data, providing valuable insights into elder care and aging research. The current research draws upon a comprehensive dataset spanning these five waves of the CHARLS, covering the period from 2011 to 2020.

The subjects of this study consisted of individuals aged 45 and above without a history of CVD. The baseline survey encompassing a wide-ranging sample of 17,708 participants in 2011. However, due to various reasons, certain individuals were excluded from the analysis. These reasons included being diagnosed with CVD at baseline (n = 2,467); incomplete baseline CVD information or being lost to follow-up (n = 641); incomplete data on triglycerides (TG), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), or height (n = 6,536); extreme values for TyG, BMI, WC, or height (n = 167); age < 45 years at baseline (n = 254); and missing data on baseline characteristics (n = 111). Ultimately, as visually illustrated in Fig. 1, 7,532 individuals were recruited for this study.

Outcome assessment

The study yielded CVD events, including stroke and heart disease, as its outcomes. Information regarding CVD diagnosis history was obtained through questions such as “Have you ever been diagnosed with a heart attack, angina, coronary heart disease, heart failure, or other cardiac issues by a doctor?” or “Have you ever been diagnosed with a stroke by a doctor?“. CVD was defined as participants who reported that they had experienced a heart attack or stroke [24].

Ambient PM exposure assessment

The ChinaHighAirPollutants (CHAP) dataset serves as the source in this study to collect annual concentration data on PM1, PM2.5, and PM10. The spatial resolution for PM is precisely defined at a scale of 1 km by 1 km. This dataset has been effectively utilized in research evaluating the correlation between population health and air pollutants [25, 26]. The CHAP dataset was crafted through the fusion of Space-Time Extra-Trees (STET) modeling techniques and a diverse array of data sources, such as observations from the ground, atmospheric reanalysis, and satellite remote sensing products, to deliver extensive information on various air pollutants at the ground level in China [27–30]. The dataset demonstrates robust predictive performance in estimating the concentrations of PM, with high cross-validation coefficients of determination (CV-R²) recorded as 0.77 for PM1, 0.92 for PM2.5, and 0.90 for PM10. Due to privacy concerns, obtaining detailed individual residential addresses from the CHARLS was not feasible. Therefore, in this study, ArcGis 10.8 was utilized to estimate participants’ average annual exposure concentrations of PM based on their home addresses (accurate to city-level geocoding). The average concentration of PM in the 3 years before the baseline survey in 2011 was defined as the long-term exposure level of the individual.

Mediators

The mediators of this study were TyG-related indicators, including TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR. The FPG and TG values were utilized in the computation of the TyG index [31]. In the CHARLS, medical professionals collected fasting blood samples to measure the FPG and TG concentrations via enzyme colorimetry. Moreover, anthropometric data, including height, weight and WC, were accurately collected using standard methods. The measurements of height and weight were conducted without footwear, using a vertical altimeter with a precision of 0.1 cm for height assessment and a scale accurate to 0.1 kg for weight determination. After that, BMI was calculated by dividing the individual’s weight in kilograms by the square of their height expressed in meters [32]. For the WC measurement, the umbilical level was marked at end-expiration [33]. The WHtR was derived by dividing the WC value by the individual’s height [34].

Covariates

The following collected variables were considered covariates based on the baseline structured questionnaire and previous studies: age, gender (male or female), place of residence (rural or urban), educational level (primary school and below, junior high school, senior high school, or university and above), marital status (married or other), drinking status (never, former, or current), smoking status (never, former, or current), diabetes (no or yes), and hypertension (no or yes). Diabetes was defined as an FBG level of ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, a self-reported history of diabetes, or previous use of antidiabetic medication [35]. Hypertension was defined as an SBP of ≥ 140 mmHg, a DBP of ≥ 90 mmHg, a self-reported history of hypertension, or previous use of antihypertensive medication [36].

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of individuals were categorized into two groups according to whether they had experienced CVD events: one for CVD patients and the other for non-CVD patients. Continuous variables were represented by means and standard deviations, with Student’s t tests being used to evaluate group differences. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and chi-square tests were used to assess group differences. Beginning with the baseline survey, the follow-up duration was measured until the initial CVD diagnosis, loss to follow-up, completion of the study (Wave 5 in 2020), or other reasons.

Cox proportional hazards models were conducted to assess the association between long-term PM exposure and CVD incidence. Adjustments were gradually implemented in three models to account for potential confounding factors: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, marital status, education level, and place of residence; and Model 3 further included adjustments for smoking status, drinking status, diabetes, and hypertension based on Model 2. The Schoenfeld residual was examined to evaluate the assumption of proportional risk. The results did not indicate any violation of this hypothesis (Figs. S1-S3). To evaluate the potential risk of CVD attributable to every 10 µg/m³ elevation in PM concentration, hazard ratios (HRs), alongside their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were employed as the primary analytical metrics. Furthermore, the risk of CVD was also assessed by comparing varying quartiles of PM concentrations to the first quartile. Trend tests were conducted based on the median PM concentration per quartile across three models.

In addition, restricted cubic splines (RCSs) with 4 knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles were employed in fully adjusted model to investigate potential nonlinear relationships between CVD risk and long-term PM exposure. Moreover, subgroup analyses were performed considering various factors, including age (< 60 years or > = 60 years), gender (male or female), place of residence (rural or urban), drinking status (never, former, or current), smoking status (never, former, or current), hypertension (no or yes), and diabetes (no or yes). Interaction variables were utilized to examine potential covariate effects in the fully adjusted model.

A mediation analysis was conducted to investigate whether TyG-related indicators serve as mediators in the connection between PM and CVD, separating the overall impact into two parts: the average direct effect (ADE) representing direct influence, and the average causal mediation effect (ACME) explaining the indirect pathway through these indicators. The mediation proportion was defined as ACME/(ACME + ADE), and its significance was evaluated via a bootstrap method with 1,000 resamples [37, 38].

To ensure the credibility and dependability of the results, a comprehensive suite of sensitivity analyses was conducted. First, average exposure concentrations of PM were utilized for periods of 1 year and 2 years before the baseline survey for the primary outcome analysis. Second, the multiple imputation approach was employed to address missing baseline characteristic data in order to maintain a substantial sample size. Additionally, in order to reduce the possibility of reverse causality bias, the analysis excluded individuals (n = 234) diagnosed with CVD within the initial year of follow-up.

The statistical analysis of this study was conducted using R Studio 4.2.3, along with Zstats v1.0 (www.zstats.net). A bilateral P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

The demographic details of the participants are concisely outlined in Table 1. The average age among the 7,532 individuals was 58.68 (9.17) years, with females accounting for 52.7% of the sample. The majority of the participants had completed primary school or below (70.6%) and were married (88.2%). Additionally, over half of the individuals resided in rural regions (66.9%), had never drank alcohol (58.1%), and had never smoked (60.8%). The sample included 2899 (38.5%) participants with hypertension and 1,185 (15.7%) participants with diabetes. During the 7.4-year follow-up period, 1,865 individuals (24.8%) were ultimately diagnosed with CVD. Importantly, with the exception of place of residence, baseline characteristics differed significantly between participants with and without CVD (P < 0.05). Individuals with CVD showed significantly higher 3-year average concentrations of PM compared to those without CVD. Specifically, the 3-year average concentrations were 31.29 (9.05) µg/m³ for PM1, 56.03 (16.83) µg/m³ for PM2.5, and 95.73 (30.76) µg/m³ for PM10. Associations between PM and CVD

Table 2 illustrates the correlation between PM exposure levels and CVD risk. The Cox proportional hazards models revealed that after all potential confounders were accounted for in Model 3, a rise of 10 µg/m³ in PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 concentrations corresponded to increased CVD risk, with HRs (95% CI) of 1.135 (1.078–1.195), 1.092 (1.062–1.123), and 1.075 (1.059–1.090), respectively. Additionally, CVD risk increased with increasing quantiles of the 3-year average exposure concentrations of PM (Figs. S4-S6). As shown in Table 2, participants in the fourth quartile (Q4) of PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 exposure presented 37.1% (HR: 1.371, 95% CI: 1.210–1.553), 55.6% (HR: 1.556, 95% CI: 1.369–1.768), and 76.8% (HR: 1.768, 95% CI: 1.551–2.016) higher risks for CVD, respectively, than those in the first quartile (Q1).

Exposure dose‒response relationships and subgroup analyses

Mediation analyses

TyG-related indicators were found to be partially responsible for mediating the connection between PM and CVD, as demonstrated by the results of the mediation analyses (Table 3). The mediation proportions and their corresponding 95% CIs for TyG-related indicators in the relationship between PM1 and CVD were as follows: 13.18% (8.55%, 17.81%) for TyG-BMI, 15.30% (10.05%, 20.55%) for TyG-WC, and 10.56% (6.18%, 14.95%) for TyG-WHtR. Mediating effects were also found between PM2.5 and CVD, with TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR acting as mediators at percentages of 10.61% (6.89%, 14.33%), 11.98% (7.84%, 16.12%), and 8.51% (4.97%, 12.05%), respectively. Additionally, the association between CVD and PM10 was also significantly mediated by TyG-BMI (7.35%, 95% CI: 4.70%, 9.99%), TyG-WC (8.61%, 95% CI: 5.54%, 11.67%) and TyG-WHtR (5.54%, 95% CI: 3.14%, 7.93%).

Sensitivity analysis

The robustness of the primary results was confirmed by the consistency of findings obtained via various validation methods. First, the average exposure concentrations of PM for periods of 1 year and 2 years were utilized for the primary outcome analysis, resulting in consistent findings (Supplementary Tables S1-S5). Similar results were found when multiple imputations were implemented to address the issue of missing data. (Supplementary Table S6). Furthermore, the significant associations between PM exposure and CVD risk persisted even after excluding CVD cases during the first year of follow-up (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

The findings from this study underscore the significant impact of long-term PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 exposure in elevating CVD risk. This association persisted across diverse subgroups, including those stratified by age, gender, place of residence, drinking status, smoking status, diabetes, and hypertension. Notably, TyG-related indicators, such as TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR, were identified as partial mediators of this relationship. This study may be the first in China to utilize a national longitudinal cohort study to examine the potential mediating influence of TyG-related indicators between long-term PM exposure and CVD events.

Extensive epidemiological research has previously probed the association between long-term PM exposure and CVD morbidity [39, 40]. Notably, most of these studies assessed the health impact of PM in regions with relatively low concentrations. For example, a UK study found that increasing PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations by an IQR was linked to increased CVD risk, with average exposure concentrations of 10.0 µg/m3 and 21.9 µg/m3, respectively [41]. These values were clearly below the levels observed in this study, which were 56.03 µg/m3 for PM2.5 and 95.73 µg/m3 for PM10. Moreover, a 6-year longitudinal research encompassing 65,893 U.S. women demonstrated a 24.0% increase in CVD occurrence for every 10 µg/m3 elevation in PM2.5 and the mean PM2.5 concentration was quantified at 13.5 µg/m3 [42]. Given that PM exposure levels in developing countries typically exceed those in developed countries, the findings derived from studies in the UK and the US may not adequately represent the correlation between PM exposure and CVD in populations in developing countries, such as China.

The findings align with those of other studies conducted in China. A study across 33 communities in Northeast China demonstrated that each additional 10 µg/m3 rise in PM2.5 and PM1 concentrations was correlated with a greater CVD occurrence, with ORs (95% CIs) of 1.06 (1.01–1.11) and 1.12 (1.05–1.20), respectively [43]. Another cohort study provided further evidence of the relationship between an elevated risk of CVD and increased levels of PM exposure, including PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 [44]. However, a study of 27,134 Chinese adults reported no significant correlation between PM10 exposure concentrations and CVD risk [45]. Differences in lifestyle across regions and disparities in PM concentration and composition may explain the inconsistencies in the impact of PM exposure on CVD. Additionally, this analysis revealed that no subgroups—including those defined by age, gender, place of residence, drinking status, smoking status, diabetes, or hypertension—exhibited significant interaction effects, suggesting that the results are broadly applicable across most individuals.

Furthermore, the findings indicated that TyG-related indicators (including TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR) partially mediated the relationship between PM exposure and CVD. These indices serve as reliable alternative indicators for assessing IR [46], a condition that has been connected to PM2.5 exposure in animal research, indicating its potential to increase IR via pulmonary oxidative stress [47]. Moreover, recent epidemiological evidence links PM2.5 exposure to an increase in the IR evaluation index, suggesting that TyG-related indicators can effectively reflect changes in IR caused by PM exposure [48]. Additionally, TyG-related indicators have been recognized as prognosticators of CVD onset [49], with recent findings confirming their association with CVD, particularly in patients with Type 2 diabetes [50]. Therefore, PM exposure may affect CVD risk by disrupting insulin secretion and glucose metabolism. Compared with HEC, the traditional gold standard for IR evaluation, TyG-related indicators are considered simpler and more effective indicators and can be regarded as potential surrogates for predicting the mechanism of CVD risk due to PM exposure.

The precise mechanisms through which PM exposure impacts CVD remain incompletely elucidated. Several studies have proposed that being exposed to PM could potentially initiate systemic oxidative stress, resulting in an elevation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation [51]. This increase not only disrupts the insulin signaling pathway, reducing cellular sensitivity to insulin and promoting the development of IR [21] but also causes vascular endothelial dysfunction, which increases the risk of vascular endothelial inflammation and thrombosis [52]. Additionally, PM can lead to vascular damage, such as atherosclerosis, which exacerbates the progression of CVD [53]. A cohort study of 3,017 participants from southern India revealed a positive relation between PM2.5 exposure and carotid intima‒media thickness [54]. Furthermore, PM exposure triggers a systemic inflammatory response that produces inflammatory cytokines, leading to increased inflammation in the vessel wall [55]. Moreover, this inflammatory response can interfere with the expression and function of insulin receptors, weakening the effect of insulin, thus aggravating IR [56].

Strengths and limitations

This study’s strength rests in its innovative investigation of how PM affects CVD through TyG-related indicators, as well as the utilization of national longitudinal follow-up and prospective design to enhance the quality of data and results. Nevertheless, certain limitations should be recognized. First, given the absence of specific addresses for the participants, the measurement of PM exposure was confined to the city level. The potential for misclassification bias in exposure cannot be fully disregarded. Second, this study’s CVD definition relied on participant self-reported data, which could introduce a certain level of misclassification bias. Third, even though diverse covariates have been taken into consideration, certain environmental exposure factors, such as noise and indoor air pollutants, have not been included in the analysis. The potential confounding impacts of these factors on the outcomes remain unaddressed and cannot be ignored. Finally, the findings were derived from middle-aged and elderly individuals, so caution is advised when applying these results to other age groups.

Conclusions

This national longitudinal cohort study revealed that long-term exposure to PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 was significantly associated with an elevated risk of CVD. Increases in TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR could partially mediate the associations between PM and CVD in different proportions. These findings hold profound significance in pinpointing vulnerable populations at elevated CVD risk, facilitating the implementation of tailored preventive strategies, and further reducing the burden of CVD attributed to exposure to PM.
