Molecularly Imprinted Polymer Nanoparticles for Reducing Herpes Simplex Virus Type 2 Infection
Authors: Maliwan Srisuk, Piyawut Swangphon, Aekkaraj Nualla-ong, Chamsai Pientong, Tipaya Ekalaksananan, Soemwit Khongwichit, Peter A. Lieberzeit, Suticha Chunta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molecularly imprinted polymer nanoparticles (nanoMIPs) were synthesized via solid-phase imprinting for the selective recognition and inhibition of herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2). An optimized copolymer composition yielded spherical nanoMIPs with a hydrodynamic diameter of 146 ± 4 nm, comparable to that of HSV-2 virions (94 ± 6 nm). Electrochemical analysis showed a 3-fold higher current shift for HSV-2-imprinted nanoMIPs compared to nonimprinted controls, confirming enhanced binding ability toward HSV-2. The nanoMIPs exhibited highly preferential recognition of HSV-2 over related viruses, with cross-reactivity of 62 ± 2% for HSV-1, 30 ± 1% for human papillomavirus type 16, and less than 3% for hepatitis B virus and dengue virus type 1. The cytotoxicity assay confirmed that the nanoMIPs were biocompatible with Vero cells and human primary peripheral blood mononuclear cells at concentrations up to 2.92 × 106 particles/mL. The nanoMIPs significantly reduced HSV-2 infection during the early stages of infection, achieving inhibition efficiencies of 80 ± 1% and 49 ± 3% in virus pretreatment and cotreatment groups, respectively. This antiviral mechanism is attributed to direct binding of nanoMIPs to intact viral particles and masking of viral glycoproteins required for host cell attachment, leading to blocking the infection pathway. The nanoMIPs maintained robust antiviral efficacy under extreme conditions, retaining inhibition efficiencies of 73 ± 6% at pH 4.0, 67 ± 5% at pH 10.0, and 63 ± 2% following autoclave sterilization. These findings demonstrate that nanoMIPs function as highly stable, robust, and selective biomimetic receptors, offering a promising platform for antiviral nanomedicine and the prevention of HSV-2 transmission.

Introduction

Herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2) is a causative agent of genital herpes, which has a high incidence rate of sexually transmitted infections worldwide. This virus is an enveloped, double-stranded linear DNA genome belonging to the family Herpesviridae, subfamily Alphaherpesvirinae, and genus Simplexvirus. Preclinical and clinical testing of prophylactic HSV-2 vaccines are still ongoing, and they remain unauthorized. Genital herpes is transmitted via direct contact with the genital fluids or secretions from an infected person with the lesions, although it can be spread from some asymptomatic individuals. HSV-2 entry into host cells is initiated by the attachment of viral envelope glycoproteins gB and gC to cell surface heparan sulfate proteoglycans. Subsequent interaction of glycoprotein gD with its entry receptors, Nectin-1 or herpesvirus entry mediator (HVEM), triggers membrane fusion through the coordinated action of gD, gB, and the gH/gL complex, thereby facilitating viral entry. Viral glycoproteins serve as the binding molecules to extracellular host cell receptors and may provide a new strategy for inhibiting the ability of the virus to enter host cells. Protected intercourse, such as using condoms, is the most effective prevention method, but it does not provide complete protection. Vaginal microbicides, such as nonoxynol-9 (N-9), benzalkonium chloride, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), cocamidopropyl betaine, and cetyl betaine (C31G), have been introduced as vaginal applications for prophylactic intervention but are not fully effective for prevention. Several pharmacological agents, natural compounds, and nanomaterials have been investigated for their ability to block the HSV-2 entry step. Chloroquine and 1-adamantyl (5-bromo-2-methoxybenzyl) amine (ABMA) have been found to inhibit HSV-2 infection by impairing viral entry and disrupting the later stages of the viral life cycle. Carrageenan-based acyclovir and zinc oxide tetrapod nanoparticles (ZOTEN) have also shown efficacy in inhibiting during the cell entry step. Neutralizing antibodies and aptamers have been reported as inhibitors of HSV-2 by binding to the viral envelope gD, leading to blocking the infection pathway. Nanomaterials designed to inhibit HSV-2 entry primarily interfere with viral attachment, receptor engagement, or membrane fusion. Metal nanoparticles (i.e., silver nanoparticles , and gold nanoparticles) and carbon-based nanoparticles (i.e., carbon dots, fullerenes, and graphene) typically act as antiviral agents through electrostatic interactions with viral envelopes and steric interference with receptor engagement. In contrast, polyanionic nanomaterials function as heparan sulfate mimetics, competitively inhibiting the initial attachment of virions to host cell surfaces. However, nanomaterial-based antiviral strategies remain constrained by limited specificity, viral entry redundancy, cytotoxicity at effective concentrations, and altered surface properties under physiological conditions.

Biocompatible synthetic materials are now replacing biological/chemical agents, providing next-generation protection with superior consistency and structural performance. Molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) serve as a promising alternative synthetic, biocompatible, and selective material that directly binds to templates. MIPs are fabricated through a molecular imprinting process that generates polymeric matrices containing highly specific binding cavities, structurally and chemically complementary to the template molecule in terms of shape configuration and functional group orientation. MIPs are valuable in drug development and as protective agents because of their ability to generate highly selective binding sites, particularly within nanoparticle platforms (nanoMIPs) that selectively recognize whole viruses and/or viral surface components. NanoMIPs have demonstrated broad applicability as viral protective agents, including hydrogel-based MIPs targeting porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV-1) to neutralize the viral infection in vitro, nanoMIPs targeting the S3 peptide of the surface glycoprotein 41 (gp41) occurring in the human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) to prevent subsequent cascade interactions causing the killing of CD4+ T cells, and nanoMIPs capable of selective binding and inhibition of the coronavirus SARS-CoV-2 spike protein to prevent virus replication in vitro.

This study presents novel nanoMIPs designed to selectively recognize and bind whole HSV-2 virions, including the viral surface molecules (e.g., envelope gB, gC, and gD), which are responsible for binding to the host cell receptors, thereby inhibiting viral attachment and entry into the host cell, as shown in Scheme . Unlike conventional natural inhibitors or chemical-based strategies, nanoMIPs function as fully synthetic, stable, and biocompatible nanomaterials that physically capture HSV-2 with high selectivity and minimal cross-reactivity. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study reporting nanoMIPs as antiviral agents against HSV-2. Previous antiviral MIP studies have primarily focused on enveloped RNA viruses, including PRRSV-1, HIV-1, and SARS-CoV-2, whereas the present study expands the application of nanoMIPs to HSV-2, a clinically important DNA virus associated with lifelong latent infection and recurrent disease. This study introduces several notable advances in the design and application of synthetic antiviral material. Unlike the hydrogel-based MIPs reported for PRRSV-1 neutralization, the present work employed solid-phase imprinting to synthesize uniformly sized nanoMIPs with controlled morphology and dimensions comparable to HSV-2 virions, thereby improving virus–nanoMIP interactions and selective recognition. In contrast to HIV-1 and SARS-CoV-2 studies that targeted isolated gp41 peptide epitopes or spike proteins, respectively, this study employed whole-virus imprinting to enable recognition of the native structural features present on intact HSV-2 particles. Compared with previously reported antiviral MIPs, this study provides a more comprehensive evaluation of viral selectivity and practical applicability under harsh physicochemical conditions, including acidic and alkaline pH environments as well as after autoclave sterilization. The combination of selective whole-virus recognition, mechanistic antiviral inhibition, biocompatibility, and environmental stability distinguishes the present nanoMIPs platform from previously reported antiviral MIP systems and highlights its potential for antiviral nanomedicine and HSV-2 transmission prevention applications.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Acrylamide (AAM), methyl methacrylate (MMA), N-vinylpyrrolidone (NVP), methacrylic acid (MAA), Tris (hydroxymethyl) aminomethane, sodium chloride (NaCl), silica gel, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), ethanol, and formaldehyde were sourced from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Ammonium persulfate (APS), N-(3-aminopropyl) methacrylamide hydrochloride (APMA), aminopropyl triethoxysilane (APTES), cysteamine, and dextran sulfate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). Glutaraldehyde (GA), N-tert-butylacrylamide (TBAm), N,N′-methylene-bis-acrylamide (BIS), N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAm), and tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED) were sourced from Acros Organics (Geel, Belgium). The Vero cell line (CCL-81) from the kidney tissue derived from a normal, adult African green monkey and human primary peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (PCS-800-011) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection Cell Bank. Fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100× antibiotic-antimycotic (contains penicillin, streptomycin, and amphotericin B), 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), Opti-MEM I reduced serum medium, and Trypan Blue stain solution (0.4%) were purchased from Gibco (Maryland, USA). Carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) was obtained from Lana Grow Chemical Company Ltd. (Nonthaburi, Thailand). 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) was obtained from PanReac AppliChem (Barcelona, Spain).

Preparation of Herpes Simplex Virus by Inoculation into Cell Culture

The HSV-1 strain KOS and HSV-2 clinically isolated strains were kindly provided by Prof. Dr. Pilaipan Puthavathana (Mahidol University, Thailand). HSV virion particles (i.e., HSV-1 and HSV-2) were propagated and produced with the Vero cell line, which is accessible for HSV culture and can be rapidly expanded for large-scale testing. All experimental procedures were conducted in a biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) laboratory in accordance with established safety guidelines. Cell cultures were maintained in an incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) under a humidified atmosphere of 5% carbon dioxide (CO2) at 37 °C. The Vero cells were cultured in a cell culture flask (T25) containing 3 mL of cell culture medium composed of DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) antibiotic-antimycotic solution. The morphology of the cells was examined using an EVOS inverted microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After the cells reached 70–90% confluence, the HSV was inoculated into the flask and allowed to reach complete cytopathic effect (CPE), such as rounding, detachment, or syncytium formation (typically visible at 24–72 h postinfection). The viral media, which contained extracellular virus, was collected, and the virus was purified by filtration using a 0.45 μm nylon syringe filter. The purified virus was inactivated with a 2% (v/v) formaldehyde solution before being frozen at −80 °C as virus template stock for nanoMIP synthesis.

Virus Quantification by Plaque-Forming Assay

HSV-2 causes strong CPE, and a plaque assay was conducted to quantify the infectious units within each virus production batch. The direct cell-to-cell transmission of HSV-2 leads to the formation of macroscopic focal cell detachment plaques, indicating the presence of infectious viral particles. To determine the HSV-2 particle concentration, plaque titration was performed in plaque-forming units per milliliter (pfu/mL). The Vero cells were cultured in 24-well plates for 24 h to reach 90% confluence before infection. To remove residual serum and medium, cells were gently washed once with sterile 1× phosphate-buffered saline (1× PBS), pH 7.4. HSV-2 stock was quantified by serial dilution to various dilutions (10–6–10–3) in serum-free Opti-MEM. Each virus dilution (450 μL) was added to each well, and the plate was gently shaken to spread the virus evenly across the monolayer. The plate was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1 h and shaken every 15 min to facilitate virus attachment. The virus inoculum was then carefully removed without disturbing the cell monolayer. Completed media containing 2% (w/v) carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, 500 μL) was added to prevent virus spread through the liquid medium and allowed plaque formation. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48–72 h. When the plaques were presented, the CMC overlay was removed, and the cells were fixed with 10% (v/v) formaldehyde for 60 min at room temperature (RT, 25 °C) and stained with crystal violet solution (50 μL), incubated for 15 min, and washed with deionized (DI) water. The characteristics of the plaques were examined in bright-field mode with an inverted microscope. The clear and circular plaques were formed on the stained monolayer, each well was counted, and the virus titer was calculated in pfu/mL using the formula: 1 pfu/mL=Average of plaque numbers per wellDilution×Volume of diluted virus added to the plate

Solid-Phase Synthesis of Molecularly Imprinted Polymer Nanoparticles (nanoMIPs)

The HSV-2 virion is a spherical enveloped particle with a diameter between 120 and 200 nm. The outermost layer of the virion consists of a lipid bilayer envelope adorned with glycoprotein spikes. The optimal condition for nanoMIP synthesis was studied by varying the ratio of functional monomers. Solid-phase synthesis was performed by adapting previously described procedures. , HSV-2 was covalently immobilized on silica gel, as shown in Scheme A. All separation and washing procedures were carried out by vacuum filtration using a glass filter crucible with a pore size of 50–190 μm. To generate −OH groups on the solid phase, 2 g of silica gel were dispersed in 4 mL of 1 M NaOH solution and heated to boiling for 15 min. The silica gels were then washed with DI water and acetone and dried at RT. The OH-silica gel was suspended in 2.5% (v/v) aminopropyl triethoxysilane (APTES) prepared in toluene, rotated at 20 rpm for 10 h, collected by filtration, and washed with an acetone-ethanol mixture (4:1, v/v) to yield amino-functionalized silica gel. Then, 1× PBS containing 7% (v/v) glutaraldehyde (GA) was added, incubated for 2 h, and washed with DI water to obtain imine-functionalized silica gel. Then, the HSV-2 template at a concentration of 6.59 × 1010 pfu/mL (from the previous HSV-2 plaque-forming assay) was added, incubated at 4 °C for 10 h, and washed with 1× PBS to obtain HSV-2-immobilized silica gel. The immobilized silica gel was then treated with 1× PBS containing 1 mg/mL sodium borohydride, incubated at RT for 30 min to reduce the Schiff base linkages, and washed with 1× PBS. Finally, 50 mM ethanolamine was added to block the free aldehyde group for 15 min, washed with DI water, and dried before being used as the template for nanoMIP synthesis. Previously published thin-film MIP compositions of dengue virus and influenza virus were used as the starting point for finding optimal nanoMIP synthesis for HSV-2 because the envelopes of these viruses are made up of glycoproteins. These viruses present different surface charges and characteristics; therefore, the optimal polymer composition was studied. Scheme B shows the process of nanoMIP synthesis. Three conditions of prepolymer mixtures were prepared using functional monomers, cross-linkers, initiators, and solvents, as shown in Table . HSV-2 exhibits a zeta potential of −11.88 ± 1.14 mV and possesses amphiphilic surface characteristics, comprising both negatively and positively charged side chains that contribute to its hydrophilic and hydrophobic domains. Therefore, copolymers incorporating N-tert-butylacrylamide (TBAm), N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAm), acrylamide (AAM), methacrylic acid (MAA), methyl methacrylate (MMA), and N-vinylpyrrolidone (NVP) were selected as functional monomers because their diverse chemical functionalities provided complementary electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions that were expected to facilitate favorable intermolecular interactions with HSV-2, and the optimal compositional ratio of these functional monomers was determined. The prepolymer mixture of the solution including AAM:MAA:MMA:NVP (at various ratios), 50 mg NIPAm, 43 mg TBAm, 6 mg N-(3-aminopropyl) methacrylamide hydrochloride (APMA), 3.1 mg N,N′-methylene-bis-acrylamide (BIS), 300 μL ethanol, and 3,000 μL DI water was sonicated for 10 min. Then, 2 g of HSV-2-immobilized silica gel was added to the mixture, followed by adding 30 μL of 20% (v/v) N,N,N′,N′ tetramethylethylenediamine solution (TEMED) and 30 μL of 20% (w/v) ammonium persulfate solution (APS) to initiate polymerization. The mixture was then purged with nitrogen for 10 min and incubated at RT for 2 h to allow complete polymerization before transfer into a filtration column and sequentially eluted with DI water at 4 and 25 °C to remove unreacted reagents and low-affinity polymer nanoparticles, respectively. Then, the column was rinsed with 50 mL of 65 °C DI water to collect the high-affinity nanoMIPs. Bovine serum albumin (BSA)-nanoMIPs were synthesized as a negative control to obtain polymer nanoparticles without HSV-2 imprinting, hereafter referred to as nanoNIPs. NanoNIPs were synthesized based on the previously reported nanoMIPs protocol for human serum albumin (HSA), considering the close structural and physicochemical similarities between HSA and BSA. The preparation was carried out using BSA-coated silica gel as the template support. The polymerization mixture for nanoNIPs consisted of AA (15.1 μL), NIPAm (50 mg), TBAm (43 mg), APM (6 mg), BIS (3.1 mg), 20% (w/v) APS (45 μL), and 20% (v/v) TEMED (45 μL), as shown in Table . To preserve all synthesized nanoparticles, 0.02% (w/v) sodium azide, which is a bacteriostatic agent, was added to nanoMIPs/nanoNIPs suspensions to inhibit microbial growth, and the mixtures were stored at 4 °C. To determine the yield of nanoMIPs and nanoNIPs, 2 mL of each nanoparticle suspension was transferred into preweighed glass vials. The solvent was evaporated at 65 °C to dryness, and the mass of the remaining solid residue was determined gravimetrically to calculate the nanoparticle concentration.

Characterization of Polymer Nanoparticles and HSV-2

Each suspension of nanoMIPs A, nanoMIPs B, nanoMIPs C, and nanoNIPs at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL was filtered through a 0.22 μm nylon syringe filter and sonicated for 15 min to ensure uniform dispersion before analyzing their characteristics. The hydrodynamic diameter and particle concentration of HSV-2 and polymer nanoparticles were determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) using the NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern Panalytical, UK). All measurements were conducted at 25 °C using a 488 nm laser and a light-scatter detection filter under a controlled sample flow rate of 1.5 μL/min. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate (n = 3), and the obtained data were processed using NanoSight NTA software and reported as mean ± SD. In addition, the hydrodynamic diameter and particle concentration were determined using multiangle dynamic light scattering (MADLS) with a Zetasizer Ultra Instrument (Malvern Panalytical, UK). The refractive index was fixed at 1.42 for HSV-2 and 1.56 for polymer nanoparticles, while the dispersant count rate was set to 130 kilocounts per second (kcps). The zeta potential was analyzed by electrophoretic light scattering (ELS) at 25 °C. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Talos F200i (S) TEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) was performed to characterize the morphology and particle size of HSV-2 and nanoMIPs. NanoMIPs at a concentration of 2.92 × 106 particles/mL and HSV-2 at a concentration of 6.59 × 109 pfu/mL (stained with 1% uranyl acetate) were deposited onto a TEM grid and dried before imaging.

Testing Binding Ability and Selectivity of NanoMIPs via Cyclic Voltammetry

The interaction between nanoMIPs/nanoNIPs and HSV-2 was investigated using cyclic voltammetry (CV). A screen-printed carbon electrode (model TE100, Zensor R&D, Taichung, Taiwan), incorporating a silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) reference electrode and a carbon counter electrode, was employed for electrochemical measurements. Polymer nanoparticles, e.g., nanoMIPs and nanoNIPs (2.92 × 106 particle/mL, 5 μL), were immobilized on the working electrode surface via physical adsorption and incubated at RT for 24 h, followed by a gentle rinsing step with DI water to remove any unbound polymers. All CV measurements were conducted using a 10 mM K3Fe­(CN)6 redox probe and assessed with an electrochemical analyzer-simulator (EIS model ACIP100, Zensor R&D, Taichung, Taiwan). The potential was scanned from −550 mV to +550 mV at a scan rate of 50 mV/s. The CV response of the nanoMIP-modified electrode was recorded to obtain a baseline voltammogram. The modified electrode was then incubated with a solution containing HSV-2 at a concentration of 2.88 × 109 pfu/mL for 30 min to allow for specific binding, and rinsed with DI water to remove unbound particles. The CV response was measured again under similar conditions, and the current shift was calculated to indicate the binding signal.

The selectivity of the nanoMIPs was assessed by the CV analysis against HSV-2 and other suspected viruses at the same concentration of 2.88 × 109 pfu/mL, including HSV-1, human papillomavirus type 16 (HPV 16), hepatitis B virus (HBV), and dengue virus type 1 (DENV-1). The HPV 16 pseudovirus was produced in the HEK293FT cell line by transfecting the p16sheLL plasmid, which encodes HPV 16 L1 and L2 capsid proteins (kindly provided by Dr. John T. Schiller at the National Cancer Institute, National Institute of Health, USA). All viruses were processed with a chemical inactivation procedure to maintain intact viral structure. HSV-1 was chosen because of its close genetic and structural similarity to HSV-2. HPV 16 and HBV were included because they share transmission routes (sexual contact) with HSV-2 and are relevant clinical confounders in genital infection panels. DENV-1 was included to assess nonspecific binding to an unrelated, nonsexually transmitted arbovirus. A blank control (1× PBS only) and a nanoNIP control were included to assess baseline and nonspecific signals. DNA Extraction and Genome Quantification of HSV-2 by Real-Time PCR Assay

A conventional real-time polymerase chain reaction (real-time PCR) assay was employed to quantify the genome copy number of HSV-2 DNA, indicating the presence of HSV-2 infection. Viral DNA was extracted from the infected cell suspension using the PureLink viral RNA/DNA mini kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, the cell suspension (200 μL) was lysed in lysis buffer and incubated at 56 °C for 15 min. The mixture was then mixed with absolute ethanol (250 μL) and incubated at RT for 5 min. The lysate was transferred to a viral spin column and centrifuged at 6,800 × g for 1 min to allow the nucleic acids to bind. The column was washed twice with ethanol-containing wash buffers (500 μL), each followed by centrifugation at 6,800 × g for 1 min, and then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 1 min to remove the residual wash buffer. Viral DNA was eluted into a clean recovery tube with 30 μL of RNase-free water by incubation at RT for 1 min, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 1 min to obtain the DNA. The purified HSV-2 DNA was then amplified by real-time PCR using SYBR Green qPCR master mix (Thermo Scientific) with specific primers. DNA amplification and result analysis were carried out on a CFX Opus 96 real-time PCR system (Hercules, California, USA). The forward primer (HSV pol-2 F: 5′- CAT CAC CGA CCC GGA GAG GGA C -3′) and the reverse primer (HSV pol-2 R: 5′- GGG CCA GGC GCT TGT TGG TGT A -3′) were obtained from the HSV 1/2 DNA polymerase gene from the NCBI GenBank (>MH697448.1 Human Alphaherpesvirus 2 isolate HSV2_27 DNA polymerase (UL30) gene, partial cds). The PCR reaction mixture comprised 2× SYBR Green qPCR master mix (10 μL), forward primer (10 μM, 0.5 μL), reverse primer (10 μM, 0.5 μL), template solution (2 μL), and sterile DI water (7 μL), yielding a total volume of 20 μL. The reaction conditions used to amplify the DNA were an initial denature at 95 °C for 60 s; the amplification cycle consists of 15 s at 95 °C and 30 s at a gradient of 51 °C/60 °C for 40 cycles. The melting curve analysis was established from 65 to 95 °C, with a 0.5 °C increment and a 5-s plate reading interval. A standard curve of HSV-2 copy number was established using a customized synthesis of the HSV-2 UL30 gene fragment inserted into the pGEMT plasmid. 10-fold serial dilution of the standard HSV-2 was prepared and quantified using real-time PCR, as previously described.

Investigation of the Influence of NanoMIPs on Reducing HSV-2 Infection

Investigation of NanoMIPs in Inhibiting HSV-2 Binding to Vero Cells via Cyclic Voltammetry

HSV-2 attaches to Vero cells through the interaction between viral glycoproteins and cell-surface receptors expressed on the Vero cell membrane. The effect of nanoMIPs on inhibiting the binding interaction between HSV-2 and the Vero cell surface was evaluated using CV. The experiments utilized commercially available screen-printed gold electrodes (model AUTE100, Zensor R&D, Taichung, Taiwan), with gold serving as both reference and counter electrodes. To mimic the physiological presentation of cell-surface receptors on the host membrane, Vero cells were immobilized onto the gold working electrode surface utilizing a cysteamine/glutaraldehyde cross-linking chemistry. In brief, the gold working electrodes were functionalized with a 2.5 mM aqueous cysteamine solution and incubated at 25 °C for 10 h to assemble a thiol-bound monolayer with terminal amine (−NH2) functionalities. The amine-functionalized surfaces were then activated by incubation with a 5 mM glutaraldehyde solution for 2 h to introduce aldehyde (−CHO) groups, followed by washing with DI water and drying with nitrogen gas. Then, a suspension of Vero cells (104 cells/mL, 5 μL) was deposited onto the aldehyde-functionalized gold surfaces. The electrodes were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to facilitate covalent immobilization via Schiff base formation between the surface aldehyde groups and the primary amines (−NH2) exposed on the Vero cell membrane proteins. The remaining unreacted aldehyde groups were blocked with a 0.5 mM ethanolamine solution for 20 min and washed with DI water.

HSV-2 suspension (108 pfu/mL) and nanoMIPs (2.92 × 106 particles/mL) were prepared in 1× PBS. Dextran sulfate (100 μg/mL) was used as a positive control for inhibition of HSV-2 attachment and entry into host cells. Dextran sulfate competitively binds to the positively charged domains on viral envelope glycoproteins gC and gB, thereby inhibiting attachment of virions to host cell surface receptors. The inhibitory effect of nanoMIPs on HSV-2 binding was evaluated using various experimental groups: (1) HSV-2 alone as the positive binding control; (2) virus pretreatment, in which HSV-2 was preincubated with nanoMIPs or dextran sulfate at a 1:1 ratio for 1 h before being introduced onto the immobilized cells; (3) cell pretreatment, in which immobilized Vero cells were incubated with nanoMIPs for 1 h and removed by washing with 1× PBS, followed by HSV-2 addition; and (4) cotreatment, in which HSV-2 and nanoMIPs were simultaneously added onto the immobilized Vero cells. The K3Fe­(CN)6 redox probe was utilized to facilitate interfacial electron transfer at the electrode surface, serving as an indicator to monitor changes in the electrochemical response resulting from surface modification and binding events. CV analysis was performed using the electrochemical analyzer described previously. In brief, 5 μL of each sample was introduced on the Vero cell-immobilized working electrode and incubated at 25 °C for 1 h. The electrode was then rinsed with DI water, followed by the addition of 10 mM K3Fe­(CN)6. The scan potential range was from −550 mV to +550 mV at a scan rate of 50 mV/s.

Cytotoxicity Testing

The safety profile of the nanoMIPs was evaluated by assessing cytotoxicity in two different models: Vero cells (an immortalized lineage) and primary human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Vero cells were seeded into a 96-well tissue culture plate with the concentration at 2 × 104 cells/well with complete cell culture medium and incubated for 24 h. At the subconfluence cell growth, 100 μL of various concentrations of nanoMIPs (0.73–11.68 × 106 particles/mL) were added into cells and incubated for 24 h. Untreated cells served as the normal growth control (defined as 100% viability). The cytotoxicity of nanoMIPs toward Vero cells at varying concentrations was evaluated using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. In brief, the treated cells were rinsed twice with 1× PBS, and 1 mg/mL (w/v) of MTT solution was added and incubated for 4 h to allow formazan crystal conversion. Cell culture medium was removed, and formazan crystal was dissolved with DMSO. The absorbance (Abs) was measured with a microplate spectrophotometer at 570 nm. The percentage of cell viability was calculated as: 4 % cell viability=Abs570of sampleAbs570of untreated cell control×100

In the primary cell investigation, human PBMCs were inoculated at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS. The cells were treated with nanoMIPs at concentrations identical to those used in the Vero cell assays and incubated for 48 h. PBMC viability was assessed using the Trypan Blue exclusion assay. Briefly, the cell suspension was harvested and mixed with 0.4% (w/v) Trypan Blue solution at a 1:1 ratio. The mixture was incubated for 3 min at room temperature. Total and viable (dye-excluding) cells were quantified using a Neubauer hemocytometer under a light microscope. Cell viability was calculated as the percentage of viable cells relative to the total number of cells. The different exposure durations used for Vero cells and PBMCs were chosen based on the biological characteristics of each cell type and the specific endpoints evaluated. Vero cells are rapidly proliferating epithelial cells; therefore, a 24-h exposure period is commonly employed to assess acute cytotoxic effects and metabolic alterations during the exponential growth phase. In contrast, PBMCs are primary immune cells that typically exhibit a more quiescent physiological state under in vitro conditions. Accordingly, a longer exposure period of 48 h was applied to allow sufficient time for the detection of potential delayed toxic effects or membrane destabilization.

Investigation of the Antiviral Mechanism of NanoMIPs Using a Time-of-Addition Assay

The antiviral activity of nanoMIPs at different stages of HSV-2 infection was investigated using a time-of-addition assay. Vero cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h until reaching approximately 80–90% confluence. HSV-2 suspension (108 pfu/mL), nanoMIPs (2.92 × 106 particles/mL), and dextran sulfate (100 μg/mL) were prepared in serum-free Opti-MEM. Four treatment conditions were performed to determine the stage at which nanoMIPs exert antiviral activity. For the virus pretreatment group, HSV-2 was preincubated with nanoMIPs at a 1:1 ratio for 1 h prior to infection. The virus–nanoMIP mixture was then added onto Vero cells and incubated for 1 h to allow viral adsorption. Subsequently, the inoculum was removed, cells were washed with 1× PBS, and complete culture medium was added. For the cell pretreatment group, Vero cells were preincubated with nanoMIPs for 1 h at 37 °C before viral infection. After treatment, excess nanoMIPs were removed by washing with 1× PBS, followed by infection with HSV-2 for 1 h. For the cotreatment group, HSV-2 and nanoMIPs were simultaneously added to Vero cells and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. For the postentry treatment group, Vero cells were first infected with HSV-2 for 1 h at 37 °C to permit viral adsorption and entry. Unbound virus was subsequently removed by washing with 1× PBS, after which nanoMIPs were added to the cells and further incubated. Following each treatment condition, cells were maintained in complete culture medium at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 h. Antiviral activity was evaluated by quantification of HSV-2 viral DNA copy number using real-time PCR as previously described.

Investigation of the Concentration-Dependent Influence of NanoMIPs on HSV-2 Infection Inhibition

Vero cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h before treatment. Positive control of HSV-2 infection was performed with infected cells with HSV-2 (108 pfu/mL), whereas control of no viral infection was treated with heat-inactivated HSV-2. Cells treated with nanoMIPs alone were included as a material-treated control. To assess the inhibitory effect of nanoMIPs on HSV-2 infection, nanoMIPs at concentrations ranging from 2.92 × 104 to 2.92 × 106 particles/mL were preincubated with HSV-2 (108 pfu/mL, at a ratio of 1:1) for 1 h before being added to Vero cells. The treatment mixture, including controls, was added to the cells and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1 h. The inoculum was removed, complete culture medium was added, and the cells were further incubated for 24 h under the same conditions. The infected cells with culture media were collected, and DNA was extracted for viral quantification by real-time PCR. The HSV-2 viral load was shown as the DNA copy number to determine the inhibitory effect of nanoMIPs on HSV-2 infection compared to control groups. The relative impact (%) was defined as the ratio of HSV-2 DNA detected in the nanoMIP-treated samples to the DNA detected in the HSV-2-only control group. Each experiment was conducted in triplicates, with results presented as mean ± SD.

NanoMIPs Stability Testing

Effect of pH on NanoMIP Performance

The vaginal pH in healthy reproductive-age women typically ranges from 3.5 to 4.5 but may increase under certain conditions, including menstruation, semen exposure, postmenopausal status (pH 5.0–7.0), or vaginal infections, where pH values often exceed 4.5. To evaluate pH-dependent behavior, nanoMIPs were dispersed in 1× PBS adjusted to pH 4.0, 7.4, and 10.0. These conditions were selected to represent acidic, physiological, and basic environments, respectively. Assessing nanoMIP performance across this range provides insight into their stability and interaction profiles under varying conditions relevant to biomedical applications. In brief, nanoMIPs (2.92 × 106 particles/mL) at the indicated pH values were preincubated with HSV-2 (108 pfu/mL) for 1 h prior to infection of Vero cells, as previously described. Antiviral activity at each pH was examined by quantifying viral DNA copy number using real-time PCR.

Effect of Autoclaving on NanoMIP Performance

Sterility is critical for in vitro and in vivo applications, as microbial contamination can adversely affect experimental reliability and safety. NanoMIP and nanoNIP suspensions (2.92 × 106 particles/mL) were sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C under a pressure of 15 pounds per square inch for 15 min to denature the biological contaminants, including bacterial cells, endospores, and fungal spores. To assess the effects of high-temperature, high-pressure sterilization on the particle size and colloidal stability of MIP NPs, their hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were measured before and after the sterilization process. The inhibitory activity of nanoMIPs after sterilization in Vero cells was examined by quantifying viral DNA copy number using real-time PCR. The results were compared with those obtained using nonautoclaved suspensions.

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD of at least three independent experiments. All replicates described in this study represent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test, while comparisons involving multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, as appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Characteristics of Polymer Nanoparticles and HSV-2

Figure A shows the yield of polymer nanoparticle synthesis. The elution of 2 g of silica gel with 50 mL of DI water yielded a nanoparticle mass concentration in the range of 0.5–0.75 mg/mL. Correspondingly, NTA demonstrated particle concentrations in the range of 3.19 × 108 to 8.54 × 108 particles/mL (Figure B). These results indicate that solid-phase synthesis enables the reproducible production of polymer nanoparticles with consistently high yields across the different synthesis conditions. Figure C shows the hydrodynamic diameters of HSV-2, nanoMIPs, and nanoNIPs measured by NTA. The particle size distribution by number and video screenshot of particles in each sample (inset) from NTA was shown in Figure S3. The HSV-2 showed an average size of 94 ± 6 nm. The average diameters of nanoMIPs were 123 ± 6 nm for nanoMIPs A, 106 ± 7 nm for nanoMIPs B, and 146 ± 4 nm for nanoMIPs C. The nanoMIPs exhibited larger particle sizes compared to the size of HSV-2. This result showed that template-directed imprinting effectively customized nanoparticle size and morphology to mimic template dimensions, hence improving specificity and recognition ability. The nanoNIPs exhibited a smaller average diameter of 101 ± 3 nm, which can be attributed to the use of the smaller BSA template compared to HSV-2.

Characterization of HSV-2 and polymer nanoparticles, including polymer synthesis yield (A), particle concentration (B), hydrodynamic diameter (C), and zeta potential (D).

In addition, hydrodynamic diameter and particle concentration were examined by MADLS. As shown in Figure S4, MADLS yielded larger hydrodynamic diameters for both HSV-2 and nanoMIPs than NTA (Figure S4A), whereas it reported particle concentrations that were an order of magnitude lower (∼107 particles/mL) than those determined by NTA (∼108 particles/mL) (Figure S4B). These differences arise from the distinct measurement principles of the two techniques. MADLS provides an intensity-weighted average size, wherein larger particles or small aggregates disproportionately influence the scattering signal. In contrast, NTA tracks individual particles to resolve a number-based particle size distribution, making it highly effective for evaluating aggregation behavior in heterogeneous nanoparticle systems.

The zeta potential of HSV-2 was −11.88 ± 1.14 mV. NanoMIPs A, B, C, and nanoNIPs exhibited negatively charged particle surfaces with zeta potentials of −35.03 ± 1.75, −38.02 ± 1.90, −39.76 ± 1.98, and −32.42 ± 1.62 mV, respectively, as shown in Figure D. Zeta potential values with an absolute magnitude greater than 30 mV are generally regarded as indicative of colloidal stability, as the resulting surface charge provides sufficient electrostatic repulsion to prevent particle aggregation in suspension.

Figure shows the TEM images of HSV-2 dispersed in complete cell culture medium (Figure A) and 1× PBS (Figure B), while polymer nanoparticles were dispersed in DI water. HSV-2 particles exhibited spherical morphologies with an average diameter of 83 ± 5 nm. TEM images of HSV-2 dispersed in complete cell culture medium and stained with uranyl acetate showed lower clarity than samples dispersed in 1× PBS, likely due to medium components interfering with negative staining and increasing background electron density. NanoNIPs (Figure C) also displayed spherical morphology with diameters of 74 ± 5 nm, while the nanoMIPs synthesized under conditions A–C (Figure D–F) exhibited average diameters of 97 ± 21 nm, 88 ± 18 nm, and 103 ± 23 nm, respectively. The diameter of nanoMIPs was larger than the HSV-2 template, indicating effective imprinting, with the steric and surface characteristics of HSV-2 guiding polymer chain propagation during nanoparticle formation. The hydrodynamic diameters of HSV-2 and polymer nanoparticles measured by NTA were larger than those obtained from TEM diameters, attributed to the dispersant’s influence on particle size in solution.

Binding Ability and Selectivity of Polymer Nanoparticles

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was employed to directly evaluate the interaction between polymer nanoparticles immobilized on the sensor electrode surface and HSV-2. An indirect cyclic voltammetry approach was adopted as both nanoMIPs and HSV-2 are electrochemically inactive and do not generate intrinsic redox signals. Potassium ferricyanide was employed as a redox probe to mediate electron transfer at the electrode interface, thereby enabling the evaluation of the electrochemical response. The modification of the electrode surface with a material layer, including nanoMIPs, nanoNIPs, and HSV-2, can alter the peak current responses compared with those of the bare electrode. When HSV-2 bound to the nanoMIPs on the electrode surface, a physical barrier was created that impeded the diffusion of the redox probe to the electrode surface, thereby reducing the corresponding peak currents.

Figure A–C presents cyclic voltammograms of the interaction between three different conditions of nanoMIPs and HSV-2. NanoMIPs A (Figure A), nanoMIPs B (Figure B), and nanoMIPs C (Figure C) were employed as the recognition element on the sensor electrode. The bare carbon electrode (blue line) exhibited the highest peak currents, while the immobilized nanoMIPs (orange line) showed reduced peak currents, confirming that the nanoMIPs were successfully immobilized on the carbon electrode. On exposure to HSV-2 (gray line), the nanoMIP-modified electrodes exhibited substantial reductions in peak currents compared to their baselines (current shifts of nanoMIPs A, B, and C = 35.0 ± 1.8, 38.2 ± 1.9, and 43.0 ± 2.2 μA, respectively, as shown in Figure F), indicating that binding ability between the nanoMIPs and HSV-2 had occurred. NanoMIPs C exhibited the greatest current shift compared to the other nanoMIPs, indicating superior binding ability for HSV-2; hence, this condition was chosen for further experiments. Figure D–E shows cyclic voltammograms of the interaction between nanoNIPs and BSA and HSV-2, respectively. The interaction between nanoNIPs and BSA exhibited reductions in peak currents (current shift = 45.4 ± 2.3 μA), while the interaction between nanoNIPs and HSV-2 exhibited a slight decrease in current (current shift = 13.3 ± 0.7 μA), demonstrating negligible binding between the nanoNIPs and HSV-2. Therefore, the nanoNIPs were employed as a negative control, representing polymer nanoparticles lacking specific recognition sites for HSV-2 in subsequent experiments. The CV analysis demonstrated that selective recognition sites for HSV-2 were successfully integrated into the polymer nanoparticles through molecular imprinting. The nanoNIPs exhibited poor recognition ability toward HSV-2, while the imprinted HSV-2 nanoMIPs A, B, and C demonstrated improved selectivity.

HSV-1 and HSV-2 had larger particle diameters, ranging from 120 to 200 nm, while HPV 16, HBV, and DENV-1 were smaller, with diameters of 42 to 60 nm. The surfaces of HSV-1 and HSV-2 were dominated by viral glycoproteins embedded in a lipid envelope. Figures shows the current shifts of nanoMIPs and nanoNIPs after exposure to each virus and the corresponding selectivity factors (SF). The interaction between nanoNIPs and all viruses resulted in only a slight decrease in current (current shift = 1.36 ± 0.45 μA), indicating low nonspecific binding. NanoMIPs exhibited the highest binding response to HSV-2 (current shift = 43.70 ± 1.31 μA, SF = 31.21 ± 1.24) due to the exact match between the imprinted polymer cavities and the structural and molecular characteristics of the target virion. HSV-1, despite sharing a similar enveloped structure and particle size, exhibited a reduced binding response (current shift = 40.05 ± 1.20 μA, SF = 19.29 ± 0.77), resulting in 62 ± 2% of the HSV-2 signal. This reduced interaction was attributed to the sequence and conformational differences in homologous surface glycoproteins, as well as the altered surface charge distribution. HSV-2-imprinted cavities were created to fit the larger HSV-2 virion, while smaller particles, including HPV 16 (50–60 nm), HBV (∼42 nm), and DENV-1 (∼50 nm), were partially adsorbed into these cavities. This allowed for localized interactions without full structural or multivalent complementarity, resulting in 30 ± 1%, 1.73 ± 0.05%, and 2.85 ± 0.09% of the HSV-2 signal, respectively. The elevated HPV 16 cross-reactive signal was due to nonspecific electrostatic and hydrogen-bond interactions between the highly charged, repetitive L1 capsid surface and functional groups within the nanoMIPs cavities, rather than true size- or topology-matched molecular recognition. The cross-reactivity of the nanoMIPs with HSV-2, HSV-1, and HPV 16 did not compromise their intended protective application. The capacity to bind multiple sexually transmitted viruses may provide a beneficial sequestration effect, potentially reducing viral load and limiting host cell infection.

Influence of NanoMIPs on Reducing the Binding Interaction Between HSV-2 and Vero Cells

Figure presents cyclic voltammograms of seven different sample groups measured using the cysteamine/glutaraldehyde-modified electrode. Vero cells expressed surface receptors as the selective recognition layer on the sensor electrode. The peak current of the Vero cell-modified electrode was used as the baseline response in the absence of HSV-2 interaction. Upon addition of HSV-2 alone (red line), the peak current decreased to 57 ± 1 μA, corresponding to the current shift (ΔI) of 35 ± 1 μA. This decrease indicates reduced electron transfer resulting from binding interactions between HSV-2 and the immobilized Vero cells. Cytotoxicity Testing

Figure shows the viability of Vero cells, as determined by the MTT assay after 24-h exposure to nanoMIPs at concentrations ranging from 0.73 × 106 to 11.68 × 106 particles/mL. At concentrations of 0.73 × 106, 1.46 × 106, and 2.92 × 106 particles/mL, cell viabilities were 106 ± 0.82%, 115 ± 1.38%, and 97 ± 3.58%, respectively. In this range, metabolic activity remained well above the 80% threshold, indicating no significant cytotoxic effects. However, a significant reduction in viability was observed at higher concentrations, with values dropping to 56 ± 1.80% at 5.84 × 106 particles/mL and 15 ± 0.63% at 11.68 × 106 particles/mL, suggesting dose-dependent toxicity at elevated levels.

Cytotoxicity profile of nanoMIPs. Percentage viability of Vero cells following 24 h exposure, measured by MTT assay (blue bars). Percentage viability of human PBMCs following 48 h exposure, determined by Trypan Blue exclusion staining (orange bars).

To improve translational relevance, primary human PBMCs were treated with the same concentration range of nanoMIPs for an extended duration of 48 h. Cell viability was assessed via Trypan Blue exclusion staining to monitor membrane integrity. PBMC viability remained above 80% for nanoMIP concentrations up to 2.92 × 106 particles/mL. This indicates that nanoMIPs do not induce primary cellular toxicity or significant membrane disruption in human immune cells at these concentrations. The consistency between the metabolic activity assessed in Vero cells and the membrane integrity observed in primary PBMCs further supports the biocompatibility of the synthesized nanoMIPs. However, future studies should include human-derived mucosal cell lines that more closely represent the natural site of HSV-2 infection. In addition, further safety assessments, including analyses of oxidative stress, apoptosis, and long-term cell viability, are necessary to establish a more comprehensive toxicological profile for potential clinical application.

Based on these findings, all subsequent experiments were carried out using nanoMIPs within the concentration range up to 2.92 × 106 particles/mL to ensure both biocompatibility and experimental reliability. Vero cells were selected as the in vitro model because they are highly permissive to HSV-2 infection and express the host cell-surface receptors required for viral attachment and entry. In addition, Vero cells are robust, easy to culture, and provide high experimental reproducibility with lower variability compared with primary cells.

Table summarizes the comparative performance of the nanoMIPs against clinically used HSV-2 therapeutics and alternative entry inhibitors. The nanoMIP platform offers a mechanistically distinct advantage over clinically used nucleoside analogues (e.g., acyclovir and valacyclovir), which operate postinfection by targeting viral DNA polymerase during intracellular replication. In contrast, nanoMIPs actively intercept the virus at the earliest stage of infection; their multivalent engagement with conserved viral envelope glycoproteins sterically obstructs attachment, preventing host-cell entry. This upstream therapeutic intervention minimizes the potential for mutational escape, such as mutations in viral thymidine kinase (TK) or DNA polymerase, which frequently drives drug resistance in immunocompromised patients undergoing long-term nucleoside therapy. Furthermore, unlike biologic entry inhibitors such as antibodies and recombinant peptides or nonselective nanomaterials with limited target specificity, nanoMIPs uniquely integrate biomimetic molecular recognition with the inherent robustness of synthetic polymers. This combination yields superior physicochemical stability, resistance to enzymatic proteolysis, and the potential for scalable cold-chain-independent manufacturing. These strategic advantages establish the nanoMIP platform as a robust and highly differentiated antiviral modality for HSV-2 intervention.

Conclusions

NanoMIPs were successfully synthesized to selectively bind to HSV-2 particles as an alternative material for the reduction of HSV-2 infection. The cytotoxicity test results indicated that nanoMIPs were biocompatible at concentrations up to 2.92 × 106 particles/mL, exhibiting no significant toxicity in Vero cells and human PBMCs. The HSV-2 copy numbers of infected Vero cells, when HSV-2 was pretreated with nanoMIPs (2.92 × 106 particles/mL), decreased by 80 ± 1% compared to HSV-2 alone. NanoMIPs inhibit HSV-2 primarily during viral attachment by binding to entire HSV-2 particles, thereby covering and limiting the glycoproteins that serve as binding proteins during HSV-2 entry into Vero cells. NanoMIPs maintained their antiviral activity after exposure to extreme pH conditions and autoclave sterilization, demonstrating robust physicochemical stability. Hence, these nanoMIPs offer a novel, stable, and biocompatible material to reduce HSV-2 infection, demonstrating potential strategies for future protective/therapeutic agents to prevent sexually transmitted HSV-2 infection. The nanoMIPs demonstrated inhibitory activity under the present in vitro conditions, but additional evaluation of cytotoxicity, pharmacokinetics, and antiviral efficacy in relevant in vivo models is required to further assess their safety, biodistribution, and therapeutic potential. Such studies will determine whether the observed entry inhibition in this study is maintained in complex biological environments.
