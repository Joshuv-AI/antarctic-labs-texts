Psychometric properties of the Chinese version of the attitude and confidence with oral healthcare among nursing students (ACORN) scale: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xuancheng Chen, Yangyi Chen, Yuhuan Xie, Xuejiao Zhu, Linan Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNursing students’ attitudes and confidence in oral healthcare are essential for service improvement. While the Attitude and Confidence with Oral Healthcare among Nursing Students (ACORN) scale is a validated assessment tool, its reliability and validity in China remain unverified. This study aims to translate the ACORN scale into Chinese and evaluate its validity and reliability among nursing students.MethodsThis cross-sectional methodological study involved the translation and validation of the ACORN scale into Chinese. A total of 474 nursing students from two universities in China were surveyed between February and April 2024 using the finalized Chinese version of the scale. Eight nursing education experts verified the content validity by assessing the relevance and clarity of the items. The exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) samples comprised 237 students, respectively, to test the structural validity of the inventory. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α and Spearman-Brown split-half reliability, test-retest reliability was evaluated to determine the stability of the scale.ResultsA total of 474 students were included. The Chinese ACORN scale consists of 24 items organized into four factors. The item-level and scale-level content validity index was 0.891 and 0.906, respectively. EFA identified four factors, accounting for 72.626% of the cumulative variance; CFA supported a good model fit. The scale demonstrated strong internal consistency (0.938). Additionally, the test-retest and Spearman-Brown split-half reliability were 0.906 and 0.729, respectively.ConclusionsThe Chinese ACORN scale demonstrated strong validity and reliability, making it a suitable tool for assessing nursing students’ attitudes and confidence in oral healthcare. Its application can inform targeted nursing curriculum enhancements and training interventions and guide clinical training to better integrate oral healthcare into nursing practice.

Introduction

Since the early 21st century, oral diseases have become a major global public health challenge, affecting an estimated 3.5 billion people worldwide [1]. Oral health conditions, including dental caries and periodontal disease, are among the most common and preventable chronic diseases, affecting 90% of individuals across the lifespan [2]. Numerous studies have shown that oral health is closely linked to overall health [3–5], with poor dental hygiene being associated with dementia [6], increased mortality [7], and reduced life expectancy [8]. Oral healthcare plays a pivotal role in the prevention of dental caries and periodontal disease, as well as in the maintenance of overall oral health [9]. Research also indicates that proper oral care can effectively prevent aspiration pneumonia [10] and pneumonia associated with respiratory conditions [11]. Accordingly, the importance of oral healthcare has been increasingly emphasized in both research and clinical settings.

Nurses play a crucial role in assessing the oral health status of hospitalized patients and providing oral care to maintain optimal oral health [1]. Evidence suggests that nursing interventions in oral healthcare can improve treatment outcomes and enhance quality of life, particularly for patients with special healthcare needs [12]. International organizations such as the Institute of Medicine (IOM) [13], the International Dental Federation [14], and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) [15] have highlighted the importance of involving non-dental healthcare professionals, including nurses and other specialized providers, in collaborative oral care efforts to achieve optimal oral and overall health outcomes for patients. However, studies have shown that nurses often have limited knowledge and awareness of oral health issues [16], demonstrate negative attitudes [17], and tend to undervalue oral healthcare in clinical practice [18, 19], compounded by inadequate education [20] and training [21]. Additional research indicates that routine assessments of new admissions by nurses frequently exclude oral health evaluations [22], making oral care one of the most commonly overlooked aspects [23]. Moreover, 50.3% of patients reported that nurses did not provide oral care [24]. These factors significantly hinder nurses’ ability to conduct oral health assessments and provide high-quality oral care, thereby impeding the improvement of patients’ oral health and quality of life [25–27].

As future key providers of oral care, nursing students play a key role in advancing the oral health of the general population [28]. However, due to a lack of interest, inadequate attitudes, and limited confidence in oral care [18, 25], combined with low levels of personal oral health literacy [29] and insufficient knowledge of oral healthcare [16], they may underestimate the importance of oral health in patient care. This increases the likelihood that effective oral care will not be delivered [30], thereby failing to improve patients’ oral health and quality of life [31]. Therefore, it is essential to conduct timely and effective quantitative assessments of nursing students’ attitudes and confidence toward oral healthcare during their education and clinical practice.

To address this gap, Australian scholar Rojo et al., following Davis’s development guidelines [32], developed the Attitude and Confidence with Oral Healthcare among Nursing Students (ACORN) Scale, specifically designed to assess nursing students’ attitudes and confidence toward oral healthcare. This scale was scientifically developed and validated among Australian nursing students, demonstrating excellent reliability and validity [33]. However, in China, research on nursing students’ attitudes and confidence toward oral healthcare remains limited, and there is a lack of scientifically reliable tools to assess their attitudes and confidence regarding oral health education.

Therefore, the objective of this study is to translate the ACORN scale into Chinese and validate its applicability for Chinese nursing students. This research will assist nursing educators in effectively evaluating nursing students’ attitudes and confidence in providing oral care, as well as assessing the impact of oral health education programs during their education and clinical practice.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional design and followed the strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Participants

This cross-sectional study, conducted between February and April 2024, employed convenience sampling to recruit 474 full-time nursing undergraduates from two universities in hangzhou, Zhejiang province, china. The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: (1) full-time undergraduate nursing students; and (2) willing to provide informed consent and voluntarily participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were: (1) students on leave due to illness, personal reasons, or academic suspension; and (2) those unwilling to participate. Additionally, 30 nursing undergraduates who Met the inclusion criteria were selected to assess the test-retest reliability of the scale, with a one-week interval between measurements. Importantly, these 30 participants were excluded in the primary survey cohort.

Instruments

The demographic questionnaire includes information such as gender, age, grade, place of residence, and self-assessed oral health status. The attitude and confidence with oral healthcare among nursing students (ACORN) scale, developed by Rojo et al. in 2022 [33], assesses nursing students’ attitudes and confidence toward oral healthcare. It consists of two dimensions: attitude (8 items) and confidence (16 items), totaling 24 items. Both dimensions are rated on a 7-point likert scale, with attitude ranging from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (7), and confidence ranging from “not confident at all” (1) to “extremely confident” (7). The total score ranges from 24 to 168, with higher scores indicating more favorable attitudes and greater confidence in oral healthcare. The scale demonstrates strong reliability, with a cronbach’s α of 0.938 and dimension-specific values ranging from 0.880 to 0.946 [33]

Translation procedure

With authorization from the original author via email, the scale was translated into Chinese using the Brislin back-translation method [34]. Initially, one nursing postgraduate fluent in English and one nursing Ph.D. with extensive study-abroad experience independently translated the scale into two versions, A1 and A2. The research team then reviewed the differences between these versions with the translators, making revisions through mutual consultation to produce version A3. Subsequently, two master’s students specializing in English translation, who were not involved in the original translation and had not seen the English version of the scale, performed a back-translation of version A3 into English, resulting in back-translation versions B1 and B2. The research team consolidated these back-translations and addressed any discrepancies with the translators. After further consultation, version B3 was finalized. To ensure semantic equivalence throughout the process, version B3 was sent to the original author for review. The team then incorporated the author’s feedback to clarify any ambiguities, resulting in the preliminary Chinese version of the ACORN scale. Eight nursing education professionals, including three associate professors and two professors, evaluated the dimensions and items of the scale using a 4-point Likert scale. Based on their feedback, the research team revised the scale, producing the revised Chinese ACORN scale. Following cross-cultural adaptation guidelines, 30 nursing undergraduates (they were excluded from the formal survey sample) meeting the inclusion criteria were selected randomly for a pre-survey using the revised Chinese version of the ACORN scale. In this survey, participants reported that the items of the revised Chinese version of the ACORN scale were easy to understand, aligned well with Chinese reading conventions, and effectively reflected the intended constructs. As no substantive modifications were necessary, the final version of the Chinese ACORN scale was thus established.

Data collection

Data were collected using the online survey platform Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/), which facilitates the completion of questionnaires by participants. Researchers ensured that participants met the inclusion criteria before distributing the survey link to nursing students via DingTalk, a Chinese messaging app. The online survey consisted of an introduction to the study’s purpose, an informed consent form, and the final Chinese version of the ACORN scale. Upon completion, the research team reviewed the responses and excluded invalid questionnaires. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) response time < 120 s, (2) evident logical inconsistencies in responses, and (3) identifiable response patterns. According to the sample size estimation method proposed by Kendall [35], the sample size should be 10 to 15 times the number of items. With 24 items in this study and accounting for a 10% attrition rate, the sample size should range from 264 to 396 participants. However, considering the requirements for confirmatory factor analysis, the sample size should be ≥ 200 participants [36]. Thus, the final sample size was determined to be at least 464 participants.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and AMOS 24.0 software. Descriptive statistics, including the mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range, were used to report continuous variables, while frequency and percentages were applied to categorical variables. A normality test was performed on the scores collected from the Chinese version of the ACORN scale. A significance level of P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Project analysis

Item analysis of the Chinese version of the ACORN scale was conducted using the critical ratio (CR) method and Pearson correlation coefficient method to assess item discrimination and correlation. In the CR method, scale scores were ranked from low to high, with the top 27% and bottom 27% classified as high and low groups, respectively. Independent samples t-tests were performed to compare score differences between the two groups for each item. Items with a CR value < 3.00 or those showing no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05) were considered to have insufficient discrimination and were excluded [37]. Additionally, the Pearson correlation coefficient method was used, with items demonstrating correlation coefficients between 0.3 and 1.0 considered to show good discrimination and correlation. Items with correlation coefficients below 0.3 or lacking statistical significance (P > 0.05) were considered for removal [38].

Validity analysis

Content validity

Eight experts contributed to the cultural adaptation of the scale, evaluating the relevance of the items and dimensions of the Chinese version using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = Not Relevant, 2 = Slightly Relevant, 3 = Relevant, 4 = Very Relevant). The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) were calculated. An I-CVI ≥ 0.780 and an S-CVI ≥ 0.800 indicate good content validity [39].

Construct validity

The structural validity of the scale was evaluated using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The research team randomly selected 50% of the sample for EFA (n = 237) [40]. Principal component analysis with varimax rotation was used to extract common factors with eigenvalues > 1.00, and items with factor loadings < 0.40 or multiple loadings were removed. A Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value > 0.8 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ²) with statistical significance (P < 0.05) indicated that the data were suitable for EFA. A factor loading > 0.50 for an item on its corresponding factor and a cumulative variance contribution rate (CVCR) > 60% indicated good structural validity [41]. Subsequently, CFA was conducted on the remaining 237 samples using AMOS 24.0, constructing a model with common factors as latent variables and corresponding items as observed variables. Multiple fit indices were used to evaluate the model’s goodness of fit, including the chi-square/degrees of freedom ratio (χ²/df), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), normed fit index (NFI), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and incremental fit index (IFI). The acceptable criteria for model fit were: χ²/df < 5, CFI, TLI and IFI ≥ 0.9, and RMSEA < 0.08 [42].

Reliability analysis

The reliability of the Chinese version of the ACORN scale was assessed through internal consistency and stability analyses. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α coefficient and Spearman-Brown split-half reliability [43]. A Cronbach’s α coefficient > 0.7 indicates good internal consistency, and a split-half reliability coefficient ≥ 0.7 suggests that the internal consistency is acceptable [44]. Stability was assessed using test-retest reliability. After a one-week interval, 30 randomly selected participants who met the inclusion criteria were retested, and the correlation coefficient between the two test scores was calculated. An intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) > 0.610 indicates good test-retest reliability of the scale [45].

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the School of Nursing, Hangzhou Normal University (2024008). All methods used in this study were in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The online questionnaire included an informed consent section, and participants were required to confirm their consent before completing the formal survey. Participation was voluntary, and participants were free to withdraw or discontinue their involvement at any time.

Results

Cross-cultural adaptation results of the Chinese ACORN scale

The research team, in consultation with eight experts, revised the wording of several questionnaire items to enhance the instrument’s applicability. The number of items remained unchanged. The revisions were as follows: (1) Item 4, “Nurses are well-positioned to provide early detection of oral health problem(s)”, was revised to “Nurses are very capable of discovering oral health problems in the early stage”, (2) Item 6, “It is the role of the nurse to remind patients to attend to their oral care”, was revised to “Reminding patients to participate in oral care is the responsibility of nurses” and (3) Item 12, “Referring a patient to dental services following your oral health assessment”, was revised to “Referring patients to dental services based on their oral health assessment results”. These revisions were reviewed and approved by the original authors.

Demographic data

A total of 496 questionnaires were distributed, satisfying the sample size requirements. After excluding 22 responses that did not meet the inclusion criteria, 474 valid responses were obtained, resulting in a response rate of 95.56%. The participants had a mean age of 19.87 ± 1.41 years, with the majority aged between 20 and 21 years (n = 235, 49.58%). Of the participants, 388 (81.86%) were female, 199 (41.98%) were freshman students, 200 (41.98%) were from urban areas, and 215 (45.36%) rated their oral health status as average (Table 1).

Item analysis

In this study, the results of the CR method indicated that the CR values for the 24 items ranged from 6.15 to 13.73, all exceeding 3.0, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.001), suggesting that the items demonstrated excellent discriminatory power. The analysis utilizing Pearson’s correlation coefficient revealed that the correlation coefficients between all items and the total score of the questionnaire ranged from 0.530 to 0.707, indicating excellent correlations and strong discriminative ability for each item (P < 0.001). All 24 items were retained.

Validity analysis

Content validity

An expert panel of eight members evaluated the content relevance of the dimensions and items of the Chinese version of the ACORN scale. The results indicated that the I-CVI was 0.891, S-CVI was 0.906, both meeting the required standards. This suggests that the scale demonstrated good content validity.

Construct validity-exploratory factor analysis

The scores obtained from the Chinese version of the ACORN scale exhibit a normal distribution. EFA was performed on the 24 items of the Chinese version of the ACORN scale. The results indicated that the KMO value was 0.899, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity yielded χ² = 4786.184 (P < 0.001), confirming that the data were suitable for EFA. Principal component analysis with varimax rotation was employed to extract four common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.000, explaining a cumulative variance contribution of 72.626%. Based on the original scale reference and the original author’s suggestions [28], the four factors were labeled as follows: “Attitude towards the role of nurses in oral health care”, “Confidence in providing comprehensive oral health care”, “Confidence in undertaking basic oral health assessments”, and “Confidence in undertaking advanced oral health assessments”. The factor loadings for all 24 items were ≥ 0.4, and no multiple loadings were observed, no items were removed. The results of the EFA are presented in Table 2.

The initial CFA results showed the following model fit indices: χ²/df = 3.034, RMSEA = 0.093, NFI = 0.850, CFI = 0.893, TLI = 0.880 and IFI = 0.894. After model modification, these indices improved significantly, with the revised model yielding the following fit indices: χ²/df = 2.408, RMSEA = 0.077, NFI = 0.883, CFI = 0.928, TLI = 0.917, IFI = 0.928. These indices suggest that the revised model fits the data well (Fig. 1).

Reliability analysis

The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient for the Chinese version of the ACORN scale was 0.938. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the dimensions “Attitude towards the role of nurses in oral health care”, “Confidence in providing comprehensive oral health care”, “Confidence in performing basic oral health assessments”, and “Confidence in performing advanced oral health assessments” were 0.946, 0.927, 0.880, and 0.921, respectively. The test-retest reliability coefficient was 0.906, and the Spearman-Brown split-half reliability coefficient was 0.729. These results suggest that the scale demonstrates good internal consistency and stability (Table 3).

Discussion

Guided by the Guidelines for the Cross-Cultural Adaptation Process of Self-Reporting Scales [46], this study conducted a culturally sensitive adaptation of the ACORN scale to the Chinese context. The final version comprises four dimensions and 24 items, with a cumulative variance contribution of 72.626%. EFA and CFA demonstrated strong structural validity, and the scale showed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.938) and test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.903). The validated scale provides a concise and reliable tool for nursing educators to assess students’ attitudes and confidence in oral healthcare. It can be incorporated into curriculum evaluations, used to identify specific areas where students lack confidence or hold suboptimal attitudes, and thereby inform targeted teaching strategies. Furthermore, the scale can serve as an outcome measure for oral healthcare training programs, helping to assess their effectiveness and support ongoing curriculum refinement. In clinical practice, it may guide educators in preparing students for interdisciplinary collaboration in oral-systemic health management, ultimately enhancing patient care.

Validity refers to how accurately an instrument captures the construct it intends to measure, typically assessed through content and structural validity [47]. Content validity pertains to the degree to which a measurement tool comprehensively and accurately reflects the target construct. In this study, content validity was assessed by eight experts who evaluated the relevance and clarity of the scale items. The I-CVI and S-CVI for the Chinese version of the ACORN scale were 0.891 and 0.906, respectively, exceeding the recommended thresholds and indicating strong content validity. Furthermore, cultural modifications were implemented based on cross-cultural adaptation guidelines and preliminary surveys to ensure the scale’s relevance to Chinese nursing education. These adaptations improved item clarity, contextual relevance, and linguistic appropriateness, making it suitable for assessing the attitudes and confidence of Chinese nursing students in oral healthcare. As a result, the scale is better aligned with local educational norms and clinical expectations, supporting its practical use in both course and training environments.

Structural validity, which confirms the alignment between the theoretical constructs of the scale and its actual measurement outcomes, was evaluated using both EFA and CFA. Both analyses supported a stable four-factor structure with satisfactory model fit, confirming the stability of the scale’s design.

Reliability refers to the consistency and stability of results obtained from a measurement tool [48]. In this study, internal consistency, split-half reliability, and test-retest reliability were used to evaluate the scale’s reliability. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the overall scale and its sub-dimensions all exceeded 0.70, indicating high internal consistency. Additionally, both split-half and test-retest reliability met the recommended threshold of > 0.70, confirming the scale’s stability over time and across samples. These findings emphasize the scale’s dependability for repeated use in evaluating nursing students’ oral healthcare readiness and tracking their progress throughout training.

It is noteworthy that the original dimension “Confidence” was subdivided into three dimensions: “Confidence in providing comprehensive oral healthcare”, “Confidence in performing basic oral health assessments”, and “Confidence in performing advanced oral health assessments”; as confirmed by the original author, Professor Rojo [33]. This refined structure enables a more nuanced assessment of nursing students’ confidence across different levels of oral healthcare practice. It supports the identification of specific strengths and weaknesses, as well as subtle or progressive changes over time. The modification enhances the scale’s practical relevance, making it more adaptable to diverse educational contexts and responsive to evolving training needs. It can also guide the development of targeted educational strategies to strengthen specific competencies in students’ oral health training.

Attitudes and confidence play a crucial role in determining the quality of clinical oral healthcare. Nursing students’ oral healthcare abilities are often assessed based on academic performance in China [49], with oral healthcare generally not being a primary focus. Consequently, nursing students may neglect both theoretical and practical aspects of oral healthcare. Furthermore, nursing educators often face challenges such as heavy course loads [31], limited teaching resources [17], and a lack of experience in oral healthcare education. These challenges hinder the effective implementation of oral healthcare education, leading to limited knowledge, low confidence, and a lack of interest among nursing students, ultimately compromising their ability to deliver high-quality oral healthcare services to patients [25]. By addressing this gap, the ACORN scale enables educators to identify areas of neglect and implement targeted, competency-based interventions to enhance students’ clinical preparedness in oral health.

The first dimension, “Attitude towards the role of nurses in oral healthcare”, aligns closely with the original construct “Attitude in oral care in nursing”, as both reflect students’ perceptions of nurses’ roles in oral healthcare. This dimension serves as a foundation for nursing educators to design and implement strategies aimed at fostering more positive attitudes toward oral healthcare responsibilities. The original construct, “Confidence in providing comprehensive oral healthcare”, is further delineated through three specific dimensions: “Confidence in providing comprehensive oral healthcare”, “Confidence in undertaking basic oral health assessments”, and “Confidence in undertaking advanced oral health assessments”. While these dimensions disaggregate confidence into specific levels and tasks, they remain conceptually consistent with the original structure, collectively capture nursing students’ confidence in delivering oral healthcare. Together, they allow for a more detailed evaluation of students’ preparedness to address both routine and complex oral health issues.

In summary, following rigorous cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation, the Chinese version of the ACORN scale demonstrates strong cultural appropriateness, reliability, and validity. Its application offers valuable insights for nursing educators in curriculum development, training interventions, and clinical instruction, thereby supporting effective strategies to more fully integrate oral healthcare into nursing education and practice.

Limitations

Although this study has established the validity and reliability of the Chinese version of the ACORN scale, several limitations remain. First, the study sample was confined to nursing students from two universities in China, and the regional nature of the sample limits the generalizability and applicability of the results. Future research should consider broadening the sample to include nursing students from a wider array of regions and backgrounds to improve the external validity of the findings. Second, the study predominantly utilized a cross-sectional design, which does not allow for the assessment of the scale’s long-term stability or trends over time. Longitudinal studies in the future could further validate the effectiveness and stability of the Chinese version of the ACORN scale through extended follow-up and investigate the factors influencing nursing students’ attitudes toward and confidence in oral healthcare, with particular attention to the roles of curricular stage, clinical performance, and knowledge level. Finally, due to the lack of a reliable and validated criterion measure, this study did not assess the criterion validity of the scale. Future research should consider developing or introducing rigorously validated instruments to further examine the criterion validity and enhance the practical utility of the scale.

Conclusions

The Chinese version of the ACORN scale, as a valid and reliable measurement tool, effectively assesses nursing students’ attitudes and confidence towards oral healthcare. It offers practical value for both research and education by enabling nursing educators to identify learning needs and improve oral health training programs. Its integration into the nursing curriculum can help identify educational gaps, inform targeted training strategies, and reinforce oral healthcare competencies. As a result, enabling them to deliver comprehensive, high-quality oral care in clinical settings, address patients’ oral health problems, and improve related health outcomes.
