Retrospective Analysis of 1093 Non-Syndromic Supernumerary Teeth From 782 Patients in Northwest China: Clinical, Radiographic and Epidemiological Insights
Authors: Xin Xue, Haozhen Wang, Tianyu Zhang, Wenlong Li, Xinwei Li, Shukai Sun, Lian Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and AimsThis study investigated the epidemiological, clinical and radiologic characteristics of non-syndromic supernumerary teeth (ST) using cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) and provided references for the clinical management of ST.MethodsClinical and CBCT image data from 782 patients (1093 ST) were collected. The age, sex and chief complaint of the patients, as well as the number, location, morphology, orientation, crown position, eruption status and complications of the ST, were recorded for analysis.ResultsThe male-to-female ratio was 2.30:1. A single supernumerary tooth was observed in most cases (64.2%) and located in the maxillary central incisor region (89.3%). Most ST were conical, inverted, unerupted, palatally positioned and had complete root development. A total of 57.82% of the ST caused complications. About 53.45% of patients discovered ST by taking X-ray. Chief complaints varied significantly by age group (P < .05). Morphology, location, orientation, crown position and root development varied significantly by eruption status and complication type (P < .05). Location, morphology, orientation, crown position and eruption status varied significantly by region (P < .05). Significant differences in complications were noted among the different age groups (P < .05).ConclusionThis study provides a reference for the clinical management of non-syndromic ST in children, emphasising the importance of early screening, precise diagnosis (particularly with CBCT) and personalised treatment. Clinicians should adopt comprehensive, stratified intervention strategies to optimise outcomes and reduce healthcare costs.Clinical SignificanceOur findings provide a comprehensive understanding of the characteristics of ST in children, offering new insights for clinical management.Key words: Supernumerary teeth, CBCT, Retrospective study, Treatment, Prevention

Introduction

Supernumerary teeth (ST) are a common developmental anomaly of tooth number. The normal number of teeth is 20 in the primary dentition and 28 to 32 in the permanent dentition. Any teeth or odontogenic structures beyond this range are classified as ST.1 Previous studies have reported that the prevalence of ST in permanent dentition ranges from 0.1% to 4%, whereas the prevalence in primary dentition is between 0.2% and 0.8%.2,3 The incidence is higher in males than in females, with a sex ratio ranging from 1.18:1 to 4.5:1.4 ST can occur in any region of the jaw, although they are most commonly found in the maxillary incisor region.5 Most ST are isolated cases. However, some may be familial inherited, and some may be syndrome-associated events.6,7 Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain the aetiology and development of ST, including tooth bud dichotomy, dental lamina hyperactivity, atavism and environmental and genetic factors. However, the exact aetiology and underlying mechanisms of ST remain unclear. ST can lead to various complications, such as eruption failure, root resorption, rotation, impaction and malposition of adjacent teeth, as well as cyst formation.8,9 Additionally, the location, morphology and orientation of ST vary considerably, making accurate assessment of their growth characteristics and potential associated complications essential.10

In clinical practice, the localisation of ST is traditionally performed using apical radiographs and panoramic radiographs (PRs).11 However, owing to the limitations of two-dimensional imaging, these examinations cannot accurately determine the morphology, three-dimensional position and spatial relationship of ST relative to those of adjacent tissues. The advent of cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) has significantly advanced the diagnosis and treatment of oral diseases, particularly in oral imaging diagnostics. CBCT is now internationally recognised as a reliable method for the precise localisation of ST. It facilitates the visualisation of ST in three dimensions and provides their relationships with adjacent anatomical structures.12 CBCT offers advantages, including a relatively low radiation dose, rapid image acquisition and high spatial resolution, aiding clinicians in formulating accurate preoperative diagnoses and surgical plans.13

The aim of this retrospective study was to collect and analyse the number, location, morphology, orientation, eruption status, associated complications and other characteristics of ST in children from northwest China using CBCT. Furthermore, the correlations between the location, eruption status, associated complications and these characteristics were statistically analysed. The clinical data collected serve as a reference for the diagnosis, treatment and prevention of ST.

Materials and methods

Study participants

In this study, patients (aged ≤ 14 years) who underwent CBCT examinations at the Department of Pediatric Dentistry, Stomatological Hospital of the Fourth Military Medical University between January 2023 and December 2023 were screened retrospectively. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki on research involving human subjects and was approved by the Ethics and Research Committee of Stomatological Hospital of the Fourth Military Medical University (Ethics Committee Reference Number: KQ-YJ-2024-271).

Evaluation criteria

In 2023, a total of 4673 patients underwent CBCT examination in the Department of Pediatric Dentistry, Stomatological Hospital of the Fourth Military Medical University because of dental trauma, ST, impacted teeth and other oral diseases, 798 of whom were diagnosed with non-syndromic ST. After excluding patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria, 782 patients (1093 ST) were ultimately included in this study. Clinical and CBCT imaging data, including patient age, sex and ST number, location, orientation, morphology, crown position, eruption status, associated complications and chief complaints, were collected. The correlations between the location, eruption status, associated complications and these characteristics were further statistically analysed.

To evaluate the imaging characteristics of the ST, the CBCT images were independently assessed by two experienced dentists, with agreement reached between them. In cases of disagreement, a third dentist was consulted to provide a final diagnosis. CBCT data and 3D image reconstructions were used to record the three-dimensional characteristics of the ST, including their location, morphology, orientation, crown position and associated complications.

All the CBCT images were obtained using a CBCT scanner (3D Accuitomo, Morita Mfg. Corp. Japan) at 85 kV and 5.5 mA. The relevant CBCT data in DICOM format for each patient were collected and imported into (miPlatform) (version 3.0) for 3D image reconstruction. The ST characteristics are detailed as follows:

Statistical analysis

All the relevant clinical and radiographic data of the patients were collected. Associations between categorical covariates were assessed using chi-square (χ²) tests or Fisher’s exact tests as indicated. The inter-observer variability and reproducibility of these radiographic measurements were assessed with Cohen’s kappa values. All tests were two-sided, and P values less than .05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. All the statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 software.

Results

1.General information of 782 patients with 1093 STAmong the 782 patients, there were more males (69.69%) than females (30.31%), yielding a male-to-female ratio of 2.30:1. ST occurrence peaked in the 7- to 9-year age group (51.15%). Patients most commonly had one supernumerary tooth (64.19%), followed by two ST (33.25%); only 2.56% had more than two ST. Maxillary ST (93.96%) significantly outnumbered mandibular ST (6.04%) (Table 1). Incidentally finding ST during a dental X-ray examination was reported by 53.45% of patients as their chief complaint. Other complaints included adjacent tooth malposition (21.87%), eruption of ST/abnormal teeth (15.22%), delayed permanent tooth eruption (8.70%), local mucosal discomfort (0.64%) and intermittent epistaxis (0.13%) (Table 2). Complaints varied significantly by age group (P < .05) (Table 3). Incidental radiographic discovery remained most common overall. From age 4 years onwards, active seeking of treatment due to ST eruption increased. Starting at approximately age 6 years, complaints related to ST-induced complications became more frequent.

2.Characteristics of the 1093 STAmong the 1093 ST, 89.30% were located in the maxillary central incisor region. The detailed distribution of ST in the maxillary and mandibular dental arches is shown in Figure 6. In terms of morphology, conical ST were most common (73.65%), followed by supplemental teeth, tuberculate teeth and odontoma. The majority of ST (82.16%) were located on the palatal/lingual side, followed by the middle of the dental arch and the buccal/labial side. In terms of the orientation of the ST, inverted orientation was the most common type (49.68%), followed by normal, transverse, horizontal, inclined and undefinable orientations. Regarding eruption status, 80.15% of ST were unerupted, whereas 19.85% had erupted. In total, 47.58% of the ST roots were fully developed, and 36.51% of the ST roots had developed 2/3. ST can lead to a variety of complications, such as malposition (57.12%), invasion of adjacent anatomical structures (18.20%), impaction of adjacent teeth (16.93%), root resorption of adjacent teeth (5.22%) and dentigerous cysts (2.53%) (Table 4).

3.Analysis of the eruption status characteristics of STThere were no statistically significant differences in eruption status between males and females. However, significant differences in morphology, location, orientation, crown position, root development and associated complications were detected (P < .05) (Table 5). Most erupted ST were located in the incisor region in a normal orientation. Additionally, 96.78% of the erupted ST had roots that were at least 2/3 developed or fully developed. In contrast, most of the unerupted ST were located in the incisor region in an inverted orientation, and root development varied. Associated complications of the erupted ST primarily included malposition of adjacent teeth. In contrast, unerupted ST often cause impaction of adjacent permanent teeth, torsion of the tooth germ, the formation of dentigerous cysts, or the invasion of adjacent anatomical structures.

4.Analysis of the associated complication characteristics of STTo analyse the potential associations between these associated complications and diverse characteristics of ST, statistical analyses were performed, and the results are presented in Table 6. The results demonstrated that the occurrence of complications was significantly correlated with the characteristics of the ST. For instance, malposition of adjacent teeth was most commonly associated with conical ST in the incisor region. These teeth typically exhibited a normal or inverted orientation. Cysts were most commonly associated with conical ST in the incisor and premolar regions, with normal or inverted orientations. These ST often had poorly developed roots (75% were undeveloped or had developed only 1/3).

Discussion

ST are relatively common developmental anomalies in terms of tooth number. The aetiology of ST is still uncertain, though familial aggregation cases substantiate the pivotal role of genetic factors. The inheritance patterns of syndromic and non-syndromic ST differ significantly. Syndromic ST is frequently associated with specific monogenic disorders, exhibiting clear Mendelian inheritance patterns and high penetrance. Cleidocranial dysplasia represents the most classic example, caused by mutations in the RUNX2 gene and inherited in an autosomal dominant manner.14,15 Non-syndromic ST demonstrates greater genetic heterogeneity. Genome-wide association studies suggest involvement of polygenic additive effects. Whole-exome sequencing in non-syndromic ST patients has identified novel candidate genes, including FER1L6, suggesting the complexity of the underlying genetic mechanisms.16 Takahashi et al.17 performed whole-exome sequencing analysis on four Japanese families with non-syndromic ST. The sequencing results did not identify any common gene variants in all four families, but three families all had variants in SCO-spondin. Genetic analyses indicate that familial cases often follow an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern with incomplete penetrance. The higher incidence in males suggests possibility of sex-linked heredity.18 Cassia et al.19 suggested the possibility of autosomal recessive transmission in a large Lebanese family having consanguineous marriages.

Although many studies have reported the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of ST, some discrepancies in the findings remain. A significant sex difference in the occurrence of ST is noted as most patients are male, and the male-to-female ratio is approximately 1.18:1 to 4.5:1.20 A single supernumerary tooth represents the most common presentation, accounting for approximately 63.0% to 77.4% of cases, whereas two ST are noted in 18.4% to 30.8% of patients.21 Previous studies have indicated that the incidence of ST varies across regions and ethnicities, with the majority being detected during mixed dentition.22, 23, 24 In this study, 85.55% of patients between the ages of 6 and 12 years visited the hospital. Abnormalities in mixed dentition often prompt parents to seek medical advice, leading to early detection and intervention.

With respect to the location of the ST, previous studies have reported that the maxillary incisor region is the most common site.25 In terms of morphology, previous studies have reported that the most common morphology of ST is conical.20 In our study, we found that ST in the incisor and canine regions were mostly conical, whereas those in the premolar and molar regions were more often tuberculate or supplemental. This pattern suggests that the ST morphology tends to resemble that of adjacent teeth.26 The tooth germ dichotomy theory and the dental lamina function theory suggest that ST form as a result of the division or overproliferation of the developing tooth germ, possibly because of trauma or other developmental disturbances.27,28

With respect to the development of roots, previous research has indicated that unerupted ST often tend to migrate towards the crown as their roots develop.29 The greater the root development stage of the ST is, the more space it needs to occupy in the jaw, and the more likely it is to cause complications to adjacent permanent teeth or anatomical structures.

Previous studies have shown that ST are mostly in an unerupted state. Most of these unerupted teeth were inverted, with conical or tuberculate morphology and varying stages of root development. On the other hand, erupted ST were predominantly conical in morphology, located in the incisor region and had a normal orientation.20

In terms of associated complications, previous studies have shown that regardless of eruption, ST can cause complications to adjacent teeth or anatomical structures.30 Moreover, studies have shown that increasing age and abnormal eruption orientations are associated with a higher risk of complications and that unerupted ST may continue to move towards the crown over time.31 As the root develops, the ST occupy increasing space within the jawbone. Given the relatively limited space in the anterior and premolar regions, ST in these locations are more prone to applying pressure on the roots of adjacent teeth, potentially resulting in root resorption. Dentigerous cysts associated with ST are relatively uncommon.32 Research has demonstrated that dentigerous cysts develop in 6% of ST.33 Approximately 90% of dentigerous cysts from ST develop around mesiodens in the anterior maxilla.34 These findings can be explained by the significantly greater prevalence of ST in the maxillary anterior region than in other regions.35 As the teeth develop, the risk of odontogenic cyst formation increases.29

Currently, the indications and timing for the extraction of ST remain controversial. Some scholars believe that the extraction of ST before the age of 6 years can effectively prevent or reduce the occurrence of complications. Other scholars believe that the operation during tooth extraction may damage the adjacent developing permanent tooth germ and suggest that the extraction should be delayed until the root development of the adjacent permanent tooth is completed.36, 37, 38 A previous study revealed that tooth crowding was the most common complication among 283 children with ST in southern China.4 In the Chinese adult dental cohort, the most common complications of 1149 patients with ST were root resorption and impaction of adjacent teeth.30 Studies have shown that in the 7- to 8-year-old age group, the primary complication of ST is an excessive gap between the central incisors, followed by malposition of adjacent teeth. In the 9- to 10-year-old age group, malposition of adjacent teeth were the most common complications. In the 11- to 12-year-old age group, delayed eruption or impaction of adjacent teeth becomes more frequent.5 These findings indicate that irreversible complications are more likely to occur, if ST are not extracted timely.

Unerupted ST should be extracted as early as possible upon discovery if they pose a potential risk of adverse complications to adjacent teeth. The optimal age for extraction is before 6 to 7 years, as removal at a later age is correlated with a higher incidence of complications. Shih et al. reported that most patients who experienced complications associated with ST underwent extraction after age 9. In contrast, extraction before age 5 or prior to crown formation of adjacent teeth significantly reduces the subsequent need for orthodontic treatment or secondary surgery.39 Early extraction offers several advantages. First, the relatively porous bone structure in children allows leveraging the malleable nature of the jawbone during ST extraction. Second, during mixed dentition, both the jawbone and the tooth germ undergo rapid growth and development. Permanent teeth exhibit a strong tendency for mesial and occlusal movement. Consequently, removing ST eliminates obstruction to adjacent teeth, enabling spontaneous correction of malocclusion in a significant proportion of cases.40 However, extraction may be deferred under the following circumstances: First, if ST are located near the apex of a developing permanent tooth, surgical removal inevitably involves contact with the permanent tooth root. If no complications are present, conservative monitoring with periodic radiographic follow-up is advised. Second, extraction may be postponed if ST are at an early developmental stage, as incomplete removal of the dental follicle is associated with a risk of recurrence and potential formation of a new ST.41 Third, unerupted ST exhibiting a normal orientation and causing no adverse complications may be conservatively managed with delayed extraction. Extraction following partial eruption can reduce intraoperative bone removal and minimise surgical trauma.

Recent advances in clinical technology have greatly facilitated the treatment of ST. The use of CBCT allows for accurate localisation of ST. The application of sedation or general anaesthesia for paediatric dental treatment has made the treatment process more comfortable. The application of surgical robots has also increased the precision of ST extraction and intraoperative bone removal, significantly reducing the difficulty and risks of extraction. On the basis of the aforementioned technological advancements, we propose the following recommendation: ST confirmed to cause adverse complications should be extracted as early as possible. For ST that have not yet caused adverse complications, careful assessment is needed to determine the potential risks during extraction, such as injury to adjacent nerves and vessels or perforation of anatomical structures. Damage to the dental follicles of permanent tooth roots may result in ankylosis. In all cases, the risk-benefit ratio must be carefully considered.42 The management of a supernumerary tooth should form part of a comprehensive treatment plan and should not be considered in isolation.

The majority of patients in each age group were passively seeking medical attention after ST were incidentally detected during X-ray examination. After the age of 6 years, with the emergence of complications caused by ST, medical treatment was sought. However, at this time, not only is it necessary to extract ST, but further treatment may also be required for different complications, which greatly increase the cost of treatment. Therefore, we propose a three-level prevention strategy for ST:

Primary prevention: Oral health promotion. Conduct public education on ST, highlighting oral manifestations, such as excessive anterior spacing, delayed permanent tooth eruption, or tooth malposition, which may indicate ST. Educating parents about ST through science popularisation activities facilitates early detection and treatment, significantly reducing complication risks.

Secondary prevention: Early diagnosis and treatment. Panoramic radiographs during the early mixed dentition stage are recommended to screen for ST, congenital missing teeth, ectopic teeth and other oral diseases, enabling early detection and timely extraction to minimise complications.

Tertiary Prevention: Management of complications. Complications arising from untreated ST, such as impaction, malposition, cysts and root resorption, should be addressed. Multidisciplinary approaches should be adopted to prevent further issues and maintain oral and overall health.

Conclusion

This retrospective study investigated the clinical and radiographic characteristics of ST in children aged 0 to 14 years in northwest China. The findings indicated a predominance of single ST with a higher prevalence among males. ST occurred most frequently in the maxillary central incisor region, which typically exhibited a conical morphology, normal or inverted orientation and a palatal position. The majority were unerupted, which may lead to various complications. These findings provide a reference for the clinical management of non-syndromic ST in children, emphasising the importance of early screening, precise diagnosis (particularly with CBCT) and personalised treatment. Clinicians should adopt comprehensive, stratified intervention strategies to optimise outcomes and reduce healthcare costs. Given the inherent limitations of the cross-sectional research design, future longitudinal studies are needed to scientifically and comprehensively investigate the optimal timing for ST extraction and refine clinical management protocols.
