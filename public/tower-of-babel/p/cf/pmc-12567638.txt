Mitochondrial haplogroups, but not heteroplasmy, are associated with midlife cognitive function
Authors: Nien Yeh Daphne Yu, Shea J Andrews, Lifang Hou, Myriam Fornage, Kristine Yaffe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONMitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) variation may influence cognitive performance, but prior findings are inconsistent.METHODSWe analyzed data from 2308 participants in the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) study, assessing heteroplasmy (mtHz) burden and haplogroups (mtHg) from blood‐derived mtDNA at three timepoints and cognitive function across multiple domains. INTRODUCTION

Mitochondrial dysfunction is recognized as one of the hallmarks of aging, playing a crucial role in the decline of cellular health and contributing to age‐related diseases. 1 As we age, mitochondrial function deteriorates, resulting in energy deficits and oxidative stress, which further worsen other hallmarks of aging, such as genomic instability, cellular senescence, and stem cell exhaustion, contributing to a cascade of cellular decline. 2 The brain's high energy demand makes it especially vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction, and may thereby contribute to pathophysiological changes, such as abnormal protein aggregates, neuroinflammation, and neuronal death, that promote neurodegeneration and accelerated cognitive aging. 3 , 4 Understanding the role of mitochondrial dysfunction in cognitive aging is crucial, as it underlies key biological processes that drive neurodegeneration and cognitive aging. By clarifying these pathways, we can potentially identify targets for intervention to promote healthy brain aging and prevent age‐related neurodegenerative diseases.

Mitochondria contain multiple copies of their own circular genome (mtDNA), separate from the nuclear genome, that encode components essential for cellular energy production. Genetic variation on the mitochondrial genome is often defined as mitochondrial haplogroups (mtHg), which are identified by sets of mtDNA variants inherited from a common ancestor. Analyses of mtHg have shown that mtHg H and V are associated with a higher risk of Alzheimer's disease (AD), while mtHg J and T are associated lower risk of Parkinson's disease (PD). 5 , 6 , 7 Reports of other mtHgs have not been consistent, with associations driven by specific subgroups depending on the study population. 7 In addition to these inherited variants, mtDNA can harbor heteroplasmic mutations, a condition where two or more mtDNA alleles coexist within a cell or tissue. The functional consequences of mtHz depend on its level relative to the biochemical threshold. When this threshold is exceeded, mtHz are more likely to exert deleterious effects. 8 With age, mtDNA mutations accumulate, increasing both the heteroplasmic load and the number of heteroplasmic sites, which can push silent mutations past the pathogenic threshold. 9 This growing mutational burden may impair mitochondrial function and contribute to cognitive aging.

Despite growing evidence linking mtDNA variation and cognitive aging, significant research gaps remain. Attempts to characterize the relationship between heteroplasmy and cognitive function have found inconclusive results. 10 , 11 , 12 Other studies on mtHg and cognitive outcomes were mostly conducted in older adults of European descent, 6 , 7 , 13 leaving a critical gap in understanding how these factors may influence cognitive aging in other ancestry groups. Moreover, prior research predominantly examined older, clinically diagnosed AD populations, despite emerging evidence of the importance of risk factors and cognitive trajectories in midlife linked to AD pathogenesis. 14 , 15 To address these gaps, there is a need for longitudinal studies of diverse populations to investigate differences in cognitive trajectories between mtHg and heteroplasmy earlier in life.

Our study addresses these gaps by examining the association between blood‐derived mtDNA variation and cognitive function in a diverse midlife cohort. Our goal was to determine prospective associations between heteroplasmy levels and cognitive performance and to examine haplogroup‐specific cognitive trajectories using data from the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) study. Determining the relation between mtDNA variation and cognition at midlife may improve our understanding of early biological processes underlying cognitive aging.

Our analysis included the CARDIA visits in 2005‐2006 (our study baseline), 2010–2011 (5 years from baseline), 2015–2016 (10 years from baseline), and 2020–2022 (15 years from baseline). To maximize sample size, we analyzed data from 2308 participants with at least one cognitive test in 2015–2016 and at least one mtDNA sequencing from 2005 to 2016 (Figure 1c). Among these, 2183 participants with heteroplasmy count from 2010–2011 were included for a 5‐year prospective analysis of heteroplasmy burden and cognitive function (Figure 1b). A subset of 1308 participants with cognitive test data available from 2010 to 2022 was used for the longitudinal analysis (Figure 1c).

Cognitive domains

Interviewers administered a battery of cognitive tests, including the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST; processing speed), Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT; verbal memory), Stroop Test (executive function), Letter Fluency Test (verbal fluency), Category Fluency Test (semantic fluency), and Montreal Cognitive Assessment. All scores were z‐standardized, with higher scores indicative of better performance (inverse standardized scores were used for the Stroop Test). We computed a global cognition score by averaging the z‐standardized test scores of all six tests.

DSST, RAVLT, and Stroop Test were available from Years 5, 10, and 15 from baseline. In the longitudinal analysis, test scores were standardized to the mean and standard deviation of the first cognitive test, allowing changes to be expressed in standard deviation (SD) units relative to the first cognitive test. We computed a composite cognitive score as a proxy for global cognition by averaging z‐standardized scores of the three tests.

mtDNA sequencing, variant calling, haplogroup classification, sample QC, and heteroplasmy burden

Whole blood samples were collected from 2503 subjects in 2005–2006 (n = 1557), 2010–2011 (n = 2423), and 2015–2016 (n = 2418). gDNA was extracted from these samples, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) was subsequently amplified and sequenced. Sequencing was performed by the Genomics, Epigenomics, and Sequencing Core at the University of Cincinnati on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer. Whole mtDNA genome was amplified following Illumina (San Diego, CA, USA) protocol (Document #15037958 v01) with some modifications: briefly, long‐range polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted using 2 ng human gDNA as input for each 50 µL reaction, using TaKaRa LA Taq and two sets of primers (MTLF1: AAAGCACATACCAAGGCCAC [9397 – 9416]; MTLR1: TTGGCTCTCCTTGCAAAGTT [1893 – 1874]; MTLF2: TATCCGCCATCCCATACATT [15195 – 15214]; MTLR2: AATGTTGAGCCGTAGATGCC [9796 – 9777]). Target amplicon of sizes 9065 and 11170 bp were selected using gel electrophoresis and cleaned using Wizard SV Gel and PCR Clean‐Up kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). 100 ng DNA containing roughly equal amounts of the two PCR products was used as input for library preparation using NEBNext Ultra II FS DNA Library Prep kit (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). Dual‐indexed libraries were proportionally pooled and sequenced using NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina), using paired‐end 2×150 bp reads, to generate a coverage of at least ∼10,000×. Sequencing reads from FASTQ files were aligned to the revised Cambridge Reference Sequence (rRCS) of human mtDNA and compressed to binary BAM format for variant calling.

The mtDNA‐Server 2 17 pipeline was used for calling variants from BAM files via a Nextflow pipeline. The mtDNA‐Server 2 pipeline contains several steps. First, samples pass through the input validation and quality control. A total of 255 of these were excluded from variant calling (mean coverage percentage < 50, mean depth < 50, mapping quality < 20). Variants were detected using fusion mode, which increases detection accuracy by combining indels detected by GATK Mutect2 and SNVs detected by mutserve2. 17 Second, haplocheck was used to assess sample contamination by classifying haplogroup according to the major and minor profiles for each sample identified by heteroplasmic positions. 17 , 18 Finally, mtDNA‐Server 2 classifies sample haplogroups according to PhyloTree Build 17 through Haplogrep3. 19

After variant calling, the following variant‐ and sample‐level quality control (QC) steps were performed. We removed indel variants. The average read depth was ∼35000×, and we excluded variants with coverage lower than 5250. We excluded variants that were absent in all three reference databases, UK Biobank, gnomAD, or Helix Mitochondrial Database, and additionally removed artifact‐prone sites (300−315, 513−526, 3106−3107, 16181−16194) identified in these databases. 20 , 21 , 22 We additionally removed variants that did not receive a passing filter in the gnomAD dataset, even if they are present in other datasets. In 6365 samples that underwent variant calling, we removed 465 samples with contamination score > 0.03, heteroplasmy count > 2.5 SD, haplogroup mismatch, or were duplicates of lower quality. Haplogroup mismatch was defined as any participant whose mtHg assignments differed across visits. For any individual, if two of three mtHg assignments were identical, we excluded the visit that had a different mtHg assignment. If mtHg assignment from all visits were different, the participant was excluded from the analysis. For example, if a participant had mtHg H at the first visit, mtHg H at the second visit, and mtHg A at the third visit, only samples from the first and second visit will be included for this participant, the sample from the third visit would be excluded. If a participant had only two visits with mtHg L at the first visit and mtHg H at the second visit, all samples from this participant would be excluded from the analysis. Among 1408 participants who had mtDNA sequencing data for all three visits, only seven had different mtHg at all visits. A flowchart summarizing the sample‐level QC steps is presented in Figure S1. Following quality control, mtDNA sequencing generated heteroplasmy and haplogroup data for 2465 participants (1244 participants at baseline, 2335 at 5 years post‐baseline, and 2321 at 10 years post‐baseline).

We then collapsed mtHgs with low sample sizes into super‐haplogroups (super‐mtHgs) or macro‐haplogroups (macro‐mtHgs): mtHgs B (n = 14), F (n = 2), HV (n = 14), R (n = 9), and V (n = 36) into super‐haplogroup R; A (n = 15), I (n = 37), N (n = 8), W (n = 34), and X (n = 26) into super‐mtHg N; and C (n = 4), D (n = 6), G (n = 2), and M (n = 11) into macro‐mtHg M.

Heteroplasmies were defined as variants with an allele frequency between 0.03 and 0.97. Heteroplasmy count was determined by the number of sites within a sample where the allele frequency falls within this range. We further quantified heteroplasmy burden using a modified Mitochondrial Local Constraint Score Sum 23 , 24 (mMSS), adjusted for homoplasmy count from gnomAD, 20 offering a more biologically meaningful measure of heteroplasmy pathogenicity compared to a simple count of heteroplasmic sites.

Covariates

Covariates were from baseline visit unless otherwise noted. Demographic characteristics (age, sex, race, and years of education) were obtained from self‐report and interviewer‐administered questionnaires. Physical activity was measured using the total exercise intensity score and a summary score of the exercise duration, frequency, and intensity listed in the Physical Activity questionnaire. 25 Smoking status was dichotomized into current smokers or not. Alcohol use (mean milliliters of alcohol consumed per day) was assessed by self‐report. body mass index (BMI) was calculated using height measured in meters and weight measured in kilograms, using the formula: BMI = weight (kg)/[height (m)]2. Hypertension was defined as having systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or on hypertensive medications, and diabetes was defined as having fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL or on diabetic medications but not pregnant. Depression (score ≥ 16) was assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale. 26 Apolipoprotein E (APOE) was determined from plasma samples taken 13 years before baseline 27 and coded as positive or negative for the APOE ε4 allele (ε2/ε4, ε3/ε4, ε4/ε4). Missing age was computed with linear imputation, and missing education was imputed with the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method. Other missing lifestyle and comorbidity variables were imputed using missForest, 28 a multiple imputation method based on random forest algorithms

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics between those included and excluded from longitudinal analysis were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables, Fisher's exact test with simulated p‐value (based on 2000 replicates) for mtHgs, and χ 2 tests for other categorical variables.

We implemented linear regression models to conduct a 5‐year prospective analysis of the association between heteroplasmy and cognitive function, adjusting for education, race, age, and sex. We also used linear regression models to assess the cross‐sectional associations between mitochondrial haplogroups with z‐standardized cognitive performance, adjusting for education, race, age, and sex. Effect sizes were reported as beta coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Longitudinal analysis was conducted using linear mixed models, implemented with the lme4 package 29 (version 1.1−35.5). In longitudinal analysis, age was centered at the baseline mean and scaled by decade, so the beta coefficients represent the effects of a 10‐year change in age. In mtHg analyses, we excluded individuals with macro‐mtHg M due to low sample size (Table 1).

To account for multiple hypothesis testing across different cognitive domains, we applied a false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment, using a threshold of FDR q = 0.05 to determine statistical significance.

Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of our findings. We adjusted for the social determinants of health (SDOH) index, 30 a summary score based on the five SDOH domains described in Healthy People 2030 initiative, 31 including economic stability, education access and quality, health care access and quality, neighborhood and built environment, and social and community context. Associations between additional covariates and outcomes, mtHg or mtHz, were assessed using appropriate statistical tests. For mtHg, Fisher's exact test with simulated p‐values (2000 replicates) was used for categorical variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for continuous variables. For mMSS, the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was applied to categorical variables, and the Spearman correlations to continuous variables. Comparisons between cognitive test scores and continuous variables were assessed using Spearman correlations, while group comparisons were evaluated using Wilcoxon rank‐sum test or Student's t‐test, depending on test score distributions. Covariates significantly associated with predictors (mMSS or mtHg) and outcomes were included as secondary variables in the sensitivity analyses (Table S1). Additional sensitivity analyses incorporated the presence of APOE ε4, lifestyle factors (alcohol consumption, smoking status, exercise score), and comorbidities (depression, diabetes, hypertension, BMI), excluding participants without APOE genotype information (n = 255).

Finally, we assessed model stability by excluding influential points identified through diagnostic tests for outliers. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1. All code used for analysis is available on GitHub.

RESULTS

Baseline demographics

Of the 2465 CARDIA participants with mtDNA sequencing results that passed QC, 2308 had cognitive tests 10 years after baseline. The mean age at baseline was 45.3 (SD: 3.5), and 58% were female (Table 1). The most common haplogroup in this cohort was mtHg H, which accounted for 25% of participants, followed by mtHg L3 at 17% and L2 at 13% (Table 1). In White participants, the most common mtHg was mtHg H (42%), while in Black participants, mtHg L3 (38%) was the most common.

Heteroplasmy burden data were available in 2183 participants 5 years after baseline. For those included in the cross‐sectional analysis, the mean heteroplasmy count at 5 years from baseline was 0.04 ± 0.14, and the mean mMSS was 0.54 ± 0.76 (Table 1). There was no significant difference in heteroplasmy count and mMSS between participants included and excluded from longitudinal analysis (Table 1).

Longitudinal cognitive performance

Cognitive performance across the three visits showed different changes across different cognitive domains. Among the tests available at all three timepoints, mean DSST scores declined over three visits, indicating a gradual decrease in processing speed; and mean Stroop Test score showed a gradual increase over time, reflecting a decrease in executive function. In contrast, mean RAVLT showed a slight increase in performance. Missing data increased over time, particularly at Year 35 during Covid‐19 pandemic (Table S2). In linear mixed‐effects models with visit year as the sole predictor and random intercepts and slopes for participant ID, DSST scores, and Stroop test showed a significant declining trend over time (both p = < 0.001), whereas the change in RAVLT scores was not significant (p = 0.4). These trends provide context for examining the associations between mtDNA variation and cognitive change over time.

Cross‐sectional analysis of haplogroup effects on cognitive function

We found that mtHgs from the macro‐haplogroup L were associated with poorer cognitive performance in cross‐sectional analysis across multiple domains compared to reference mtHg H (Figure 2, Table S3). Participants with mtHg L1, L2, and L3 showed lower processing speed (L1: −0.36 [−0.59, −0.13]; L2: −0.34 [−0.56, −0.12]). Those with mtHg L0, L1, and L3 were associated with lower executive function (L0: −0.32 [−0.67, 0.02], L1: −0.24 [−0.49, 0.01], L3: −0.29 [−0.53, −0.06]). Participants with mtHg L1 and L2 were associated with poorer verbal fluency (L1: −0.28 [−0.53, −0.03], L2: −0.28 [−0.52, −0.04]). Participants with mtHg L1 and L3 had poorer semantic fluency (L1: −0.29 [−0.53, −0.05], L3: −0.23 [−0.46, −0.01]). Lower global cognition was observed for L0, L1, L2, and L3 (L0: −0.24 [−0.44, −0.04, L1: −0.29 [−0.44, −0.14], L2: −0.23 [−0.37, −0.09], L3: −0.23 [−0.44, −0.01]). No association was found with verbal memory. Apart from macro−mtHg L, only mtHg N showed an association with processing speed (0.18 [0.01, 0.35]). After adjusting for multiple testing, mtHg L1 and L2 remained significantly associated with lower processing speed, and mtHg L1, L2, and L3 remained significantly associated with lower global cognition.

Longitudinal analysis of haplogroup effects on cognitive function

To examine the effects of haplogroups over time, we conducted a longitudinal analysis. We found that longitudinal analysis results were consistent with cross‐sectional findings, showing significant associations between macro‐haplogroup L and worse cognitive performance compared to the reference mtHg H over time (Figure 3, Table S4). mtHgs L0, L1, L2, and L3 were associated with lower baseline processing speed (L0: −0.46 [−0.88, −0.03], L1: −0.47 [−0.77, −0.17], L2: −0.42 [−0.7, −0.13], L3: −0.39 [−0.67, −0.11]). Baseline executive function was worse among individuals with mtHg L0, L1, and L3 (L0: −0.45 [−0.89, −0.01], L1: −0.33 [−0.64, −0.03], L3: −0.31 [−0.60, −0.02]), consistent with cross‐sectional analysis. Individuals with mtHg L0, L1, and L3 showed worse baseline composite score (L0: −0.39 [−0.68, −0.09], L1: −0.3 [−0.51, −0.09], L3: −0.27 [−0.47, −0.07]). However, among all baseline associations, only the association between L1 and processing speed was significant after adjusting for multiple testing.

While no association was observed between mtHgs and verbal memory in the cross‐sectional analysis, longitudinal analysis revealed that mtHgs L2 and L3 were associated with a faster rate of decline in verbal memory (L2: −0.22 [−0.35, −0.08], L3: −0.19 [−0.31, −0.07]). Additionally, mtHg L3 was associated with a faster rate of decline in executive function over time (−0.16 [−0.31, −0.01]). mtHg L2 and L3 also experienced a faster rate of decline in composite score (L2: −0.10 [−0.19, −0.01], L3: −0.14 [−0.22, −0.06]). Significant associations also persisted for all associations to the rate of decline in verbal memory and mtHg L3 to composite cognitive score after adjusting for multiple testing.

Sensitivity analysis

Macro‐mtHg L is predominantly of African descent, raising the possibility that the observed associations may be confounded by SDOH or risk factors that differ among mtHgs. To explore this, we conducted sensitivity analyses, additionally adjusting for additional covariates to assess potential confounding by SDOH or APOE ε4, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities (Figures 2 and 3, Tables S3–S4). In cross‐sectional analysis, most macro‐mtHg L were associated with worse executive function, verbal fluency, semantic fluency, and global cognition after adjusting for SDOH. However, under SDOH adjustment, the associations between processing speed and mtHgs L3 and N were no longer significant.

After controlling for APOE ε4, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities, associations between mtHg L3 and processing speed were non‐significant, but mtHg N remained significant (0.18 [0.01, 0.36]). For executive function, only the association with mtHg L3 remained significant (−0.29 [−0.53, −0.06]); associations with mtHg L0 and L1 were attenuated. Associations to verbal fluency remained significant (L1, L2), but all associations to semantic fluency (L1, L3) were attenuated. Associations with global cognition remained statistically significant in mtHg L1, and significant in L2 and L3, but attenuated in mtHg L0.

In sensitivity analysis for the longitudinal analysis, the majority of mtHg associations remained significant. Associations with baseline processing speed remained significant for mtHg L1, L2, and L3 across both sensitivity analyses. However, the association for mtHg L0 was sensitive to covariate adjustment and became non‐significant after adjusting for SDOH, or APOE ε4 status, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities. Notably, the mtHg L1 association, initially statistically significant, was attenuated and became only significant after full adjustment. Additionally, all mtHg associations with baseline executive function were non‐significant after SDOH adjustment, but the association between mtHg L1 and baseline executive function remained significant even after adjusting for APOE ε4, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities. Associations with baseline composite score remains robust against both sensitivity analyses for all mtHgs (L0, L1, L2). All previously observed associations between mtHg and rates of cognitive decline, including verbal memory (L2, L3), executive function (L3), and composite score (L2, L3), remained significant after adjusting for SDOH. Similarly, most associations between mtHg and rates of cognitive decline also remained significant after adjusting for APOE ε4, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities, except the association between mtHg L2 and rate of decline of composite score.

In summary, mtHg L3 and L1 associations were robustly associated with processing speed and composite or global cognition, while mtHg L0 associations with processing speed and executive function were attenuated after adjustment for multiple testing. Executive function and semantic fluency were also particularly sensitive to covariate adjustment.

Heteroplasmy burden and cognitive function

Heteroplasmy burden, measured as mMSS, was not significantly associated with any cognitive domain (Table S5). We also tested for heteroplasmy count but did not find associations with cognitive performance at mid‐life (Table S5).

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to investigate the associations between mitochondrial DNA variation and cognitive performance in a diverse midlife cohort from the CARDIA study, focusing on cross‐sectional and longitudinal changes in cognitive performance. We found that while heteroplasmy burden and heteroplasmy count were not significantly associated with cognitive performance, mtHgs—particularly from the macro‐mtHg L—were consistently associated with poorer cognitive outcomes.

We found that mtDNA heteroplasmy burden and count were not associated with cognitive performance across any domain. There are two main factors that may explain this finding. First, heteroplasmy accumulates less in blood compared to other energy‐demanding tissues, 32 including the brain. However, in our prior work analyzing heteroplasmy in brain tissue among elderly, we did not observe associations with cognition. 12 It is important to acknowledge that blood‐based heteroplasmy measures may not fully reflect heteroplasmy in the brain. Second, our cohort is relatively young with low levels of heteroplasmy. In contrast, prior studies in older populations have reported higher levels of heteroplasmy in AD patients and those with worse cognitive function. 10 , 33 While our results suggest that mtDNA heteroplasmy may not have a significant impact on cognitive function at midlife, previously observed age‐related increase in heteroplasmy raises the possibility that heteroplasmy may vary more later in life, warrant further monitoring as the CARDIA cohort ages. 9

In contrast to heteroplasmy, mtHgs are stable across tissues as they reflect inherited lineage rather than acquired mutations. Therefore, inferences about mtHgs from blood are expected to generalize to other tissues. We observed that mtHgs, particularly those in macro‐mtHg L, were significantly associated with poorer cognitive performance. In both cross‐sectional and longitudinal analyses, individuals with macro‐mtHg L exhibited significantly lower cognitive performance, particularly in processing speed and global cognition, compared to the reference mtHg H. These associations remained robust in longitudinal models, suggesting that mtHgs may influence both baseline cognitive function and the trajectory of cognitive decline. While we did not find significant associations with verbal memory cross‐sectionally, longitudinal analyses revealed that some macro‐mtHg L subgroups were associated with a faster rate of verbal memory decline over time. These findings highlight a potential role of mtHg variation in cognitive aging in midlife and suggest that its effects may continue to influence cognitive trajectory over time.

In sensitivity analyses adjusting for SDOH or APOE ε4, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities, many of the observed associations between mtHgs and cognitive function remained significant. Although some associations, particularly in mtHg L0 or with executive function and semantic fluency, were attenuated, others, such as the association between macro‐mtHg L and global cognition, processing speed, and composite scores, remained robust. Findings from sensitivity analyses suggest that while some mtHg associations partly be explained by differences in other risk factors, others are robust to adjustment and appear to independently contribute to cognitive function.

Our study adds to the growing but still limited body of research on mtDNA variation and cognitive aging, particularly in diverse and midlife populations. Previous studies have examined associations of mtHgs with cognitive performance in populations of a single race, with most studies focusing on those of European descent. Several previous studies have linked mtHgs H, V, and J to AD risk, with inconsistent findings depending on the population and disease subtype. 7 Few studies have examined the association of mtHgs with cognitive performance in populations of African descent, where mtHg L1 has been previously associated with a faster decline in processing speed compared to mtHg L3 in African Americans. 34 Other than the strong associations observed in macro‐mtHg L, our results also show that mtHg N was associated with better processing speed. The main contributing haplogroups in mtHg N were mtHg I, W, and X. A previous study found that mtHg W was associated with lower semantic memory, while mtHg I showed mixed findings with AD. 12 , 13 , 35 The mtHg cluster IWX has been previously associated with higher risk of frontotemporal lobar degeneration but not for early‐onset AD. 36 These mtHgs are present in the population in low levels, making it difficult to detect and compare associations across different clustering methods. However, the largest studies of AD have not found associations with mtHg. 35

It is crucial to consider that, although cognitive aging is an aspect of AD, not everyone who experiences cognitive aging will progress to AD. Moreover, our cohort is relatively young compared to clinical AD cohorts, which may influence the observed patterns. It is also important to note that findings from population‐specific studies may differ. For example, a recent study of the Amish population found no association between mtHgs U, J, or K and cognitive impairment, 37 underscoring the complexity and heterogeneity of mtHg‐related effects across different populations. Overall, these results highlight the need for continued exploration of the role of mtHgs in cognitive aging, particularly with regard to population‐level differences, as findings may vary depending on the population studied and the specific mtHg subgroups that drive associations within each haplogroup.

Findings from this study should be interpreted alongside some limitations. It may be that a relatively limited sample size and increased degrees of freedom likely contributed to the null results of haplogroup effects and non‐significance of sensitivity analysis results after applying FDR adjustments. Also, CARDIA participants are younger than those from clinical AD studies, which may limit the variation in mtHz count as well as the decline in cognition, making it more difficult to detect significant associations. Finally, we conducted analysis in the total cohort, as stratified analysis would likely be underpowered (local effect size f2<0.02 for all models). Future work may benefit from stratified analyses in a larger, older cohorts to examine whether associations differ across subgroups, such as by ancestry, sex, or other demographic and clinical characteristics. Such approaches could help clarify potential effect heterogeneity and identify populations in which mtDNA variation has the strongest impact on cognitive outcomes.

Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths. First, we studied a multiracial cohort, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of mtDNA on cognitive aging across a diverse population. Studying this cohort enabled us to explore mtDNA variation across racial groups, providing insights that may not be observable in single‐race populations. Second, by quantifying SDOH, we were able to account for potential factors that can differ significantly between racial groups due to historical and systemic inequalities, affecting health outcomes, including cognitive aging. Third, our focus on midlife is important, as this is a critical period for preclinical cognitive changes and their trajectories.

Our findings underscore the role of mtDNA variation in cognitive aging, highlighting its potential to contribute to biological pathways that underlie age‐related cognitive declines. Further understanding of how these variations relate to mitochondrial dysfunction may shed light on mechanism driving neuronal and cellular changes in aging. By identifying targets involved in these pathways, researchers may be able to develop interventions to mitigate age‐related cognitive impairment and improve the quality of life for aging populations.
