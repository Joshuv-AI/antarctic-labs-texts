Bacillus velezensis alleviates PM2.5-induced intestinal injury and dysbiosis in broilers via the “lung-gut axis”
Authors: Xuan Liu, Xunbozan Zhang, Christopher Peterson Daniel, Dadan Liu, Liang Chen, Hongfu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated how tracheal instillation of Bacillus velezensis mitigates PM2.5-induced lung injury and counteracts its adverse effects on distal intestinal health in broilers via the “lung-gut axis”.ResultsFive groups were randomly selected from among seventy-five 14-day-old AA broilers: Control, Saline, PM2.5, B. velezensis, and B. velezensis + PM2.5. Results showed that compared with the Saline group, PM2.5 exposure disrupted lung structure and significantly upregulated TLR4, MyD88, NF-κB, IL-18, and IFN-γ related inflammatory factors via the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling axis (P < 0.05). In addition, it significantly reduced jejunal villus height through the action of the “lung-gut axis” (P < 0.05), decreased the levels of IL-10, Claudin-1, and Occludin while increasing that of IL-22 (P < 0.05). Analysis of the cecal microbiota found that PM2.5 reduced the Shannon index of bacterial communities and raised the relative abundance of Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, and Flavonifractor. Co-treatment with B. velezensis + PM2.5 reversed the above changes, significantly improved lung and intestinal morphology, enhanced jejunal barrier function, reduced lung and jejunal inflammatory responses, and increased the relative abundance of Faecalibacterium. Based on KEGG functional prediction, the PM2.5 group was enriched in microbial metabolic pathways, such as two-component systems and ABC transporters.ConclusionsTracheal instillation of B. velezensis can improve the expression of genes associated with lung injury via the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling axis and effectively alleviate PM2.5-induced distal jejunal damage, barrier dysfunction, inflammatory response, and dysbiosis by mediating the “lung-gut axis”. This could provide a theoretical support and scientific basis for seeking probiotics to improve PM2.5 harm.Graphical abstract Keywords: Bacillus velezensis, PM2.5, Lung-gut axis, Intestinal health, Broiler

Background

Old free-range farming has been replaced by an intensive agricultural approach due to the rapid advancements. In this poultry farming system, broilers are vulnerable to external influences like diseases, nutrition levels, and air pollution, impacting their productivity [1]. Air pollutants in breeding houses contain considerable particulate matter (PM), which can directly stimulate the respiratory system and harm the animal’s immune, circulatory, and energy metabolism systems. Among them, fine particulate matter (PM2.5) in the air of livestock and poultry houses can carry various harmful substances owing to its small particle size, long suspension time, and strong adsorption properties [2]. In recent years, research has gradually revealed that the negative effects of PM exposure are not limited to the lungs but can significantly impact distal intestinal organs through the bidirectional communication pathway of the “lung-gut axis” [3]. PM can enter the gastrointestinal tract through various routes. In addition to direct ingestion, while much PM is able to penetrate the respiratory system as far as the lung [4]. It still accumulates in the nasopharynx and oropharynx, and various gastrointestinal diseases (appendicitis, inflammatory diseases, irritable bowel syndrome, and intestinal infections) are associated with exposure to air pollution [5].

Multiple epidemiological studies have shown a high correlation between gut microbiota and the lungs (known as the “lung-gut axis”). It has been confirmed that the ecological imbalance caused by smoking has a pro tumor effect in colorectal cancer. Smoking related dysbiosis of gut microbiota can alter gut metabolites and reduce gut barrier function, activating oncogenic MAPK/ERK signaling in intestinal epithelial cells [6]. Study indicated that, taking Bifidobacteria as an example, the abundance of Bifidobacteria in smokers is significantly lower than in non-smokers, regardless of ethnicity [7]. Wang et al. [8] proposed that alterations in oxidative stress-related enzymes (such as xanthine-oxidase and superoxide dismutase-1) and increased levels of two key stress signaling mediators, JNK and p38, in the colon of mice exposed to smoke are responsible for gut dysbiosis. Concurrently, Tomoda et al. [9] demonstrated that smoking reduces intestinal organic acid levels, and local acid-base imbalance may induce gut dysbiosis by increasing the relative abundance of harmful bacteria. In addition, exposure to PM2.5 can lead to increased oxidative stress, glucose intolerance, insulin resistance, and impaired hepatic glucose production in mice [10]. Prolonged and high-level exposure can also result in decreased richness and diversity of the gut microbiota, decreased levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [11], increased mucosal barrier permeability, induction of mucosal immune responses, and intestinal inflammation [4, 12, 13]. Meanwhile, dysbiosis of gut microbiota and translocation of gut microbiota products can also accelerate lung inflammation [14]. Exposure to multiple environmental pollutants can cause varying degrees of damage to the gut. Impairment of the intestinal barrier is often manifested by damage to intestinal microvilli and compromised tight junctions. In particular, the jejunal tissue possesses a rich microvilli structure. Meanwhile, Jejunum, being the primary location for digestion and nutrient absorption in broilers, is frequently the initial part of the intestine to experience inflammation [1]. Therefore, exploring the possible mechanisms through which PM2.5 influences gut health and microbiota through the “lung-gut axis” is highly meaningful.

Bacillus promotes intestinal health by producing antimicrobial peptides to inhibit the toxicity of bacterial pathogens, and can also stimulate beneficial metabolites produced by gut microbiota to regulate intestinal health [6]. Bacillus velezensis is a probiotic strain with high carbohydrate metabolism ability. It synergistically produces SCFAs through intrinsic metabolism and regulation of gut microbiota, exerting anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects [15]. Previous studies have reported that B. velezensis can enhance the absorption capacity of the intestinal mucosa (increase villus height) and alleviate inflammatory reactions by promoting the gut microbiota (increasing Faecalibacterium abundance) [15]. While the gut-protective effects of probiotics are well-documented, their role in mitigating extra-intestinal organ injury via systemic pathways remains less explored. This is particularly relevant in the context of environmental hazards such as PM2.5, which can induce lung injury and subsequently lead to distal intestinal damage through the “lung-gut axis”. However, the potential of B. velezensis to alleviate PM2.5-induced lung injury, and its possible mechanism in coordinating gut protection via this axis, have not been empirically established. Tracheal instillation is a common and controllable laboratory exposure model aimed at clearly demonstrating the existence of the “lung-gut axis” and the distal effects, while excluding other confounding factors [5, 16]. Therefore, our research examined the impact of B. velezensis on lung and distal intestinal injury and microbiota under exposure to PM2.5, establishing a theoretical foundation for the implementation of probiotics in environmental stress.

Results

B. velezensis alleviates PM2.5-induced pulmonary inflammation and modulates the expression of genes in the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway

Tracheal instillation of PM2.5 results in the destruction of lung morphology, causing partial detachment of alveolar wall cells, enlargement of alveolar cavities, and inflammatory cell infiltration. However, treatment with B. Velezensis bacterial solution reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and improved alveolar cavity structure (Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. 1B-G, PM2.5 treatment significantly increased the relative mRNA levels of TLR4, MyD88, NF-κB, IL-18, and IFN-γ in broiler lungs, whereas co-treatment with B. velezensis significantly improved the lung inflammatory response (P < 0.05). The coordinated upregulation of this set of genes—from the surface receptor (TLR4) and adaptor (MyD88) to the key transcription factor (NF-κB) and its downstream inflammatory effectors (IL-1β, IFN-γ)—suggests the potential involvement of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling axis in mediating PM2.5-induced lung injury.

B. velezensis repairs injury to the jejunum exposed to PM2.5

As illustrated in Fig. 2A, in comparison to the Saline group, PM2.5 treatment resulted in partial shedding of intestinal villi, and co-treatment with B. velezensis bacterial solution improved the shedding of intestinal villi caused by PM2.5. Compared with the Con group, both the Saline and PM2.5 groups exhibited a reduction in jejunal villous height (VH), suggesting that the tracheal instillation procedure itself could induce mild physical stress affecting intestinal morphology. Notably, the VH in the PM2.5 group showed a further significant decrease compared to the Saline group (Table 1, P < 0.05), indicating an additive detrimental effect of PM2.5 exposure on top of the procedural stress. Co-treatment with B. velezensis effectively restored the VH to a level comparable to the Con group.

B. velezensis enhances the expression of jejunal intestinal barrier genes under PM2.5 exposure conditions

Although PM2.5 exposure alone did not lead to statistically significant changes in the expression of Claudin-1, Occludin, ZO-1, and MUC2 compared to the Saline group (Fig. 2B-E). It is noteworthy that co-treatment with B. velezensis significantly elevated the mRNA levels of Occludin and ZO-1 compared to the PM2.5 group (P < 0.05). Moreover, treatment with B. velezensis alone also significantly upregulated Occludin, MUC2, and ZO-1 relative to the Saline group, indicating its intrinsic barrier-strengthening capacity.

B. velezensis supplementation mitigated the PM2.5-induced upregulation of inflammatory cytokines in the jejunum

Figure 3 illustrates a comparison with the Con group, PM2.5 treatment significantly increased the relative expression of IL-22 pro-inflammatory factors and decreased the levels of IL-10 anti-inflammatory factors (P < 0.05). The co-treatment of B. Velezensis + PM2.5 effectively reduced IL-22 and significantly increased the relative expression level of VEGFA compared to the PM2.5 group (P < 0.05, Fig. 3E).

Discussion

Smaller PM2.5 diameters are engulfed via alveolar macrophages and then cleared returned to the oropharynx through mucociliary clearance, indirectly clearing deposited PM through mucociliary clearance. After ingestion, PM2.5 enters the distal gastrointestinal tract [17]. The research report states that PM2.5 can disrupt the necrosis and necrosis of broiler alveolar epithelial cells, while also playing a role in increasing pro-inflammatory cytokines, damaging the microbiota, and impairing intestinal function [18, 19]. B. velesensis, as a safe probiotic, has not shown hemolytic activity, virulence factors, or antibiotic resistance [20]. It is beneficial to the host in regulating gut microbiota, enhancing immune and antioxidant levels, and promoting the digestion and absorption of nutrients [20]. This study successfully established a “lung-gut axis” mediated distal intestinal injury model in broilers by infusing PM2.5 from broiler farms through the trachea. Our research found that tracheal infusion of B. velezensis not only directly alleviates PM2.5-induced lung inflammation through the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling axis, but also significantly improves the barrier function, inflammatory response, and dysbiosis of the distal gut.

B. velezensis alleviates systemic pre-inflammatory state by regulating lung NF-κB and inflammasome axis. Exposure to PM2.5 in the lungs induces acute inflammation, which is the initial event that initiates harmful communication between the lung and gut. At the genetic level, our research results show that PM2.5 exposure triggered a coordinated upregulation of key molecules along the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling cascade in broiler lungs and synergistically activated the key inflammasome element Caspase-1. While this study did not provide direct protein-level evidence of pathway activation, this specific gene level expression is highly consistent with the early findings of TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB axis activation in response to particulate matter [18]. Early studies have confirmed the importance of the TLR4 signaling pathway in the worsening of PM2.5-induced pulmonary eosinophilia [18]. As a key adaptor molecule in this event, MyD88 has been shown to activate the innate immune system in a MyD88-dependent manner by PM2.5 [18]. By activating NF-κB, the production and release of early cytokine IL-18 by immune cells is promoted. The release of IL-18 activates natural killer cells and T lymphocytes, thereby initiating the transcription and protein synthesis of IFN-γ [21]. And Caspase-1 activation can cleave a large amount of already synthesized pro-IL-18 into mature and active IL-18, and the two synergistically drive a severe pulmonary inflammatory response. Meanwhile, mature cytokines such as IL-18 can enter the circulatory system, creating a systemic inflammatory environment. Caspase-1 can also cleave Gasdermin D, and its N-terminal fragment can bind to the cell membrane, forming pores that promote the release of inflammatory cytokines and exacerbate cell apoptosis [22]. The intervention of B. velesensis can downregulate the expression of TLR-4, Caspase-1, etc., which means that it may block the release and maturation process of IL-18 from the source by inhibiting the expression of TLR-4 receptors and the activation of inflammasomes. We propose that this is not only a direct mechanism for its protection of the lungs, but also a crucial first step in cutting off “inflammatory spillover” and preventing systemic inflammatory signals from attacking distant organs such as the intestine. This effect is consistent with reports that composite probiotics alleviate allergic lung inflammation through the TLR4/NF-κB pathway [23]. Additionally, Alipanah et al. [24] also found that Lactobacilli strains can improve the expression of cytokines associated with inflammation in rat lungs induced by chlorpyrifos. Wang et al. [25] also showed that intervention with Ligilactobacillus salivarius can reduce the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and Caspase-1, and alleviate the lung damage caused by Mycoplasma gallisepticum.

Potential communication mechanism of the “lung-gut axis” enables the distal intestinal regulation of B. velesensis. The decrease of VH and villous height/crypt depth (VH/CD) ratio is a typical morphological sign of impaired intestinal absorption function. This study indicates that exposure to PM2.5 can significantly reduce jejunal villus height in broilers, which is consistent with previous research on the deterioration of intestinal morphology caused by environmental stress [4, 13, 26, 27]. It is worth noting that co-treatment with B. velesensis not only repaired lung tissue structure but also increased VH and VH/CD ratio, indicating that it may enhance the repair ability of intestinal epithelial cells through systemic regulation. Intestinal barrier function relies on tight junctions among epithelial cells [28]. The initial primary defense against harmful microbes entering the intestine is the mechanical barrier made up of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 [29, 30]. A chemical barrier that covers the gastrointestinal tract, one of the most crucial of these is MUC2, which is produced by goblet cells in the epithelial cell layer [31]. In the mouse colon epithelium, ingesting PM2.5 can cause intestinal permeability to rise and tight junction protein production to decrease [4]. Li et al. [32] reported that B. velesensis can alleviate intestinal tight junction damage caused by fungal toxins and raised the mRNA levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1. We hypothesize that the enhanced intestinal barrier function and downregulation of inflammatory factors induced by probiotics may be attributable to biomolecules produced by probiotics and gut microbiota, including proteins, extracellular polysaccharides, cell wall components, and microbial metabolites [33]. These substances collectively improve immune system efficiency, thereby promoting intestinal health. Research also confirms that B. velezensis possesses the ability to produce secondary metabolites with antibacterial activity, including bacteriocins, antimicrobial proteins, and lipopeptides [33]. These substances may play a crucial role in inflammatory responses and immune defense against infections. These are similar to the results of our study, which found that B. velesensis increased the mRNA expression levels of Claudin-1, Occludin, ZO-1, and MUC2. Therefore, we further infer that the intervention of B. velesensis reflects a systemic protective mode of “lung-derived, intestinal beneficial”. It establishes an “anti-inflammatory front line” in the lungs, which weakens the driving signals that initiate harmful communication in the “lung-gut axis” from the source, creating a favorable environment for the subsequent repair of intestinal barriers and inflammatory responses [34]. Changes in the expression levels of inflammatory markers further support the direct reparative effect of B. velesensis on the intestine. Local inflammation in the jejunum is indicated by the increase of mRNA expression of IL-22 and the downregulation of mRNA expression of IL-10 caused by PM2.5 exposure. This inflammatory environment is likely an important factor in disrupting the intestinal barrier and microbiota. In addition, in the B. velezensis+PM2.5 group, VEGFA was significantly upregulated and IL-22 was significantly downregulated, although PM2.5 itself was not significant change, indicating that probiotics induce a dual potential of active anti-inflammatory and promoting mucosal repair. Previous study has confirmed that adipose-derived stem cells stimulated by VEGF-C promote lymphatic drainage and enhance paracrine signaling by activating signal transduction mediated by VEGF-C/VEGFR-3 and inhibiting the NF-κB pathway, thereby improving chronic intestinal inflammation [35]. In addition, VEGFA overexpression promotes the reproduction of crypt epithelial cells and contributes to the formation of better-developed villous structures [36]. Research has indicated that B. velesensis can also promote the production of SCFAs [6]. SCFAs can act as signaling molecules, activating G protein-coupled receptors extracellularly and inhibiting histone deacetylase activity intracellularly [6]. This induces host immune responses, reduces intestinal inflammation, and enhances the intestinal barrier.

B. velesensis can also exert its protective effect on the intestine through indirect pathways. In terms of indirect effects, the core function of B. velesensis is to reshape the gut microbiota. Our 16S rRNA sequencing results showed that after intervention with B. velesensis, the relative abundance of Faecalibacterium increased, and the Simpson index and relative abundance of Bacteroidetes, Pseudomonadota, and Flavonifractor caused by PM2.5 were effectively reduced. These results align with the findings reported by Liu et al. [5], who found that tracheal instillation of PM2.5 can alter the Shannon and Simpson indexes of intestinal microbiota in mice. Bacillota, Bacteroidetes, and Pseudomonadota are the three dominant bacterial-phylum in the cecum of broilers [37]. Torok et al. [38] found that Bacteroidetes are associated with the growth performance of broilers. In addition, prolonged exposure to stressful environments may result in an elevated relative abundance of Bacteroidetes in the cecum, thereby inducing dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, damage to the intestinal villi, and compromise of intestinal barrier integrity [39]. The Pseudomonadota, which belongs to conditional pathogenic bacteria, rapidly proliferates and inhibits the Bacillota, resulting in a reduction of advantageous bacteria responsible for producing SCFAs [40]. Meanwhile, the Pseudomonadota contains a large number of LPS (Lipopolysaccharide)-producing bacteria, whose cell wall component LPS can induce an increase in IL-6 and TNF-α via the TLR4-NF-κB pathway, causing low-grade chronic inflammation and increasing mucosal permeability [41]. Pseudomonadota have the capacity to disrupt the cytoskeletal structure of macrophages, thereby impairing their phagocytic activity and inducing inflammatory responses, resulting in acute septicemia fever in poultry [42]. Flavonoid-degrading bacteria are strictly anaerobic and spore-producing. Large-scale reproduction consumes residual oxygen in the intestinal lumen, leading to a sudden drop in local redox potential and an explosion of reactive oxygen species in epithelial cell mitochondria, resulting in the phosphorylation and degradation of tight junction proteins [43]. Besides, the Flavonifractor can also hydrolyze flavonoids such as quercetin and catechol in feed through “flavonoid C-ring lyase” to obtain their own carbon source and reduce the availability of flavonoids to the host [44, 45]. Broilers lack flavonoids, which can reduce the body’s antioxidant capacity and increase malondialdehyde and related inflammatory factors [46]. Faecalibacterium is a marker of intestinal health, and an increase in its relative abundance can reduce the pathological state of the intestine and improve inflammation-related diseases [47]. Faecalibacterium has the potential to mitigate inflammation through the production of substantial quantities of SCFAs, with butyrate being particularly prominent [48]. Butyrate is recognized for its anti-inflammatory and anticancer effects, which contribute to the activation of the immune system and the strengthening of the intestinal barrier. These underlying materials also serve as the main energy providers harnessed by the cells of the intestinal epithelium [49]. Predicting the functions of microorganisms enables us to comprehend the part played by the gut microbiota within the host. Purine metabolism, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, ABC transporters, and two-component system are widely enriched in the gut microbiota of broilers [50]. Two-component system is the core signal mechanism by which bacteria perceive and respond to environmental changes, such as osmotic pressure, nutrient deficiency, and harmful substances [51]. The synthesis of cell wall components, peptidoglycan and extracellular polysaccharides, relies on the metabolism of amino sugars and nucleoside sugars, while ABC transporters, as an important transmembrane transporters system, can obtain scarce nutrients from the environment to meet the normal growth and development needs of bacterial cells [52]. Our results indicated that co-treatment with B. velesensis and PM2.5 reduced expression in the Two-component system, likely because B. velesensis decreased harmful substance accumulation and mitigated PM2.5-induced damage to the intestinal environment [51]. Conversely, the ABC transporters showed a slight increase following B. velesensis treatment. This may result from bacteria facilitating the transport of various essential molecules (such as monosaccharides, amino acids, peptides, iron-siderophores, metal ions, polyamine cations, and vitamins) and large molecules (such as proteins) across the cell membrane, thereby promoting nutrient absorption by beneficial bacteria [53]. Therefore, our study infers that B. velesensis can counteract the negative effects of intestinal damage and dysbiosis caused by PM2.5 exposure. This provides a favorable scientific basis for developing new environmental stress strategies based on probiotics.

In conclusion our study emphasizes the mechanism by which B. velesensis alleviates PM2.5-induced lung and intestinal damage in broilers through the “lung-gut axis”. Nevertheless, our research on this mechanism remains insufficient, with only inferences that antimicrobial peptides or SCFAs play a role in regulating gut health. The specific component responsible has not been clearly identified. Therefore, this study can provide some reference for further exploration of the “lung-gut axis” communication in the future.

Conclusions

This study elucidates a multi-level mechanism by which B. velesensis alleviates PM2.5 “lung-gut axis” injury. B. velesensis reduces systemic inflammatory burden and cuts off the source of harmful lung gut communication by inhibiting the gene expression of TLR4/NF-κB/inflammasome pathway in the lungs. And B. velesensis synergistically repairs the structure and function of the distal intestine by directly strengthening the intestinal barrier and indirectly reshaping beneficial microbiota (such as enhancing Faecalibacterium). This provides a new theoretical basis for applying probiotics to alleviate systemic health problems caused by agricultural environmental pollutants.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

One hundred 1-day-old AA broilers were raised until 14 days of age. Form these, 75 broilers of similar body weight were randomly allocated to five groups: a control group (Con), a normal saline group (Saline), a PM2.5 treatment group (PM2.5), a B. velezensis group (B. Velezensis), and a B. velezensis and PM2.5 co-treatment group (B. Velezensis + PM2.5). Perform tracheal instillation test once every 3 days for a total of 5 times. The experiment employed a completely randomized design with three replicates per group and five broilers per replicate. All groups of broilers used in the experiment were fed according to the “AA Broiler Feeding and Management Manual” and were allowed to freely consume and collect water. They were fed a basic diet normally, and the basic diet formula was formulated according to the NRC (1994) broiler nutrition standards. The nutritional components are showed in Table 2.

Sample collection

At the end of the experimental period, broilers were fasted for 12 h with free access to water. From each group (total of 15 broilers per group, comprised of three replicates with 5 broilers each), nine broilers were systematically and randomly selected for slaughter and sample collection—specifically, three broilers were chosen at random from each of the three replicates. A segment of the jejunum was excised and carefully rinsed with pre-cooled physiological saline to eliminate intestinal contents, followed by immediate fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde. Simultaneously, lung, jejunal tissue, and cecal contents were rapidly frozen using liquid nitrogen for subsequent RNA extraction and 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing (Shanghai Major Bio-Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd).

Morphological analysis

Fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for a duration of 24 h, lung and jejunal tissue specimens were dehydrated and subsequently embedded in paraffin blocks following established standard protocols. The intestine was sectioned into slices measuring 5 μm in thickness and subsequently stained using hematoxylin and eosin. Stained section images were captured using the Leica microscope DM500 (Leica Corporation, Wetzlar, Germany). VH and crypt depth (CD) were measured using Image Pro software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD, USA).

qRT-PCR analysis

In accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, total RNA was isolated from the lung and jejunal tissues utilizing the Trizol assay kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). ABI QuantStudio™ 7 Flex System (Thermo Fisher, USA) was used in the qRT-PCR finished by the SYBR® Green Reagents and specific primers (Table 3). The detection data was analyzed utilizing the 2 –ΔΔCt method.

Analysis of microbial communities using 16S rRNA sequencing

Genomic DNA from the gut microbiota was isolated from cecal contents employing the CTAB extraction protocol, followed by amplification of the 16S rRNA gene (V3-V4). After detecting the PCR product, recover the target band. Afterwards, the library construction was carried out, and the Agilent 5400 was used for qRT-PCR quantification. After the library construction was qualified, NextSeq 2000 was used for machine sequencing. After sequencing, Vsearch (v2.3.4) was used to denoise the amplicon data and cluster them to present 97% sequence identity. The OUT abundance was normalized utilizing the SILVA (v138) classifier, while α diversity and β diversity metrics were computed employing QIIME1 (v1.91). Conduct sequence alignment utilizing BLAST and perform annotation of OTU sequences against the SILVA database.

α diversity analysis showed Shannon and Simpson indices. Microorganisms at the phylum level show the top 50 species in abundance. In order to further find out the difference microorganisms between groups, the difference test between groups was carried out. PICRUSt2 function prediction analysis is helpful to understand the function of microorganisms. The 16 S sequencing data were compared to KEGG database by R software (v3.5.2) to obtain KEGG level 3 metabolic pathway. These analyses are implemented by the R software (v3.5.2).

Statistical analysis

After preliminary sorting of the raw data, ANOVA was conducted, multiple comparisons were conducted using Duncan’s method, and data visualization was performed utilizing GraphPad Prism version 8 software. For 16 S sequencing data, Kruskal-Wallis est was used for α diversity analysis and differential microbial screening, and PLS-DA was used for microbial clustering analysis methods. The error bars were displayed using “mean ± standard error”, with P < 0.05 indicating significant differences.
