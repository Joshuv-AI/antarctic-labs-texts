Mechanisms, optimization strategies, and applications of highly sensitive piezoelectric nanomaterials in tumor therapy
Authors: Haohao Zhang, Mingyuan Pang, Juan An, Min Yang, Zhen Kong, Linzheng Ren, Zifan Wang, Jiajia Ye, Yen Leng Pak, Xing Gao, Jibin Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Piezocatalytic therapy based on ultrasound stimulation is an emerging tumor treatment technology. The ultrasound-activated piezoelectric effect can induce reactions of molecules in the surrounding environment, generating reactive oxygen species with strong oxidizing properties, which trigger cell apoptosis or activate the immune system to enhance the body's anti-tumor immune response. However, existing conventional piezocatalytic materials suffer from limitations such as functional restrictions and poor catalytic efficiency, failing to achieve efficient piezocatalytic therapy. Therefore, the development of piezocatalytic nanomaterials with high sensitivity and synergistic multiple therapeutic mechanisms is urgently needed. Against this backdrop, this paper provides a detailed introduction to the catalytic mechanisms of piezocatalytic materials, material modification strategies for enhancing piezocatalytic sensitivity and efficiency, as well as their application effects in clinical fields including tumor treatment and wound healing. Finally, this paper also discusses the current challenges and future directions for the further development of piezocatalytic materials and their applications.

Introduction

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are oxygen-containing substances with high oxidative potential, encompassing unpaired radical electrons such as superoxide ions (•O2−) and hydroxyl radicals (•OH), as well as other oxygen-containing species like singlet oxygen (1O2) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) [1]. These highly reactive ROS species have been extensively utilized in cancer therapy due to their ability to simultaneously damage multiple cellular components, including proteins, DNA, and lipid membranes, which are critical for cell survival and proliferation. Leveraging ROS for cancer cell eradication represents a promising tumor treatment strategy, as it can circumvent drug resistance mechanisms and address limitations associated with conventional chemotherapy. The advent of nanomedicine has further propelled research into strategies that employ nanomaterials to generate excessive ROS under endogenous or exogenous stimuli (e.g., NIR laser [2], ultrasound [3], radiation [4]) for therapeutic purposes. Taking the abdominal cavity as an example, the penetration depth of near-infrared laser in superficial tissues is approximately 0–2 cm. For deep tissues, the penetration depth may exceed 2 cm, but the energy attenuation is significant, with the penetration depth usually not exceeding 5 cm and the signal intensity being relatively low. In contrast, the penetration depth of abdominal ultrasound can reach 10–20 cm, accompanied by strong signal intensity. Given the inherent limitation of shallow laser penetration in human tissues (Fig. 1), ultrasound-activated ROS generation has emerged as a more favorable approach in this field.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of penetration depth for different energy stimuli.Fig. 1

Schematic diagram of penetration depth for different energy stimuli.

Ultrasound, as a mechanical wave, exhibits multiple energy conversion pathways when interacting with liquid-phase matrices and sonosensitizers. The mechanistic pathways of sonodynamic therapy (SDT) can be categorized into thermal, mechanical, and chemical injury modalities based on the damage patterns induced in tumor cells (Fig. 2). Ultrasonic cavitation occurs when acoustic pressure overcomes the liquid-phase tension, forming cavitation bubbles. Gas molecules, microdroplets, or particulates within the liquid phase can reduce the cavitation threshold [5]. Cavitation is classified into stable cavitation and transient cavitation: stable cavitation generates high shear forces that directly damage cells, while transient cavitation induces mechanical injury through shock waves and microjets, and concurrently activates sonosensitizers or pyrolyzes solvents to produce ROS via sonoluminescence. Generally, ultrasound frequencies below 1 MHz provide stronger mechanical forces during violent bubble collapse, whereas frequencies above 1 MHz tend to promote sonochemical reactions. Research in the field of sonodynamic therapy predominantly employs frequencies ranging from 1 to 3 MHz.Fig. 2Diagram illustrating the mechanism of sonodynamic therapy.Fig. 2

Diagram illustrating the mechanism of sonodynamic therapy.

Sonosensitizers are classified into organic, inorganic and nano-composite material categories. Inorganic sonosensitizers primarily comprise semiconductor nanomaterials and their composite structures, such as TiO2 [6], MnWOx [7], and PtCu [8]. These nanomaterials can be excited by ultrasound (US), inducing electron-hole separation and subsequent redox reactions on their surfaces to generate ROS. Compared to their organic counterparts, inorganic sonosensitizers exhibit superior stability and biosafety. However, traditional inorganic sonosensitizers often suffer from relatively low ROS generation efficiency due to inefficient electron-hole separation and rapid recombination under US stimulation. Improving electron-hole separation efficiency while mitigating recombination in inorganic sonosensitizers is crucial for advancing SDT. Conventional organic sonosensitizers, such as porphyrin [9] and phthalocyanine derivatives [10], exhibit high blood clearance rate, poor water solubility, low stability and limited responsiveness and sensitivity to ultrasound, necessitating high acoustic intensities or prolonged irradiation times to effectively produce ROS. This may compromise therapeutic efficacy or cause unintended damage to adjacent healthy tissues. Furthermore, energy loss occurs during the conversion of ultrasonic energy to chemical energy (for ROS generation), resulting in lower ROS yields compared to inorganic nanosensitizers or novel composite materials. In order to improve the effectiveness of SDT, it is imperative to develop highly efficient and stable sonosensitizers.

Piezoelectric materials, characterized by their dual semiconductor and piezoelectric properties, demonstrate robust responsiveness to exogenous stimuli such as light, electricity, and mechanical force. These materials can generate electric fields under mechanical stress, including ultrasound and vibration [11,12]. Upon US exposure, piezoelectric materials undergo polarization, leading to electron-hole pair separation and migration to opposite surfaces, thereby establishing a piezoelectric potential. If the resultant bent conduction and valence bands meet the thermodynamic requirements for redox reactions, ROS generation becomes feasible [13,14]. Harnessing the piezoelectric effect to convert ultrasonic mechanical energy into electrical energy for driving ROS production offers a novel avenue for developing advanced sonosensitizers [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. This approach effectively addresses the issue of low ROS yield in traditional inorganic sonosensitizers, which is primarily caused by the inefficient separation and rapid recombination of electrons and holes when responding to ultrasonic signals. Piezoelectric materials applicable in SDT include perovskites [20], wurtzite [21], flexible polymers [22], layered transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) [23], metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) [24], 2D carbon materials [25], layered bismuth-based materials [26], black phosphorus [27] among others, which may exist as single crystals, polycrystals, solid solutions, or thin films (Fig. 3). Existing review articles related to biological piezoelectric catalytic materials primarily focus their research on the fundamental theories of piezoelectric catalysis and material applications. They discuss the preparation of relevant piezoelectric materials, potential reaction mechanisms, as well as the applications of piezoelectric catalytic materials in the fields of healthcare and biomedicine, with a specific focus on the biological effects of ultrasound-activated piezoelectric nanomaterials. Starting from the structure and properties of piezoelectric semiconductors, this article systematically introduces the potential mechanisms underlying the application of piezoelectric semiconductor nanomaterials in SDT. From the perspective of the materials themselves, it comprehensively discusses the modification strategies employed to enhance catalytic sensitivity, with a key analysis of how the structural regulation and compositional elements of piezoelectric materials influence the efficacy of sonodynamic piezoelectric catalytic therapy. Additionally, it focuses on the latest research progress of piezoelectric semiconductor nanomaterials as sonosensitizers in cancer treatment, antibacterial applications, and other related areas. Meanwhile, it provides an outlook on the existing problems, challenges, and future development trends in this field. The aim is to offer guidance for the rational development of next-generation piezoelectric nano-theranostic platforms.Fig. 3Piezoelectric materials and modification methods.Fig. 3

Structural characteristics of piezoelectric semiconductors

When piezoelectric semiconductors are subjected to external stress, the crystal undergoes polarization, generating piezoelectric charges (bound charges) and a piezoelectric potential with opposite signs at both ends, which is known as the piezoelectric effect. Furthermore, the piezoelectric effect can influence the transport properties of excited carriers within the semiconductor, thereby affecting its catalytic performance and other characteristics—a phenomenon termed the piezoelectronic effect.

Asymmetry of crystal structure

From the crystallographic perspective, the piezoelectric effect originates from the asymmetry of crystal structures [28]. The most extensively studied piezoelectric semiconductors exhibit perovskite structures belonging to the cubic crystal system. The general chemical formula for perovskites is ABO3, where A represents lanthanide or alkaline earth metal ions, and B denotes transition metal ions (Fig. 4a). Both O2− and A-site cations possess relatively large ionic radii, forming a cubic close-packed framework, while smaller B-site cations occupy the octahedral interstitial sites. When subjected to external mechanical stress, BaTiO3 crystals exhibit strong polarity arising from the displacement of Ti4+ ions within the tetragonal unit cell.Fig. 4Structural characteristics of piezoelectric semiconductor. Another prevalent piezoelectric material structure is the wurtzite configuration, which belongs to the hexagonal crystal system with AB-type tetrahedral coordination. In such crystals, A-site atoms arrange in a hexagonal close-packed manner, while B-site atoms occupy tetrahedral interstitial sites [29]. Representative examples include zinc oxide (ZnO), aluminum nitride (AlN), and gallium nitride (GaN). Taking ZnO as an illustration, its crystal structure features layer-by-layer stacking of Zn2+ and O2− ions along the c-axis, with coinciding charge centers of cations and anions, resulting in a non-centrosymmetric structure (Fig. 4b). The magnitude of piezoelectric potential generated under external stress depends on factors such as dopant concentration and applied mechanical strain.

Asymmetry arising from interlayer stacking

From a macroscopic structural perspective, nanoscale piezoelectric semiconductor materials can effectively traverse various physiological barriers such as blood vessels or cell membranes. For SDT mediated by piezoelectric semiconductor nanomaterials, the nanoscale dimension also provides higher electron transfer rates and more active sites. Based on dimensionality, they can be classified into zero-dimensional (0D), one-dimensional (1D), and two-dimensional (2D) piezoelectric semiconductor nanomaterials. When piezoelectric or even non-piezoelectric materials are thinned to 2D structures, they lose centrosymmetry in one direction and exhibit piezoelectricity [31]. Taking TMDs as an example, when TMDs exist as bulk stacked materials, they are centrosymmetric and their bulk forms do not exhibit piezoelectricity; however, when thinned to monolayer thickness, their inversion center disappears, resulting in piezoelectric properties. Research by Tan et al. [30] demonstrates that monolayer MoS2 exhibits strong piezoelectricity and can rapidly degrade organic dyes under US stimulation (Fig. 4c). However, MoS2 sheets with an odd number of atomic layers exhibit piezoelectric activity, while those with even-numbered layers do not, due to the inversion-symmetric structure formed by oppositely stacked atomic layers in even-layered sheets. When atomic layers are arranged in the same orientation as 3R-type stacking, the piezoelectric effect accumulates between layers, i.e., it intensifies with increasing volume.

Mechanism of ROS generation by piezoelectric semiconductors

Under the periodic action of US, gas molecules and microdroplets in liquid media undergo processes of nucleation, growth, and microbubble formation, a phenomenon termed cavitation effect. Currently, the mechanism by which sonosensitizers generate ROS under cavitation effects remains inconclusive. Below, we introduce the widely accepted mechanisms for ROS generation by piezoelectric semiconductors under US stimulation.

Ultrasonic cavitation effect

Ultrasonic cavitation refers to the phenomenon where pressure variations in a liquid under ultrasonic irradiation lead to the formation of localized gas bubbles (typically dissolved gases or volatile liquids) and trigger a series of dynamical processes [32]. These bubbles undergo nucleation, growth, oscillation, collapse, and even implosion under the periodic action of ultrasound (Fig. 5). Based on bubble behavior, ultrasonic cavitation can be classified into two modes: inertial cavitation (transient cavitation) and non-inertial cavitation (steady-state cavitation).Fig. 5Schematic diagram of ultrasonic cavitation effect.Fig. 5

Schematic diagram of ultrasonic cavitation effect.

Non-inertial cavitation bubbles oscillate near their equilibrium radius, persisting for multiple ultrasonic cycles and maintaining a prolonged stable state while inducing liquid flow. This cavitation mode plays a significant role in liquid agitation and mixing. In contrast, inertial cavitation involves bubble nucleation and gradual growth to near-resonance sizes, followed by rapid collapse and implosion. This process generates extreme physicochemical conditions, including high temperatures (>5000 K), high pressures (>100 MPa), shock waves, and microjets. Although the lifespan of inertial cavitation bubbles is limited to one or a few ultrasonic cycles, the extreme conditions they produce exert a profound impact on chemical reactions and physical processes.

During inertial cavitation, the rapid collapse and implosion of bubbles create localized high temperatures and pressures, sufficient to decompose H2O and other substrate molecules into •OH. The shock waves and microjets generated during bubble collapse enhance mass transfer efficiency within the liquid, facilitating contact and reactions between reactants. These physical effects aid in transporting produced ROS to broader regions, thereby expanding their scope of action.

Sonoluminescence and photocatalysis

When the frequency and intensity of US are sufficiently high, cavitation effects manifest as rapid growth and collapse of microbubbles, releasing energy in the form of shockwaves and light—a phenomenon known as sonoluminescence (SL). SL-induced photocatalytic reactions are considered one of the plausible mechanisms for piezoelectric semiconductor nanomaterials in SDT. The wavelength of SL typically ranges from 200 to 800 nm, depending on both the properties of the medium and the US frequency/intensity. According to semiconductor band theory, electrons within semiconductors exhibit energy differences, forming distinct energy bands: the conduction band (CB), valence band (VB), and the forbidden bandgap (Eg) between them. When the energy absorbed by the material exceeds the bandgap width, electrons (e−) in the VB are excited and transition to the CB, leaving positively charged holes (h+) in the VB. These excited electrons can participate in redox reactions with surrounding substances, while electron-hole recombination may occur with energy dissipation, releasing energy in alternative forms (Fig. 6a).Fig. 6Schematic diagram of sonoluminescence and photocatalytic effect (a), schematic diagram of piezoelectric catalysis (b).Fig. 6

Schematic diagram of sonoluminescence and photocatalytic effect (a), schematic diagram of piezoelectric catalysis (b).

For a piezoelectric semiconductor to catalyze O2 into •O2− (O2 +e−→•O2−), the CB potential must be more negative than the redox potential of O2/•O2− (−0.33 V vs. RHE). Similarly, for •OH generation (H2O + h+→•O2−), the VB potential must be more positive than the redox potential of H2O/•OH (+2.01 V vs. RHE). From a semiconductor perspective, the band positions of piezoelectric semiconductor nanomaterials used in SDT must at least align with one of these redox potentials (O2/•O2− or H2O/•OH) to enable ROS production via catalytic reactions.

Piezo-catalysis

Band theory can partially elucidate the mechanism of ROS generation in piezoelectric semiconductors under US irradiation. However, from the perspective of the piezoelectric effect, researchers have hypothesized that mechanical vibrations induced by US irradiation can directly generate charge carriers on the surface of piezoelectric semiconductors and, by regulating the migration of these charge carriers on the material surface, catalyze the production of ROS—a phenomenon termed piezo-catalysis. It is important to note that in piezo-catalytic reactions, the generation of charge carriers is primarily triggered by mechanical stimulation rather than light-induced processes. The catalytic reactions initiated by the piezoelectric effect are typically attributed to the charge screening effect.

On the polarized surface of a piezoelectric semiconductor, bound charges and screening charges exist in equilibrium, rendering the material electrically neutral as a whole. Under US irradiation with specific frequencies and intensities, microbubble collapse occurs, releasing ultrahigh pressures (up to 108 MPa) that act on adjacent piezoelectric semiconductors. At this point, compressive stress (negative strain) arising from the piezoelectric effect reduces the material's polarization intensity, inducing redistribution of charge carriers on the surface and the release of excess screening charges (denoted as q- and q+ for negative and positive charges, respectively) from the material (Fig. 6b). These excess screening charges then disperse into the surrounding solution, forming free charges that ultimately react with H2O or O2 in the environment to generate •OH or •O2− radicals (O2 + q-→•O2−, H2O + q+→•O2−).

When the applied compressive stress decreases (positive strain), the material's polarization intensity increases, causing the surface to adsorb surrounding free charges to replenish the screening charges and balance the bound charges generated by the piezoelectric effect. Simultaneously, oppositely polarized charges in the electrolyte re-engage in redox reactions, continuously generating ROS [33]. Consequently, piezoelectric semiconductor nanomaterials subjected to alternating stress within an electrolyte environment can sustainably supply charges to produce •OH or •O2− radicals for SDT.

Piezo(photo)tronic effect

With the development of piezoelectric semiconductors, the interplay between piezoelectric and semiconductor properties has given rise to a series of novel effects. Professor Zhonglin Wang pioneered the concepts of piezoelectronics and piezophototronics to investigate the transport behavior of charge carriers in piezoelectric semiconductors under the influence of mechanically induced piezoelectric potentials [34]. These discoveries provide a viable strategy for modulating redox reactions at the surface of piezoelectric semiconductors.

Upon contact with an electrolyte solution, charge carriers in piezoelectric semiconductors transfer through the solid-liquid interface into the solution, inducing upward band bending [12]. This type of band bending acts as a barrier to electron transfer in reduction reactions but facilitates hole transport in oxidation reactions. Under this premise, when US irradiation is applied to a piezoelectric semiconductor, external strain in the material can enhance or attenuate polarization, spontaneously generating a dynamic built-in electric field. If the solid-liquid interface carries positive polarization charges, downward band bending occurs, implying that US irradiation promotes electron migration to the electrolyte (lowering reduction potential) while suppressing hole transfer (increasing oxidation potential) (Fig. 7a). Conversely, when periodic US vibration diminishes, the built-in electric field reverses direction, and the solid-liquid interface acquires negative polarization charges, resulting in upward band bending. This further impedes electron transfer to the solution and elevates the reduction potential (lowering the oxidation potential) [35]. Consequently, certain piezoelectric semiconductors with unfavorable band structures for ROS generation may exhibit ROS production capability under US irradiation. Metal-piezoelectric semiconductor heterojunction

Piezoelectric semiconductors exhibit a smaller work function compared to metals. Upon intimate contact with a metal of larger work function, free electrons transfer from the piezoelectric semiconductor to the metal across the interface until Fermi level alignment occurs. This process induces upward band bending in the piezoelectric semiconductor and forms a Schottky barrier at the metal-piezoelectric semiconductor heterojunction [36]. Generally, the Schottky barrier acts as an energy barrier for electrons, requiring electrons to possess energy exceeding the barrier height to traverse the heterojunction interface and initiate reduction reactions. Under US irradiation, if the piezoelectric effect induces positive polarization charges at the metal-piezoelectric semiconductor interface, the Schottky barrier height decreases, facilitating smooth electron flow and accelerating reduction reactions (Fig. 7b). Conversely, when negative charges accumulate at the interface, the Schottky barrier height increases, blocking electron transfer from the semiconductor to the metal and ultimately suppressing reduction reactions.

P-N junction

To modulate charge carrier transport behavior, p-n junctions and Z-scheme heterojunctions based on piezoelectric semiconductors (PS) and non-piezoelectric semiconductors (nPS) have been developed. For p-n junction construction, when an n-type piezoelectric semiconductor contacts a p-type non-piezoelectric semiconductor, free electrons migrate from the piezoelectric semiconductor to the non-piezoelectric semiconductor, forming a depletion region. Upon excitation, electrons in the conduction band transfer to the piezoelectric semiconductor, while holes in the valence band migrate to the non-piezoelectric semiconductor, enabling enhanced charge carrier separation (Fig. 7c). However, due to band structure characteristics, the retained electrons and holes in p-n junctions exhibit reduced redox capability.

Fortunately, under US irradiation, the built-in electric field induced by piezotronic effects plays a decisive role in regulating interfacial charge transfer [37]. Specifically, if positive polarization charges accumulate at the p-n junction interface under US irradiation, electrons are strongly attracted, causing downward band bending and restricting charge carrier transport. In contrast, negative polarization charges repel free electrons, inducing upward band bending and improving charge carrier migration.

Z-scheme heterojunction

In Z-scheme heterojunctions, if the non-piezoelectric semiconductor possesses higher band energy levels and Fermi levels than the piezoelectric semiconductor [38], Fermi level alignment drives electron transfer from the non-piezoelectric semiconductor to the piezoelectric semiconductor upon contact [39]. Upon excitation, the interfacial electric field promotes recombination of holes from the non-piezoelectric semiconductor with electrons from the piezoelectric semiconductor, while retaining electrons and holes with elevated redox activity (Fig. 7d). Interestingly, under US irradiation, the dynamic built-in electric field generated by the piezoelectric effect within the piezoelectric semiconductor further modulates electron-hole migration. Specifically, US-induced positive polarization charges cause downward band bending in the piezoelectric semiconductor, accelerating charge carrier recombination. Conversely, negative polarization charges at the interface suppress recombination, preserving charge carriers for redox reactions.

Thus, constructing sonosensitizers based on piezoelectric semiconductor heterojunctions effectively improves electron-hole transport and promotes ROS generation under US irradiation. Additionally, the US-induced dynamic built-in electric field regulates surface redox reactions through band bending, potentially enabling piezoelectric semiconductor heterojunctions to energetically favor ROS generation.

Construction of highly sensitive ultrasound-activated piezoelectric systems

Piezoelectric materials, a class of specialized materials with non-centrosymmetric crystalline structures, are highly susceptible to mechanical stress. When subjected to mechanical stress, these materials exhibit net polarization due to charge imbalance within the crystal lattice. The polarization characteristics of such materials enable both current generation and the initiation of interfacial redox reactions, leading to the production of ROS. Piezoelectric materials offer wireless electrostimulation capabilities, presenting a promising alternative to electrode/battery-powered stimulation in diverse biomedical applications. Notably, these materials can be wirelessly activated via ultrasound-induced external pressure.

Consequently, piezoelectric nanoparticles enable on-demand electrostimulation with precise spatial focusing on specific organs or intracellular compartments. Although ultrasound is widely employed in medical diagnostics and therapeutics, and piezoelectric materials have found applications across various biomedical domains, the integration of piezoelectric nanoparticles with ultrasound for remote charge generation and associated chemical reactions demands piezoelectric nanomaterials with ultra-high sensitivity, enhanced specificity, or synergistic therapeutic functions.

The evolution of piezoelectric materials has advanced significantly over recent decades, transitioning from naturally mined quartz crystals to a diverse array of synthetically engineered materials [77]. Based on their structural features and properties, piezoelectric materials can be categorized into several major classes: ABO3-type perovskites, wurtzite-structured materials, piezoelectric flexible polymers, layered transition metal dichalcogenides, metal-organic frameworks, two-dimensional carbon materials, bismuth-layered structural materials, and black phosphorus, among others. Table 1 presents the corresponding relationships among the piezocatalytic coefficient, performance, and application of various piezoelectric materials. As the piezocatalytic coefficient of piezoelectric materials, d33 is one of the core parameters for measuring the electromechanical conversion efficiency of piezoelectric materials. It mainly characterizes the ability of piezoelectric materials to generate mechanical strain under the action of an electric field or produce an electric field under the action of mechanical stress. The larger the value of d33, the stronger the deformation capacity of the material under electric field excitation, and the more significant its ability to generate charges under mechanical force. However, the standalone use of any single piezoelectric material often fails to meet the performance requirements of practical applications. Following in-depth analysis of piezoelectric generation mechanisms and piezocatalytic principles, researchers have employed targeted modifications to existing piezoelectric materials to achieve desired high-performance characteristics. Common strategies include defect engineering, ionic doping, morphological regulation, material compositing, and surface coating. Rational modification facilitates the design and development of more efficient piezoelectric therapeutic systems. Lattice polarization treatment

Perovskite-type piezoelectric materials represent the most widely utilized and typical semiconductor materials in the field of piezoelectric catalysis. Recently, biocompatible perovskite-type piezoelectric semiconductor materials have garnered increasing attention, including barium titanate (BaTiO3) [78,79], strontium titanate (SrTiO3) [[80], [81], [82]], calcium titanate (CaTiO3) [83,84], lithium tantalate (LiTaO3) [85], lithium niobate (LiNbO3) [86], zinc stannate (ZnSnO3) [87,88], and bismuth tungstate (Bi2WO6) [89,90]. These materials have attracted substantial interest due to their favorable biocompatibility, rapid and environmentally friendly synthesis processes, and facile modifiability.

The core characteristic of ferroelectric materials lies in their spontaneous polarization, which refers to the presence of an electric dipole moment within the material without the application of an external electric field. This spontaneous polarization serves as the foundation for the piezoelectric effect.

Barium titanate (BTO) exhibits a non-centrosymmetric crystal structure, endowing it with significant spontaneous polarization and consequently excellent piezoelectric, ferroelectric, and dielectric properties. To further enhance the piezoelectric performance of commercial cubic-phase BTO, the hydrothermal method can be employed to synthesize tetragonal-phase BTO. Subsequently, a polarization device is used to induce additional polarization in the tetragonal-phase structure. This approach enables a substantial improvement in the piezoelectric performance of BTO. As nanomaterials with a perovskite structure, non-centrosymmetric tetragonal-phase barium titanate nanoparticles can establish a dynamic internal electric field under periodic US irradiation. This field continuously induces electron and hole transport to the material surface, subsequently triggering redox reactions and promoting ROS generation. Qi et al. [40,41] conducted a series of studies on the piezoelectric response of barium titanate with different crystalline phases (Fig. 8a–f), as well as its mechanistic role and efficacy in tumor diagnosis and therapy. They investigated cubic BTO, tetragonal unpolarized barium titanate (U-BTO), and tetragonal polarized barium titanate (P-BTO) under exogenous mechanical stimulation. Despite similar microscopic morphologies, these particles exhibited distinct internal energy spectrum intensities: 425 mV for U-BTO and 886 mV for P-BTO. This demonstrates a significant influence of barium titanate crystallographic phase on piezoelectric performance (Fig. 8g–k). Defect engineering of piezocatalytic systems

Defect engineering of perovskite piezoelectric nanomaterials

Perovskite piezoelectric materials exhibit superior piezoelectric effects due to their non-centrosymmetric crystal structures, enabling charge separation under mechanical stress. Defect engineering provides a critical avenue for optimizing perovskite piezoelectric material performance through the introduction or modulation of defects (e.g., oxygen vacancies, doping). The incorporation of crystalline defects has been demonstrated to enhance piezocatalytic efficiency. Notably, metal doping serves as an effective defect introduction strategy, enabling the regulation of carrier migration behavior, promotion of ROS generation, and potentiation of SDT efficacy. In addition to piezoelectric catalysis, the chemical catalytic activity driven by oxygen vacancies may also play a crucial role. Nanomaterials with abundant oxygen vacancies exhibit significantly reduced oxygen adsorption energy, indicating that oxygen vacancies can serve as catalytic centers to enhance oxygen adsorption strength. Furthermore, the activation energy barrier for O2 dissociation reactions is markedly lowered in the presence of oxygen vacancies. Consequently, oxygen vacancies facilitate electron transfer from the d-orbitals of cations to O2, thereby promoting the generation of ROS in a manner analogous to the function of natural oxidases.

Extensive efforts have focused on narrowing the bandgap of BaTiO3 and enhancing carrier separation, including strategies such as oxygen vacancy formation, elemental doping, and heterojunction construction. Semiconductor hydrogenation offers broad-spectrum absorption and intrinsic bandgap reduction; however, conventional hydrogenation requires high-pressure/high-temperature conditions and substantial H2 supply due to the sluggish interaction between hydrogen molecules and metal oxides. Zhou [91] developed a palladium-catalyzed hydrogenation strategy to reduce BTO's bandgap and enable subsequent multienzyme-piezoelectric synergistic therapy (Fig. 9a and b). Pd incorporation not only lowered the hydrogenation temperature but also narrowed the intrinsic bandgap from 2.98 eV to 2.33 eV, facilitating e−-h+ separation and reducing recombination rates (Fig. 9c). Furthermore, Pd exhibits dual enzyme-mimetic properties, including peroxidase- and catalase-like activities, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes through amplified ROS generation and hypoxia reversal in tumor microenvironments. Beyond palladium, strontium doping has also been employed in barium strontium titanate (Ba1-xSrxTiO3, BST) piezoelectric nanoparticles. Zhao [92] constructed a robust sonodynamic and gas cancer therapeutic platform based on L-arginine-functionalized strontium-doped BST piezoelectric nanoparticles (Fig. 9d and e). Substituting smaller-radius Sr2+ (rSr = 1.13 Å) for Ba2+ (rBa = 1.35 Å) in the A-site of ABO3 piezoelectric nanocrystals exacerbates lattice asymmetry. This modification introduces oxygen vacancies, enhances intrinsic piezoelectricity, narrows the semiconductor bandgap (3.3 eV→3.0 eV), and improves carrier migration—collectively promoting sonodynamic generation of •O2− and •OH (Fig. 9f). Under ultrasonic excitation, BST demonstrates 1.9-fold higher catalytic efficiency in •OH generation compared to undoped BTO.

The incorporation of dopant ions into piezoelectric materials induces lattice distortion, thereby narrowing the bandgap and promoting more efficient charge separation. Concurrently, the introduction of specific transition metal ions (e.g., Fe, Mn, Ce) [42,48,92,93] endows piezoelectric catalytic materials with Fenton/Fenton-like catalytic activity potential. This enables the utilization of overexpressed H2O2 at tumor and wound sites via Fenton/Fenton-like reactions, generating cytotoxic •OH. As prominent members of the Aurivillius oxide family, bismuth molybdate (Bi2MoO6) [26], bismuth tungstate (Bi2WO6) [93] and bismuth titanate (Bi4Ti3O12) [47] have demonstrated exceptional photocatalytic performance due to their suitable bandgaps and robust piezoelectric properties.

However, applications of these nanosheets for cancer SDT remain scarcely reported. Given their appropriate bandgaps, strong piezoelectricity, and unique two-dimensional structures, doping transition metals into nanosheets enables efficient SDT through rational regulation of carrier transport, full exposure of Fenton reaction active sites, and significant bandgap narrowing. Herein, Li et al. [93] successfully synthesized Fenton-active iron-doped oxygen-deficient bismuth tungstate nanosheets (BWO-Fe NSs) for enhanced sonodynamic therapy of breast cancer (Fig. 10a). BWO-Fe NSs were synthesized via a facile one-pot hydrothermal method, followed by surface modification with polyethylene glycol (BWO-Fe/PEG) to enhance dispersibility and biocompatibility (Fig. 10b). Huang [48] designed and engineered atomically thin iron-doped ultrathin barium titanate nanosheets (Fe-UBTO NSs), exhibiting synergistic piezo-chemodynamic catalytic capabilities for cancer treatment via apoptosis and autophagy pathways, as well as bacterial eradication for wound healing (Fig. 10d–f). The introduction of transition metal ions induced oxygen vacancies and lattice distortion, narrowing the UBTO bandgap from 3.36 eV to 3.16 eV (Fig. 10g). Oxygen vacancies not only act as charge trapping centers to prevent electron-hole recombination but also serve as catalytic active sites for O2 molecule adsorption, enhancing their participation in ROS generation. These advantages enable Fe-UBTO NSs to augment piezocatalytic 1O2 and H2O2 production. Furthermore, the multivalent Fe ions doped into BWO and UBTO NSs possess unique Fenton catalytic activity, decomposing overexpressed H2O2 in pathological microenvironments to generate •OH. Critically, Fe doping establishes a synergistic piezo-chemodynamic catalytic system, enhancing Fenton reactions through self-generated H2O2 and accelerating electron transfer rates via piezoelectric catalysis. Additionally, the engineered ultrathin structure and enlarged surface area enhance sensitivity to US stimulation while exposing more surface oxygen vacancies and Fe active sites for piezoelectric catalysis and Fenton reactions. Ultimately, this synergistic piezo-chemodynamic catalytic system results in significantly elevated ROS production, disrupting redox homeostasis and promoting apoptosis and autophagy.

As one of the few materials exhibiting room-temperature single-phase multiferroicity, bismuth ferrite (BiFeO3, BFO) belongs to an important class of piezoelectric materials. However, as a piezoelectric catalyst, BFO suffers from relatively low piezoelectric performance. Additionally, its CB potential is approximately 0.33 V, which is more positive than the redox potential of O2/•O2− (−0.13 V vs. NHE). This limits its performance in ROS generation. Currently, common methods for tuning energy levels and band structures include doping, semiconductor compounding, surface modification, and solid solution formation. Among these, the solid solution formation method can form new VB and CB by coupling the valence and conduction bands of two semiconductors with different band structures. This technique enables simultaneous regulation of the material's bandgap and band edge positions, offering broader technical space and application prospects. Therefore, researchers have paid significant attention to it in recent years. Yu et al. [42] introduced cerium (Ce) doping into bismuth titanate (Fig. 10h–j), and the resulting product, CBFO, generated a piezoelectric potential that amplified redox reactions, enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of chemodynamic therapy (CDT), and thus promoted the generation of ROS. Furthermore, due to the excellent CDT performance of cerium ions, a higher Ce doping ratio leads to increased CDT efficiency. Ce doping inhibits the recombination of electrons and holes, thereby improving the electrical conductivity and catalytic activity of CBFO (Fig. 10k).

In piezoelectric catalysts, barium titanate has a conduction band edge potential of approximately −0.5 V and exhibits excellent piezoelectric performance. By forming a solid solution (BiFeO3-BaTiO3) [49] between BFO and BTO, composite materials with both suitable piezoelectric properties and tunable band structures can be obtained, which is energetically favorable for the simultaneous generation of O2 and ROS. Beyond its role as a piezoelectric catalyst, BFO also possesses extremely high Fenton catalytic activity, making it applicable for cancer therapy. CDT utilizes Fenton or Fenton-like catalysts to convert overexpressed H2O2 in the tumor microenvironment (TME) into •OH. Consequently, BFO can naturally achieve synergistic SDT and CDT.

Designing and synthesizing "customized" piezoelectric materials according to tumor treatment needs (such as O2 generation, different ROS types, and hydrogen production) represents an emerging multifunctional tumor treatment strategy. Methods like solid solution formation, doping, and semiconductor compounding can be used to tailor the band structures of piezoelectric materials, endowing them with pre-defined functions instead of simply adopting traditional piezoelectric materials [[94], [95], [96], [97], [98], [99]]. This band structure customization strategy allows for theoretical prediction of material functions, meeting the practical needs of tumor treatment to achieve more efficient and targeted therapeutic effects. This significantly expands the application of piezoelectric nanomaterials in the biomedical field.

In addition to the aforementioned classical perovskite materials, researchers have recently explored the applications of defect-engineered (K, Na)NbO3 (KNN) in the field of tumor diagnosis and therapy. Wang et al. prepared KNN nanocrystals with abundant oxygen vacancies by combining ball milling and solid-state sintering [100]. The combination of ultrasound-triggered piezoelectric catalysis and oxygen vacancy-driven free radical catalysis achieved efficient therapeutic effects on osteosarcoma. KNN can induce apoptosis and autophagy of osteosarcoma cells. In terms of the material itself, KNN has shown good histocompatibility in mouse models.

Defect engineering of bismuth-based 2D piezoelectric nanomaterials

Bismuth-based 2D nanomaterials (BiOCl [[59], [60], [61], [62], [63], [64]], BiO2-x [67], CaBi2Nb2O9 [66]) possess a layered structure and exhibit a high degree of structural anisotropy. This structural characteristic renders them prone to deformation under mechanical stress, thereby generating a piezoelectric effect.

The general chemical formula for bismuth oxyhalide is BiOX, characterized by a crystal structure composed of alternating [Bi2O2]2+ layers and halide ion layers (Cl−, Br−, I−), forming a highly anisotropic layered architecture (Fig. 11a). This structural configuration imparts piezoelectric properties: when subjected to an electric field or mechanical stress, crystal deformation occurs, inducing a relative displacement of positive and negative charge centers and resulting in polarization. On one hand, the piezoelectric catalytic process mediated by BiOCl nanocatalysts generates free radicals and H2O2 under ultrasonic stimulation. On the other hand, H2O2 simultaneously acts as both a catalytic substrate and an electron scavenger in this system, ultimately accelerating the subsequent production of •OH during BiOCl-mediated radiation catalysis (Fig. 11c). Through this synergistic interaction of dual catalytic mechanisms, BiOCl nanocatalysts demonstrate remarkable combined piezoelectric-radiation catalytic therapeutic efficacy.

Defect engineering, as a critical modification strategy in the field of catalytic materials, plays a pivotal role in enhancing the catalytic sensitivity of two-dimensional bismuth-based piezoelectric nanomaterials. Oxygen vacancies, acting as charge traps, further strengthen the separation efficiency of electrons and holes. Heteroatom doping introduces intermediate energy levels, modulates surface polarity, and alters crystal symmetry, thereby narrowing the material bandgap and increasing the piezoelectric coefficient. To further improve therapeutic outcomes, researchers have combined oxygen vacancy modification with heteroatom introduction (e.g., Fe or Cu) in piezoelectric catalytic materials, integrating piezoelectric catalysis with Fenton-mediated chemodynamic therapy. Additionally, functional modifications such as coating with Tween-20 or peptides optimize piezoelectric materials to achieve synergy between piezoelectric catalysis and microenvironmental responsiveness [60].

The crystal structure of CaBi2Nb2O9 consists of alternating [Bi2O2]2+ layers and [CaNb2O7]2- layers stacked along the c-axis, forming a typical bismuth layered structure (BLSFs). This layered arrangement results in a highly anisotropic crystal lattice with unit cell parameters of a≈b ~ 5.4 Å and c ~ 25.0 Å, indicating significantly larger interlayer spacing along the c-axis compared to the a and b axes. This structural anisotropy is further emphasized by the material's ferroelectric properties, which exhibit pronounced anisotropy with spontaneous polarization predominantly oriented along the a-axis. External electric field polarization treatment can further augment the net polarization intensity and enhance the piezoelectric response.

Ultrathin CaBi2Nb2O9 nanosheets with tunable oxygen vacancies (OVs) demonstrate optimized OV concentrations that markedly enhance piezoelectric effects under ultrasonic irradiation, leading to substantially elevated ROS generation. The synergistic action of ROS and Ca2+ collectively induces necroptosis in tumor cells.

Defect engineering of TMDs piezoelectric nanomaterials

The application basis of TMDs in the field of tumor piezoelectric catalysis has garnered extensive attention. Transition metal sulfides (e.g., MoS2, SnS2, Ni3S2, WS2) [[56], [57], [58],101], characterized by non-centrosymmetric crystal structures, can generate piezoelectric potentials under mechanical stress, driving the separation of e−-h+ pairs. This piezoelectric effect enables the conversion of mechanical energy into electrical energy, thereby providing the driving force for catalytic reactions. Modifications of TMDs often involve heterovalent substitution (e.g., Cu2+-doped SnS2) [56] to introduce sulfur vacancies, narrow the material's band gap, and enhance carrier separation efficiency. Alternatively, single-atom doping strategies are employed to redistribute charges, amplify piezoelectric polarization, and confer multifunctional enzyme-mimetic activities, such as peroxidase- and glutathione oxidase-like activities, achieving synergistic catalysis. Further optimization of high-surface-area two-dimensional nanosheet structures through morphological and interfacial design enhances the exposure of active edges, facilitating ultrasound energy absorption and ROS generation. Additionally, core/shell or heterostructured architectures, leveraging bimetallic synergies or functional macromolecular coatings, represent viable approaches to further optimize catalytic activity.

Ma [56] constructed an ultrathin two-dimensional sulfur vacancy-engineered Cu@SnS2-x nanosheet with enhanced piezoelectric effect through a heterovalent substitution strategy involving Cu2+ replacing Sn4+ (Fig. 14a and b). The incorporation of Cu2+ ions not only augmented polarization effects through crystal structural modifications but also generated abundant sulfur vacancies (Fig. 14c), narrowing the band gap from 2.16 eV to 1.62 eV and suppressing e−-h+) pair recombination. These collective effects enabled highly efficient generation of ROS under ultrasonic irradiation (Fig. 14d and e). Furthermore, Cu@SnS2-x exhibited ultrasound-enhanced TME-responsive Fenton-like catalytic activity and GSH depletion capability, exacerbating oxidative stress. Both in vitro and in vivo experimental results demonstrate that Sv-engineered Cu@SnS2-x nanosheets can significantly kill tumor cells in a biocompatible manner while achieving high-efficiency piezocatalytic tumor therapy. Optimization of piezocatalytic systems via heterojunction interface engineering

When mechanical stress is applied to piezoelectric materials, a piezoelectric potential is generated, leading to charge separation. The presence of a heterojunction interface creates a built-in electric field at the interface due to the band offset between different materials, which optimizes the separation and transport of interfacial charges and further enhances this process. Meanwhile, interface engineering can improve the structural stability of heterojunction materials, ensuring no performance degradation during long-term catalytic processes.

Heterojunctions exhibit excellent energy band structures by forming interfaces between two or more different materials. The built-in electric field generated at the interface facilitates the efficient separation of electrons and holes and suppresses their recombination. The piezoelectric polarization electric field induced by external mechanical stress can synergize with the built-in electric field of the heterojunction, providing an additional driving force for carrier separation and thereby enhancing its catalytic performance during long-term catalytic processes [[102], [103], [104], [105]].

Piezoelectric catalytic materials may undergo structural fatigue under prolonged mechanical vibration or stress, leading to performance degradation. The stability of heterojunctions can be enhanced through methods such as strengthening interfacial bonding and composite material design. By optimizing the interfacial structure and material selection, interfacial stress can be regulated to prevent structural damage caused by stress concentration [106,107]. For instance, employing p-n junction engineering at the interface can influence the piezoelectric properties of ZnO nanorods and reduce their inherent screening effect by modulating carrier concentration [108].

Heterojunction construction of perovskite-based piezoelectric nanomaterials

For pure BTO nanocrystals, the VB edge at 3.03 V is more positive than the redox potential of the H2O/•OH couple (2.01 V vs. RHE), indicating that US-induced holes in the VB can react with H2O to generate •OH radicals. The relative energy level difference (ΔE) between the band edges and redox potentials serves as a critical determinant of reaction activity, where a larger ΔE ensures enhanced electron transfer capability and chemical reactivity. However, the reduction potential of the O2/•O2− couple (−0.28 V vs. RHE) is slightly higher than the CB energy level of BTO NCs (−0.2 V), rendering the energetic conditions for •O2− generation unfavorable. Upon US irradiation, BTO nanocrystals generate a piezoelectric potential estimated at approximately 0.18 V, inducing band bending. This piezoelectrically driven band bending shifts the CB edge to a more negative potential than the O2/•O2− redox potential, enabling BTO nanocrystals to catalyze •O2− formation. Nevertheless, rapid recombination of electron-hole pairs in pure BTO NCs remains a key factor limiting ROS generation efficiency. A heterojunction is an interface formed between two or more distinct materials (e.g., semiconductors, conductors, or insulators), characterized by disparities in energy band structures and synergistic effects of interfacial electric fields. At the heterojunction interface, the energy band structures (conduction band and valence band) of the two materials are misaligned, creating a built-in electric field. This field drives the migration of photogenerated or piezoelectrically induced electron-hole pairs toward different materials, thereby reducing recombination losses. The interfacial electric field of heterojunctions enables efficient separation of electrons and holes, minimizing recombination probabilities. The synergistic interaction between piezoelectric fields and heterojunction electric fields achieves sustained charge separation, underscoring the significant value of heterojunction interface engineering in modifying the performance of piezocatalytic materials.

In the Cu2-xO-BTO heterojunction (Fig. 15a–f), both BaTiO3 and Cu2-xO are excited under US irradiation, enabling efficient electron-hole pair separation [109]. Electrons and holes in the CB and VB of BaTiO3 migrate toward Cu2-xO due to its lower energy levels, effectively suppressing recombination. The d33 of BaTiO3 NCs and Cu2-xO-BTO-1.0 nanocomposites were measured via characteristic amplitude butterfly loops, yielding d33 values of approximately 4.77 p.m./V and 6.32 p.m./V, respectively (Fig. 15g). This indicates a slight enhancement in piezoelectric response for Cu2-xO-BTO compared to pristine BaTiO3. Finite element method simulations revealed that under US mechanical vibration (1.0 MHz, 1.0 W/cm2), the maximum piezoelectric potential across the Cu2-xO-BTO nanocrystal reaches 0.18 V, sufficient to induce band bending and energetically favor •OH and •O2− radical generation. To evaluate the carrier separation efficiency, time-dependent photocurrents of different samples were measured during a simulated US-triggered process. Under full-spectrum light illumination, the photocurrent of Cu2-xO-BTO composites gradually increased with the rising content of Cu2-xO, and all Cu2-xO-BTO samples exhibited higher photocurrents than that of BTO (Fig. 15h). Notably, the Cu2-xO-BTO-1.0 sample achieved a photocurrent density of 0.3 μA/cm2, which was three times higher than that of pure BTO (0.1 μA/cm2), indicating superior carrier separation performance in the Cu2-xO-BTO-1.0 heterostructure. Importantly, ultrasound (1.0 MHz, 1.0 W/cm2) was also capable of triggering current generation, with a current density (0.4 μA/cm2) comparable to that under light illumination (Fig. 15i). This confirmed that US irradiation could promote the migration and separation of carriers. When subjected to simultaneous light and US irradiation, the current density of the Cu2-xO-BTO-1.0 sample reached 0.58 μA/cm2. Furthermore, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy studies on the transport of e− and h+ at the heterojunction interface reveal that, with the increasing content of Cu2-xO in the Cu2-xO-BTO samples, the arc diameter gradually decreases and becomes smaller than that of pure barium titanate. Heterojunction construction of wurtzite-based piezoelectric nanomaterials

While wurtzite piezoelectric materials are less widely utilized than perovskites in commercial applications, the wurtzite structure forms an AB-type hexagonal crystal system characterized by tetrahedral coordination geometry. In this structure, both A-site and B-site atoms independently form hexagonal close-packed (hcp) sublattices, which subsequently interpenetrate to construct the wurtzite framework. This structure exhibits non-centrosymmetry. When atoms are arranged along the c-axis, spontaneous polarization is formed, meaning an intrinsic electric field exists inside the material. Upon the application of mechanical stress, this structure generates a macroscopic electric dipole moment and piezoelectric charges, thereby inducing a piezoelectric field with separated positive and negative charges on the material surface. ZnO nanostructures exhibit a wide bandgap (≈3.3 eV) at room temperature and generate electron-hole pairs under UV irradiation [110]. These charge carriers participate in redox reactions with surrounding water and oxygen molecules in aqueous suspension, producing cytotoxic ROS [111]. Consequently, ZnO nanostructures demonstrate potential as photosensitizers for selective cancer cell damage [112]. However, the high recombination rate of photogenerated electron-hole pairs in ZnO-based photosensitizers limits photonic energy utilization efficiency [113]. To address this limitation, leveraging piezoelectric effect of ZnO to establish an internal electric field for reducing carrier recombination rates may enhance PDT efficacy. Applied mechanical deformation induces polarization in non-centrosymmetric wurtzite ZnO, generating piezoelectric potentials across opposite crystal faces [114,115]. The internal piezoelectric field directs carrier migration, thereby suppressing recombination probability [116]. Consequently, rational structural modulation to amplify piezocatalytic effect of ZnO while synergizing with other therapeutic modalities has emerged as a focal area of investigation for achieving enhanced tumor ablation efficacy.

Given the unique advantage of the concurrent photocatalytic of ZnO, piezoelectric, and piezocatalytic properties, this material system holds promise for simultaneous activation of dual catalytic processes and development of novel coupled catalytic mechanisms. Beyond its piezoelectric and photocatalytic capabilities, ZnO nanostructures have received safety certification from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration as biomedical materials. Chen et al. [50] proposed a battery-free wireless therapeutic dot platform employing piezo-potentiated PDT for enhanced tumor ablation efficacy (Fig. 17a and b). This platform utilizes ZnO-based nanocomposites composed of gold nanoparticles, zinc oxide nanorod, and hydrophilic polyethylene glyco to synergistically enhance ROS generation and suppress tumor growth. The therapeutic dot features a compact 3 mm radius design enabling direct implantation at targeted tumor sites. Wireless operation is achieved through ultrasound-activated piezoelectric energy conversion, delivering therapeutic light doses without external power sources (Fig. 17c). This dual-activation mechanism enables electron-hole pair generation through combined ultrasonic and UV stimulation. Preclinical evaluations in murine lung cancer models demonstrated superior therapeutic outcomes compared to PDT or SDT monotherapies. Ultrasound penetration through 5 cm of biological tissue and enhanced UV light delivery were achieved using miniaturized wireless, battery-free implantable devices. As an n-type semiconductor with 3.37 eV bandgap energy, ZnO exhibits significantly improved charge carrier generation efficiency under combined thermal and optical excitation. This bimodal stimulation paradigm induces massive ROS production in aqueous environments. Under piezoelectric fields generated by mechanical strain in ZnO nanorods, directional carrier drift occurs: electrons migrate to positively polarized surfaces while holes transport to negatively polarized counterparts. The positive piezo-potential mitigates conduction band bending, reducing energy barriers for electron participation in ROS-generating reactions. Concurrently, negative piezo-potential enhances valence band edge bending, energizing holes for active ROS production (Fig. 17d). Additionally, reduced piezoelectric barriers enable electron transfer from ZnO NR conduction bands through Au NPs, effectively suppressing electron-hole recombination. Heterojunction construction of MOFs piezoelectric nanomaterials

UIO-66 has been successfully utilized in piezoelectric catalytic water splitting for hydrogen production and in enhancing photocatalytic activity through piezoelectric effects. Heterojunction construction of 2D inorganic non-metallic-based piezoelectric nanomaterials

Over the past decades, 2D materials such as graphene, transition metal carbides/carbonitrides (MXene), and their derivatives have demonstrated significantly enhanced ROS scavenging activity [25,27]. This enhancement is attributed to their large surface-to-volume ratios, exceptional electron transfer capabilities, abundant edge/defect sites, and electrophilic basal planes. The ROS scavenging activity is facilitated through mechanisms including free radical conjugation, electron transfer at edge/defect sites, and hydrogen capture, making these materials potential candidates for maintaining antioxidant balance. Notably, vanadium carbide MXene nanosheets (V2C) have been reported to exhibit multiple intrinsic enzyme-mimetic activities, such as superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, and catalase, which effectively eliminate ROS species including H2O2, O2−•, and •OH. These properties endow V2C with significant potential for scavenging excessive ROS post-SDT and mitigating subsequent ROS-induced inflammation. Despite its remarkable ROS scavenging capability, V2C's ability to generate ROS under ultrasonic stimulation for antimicrobial efficacy remains insufficient, rendering it inadequate as a standalone "ROS modulator". Consequently, functionalizing V2C to enable sufficient ROS generation under ultrasonic excitation is crucial for achieving efficient bactericidal effects.

Geng [27] developed an intelligent macrophage-mimetic nanocatalytic membrane (Fig. 19e) via a simple hydrothermal synthesis approach. This membrane, designed as a novel wound dressing, integrates electrospun poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) scaffolds with two-dimensional black phosphorus/vanadium carbide (BP/V2C) bio-heterojunctions (2D2-bioHJs) (Fig. 19d). Leveraging a "ROS modulation" strategy, this system achieves treatment of drug-resistant infections and promotion of cutaneous regeneration. The incorporation of bio-heterojunctions within the 2D-2D architecture enables efficient catalytic generation of ROS under ultrasonic stimulation (Fig. 19f). Initially, ultrasound-excited electron transfer from black phosphorus to V2C MXene is enhanced, facilitating the separation of negatively charged electrons and positively charged holes and inhibiting electron transport chain activity on bacterial membranes. This process simultaneously induces rapid ROS generation and effective bacterial inactivation. Upon cessation of ultrasonic irradiation, the enzyme-mimetic 2D2-bioHJs scavenge excessive ROS induced by SDT and bacterial activity on the wound surface, thereby reducing inflammatory responses and promoting skin repair. Systematic in vitro and in vivo evaluations of cytocompatibility and wound healing efficacy have validated the exceptional potential of this nanocatalytic membrane in addressing persistent bacterial-infected wounds, offering a novel paradigm for the advancement of therapeutic wound dressing technologies.

Polarization modification of piezoelectric nano-polymer

Inorganic piezoelectric materials, such as barium titanate or zinc oxide, while exhibiting relatively high piezoelectric coefficients, raise concerns regarding their biocompatibility and biodegradability when applied in vivo. In contrast, organic piezoelectric polymers, such as poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) and its copolymer poly(vinylidene fluoride-trifluoroethylene) (P(VDF-TrFE)), despite having slightly inferior piezoelectric properties, present a promising alternative due to their excellent biocompatibility, greater ease of control, and processability [69]. Pure P(VDF-TrFE) thin films and their composites, when combined with appropriate ultrasonic stimulation, can successfully induce cellular stimulation and differentiation.

However, a significant challenge in the field of organic piezoelectric nanomaterial development lies in enabling these organic piezoelectric materials to self-assemble into small organic nanoparticles while maintaining or enhancing their piezoelectric output characteristics, without relying on other inorganic materials to boost performance. P(VDF-TrFE) is a copolymer of vinylidene fluoride (VDF) and trifluoroethylene (TrFE). The strong electronegativity of the fluorine atoms in its molecular chains endows the material with spontaneous polarization capability [22,68]. By adjusting the copolymerization ratio (e.g., 70 % VDF and 30 % TrFE) and processing techniques (such as annealing and electric field polarization), its crystallinity and the content of the polar β-phase, which are crucial determinants of piezoelectric performance, can be optimized.

When the material is subjected to mechanical stress (e.g., ultrasonic vibrations), changes in the orientation of internal dipoles lead to charge separation, generating an electric potential difference. This piezoelectric effect can modulate the cell membrane potential, triggering an influx of Ca2+ and ultimately inducing apoptosis through a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential. Additionally, the ROS generated by piezoelectric catalysis can further exacerbate cellular damage, enhancing the anti-tumor effect.

In recent years, the potential of P(VDF-TrFE) in bone repair by regulating its surface potential to promote osteoblast differentiation has been explored [71,126]. Moreover, its flexible piezoelectric properties have been extended to applications in self-powered electrical stimulators, wound dressings, and other fields.

Ultrasoundactivated piezoelectric nanoparticles for medicine

In the field of biomedical diagnosis and therapy, piezoelectric nanomaterials exhibit antitumor effects through multidimensional mechanisms—including membrane perforation, apoptotic regulation, drug delivery enhancement, oxidative stress induction, epigenetic modulation, and catalytic gas generation by virtue of their advantages such as remotely activatable properties, superior ultrasound penetration depth compared to near-infrared light, and absence of systemic toxicity. They enable precise intervention in various solid tumors, including lung cancer, breast cancer, osteosarcoma, melanoma and glioma, and have demonstrated dose-dependent therapeutic efficacy in animal models. Specifically, some materials modified via doping engineering or heterostructure construction can further enhance therapeutic outcomes by synergizing with immunomodulation, chemotherapy, and other approaches, while maintaining excellent biosafety.

In terms of wound healing, piezoelectric nanomaterials accelerate the repair of acute and chronic wounds through complex mechanisms, such as electrical stimulation, regulation of cellular behavior, modulation of inflammatory responses, and antibacterial activity. Among them, flexible piezoelectric materials are more adaptable to the mechanical demands of physiological activities. Piezoelectric nanomaterials possess high medical application prospects and research value.

Mechanisms and applications of piezoelectric nanoparticles in tumor therapy

In the field of cancer therapy, piezoelectric nanoparticles exhibit considerable application potential due to their unique combination of properties. These advantages not only address certain bottlenecks of traditional therapeutic approaches but also provide a new direction for precision cancer treatment. First, their remotely activatable property breaks the reliance of traditional therapies on invasive procedures without the need for surgical implantation or direct contact with tumor tissues, their therapeutic function can be triggered solely by external physical stimulation. This significantly reduces the risk of trauma and infection during treatment and improves patients’ treatment tolerance. Second, compared with commonly used activation methods such as NIR light, piezoelectric nanoparticles rely on ultrasound, which offers superior penetration depth. This enables effective targeting of solid tumors in deep tissues and avoids the attenuation of therapeutic efficiency caused by the absorption of NIR light by tissues like skin and fat, thus providing a feasible approach for non-invasive treatment of deep-seated tumors. Furthermore, their lack of systemic toxicity is crucial for ensuring treatment safety: unlike some chemotherapeutic drugs or inorganic nanocarriers that tend to accumulate in normal tissues and induce toxic side effects, piezoelectric nanoparticles, under rational design and regulation, can specifically target tumor regions to exert their effects. They have minimal impact on normal cells and organs throughout the body, significantly reducing the incidence of treatment-related adverse reactions. Meanwhile, piezoelectric nanoparticles also possess strong synergistic integration capabilities, which further expand their application value. By combining inorganic piezoelectric materials with ultrasound activation technology and tumor-specific targeting technology, a multimodal therapeutic system can be constructed: ultrasound not only serves as the "energy source" for activating piezoelectric nanoparticles but also helps enhance their penetration and accumulation in tumor tissues; tumor-specific targeting technology (e.g., target receptor modification) can guide nanoparticles to accurately recognize and bind to tumor cells, reducing the unintended damage to normal cells.

Anticancer mechanisms of piezoelectric nanomaterials

The therapeutic effects of piezoelectric nanoparticles on cancer stem from their multidimensional and synergistic biological mechanisms [11]. These mechanisms collectively inhibit tumor growth and metastasis from multiple levels, including cell structure, signaling pathways, and microenvironment regulation, and can be specifically categorized into three types: direct killing of tumor cells, regulation of cell behavior, and improvement of the tumor microenvironment.

In terms of direct killing of tumor cells, piezoelectric nanoparticles act through two pathways: First, the membrane perforation effect. Under external mechanical stimulation, the mechanical stress generated by piezoelectric nanoparticles can directly disrupt the integrity of tumor cell membranes, leading to the leakage of intracellular contents and subsequent induction of cytotoxic effects, thereby achieving direct elimination of tumor cells. Second, the oxidative stress induction effect. A large amount of ROS are generated during the piezoelectric process. These ROS can cause oxidative damage to biological macromolecules such as DNA, proteins, and lipids in tumor cells, disrupt normal cellular physiological functions, and ultimately result in cell death [125]. At the same time, they can also disturb the redox balance in the tumor microenvironment, further impairing the viability of tumor cells.

In the aspect of regulating tumor cell behavior, the mechanisms of piezoelectric nanoparticles exhibit characteristics of precision and multi-targeting. Through the apoptosis regulation effect, they activate the intrinsic apoptotic pathway of cells (e.g., the p53-mediated mitochondrial apoptotic pathway). This pathway can precisely initiate the programmed death process of tumor cells, avoiding non-selective damage to normal cells and thus realizing precise regulation of tumor proliferation [64,67]. Via the epigenetic regulation effect, they target and interfere with the gene expression profile of tumor cells—by influencing the expression of genes related to the malignant phenotype of cells (e.g., oncogenes, metastasis-related genes), they reprogram the malignant phenotype of tumor cells and inhibit their proliferation and invasion capabilities. Using their own mechano-electrical transduction capability, they regulate the dynamic process of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) —EMT is a key link for tumor cells to acquire migration and invasion abilities. The electrical signals generated by piezoelectric nanoparticles can interfere with EMT-related signaling pathways, preventing tumor cells from transitioning from the epithelial phenotype to the mesenchymal phenotype, thereby inhibiting cancer cell migration and distant metastasis. Additionally, at the molecular level, piezoelectric nanoparticles can disrupt calcium homeostasis, causing tumor cells to arrest in the G0/G1 phase of the cell cycle and preventing them from entering the division stage. Simultaneously, they upregulate the expression of Kir3.2 inward rectifier potassium channels, affecting intracellular ion balance and further inhibiting tumor cell proliferation.

In the improvement of the tumor microenvironment and enhancement of therapeutic synergy, piezoelectric nanoparticles can also work in conjunction with gas catalytic generation and drug loading [118,127,128]. The gas catalytic generation effect refers to their use of charges produced during the piezoelectric process to catalytically generate gas molecules with anti-tumor activity (e.g., NO, H2S, H2): these gas molecules can not only cut off the nutrient supply of tumors by inhibiting angiogenesis and disrupting tumor blood vessels but also regulate the immune response in the tumor microenvironment (e.g., promoting immune cell infiltration). Meanwhile, they can reduce treatment-related microenvironment damage through antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, indirectly improving therapeutic efficacy; the drug delivery enhancement effect is reflected in their function as intelligent drug carriers—through surface modification or structural design, piezoelectric nanoparticles can efficiently load therapeutic molecules such as chemotherapeutic drugs and gene drugs. After targeted delivery to tumor regions, they release these molecules precisely, which not only increases the intracellular accumulation of drugs but also significantly reduces the off-target effects of drugs on normal tissues, realizing the synergistic enhancement of "therapeutic agent delivery + piezoelectric therapy".

These biological mechanisms do not exist in isolation but are mutually synergistic and reinforcing. Together, they form an efficient and precise anti-tumor system of piezoelectric nanoparticles, laying a solid theoretical foundation for their clinical translation in cancer therapy.

Their mechano-electrical transduction capability precisely regulates epithelial-mesenchymal transition dynamics, inhibiting cancer cell migration. Synergistic integration of inorganic piezoelectric materials with ultrasound activation and tumor-specific targeting enables multimodal intervention in solid tumors (e.g., lung, breast, glioblastoma) (Fig. 21). In triple-negative breast cancer, exogenous mechanical stimulation modulates piezoelectric nanoparticle-generated electric fields to suppress MDA-MB-231 cell migration. This electrical perturbation disrupts cytoskeletal architecture while reversing metastatic phenotypes through osteoglycin upregulation and focal adhesion kinase (FAK)/β1-integrin downregulation [129]. Animal studies confirm dose-dependent antitumor efficacy across osteosarcoma, colorectal cancer, and glioblastoma models, with enhanced performance when combined with temozolomide.

For brain tumors, transferrin receptor (TfR)-targeted barium titanate nanoparticles achieve triple functionality: blood-brain barrier penetration, glioma cell targeting, and remote electrical modulation. Ciofani et al. [130] demonstrated significant glioma proliferation inhibition via ultrasound-mediated piezo-stimulation, with synergistic effects when paired with subtoxic temozolomide doses. This mechano-electrical coupling further disrupts mitotic spindle assembly, halting malignant proliferation at the organelle level.

Application of piezoelectric nanomaterials in the treatment of melanoma

Piezoelectric materials function as "energy transducers" that drive charge separation and ROS generation through mechanical stimulation. By integrating multiple approaches such as electroporation and microenvironment modulation, they achieve precise and synergistic killing of melanoma cells, while also providing a novel non-invasive therapeutic paradigm for melanoma treatment. Skin wounds naturally experience mechanical movements like stretching and compression during daily activities. Piezoelectric catalytic technology can "harness these natural forces" by converting these physiological activities into therapeutic energy without requiring additional invasive procedures. The low-concentration ROS generated by piezoelectric materials under mechanical stimulation can directly eliminate bacteria and fungi on wound surfaces, reducing healing delays caused by infections. Unlike antibiotics, ROS exert their effects by disrupting microbial cell membranes and DNA, making resistance development less likely. Simultaneously, the piezoelectric potential (local weak electric field) can guide fibroblast and keratinocyte migration toward the wound center via electrotaxis, while activating cell proliferation-related signaling pathways to accelerate granulation tissue formation and epithelial regeneration. Electrical signals also enhance vascular endothelial cell migration and tube formation, stimulating neovascularization to improve local blood oxygen and nutrient supply, thereby addressing the "ischemic delay" issue in traditional healing processes. Additionally, they can regulate collagen synthesis and remodeling to minimize scar formation. Therefore, piezoelectric materials used for cutaneous melanoma treatment can simultaneously achieve multiple effects of "physical stimulation & antibacterial action & wound repair promotion" without requiring combination with multiple drugs or devices, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy.

Qu et al. [131] employed a doping engineering technique to incorporate manganese ions into hydroxyapatite (HAP) for enhanced piezoelectric catalytic performance, combined with exosome inhibition to achieve synergistic improvement in cancer therapy. The manganese-doped HAP was synthesized via a one-pot hydrothermal method and subsequently modified with ROS-cleavable DSPE-TK-mPEG lipid. During the modification process, the exosome inhibitor GW4869 was loaded, forming GW4869-loaded manganese-doped HAP lipid nanocomposites (GMHL) (Fig. 22a). The introduction of manganese ions significantly reduced the bandgap of HAP, thereby enhancing its piezoelectric catalytic activity for ROS generation US stimulation. This triggered the cleavage of ketone-thiol bonds in DSPE-TK-mPEG, leading to effective release of GW4869. In animal experiments, a highly aggressive B16F10 melanoma tumor model was selected to evaluate the anti-tumor efficacy of this therapeutic approach. GMHL + US treatment induced a 10.3-fold increase in total leukocyte infiltration within tumor compared to control, indicative of enhanced immunogenicity. Meanwhile, GMHL + US treatment also augmented intratumoral effector CD8+-T cell populations, with a concomitant elevation in cytotoxic activity as evidenced by Granzyme B expression within this subset, confirming potentiation of adaptive antitumor immunity (Fig. 22b). In addition, innate antitumor immune engagement was evident through a 3.7- fold increase in tumor-infiltrating natural killer cells relative to control. The GMHL + US treatment could greatly enhance the intratumoral secretion of effector cytokines tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), as well as proinflammatory cytokine IL-1β. Application of piezoelectric nanomaterials in the treatment of breast cancer

Jing et al. [48] employed a doping engineering strategy to induce oxygen vacancies and lattice distortion in Fe-UBTO nanosheets (Fig. 22c). The subsequent gel-coating process significantly enhanced ROS generation, achieving a tumor inhibition rate of 76.2 %. The iron-superoxide dismutase (Fe-SOD) mediated piezo-chemocatalytic therapy induced intracellular oxidative stress, triggering apoptosis and excessive autophagy in tumor cells (Fig. 22d and e). Additionally, this strategy accelerated wound healing by promoting bactericidal effects, angiogenesis, and collagen deposition, offering a unique approach for developing doped ultrathin nanosheets with enhanced piezo-chemocatalytic activity. Wu et al. [132] constructed a novel Ir-C3N5 nanocomposite using C3N5 nanosheets as nanoligands and Ir(tpy)Cl3 complex as precursor. This nanocomposite demonstrated an exceptional tumor inhibition rate of 96.6 %.

Piezocatalytic therapy has demonstrated exceptional performance in breast cancer treatment. Ding et al. [93] synthesized Fenton-reactive, oxygen-deficient iron-doped Bi2WO6 nanosheets (BWO-Fe/PEG) that exhibited remarkable therapeutic efficacy against breast cancer. BWO-Fe/PEG demonstrated excellent blood compatibility, with a hemolysis rate of only 5 % at 800 μg/mL. In animal experiments, ultrasound treatment (1.0 MHz, 1.5 W/cm2) applied at specific frequencies to the affected area revealed that the BWO-Fe/PEG + US group achieved the highest tumor growth inhibition rate of 68.4 %, with no significant fibrosis or lesions observed in major organs post-treatment (Fig. 23a–e). Zhao et al. [109] developed Cu2O-BaTiO3 nanocubes (Cu2O-BTO NCs) as a heterostructured system, utilizing synergistic SDT and CDT to enhance cancer treatment outcomes. The tumor inhibition rate reached 76 % based on tumor weight calculations. Throughout the treatment period, no significant body weight changes were observed in any mice, indicating high biosafety. Pathological staining revealed the Cu2O-BTO + US group exhibited the most extensive tumor necrosis areas in residual tissues. [56] constructed sulfur vacancy-regulated 2D Cu@SnS2-x nanomaterials through heterovalent substitution, demonstrating a tumor growth inhibition rate as high as 89 %. Application of piezoelectric nanomaterials in the treatment of glioma

Glioma, an aggressive and prognostically unfavorable brain tumor, presents significant therapeutic challenges. The blood-brain barrier (BBB) impedes drug delivery from the bloodstream to the brain, limiting the efficacy of many chemotherapeutic agents. Additionally, glioma cells frequently develop resistance to chemotherapy, reducing treatment effectiveness. Even with combined surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, tumor recurrence remains common. By integrating piezoelectric materials with nanotechnology, ultrasound stimulation can temporarily disrupt the BBB, enabling magnetically guided targeted delivery of nanomedicines to glioblastoma sites. Under ultrasonic activation, nanoparticles perform piezocatalytic hydrolysis within the tumor interstitium, reducing fluid pressure and facilitating drug penetration into the tumor core, thereby enhancing local drug concentration. Piezoelectric materials exert antitumor effects through multiple mechanisms, including ROS-mediated cytotoxicity. Targeted drug delivery systems enable precise medication administration to tumor sites, minimizing damage to normal tissues and reducing systemic side effects [133].

Piezoelectric materials generate weak direct current (typically in the microvolt to millivolt range) under mechanical stimulation, disrupting tumor cell membrane potential equilibrium and interfering with intracellular calcium channels, mitochondrial function, and DNA repair mechanisms, ultimately inducing apoptosis (programmed cell death). Electrical signals can interfere with tumor cell signaling pathways (e.g., PI3K/Akt, MAPK), inhibiting proliferation, division, and invasiveness, thereby slowing tumor growth and metastasis. This approach also enables synergistic enhancement with chemotherapy/radiotherapy.

The local electric fields or micro-vibrations produced by piezoelectric materials under mechanical stimulation enhance tumor vascular permeability, facilitating chemotherapy drug penetration into tumor tissues. Additionally, piezoelectric nanoparticles serve as drug carriers for targeted delivery, reducing toxicity to normal cells. Yuan et al. [134] developed a dual-responsive nanomedicine by combining BTO with magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles (BF) to enhance intratumoral drug delivery for glioblastoma. Under ultrasound stimulation, these nanoparticles perform piezocatalytic hydrolysis within the tumor interstitium, reducing fluid pressure and enabling drug penetration into the tumor core. Upon reaching the tumor interior, the nanomedicine generates electrons via piezoelectric catalysis, reducing Fe3+ to Fe2+. The sustained Fe2+ activates a piezo-Fenton coupled catalytic system, producing abundant ROS to induce oxidative stress. This study proposed a three-step ultrasound/magnetic navigation and penetration strategy for glioma treatment, optimizing drug delivery paradigms (Fig. 24a–d). Electrical stimulation can modulate tumor cell oxidative stress levels or DNA damage repair capacity, enhance radiotherapy sensitivity and killing efficacy. The glioma microenvironment (e.g., hypoxia, inflammation, vascular abnormalities) promotes tumor progression. Electrical signals generated by piezoelectric materials improve regional blood circulation, alleviating hypoxia; suppress inflammatory cytokine release to reduce immunosuppressive microenvironment; and interfere with pro-angiogenic factors expression to inhibit neovascularization and nutrient supply.

Given the intracranial location of gliomas, surgical risks are high with challenges in complete resection. Piezoelectric materials enable energy conversion via external non-invasive stimulation (e.g., focused ultrasound, magnetic-field-driven mechanical vibration) without requiring implantable devices or craniotomy, minimizing damage to normal brain tissue and reducing infection/complication risks.

To enhance BBB penetration, Huang et al. [135] developed layered piezoelectric SrBi2Ta2O9 nanoparticles (SBTO NPs) that effectively depolarize glioma cell mitochondrial membrane potential under US irradiation (Fig. 24e–h). Ultrasound-induced belt-like bending of SBTO NPs enhances redox capacity, promoting ROS generation. In vitro studies demonstrate selective mitochondrial accumulation of SBTO NPs under US exposure, inducing apoptosis through ROS- and free charge-mediated mitochondrial depolarization. Additionally, SBTO NPs crosses the BBB via US/microbubble processes and protein-mediated transport, accumulating in gliomas. The therapeutic efficacy of piezoelectric SBTO NPs-mediated SDT was validated in orthotopic glioma mouse models, showing significant tumor reduction and therapeutic effectiveness.

Wound healing applications

The application of piezoelectric materials in wound healing primarily leverages their unique electromechanical conversion capability. By generating electrical signals to simulate and enhance the inherent bioelectric environment of living organisms, these materials promote cellular behavior and tissue regeneration [136,137].

An inherent bioelectric field exists within living organisms, playing a guiding role in tissue development, repair, and regeneration. After skin injury, the damaged epithelial barrier generates an endogenous electric field, which directs cells to migrate toward the center of the wound for repairing the damaged area. This endogenous electric field is crucial for coordinating cell migration and initiating the wound healing process. However, tissue defects caused by trauma disrupt the electrophysiological microenvironment of the injured area, impairing the natural healing ability. Piezoelectric materials can convert mechanical stimuli (such as body movement, respiration, etc.) into electrical signals, thereby providing continuous, self-powered electrical stimulation to the wound site. This simulation and enhancement of the damaged bioelectric field are particularly important for accelerating the healing of chronic and refractory wounds. The main mechanisms involved in this process include electrical stimulation, regulation of cellular behavior, modulation of inflammatory response, and antibacterial activity [[138], [139], [140], [141]].

Electrical stimulation can trigger cell migration, proliferation, and differentiation, effectively promoting the migration of fibroblasts to the wound bed. In articular cartilage regeneration, electrical stimulation facilitates chondrocyte proliferation and the synthesis of extracellular matrix molecules. Meanwhile, it plays a key role in the repair of skin injuries, including promoting collagen deposition and angiogenesis. Piezoelectric films have been observed to enhance collagen deposition and vascular regeneration by upregulating the PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby promoting skin wound healing. Neovascularization is a critical step in tissue repair and regeneration, as it supplies oxygen and nutrients to the healing tissue.

Electrical stimulation can also inhibit pro-inflammatory signals and reduce the risk of infection. Piezoelectric materials can induce the M2 polarization of macrophages, which contributes to anti-inflammation and tissue repair. By generating electrical signals, piezoelectric materials regulate cell proliferation and differentiation, which is essential for the tissue regeneration process. In bone tissue engineering, the regulation of cell functions induced by piezoelectric materials facilitates the repair and regeneration of bone tissue.

Many piezoelectric materials also possess antibacterial properties, which are crucial for the healing of infected wounds. Through piezoelectric self-stimulation, these materials can cause electrical interference in bacteria, disrupt bacterial membranes, and generate ROS to kill bacteria. Nanopiezoelectric films can promote cell proliferation and tissue regeneration through electric field generation while reducing bacterial infection. ZnO nanorod-based piezoelectric patches promote wound healing by generating electric fields and may simultaneously exhibit antibacterial effects.

Acute and chronic wounds from trauma, cancer, or diabetes exhibit accelerated healing when augmented with exogenous electric field stimulation. Flexible piezoelectric materials address this need through high-performance polyvinyl alcohol PVA/PVDF composite hydrogels [53,72]. These materials exhibit superior piezoelectric coefficients, stable electro-mechanical output, and robust mechanical properties, promoting fibroblast proliferation, migration, extracellular matrix secretion, and growth factor expression via AKT/ERK1/2 pathway activation. In the treatment of medical diseases, piezoelectric nanomaterials exert their effects through direct killing, regulation of cell behavior, and improvement of the microenvironment, leveraging their advantages of remote activation, deep tissue penetration via ultrasound, and low systemic toxicity. After modification, they can be combined with other therapeutic approaches for lung cancer, breast cancer treatment. Animal experiments have demonstrated dose-dependent therapeutic efficacy, and they are also capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier for glioblastoma treatment. In the field of wound healing, these materials convert mechanical stimuli into electrical signals, which guide cell migration, promote angiogenesis and collagen formation, regulate macrophages for anti-inflammatory effects, and generate ROS to kill bacteria. Flexible piezoelectric materials are adaptable to physiological needs, thereby accelerating wound repair. Currently, most research remains in the experimental stage. For clinical translation, it is necessary to verify long-term biocompatibility, and optimize loading efficiency as well as targeting ability.

Conclusion

Centered on piezoelectric materials, ultrasound-driven piezoelectric catalytic tumor therapy generates ROS via charge separation under ultrasonic excitation. It destroys tumor cell DNA and proteins through oxidative stress, induces ICD, activates T-cell immune responses, and simultaneously produces endogenous electrical stimulation to regulate cellular behavior. This therapy possesses three core advantages: non-invasive precision (ultrasound enables deep tissue penetration and focused localization), synergistic efficacy enhancement (easy combination with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, etc.), and tumor microenvironment tolerance (ROS production remains feasible under hypoxic/acidic conditions). At the laboratory level, research has achieved favorable ablation effects in breast cancer, lung cancer and glioblastoma models.

Currently, this field still faces significant bottlenecks: some materials exhibit insufficient piezoelectric catalytic efficiency, and inorganic piezoelectric systems have poor biocompatibility and targeting ability; the therapy requires external stimulation with high demands for parameter regulation, long-term safety data are scarce, and the risk of chronic toxicity from in vivo accumulation of nanomaterials remains to be evaluated; single-modal therapeutic designs necessitate additional imaging agents, resulting in a low level of theranostic integration(Fig. 26).Fig. 26Challenges and Future Prospects of piezoelectric bio-materials.Fig. 26

Challenges and Future Prospects of piezoelectric bio-materials.

The future research should pursue breakthroughs in three key directions: First, focus on material innovation. Develop new materials with both high piezoelectric performance and biocompatibility through defect engineering and ion doping. For instance, adopt A-site and B-site co-doping or aliovalent doping to induce local lattice distortion and reduce symmetry, or combine phase transition boundary engineering to further enhance piezoelectric performance. Select biologically essential or biocompatible ions (such as Mg, Ca, Zn, and Sr) for doping to reduce the cytotoxicity of materials and promote the integration of materials with tissues. Second, strengthen multimodal synergistic therapy. By designing TME-responsed piezoelectric nanocarriers (materials responsive to the characteristics of tumor tissues such as low pH, high GSH levels, hypoxia, overexpressed enzymes, and high stiffness), achieve precise drug delivery and controlled release of ROS. Integrate the photo-responsiveness and X-ray absorption of piezoelectric materials to deeply combine piezoelectric therapy with therapeutic modalities such as chemotherapy, photodynamic therapy, and radiotherapy. Enhance the synergy between chemotherapy and radiotherapy by using ultrasound to affect DNA repair mechanisms; alternatively, based on piezoelectric catalysis, realize precise therapy that combines X-ray-induced photodynamic therapy with hypoxia-activated chemotherapy. Finally, accelerate the development of theranostic integration. Integrate multiple imaging probes (e.g., MRI, NIR fluorescence imaging, and CT imaging probes) into piezoelectric nanoplatforms to achieve real-time monitoring of physiological parameters at lesion sites and visualized treatment of diseases. Meanwhile, improve in vivo translational validation and long-term toxicity assessment to facilitate the transition of this technology from basic research to clinical application.
