Genetic Insights Into AVP Deficiency: Identification of a Novel AVP Variant and Compilation of a Curated Catalogue of Pathogenic Variants
Authors: Jennifa Joseph, Esben Søndergaard, Sine Knorr, Jane Hagelskjær Knudsen, Søren Rittig, Jane Hvarregaard Christensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCentral diabetes insipidus, or arginine vasopressin deficiency (AVP‐D), is a rare disorder caused by insufficient release of antidiuretic hormone from the pituitary gland. The disease is characterised by severe polyuria and polydipsia, which, in rare cases, can be caused by variants in the AVP gene. The aim of the present study was to identify the genetic cause of AVP‐D in two families and compile and discuss an updated and complete catalogue of variants in AVP associated with AVP‐D. Two Danish families with autosomal dominant inheritance of symptoms indicating AVP‐D were included, and probands underwent an arginine‐stimulated copeptin test. Genetic testing included direct sequencing of the coding regions of AVP. HGMD professional, ClinVar, and literature mining combined with expert‐assisted filtering and curation were applied to compile all pathogenic variants in AVP reported until now. Genetic analysis of the two probands, both of which had abnormally low levels of arginine‐stimulated copeptin, and selected family members revealed co‐segregation of AVP‐D and heterozygosity for a novel pathogenic missense variant in AVP (c.122G>A, p.(Cys41Tyr)). This variant was included in an updated and curated list of 110 pathogenic AVP variants associated with AVP‐D. These findings expand the known mutational spectrum of AVP and provide a consolidated resource to support clinical interpretation and future genotype–phenotype studies.Keywords: arginine vasopressin deficiency, AVP, central diabetes insipidus, missense variant, pathogenic variant

Introduction

Central diabetes insipidus, or arginine vasopressin deficiency (AVP‐D), is a rare disorder caused by impaired synthesis or secretion of arginine vasopressin (AVP), also known as the antidiuretic hormone from the posterior pituitary gland [1]. This deficiency leads to hypotonic polyuria and compensatory polydipsia. AVP‐D affects both sexes equally and symptom onset is highly variable. If untreated, it may result in dehydration, sleep disturbances, and a marked decline in quality of life [1, 2, 3].

AVP regulates water homeostasis via V2 receptor‐mediated insertion of aquaporin‐2 (AQP2) channels in the renal collecting ducts, facilitating water reabsorption [4, 5]. Its release is tightly controlled by plasma osmolality and blood volume, monitored by hypothalamic osmoreceptors and baroreceptors located in the walls of major vessels [5]. Familial forms of AVP‐D, representing ~1% of cases, are usually inherited autosomal dominantly and linked to pathogenic variants in the AVP gene on chromosome 20p13 [6, 7]. These variants seem to cause misfolding and impaired intracellular processing of the AVP precursor (pre‐proAVP), leading to endoplasmic reticulum retention and stress, and ultimately, degeneration of magnocellular neurons [7, 8].

Diagnosis traditionally relies on the water deprivation test, but direct measurement of AVP is limited by its plasma instability [9]. Copeptin, a stable byproduct of pre‐proAVP processing, serves as a surrogate marker. The arginine‐stimulated copeptin test has emerged as a reliable diagnostic tool, with high sensitivity (93%) and specificity (92%) for distinguishing AVP‐D from nephrogenic diabetes insipidus and primary polydipsia [9, 10]. First‐line treatment for AVP‐D is a selective V2 receptor agonist, desmopressin. It is available in oral, intranasal, and parenteral formulations and effectively reduces urinary volume and thirst [6].

Here, we present two Danish families with several cases of suspected AVP‐D and describe the identification of a novel AVP variant. We assess its potential pathogenicity and formally classify it according to ACMG/AMP guidelines [11]. Lastly, we present and discuss a detailed, curated, and updated compilation of pathogenic variants in AVP.

Both the paternal grandmother of the proband in family A (I‐2) and the father of the proband (II‐1) presented clinical symptoms consistent with AVP‐D. II‐1 had a daily fluid intake of 5–6 L and was initially treated with a thiazide, which gave him slight relief of symptoms. His daily fluid intake was reduced to 3–4 L. At Aarhus University Hospital he underwent a spot copeptin test that showed a baseline copeptin level below 2.6 pmol/L, further supporting the inheritance of AVP‐D in family A. He underwent a trial period with desmopressin treatment, but it was not well tolerated due to adverse effects, including fluid retention, which led to a return to thiazide therapy [12].

I‐2 from family A does not receive any medical treatment for her symptoms. Desmopressin treatment was attempted but discontinued because of fluid retention.

Only the proband in family A (III‐3) underwent a non‐contrast MRI scan to search for a “bright spot” indicative of vasopressin storage on T1‐weighted images. The scan revealed normal hyperintensity of the neurohypophysis and no tumours were found.

The proband in family B (III‐2) visited his primary care physician in early adolescence due to polyuria and polydipsia but was, without any further examinations, told it was normal. He then visited another general practitioner at age 30 and was referred to the Department of Endocrinology and Internal Medicine at Aarhus University Hospital. Here, he underwent a 3‐day fluid intake and urine output registration upon referral. This showed that he had an average fluid intake of 9.8 L per 24 h, 4.4 L of which was consumed between 7:00 PM and 7:00 AM. The average daily urine output was 10.1 L, with 3.6 L occurring after bedtime, and 24‐h Uosm was 81 mmol/kg. An arginine‐stimulated copeptin test was performed that showed a copeptin level after 60 min stimulation that was less than 2.4 pmol/L, thus indicative of complete AVP‐D (Table 1). He began treatment with sublingual desmopressin with good effect (60 μg three times daily).

The sister of the proband in family B (III‐1) was diagnosed with AVP‐D at age 34 years. She became aware of her symptoms after her brother was diagnosed. She had a 3‐day average fluid intake of approximately 6.3 L per 24 h and an average urine output of 4.8 L per 24 h, with 3–4 voidings at night, and a 24‐h Uosm of 107 mmol/kg. A spot copeptin test showed a copeptin level below 2.6 pmol/L, further supporting the inheritance of AVP‐D in family B. She responded well to desmopressin. The maternal grandmother of the proband in family B (I‐2) and the mother of the proband (II‐2) both reported a high fluid intake, but neither has been diagnosed with AVP‐D due to lack of diagnostic assessment.

Genetic Testing and Evaluation of Variant Pathogenicity

QIAGEN QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Hilden, Germany) was used to extract genomic data from 200 μL of peripheral blood collected in EDTA for DNA amplification. In probands, the entire coding regions of all three exons of AVP were amplified by a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) as previously described [8]. The amplified PCR products were sequenced by bidirectional direct sequencing using a BigDye Terminator v1.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) and analysed on an ABI PRISM 3100‐Avant Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The sequence data were analysed using the Mutation Surveyor v.3.20 software (SoftGenetics LLC, State College, PA, USA), with the genomic sequence of AVP (GenBank accession number: M11166.1) serving as a reference. Variants in AVP were annotated using both the GenBank accession number: M11166.1 genomic sequence and the NCBI Reference Sequence: NM_000490.5 coding DNA as reference sequences.

Variant pathogenicity was evaluated by identifying its allele frequency in the general population (gnomAD, https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/, search: 24 May 2025) [13] as well as its molecular effects using a panel of seven independent in silico tools implemented in the SpliceAI Lookup Tool, https://spliceailookup.broadinstitute.org/ (analysed: 22 April 2025) [14]. Additionally, pathogenicity was classified according to ACMG/AMP guidelines.

Ethical Standards for Investigation and Publication

All participants were informed, both verbally and in writing, about the use of their data for research purposes. They provided informed consent for clinical data collection, investigation, and publication. During the preparation of this work, the authors did not use any Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools.

Compilation of AVP Variants and Evaluation of Variant Pathogenicity

Pathogenic AVP variants were extracted from the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) Professional [15] (search: 7 April 2026), a subscription version of HGMD, https://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/ and from an inhouse compilation of AVP variants identified in families with AVP‐D that had been fully updated and curated up until 2016. A literature search was performed using PubMed, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ and Scopus, https://www.scopus.com/sources databases (search: 7 April 2026), covering the period from 2016 to 2026. The search string used was “arginine vasopressin gene mutation OR arginine vasopressin gene variant OR AVP gene mutation OR AVP gene variant OR AVP‐NPII”. The search resulted in 300 studies for initial screening. Following title and abstract screening, 51 studies were selected for full‐text review. Fifty studies were excluded, primarily due to irrelevant outcomes or if the identified variants were already listed in the HGMD. Ultimately, one study was included. The pathogenicity of the variant reported was classified according to ACMG/AMP guidelines.

Supplementary searches were carried out in ClinVar, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/ (search: 5 May 2026) [16]. The inclusion criterion for variants identified in ClinVar was that the individuals with the variant were clinically affected. Accordingly, the “affected status” was reviewed for each variant in the context of central diabetes insipidus/AVP‐D. Variants already listed in the HGMD and variants affecting multiple genes (including copy number gain/loss as well as large deletions and duplications) were excluded. Ultimately, five variants met the criteria and were included. The pathogenicity of these variants was classified according to ACMG/AMP guidelines.

As many historical reports predate ACMG/AMP recommendations, a systematic reclassification of all previously published variants was considered beyond the scope of the present study.

Results

Identification of the Genetic Cause of AVP‐D in Two Families

The segregation of AVP‐D in the two families is consistent with an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern (Figure 1), thus making AVP a strong candidate for harbouring pathogenic variants [17]. Genetic testing was performed in five members in family A and two members in family B. Five were diagnosed with AVP‐D and two (IV‐1 and IV‐2, Fam. A) were at risk of having AVP‐D due to the presence of symptoms in their father and the delayed onset of symptoms in family members.

Direct sequencing of all three exons of AVP in III‐3 (the proband from Fam. A) revealed that she was heterozygous for a c.122G>A base pair substitution, predicting a replacement of cysteine for tyrosine at codon 41 (p.(Cys41Tyr)), (Figure S1). No other abnormalities were found in the coding regions of AVP.

Targeted sequencing of exon 2 in II‐1, III‐1, IV‐1, and IV‐2 in family A revealed heterozygosity for the same variant as identified in III‐3 (Fam. A) in all but IV‐1, who did not have the variant. Based on the results of the genetic testing, it was possible to confirm the suspicion of the parents, as IV‐2 (Fam. A) had presented symptoms indicative of AVP‐D with thirst and frequently heavy diapers. This information was mentioned after the genetic testing.

Sequencing of all three exons in AVP in III‐2 (Fam. B) and subsequent targeted sequencing of exon 2 in III‐1 (Fam. B) revealed heterozygosity for the same variant (c.122G>A, p. (Cys41Tyr)) as in family A in both (Figure S1).

The identified missense variant (c.122G>A) is absent in gnomAD and is thus extremely rare. A panel of seven independent in silico tools consistently suggested a damaging effect of the variant (c.122G>A equalling chr20‐3 083 177‐C‐T, GRCh38) on protein function (Table S1), further supporting its pathogenicity. When assessed using the ACMG/AMP guidelines, c.122G>A, p. (Cys41Tyr) is pathogenic since one strong (PS4), three moderate (PM1, PM2, and PM5), and four supporting (PP1–PP4) evidence codes can be attributed to the variant (see discussion for further elaboration).

Compilation of AVP Variants Causing AVP‐D

To provide an updated, curated, and complete overview of the number and characteristics of pathogenic variants in AVP causing AVP‐D, we combined and curated information from HGMD [15], inhouse compilations, as well as searches of the literature and ClinVar (Tables S2 and S3). The starting point for this compilation was a search in HGMD Professional, which included 102 variants with the latest update in 2025. A literature search identified one additional variant: p. (Glu78Asp) (c.234G>C) [18] that, when assessed using the ACMG/AMP guidelines, is pathogenic. Furthermore, five variants were identified through a systematic screening and filtering of all variants registered in ClinVar: p.(Cys44Phe), p.(Cys65Ter), p.(Ser87_Gln89del), p.(Cys110Ser), and p.(Cys116Tyr). When assessed using the ACMG/AMP guidelines, these variants are either pathogenic or likely pathogenic (Table S2). One additional variant (p.(Cys44Trp), variant no. 21 in Table S2) was added through an inhouse compilation updated up until 2016. The variant was not present in any of the other sources. The final variant included in the current updated compilation is p.(Cys41Tyr), identified in the present study. Consequently, the updated and curated compilation now includes a total of 110 AVP variants, 106 of which are causing AVP‐D in heterozygous individuals. Discussion

In this study, we identified a novel variant in AVP (c.122G>A, p.(Cys41Tyr)) in two Danish families with autosomal dominant inheritance of AVP‐D in several individuals and generations. Furthermore, we present a detailed and curated compilation of identified pathogenic variants in AVP.

The pathogenicity of the novel AVP variant is supported by the following observations: (1) its presence in all affected individuals investigated in two apparently unrelated families and its absence in the general population (ACMG/AMP guideline evidence codes PS4, PM2, PP1, and PP4), (2) it affects an amino acid residue that has previously been involved in AVP‐D, although with a different substitution (p. (Cys41Trp)) [19] (evidence code PM5), (3) consistent pathogenicity predictions using seven independent in silico tools (evidence code PP3), and (4) it is very likely that it has an effect on folding, dimerisation, and cellular processing of the AVP prohormone as it disrupts the disulphide bridge between Cys41 and Cys85 of the neurophysin II domain of the AVP prohormone [20, 21] (evidence codes PM1 and PP2).

Our compilation of variants in AVP show that 13 of the 14 highly conserved disulphide bridge‐forming cysteine residues in neurophysin II [21] are now identified as causing AVP‐D.

This clearly indicates that proper disulphide bond formation is essential for processing of the AVP prohormone and that the autosomal dominant form of AVP‐D is indeed a protein misfolding disease [22]. This further implies that: (1) the p.(Cys44Phe) variant (variant no. 20 in Table S2), for which ClinVar has no information about mode of segregation, most likely is inherited in a dominant form, (2) that the p.(Cys110Ser) variant (variant no. 96 in Table S2) most likely is associated with autosomal dominant AVP‐D (ClinVar is equivocal if it is neurohypophyseal or nephrogenic diabetes insipidus), and (3) that classification of the p.(Cys116Tyr) variant (variant no. 104 in Table S2) as pathogenic/likely pathogenic is correct.

In addition to providing affected individuals with an informative genetic diagnosis, our investigation also allowed testing of individuals at risk of developing AVP‐D (IV‐1 and IV‐2, Fam. A). Our findings relieved further parental worries and repeated clinical testing of IV‐1 and a confirmatory molecular diagnosis of IV‐2 that retrospectively already had shown parental reported signs of AVP‐D. In this context, it is noteworthy and concerning that the very severe and troublesome clinical symptoms of III‐2 in family B had not been identified by his general practitioner as indicative of diabetes insipidus when consulted, resulting in a very delayed diagnosis and initiation of treatment. This highlights the need for increased awareness and recognition of rare genetic disorders within the primary healthcare sector.

The AVP‐D diagnosis was based on clinical symptoms, measurements of fluid intake, diuresis, and urine osmolality as well as arginine‐stimulated copeptin tests in the probands and baseline copeptin tests in additional family members. These findings support the suspicion of AVP‐D; however, complete data required for definitive verification are lacking. Furthermore, it is relevant to consider whether the identification of the same very rare pathogenic variant in two families from the same relatively small population could indicate that the families are unknowingly related. Further exploration of this, specifically, attempts to demonstrate that the variant arose independently on two separate occasions would, however, not add any substantial value to the interpretation of the pathogenicity of the variant or aid the families.

Molecular diagnostics in AVP‐D is an important task as it supports clinical diagnosis, helps direct treatment choices, and offers families the possibility to undergo presymptomatic testing in children at risk. Evaluation of the pathogenicity of novel AVP variants can be challenging especially if identified in sporadic cases in which co‐segregation of AVP‐D and the variant cannot be sufficiently confirmed. Several projects have been launched to assist in variant interpretation, for example, by allowing direct variant effect prediction (e.g., AlphaMissense or SpliceAI) or by aggregating and annotating variants from external submitters (e.g., ClinVar) or from publications (e.g., HGMD). The compilation and characterisation of 110 AVP variants associated with AVP‐D presented here could not have been reliably extracted from any of the individual sources because HGMD contained only 102 variants (and even fewer (n = 87) in the publicly available version of the last update in 2019). ClinVar missed several pathogenic variants even though it contains many variants affecting AVP (n = 193) and on the other hand provided, after comprehensive expert‐assisted filtering, five additional pathogenic or likely pathogenic AVP‐D associated variants. Two variants were only identified by expert‐assisted literature searches. Still, it is possible that additional variants remain to be identified because the literature search (PubMed and Scopus) was incomplete as some AVP variants listed in HGMD Professional (for the period 2016–2026) were not retrieved.

Additionally, the compilation of AVP variants presented here is very useful to make some important generalisations about their structural‐functional effects on the AVP prohormone. As to the variants associated with dominantly inherited AVP‐D, they all likely produce an aberrantly folded dominant negative mutant form of the AVP prohormone as they either: (1) impair signal peptide processing, (2) affect folding sensitive amino acid residues in AVP or neurophysin II or (3) lead to the production of a truncated AVP prohormone either due to premature stop codons in late exon 2 or exon 3 or splicing defects of intron 2. On the contrary, the few AVP variants associated with a recessive form of AVP‐D, which often present with an early‐onset (but not always) and more heterogeneous form of diabetes insipidus [23], are all characterised by loss‐of‐function. Loss‐of‐function occurs in some cases due to the production of a large amount of non‐functional AVP hormone (p.(Pro26Leu)) and in other cases due to complete loss of AVP prohormone production because of: (1) faulty splicing of intron 1 (c.121‐2A>G), (2) a premature stop codon in early exon 2 (p.(Cys65Ter)), or (3) a large 10 kb deletion removing almost the entire AVP gene and its regulatory regions.

In ClinVar, the pathogenicity of the p.(Cys65Ter) variant is categorised as being of ‘uncertain significance,’ despite being thoroughly described and evaluated as pathogenic by the submitter as well as by us. Indeed, it was predicted that the early premature stop codon likely results in nonsense‐mediated decay (NMD) of the AVP mRNA and thus cannot exert a dominant negative effect, in line with the observation that heterozygous individuals are unaffected and homozygous individuals show early onset. We also identified one variant, p.(Pro7Leu), classified as pathogenic in ClinVar based on literature describing functional characterization of p.(Pro26Leu), a distinct variant affecting the full‐length AVP precursor protein. While historical nomenclature has sometimes referred to p.(Pro26Leu) as p.(Pro7Leu) based on numbering within the AVP peptide moiety, no independent evidence supporting pathogenicity of p.(Pro7Leu) was identified. Consequently, p.(Pro7Leu) was not considered conclusively pathogenic in the present curated catalogue. These cases exemplify some of the limitations of submitter‐based repositories and further highlight the importance of systematic and expert curated data collection from multiple sources.

It is further worth considering whether systematic application of pathogenicity prediction tools may offer improved curation. AlphaMissense is among the leading predictors for missense variants and has been reported to exceed ClinVar‐based classifications in certain aspects [24]. For instance, it has been noted that heterozygosity complicates the interpretation of mutational impact. Nevertheless, the inclusion (though with expert‐assisted filtering) of ClinVar variants in the present compilation enhanced the completeness of the compilation.

In conclusion, in this study we identified a novel pathogenic AVP variant in two Danish families with autosomal dominant inheritance of symptoms of AVP‐D. In addition, we compiled a catalogue of variants identified in AVP that cause AVP‐D and demonstrated the advantage of combining expert‐assisted curation, literature search, and online repositories to ensure the validity and completeness of such a catalogue. The last variant added to the catalogue is the novel variant described in this paper.
