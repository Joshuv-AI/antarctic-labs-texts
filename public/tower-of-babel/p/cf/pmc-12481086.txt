Fistulized renal cyst in the stomach: A diagnostic and therapeutic challenge
Authors: Ahmed Chaabouni, Hazem Hachicha, Firas Zouari, Mohamed A Mseddi, Nouri Rebai, Mourad H Slimen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRenal cysts are usually asymptomatic, and superinfection is rare. We report the case of a 90-year-old woman presenting with fever, flank pain, and vomiting. Laboratory tests showed leukocytosis and elevated C-reactive protein. CT imaging revealed an infected upper-pole renal cyst with inflammatory changes, without obstruction. Intravenous antibiotics (third-generation cephalosporin plus aminoglycoside) led to rapid improvement. Follow-up imaging demonstrated partial cyst regression and a small renogastric fistula, which closed spontaneously after one month of continued medical therapy. This case highlights an exceptionally rare complication of an infected renal cyst successfully managed without intervention.Keywords: Renal cyst, Infection, Fistula, Complication

Introduction

Renal cysts are common, particularly in elderly patients, and are usually asymptomatic. Superinfection is rare and can lead to severe complications, including fistulization to adjacent organs. Renogastric fistula is exceptionally rare and poses diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

Case presentation

A 90-year-old woman, with no surgical history or known allergies, consulted for left lower back pain lasting for 3 days, associated with fever (T 38.5 °C) and vomiting, as well as diffuse abdominal pain that appeared spontaneously.

On physical examination, the patient was conscious, cooperative, tachycardic (HR = 110 bpm) with mildly elevated blood pressure (BP = 15/8 mm Hg), depressible abdomen, pain on palpation of the left lumbar fossa and preserved diuresis.

The biological results showed a biological inflammatory syndrome with an elevated blood count (WBC = 28,000/mm3), an elevated C-reactive protein (380 mg/L) and a hemoglobin of 10.4 g/dL. Serum creatinine was slightly elevated at 112 μmol/L.

The cytobacterioligical urine exam revealed a cloudy appearance with significant leukocyturia and a positive culture for multi-susceptible Escherichia coli.

The uroscan revealed bilateral uretero-pyelo-calyceal dilation without detectable obstruction (APD on the left = 16 mm and APD on the right = 14 mm). In the left kidney, a superior polar cortical cyst measured 74 × 70 mm on an axial plane, with exophytic development, a thick wall, and heterogeneous content. This cyst was associated with densification of the adjacent fat, suggestive of an infected cyst (Fig. 1).

Access to the cyst for CT-guided drainage was considered difficult due to its anterior location and the interposition of the digestive tract, according to the opinion of the radiologists.

The patient was started on antibiotic therapy with a third-generation cephalosporin combined with an aminoglycoside.

After 48 hours, clinical improvement was observed, with the disappearance of pain and fever. Laboratory parameters showed a decrease in CRP (110 mg/L) and white blood cell count (9000/mm3), as well as normalization of serum creatinine (89 μmol/L).

A follow-up uroscan performed after 2 weeks revealed partial regression of the cyst with a fistulous communication to the stomach measuring 5 mm in maximum thickness. Clinically, the patient was asymptomatic and afebrile, without any transit disorders. Our approach was to extend the antibiotic therapy and continue monitoring (Fig. 2).

A follow-up uroscan performed after 1 month showed almost complete regression of the left renal cyst with closure of the fistulous tract.

Discussion

Renal cysts are common in elderly patients and are usually benign and asymptomatic. However, superinfection of a renal cyst is a rare but significant complication. Although the literature reports few cases of fistulization of renal cysts towards adjacent organs,1 such as reno-colic fistula, cases involving kidney-stomach fistula are even rarer. This unusual complication requires special attention due to its atypical presentation and high potential for morbidity.

Renogastric fistula formation is a complex phenomenon resulting from persistent inflammation and infection of the renal cyst. Chronic inflammation can lead to the destruction of adjacent tissues, facilitating the formation of an abnormal communication between the kidney and a neighboring organ, notably the stomach.

Diagnosis is based on a combination of clinical signs, biological results and medical imaging. The clinical presentation with lower back pain, fever and vomiting pointed towards a complicated kidney infection. Biological inflammatory syndrome and leukocyturia support the diagnosis.

The uroscan played a crucial role in confirming the presence of a large renal cyst with characteristics suggestive of superinfection (thick wall and heterogeneous content) and in revealing the renogastric fistula. This imaging modality is essential to assess the extent of the disease and plan the appropriate treatment.2

The treatment of superinfected renal cysts generally relies on appropriate antibiotic therapy, based on the combination of third-generation cephalosporin and aminoglycoside.

If fistulization occurs, percutaneous drainage of the cyst or a surgical approach may be necessary.3

Diagnosis and management of complicated kidney cysts can be difficult. Which highlights the importance of early recognition and adequate management of kidney cyst complications.

Regular imaging monitoring and appropriate antibiotic therapy are crucial to prevent serious complications such as fistulization.

Conclusion

This case illustrates an extremely rare complication of an infected renal cyst that resolved with Medical treatment.

Prompt recognition, appropriate antibiotic therapy, and close imaging follow-up can obviate the need for invasive procedures.
