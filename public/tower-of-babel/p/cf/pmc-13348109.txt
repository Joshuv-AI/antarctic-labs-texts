Family dynamics after sibling loss in a migration context: the experiences of Ethiopian bereaved siblings
Authors: Eden Etsegenet Kassa, Wisam Maree, Jonathan Guez, Rivi Frei-Landau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Sibling bereavement profoundly affects family systems, yet little is known about bereaved siblings from immigrant communities where family structures have been destabilised by migration prior to the loss. The Ethiopian-Israeli community constitutes a distinct minority group of immigrants in Israel, characterised by unique cultural heritage and by skin colour that distinguishes its members from the general population. This visible difference subjects the community to racism and discrimination in ways that parallel the experiences of Black communities worldwide.Objective: This study's goal was to examine the experiences of family dynamics following sibling loss among bereaved siblings from the Ethiopian-Israeli community, that is considered a minority group parallel to Black communities, who face racism and discrimination based on their skin colour.Method: Using qualitative methodology, in-depth interviews with 17 bereaved siblings (ages 21–43), who reflected on their grief experiences, were analyzed using the narrative analysis model.‏Results: Analysis revealed four interconnected themes: children's roles as cultural mediators within the Ethiopian community prior to the loss; post-loss sibling relationship dynamics; sibling loss as loss of parental function; and parents’ transformation alongside bereaved siblings’ caregiving burden.Conclusions: The findings are discussed in light of Doka's concept of disenfranchised grief. Bereaved siblings from immigrant communities experience disenfranchised grief shaped by sibling status, immigrant identity, and pre-existing family disruptions. Culturally adapted interventions are needed.

Introduction

Sibling loss constitutes one of life's most significant bereavements, carrying potentially lasting effects on bereaved siblings’ psychological well-being and family functioning across the lifespan (Fletcher et al., 2018). Sibling relationships represent the longest-lasting family bonds most individuals will experience, often spanning the entire life course from shared childhood through old age. These relationships, formed at birth and grounded in shared experiences and mutual trust, mean that their loss can profoundly disrupt the bereaved sibling's world (Eyetsemitan, 2022). However, bereaved siblings often experience disenfranchised grief, as family systems focus on parental grief and siblings’ emotional needs are systematically overlooked (Davidson, 2018; Doka, 2008). Recent evidence demonstrates that lack of social acknowledgment directly predicts more severe prolonged grief symptoms (Zhou et al., 2023). Bereaved siblings may experience depression, anxiety, and behavioural difficulties that persist for years (Fletcher et al., 2018; Sepehrtaj et al., 2020).

Research has identified multiple psychological consequences following sibling loss. Beyond immediate grief reactions, bereaved siblings face elevated risk for prolonged grief disorder, characterised by persistent yearning and preoccupation with the deceased that interferes with daily functioning. Studies have documented increased rates of depression, anxiety disorders, and post-traumatic stress symptoms among bereaved siblings compared to non-bereaved peers (Sepehrtaj et al., 2020). The developmental timing of sibling loss appears particularly significant, with losses occurring during childhood and adolescence associated with distinct challenges related to identity formation and attachment security.

Although research on sibling bereavement has historically been limited, scholarly attention has lately increased. Nevertheless, understanding this cohort requires examining not only individual grief responses but also the family dynamics within which grief unfolds. The literature increasingly recognises that grief occurs within relational contexts where family members influence one another's mourning processes (Breen et al., 2019; Walsh & McGoldrick, 2013). Parental grief responses may intensify bereaved siblings’ difficulties, with some experiencing a ‘double loss’ of both sibling and, functionally, the grieving parent (Davidson, 2018). When parents become emotionally unavailable due to their own overwhelming grief, surviving children may find themselves without the support they need while simultaneously feeling responsible for their parents’ well-being.

Bereavement and ethnicity

Ethnic minority status shapes bereavement experiences in ways that remain understudied. Research has documented that racial and ethnic minorities experience bereavement at disproportionately higher rates and under more adverse conditions than majority populations. Burns et al. (2020) found that ethnic minority children in the United States were significantly more likely to experience parental or sibling bereavement compared to White children, with compounding social and economic adversity amplifying the psychological impact. Similarly, Hillis et al. (2021a, 2021b) demonstrated that children from minority and low-income backgrounds suffered disproportionately higher rates of caregiver loss during the COVID-19 pandemic, underscoring how structural inequalities heighten bereavement burden. Laurie and Neimeyer (2008) further documented that African American bereaved individuals reported lower social support and more complicated grief trajectories compared to their White counterparts, reflecting the compounding effect of structural marginalisation on grief processes. Mayland et al. (2020) similarly found that ethnic minority groups faced systemic barriers to bereavement support, including cultural mismatches in services, language barriers, and mistrust of institutions. Importantly, some minority communities are characterised by limited openness to outsiders and researchers, reflecting histories of institutional distrust, which further reduces their representation in bereavement research and their access to culturally appropriate services (Coleman-Brueckheimer & Dein, 2011). These findings collectively highlight the importance of studying bereavement within specific cultural and structural contexts, particularly among immigrant and minority communities whose grief experiences may be systematically overlooked. Within the Israeli context specifically, recent qualitative research has documented how minority communities experience grief shaped by cultural silencing and societal marginalisation, highlighting the need for culturally grounded bereavement inquiry (Abo-Mokh et al., 2025; Frei-Landau et al., 2025; Boutton-Laor et al., 2026).

Theoretical framework

Family systems theory conceptualises families as interconnected emotional systems where change in one member affects the entire system (Bowen, 1993; Walsh & McGoldrick, 2013). Within this framework, sibling loss represents not merely an individual tragedy but a disruption to the entire family system requiring reorganisation of roles, relationships, and patterns of interaction. Research on intergenerational trauma has demonstrated that parental trauma is associated with psychosocial problems in children through multiple pathways, including disrupted attachment patterns, altered parenting behaviours, and changes in family communication (Mooren et al., 2023; Sleijpen et al., 2016). In families affected by migration-related adversity, these intergenerational processes are further compounded by pre-existing role disruptions. Mooren et al. (2023) describe the impact of parental trauma on children as part of a dynamic systemic process of family adaptation, in which the accumulation of family stressors – including disrupted attachment, altered communication patterns, and role reversals – shapes how subsequent losses are experienced across the family system (see also Dashorst et al., 2019). When children have already assumed parental functions prior to bereavement, sibling loss is absorbed into a family system that has already undergone significant structural reorganisation, amplifying the intergenerational effects of psychotrauma (Mooren et al., 2023; Olff et al., 2025). This dimension distinguishes these families from those in which bereavement occurs without prior systemic disruption. However, applying these frameworks to sibling bereavement in immigrant families remains understudied. The intersection of migration-related family disruptions with subsequent bereavement creates a unique context that existing theories may not adequately address.

The Ethiopian-Israeli community

The Ethiopian-Israeli community constitutes a distinct minority group in Israel, characterised by unique cultural heritage and by skin colour that distinguishes its members from the general population. This visible difference subjects the community to racism and discrimination and structural exclusion (Hermann, 2019). As an analytical parallel – not an equivalence between distinct communities with different histories – research on racially marginalised communities internationally has shown that structural barriers to resources, institutional discrimination, and race-based traumatic stress compound adverse life experiences and shape family dynamics across generations (Lee, 2023). The dynamics documented in the context of the Black Lives Matter movement, particularly around the compounding effects of structural marginalisation on family systems and grief, offer a conceptual lens for understanding how Ethiopian-Israeli families navigate multilayered adversity. Comprising less than 2% of the Israeli population, Ethiopian-Israelis face persistent barriers in education, employment, and social integration, and remain significantly underrepresented in bereavement research despite experiencing multiple layers of adversity (Hermann, 2019). Recent calls within psychotraumatology have emphasised the critical importance of examining trauma and its effects beyond Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) populations, recognising that marginalised communities may experience and process trauma in culturally distinct ways (Olff, 2017; Olff et al., 2025).

The Ethiopian-Israeli community provides a valuable platform for examining bereaved siblings’ experiences in the context of migration. Their immigration to Israel occurred in major waves including Operation Moses (1984–1985) and Operation Solomon (1991) (Talmi-Cohen, 2019). The migration journey constituted a profoundly traumatic experience; many travelled by foot through Sudan, experiencing multiple traumatic exposures including witnessing deaths and extreme hunger. Individual losses became woven into collective traumatic loss (BenEzer, 2017; Talmi-Cohen, 2019).

The transition from rural Ethiopian village life to modern urban Israeli society created additional challenges including culture shock, language barriers, and socioeconomic marginalisation (Grisaru et al., 2008). These experiences destabilised family structures, creating intergenerational rifts. Parents found themselves unable to navigate Israeli systems; simultaneously, younger family members integrated more rapidly. These differences led to systematic role reversal wherein youth assumed parental responsibilities, serving as cultural brokers (Edelstein, 2012; Orellana et al., 2003). These altered hierarchies would fundamentally shape how subsequent losses were experienced.

Despite the community's significant size, bereaved siblings from this community remain virtually absent from the literature. Existing research has been conducted primarily among majority-culture families with stable pre-loss family structures (Funk et al., 2018). When families have already experienced role reversals before bereavement occurs, the landscape of grief may differ substantially from documented patterns.

In the current study we examine how family dynamics unfold following sibling loss among bereaved siblings from the Ethiopian-Israeli community.

Method

Research design

In the current study, we employed a qualitative narrative approach, a methodology that enables an in-depth examination of how bereaved siblings construct meaning from their experiences within their cultural contexts (Lieblich et al., 2010). This methodological approach is particularly appropriate for exploring grief and family dynamics in cultural contexts where experiences may not align with Western theoretical frameworks (Frei-Landau, 2023; Frei-Landau et al., 2024a).

Participants

The study included 17 bereaved siblings (11 women, 6 men) between the ages of 21–43, from the Ethiopian-Israeli community, who had experienced the loss of a sibling. The decision to include only adult participants (age 18 and above) was guided by two considerations. First, this study employed retrospective narrative analysis, which requires the capacity for extended and reflective meaning-making. Second, ethical constraints precluded interviewing minors without parental consent, and given the Ethiopian-Israeli community’s relative closure to outside researchers and its norms of limited emotional disclosure around bereavement (Coleman-Brueckheimer & Dein, 2011), obtaining parental consent within bereaved families would have been practically unfeasible. The strategy of recruiting adult participants from a community characterised by limited openness to outside researchers has been validated in prior qualitative research with similarly hard-to-reach closed populations (Levitan et al., 2025).‏ Most participants remained closely connected to their nuclear family of origin, which is normative within this community’s cultural values, rendering retrospective accounts directly relevant to ongoing family dynamics. The deliberate inclusion of participants with varied time since loss (18 months to 25 years) reflects the well-documented understanding that grief integration is a long-term process, and retrospective narrative accounts often yield deeper insight into how bereavement reshaped family functioning (Davidson, 2018). Only two participants reflected on losses occurring approximately 20–25 years prior; the majority (15 of 17) experienced losses between three and ten years ago, a range that is consistent with qualitative grief research. Participants were recruited through purposeful and snowball sampling strategies (Noy, 2008; Patton, 2002) via Ethiopian community social media groups and WhatsApp networks. Of the 39 individuals who expressed initial interest, 17 participants ultimately participated. This attrition likely reflects patterns of avoidance and silencing within the community around sensitive topics such as bereavement (Taye et al., 2025). The final group represented diversity across multiple dimensions: gender, age, time since loss, and the circumstances of the death (including military service, illness, suicide, homicide, and accidents). All participants were fluent Hebrew speakers. All participants were from the 1991 Operation Solomon immigration wave; three had immigrated as young children and are considered part of the 1.5 generation, while 14 were born in Israel to immigrant parents and represent the second generation. Research consistently demonstrates that both the 1.5 and second generations carry the complexity of migration and frequently occupy the role of cultural mediator between parents and Israeli society (Edelstein, 2012; Talmi-Cohen, 2019). Procedure and data collection

Data collection occurred between October 2024 and April 2025. The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the academic institution (Approval #0148), and the participants gave their written informed consent and were told they could discontinue at will. Interviews followed a two-stage structure common in narrative grief research (Frei-Landau & Schonfeld, 2025; Frei-Landau et al., 2024b), beginning with an invitation to offer an open-ended narrative followed by focused follow-up questions exploring specific themes that emerged from participants’ narratives. The interview guide, including the opening narrative prompt and all follow-up questions, is provided in Appendix A. All interviews were conducted in Hebrew by the first author, a member of the Ethiopian-Israeli community, and audio-recorded with participants’ consent. Interviews lasted between 90 and 180 min and were transcribed verbatim. When Amharic words and phrases arose during interviews, they were translated and interpreted by three community members fluent in both languages, ensuring accuracy in understanding cultural and emotional meanings.

Data analysis and trustworthiness

Analysis followed the Lieblich et al. (2010) holistic-content and categorical-content approaches to narrative analysis. The analysis process began with the researchers reading all interview transcripts multiple times to familiarise themselves with participants’ experiences, followed by in-depth content analysis in which meaning units were identified and initial codes across all data sets were assigned. The researchers then organised initial codes into broader categories and created relationship maps describing connections between thematic elements. This iterative process led to the formulation of central themes reflecting bereaved siblings’ experiences.

The study's trustworthiness was ensured through several methodological practices. The first author maintained reflexivity throughout data collection, documenting preconceptions stemming from her own position as an Ethiopian-Israeli community member. To address credibility, all authors participated in the analysis process, and two additional researchers independently analyzed a subset of interviews to validate the emerging themes. The research team was diverse: the first author is a second-generation Ethiopian-Israeli; the second author is a researcher from the Arab community minority in Israel; the third author is the founder of a one-of-its-kind culturally sensitive psychology programme and belong to the majority non-religious group in Israel; the fourth author, the research supervisor, heads a research lab focused on loss via a multicultural lens and she herself experienced a stillbirth experience a decade ago and belong to a religious minority group. Two research assistants also participated. Initial coding agreement was approximately 78%; discrepancies were resolved through deliberation. Triangulation was achieved through the inclusion of participants with diverse loss circumstances, ages, and time since bereavement. The detailed documentation of the analysis process and the inclusion of extensive participant quotes in the results section were designed to enhance the study's transferability and allow readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other contexts.

Ethical considerations

The study followed ethical guidelines for sensitive research. Participants were informed of their right to refuse to answer questions or withdraw at any time. Given the emotional nature of the interviews, the first author monitored participants’ well-being and provided referrals to appropriate support services when needed. At the conclusion of each interview, all participants reported experiencing the interview as an opportunity for ventilation and reflection. None required referral to therapeutic services; however, some participants requested treatment recommendations, which were provided.

Results

The analysis revealed four interconnected themes that illuminate the complex dynamics of bereavement within the Ethiopian-Israeli community: children as cultural mediators between their parents and Israeli society prior to the loss; sibling relationship dynamics following the loss of the sibling; Loss of a Sibling as Loss of Parental Function; and the transformation of parents alongside the burden of care placed on the children. Together, these themes describe a family system in which the siblings occupied unique mediating positions prior to the loss, a context which profoundly shaped their subsequent bereavement experience and led to a unique struggle to balance their personal grief with their parents’ needs. Pre-loss roles as cultural mediators: the foundation shaping bereavement

The experiences described in this theme reflect a foundational dimension of disenfranchised grief: the pre-loss context in which these siblings were embedded rendered their grief needs structurally invisible even before the loss occurred, as their caregiving role left no recognised space for their own vulnerability. Sixteen participants described how the children of Ethiopian parents filled central mediating roles between their parents and Israeli society, even prior to the loss of the sibling. These roles extended far beyond the simple translation of language to encompass navigating complex institutional systems, cultural interpretation across Ethiopian and Israeli contexts, and serving as bridges that enabled parents to access basic societal functions they could not access independently. As will become evident throughout the findings, these pre-loss roles are not merely background context; they are the foundation upon which the entire experience of bereavement was built, shaping how siblings experienced the loss, how family relationships changed after it, and why parental grief proved so disruptive to surviving siblings. Yafit (21), a young woman whose brother died during his military service three years prior to the interview, described how the children's mediating responsibilities extended across multiple domains of family life: They [her siblings] would usually come with my mother … because they had to help her in the technical aspect of getting to the school and finding the classroom, and the language issue.Yafit's words illustrate how children assumed responsibilities far beyond typical childhood roles, navigating entire systems of institutional expectations and cultural norms, becoming the essential infrastructure through which parents could access basic functions such as school communications. After her brother's death, this role did not diminish – it extended into the bereavement process itself. Yafit described finding herself managing bureaucratic procedures related to the loss on her parents’ behalf: After he died, I was the one dealing with the army, the forms, everything. My parents couldn't.The mediation that had characterised her childhood became, naturally and without deliberate choice, the framework through which she navigated grief.

They [her siblings] would usually come with my mother … because they had to help her in the technical aspect of getting to the school and finding the classroom, and the language issue.

After he died, I was the one dealing with the army, the forms, everything. My parents couldn't.

Tshayinesh (28), a woman who lost her brother to suicide during his military service three years prior to the interview, emphasised how these responsibilities emerged organically: Neither of them [her parents] knew Hebrew well so I would do a lot for them … when they came back frustrated after walking around all day trying to arrange something and didn't succeed, it frustrated me too.Tshayinesh's description captures how her emotional state became deeply intertwined with her parents’ daily struggles. For her, mediating seemed to carry significant emotional weight beyond practical tasks, as her own well-being depended on her ability to resolve her parents’ difficulties; this situation created a pattern wherein children felt responsible not only for helping with tasks but also for their parents’ emotional states. Following the loss, this emotional attunement took on a new form. Tshayinesh described feeling that she could not allow herself to grieve openly: I couldn't fall apart. They needed me to be strong.This was not experienced as an external demand, but as an expression of her deep connection to her parents and her place within the family.

Neither of them [her parents] knew Hebrew well so I would do a lot for them … when they came back frustrated after walking around all day trying to arrange something and didn't succeed, it frustrated me too.

I couldn't fall apart. They needed me to be strong.

Despite the fundamental shift that had taken place in family roles (i.e. parents becoming children; children becoming parents), traditional Ethiopian values continued to shape how the children understood their responsibilities toward parents. Moshe (28), a man who lost his sister to an illness seven years prior to the interview, articulated this complexity: It was a bit like carrying them on my shoulders. At one time it was a burden for me, but over the years as I matured, I loved helping them … that's why I define them as absent-wonderful.Moshe's characterisation captures a profound paradox wherein his parents were functionally limited in navigating Israeli society yet remained valued in other dimensions of parenthood. The burden he carried transformed over time from resentment to love, reflecting a developmental process in which he reconciled their limitations with his deep appreciation of them.

It was a bit like carrying them on my shoulders. At one time it was a burden for me, but over the years as I matured, I loved helping them … that's why I define them as absent-wonderful.

The importance of understanding this pre-loss context was articulated powerfully by Moshe: Whoever doesn't understand the foundation upon which the loss occurred won't understand the abyss created after it.This metaphor underscores how the pre-loss family structure, already marked by role reversals and inverted hierarchies, fundamentally shaped the impact of their subsequent loss. Together, these accounts reveal that the pre-loss roles of cultural mediation were not simply background to the bereavement experience – they were woven into its very fabric. For these participants, the loss did not dissolve their mediating position within the family; rather, the existing bonds of responsibility and care deepened in the face of shared grief. Alongside the pain of loss, many participants continued to find meaning in their roles as connectors and caregivers – yet this continuity also meant that their own grief unfolded within a context in which they were simultaneously expected, often by themselves, to hold the family together.

Sibling relationship dynamics following loss

The relational disruptions described in this theme represent a further dimension of disenfranchised grief (Doka, 2008): the family-level consequences of sibling loss remain unrecognised not only by wider society but often within the sibling subsystem itself, as surviving siblings navigate grief in isolation or conflict rather than receiving mutual support. Analysis of the interviews revealed a complex picture of changes in sibling relationships following the loss of a sibling, with some families experiencing closeness while others experienced distancing and withdrawal.

Intensification of sibling connection

Eight of the 17 participants described significant changes in patterns of communication and connectedness between siblings after the loss, expressed through intensive communication and high mutual involvement. Almaz (37), a woman who lost her sister to a sudden illness eight years prior to the interview, described: The loss brought us even closer … We became even closer sisters than before. The girls are much more involved in each other's lives now, talking almost daily.Almaz's description reflects a process of continuity and intensification of existing connection patterns. The transition to intensive daily communication points to a deep need to preserve and strengthen connection precisely in the face of the experience of rupture and loss. However, whereas some siblings succeeded in developing a closer connection, others reported difficulty in direct emotional expression even as they attempted to build bridges. Sarah (31) articulated this complexity: I tried to connect us, so that we would have a better connection … With the younger ones, I would share with them how it was for me at work … [however] it wasn't very deep at the level of psychological and emotional depth.Sarah's words point to different patterns. The connection with the younger siblings developed through sharing daily experiences, whereas her relationships with the older siblings remained constrained by intergenerational and cultural gaps. Overall, while loss led to increased communication and mutual involvement, and in some cases even repair of disconnected relationships, the persistent difficulty in expressing and receiving emotional support remained evident.

The loss brought us even closer … We became even closer sisters than before. The girls are much more involved in each other's lives now, talking almost daily.

I tried to connect us, so that we would have a better connection … With the younger ones, I would share with them how it was for me at work … [however] it wasn't very deep at the level of psychological and emotional depth.

Distancing and withdrawal

Conversely, nine participants described significant distancing and withdrawal. Two interrelated factors emerged as central: intergenerational gaps and unequal distribution of caregiving burdens.

The complexity in sibling relationships was especially evident in different relationship patterns with older versus younger siblings. Yerus (29) said: I'm happy that I have more connection with the younger siblings … but with my older sisters I know them less well. With the younger ones the connection strengthened even more.This pattern reflects the split created in the family relationship system when intergenerational and cultural gaps affect the ability to create meaningful connection.

I'm happy that I have more connection with the younger siblings … but with my older sisters I know them less well. With the younger ones the connection strengthened even more.

Some siblings found themselves in a complex position of carrying heavy responsibility for the family after the loss. This situation created a dynamic in which the imbalance in the distribution of responsibility led to deep feelings of anger and frustration. Moshe (28) described: My siblings, each one withdrew into their own corner. I felt like I was the only one taking care of my parents, so maybe I also took some revenge on them by leaving.In cases of sudden loss, siblings may find themselves required to deal simultaneously with the loss itself and with new family roles. Rivka (32) described: We barely talk. This death paralyzed us. It surprised and paralyzed us … We fought a lot about the roles we had to take on. We didn't have time to think about the loss at all.

My siblings, each one withdrew into their own corner. I felt like I was the only one taking care of my parents, so maybe I also took some revenge on them by leaving.

We barely talk. This death paralyzed us. It surprised and paralyzed us … We fought a lot about the roles we had to take on. We didn't have time to think about the loss at all.

Loss of a sibling as loss of parental function

This theme illustrates a particularly acute form of disenfranchised grief: when a sibling has fulfilled parental functions, their loss constitutes a primary attachment loss – yet because it occurs within the sibling relationship, it receives neither the social recognition accorded to parental bereavement nor the clinical attention that primary attachment loss demands (Davidson, 2018; Doka, 2008). Building upon the understanding that children of Ethiopian parents often occupy unique mediating positions, eleven participants described their deceased siblings in explicitly parental terms regardless of age or birth order, suggesting a fundamental reorganisation of attachment systems within migration-affected families. Almaz (37), a woman who lost her sister to a sudden illness eight years prior to the interview, described how most of the parental roles had been distributed among the siblings: And there are many memories. She was like a second mother. She took care of us, pampered us … and she was actually mother number two. When mother wasn't home, she and my sister who comes after her in position were just like mother.Almaz's description suggests that maternal care was distributed across multiple siblings rather than concentrated solely in her biological mother, with her sister providing not just practical help but emotional nurturance; this situation created a multi-source attachment system where security came from multiple family members rather than parents alone. This parental attribution was not limited to older siblings but extended to younger siblings as well. Rivka (32), a woman whose younger brother had been murdered four years prior to the interview, described the way they reciprocally protected one another, transcending conventional age hierarchies: He was my little brother but also a father, the one I protected all my life and he protected back … he gave me this kind of protection.Despite being chronologically younger, Rivka's brother provided her with what she experienced as paternal protection. The bidirectionality in her description is notable, suggesting that caregiving flowed in both directions rather than following conventional age hierarchies. Maharat (30), another study participant, powerfully expressed the compound nature of such loss. Maharat had lost his sister to a work accident four years prior to the interview: I lost three parents […] Like, I lost both the parents and her, and the parents are here alive, you know? But that's the feeling.Maharat's statement captures a paradox. Although his parents remained physically present and alive, he experienced their loss alongside the loss of his sister because she had fulfilled the parental functions that his living parents could not fulfil. These accounts demonstrate that when siblings took on attachment roles within families where migration disrupted parental functioning, the loss of such siblings constituted a fundamentally different bereavement experience, involving not merely the loss of a peer but the loss of primary attachment relationships.

And there are many memories. She was like a second mother. She took care of us, pampered us … and she was actually mother number two. When mother wasn't home, she and my sister who comes after her in position were just like mother.

He was my little brother but also a father, the one I protected all my life and he protected back … he gave me this kind of protection.

I lost three parents […] Like, I lost both the parents and her, and the parents are here alive, you know? But that's the feeling.

Parental transformation and bereaved siblings’ caregiving burden

The caregiving burden described in this theme represents the most concrete manifestation of disenfranchised grief in this study: bereaved siblings were not only denied social recognition of their loss, but were simultaneously recruited into active caregiving for their parents, leaving no space – temporal, emotional, or relational – within which their own grief could be acknowledged or processed (Davidson, 2018). Fifteen participants described the transformation of their parents after the loss of their child as perhaps the most profound disruption to family functioning, though parental responses diverged markedly along gender lines, necessitating an examination of maternal and paternal grief responses as distinct phenomena. Prior to this loss (i.e. in terms of acclimating to life in Israel), mothers had shown remarkable resilience. Mazal (30) stated: ‘My mother was … my mother was a lioness.’ Adiso (26) described his mother: ‘She was the figure in the house who, like, held us.’ Solomon (35) said: ‘This was my mother, the one who speaks Hebrew relatively okay compared to my father who barely speaks Hebrew. She managed to do the things she needed to do.’ However, the loss of their child created a profound transformation in both parents, although the nature and expression of this transformation differed substantially between mothers and fathers.

Maternal grief and internal withdrawal

Mothers experienced what appeared to the bereaved siblings as a profound internal withdrawal following the loss of their child. Rivka (32) said: The loss greatly affected them. They're not the same parents they were before. There's no life in them … it really became like a holy of holies. It's forbidden to enter, forbidden to speak, forbidden to touch. Especially for my mother.Rivka invoked the ‘holy of holies,’ the most sacred and inaccessible chamber in the ancient Jewish Temple, to convey how her mother's grief became a space from which she and her siblings were completely excluded. This metaphor captures how some of the mothers’ grief processes created a situation in which bereaved siblings found themselves without maternal emotional support precisely when they most needed it. Some mothers experienced severe psychological distress requiring emergency intervention. Mazal (30) described taking her mother to psychiatric emergency services multiple times, stating: She's not well … she's not with us.Although mothers maintained their external functioning in many cases, their internal withdrawal and inaccessibility to their children created challenges for bereaved siblings attempting to navigate their own grief.

She's not well … she's not with us.

Paternal withdrawal and silent mourning

Paternal grief responses followed a different pattern, characterised by selective disengagement and emotional unavailability at home while maintaining their ability to function in public. Most participants did not spontaneously mention their fathers when describing family responses to the loss of the child; the researchers had to specifically inquire about paternal responses. Mazal (30) said: With my father the connection disconnected. He's here physically but he's not here … Since the death, he speaks to me only with hand gestures … He only speaks with people from the outside. But from the moment he comes home, there's no father.For Mazal, her father's withdrawal into silence at home while continuing to function in public places created a different form of absence than her mother's psychological withdrawal. This pattern likely reflects culturally shaped masculine grief expressions where emotional vulnerability remains constrained by gender expectations, resulting in fathers maintaining external resilience while becoming emotionally absent within the family.

Bereaved siblings as caregivers

The transformation of both parents created situations where bereaved siblings found themselves taking on caregiving responsibilities for parents who were overwhelmed by their own grief. Mazal (30) articulated the complex role reversal that emerged: Maybe from being a mother for my siblings, I became a mother of my mother. It was a complicated sentence but, like, my concern shifted from my siblings to concern for my mother. Like something in that lioness cracked and I had to be the strong one for her.Mazal's metaphor of the ‘cracked lioness’ conveys how her mother's grief process required support from her children. Her description reflects the complicated emotional terrain that bereaved siblings must navigate, simultaneously grieving their siblings while responding to their parents’ suffering. For Moshe (28), the mediating responsibilities he held during his childhood turned into a much larger responsibility for his parents’ basic functioning after the loss of his sibling: After the loss I felt the burden continued to be on me. I'm the one who gave up work and my social life to arrange things for them, I'm the one who made sure they ate something, drank, showered.For Moshe, ensuring that his parents maintained basic self-care became his responsibility, requiring him to sacrifice his work and social life during a developmental period typically focused on establishing independence. Yafit (21) also articulated the developmental bind created by these circumstances: Since I was little I have the vision of leaving home, getting married, moving forward … And suddenly after the loss I became a full-time caregiver. There's really a need to help my parents … Because they don't take care of themselves, and we don't have the heart to abandon them.Yafit's description reveals how normal developmental aspirations toward independence became experienced as the potential abandonment of parents who were overwhelmed by grief. Her use of ‘abandon’ transforms age-appropriate separation into moral betrayal, creating a profound bind where pursuing developmental goals felt impossible. These patterns illustrate how the parents’ responses to the loss of their child created complex situations for the bereaved siblings who found themselves simultaneously grieving their siblings, witnessing their parents’ profound suffering, taking on multiple caregiving responsibilities, and feeling unable to pursue age-appropriate developmental tasks.

Discussion

The focus of the current study was on family dynamics following sibling loss among bereaved siblings from the Ethiopian-Israeli community. Four interconnected patterns characterised the siblings’ experiences: prior to the loss, the children occupied unique mediating positions between their parents and Israeli society due to migration-related challenges; following the loss, complex patterns were identified in the sibling relationships, characterised by both intensification and fragmentation; some of the deceased siblings, prior to their deaths, had fulfilled parental functions within families, thereby creating a compound loss for the surviving siblings (i.e. they lost both a peer and an attachment figure); and following loss, parents underwent a profound transformation diverging along gender lines, with mothers withdrawing internally and fathers retreating into selective silence, leaving bereaved siblings to manage their own grief while also providing parental care.

Extending family systems theory in migration contexts

Family systems theory has been widely applied to understand family reorganisation following loss, with research demonstrating how families typically reorganise roles and relationships to restore equilibrium (Bowen, 1993; Walsh & McGoldrick, 2013). The assumption underlying this body of work is that families possess the adaptive capacity to reorganise effectively when provided with the appropriate support and time. The current findings, however, suggest an important reconsideration of core theoretical assumptions. When hierarchies were already inverted, children already served parental functions, and parents already depended on their children for basic functioning before the loss occurred, losing a child who served essential mediating functions means losing a critical infrastructure that enabled family functioning, not merely a beloved family member. This situation differs fundamentally from loss in families with intact hierarchies where parental capacities may remain available to support the surviving children's grief.

The transformation in the parents following the loss of their child represents a particularly striking finding. Mothers who had previously demonstrated remarkable resilience in navigating migration challenges found their grief overwhelming following the loss of their child, and as such they required support from their surviving children. This finding stands in contrast to assumptions that successfully overcoming past adversity necessarily builds the capacity for facing all future challenges (Masten, 2018; Rutter, 2012). Instead, the findings suggest that coping capacities developed through navigating migration may prove highly context-specific, particularly when confronting losses fundamentally different from the challenges that originally required such resilience. This notion aligns with recent theoretical work questioning whether resilience is a stable, generalised trait that transfers across all domains of adversity (Aalto et al., 2025); these studies have demonstrated that resilience may be domain-specific and situationally bounded.

The pattern of parental internal withdrawal and reduced communication observed in the current study resonates with recent research on intergenerational trauma transmission in East African families in Israel. Bachem et al.'s (2024) study of Eritrean asylum-seeking mother-child dyads in Israel revealed that maternal complex post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (characterised by disturbances in self-organisation including emotional dysregulation, negative self-concept, and interpersonal difficulties) significantly predicted the children's socioemotional difficulties, even after controlling for their direct trauma exposure. The current study extends this understanding to bereavement contexts, demonstrating how parental grief responses in Ethiopian-Israeli families profoundly affect bereaved siblings’ experiences. The gendered dimension of parental grief, a central finding in this study, warrants particular attention. The findings illuminate how mothers, in particular, who had previously demonstrated remarkable resilience in navigating migration challenges, simultaneously experienced a profound internal collapse following bereavement that rendered them emotionally unavailable to their surviving children, even while maintaining some surface-level daily functioning. Fathers, by contrast, withdrew through silence and physical absence. It is important to note that, given the retrospective and interview-based nature of this study, we cannot determine whether participants’ families experienced traumatic migration in any clinically defined sense; what is clear is that migration-related role restructuring occurred in all families and shaped the bereavement context.

The findings also challenge existing conceptualizations of sibling relationships. Prior research has characterised sibling relationships primarily as horizontal bonds between equals involving companionship and rivalry (Funk et al., 2018). The present findings add nuance to this characterisation. When migration challenges prevented these parents from fully meeting their children's attachment needs, the siblings assumed functions typically associated with primary caregivers. This pattern extends the previous documentation of parentification in immigrant families (Orellana et al., 2003) by demonstrating how such role redistributions fundamentally alter the nature of sibling loss. Losing siblings who served as attachment figures represents losing primary attachment relationships, not merely peer relationships. This reconceptualization carries important clinical implications, as current assessment approaches and intervention protocols designed for peer relationship losses may not adequately capture the depth and complexity of grief experienced by bereaved siblings from immigrant families.

The current findings also extend existing research on birth order and bereavement in a notable way. The literature has typically associated caregiving burdens following sibling loss with older, firstborn siblings (Funk et al., 2018). The present study complicates this picture: in the Ethiopian-Israeli context, where migration-related role disruptions had already restructured family hierarchies prior to the loss, caregiving and mediating functions were not confined to older siblings. As documented in Theme 3, younger siblings also fulfilled parental functions for their families – a pattern attributable to the unique dynamics of the immigrant context shared by both the 1.5 and second generations in this community, in which role reversal operated across birth order rather than being determined by it. This finding suggests that in immigrant families where pre-loss parentification is widespread, birth order may be a less reliable predictor of post-loss caregiving burden than in majority-culture families. Future research should examine how migration-related role restructuring moderates the relationship between birth order and bereavement experience.

Disenfranchised grief, double invisibility, and minority status

A well-established finding in the sibling bereavement literature is that siblings’ grief is frequently overshadowed by parental grief, with grieving parents often unable to maintain the same level of emotional availability as before the loss (Davidson, 2018; Doka, 2008). The current findings powerfully resonate with and extend this framework. Not only are the bereaved siblings’ grief needs overlooked by their overwhelmed parents – this family-level invisibility – but the grief of Ethiopian-Israeli bereaved siblings is simultaneously unrecognised at the societal level. In Doka’s (2008) terms, sibling grief is already disenfranchised relative to parental grief. The findings of the current study suggest an additional layer: when the deceased sibling fulfilled parental functions, the bereaved sibling loses not a peer but a primary attachment figure – a loss that remains doubly unacknowledged, both within the family (where parental grief dominates) and within wider society (which does not recognise the magnitude of sibling loss to begin with). This ‘double invisibility’ – being simultaneously overshadowed within the family system and marginalised by societal structures – is further compounded for Ethiopian-Israeli bereaved siblings by their minority status. As a racialized and structurally marginalised community, Ethiopian-Israelis face systemic barriers to accessing grief support services, cultural mismatches in available interventions, and institutional mistrust that reduces help-seeking (Coleman-Brueckheimer & Dein, 2011; Hermann, 2019). The dynamics documented in broader research on racially marginalised communities – including the compounding of structural discrimination with grief – are thus directly relevant to understanding the experiences described in the current study (Lee, 2023). This broader perspective positions the findings not as locally specific, but as illuminating a pattern likely shared by racialized minority communities in other national contexts where structural barriers compound bereavement burden.

Clinical and community implications

The findings point to intervention needs at multiple levels delivered with cultural sensitivity and respect for the Ethiopian-Israeli community's strengths. Clinically, the compound nature of loss in migration-affected families must be recognised during assessment. Clinicians should explore whether deceased siblings fulfilled attachment functions, as sibling loss may represent primary attachment figure loss requiring more comprehensive interventions. Current caregiving burdens must also be assessed, as providing parental care while suppressing personal grief creates elevated risk for prolonged grief.

Given documented challenges in accessing mental health services for the Ethiopian community in Israel, effective interventions must be delivered through trusted community settings by culturally matched clinicians. Culturally adapted peer support groups could provide appropriate spaces for bereaved siblings to share experiences. Family-level interventions should address parental grief with sensitivity; integrative attachment-focused approaches combining trauma processing with family therapy have demonstrated particular promise for families in which attachment disruptions and trauma symptoms co-occur (van der Hoeven et al., 2024), and community-level interventions should work to increase recognition of bereaved siblings’ experiences while respecting community values. The role of social support and bonding in facilitating recovery from traumatic loss underscores the importance of strengthening relational resources alongside individual treatment (Olff, 2012).

Study limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. The study occurred during ongoing security challenges in Israel, which may have influenced participants’ narratives. All participants spoke Hebrew fluently and voluntarily participated, likely introducing selection bias toward individuals more integrated into Israeli society. The retrospective nature of narrative accounts means they reflect current constructions of events. Finally, although the sample size was appropriate for qualitative research, the findings may not represent all bereaved siblings within the Ethiopian-Israeli community. Additionally, no standardised psychological screening was conducted prior to or during the interviews; future research should incorporate validated instruments to assess grief severity and trauma-related symptomatology. Finally, two participants had experienced their loss within approximately 18–24 months prior to the interview; while this range is not atypical in qualitative grief research, acute grief processes may have shaped their narratives differently from participants with greater temporal distance from the loss, and this represents a methodological consideration for interpretation.

Future directions

Longitudinal research examining how bereaved siblings’ experiences evolve across bereavement stages would illuminate both resilience processes and risk trajectories, potentially identifying critical periods for intervention.

Conclusion

This study illuminated the complex dynamics experienced by bereaved siblings from the Ethiopian-Israeli community following the loss of a sibling. The findings demonstrate how migration-related family reconfigurations create unique bereavement experiences: compound loss involving both peer and attachment relationships; grief that remains hidden at multiple simultaneous levels across family and community systems; and developmental challenges preventing age-appropriate progression toward independence. The study extends family systems theory by demonstrating how voluntary yet traumatic migration creates pre-loss disruptions that profoundly shape post-loss reorganisation possibilities. These insights underscore the critical importance of culturally responsive approaches to grief support that honour the community's strengths while addressing both individual bereavement and migration-related family complexities.
