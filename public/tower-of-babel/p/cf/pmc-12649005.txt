Genetic drivers of Chinese hamster ovary cell proliferation revealed by functional genomics
Authors: Jannis Marzluf, Merle Rattay, Maximilian Goertz, Christoph Zehe, Ann-Cathrin Leroux
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCell lines used in biopharmaceutical manufacturing are engineered to optimize protein yield, quality, and production speed. Cellular growth rate is a key bottleneck in the development of biopharmaceutical production cell lines. To uncover genetic drivers of proliferation in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR knockout-screen targeting 17,761 expressed genes. Using a stable Cas9-expressing CHO pool, low-multiplicity of infection (MOI) lentiviral transduction, and cultivation under both shake-flask and 3 L perfusion bioprocess conditions, we maintained >5,000× library coverage with predominantly single-copy integrations. We identified 235 putative growth-enhancing gene knockouts, clustered into 155 functional gene sets, spanning pathways related to chromatin regulation, cell cycle progression, and nutrient sensing. In parallel, we identified a set of 3,110 genes essential for cell fitness (CHO_EG2025), providing a resource for CHO functional genomics. This work establishes a CRISPR screening framework and a genetic blueprint for engineering faster-growing CHO cell lines to support accelerated cell line development.

Introduction

Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells are a dominant platform for therapeutic protein production, accounting for the vast majority of biopharmaceuticals due to their robust growth in bioreactors, high product yields, human-like post-translational modifications, proven safety profile with an extensive history of regulatory approvals, and well-established production guidelines.1,2,3 Despite decades of development work, the genetic determinants underlying favorable growth, productivity, and stability remain poorly defined.4,5,6 In particular growth rate is a key trait whose genetic basis remains unresolved, although it represents a main bottleneck in cell-line development and scale-up. Mechanistic insights into this growth limitation have emerged from proteomic profiling, which demonstrate that high peak viable cell density clones upregulate DNA replication and mitotic cell cycle regulators while downregulating nutrient response and oxidative metabolism,7,8 highlighting candidate mechanisms for growth-enhancing interventions.

Only few studies have indirectly focused on rescuing favorable growth characteristics in limiting conditions by optimizing metabolic byproduct formation. In fed-batch processes, CHO cells consume large quantities of nutrients, but some of this input is diverted toward byproduct formation, such as lactate and ammonia, which accumulate to levels that inhibit growth, productivity, and product quality.9 Metabolic engineering strategies such as disruption of amino acid catabolic genes or branched chain amino acid) pathway regulators such as BCAT1 are proven to be effective in reducing these inhibitory byproducts, improving growth rates and cell density in fed-batch production settings.9,10 However, no systematic studies have investigated improvements for growth-rate engineering under ideal growth conditions.

Recently, re-isolation of CHO cells from primary tissue (CHO-MK) has yielded cell lines with significantly faster doubling times, underscoring the existence of genetically encoded proliferative capacity that might be engineered if the genetic basis was more clearly understood.11,12

Efficient CHO cell growth relies on essential genes that support core cellular functions, proliferation, and survival. Engineering efforts that inadvertently disrupt these genes risk fitness collapse, making accurate essentiality maps critical not only for rational genome minimization but also for maintaining cell viability and stability during cell line engineering and optimization. As modern CHO lines have diverged from their epithelial origins, many native functions may no longer be necessary under optimized, suspension-based bioprocess conditions. While large-scale genomic deletions are technically feasible,13 their successful application requires distinguishing dispensable genomic elements from those that are essential. Recent foundational studies have first created the catalog of CHO essential genes,4,14,15 incrementally building confidence in a core essential gene set. However, functional redundancy, methodological variability, and incomplete genome annotation continue to hinder the definition of a universally applicable CHO essential gene set.15 This has been improved by the availability of high-quality CHO genomic assemblies,16 enabling further rational design of CHO-optimized genome-scale libraries, addressing prior limitations caused by incomplete or misannotated assemblies.

Building on early targeted editing approaches, the CRISPR-Cas917 toolkit has rapidly evolved, from single or limited multiplex strategies18,19 to pooled, genome-scale knockout (KO) libraries14 capable of interrogating thousands of loci in parallel, thus enabling comprehensive functional genomics in mammalian cells. Pioneering CRISPR-based screening efforts in mammalian systems demonstrated the feasibility of genome-wide KO libraries for high-throughput functional interrogation, laying the foundation for adaptation of such platforms to CHO cells.20,21 This rapid technological progress marks a significant departure from earlier engineering strategies, which focused on modifying individual genes to improve specific cellular traits. Early engineering approaches relied on targeted single gene KOs or overexpression, improving specific traits like host cell protein profile,22,23,24 growth control10 or productivity,25,26,27,28 and product stability.23,29 While effective for addressing specific phenotypes, such targeted interventions were inherently limited in scope, struggling to capture genome-wide dependencies or systems-level regulatory interactions. Subsequent efforts expanded the CRISPR toolkit to enable efficient multiplexed editing,5,30 clone-free genetic screening,31 and the application of gene editing to abolish entire pathways, such as lactate biosynthesis, through coordinated multiplexed gene KOs.32,33 With the advent of genome-wide CRISPR screening tools, researchers have begun to interrogate gene function at scale, shedding light on molecular mechanisms underlying key phenotypes. These platforms have been leveraged to uncover genetic drivers of glutamine sensitivity,34 identify productivity-enhancing genes,28,35 enable the expression of difficult-to-express proteins,36 and model CHO fitness determinants.14,35 Further studies identified genetic levers for stress resilience under osmotic pressure37 and glutamine limitation34 and ways for mitigating growth inhibition induced by histone deacetylase inhibitors in CHO cells.38 Despite growing interest in genome-wide perturbation screens in CHO, surprisingly little attention has been directed toward systematically identifying genetic determinants that enhance CHO cell growth, although Kretzmer et al. proposed this concept when introducing tools to enable genome-scale screening in CHO cells.16 Previous genome-wide CRISPR screens in CHO cells have significantly advanced our understanding of gene function by employing standard-coverage libraries,14,35,37 virus-free recombination systems,14,37,38 and diverse cultivation strategies including static batch cultures. These foundational studies established important milestones and enabled large-scale functional interrogation in CHO cell systems. However, certain experimental setups, such as the use of clonal host lines,14,37 public genome annotations,14 and universal gene inclusion,14,35 may introduce constraints when specifically aiming to identify subtle growth-enhancing phenotypes. For instance, using generic annotations may overlook cell-line-specific context, and broad inclusion of genes regardless of expression could dilute relevant signals. These factors can make it more difficult to resolve fine-grained fitness effects that are critical for optimizing CHO cell proliferation. In our study, we aimed to address these challenges by designing a CHO-optimized CRISPR screen that maintains host cell diversity, focuses on the expressed genome, and applies conditions closely aligned with biomanufacturing-relevant growth conditions. Built on a stably integrated Cas9 host cell line without clonal selection and leveraging low-multipliciity of infection (MOI) lentiviral delivery, our approach maintains single-copy sgRNA integration while preserving library heterogeneity. Generating clonal cell lines often reveals substantial heterogeneity in cell characteristics like growth and productivity,39,40,41 we used a pooled Cas9 population to better conserve host cell diversity and avoid clone-specific effects that could confound identification of broadly relevant growth-enhancing KOs. Coupled with a bioreactor-based perfusion setup and multi-timepoint sampling, this approach ensures deep library coverage and high temporal redundancy. The screen identified candidate growth-enhancing KOs and enabled compilation of the CHO_EG2025 essential gene set. Together, these data provide a foundation for CHO systems biology, host cell engineering, and rational design of high-performance production cell lines with faster growth rates.

Results

A reliable CRISPR-competent CHO cell line was established

We established a stable, high-efficiency, and Cas9-expressing CHO pool suitable for pooled lentiviral library delivery without the need for clonal pre-screening and isolation to investigate CHO cell growth and essential genes. To this end, we tested three bicistronic Cas9 expression constructs (CO3626, CO3627, and CO3628), all driven by an SV40 promoter but differing in the translational coupling mechanism to confer G418 resistance (Figure S1; Table S1). Stable CHOCas9+ pools were generated via transfection and G418 selection and assessed for gene editing performance using a plasmid-encoded sgRNA targeting FUT8 (psgRNA-FUT8) coupled to a selection marker encoding green flurescent protein (GFP) and conferring puromycin resistance (Figures S2A and S2B). At day two and two weeks post-puromycin-selection, the KO scores were determined by PCR amplification of the sgRNA cut site, Sanger-sequencing, and subsequent inference of CRIPSR edits (ICEs) analysis.42

All Cas9-expressing pools exhibited high editing activity, with mean KO-scores exceeding ∼62.0 at day two post-transfection (Figure S2B). Among them, CO3627-derived pools demonstrated the highest transient CRISPR competence (mean KO-score, 87.0). Following 13 days of puromycin selection, KO-scores further increased until day 15 to a mean of 91.0 for CO3626 and 97.0 for CO3627, indicating a near-complete editing (Figure S2B). The efficient selection was also reflected in the high proportion of GFP-positive cells (>99%) across all pools on day 15, confirming successful sgRNA delivery and sustained Cas9 activity (Figures S2A and S2B).

To assess the tunability and long-term stability of CRISPR competence via the expressed Cas9, transfections were carried out with all three candidate Cas9-expression plasmids, and each pool was subjected to G418 selection at increasing concentrations (250, 750, and 2,000 μg/mL). Western blot analysis revealed a dose-dependent increase in Cas9 protein levels, with an up to 3-fold increase observed in the high-antibiotic (H) condition (Figures 1A–1C) (see full blots in Figure S3). The lowest editing efficiencies were produced with CO3628-derived pools, although they exhibited the highest Cas9-expression levels. Functional stability of editing was tested at three- and nine-weeks post transfection and retained across CO3626 and CO3627 generated pools, with KO scores between ∼80.0 and 99.0 measured after transient sgRNA delivery expressed from psgRNA-FUT8, regardless of continued selection pressure (Figure 1D). This demonstrates that Cas9-expression is responsive to selective amplification and CRISPR competence remains stable over time.

Previous studies have shown that the presence of Cas9-inactive cells within pooled CRISPR libraries can compromise screen resolution and reduce data quality.43,44 In our stable Cas9-expressing CHO pools, we demonstrate that with sufficiently high and stable sgRNA expression, the entire population achieves near-complete editing, indicating that most cells are functionally CRISPR-competent. To benchmark the CRISPR editing competence of the top-performing CHOCas9+ cell pool, generated under 2,000 μg/mL G418 selection, six sgRNAs targeting well-characterized loci (based on prior internal studies at Sartorius Stedim Cellca GmbH) were delivered via electroporation. Editing outcomes were compared to those obtained by electroporating pre-assembled Cas9- ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes into the Cas9-negative parental CHO cell line. Editing outcomes across all sgRNAs, with KO scores ranging from 53.5 (FN1) to 87.0 (FUT8) in the CHOCas9+ pool, and from 69.0 (EEF1A1) to 96.0 (FUT8) in the RNP control condition (Figure S4) (Figure 1F). Median editing efficiency reached 65.0 in the CHOCas9+ condition and 77.5 with RNPs. Notably, the stable Cas9 expression in CHOCas9+ cells achieved editing efficiencies close to those of the benchmark RNP delivery approach (Figure 1E). The ∼12.5 higher median KO score observed with RNPs likely reflect enhanced complex assembly efficiency in extracellular Cas9-sgRNA assembly before delivery. Pre-assembled RNPs enable high-efficiency genome editing, protect sgRNA from RNase degradation, and enhance overall editing efficiency.45 The lentiviral whole genome pooled CRISPR study was performed with the benchmarked high performing CHOCas9+ pool amplified under 2,000 μg/mL using CO3627.

To assess Cas9 functionality and its impact on screen performance, we performed a proof-of-concept screen using a small library targeting 20 genes, including well-characterized lethal gene controls. We observed a Cas9 expression-dependent depletion of sgRNAs targeting essential genes ACTB, PRDX1, and DHFR, with increasing log2-fold change shifts correlating with higher Cas9 expression levels: 82.4% mean depletion of sgRNAs in low (L), 92.7% in mid (M), and 95.9% in high (H) Cas9-expression pools (Figure 1F). Elevated Cas9 levels selectively affected lethal gene-targeting sgRNAs without altering neutral, non-targeting, or intron-targeting controls, suggesting only minimal increases in off-target effects or amplified unspecific activity of Cas9. To ensure unbiased screening, we employed a diverse, non-clonal cell pool, avoiding the confounding effects that can arise from clonal variability.40,41,46 This design enhances the reliability and interpretability of screening outcomes, supporting reliable detection of fitness-relevant gene effects.

Optimized generation and cultivation of genome-wide cellular CRISPR KO library

A pooled cell-based KO library was generated by lentiviral transduction of the validated, high-efficiency CHOCas9+ cell pool with a comprehensive sgRNA library targeting the expressed genome, encompassing 17,759 genes with 109,543 unique guides. To reduce redundancy and focus on functionally relevant genes, the expressed genome was based on an in-house CHO cell genome assembly comprising 27,017 genes, filtered by gene expression and clustered at 99% sequence identity on mRNA level. While sequence similarity clustering reduced the gene set to 26,353 unique genes, removing genes without expression reduced the number of genes to 18,000. Most of the genes filtered out for lack of expression were also duplicated in the genome, so including them would have increased redundancy and noise in the analysis without adding meaningful biological insight. Finally, 17,759 expressed unique genes were used for further analysis.

Each gene was targeted by six sgRNAs with the library containing non-targeting (1,834) and intron-targeting sgRNAs (2,021) across 114 genes as controls. The experimental workflow, including viral transduction, selection, cultivation, and analytics, is outlined in Figure 2A. An MOI of 0.15 was selected after evaluation of the Poisson distribution model predicting the probability of sgRNA integration per cell (Figure S5A) to maximize the proportion of single-copy viral sgRNA integrations while maintaining technical feasibility transducing billions of cells. This strategy ensures high library resolution and minimizes potential confounding effects from multiple viral integrations and transgene expressions.47,48 Four pools were transduced (P1–P4), with culture P1 transduced at higher library coverage and scaled up for a high-complexity coverage (>5,000x) continuous perfusion cultivation. Cultures P2–P4 were maintained at standard-coverage (>1,000x) in shake-flask conditions being passaged every three to four days. Maintaining high coverage is essential to ensure sufficient statistical power and uniform representation of all sgRNAs, minimizing random dropout or enrichment effects and maximizing sensitivity for detecting changes in sgRNA read counts resulting from phenotypic changes. Previous studies have shown that low coverage can reduce reproducibility and increase noise, whereas high-coverage designs improve hit identification and screen fidelity.47,49 This is particularly critical for the screen conducted in this study, as the goal was not to induce dropout but to preserve full library complexity over an extended cultivation period. Transduction efficiency was measured three days post transduction to confirm infection rates ranging from 8.8% to 12.1% via the virally integrated and expressed GFP (Figure 2B). These results correspond to the calculated MOIs between 0.09 and 0.13, validating the low MOI transduction strategy. Under puromycin selection (5 μg/mL), all transduced populations showed a gradual increase in GFP-positive cells over time (Figure 2C). To assess the distribution of integrated sgRNA copy numbers, 384 single-cell clones were isolated across all transduced cultures, targeting 96 clones for each transduced pool. Orthogonal copy number analysis using both droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) and Sanger sequencing confirmed that the vast majority of clones passing quality checks harbored a single integrated sgRNA construct (ddPCR, 73.8% [286 clones]; Sanger-sequencing, 78.0% [261 clones]) (Figure 2D). ddPCR and Sanger results showed high concordance, with 198 clones confirmed as single copy by both methods. Minor discrepancies were attributable to low sequencing quality or variations in ddPCR reference telomerase reverse transcriptase in some clones; however, this did not affect the overall single-copy integration conclusion.

Following puromycin selection, a transient decline in culture viability to ∼80% was observed for P1–P4 between days 7 and 12, consistent across all conditions. In shake-flask cultures, this initial dip was followed by a full recovery and stable growth dynamics throughout the remaining screening period (Figures S5B–S5D). The untransduced sample P5 failed to survive the applied selective pressure and reached near zero viability on day 11.

The high-complexity KO library (P1) was further scaled up seven days after infection to 3 L perfusion continuous cultivation to enable maximum exponential growth and maintain high library complexity conservation (>5,000× coverage). While the perfusion culture exhibited overall stable viability and growth, periodic increases in viable cell concentration were induced by the intentional suspension of bleed control one day prior to scheduled sampling to ensure sufficient biomass accumulation for downstream genomic DNA extraction (Figure 2E). These increases in biomass were mirrored in metabolite profiles, with transient increases in glutamine consumption and modest lactate accumulation during peak cell densities (Figure 2F). A key objective of this screen was to maintain broad library representation over time, as the experimental design aimed at preserving genome-wide coverage, naturally excluding only genes essential for cell fitness. To this end, theoretical library complexity was tracked throughout the cultivation period by calculating the multiplicity of sgRNA representation relative to the original library size and GFP-positive fraction of cells. All transduced pools maintained complexity at well above the 1,000× threshold required for phenotypic screening post initial transduction (Figure S5C). The perfusion culture (P1) consistently preserved >5,000× coverage, demonstrating the effectiveness of this format in supporting continuous exponential growth while conserving library heterogeneity. Library complexity was temporarily lower at the time of transduction in transduced shake-flasks due to a lower-than-expected infection rate and volumetric constraints.

All analyses confirmed that the selected MOI achieved highly efficient single copy sgRNA integration, enabling consistent cell-based KO library generation. The cultivation setups, both in shake-flasks and perfusion, provided stable conditions for downstream phenotypic growth, enrichment of fast-growing variants, and essentiality screening over a six-week period.

Functional profiling of growth-enhancing and essential fitness genes

In this section, we systematically classify gene KOs impacting CHO cell fitness, distinguishing essential (depleted) genes from growth-enhancing (enriched) genes. This classification underpins subsequent analyses of gene function, pathway enrichment, and regulatory mechanisms in the following sections. The CRISPR KO screen was designed with two primary goals: (1) systematically identify gene KOs that enhance CHO cell growth and (2) expand the catalog of essential genes in CHO cells beyond existing foundational datasets.14 Following high-coverage cultivation, next-generation sequencing and differential sgRNA abundance analysis was performed. Gene- and sgRNA-level statistics were calculated using the MAGeCK’s50 robust rank aggregation (RRA) algorithm, yielding α-RRA scores and α-mean log2-fold changes that quantify statistical confidence and effect size, respectively. Inclusion of intron-targeting gRNAs follows best practices recommended by Cellecta (Mountain View, CA, USA) and supported by the literature, providing robust negative controls while minimizing the risk of splicing-related artifacts through redundancy across six independent sgRNAs per gene.49 To empirically define thresholds for hit calling, 2,021 intron-targeting sgRNAs across 114 genes were used as functionally inert negative controls, capturing editing-related toxicity without impacting gene function. Thresholds for statistical significance were selected such that <0.05% of intron-targeted genes were falsely classified as hits (Figure S6) (Table 1). Only six intron-targeted genes were flagged as significant across all conditions and comparisons, most of which arose under conditions with elevated background noise (e.g., early timepoints, high fraction of low read count sgRNAs). False discovery rate (FDR), raw p-value, and log2-fold change (LFC) were evaluated for each gene. Genes exceeding these thresholds were classified as biologically relevant hits.

For differential sgRNA analysis, six comparisons were analyzed, spanning individual timepoints (weeks 2, 4, and 6), cultivation formats (shake-flask and perfusion), and a pooled replicate analysis, to quantify fitness effects by measuring how sgRNA abundance changes during the screen compared to their initial levels in the plasmid library. Scoring of screen hits and functional grouping of growth-enhancing gene KOs

To enable focused downstream validation of the most promising genetic perturbations enhancing CHO cell growth, we implemented a systematic gene scoring algorithm that integrates replicate consistency, effect size, and sequencing quality across all comparisons. Although the same scoring was applied to depleted gene KOs (negatively selected), this section focuses on the enriched gene KOs (positively selected), as the depleted genes were not analyzed further here.

Starting with the full set of unique gene hits identified across the six differential comparisons (3,110 depleted and 932 enriched genes [Table S12]), each gene was assigned a composite score reflecting three parameters: (i) replicate consistency, defined as the number of comparisons in which the gene was significantly enriched; (ii) effect size, measured by the magnitude of log2-fold change; and (iii) sequencing quality, calculated from median read count, skewness, low-count percentage, and sample purity (GFP percentage at sampling), aggregated into a comparison-level quality score (quality control metrics summarized in Table S3). Replicate consistency received the highest weight in the scoring algorithm (see Methods Section 5.8).

To account for variability across comparisons, log2-fold changes were normalized by each genes minimum non-zero log2-fold change, ensuring the score reflected relative signal strength and avoiding a reduction in score at log2-fold changes between zero and one. The resulting gene scores were subjected to unsupervized hierarchical clustering to determine the optimal number of score clusters, which reported six cluster as optimal (data not shown). In the following k-means clustering, a cutoff was defined for the lowest scoring cluster to be excluded as this was enriched for genes with marginal effects or limited support across comparisons (Figures 5A and 5D). Cleaned single-gene score density plots revealed that the defined clusters were well separated with clear multimodal distributions (Figures 5B and 5E). Following this ranking, a gene set was retained, comprising 2,467 depleted essential genes and 224 enriched genes representing growth-enhancing KOs. These genes constitute the most reliable outcome of the genome-wide CRISPR screen and provide a strategic target space for functional follow-up studies and genome engineering.

To avoid overlooking potentially impactful genes with lower or less consistent scores, a focused evaluation of known tumor suppressor genes from the literature was performed. Among the 932 enriched genes identified prior to filtering, 28 are known tumor suppressors (see Table S4). Of these, 7 surpassed the ranking threshold, while 21 were excluded due to limited consistency across comparisons or lower log2 fold-change values. Despite their exclusion from the final high confidence set, these genes are of interest due to the likelihood of their synergistic effects when targeted in multiplexed KO studies as these are directly linked to cell growth. Accordingly, all 28 tumor suppressor genes were retained for inclusion in the construction of function and pathway-informed gene sets. The initially excluded 21 tumor suppressor genes in addition to the 214 ranked gene hits consist of the final 235 growth-enhancing gene KO candidates (Table S10).

To facilitate the rational design of multiplexed KO strategies and capture potential combinatorial effects, functional gene sets were derived from the ranked enriched gene list. Annotations from KEGG, GO, and Reactome databases were systematically assigned to each gene, capturing both molecular functions and pathway-level associations. Genes sharing functional annotations were grouped together to form initial sets, which were then refined by removing subsets fully contained within larger sets to reduce functional redundancy of target genes and improve technical feasibility for laboratory-based evaluation of targets. Remaining sets were merged using a Jaccard similarity index threshold (>50%). For biological relevance, only pathways related to growth and proliferation were retained based on literature-derived criteria. The key terms used for growth-related filtering are summarized in Table S5 and the resulting 155 gene sets are listed in Table S6.

This systematic curation revealed functionally enriched categories associated with cellular growth and morphogenesis, transcriptional regulation, key signaling pathways (including MAPK, Wnt, and TGF-β), amino acid metabolism, immune modulation, and stress adaptation (Figure 5H). These categories were prominently represented among the final ranked 235 putative growth-enhancing KOs.

To assess the functional convergence within this refined combinatorial gene sets, the 20 most interconnected genes, defined by recurrence across gene sets, were analyzed (Figure 5G). Notably, ANGPT2, CASP9, CDH1, CHD7, DDIT4, EXT1, FOXO3, and MAPK11 appeared in more than ten functional sets, highlighting their broad involvement across multiple growth-relevant pathways and/or molecular functions.

The dominant functional themes, growth regulation, metabolism, immune modulation, and pathway signaling, are the most frequent and interconnected categories in the curated gene sets (Figure 5H). These results pinpoint interconnected gene networks that can be leveraged to engineer CHO cells with an enhanced growth rate.

Discussion

This study establishes a holistic genome-wide CRISPR KO screen in CHO cells, designed specifically to uncover gene disruptions that may enhance cellular proliferation, a key constraint in cell line development and upstream biomanufacturing processes.53 Leveraging stable Cas9 expression, low-MOI lentiviral delivery, continuous perfusion cultivation and high sequencing depth, we achieved high editing efficiency, mostly single-copy integrations, and deep coverage across 17,761 expressed genes, improving screen fidelity compared to prior efforts in CHO cells.14,35,37,38

Our genome-wide CRISPR screen in CHO cells effectively overcomes some limitations of earlier approaches, establishing a platform for identifying essential and putative growth-enhancing gene KOs that warrant further validation. Importantly, our approach circumvents limitations associated with clonal landing pad hosts or virus-free integration systems,14,35,38 which may suffer from clonal bias or limited scalability due to necessary secondary delivery of CRISPR enzymes after transduction. Clonal CHO cell lines are known to exhibit substantial divergence in genetic, ploidy, epigenetic, and transcriptional profiles, leading to variable phenotypes and stability.40,41,46 Our benchmarked CHOCas9+ cell line with a pool origin showed high CRISPR competence, nearly reaching RNP levels of efficiency, with additional benefits on signal noise to ratio shown by increased log2-fold changes in our pilot study. This confirmed that elevated Cas9 expression significantly enhances KO efficiency and the effect size in pooled CRISPR screens, which is consistent with previous reports demonstrating improved screening sensitivity under increased Cas9 activity.49 Moreover, filtering for expressed and duplicated genes reduces library size effectively and may reduce CRISPR toxicity from multi-copy edits. The used low-MOI lentiviral delivery achieved ∼80% single sgRNA integrations, dramatically reducing potential confounding effects from multiple integrations compared to prior studies reporting only ∼40%.14

Our genome-wide screen identified 932 enriched and 3,110 depleted genes prior to scoring, ranking, and filtering, providing an essential gene map and candidate genes for targeted KO for engineering of faster growing CHO cell lines. The use of intron-targeting sgRNAs as empirical nulls,54 combined with stringent multi-temporal resolution, enabled accurate separation of biological signal from screen noise. Non-targeting sgRNAs typically show enrichment in pooled screens, as they do not induce CRISPR-mediated gene editing toxicity. However, intron-targeting control sgRNAs still yield some CRISPR-mediated gene editing toxicity with mostly no long-term phenotypic impact on the cells, serving as improved, functionally inert negative controls. This aligns with studies showing intronic sgRNAs as offering a more accurate baseline than non-targeting controls, facilitating the separation of true biological signals from noise and thereby capturing editing-related toxicity without impacting gene function.54 The majority of genes identified through our screen were depleted rather than enriched, consistent with the expectation that gene disruption generally results in loss-of-function phenotypes leading to impaired cell proliferation.55 Still, a smaller subset of gene disruptions conferred a growth advantage under the applied screening conditions. Functional evaluation of the screen’s results through pathway analysis underscored the biological relevance of the identified genes. Notably, depleted gene KOs were highly associated with core cellular processes essential for CHO cell proliferation, including ribosome biogenesis, proteasome function, DNA replication, RNA processing, and cell cycle regulation. These findings align with prior CRISPR-based essentiality screens in human and other mammalian cell lines, which consistently highlight these pathways as key determinants of cellular fitness.43,55 The consistent depletion of these genes across multiple timepoints and cultivation formats validates the screen’s ability to capture universally essential genes, while also providing a CHO-specific essential gene compendium. Conversely, enriched gene KOs associate with pathways related to ECM-receptor interactions, and amino acid and lipid metabolism, suggesting that disruption of stress-response pathways and metabolic reprogramming can confer a growth advantage under certain conditions. Beyond core proliferation pathways, enriched genes clustered in xenobiotic metabolism and fatty acid processing, indicating roles in metabolic adaptation under serum-free, chemically defined conditions. Fatty acid metabolism supports energy supply, membrane synthesis, and secretion during exponential growth,56 while xenobiotic metabolism may aid detoxification of media-derived compounds and byproducts in CHO bioprocesses.57 These findings suggest that growth-enhancing KOs may act by alleviating metabolic bottlenecks or stress pathways specific to bioprocess-relevant environments. This duality, identifying both fitness-enhancing and fitness-critical pathways, demonstrates the capacity of CRISPR screens to capture a comprehensive landscape of genetic dependencies and offers a powerful platform for rational CHO cell line engineering.

Beyond identifying growth-enhancing gene KOs, our screen captured 3,110 depleted genes, defining an essential gene set, CHO_EG2025. CHO_EG2025 demonstrated the highest overlap and internal consistency compared to 23 published essential gene datasets across human, mouse, and CHO cell lines.14,38,51,55,58 Benchmarking revealed CHO_EG2025 had the greatest concordance with curated resources at an average overlap of 0.69, supporting its value as a comprehensive reference for CHO cell line engineering. Importantly, differences in essential gene sets across studies additionally arise from divergent methodologies, coverage levels, and analytical frameworks, including RNAi, single guide CRISPR, and pooled KO screens.51 Non-functional approaches, such as sequence-based essentiality prediction, frequently yield divergent results due to reliance on computational features rather than empirical fitness effects. We therefore focused on functionally validated genes derived from dropout screens to ensure accurate classification. Analysis of CHO_EG2025 also revealed that most pathway members of essential KEGG pathways were captured within our unique depleted gene set.59 These processes are well recognized as central to mammalian cell viability and proliferation and are consistently enriched in essential gene datasets across mammalian species.47,55

The number of growth-enhancing gene KOs discovered in our study is still fairly high for effective follow-up target validation studies, therefore a multi-parameter scoring framework, incorporating replicate consistency, effect size, and sequencing quality, was applied to the 932 enriched genes identified in our CRISPR screen (the scoring was also applied to the depleted genes but not used further). This scoring was designed to prioritize gene KOs with the highest potential to enhance CHO cell growth in future wet lab validation experiments. After scoring, ranking and filtering, a prioritized set of 214 high-confidence candidate growth-enhancing KOs was retained, which represents a discovery dataset for hypothesis generation rather than experimentally confirmed engineering targets. Experimental validation of these candidates remains a significant bottleneck due to the complexity and resource demands of generating and characterizing multiple KO cell lines in parallel. High-throughput, pooled validation approaches are still being optimized for CHO systems, and single-cell-based validation often requires extensive process development and analytical support. Consequently, computational ranking of candidates remains an essential strategy to focus resources on the most promising targets. Interestingly, several known tumor suppressor genes emerged within the unfiltered enriched gene KO set, but these fell below the stringent scoring threshold due to inconsistent enrichment or moderate effect size across comparisons. For example, FOXO3, a key transcription factor involved in oxidative stress response and apoptosis,60 was enriched in select comparisons but did not consistently meet the threshold for high-confidence hits. Likewise, KDM5A, a histone demethylase implicated in transcriptional repression and chromatin remodeling,61 showed moderate enrichment but insufficient replicate consistency to pass final filtering. Despite these results, all tumor suppressor gene KO candidates were retained in the extended candidate list due to their established roles in growth regulation in cancer studies and potential synergistic effects in multiplexed KO strategies. These genes were explicitly flagged as literature-informed candidates rather than high-confidence screen hits, ensuring their inclusion does not compromise the statistical rigor of the primary ranked set. All 28 tumor suppressor genes (21 below threshold and 7 above) initially flagged in the analysis were carried forward into the function- and pathway-informed gene sets, enabling the construction of multiplexed editing strategies that could reveal additive or context-dependent growth enhancements. Including these additional 21 genes, we derived the final set of growth enhancing gene KOs consisting of 235 putative engineering targets.

These gene KO candidates span diverse cellular functions, including chromatin regulation, cell cycle progression, nutrient sensing, and stress adaptation, hallmarks of phenotypes previously linked to high-density production clones.7,8 Importantly, the pathways of gene KO candidates also intersect with known bottlenecks in fed-batch processes, where inefficient nutrient use and byproduct accumulation, such as lactate and ammonia, can inhibit peak cell proliferation and productivity.9,10 Several enriched gene KOs map to chromatin remodeling complexes, epigenetic regulators, apoptosis markers, and mitotic regulators, indicating that proliferative capacity can be enhanced through targeted modulation of epigenetic and cell cycle control pathways. This is consistent with findings that long-term culture drives transcriptional adaptation in CHO cells toward upregulation of MAPK/ERK and PI3K-Akt signaling.5,46,62 While single-gene KOs may not produce substantial phenotypic changes that significantly enhance CHO cell growth, our classification of 155 functional gene sets supports multiplex KO strategies and pathway-level interventions that can more effectively drive proliferation. These gene sets were constructed using multiple database annotations and likely capture synergistic interactions that could be exploited to boost growth. These enhancements must be carefully validated to ensure they accelerate proliferation without compromising cell stability, product quality, or productivity.10,22,28,34,37

Several of the top 20 interconnected genes in the 155 gene KO sets play central roles in growth regulation and stress signaling. MAPK11, encoding p38β, integrates stress responses and has been shown to suppress proliferation in CHO cells through p38 MAPK signaling (e.g., TREK-1 overexpression), suggesting that MAPK11 disruption could relieve proliferation restraints.62,63,64,65 SMAD1, a key TGF-β/BMP pathway effector, is widely recognized across epithelial and tumor models for imposing cytostatic effects, implying that SMAD1 loss might lift inhibitory controls on cell cycle progression.60,66,67,68 CASP9 has been implicated in CHO apoptosis pathways, CRISPR KO of upstream caspases suuch as CASP3 in CHO cells improves survival and proliferation under stress, consistent with a potential growth-enhancing effect following disruption.69 CDH1 and CHD7 influence adhesion, chromatin remodeling, and transcriptional plasticity,70,71 traits associated with proliferative capacity in both CHO adaptation and cancer biology. Metabolic regulators such as PCK2 and EXT1 further underscore the contribution of nutrient sensing and glycan biosynthesis pathways to growth regulation.72,73 Together, these findings align with established knowledge of proliferative control from both CHO physiology5,7,62,68 and cancer biology,60,63,67,70,72 supporting the idea that disrupting stress, apoptotic, or differentiation checkpoints can unlock latent proliferative potential in CHO cells. Our findings complement previous observations that rapid-growth phenotypes are possible in CHO cells: primary tissue-derived CHO cell lines such as CHO-MK demonstrate shorter doubling times, exceeding typical industrial cell lines.11 These growth advantages, however, have yet to be harnessed at scale, largely due to the absence of a genome-wide map of growth-promoting perturbations, an unmet need for which this study provides an initial dataset to guide future validation and engineering efforts. Altogether, this integrated scoring and annotation framework distills the CRISPR screen into a biologically coherent target space, offering a rational foundation for single and combinatorial genome engineering for enhanced growth rates in CHO cells.

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our approach relies on a validated CHOCas9+ pool cell line, which may not be universally applicable to other CHO lineages, as achieving comparable editing efficiency could be challenging in different backgrounds. Second, the current screening platform is optimized primarily for growth and essentiality phenotypes, lacking enrichment strategies or selectable markers for other desired traits such as product quality, productivity, or stress resistance. As a result, faster-growing cells naturally dominate in pooled screens. Third, implementing this CRISPR screening strategy requires substantial bioinformatics expertize, including genome assembly and custom library design, which may limit its accessibility. Fourth, the reliance on gene KOs means that essential gene functions can only be interrogated in a loss-of-function context, potential gain of function or regulatory effects are not captured in this system. Variability in sgRNA activity leading to inconsistent gene-targeting efficiency and off-target editing events, though minimized through careful design and sgRNA redundancy, cannot be fully excluded and may introduce confounding effects. Lastly, the screen was conducted under standard culture conditions with no product expression, and essential gene requirements may differ under stress or production-relevant conditions such as during biopharmaceuticals production processes. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results and translating them to broader CHO engineering applications.

Despite these caveats, we believe through our improved methodology and utilization of a pooled, non-clonal Cas9-expressing CHO cell line with validated editing competence, a fully sequenced host genome, and reduced redundancy through sequence similarity clustering and expression-based gene target selection, we preserved population heterogeneity and critical factors for screen fidelity to detect subtle phenotypic shifts. While this screen focused on growth enhancement, its essential gene dataset could serve the broader purpose of enabling rational genome minimization. CHO cells, originally adherent ovarian epithelial cells, retain functions that may be redundant in suspension bioproduction systems. Recent studies have shown that large-scale genomic deletions are technically feasible in CHO,13 but such efforts require reliable essentiality maps to avoid fitness collapse. The CHO_EG2025 dataset provides a foundation for such efforts, enabling the development of leaner, more resilient strains tailored for biopharmaceutical production.

In summary, this work delivers a scalable CRISPR-screening platform optimized for CHO systems biology and provides a foundational dataset for exploring growth regulation and essential gene function, which will require further validation to fully harness for cell line engineering. It bridges critical gaps in our understanding of growth regulation and essential gene function, advancing the field toward faster, more rational development of genome engineering strategies for next-generation CHO production hosts. Beyond immediate gene essentiality insights, the data generated here can be leveraged to inform whole-genome metabolic models and guide further functional screens using CRISPR arrays for targeted gene validation. Looking ahead, this platform can be adapted to address other important phenotypes, such as product quality and productivity, although different library enrichment strategies, distinct from long-term cultivation, will be required to analyze the genetic basis for these traits effectively.

Limitations of the study

This study establishes a scalable genome-wide CRISPR screening framework for CHO cells identifying fitness genes and putative growth-enhancing gene modifications; however, limitations of the implemented system should be acknowledged. First, we employed a validated CHOCas9+ pool, which might lose out on cell line-specific context that is more challenging to resolve in a pooled population. One might also perform a similar screen with a high number of growth-depressed clones and identify potential remedies for such cases. Second, our screen was optimized for growth and essentiality phenotypes, traits such as productivity, product quality, or stress resistance were not assessed, as faster-growing clones dominate pooled screens under the applied conditions. These are aspects that need clarification in downstream validation of putative KO candidates. Third, the reliance on gene KOs captures only loss-of-function effects, while gain of function or regulatory mechanisms remain unexplored. Fourth, although sgRNA redundancy and careful design minimized off-target and efficiency variability, these confounders cannot be fully excluded. Fifth, the dataset remains a discovery resource, growth-enhancing candidates were not validated through isogenic KOs, so their effects on doubling time versus survival advantage require follow-up. Finally, screens were performed under standard culture without recombinant protein expression, and essential gene requirements may differ under production-relevant conditions.

Experimental model and study participant details

CHO DG44 cells (female) were obtained from Lawrence Chasin (Columbia University, New York) and adapted in-house for serum-free suspension growth in chemically defined media. Cells were cultured in serum- and antibiotics-free CD DG44 medium (Gibco™, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 6 mM L-glutamine (Sartorius Stedim Cellca GmbH, Ulm, Germany) and 0.1% Pluronic F-68 (Gibco™, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The host cell was cultivated in 50 mL in 250 mL shake-flasks (Corning, NY, USA) at 36.8°C, 7.5% CO2, and 80% humidity and passaged every 2 days to an inoculation density of 2 × 105 cells/mL. From this host cell, a DHFR-competent mock pool was generated in triplicates and pooled for cultivation in hypoxanthine- and thymidine-deficient 4Cell® SmartCHO Stock and Adaptation Medium (Sartorius, Ulm, Germany) supplemented with 6 mM L-glutamine. This mock pool served as the starting point for all experiments. Following transfection, cells were cultivated sequentially in 24-well plates (1 mL; Nunc, Roskilde, Denmark), 6-well plates (4 mL; Nunc, Roskilde, Denmark), and 125 mL shake-flasks (25 mL; Corning, NY, USA) for cell banking and experimentation. Cell density and viability were monitored using CASY® Cell Counter & Analyzer (OMNI Life Science, Bremen, Germany) or NucleoCounter® NC-200™ (ChemoMetec, Allerød, Denmark). Cells were routinely tested and confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Method details

Plasmid construction and cloning

Five constructs were cloned and assembled in this study, one to generate a mock cell line, three to evaluate strategies of creating Cas9 expression pools and one to evaluate sgRNA expression. A proprietary plasmid, pMock, encoding only the dihydrofolate reductase gene was used to generate a mock CHO cell line able to survive in hypoxanthine- and thymidine-deficient media. To generate a CRISPR competent CHO pool, a SpCas9 with 3x N-terminal Simian Virus 40 nuclear localizations signal sequence with one nucleoplasmin nuclear localization signal sequence at the C-terminus was used. The coding DNA sequence also included the neomycin resistance gene (conferring G418 resistance), linked via the Thosea asigna virus 2A peptide, ensuring efficient co-expression from a single transcript for the selection and amplification of SpCas9-expressing cells. This bicistronic expression cassette was under control of the Simian Virus 40 promotor. All Cas9 constructs (CO3626-CO3628), were generated using gBlocks™ Gene Fragments (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA) cloned into a proprietary backbone derived from pSeapP2-Basic (Clontech Laboratories, now Takara Bio, San Jose, CA, USA), originally used for monomeric biopharmaceutical production. Plasmid assembly was performed using the NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), and plasmid propagation was carried out in NEB® 5-alpha Competent E. coli (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Transfection ready plasmid DNA was purified using the PureLink™ HiPure Plasmid Maxiprep Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The sgRNA expression plasmid psgRNA-FUT8 was constructed to evaluate stable Cas9-expressing cells via GFP expression with the same backbone used for stable sgRNA expression in the lentiviral library. Cloning was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cellecta, Mountain View, CA, USA). The bicistronic lentiviral vector enables stable expression of sgRNA under the U6 promoter and selection of transduced cells. It is optimized for use in third-generation lentiviral production systems and co-expresses GFP and the puromycin resistance gene, linked via Thosea asigna virus 2A peptide under the human elongation factor EF-1α-promotor, facilitating both antibiotic selection and fluorescent tracking of transduced cells. All constructs were validated by Sanger sequencing (Microsynth Seqlab GmbH, Göttingen, Germany) and are summarized in Table S1.

CRISPR library design and cloning

A custom CHO genome-wide CRISPR KO sgRNA library was constructed, containing 109 543 unique sgRNAs targeting all 17 759 expressed genes with a target of 6 sgRNAs per gene. The library was designed based on the expressed genome, optimizing sgRNA selection for high on-target activity and minimal off-target effects. It was ensured that off-target sites are not in coding regions of non-target genes. Library design, cloning, and lentivirus production were conducted by Cellecta (Mountain View, CA, USA). Guide RNA selection followed Cellecta’s established criteria, including avoidance of intronic regions near splicing regulatory sites or known regulatory elements, and maintenance of high on-target specificity. Cellecta synthesized the oligonucleotide pool, cloned the sgRNAs into the lentiviral backbone pRSGEGP-U6-sg-EF1-TagGFP2-2A-Puro suitable for stable sgRNA expression to induce CRISPR gene knockouts, and produced high-titer VSV-G pseudotyped viral particles in HEK293T cells ready for transduction.

Cas9 cell pool generation

CRISPR competent CHO pools were generated by transfecting the Cas9-expression plasmid into the CHO DHFR competent mock pool. Transfections were performed in duplicates using the NEON™ Transfection System and NEON™ Transfection System 100 μL Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) delivering 10 μg of the linearized Cas9 plasmid into 1E6 cells at 1400 V with 2 pulses of 20 ms pulse length. Cells were dispensed at a density of 3E5 cells/mL in 4 mL 4Cell® SmartCHO Stock and Adaptation Medium (Sartorius, Ulm, Germany) supplemented to 6 mM glutamine (Sartorius, Ulm, Germany). Two days post-transfection, cells were harvested and three-way split by centrifugation into 6-well plates in media supplemented with G418 (Geneticin®) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at three different concentrations: 250 μg/mL, 750 μg/mL and 2000 μg/mL. The mock-transfected cell pool was maintained under the same selection conditions and served as a selection control to ensure specificity of antibiotic resistance linked to Cas9 expression. The selected cell pools were expanded for cell banking and validation of Cas9 expression by western-blot (see methods section 5.10) and functional determination of CRISPR competence via transient sgRNA delivery as synthetic RNA molecules or expressed from a plasmid (see methods section 5.5).

Genome editing validation

To confirm CRISPR competence, the Cas9-expressing cell lines were tested for genome-editing competence by transfecting either synthetic sgRNA molecules targeting specific genomic loci or the control plasmid psgRNA-FUT8. All gene targets and sgRNA sequences for testing are shown in Table S7. sgRNA sequences were designed using Geneious Prime (v11.0.11; Biomatters, Auckland, New Zealand) or the CRISPR Guide RNA Design Tool (https://benchling.com), targeting early exons common to all transcript variants to induce frameshift mutations and disrupt protein function. Top sgRNAs were selected based on predicted on-target efficiency49 and off-target specificity,107 ensuring that potential off-target sites resided within non-coding regions. Off-target analysis was performed against the fully sequenced and annotated host genome. The used sgRNAs were screened in previous internal studies conducted at Sartorius Stedim Cellca GmbH. TrueGuide™ Synthetic sgRNAs (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were delivered at 7.5 or 20 pmol into 2E5 cells. As controls for editing efficiency, the same sgRNAs were complexed with TrueCut™ Cas9 Protein v2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at a 1:1 molar ratio (7.5 pmol each) to form ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes. RNPs were electroporated into 2E5 cells using the NEON™ Transfection System and 10 μL Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), applying optimized standard conditions: 1700 V, 20 ms pulse width and 1 pulse. To verify the functionality of generated knockouts, sequencing was performed. Genomic DNA was extracted from cell pools using QuickExtract™ DNA Extraction Solution (Lucigen, Middleton, WI, USA). PCR was performed to amplify regions flanking the sgRNA cut site (∼200–300 bp upstream and downstream), and amplicons were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing (Microsynth Seqlab GmbH, Göttingen, Germany). Sequencing data were analyzed using the Inference of CRISPR Edits (ICE) tool (v3.0; https://ice.synthego.com, Synthego, Menlo Park, USA) following the developer’s guidelines. ICE analysis provided both the InDel percentage, representing the overall editing efficiency, and the knockout (KO) score, which reflects the proportion of cells harboring frameshift InDels or fragment deletions likely to result in functional gene knockout.

Lentiviral library transduction

The highest-performing stable Cas9-expressing CHO pool, validated for robust editing efficiency with and without G418 selection, was used for lentiviral transduction with the genome-wide sgRNA library. Two experimental conditions were employed to ensure appropriate library coverage for subsequent phenotypic screening. In the first condition, three biological replicates were generated by transducing the Cas9-expresing CHO pool in three separate 800 mL shake-flasks, each seeded at a density of 6E105 cells/mL in 4Cell® SmartCHO Stock and Adaptation Medium (Sartorius, Ulm, Germany), supplemented with 6 mM L-glutamine (Sartorius, Ulm, Germany). Transduction was carried out at a target multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.15 using the lentiviral-packaged pooled sgRNA library. The target MOI was selected based on ideal Poisson-distributed integration probabilities (References to Table S8; Equation 1, Variables in Table S8)), balancing practical feasibility with the goal of maximizing the proportion of single-copy sgRNA integrations. LentiTrans (Cellecta, Mountain View, CA, USA) was added at final 1× concentration to enhance transduction efficiency. Following overnight incubation under standard conditions (36.8°C, 7.5 % CO2, 80 % humidity, 110 rpm linear shaking), cells were washed twice with 1× DPBS (Gibco™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to remove residual viral particles and additives. Puromycin selection (5 μg/mL) was applied two days post-transduction. In the second condition, a single replicate with larger volume was generated by pooling transduced cells from three 800 mL shake-flasks, resulting in a total culture volume of 2.4 L. This approach was scaled up and used for perfusion cultivation. Transduction efficiency and puromycin selection was monitored via GFP expression encoded by the lentiviral vector using CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Viability and cell density were monitored for passaging using the CASY® Cell Counter & Analyzer (OMNI Life Science, Bremen, Germany) or the NucleoCounter® NC-200™ (ChemoMetec, Allerød, Denmark).

Cell culture screening

To assess the impact of gene knockouts on cellular growth, the CRISPR KO library pools were cultivated under two upstream-relevant conditions: batch culture in shake-flasks and perfusion in a Biostat® RM (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). For batch cultivation, the CRISPR KO library were seeded into 2 L vented shake flasks (Corning, NY, USA) with a working volume of 800 mL, cultivated in 80 % humidity at 36.8°C and 7.5 % pCO2 and shaken at 103 rpm orbital conditions with 5 μg/mL puromycin added. Cultures were sampled at three distinct time points post-transduction (Week 2, Week 4 and Week 6) to monitor sgRNA representation via next-generation sequencing (NGS). The total runtime from initial lentiviral transduction was 38 days. To support steady-state cultivation at sufficient biomass and high library complexity, perfusion was implemented as a tool to maintain consistent exponential growth rather than to achieve the very high cell densities typical of industrial perfusion processes. To this end, the high-complexity KO library pool was cultivated in a Biostat® RM 20|50 (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) with a working volume of 3 L. Perfusion was controlled based on offline viable cell concentration data obtained via Vi-CELL XR (Beckman Coulter, USA), extrapolated between sampling points to maintain stable culture conditions. Bleeding Rates were based on daily predicted growth extrapolated from last measurement to keep VCC between 1E6 and 2E6 cells/mL, the resulting CSPR ranged from 350 to 500 pL/cell/day. The feed was controlled gravimetrically to ensure constant culture volume. Bleed volume was corrected manually based on daily offline VCC measurements to maintain cell density within target range of 10-20E5 cells/mL to keep cells in exponential growth phase, maintain high library coverage and avoid overgrowth. Process parameters including pH, dissolved oxygen (DO), temperature, and rocking motion were regulated via a predefined recipe within the MFCS software (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). The previously described post-transduction time points (Week 2, Week 4 and Week 6) were also used here for sample collection to monitor growth dynamics and gene enrichment under intensified culture conditions. The use of a perfusion strategy allowed for >5.000x library coverage, maintaining consistent exponential growth without the need for disruptive cell splitting.

NGS and computational analysis

To evaluate the enrichment or depletion of knocked out genes, genomic DNA extraction, library preparation, and next-generation sequencing (NGS) of cell pellets collected throughout the screening period were performed by Cellecta (Mountain View, CA, USA) using the Illumina HiSeq platform, with a target depth of 100 million reads per sample. Cellecta also conducted demultiplexing, alignment, and initial processing of FASTQ files using proprietary Python-based pipelines, delivering deconvoluted read count tables for all samples. Subsequent data processing and analysis were performed using custom Python and R scripts.75 All R workflows were implemented in R v4.4.275 using the packages MAGeCK v0.5.9.5,50 dplyr v1.1.4,76 tidyr v1.3.1,77 openxlsx v4.2.7.1,78 ggplot2 v3.5.1,79 reshape2 v1.4.4,80 pheatmap v1.0.12,81 clusterProfiler v4.12.6,82 org.Hs.eg.db v3.19.1,83 VennDiagram v1.7.3,84 ReactomePA v1.48.0,85 readxl v1.4.4,86 purrr v1.0.2,87 stringr v1.5.1,88 scales v1.3.0,89 e1071 v1.7-16,90 readr v2.1.5,91 knitr v1.49,92 kableExtra v1.4.0.93 All Python workflows were implemented in Python v3.13.294 using MAGeCK v0.5.9.5,50 pandas v2.2.2,95 matplotlib v3.9.2,96 seaborn v0.13.2,97 and numpy v1.26.2.98 The complete analysis pipeline and reproducible code are archived on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15811670) and are also accessible via the project GitHub repository (https://github.com/Jannis-P-Marzluf/Genetic-Drivers-of-Chinese-Hamster-Ovary-Cell-Proliferation-Revealed-by-Functional-Genomics.git).

Copy number analysis

To assess copy number distribution within the whole-genome CRISPR KO screen, 96 clonal CHO cell lines were isolated for each transduced pool. Single-cell clones were generated 22 days post-transduction using the CellCelector™ (Sartorius ALS GmbH, Jena, Germany), a nanowell-based, image-verified cloning platform. Cells were seeded into nanowell plates containing proprietary cloning medium and scanned on the day of seeding to ensure monoclonality. Outgrown clones were selected and expanded into 384-well plates, then transferred to 96-well plates for further cultivation and genotyping. Both 96- and 384-well plates were cultivated in a static mode. Genomic DNA was extracted from each clone using the Quick-DNA 96 kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Copy number determination was performed using two orthogonal methods on the same DNA template: Sanger sequencing (Microsynth Seqlab GmbH, Göttingen, Germany) and droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) using the QX200™ Droplet Digital™ PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). For Sanger sequencing, the sgRNA expression cassette was amplified via PCR and sequenced using a primer located upstream of the guide region. Reads were mapped against the sgRNA reference database using custom R scripts. Clones showing a single, unambiguous sgRNA sequence mapping to the library were classified as single-copy clones. Clones with mixed sequencing traces downstream of the guide region were classified as likely multi-copy. In parallel, ddPCR was performed using virus-integrated TagGFP2-specific primers and probes in duplex with Tert as a reference. The ratio of TagGFP2-positive to Tert-positive droplets was used to estimate copy number.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed to assess Cas9 protein levels across CHO cell pools. Lysates were prepared from ∼250,000 cells by centrifugation and resuspension in 1× Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) supplemented with DTT (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Samples were denatured at 90°C for 5 min. A total of 10 μL of lysate was loaded per well onto 4-20% TGX Stain-Free™ gels (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), followed by electrophoresis at 220 V for 35 min. Proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using the Trans-Blot Turbo™ Transfer System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) with the high molecular weight program. Membranes were blocked in Bio-Rad blocking buffer (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Fluorescently labeled antibodies were used for protein detection: Cas9 (Sc-517386 AF790), β-actin (Sc-47778 AF647), and GAPDH (Sc-365062 AF790) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA). Blots were washed three times for 5 min in PBST (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and imaged using the ChemiDoc™ Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Band intensities were quantified using GelAnalyzer 19.1, normalized to housekeeping protein signals, and used for semi-quantitative comparison across samples.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Expression filtering of host genome

To reduce redundancy of the targeted genes and ensure their functional relevance, an annotated genome assembly of the host cell line was filtered to only keep expressed genes in one copy if duplicated. For the expression-based filtering, transcriptomic count data from a 14-day standard fed-batch process of 11 bioreactor vessels in an Ambr® 250 system (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) harbouring phenotypically heterogenous clones were used. To cover each physiological stage of the bioprocess including the seed culture, 10 to 14 timepoints per sample were sequenced as 150 bp paired-end reads with >30 Mio. reads per sample, after library preparation of strand-specific cDNA libraries, on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 System (Eurofins Genomics Europe Sequencing GmbH, Konstanz, Germany). Gene expression counts per sample, timepoint and gene were generated using a Snakemake v7.8.399 pipeline with Trimmomatic v0.39100 for adapter and quality trimming, Kallisto v0.48.0101 for pseudo-alignment to the host genome and R v4.275 with the Bioconductor packages tximport v1.26.0102 and rhdf5 v2.42.0103 for import of the count data.

Genes with a row sum lower read count than 100 across all 148 samples were removed. To minimize the risk of excluding biologically relevant genes, expression filtering was based on RNA-seq data from 148 transcriptomic dataset samples spanning diverse CHO cell lines, culture states, and process conditions. While we cannot fully rule out omission of genes expressed only under rare or untested conditions, this approach ensures that filtering reflects a broad range of manufacturing-relevant contexts and prioritizes genes likely to contribute meaningfully to CHO cell biology. For the filtering based on sequence similarity, mRNA sequences of all annotated genes were clustered with vsearch v2.22.1104 function --cluster_fast and option --id set to 0.99. mRNA sequence names were extracted with bash commands and translated into gene identifiers using a custom conversion table. Gene sets resulting from either expression- or similarity-based filtering were compared in R and the overlapping genes were filtered using packages plyr v1.8.9105 and tidyverse v2.0.0.106 This yielded the final expressed genome with 17 759 genes which was used for library design.

Statistical analysis of read counts

NGS Read counts were analyzed using MAGeCK package v0.5.9.550 to evaluate gene- and sgRNA-level enrichment or depletion across time points and conditions. MAGeCK’s robust rank aggregation (RRA) and false discovery rate (FDR) methods were applied according to the developer’s guidelines.50 All code used for MAGeCK analysis, including the commands for read count normalization, gene ranking (RRA and α-mean log2 fold change), and statistical testing, are fully documented and executable within the Python/MAGeCK-code.ipynb notebook on GitHub (https://github.com/Jannis-P-Marzluf/Genetic-Drivers-of-Chinese-Hamster-Ovary-Cell-Proliferation-Revealed-by-Functional-Genomics). This includes example MAGeCK test calls and instructions to reproduce all gene-level statistics and normalized count outputs. Comparisons were made against the plasmid library as baseline across three post-transduction time points (2, 4, and 6 weeks), as well as across cultivation formats (shake-flask vs. perfusion). Biological replicates were treated as independent conditions for six differential comparisons. Gene-level hit calls were filtered using thresholds based on intron-targeting sgRNAs to distinguish signal from random background noise (Figure S6). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was conducted to identify biological themes associated with gene enrichment or depletion. Mean log2 fold changes from MAGeCK were used to rank genes, which were then mapped to Entrez IDs for KEGG59 pathway enrichment. Pathway enrichment results were organized into higher-level functional categories (Metabolism, Genetic Information Processing, Environmental Information Processing, and Cellular Processes) to facilitate biological interpretation. To prioritize gene knockouts based on growth-enhancing effects observed in the pooled CRISPR screen, we implemented a multi-step scoring framework. Each sample's sequencing quality was first quantified using a weighted sum of normalized quality metrics (Equations 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6)(All Variables described in Table S9). For each sample, the following metrics were calculated: median read count, standard deviation (SD), skewness, and the percentage of sgRNAs with counts <100. Sample purity (% GFP-positive cells) was obtained from flow cytometry records. Each quality metric was normalized to a 0–1 scale, with higher values indicating better quality. Positive indicators (e.g., purity, median) were min–max scaled, while negative indicators (e.g., SD, skewness, low-count percentage) were inverted. A composite quality score per sample was computed as a weighted sum of these normalized metrics, with the following weights: Standard Deviation (SD) (0.35), low-count percentage (0.30), skewness (0.15), median (0.15), and purity (0.05). The scaled (0 to 1) quality scores of all samples within a comparison were then averaged to compute a comparison-level quality score (Supplementary Equation 3), which was integrated with replicate count by using a scaling factor (0.1 × replicate number) to produce a relative score for each comparison (Supplementary Equation 4). These relative comparison scores were used in downstream gene ranking to weight log2 fold changes by data quality and reproducibility. Final gene scores were calculated by aggregating weighted log2 fold changes (FCs) across comparisons, normalized by the smallest non-zero FC per gene, and filtered to exclude comparisons where genes do not appear as hits (Supplementary Equations 4, 5 and 6). This scoring approach emphasizes consistency across replicates and prioritizes genes with reproducible enrichment patterns across comparisons in high-quality comparisons. Data distribution assumptions were evaluated through exploratory visualization and met the assumptions of the statistical approaches applied. Statistical details, including exact values of n, definitions of n, measures of center (mean) and variation (SD), and statistical tests applied, are provided in figure description and the public repositories (GitHub and Zenodo), and explained in the results section. Biological replicates were independent Cas9 pool transductions. No randomization or blinding was performed, as these are not applicable to pooled in vitro screens. No data points were excluded. Data distributions met assumptions of the statistical tests applied. All analysis code is publicly available (Zenodo DOI: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15811670), and raw FASTQ data are deposited under BioProject ID 1288848.
