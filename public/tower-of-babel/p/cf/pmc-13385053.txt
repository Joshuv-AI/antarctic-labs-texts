A review of deep learning-based multimodal data integration of lung cancer
Authors: Ya Qin, Simin Wang, Dan Wu, Yanhua Fei, Weiping Ding, Qiong Wang, Runwei Guan, Xiao Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis is a narrative review that provides a perspective on the recent advances in deep learning (DL)-driven multimodal data integration for lung cancer. Lung cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Despite growing awareness and therapeutic innovations, the majority of cases are diagnosed at advanced stages, resulting in persistently poor survival rates. In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated transformative potential in oncology research, particularly through the integration of heterogeneous biomedical data modalities. The fusion of radiological imaging, histopathological slides, genomic and transcriptomic profiles, and electronic health records has consistently outperformed unimodal approaches by capturing complementary biological and clinical information. DL-based multimodal integration frameworks have shown promise in improving diagnostic accuracy, stratifying patients according to therapeutic response, and predicting long-term prognosis, thereby contributing to precision oncology. By leveraging the synergistic strengths of diverse data sources, multimodal AI models can enable more accurate and individualized strategies for diagnosis, treatment planning, and longitudinal disease monitoring, ultimately improving clinical outcomes for patients with lung cancer.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Deep learning, Multimodal data integration, Lung cancer

Introduction

Context

Globally, lung cancer is the most prevalent malignancy and remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality across both sexes [1, 2]. In 2022, lung cancer was estimated to account for 12.4% of all newly diagnosed cancer cases and 18.7% of total cancer-related deaths worldwide [3]. Tobacco smoking, air pollution, biomass fuel exposure, and chronic lung diseases are well-established risk factors for lung cancer and substantially contribute to its global disease burden [4–7]. The incidence and mortality of lung cancer are reducing in the United States due to lower tobacco smoking; however, they are still on the rise in many developing countries [8]. While lung cancer incidence and mortality remain consistently higher in men than in women, the sex-related disparity has progressively narrowed over recent decades [9].

Traditional diagnostic methods for lung cancer include radiographic screening such as chest radiography and computed tomography (CT), sputum examination, bronchoscopy, and lung tissue biopsy [10–12]. Based on histopathological classification, lung cancer is broadly divided into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC), with NSCLC accounting for the majority of cases [13]. In recent years, liquid biopsy, including circulating free DNA and circulating tumor cells, has emerged as a safer and faster new method for diagnosing lung cancer [14, 15]. However, due to reasons such as cost and accessibility, the early diagnosis of lung cancer remains a major clinical challenge.

In the past, the main treatment methods for lung cancer included thoracic surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. In recent years, breakthroughs have been made in the treatment of lung cancer, especially NSCLC, with a greater emphasis on individualized treatment. Targeted therapy for driver genetic mutations, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), epidermal growth factor receptor thr790met (EGFR T790M), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), and immunotherapy against immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), have significantly improved the survival of advanced lung cancer patients [16–19]. Although awareness and therapeutic strategy of lung cancer are improving, the survival remains worse. The key reason is that approximately 62% of patients have advanced lung cancer at diagnosis [20].

Therefore, the development of accessible, cost-efficient, and rapid diagnostic platforms, coupled with robust methods for treatment response monitoring and prognosis prediction, remains an urgent priority. In this context, artificial intelligence (AI) holds great promise in advancing early detection, precision therapeutics, and outcome prediction in lung cancer (Fig. 1) [21–24]. Multimodal data integration approaches play a crucial role in the research of lung cancer by overcoming the limitations of a single data source. Most of the existing reviews focused on introducing the application of multimodal learning in independent tasks such as the diagnosis or prognosis of lung cancer. To address this limitation, the present review adopts the full-cycle clinical decision-making pathway of lung cancer management as its central framework, focusing on the application of multimodal learning in diagnosis, treatment response assessment, and prognostic prediction, and systematically elucidating how these approaches optimize decision-making at each key clinical juncture.

Deep learning-based multimodal data integration in cancer research

The application and challenges of multimodal learning in cancer research

In recent years, AI has undergone rapid advancement in the medical domain, especially in the area of cancer [25–27]. As a specialized branch of machine learning, deep learning (DL) leverages artificial neural networks to model highly complex, nonlinear patterns in biomedical data, thereby significantly enhancing the accuracy of cancer diagnosis, therapeutic decision-making, and prognostic prediction [28, 29]. Although unimodal DL frameworks have demonstrated substantial progress in oncology, they remain inherently limited by their reliance on a single data source. In real-world clinical practice, oncologists routinely integrate heterogeneous information, such as electronic health records, histopathological findings, radiological imaging, and genomic or transcriptomic profiles, when formulating treatment strategies. To address this gap, DL-based multimodal learning has attracted increasing attention, particularly in biomedical research, owing to its capacity to integrate and jointly learn from diverse data modalities (Fig. 1) [30].

Highlights

In this review, we respectively listed the applications of multimodal data integration based on DL in the diagnosis, prediction of treatment effects or side effects, and prognosis of lung cancer. Compared with traditional single-modal models, multimodal models improved predictive performance and enhanced robustness by integrating clinical, imaging, genomic data and so on. Furthermore, a critical analysis was conducted on the challenges encountered by multimodal learning in lung cancer and how to overcome them. The prospects for the application of multimodal learning in lung cancer were further prospected, providing operational suggestions for researchers.

Here, we list the following contributions:

A multimodal integration framework oriented by the clinical decision-making path was proposed to achieve closed-loop clinical management

At the initial diagnosis stage, by integrating data from imaging, pathological, genomic, and clinical data, the management challenges of uncertain pulmonary nodules were addressed, and the subtypes and stages of lung cancer were predicted. During the treatment selection stage, multiple data were integrated to predict the responses of different treatment options, including surgery, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, thus moving towards dynamic treatment planning. During the follow-up and prognosis stages, risk models were constructed using multimodal data to achieve more accurate prognosis prediction. We believe that the ultimate value of multimodal learning lies in closed-loop clinical intelligent management, that is, through the learning model, the post-treatment response data is fed back to the diagnostic and prognostic models to achieve continuous optimization. This review provided a logically rigorous conceptual framework for this vision.

Critical analysis of implementation bottlenecks

Beyond merely summarizing current achievements, this review provides a critical analysis of the persistent bottlenecks hindering the translation of multimodal AI from “code to clinic.” First, models often fail to generalize beyond the controlled datasets they are trained on, due to real-world data variability in imaging, protocols, and clinical documentation. Second, AI models are inherently limited by their data-driven nature, unable to account for unobserved or dynamic clinical variables such as treatment adherence and individual immune status, which introduces bias and risks. Third, the “black-box” nature of complex multimodal systems lacks interpretability, undermining clinical trust, creating accountability issues, and preventing AI from assuming an independent decision-making role in patient care.

Future perspectives

To bridge the gap between algorithmic research and clinical utility, we propose a concrete, three-phase roadmap for implementation: (1) Standardization, focusing on open benchmarks and harmonized protocols; (2) Validation, emphasizing the shift from retrospective studies to prospective, multi-center trials; and (3) Integration, aiming for the embedding of models into Electronic Health Record (EHR) workflows. Additionally, we highlight the disproportionate scarcity of research on SCLC compared to NSCLC. We delineate specific future directions for this neglected subtype, advocating for the establishment of specialized multi-centric consortia to capture high-quality multimodal data for SCLC, thereby ensuring that the benefits of precision oncology extend to all lung cancer populations.

Multimodal learning in lung cancer research

Multimodal fusion

Multimodal data fusion aims to extract and integrate complementary information from heterogeneous data sources to enable more accurate and robust clinical decision-making, and has become increasingly important in medical applications [37]. Depending on the integration stage within the computational pipeline, multimodal fusion strategies are typically categorized into early fusion, intermediate (representation-level) fusion, and late (decision-level) fusion (Fig. 3) [38]. Each fusion paradigm presents distinct advantages and limitations in terms of information preservation, model complexity, and interpretability. Consequently, the choice of fusion strategy is often guided by the characteristics of the data modalities involved, the specific clinical task, and domain expertise.

Multimodal data fusion. (a) Early fusion integrates data of all modalities including raw data and manual data at the input level. (b) Late fusion builds models on each modality independently, and then integrate predictions at the decision-making level. (c) Intermediate fusion lies between early fusion and late fusion

Early fusion

Early fusion refers to the integration of multimodal data, ranging from raw signals to handcrafted feature, directly at the input level [39]. This strategy offers the advantage of enabling the model to capture fine-grained inter-modality interactions while requiring the training of only a single unified architecture, thus maintaining relative structural simplicity. However, early fusion is inherently constrained by its reliance on precise spatial or temporal alignment across modalities, which may be difficult to achieve in heterogeneous biomedical data. Consequently, this approach is most appropriate for scenarios where multimodal inputs are highly synchronized and well-registered, such as radiology-pathology correlation studies or multimodal physiological signal monitoring. For diagnosis in NSCLC, Zhang et al. combined DNA methylation levels, nodule subtype, and DL predictions from low-dose CT (LDCT) images into a logistic regression model, achieving an area under a receiver operator characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.894 in independent validation, outperforming the Mayo Clinic model (0.519) [40]. Early fusion allows cross‑modal interactions from the outset but does not inherently handle missing modalities and may amplify noise.

Late fusion

Late fusion refers to an approach in which independent models are trained separately on each modality, and their outputs are subsequently integrated at the decision-making stage; hence, it is also commonly termed decision-level fusion [29]. This strategy allows for the use of modality-specific architectures that are optimally tailored to the unique statistical and structural properties of each data type, thereby enhancing within-modality performance. However, a key limitation of late fusion lies in its inability to fully exploit cross-modal correlations and synergistic feature interactions, potentially leading to suboptimal integration when inter-modality dependencies are clinically informative [39]. It is the most common strategy in NSCLC multimodal learning due to its flexibility. Kuang et al. built a multimodal DL radiomics (MDLR) model for postoperative progression in stage I solid NSCLC, where a ResNet18 extracted DL signatures from CT, which were then combined with clinical factors via an extreme learning machine, yielding the AUC of 0.885 and 0.861 in the internal and external sets respectively, significantly better than the clinical model with the AUC of 0.675 [41].

Intermediate fusion

Intermediate fusion, positioned conceptually between early and late fusion, represents the most widely adopted and arguably the most powerful strategy for multimodal integration [39]. In this paradigm, high-level feature representations are first extracted independently from each modality using dedicated encoders. These modality-specific embeddings are then fused within the latent space through sophisticated interaction mechanisms—such as attention modules, cross-modal transformers, or graph-based fusion layers, to facilitate comprehensive information exchange and integration prior to decision-making. In the diagnostic domain, Cai et al. proposed a frequency domain transformer model, which transformed histopathology images to the frequency domain via discrete cosine transform, applied a coordinate‑spatial attention module to reassign channel weights, and then fused Y, Cb, Cr features through a Cross‑Domain Transformer Block before combining with genomic data, achieving 93.2% AUC for classifying lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous carcinoma [42]. For immunotherapy response, Vanguri et al. implemented DyAM, a dynamic attention‑based multiple instance learning model that assigned attention weights to each modality, robust to missing data [43]. By combining the strengths of both early and late fusion, intermediate fusion effectively captures cross-modal dependencies while retaining modality-specific discriminative features. Nevertheless, this approach is associated with increased training complexity and computational demand, which can pose challenges for large-scale biomedical applications.

Empirical comparison and clinical application of multimodal strategies in lung cancer

While early, intermediate, and late fusion strategies offer varying technical advantages, their true value in lung cancer research must be evaluated through the lens of empirical performance and clinical utility. Clinicians and researchers frequently face critical questions regarding the practical benefits of these multimodal integrations.

Meaningful versus marginal advantages

Multimodal learning yields a meaningful, transformative advantage primarily when it integrates modalities that contain orthogonal biological information. For example, fusing macroscopic anatomical features from CT scans with microscopic molecular data provides a comprehensive view of the tumor. Conversely, when models integrate highly correlated data, the performance gains are often marginal. For instance, Kuang et al. demonstrated that a MDLR model combining CT images with clinical factors achieved an AUC of 0.885 for predicting postoperative progression, which was not statistically superior to the AUC of 0.882 achieved by the DL signature (DLS) alone (p > 0.05) [41]. This indicated that the clinical variables in this specific cohort may have provided redundant information already captured by the radiomic features.

Clinical significance of fusion strategies in lung cancer

In lung cancer applications, early fusion enables joint modeling of raw multimodal data, but is highly sensitive to missing modalities and alignment issues, which limits its clinical robustness. Late fusion is simple and resilient to incomplete data, yet it fails to capture cross-modal interactions that are often critical for tasks like predicting treatment response or molecular subtypes. Intermediate fusion strikes the best balance: it learns modality‑specific representations before controlled cross‑modal integration, consistently improving performance in key clinical practice. In summary, each fusion strategy has its own applicable situations: early fusion suits well-aligned, high-quality data, late fusion is ideal for independent modalities or clinical settings with frequent missing data, and intermediate fusion demonstrates more balanced performance advantages in most studies, though its ultimate clinical significance still needs to be judged based on the specific data and task.

Genuine clinical significance versus benchmark increments

A critical appraisal of current literature requires differentiating between modest increments in. benchmark metrics and improvements of genuine clinical significance. A 1–2% increase in AUC may be mathematically optimal but clinically inert if it does not alter patient management. However, when multimodal models address critical clinical blind spots, their impact is profound. For example, Zhong et al. developed a multimodal DL nodal metastasis signature (DLNMS) integrating PET and CT radiomics that effectively identified occult lymph node metastasis (ONM) missed by standard PET/CT reads [44]. Such improvements are of genuine clinical significance because predicting ONM preoperatively directly alters the surgical approach, ensuring adequate lymph node dissection and preventing undertreatment. Ultimately, the success of a fusion strategy should be measured not solely by algorithmic metrics, but by its capacity to redirect the clinical decision-making pathway safely and effectively.

Methods

Literature search strategy

This is a narrative review of the application of multimodal learning in lung cancer. Given the broad and rapidly evolving nature of this field, we did not perform a systematic review but instead conducted a focused, non-systematic literature search to identify representative and impactful studies.

The literature search was performed in the PubMed and Web of Science databases, covering the period from January 2022 to September 2025. The search strategy combined keywords related to the following three domains: lung cancer (“lung cancer”, “non-small cell lung cancer”, “NSCLC”, “small cell lung cancer”, “SCLC”), multimodal learning (“multimodal learning”, “multimodal data”, “radiomics”, “pathomics”, “data integration”, “fusion model”), and artificial intelligence (“machine learning”, “deep learning”, “artificial intelligence”).

Study selection

Titles and abstracts of the retrieved records were independently screened by two authors. Given the narrative nature of this review, we did not apply strict inclusion and exclusion criteria for study design or sample size. Instead, we prioritized studies that: (1) proposed or validated a multimodal DL for lung cancer diagnosis, treatment response prediction, or prognosis; (2) were published in peer-reviewed journals or major conferences; (3) were considered by the authors to be representative of key methodological trends or clinical applications. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. The initial search yielded 30 records. Following full-text assessment, 15 primary studies were selected for inclusion in this review. Data extraction and synthesis

From each included study, we extracted information on: (1) study characteristics (first author, year, data source); (2) types of data modalities integrated (e.g., clinical, imaging, genomic); (3) fusion strategy (early, intermediate, or late fusion); (4) DL architecture; and (5) key findings and performance indicators. Given the heterogeneous nature of the studies, data were synthesized narratively, organized by clinical task (e.g., diagnosis, treatment response prediction, and prognosis).

Multimodal learning in lung cancer diagnosis

Early diagnosis

Genomic and imaging data integration

Previous studies have revealed the significance of short stature homeobox 2 gene (SHOX2)/prostaglandin E receptor 4 gene (PTGER4) DNA methylation in the diagnosis and staging of lung cancer [45, 46]. However, these two studies included a large proportion of patients with advanced lung cancer. There is an urgent need for a method to diagnose early-stage lung cancer. In a retrospective study, Zhang et al. constructed DL models based on LDCT with different convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures including Vgg 16, ResNet 34, Xception, and MobileNet to distinguish malignant from benign pulmonary nodules. The subjects were divided into a training set of 257 individuals and an independent validation set of 42 individuals. 5-fold cross-validation was used to train and validate DL models. There were no significant differences between these two groups in terms of age, gender, smoking status, histopathology and nodule size. Subsequently, a multimodal prediction model was developed to identify early-stage lung cancer better with the highest AUC of 0.894 (95% CI: 0.775-1.000) than the Mayo Clinic model with the AUC of 0.519 (95% CI: 0.334–0.705; p < 0.01) and LDCT-based unimodal model with the AUC of 0.842 (95% CI: 0.685–0.998; p = 0.138) in the independent validation cohort. No external validation cohort was used [40].

Predict subtypes

Histopathological and genomic data integration

Lung cancer is broadly classified into NSCLC and SCLC. NSCLC is further subdivided into adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, large cell carcinoma, and bronchial carcinoid tumor, with adenocarcinoma representing the most prevalent subtype [47]. Pathological examination remains the gold standard for lung cancer diagnosis. Traditionally, histopathological evaluation of tissue sections is performed manually by pathologists, a process that is often time-consuming and subject to inter-observer variability. To address these limitations, Cai et al. proposed a frequency domain transformer model that leverages multimodal data to predict lung cancer subtypes. Specifically, whole-slide images (WSIs) were transformed into the YCbCr color space, and their spatial frequency spectra were extracted using discrete cosine transform. A coordinate-spatial attention module was employed to capture comprehensive frequency information while preserving positional sensitivity and orientation awareness. Feature representations from the Y, Cb, and Cr channels were subsequently processed through a cross-domain transformer block, while temporally and spatially specific gene features were incorporated in parallel. The fused image–genomic features enabled accurate subtype classification of lung cancer. Compared with established medical image classification approaches such as DSMIL, Local-learning WSI, MMDL, MICC-ACO, and Swin Transformer, the proposed model achieved superior performance, attaining an AUC of 93.16% and an overall accuracy of 92.33%. Data were randomly divided into a training set and a test set in a ratio of 8:2, with 10-fold cross-validation. This study did not report whether these differences were statistically significant, nor were confidence intervals provided. External validation was not conducted [42].

Tumor Staging

Predict lymph node metastasis

Imaging and clinical data integration

Surgery is regarded as the most effective treatment for NSCLC patients among various therapeutic methods [48]. For NSCLC patients with stage I, stage II and resectable stage IIIA, surgery is recommended according to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines. When evaluating for perioperative therapy, it is important to determine whether there is regional lymph node metastasis and the location of the metastatic lymph nodes [49]. Several models based on two-dimensional (2D) images conducting by DL for lymph node staging prediction in lung cancer were developed in previous studies [50, 51]. However, the images of CT or PET/CT are three-dimensional (3D). 3D-based learning is better than 2D-based learning in the spatial information [52]. Park et al. conducted a multicenter retrospective study, enrolling patients from five large hospitals. Among them, patients from four hospitals were set as the internal dataset, and those from the other hospital were set as the external validation dataset. The internal dataset was randomly divided into the training set (70%), the validation set (10%), and the internal set (20%). They used four 3D CNN models including DenseNet121, ResNet50, SEResNet50, and Xception to extract CT image features. A multimodal model was proposed by integrating CT images and clinical data including age, sex, and lymph node stage to predict lymph node metastasis. The Xception model performed best with the AUC of 0.756 in the internal dataset and 0.736 in the external dataset respectively. Neither the statistical significance of the differences nor confidence intervals were reported in this study [53].

Multimodal radiomics

Nowadays, PET/CT has become the most reliable imaging examination method for detecting lymph node metastasis in NSCLC [54]. However, there are still 12.9% to 39.3% of lymph node metastases that have not been detected by PET/CT but are found during the operation. This is known as ONM. Zhong et al. developed a DLNMS by combining PET and CT radiomics to predict ONM. An internal cohort of 1,911 NSCLC patients was used to construct the DLNMS, and an external cohort of 355 patients and a prospective cohort of 999 patients further verified the predictive ability. The AUC of the DLNMS for predicting occult N1 were 0.958 (95% CI: 0.923–0.992), 0.879 (95% CI: 0.813–0.946) and 0.914 (95% CI: 0.877–0.949), for predicting occult N2 were 0.942 (95% CI: 0.911–0.973), 0.875 (95% CI: 0.820–0.930) and 0.919 (95% CI: 0.886–0.942) in the validation cohort, external cohort, and prospective cohort, respectively. This prediction model has been proven to perform better than unimodal DL models, clinical models, senior physicians, and junior physicians in detecting ONM (DeLong’s test: all p < 0.05) [44].

Predict distant metastasis

Multimodal radiomics

Most lung cancer-related deaths are due to distant metastasis [55]. Approximately up to 50% of lung cancer patients develop brain metastases during the course of the disease [56]. Lung cancer also accounts for about 50% of all brain metastases. The peculiarity of brain metastases lies in the blood-brain barrier, which restricts the penetration of many drugs and thus leads to a poorer prognosis [57, 58]. Traditional methods for detecting brain metastases include symptom evaluation, neurological exams, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis. However, symptom evaluation and neurological exams cannot achieve early detection and cerebrospinal fluid analysis is an invasive operation, having their limitations. Zhu et al. used PyRadiomics and 3D neural networks to extract shallow lung features and deep lung-brain features from 18F-FDG PET/CT images. 226 NSCLC patients were included in this retrospective analysis. The radiomics characteristics analysis from PET/CT images were randomly divided into a training group and a validation group in a ratio of 7:3, with 5-fold cross-validation. These two cohorts did not show significant differences in clinical characteristics. Three models namely ONLY_TRAD, C3D_Lung, and C3D_LungBrain were constructed in this study and used to compare the ability to identify the risk of brain metastasis in patients with early-stage NSCLC. The AUCs of these three models in identifying brain metastases were 0.531, 0.927 and 0.964 respectively (DeLong’s test: all p < 0.05), suggesting that the integration of shallow lung and shallow-deep lung-brain features performed well to predict brain metastases. However, confidence intervals of AUCs were not provided in this study. The finding was not validated in an independent external cohort because of a small sample size [59].

Multimodal learning for predicting response or adverse events to therapy

Surgery

Imaging and clinical data integration

Anatomic pulmonary lobectomy with hilar and mediastinal lymph node dissection is recommended for NSCLC patients according to the NCCN guidelines. The recurrence rate of patients with stage I to III lung adenocarcinoma who have undergone surgery is 18%-34.4% [60]. Moreover, previous studies reported that the recurrence rate of patients with stage I NSCLC was 18.5%-20.1% [61, 62]. Kuang et al. built a MDLR model by integrating CT images and clinical data for postoperative progression prediction of patients with stage I NSCLC. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 459 cases were included and divided into a training set and an internal validation set in a 7:3 ratio. In addition, there were 104 patients from another hospital as an external validation set. In the training, internal and external validation cohorts, the AUC of the MDLR model was 0.885 (95% CI: 0.842–0.927), 0.901(95% CI: 0.839–0.962) and 0.861 (95% CI: 0.778–0.945) respectively, which was higher than 0.675 (95% CI: 0.599–0.752), 0.632 (95% CI: 0.515–0.750) and 0.628 (95% CI: 0.482–0.773) of the clinical model, as well as 0.882 (95% CI: 0.835–0.929), 0.873 (95% CI: 0.803–0.943) and 0.852 (95% CI: 0.765–0.939) of the DLS model. DeLong’s tests showed that the MDLR model had a better predictive ability than the clinical model (p < 0.001; p < 0.001; p = 0.003), however, there was no statistical difference compared with the DLS model (all p > 0.05) [41].

Chemotherapy

Imaging and pathological data integration

Adjuvant systemic therapy is recommended for IB-IIA lung adenocarcinoma patients at high risk and stage IIB or higher patients [49]. However, the survival of patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy only increased by 5%-10% [63]. It indicates that determining treatment measures based on traditional risk factors in the past is insufficient and may even lead to overtreatment. Therefore, new, economical and rapid methods to assess tumor heterogeneity and conduct risk stratification for lung cancer patients are needed. Lin et al. conducted a retrospective study in China to establish radiopathomics signature by employing a survival support vector machine, which consisted of tumor and peritumor radiomics features and nuclei heterogeneity pathomics features. This study stratified patients with stage I-III lung adenocarcinoma into low-risk and high-risk categories through radiopathomics signature. There was no statistically significant difference in disease-free survival (DFS) between high-risk patients and low-risk patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy. However, high-risk patients who only underwent surgery had a poorer DFS. It revealed the predictive ability of radiopathomics signatures for the benefits of adjuvant chemotherapy [64].

Multimodal radiomics

Nowadays, ICIs such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) receptors inhibitor + platinum-doublet chemotherapy was recommended as a neoadjuvant treatment in advanced NSCLC [49]. In CheckMate 816, pathological complete response (pCR) of nivolumab +chemotherapy was 24%, and that of chemotherapy alone was only 2.2% [65]. Lower residue tumor burden is associated with a more favorable prognosis [66]. Therefore, pCR is a good predictive indicator for prognosis in NSCLC patients [67]. At present, pCR is mainly evaluated through histopathological examination. An accurate, economical and non-invasive examination method to predict neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy response seems to be necessary. Ye et al. initiated a retrospective study in China. Patients were from four centers, namely A, B, C, and D. 113 patients from Center A were divided into a training dataset and a validation dataset in the 8:2 ratio, while a total of 112 patients from the other three centers were assigned to the test dataset. Two predictive models (LUNAI-uCT model and LUNAI-eCT) by extracting DL features from non-contrast enhanced and contrast enhanced CT scans were constructed. Then, an integrated model called LUNAI-fCT model was proposed by fusing these two types of features. In the test cohort, the AUCs of LUNAI-fCT, LUNAI-uCT, and LUNAI-eCT were 0.866 (95% CI: 0.821–0.883), 0.762 (95% CI: 0.654–0.791), and 0.797 (95% CI: 0.724–0.844), respectively. The immunochemotherapy treatment response (Immu_TR) score represented the possibility of patients achieving pCR after receiving neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy. The Immu_TR score obtained by fusing CT imaging features was the highest, which was 0.825 (p < 0.001), and the scores of non-contrast enhanced and contrast enhanced CT images were 0.704 (p < 0.001) and 0.752 (p < 0.001), respectively. The results above revealed that the non-invasive multimodal CT model had a good performance in predicting pCR for NSCLC patients receiving neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy [36].

Targeted therapy

Histopathological and clinical data integration

It is reported that approximately half of patients with NSCLC have EGFR mutations [68]. The use of epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) are the standard first-line therapeutic strategy for advanced NSCLC patients with EGFR mutations because of improving prognosis of this cohort [49]. However, EGFR-TKIs offer limited benefits to some patients. Therefore, Chai et al. developed a multimodal model by fusing pre-treatment histopathological and clinical data, and two unimodal models based on WSIs or clinical data alone. The validation strategy in this study was limited to a single 8:2 ratio for training/ testing without cross-validation or external testing. This study reported that the multimodal model had a higher accurate ability to predict therapy response than the two unimodal models, with the AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of were 0.943 (95% CI: 0.873-1.000), 0.841 (95% CI: 0.750-1.000), 0.800 (95% CI: 0.686-1.000) and 1.000 (95% CI: 0.778-1.000), respectively [22].

Radiotherapy

Deep image, radiomic and clinical data integration

Nowadays, radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy is widely used in advanced NSCLC patients. It is well known that the main adverse event of radiotherapy for lung is radiation pneumonitis. Studies have shown that when healthy lung tissue is subjected to high-dose radiation, the incidence of acute lung injury is 5%-58% [69–71]. Immune-associated pneumonia is also a common complication in lung cancer patients receiving immunotherapy [72]. The incidence of pneumonia becomes higher when lung cancer patients undergo both radiotherapy and immunotherapy [73]. Taking into account the above reason, Yang et al. constructed a multimodal DL method by integrating deep image, radiomics and clinical data in a retrospective study in China. The model was constructed using a five‑fold cross‑validation strategy, which automatically split the dataset into training and validation subsets at an 8:2 ratio. CT scans images were cropped to a uniform size and then fed into a deep residual network, ResNet34, thereby deep image features were obtained. This multimodal model had a better performance for predicting pneumonia associated with radiotherapy and immunotherapy with the AUC of 0.922 (95% CI: 0.902–0.945, p < 0.001), than the radiomic feature model with the AUC of 0.811 (95% CI: 0.786–0.832, p < 0.001), and the clinical data model with the AUC of 0.711 (95% CI: 0.682–0.753, p < 0.001) [74].

Immunotherapy

Radiological, pathological, and genomics data integration

With the development of PD-1/programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors, the landscape of therapeutic strategy in NSCLC has been changed dramatically. For metastatic NSCLC patients without driver gene mutations, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors with or without chemotherapy is now recommended as the first-line therapy, significantly improving the prognosis of this cohort [67]. However, some patients have limited benefits from immunotherapy. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a predictive model that can evaluate the response to immunotherapy. Vanguri has made explorations in predicting response to immunotherapy in NSCLC patients. His team fused CT scans, digitized PD-L1 immunohistochemistry (IHC), and genomic data by using a machine-learning approach to propose a multimodal model. This study demonstrated that the multimodal model had a better ability to predict immunotherapy response with the AUC of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.74–0.86), than the unimodal models including PD-1 IHC score with the AUC of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.65–0.81), and tumor mutational burden with the AUC of 0.61(95% CI: 0.52–0.70). Statistical significance of performance differences was assessed using DeLong’s method for AUC comparisons, and permutation testing confirmed that the multimodal result was significantly higher. To address class imbalance (only 25% of the cohort were responders), the authors consistently applied class-balancing in their predictive models. Model development employed tenfold cross-validation, with additional subsampling analysis (100 iterations of 90/10 train/test splits) to evaluate sensitivity to training data. While the study included separate internal validation cohorts for radiology and pathology modalities, the authors explicitly acknowledged the absence of a fully independent external validation cohort due to the complexity of multimodal data assembly [43].

Multimodal learning for predicting prognosis of lung cancer

Survival prediction

Radiomics and clinical data integration

Lung cancer is the leading cause of death worldwide. Although the treatment strategies for lung cancer have undergone significant changes at present, the 5-year survival rate of stage IA is about 60%, and it ranges from 40% to less than 5% of stage II-IV [12]. In recent years, AI has been witnessed rapid development in the field of medicine. Besides the advantages we have listed above in tumor diagnosis and predicting treatment responses, by extract information from radiomics, genomics, WSI, and so on, it also plays a significant role in predicting the prognosis of tumors [75–77]. Caruso et al. proposed a multimodal ensemble to predict overall survival (OS) in patients with NSCLC. They extracted features from CT images when NSCLC patients at the first diagnosis, and collected clinical data including age, sex, histology, stage, and so on. The data above were then processed by a multimodal DL pipeline. The multimodal model acquired imaging modality through a VGG11-BN and a ResNet34 and clinical information through TABNET. Its accuracy was 75.00%, the F-score was 77.70%, and the recall was 84.00%, respectively. These results proved that the multimodal model was superior to single-modal models in predicting OS in NSCLC. However, the study did not report whether these differences were statistically significant, nor were confidence intervals provided. Class imbalance was not explicitly addressed, though the dataset was relatively balanced. Validation used five bootstrap iterations with 80/10/10 splits. No external validation was performed [78].

Le et al. constructed a multimodal model by integrating traditional-radiomics, deep-radiomics and clinical variables for predicting survival in NSCLC patients. A total of 420 patients from Lung 1 were used for training, and 156 patients from Lung 2 for testing. 256 deep-radiomics features from CT scans were extracted from a 3D CNN survival. These three parameters were input into the DeepSurv neural network. The study reported that in the training cohort, the multimodal model consisting of traditional-radiomics, deep-radiomics and clinical data had the best performance with C-index of 0.641 for Cox proportional hazards and 0.733 for DeepSurv method, in the testing cohort, the C-index is 0.746 for Cox proportional hazards and 0.751 for DeepSurv method. Both statistical significance of performance differences between models and confidence intervals for C‑indices were not provided [79].

In addition, a large retrospective study conducted in Republic of Korea proposed a multimodal model to predict survival in NSCLC patients by fusing F-18 FDG PET/CT scans and clinical data. The 3D PET images and coronal maximum intensity projection images of PET were obtained. The multimodal model outperformed clinical data model and two PET models, with the C-index of 0.756 and mean absolute error of 399 days, achieved using a ResNet3D for PET and the Cox proportional hazards model for clinical variables, statistical significance of performance differences between models was not reported. Validation used five‑fold cross‑validation with an 80/20 train/test split; no external validation was performed. A strength is the use of a large sample size and two PET scanner types to assess generalization [80].

Recurrence risk prediction

Surgery is one of the standard treatment methods for early-stage NSCLC. Although radical surgery resection is regarded as a curative treatment, there is still a high risk of recurrence after surgery, which leads to a worse prognosis [81]. In the real world, for early stage NSCLC patients who underwent surgery, the recurrence rates at stage I, stage II and stage III are 40%, 66% and 75%, respectively [82]. Traditional tumor markers and imaging examination methods such as CT are widely used in tumor assessment, but they often lack high sensitivity and specificity. In recent years, newly emerged liquid biopsies, such as circulating tumor DNA testing, have been proven effective in predicting the recurrence of lung cancer. However, before it can be widely used, large-scale trials are still needed [83]. Therefore, a convenient, economical and accurate method is needed to predict the recurrence of early-stage lung cancer, so as to intervene in a timely manner for patients with high recurrence risk and improve prognosis. For this reason, several scholars have conducted research in this aspect.

Radiomics, clinical, and pathological data integration

Kim et al. have done some work in this area. They predicted recurrence of NSCLC patients through DL using multimodal fusion imaging, clinical and pathological data. Hounsfield Units (HU) was used to standardize CT scans. And then images were displayed in grayscale by selecting a defined HU value. In the end, regions of interest images were generated for CNN analysis. clinical and pathological features included age, T stage, N stage, histology and so on. Unimodal models including clinical data, handcrafted radiomic features, and DL-based radiomics and the multimodal model were constructed to evaluate the predictive ability of NSCLC patients for recurrence. The study showed that the ensemble model outperformed single-modal models with the highest F1-score, precision, accuracy, and AUC value. However, the study did not report whether the observed AUC differences were statistically significant (no DeLong test or equivalent), nor were confidence intervals provided for performance metrics. The class imbalance between 193 recurrences and 133 non‑recurrences has not been clearly resolved. Validation used five‑fold cross‑validation with shuffled data; no external validation cohort was employed. The ensemble demonstrated improved risk stratification in Kaplan‑Meier analysis (log‑rank p < 0.05), but the lack of external validation and significance testing limited generalizability [82].

Radiomics and pathological data integration

In the section of predicting response to chemotherapy above, we mentioned a multimodal model that integrated radiomics and pathological data. This model was also used to predict DFS in 1,039 resectable lung adenocarcinoma patients across four centers. The multimodal model achieved C‑indices of 0.744 (training, n = 303), 0.719 (testing dataset 1, n = 197), and 0.711 (testing dataset 2, n = 228), outperforming radiomics‑only (0.734, 0.701, 0.689) and pathomics‑only (0.692, 0.638, 0.684) signatures. The study reported 95% CIs for C‑indices and used likelihood ratio tests to confirm added prognostic value beyond clinical factors (p < 0.001). However, class imbalance was not explicitly addressed. Validation employed multi‑center external testing (two independent datasets from different hospitals), which is a major strength [64].

Challenges and prospects

When formulating treatment strategies, clinicians must routinely integrate information from multiple sources, including imaging, histopathology, and genomic or transcriptomic features. However, a single data modality is insufficient to comprehensively capture the complex biological characteristics of tumors. To address this limitation, deep learning-based multimodal learning has attracted increasing attention in recent years, particularly in biomedical research, owing to its ability to integrate and jointly learn from heterogeneous data modalities.

Multimodal learning integrates multiple types of data, including clinical, imaging, genomic, and histopathological information, to systematically characterize the biological complexity of tumors across multiple levels, ranging from patient phenotypes and tumor morphology to molecular features, thereby providing strong support for precision diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment decision-making. Although deep learning–based multimodal models have achieved substantial progress in lung cancer diagnosis, treatment response prediction, and prognostic assessment in recent years, these studies largely remain at the stage of performance improvement and retrospective validation, and a critical gap persists before such models can be truly integrated into routine clinical decision-making workflows.

First, existing studies generally rely on single-center, small-sample retrospective datasets, in which model performance is often established on relatively idealized and highly controlled data distributions. In real-world settings, however, variations in imaging protocols, scanning equipment, histopathological staining procedures, omics platforms, and incomplete clinical records can markedly induce data distribution shifts, leading to performance degradation or even failure when models are applied to external cohorts or across institutions. Consequently, some so-called “high AUC” results may primarily reflect dataset-specific patterns, and the features captured by these models may not represent truly transferable biological mechanisms or clinically generalizable principles.

In addition, it is necessary to recognize the inherent limitations of AI models. Current mainstream artificial intelligence systems essentially remain data-driven statistical learning frameworks, whose knowledge acquisition depends on previously collected data and predefined labeling schemes. In other words, models can only learn patterns from information that has been recorded, measured, and annotated, whereas real-world clinical practice involves numerous latent variables that are unobserved or difficult to quantify. These include dynamic changes in the tumor microenvironment, differences in treatment adherence, fluctuations in individual immune status, interference from comorbidities, and heterogeneity in medical practice itself. Such clinical variables—particularly medication adherence, symptom severity, and prior medical history—are highly dependent on patient or physician reporting, which can introduce unpredictable biases and thereby pose challenges to clinical safety.

Third, insufficient interpretability represents another critical barrier to the clinical translation of multimodal models. Multimodal models typically achieve higher predictive accuracy through complex feature fusion and nonlinear mappings; however, this often comes at the cost of interpretability, causing such models to evolve into “black boxes” whose decision-making processes are difficult to trace. Model opacity not only limits clinicians’ understanding of and trust in predictive results but may also give rise to legal and ethical concerns, such as unclear accountability and challenges in risk assessment. In the absence of explicit causal explanations and decision traceability, artificial intelligence systems at the current stage remain unable to assume independent decision-making roles and can primarily function as supportive tools to assist clinical judgment, rather than directly replacing clinical decision-making itself.

Despite the challenges outlined above, multimodal learning continues to demonstrate substantial potential in advancing precision medicine. By systematically integrating multimodal information, including imaging, histopathological, genomic, and clinical data, multimodal models are able to characterize the biological complexity of tumors across multiple levels, ranging from patient phenotypes and tumor morphology to molecular features. Compared with unimodal approaches, multimodal models have shown clear advantages in both predictive performance and model robustness. However, to achieve substantive translation of multimodal artificial intelligence from methodological research into routine clinical practice, a clear and feasible implementation pathway remains essential.

To this end, we propose a three-phase roadmap for clinical implementation to systematically accelerate the clinical translation of multimodal AI (Fig. 4). Phase I (short-term) focuses on standardization and the development of automated infrastructure. The current scarcity of multimodal data largely arises from the fact that many types of clinical information—such as age, sex, histopathological findings, and survival outcomes—require manual recording by physicians, resulting in substantial annotation burden. The introduction of automated data acquisition and annotation pipelines can effectively reduce labor costs and facilitate the scaling of multimodal learning. For example, lung nodules on CT scans can be automatically localized using a detection convolutional neural network such as RetinaNet, enabling model training on datasets comprising 42,290 CT cases [84]. Researchers should prioritize the establishment of such automated pipelines and shared databases to standardize data preprocessing.

A roadmap for clinical implementation

Phase II (medium-term) emphasizes prospective and external validation. To overcome the inherent biases associated with retrospective and single-center studies, model evaluation must move beyond internal validation. Future studies should adopt prospective, multi-center, and large-sample clinical trial designs to rigorously assess model robustness and generalizability across different imaging devices, patient populations, and clinical settings.

Phase III (long-term) centers on workflow integration, with the ultimate objective of embedding mature multimodal models into clinical decision support systems. Algorithms must be directly integrated into Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems to provide real-time, actionable decision support during routine patient care.

In addition, it should be specifically noted that, compared with NSCLC, SCLC remains markedly underrepresented in multimodal learning research. Among the studies analyzed in this review, nearly all focused on NSCLC, with only 6.67% specifically addressing SCLC. This imbalance in research focus is not coincidental, but rather results from multiple contributing factors. First, NSCLC is the predominant pathological subtype of lung cancer, whereas SCLC accounts for only approximately 15–20% of all lung cancer cases [85, 86], and has therefore long been the primary focus of clinical practice and research. Second, NSCLC offers a broader range of targeted and immunotherapeutic options, whose therapeutic efficacy is highly dependent on precise molecular stratification (such as EGFR, ALK, and PD-L1 status), thereby objectively driving the development of multimodal learning approaches that integrate imaging, histopathological, and genomic data to predict treatment response and prognosis. In contrast, the treatment of SCLC has long been dominated by chemotherapy, with limited target diversity, which has to some extent reduced the clinical demand for multimodal learning. Finally, patients with SCLC generally have a poorer prognosis, and biopsy tissue is more difficult to obtain, making the systematic collection of high-quality, paired multimodal datasets—such as imaging-genomic data—substantially more challenging.

Nevertheless, this situation is gradually changing. With the emergence of novel therapeutic strategies for SCLC, such as immunotherapy-based combination regimens, there is growing interest in the identification of predictive biomarkers, and exploratory studies applying multimodal learning to SCLC are beginning to emerge. As a highly aggressive lung cancer subtype with poor prognosis, SCLC urgently requires new data-driven research paradigms. For this long-neglected disease entity, the establishment of dedicated multi-center collaborative consortia to systematically collect high-quality multimodal data will be critical for accelerating model development and validation, thereby helping to ensure that the benefits of precision oncology truly extend to all lung cancer patient populations.

It is worth noting that due to the rapid evolution of this field, the review does not claim to be systematic. The selection of 15 primary studies was intended to illustrate key methodological trends rather than to list all existing evidence in detail. A formal systematic review with broader literature coverage is urgently needed in the future.

Conclusion

Overall, although multimodal deep learning has demonstrated substantial potential in lung cancer diagnosis, treatment response prediction, and prognostic assessment, current studies largely remain at the stage of performance optimization and retrospective validation. Constrained by practical challenges such as data heterogeneity, limited generalizability, insufficient interpretability, and inadequate integration into clinical workflows, its clinical utility has yet to be fully established. Nevertheless, with the progressive development of multi-center data-sharing infrastructures, the advancement of prospective clinical validation, and the maturation of explainable artificial intelligence and automated data acquisition technologies, multimodal learning is expected to gradually overcome these bottlenecks, evolve from a research-oriented tool into a reliable clinical decision support approach, and ultimately play a substantive role in advancing precision lung cancer care and improving patient outcomes.
