A Decade of Screening and Treating Helicobacter pylori Infection Among First‐Year High School Students in Kyoto, Japan: Annual Trends in Infection, Consultation, and Eradication Success Rates (2015–2023)
Authors: Osamu Handa, Kazuhiko Uchiyama, Tomohisa Takagi, Yuji Naito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground Helicobacter pylori infection is a primary cause of gastric cancer. Although eradication therapy in adulthood suppresses carcinogenesis, its efficacy is limited due to the progression of advanced gastric mucosal atrophy before eradication. Implementing a “screen‐and‐treat” strategy during adolescence represents a promising approach to prevent advanced atrophy and subsequent gastric cancer.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included first‐year high school students in Kyoto Prefecture, Japan, from 2015 to 2023. Screening was performed using a primary urine H. pylori antibody test, followed by a secondary stool antigen test for confirmation. Confirmed positive individuals were referred to designated medical institutions for 7‐day eradication therapy. The primary therapeutic regimen was shifted from rabeprazole, amoxicillin, and clarithromycin (PAC) in 2015 to rabeprazole, amoxicillin, and metronidazole (PAM) from 2016 onward. Clostridium butyricum MIYAIRI 588 strain (CBM588) was co‐administered starting in 2017. Eradication success was verified via a stool antigen test at least 8 weeks post‐treatment.ResultsA total of 50,031 students were targeted over the 9‐year period, with an overall consent rate ranging from 59.9% to 83.9%. The confirmed H. pylori infection rate declined from 4.7% in 2015 to 1.5% in 2023. The medical consultation rate among positive screenees exhibited a declining trend, peaking at 84.6% (2015) and dropping to 15.8% (2023), despite a temporary increase in 2019 (65.9%). The eradication success rate significantly improved from 76.5% with the PAC regimen (2015) to over 93.8% with the PAM regimen (2016 onward), exceeding 96.0% after the addition of CBM588. No serious adverse events were reported throughout the study period.ConclusionsThe prospective adolescent screen‐and‐treat program demonstrated a significant decrease in H. pylori infection rates among first‐year high school students over the decade. While the safety and efficacy of the PAM‐based eradication regimen were verified, declining medical consultation rates present a continuous challenge for public health implementation.Keywords: adolescents, eradication therapy, gastric carcinogenesis, gastric mucosal atrophy, mass screening

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies, particularly in Asian nations. Accumulating evidence indicates that Helicobacter pylori infection is responsible for the vast majority of gastric cancer cases, and its eradication significantly reduces the risk of developing this malignancy [1]. In East Asian countries, including Japan, almost all clinical strains of H. pylori harbor the East Asian‐type CagA virulence factor, which exhibits high oncogenic potential [2]. Consequently, H. pylori eradication is a critical public health priority in this geographical region to mitigate gastric cancer mortality [3].

However, a recent meta‐analysis demonstrated that H. pylori eradication in adults reduces the risk of gastric cancer by approximately 50% [4], implying that half of the infected individuals still develop gastric cancer despite successful treatment. H. pylori infection is typically acquired during early childhood, usually before 5 years of age [5]. Following infection, gastric mucosal atrophy progresses continuously over decades [6]. By the time individuals undergo screening in adulthood, advanced mucosal atrophy has already established. Although eradication at this stage attenuates further progression, it does not completely reverse pre‐existing severe mucosal changes [7]. This clinical limitation is supported by experimental animal models using Mongolian gerbils, where early eradication—corresponding to a shorter duration of infection—exhibited a significantly higher protective effect against gastric carcinogenesis compared with delayed eradication [8, 9]. These findings suggest that implementing eradication therapy at an early stage, prior to the development of severe atrophy, maximizes the preventive effect against gastric cancer.

To address this hypothesis, we established a prospective “screen‐and‐treat” program targeting first‐year high school students (aged 15–16 years) in Kyoto Prefecture, Japan. Given the long natural history of gastric carcinogenesis, evaluating the reduction in gastric cancer incidence as a primary endpoint requires a follow‐up period of 30–40 years. Therefore, for this 10‐year interim analysis, the primary endpoints were determined as the annual trend in H. pylori infection rates in this cohort and the safety profile of the eradication therapy compared with established adult data.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This prospective screening program targeted approximately 24,000 first‐year high school students attending schools in Kyoto Prefecture, Japan, between 2015 and 2023 every year. In the initial pilot year (2015), three high schools were selected as model institutions in collaboration with the Kyoto Prefectural Health Policy Division to identify logistical challenges and optimize the screening workflow. The optimized protocol was subsequently expanded to other participating schools in the prefecture from 2016 onward. As the program expanded from model schools to the entire prefecture, the target population increased proportionally across both urban areas (Kyoto City) and rural/semi‐rural districts (Northern and Central Kyoto Prefecture), minimizing potential geographic selection bias in infection rate trends.

Screening Strategy and Workflow

The workflow of the screening program was conducted in cooperation with school nurses at participating institutions. Prior to sample collection, introductory briefings were conducted, and written explanatory documents were distributed to the students and their legal guardians. Written informed consent was obtained from both the students and their parents/guardians.

Screening was scheduled to coincide with annual mandatory school urine examinations. Primary screening was performed using a urine H. pylori antibody test (RAPIRUN urine H. pylori antibody EIA kit), which allowed non‐invasive, simultaneous sample collection during routine school health checkups, thereby minimizing logistical friction and student embarrassment. To overcome potential limitations regarding the diagnostic accuracy of urinary antibody assays, a two‐step diagnostic algorithm was employed: all urine‐positive individuals underwent mandatory secondary confirmation via a highly specific stool antigen test (TestMate Pylori Antigen, immunochromatographic assay). Students who tested positive in the primary screening were eligible for secondary confirmatory testing using a stool H. pylori antigen test (TestMate Pylori Antigen, immunochromatographic assay). Specimen logistics and processing were managed by Falco Biosystems Ltd., Kyoto, Japan (April 2015–March 2021) and Hoken Kagaku West Japan Co. Ltd., Kyoto, Japan (April 2021–March 2023). Comprehensive test results were compiled and sent to the school nurses, who subsequently notified the students.

Eradication Therapy and Follow‐Up

Students testing positive on both the urine antibody and stool antigen tests were diagnosed with active H. pylori infection. These individuals were referred to designated medical institutions. After obtaining independent clinical informed consent at the respective clinics and hospitals, a 7‐day primary eradication therapy was administered. In 2015, the regimen consisted of rabeprazole (10 mg), amoxicillin (750 mg), and clarithromycin (200 mg) administered orally twice daily for 7 days (PAC regimen). From 2016 to 2023, the regimen was switched to rabeprazole (10 mg), amoxicillin (750 mg), and metronidazole (250 mg) orally twice daily for 7 days (PAM regimen). Starting in 2017, Clostridium butyricum MIYAIRI 588 strain (CBM588) was co‐administered to prevent antibiotic‐associated diarrhea. Although potassium‐competitive acid blockers (P‐CABs) offer stronger acid suppression than proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) [10], a PPI (rabeprazole) was chosen because clinical safety and efficacy evidence for P‐CABs in pediatric and adolescent cohorts was insufficient when the protocol was established in 2015.

Eradication success was evaluated at least 8 weeks after the completion of therapy using a follow‐up stool H. pylori antigen test. To minimize the burden of clinic visits, students collected stool specimens at home and mailed them directly to the laboratory. The final eradication status was reported back to the school nurses and communicated to the students. Students who achieved successful eradication were provided with documentation recommending future upper gastrointestinal endoscopy in adulthood. Those who failed primary eradication were instructed to seek medical attention under standard health insurance coverage for secondary eradication and endoscopic follow‐up.

All screening expenses, clinical consultation fees, and therapeutic medications were provided free of charge to the participants. Funding was covered by the Kyoto Prefectural budget from 2015 to 2022 and by a donation from Dr. Yuji Naito (the department budget of the Department of Human Immunology and Nutrition Science, Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine) from 2023 to 2026 (with recruitment closing in 2023).

Statistical Analysis

Annual trends in consent rates, infection rates, medical consultation rates, and eradication success rates were analyzed chronologically. Continuous variables were presented as absolute numbers and percentages. Eradication success rates were evaluated using both intention‐to‐treat (ITT) approaches. Statistical differences between regimens were assessed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, with a p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software.

Ethical Considerations

This project was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine as a specific clinical research project.

Medical Consultation and Eradication Success Rates

The proportion of confirmed positive students who visited designated medical institutions decreased over time (Figure 4, Table 1). The consultation rate was highest in 2015 at 84.6% (22/26) but reached its lowest point in 2023 at 15.8% (6/38), despite a temporary rebound to 65.9% (89/135) in 2019 (Table 1).

Chronological changes in the medical consultation rate. The graph depicts the annual compliance rate for visiting designated medical institutions among confirmed positive individuals. Parentheses indicate the absolute number of medical consultees divided by the total number of confirmed positive cases.

Eradication outcomes varied significantly by therapeutic regimen. In 2015, the PAC regimen yielded a relatively low eradication success rate of 76.5% (Table 1, Figure 5). Following protocol revision approval by the IRB, the primary regimen was changed to the metronidazole‐based PAM regimen in 2016, which immediately increased the eradication success rate to 93.8%. The addition of CBM588 from 2017 onward yielded eradication success rates between 93.8% and 98.9% (Table 1). Although the overall eradication success rate with PAM plus CBM588 (2017–2023: 96.9%, 315/325) was numerically higher than that with PAM alone (2016: 93.8%, 15/16), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.415 via Fisher's exact test). No serious adverse drug reactions or major complications were reported across any of the treatment cohorts throughout the study period. Throughout the study period, a mandatory reporting system established with the designated medical institutions confirmed that there were no severe adverse events (SAEs), such as drug‐induced allergic reactions requiring hospitalization or severe colitis.

Discussion

This prospective study demonstrated that the H. pylori infection rate among first‐year high school students in Kyoto Prefecture declined chronologically over a 9‐year period, reaching 1.5% by 2023. Furthermore, the transition from a clarithromycin‐based regimen to a metronidazole‐based regimen significantly enhanced eradication efficacy without increasing serious adverse events in an adolescent population.

The observed decline in adolescent H. pylori infection rate aligns with previous epidemiological reports from other regions in Japan. For instance, Nakayama et al. reported a 3.1% infection rate among junior high school students in Nagano Prefecture evaluated between 2011 and 2013 [11]. Similarly, Kaji et al. observed a 2.55% prevalence among junior high school students in Osaka Prefecture from 2014 to 2017 [12]. In a large‐scale cohort in Saga Prefecture encompassing 21,042 junior high school students from 2016 to 2018, Kakiuchi et al. [13] demonstrated a significant reduction in infection rates from 3.6% to 2.5% within 3 years. Our study corroborates these findings, confirming that the prevalence of H. pylori among Japanese adolescents has fallen below 2.0%. This sustained cohort‐wide decline is anticipated to result in a reduction in future gastric cancer incidence.

Regarding therapeutic efficacy, the initial PAC regimen utilized in 2015 resulted in an insufficient eradication rate of 76.5%. This suboptimal performance directly reflects the high prevalence of clarithromycin (CAM)‐resistant H. pylori strains—reported to be 30% to 50%—among Japanese children and adolescents [10, 14, 15]. In contrast, the metronidazole‐based PAM regimen utilized from 2016 onward achieved excellent eradication success rates exceeding 93%. Although Japanese clinical guidelines recommend personalized treatment based on antimicrobial susceptibility testing, conducting invasive endoscopic bio‐sampling in mass school screenings is unfeasible. Implementing non‐invasive susceptibility tests, such as stool‐based PCR assays for resistance mutations, represents a desirable future strategy to optimize primary eradication. These clinical findings replicate the results of the Osaka cohort study, where the ITT eradication rate was 43.5% with PAC and 92.8% with PAM [12]. Furthermore, the introduction of CBM588 in our protocol from 2017 coincided with eradication rates exceeding 96.0%. Previous retrospective data from our group suggested that co‐administration of PAC with CBM588 increases eradication rates [16]. While the co‐administration of CBM588 did not yield a statistically significant increase in eradication success compared with PAM alone—due to the already excellent performance of PAM—it was maintained to mitigate antibiotic‐associated gastrointestinal side effects and support medication adherence in this young population.

A major clinical challenge identified in this study was the long‐term decline in the medical consultation rate among students confirmed as positive screenees, which reached a low of 15.8% in 2023. Similar adherence issues for secondary confirmatory or precision testing have been widely documented in other asymptomatic cancer screening programs. For instance, secondary diagnostic compliance following positive prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) screening in China ranges from 60% to 80% [17]. In Japan, adherence to secondary colonoscopy after a positive fecal occult blood test ranges from 58% to 74%, leaving approximately 30% of patients unscreened [18]. Documented barriers to compliance include fear of invasive procedures, prep burden, absence of subjective symptoms, economic constraints, and inadequate clinical explanations. In our cohort, adolescent‐specific psychological barriers, including embarrassment regarding urine and stool sample collection, may have influenced compliance. The temporary increase in consultation rates observed in 2019 (65.9%) requires careful interpretation, as individual socio‐environmental factors during that specific period were not fully evaluated. Furthermore, as the infection rate has fallen to 1.5%, the public health justification for continuing youth screening programs warrants careful evaluation. We strongly argue that adolescent screen‐and‐treat programs remain indispensable even in low‐prevalence settings. Eliminating H. pylori at age 15–16 prevents decades of active inflammatory progression before irreversible gastric mucosal atrophy and intestinal metaplasia become established, providing near‐complete lifetime protection against gastric cancer. To overcome the declining consultation rates and sustain cost‐effectiveness, future programs should transition toward automated notification systems, school‐nurse‐mediated health education, and streamlined clinical referral networks.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the ultimate primary endpoint—the suppression of future gastric cancer development—cannot be evaluated within the current timeframe and requires long‐term epidemiological follow‐up. Second, the study design evaluated distinct cross‐sections of first‐year high school students annually, meaning the baseline demographics of the target population shifted each year slightly. Third, systematic evaluation of minor adverse events (e.g., mild transient diarrhea) was limited due to an extremely low response rate for the follow‐up questionnaires (only two forms were returned). However, as noted above, the mandatory reporting framework ensured the complete absence of severe complications, thereby confirming that the primary eradication regimens carry no significant clinical risk in this adolescent cohort. Finally, the enrollment phase of this program was concluded in 2023 due to the expiration of available research funding and public budget allocations.

In conclusion, our 9‐year prospective screen‐and‐treat program demonstrated that H. pylori infection rates among high school students in Kyoto have decreased to 1.5%. The safety and high efficacy of the metronidazole‐based eradication protocol were validated in this adolescent cohort. To maintain the continuity and maximize the public health impact of such preventive interventions, established institutional frameworks and stable public funding mechanisms are essential.
