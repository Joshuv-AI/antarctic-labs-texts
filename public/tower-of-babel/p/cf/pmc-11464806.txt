A liquid static culture using a gas-permeable film bag contributes to microbiology
Authors: Kotaro Matsumoto, Kazuya Higashi, Yuki Naka, Kenji Ito, Motomu Akita
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe propose a simple tool for liquid static culture using a copolymer film with high gas permeability. The film bags were successfully used to culture microorganisms Escherichia coli, Komagataella phaffii (methylotrophic) and Bacillus sp. (biofilm-forming), with cells cultured under physical stress-free conditions with sufficient oxygen supply. Similar growth curves and plasmid productivity were observed for liquid shake and film bag E. coli cultures. The early growth response of the film bag culture following colony inoculation of liquid media differed from conventional shake cultures. Our results indicate that a gas-permeable film bag is a promising liquid culture tool and provides novel microbiology materials.Keywords: Shear stress, TPX, Escherichia coli, Methylotrophic culture of Komagataella phaffii, BiofilmSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Liquid culturing is primarily chosen for growing microorganisms because of its simplicity and high efficiency. Sufficient oxygen must be provided to maintain cell growth during culturing of aerobic microorganisms. However, oxygen solubility is low in liquid media, and thus forced aeration is required1. Sophisticated systems and techniques for aeration have been developed, and microorganisms compatible with these systems have also been selected and cultivated. Nonetheless, the effect of shaking and forced aeration on microorganisms, including shaking in baffled flask and agitation and air bubble supply to bioreactors, remains a potential issue because those operations cause shear stress. This stress is also observed by air bubbles in airlift bioreactors2, and cultured cells are continuously under shear stress in liquid media without adopting specially designed bioreactors3,4. Researchers have shown that the morphology and properties of microorganisms change under shear stress in liquid media5. Small-scale liquid shaking cultures are routinely performed in laboratories because these cultures are convenient and provide sufficient volume for subsequent analysis. These small-scale cultures also provide a guide for potentially increasing the culture volume and standard procedures have been established to increase culture volume. Nonetheless, energy- and space-saving requirements are often desired, and potential limited access to equipment for shaking culture can be an issue.

In this report, we propose using a film bag for liquid static culturing. The film consists of a 4-methylpentene-1 and α-olefin copolymer, TPX, providing an order of magnitude larger gas permeability (30,000 cm3/m2 d atm) than other films, including several types of fluoropolymers6. The TPX-based film has no leachability and extractability in water (https://jp.mitsuichemicals.com/en/special/tpx/properties/). The film bag is autoclavable and reusable. The concept of using air-permeable film bags for liquid culturing has been presented previously, especially for plant cell cultures7; however, we report herein for the first time that the TPX film bag is applicable for culturing several microorganisms without mechanical aeration8, indicating that sufficient oxygen is passively supplied through this film. The culture procedure is simple. Briefly, users pour a relatively small amount of liquid medium and inoculum into the bag, and then the bag is completely sealed (Fig. 1a and the set-up procedure provided in a Supplementary Video 1). Users can culture cells without shaking devices but should ensure that oxygen availability through the film is not impeded. Moreover, temperature and light regulation may be required. This TPX bag saves energy because orbital shakers are not required. This film also significantly reduces the weight (0.5 g in case of this study) and total thickness (0.1 mm) required for a culture device. We highlight that this simple method affords novel possibilities for characterizing microorganisms because samples cultured under shear stress-free conditions with a sufficient oxygen supply in different liquid media can be readily prepared. We also envisage that the film bag is suitable for analyzing biofilm development. This device should contribute to accelerating research in biology and biotechnology.

Results

Gas permeable film bags provide a compatible but energy-saving tool for liquid cultures

Escherichia coli was successfully cultured in a thin layer of liquid medium packed in the film bag (FB). The growth of E. coli was comparable in a FB and shaken tube (ST) when precultured cells prepared by a liquid shake culture were inoculated to the fresh medium (Fig. 1b). Plasmid productivity in FB was essentially the same as that of the ST culture when recombinant E. coli cells were cultured (Fig. 1c). Although oxygen supply can be a major factor that restricts cell growth in liquid cultures, the direct measurement of dissolved oxygen concentration in the bags was not possible in this report because the average medium thickness was ca. 0.5 mm. Non-invasive and high-resolution sensing will be required for such oxygen measurements. Nonetheless, the microorganisms likely absorb oxygen through direct contact with the film surface, and this route of oxygen supply may be critical for the observed growth performance of the bacterial cultures, especially for biofilm-forming microorganisms (see Fig. 2b). Because an inhomogeneous environment likely forms in the bag, analysis of oxygen movement to the film-contacting cells is required in addition to measuring the oxygen concentration of the homogeneous liquid phase. We observed methylotrophic growth of Komagataella phaffii (X-33) in FB was slightly lower (ca. 94%) than that observed when using baffled flasks (200 rpm) (Fig. 1d). This result suggests that O2 supply may be modestly reduced in this example. Theoretically, the air permeability is improved by making the film as thin as possible. Thinner films can be used, but this comes with a usability trade-off. Thus, appropriate designs should be adopted for individual research requirements.

Characteristic growth of bacteria in a film bag culture

A single colony of E. coli cultured on an agar plate was suspended in a liquid medium, and culturing started immediately. Such drastic changes in culture conditions may arrest cell growth initially, but the cell number usually rapidly and logarithmically increases after this early period, the so-called “lag phase”. A transient suspension in cell growth was observed in ST but, interestingly, not in FB (Fig. 2a). The lag phase is commonly explained as a period for cell adaptation. However, factors affecting cells during this period can be complex, e.g., shear stress, oxygen concentration, pH and osmotic pressure. Biological events during adaptation will be observable because the FB culture provides stress-free but sufficient oxygen-supplying conditions. Note that the cell number during this period does not always correlate to final growth levels (Fig. 1c). Characteristically in FB, sedimented cells are in direct contact with the surface of the bottom side of the bag, and so-called “biofilms” were formed in cultures of Bacillus sp. (Fig. 2b). In contrast, biofilms were not observed for the same strain in ST. No biofilms, except for slight aggregates, were formed when culturing E. coli laboratory strains, such as DH5α (see also an example of an E. coli culture in Supplementary Video 1).

Discussion

Liquid medium should typically be in continuous movement and contact the gas phase because oxygen transmittance from the gas–liquid interface is very low. This operation inevitably causes physical stress to the cultured cells. Effects of shear stress have been reported9–11; however, we do not fully understand its effects on liquid cultured cells because it is challenging to maintain viable cells without shear stress under sufficient oxygen supply. Here, we showed that sufficient oxygen, comparable to shaking, can be supplied to the medium in a bag prepared with TPX because a microorganism with a high oxygen requirement such as methylotrophic K. phaffii can be cultured successfully using this bag12.

FB can replace standard procedures for small-scale culturing. However, the thickness of the film and depth of the medium layer in the bag should be determined and will depend on the biological materials used because oxygen movement through the film and the distribution of substrates, including oxygen molecules, is likely to be significantly influenced. The bag shape should be carefully controlled for culturing because the bag shape is very flexible, and the depth of the medium layer can vary. However, this flexibility also offers advantages, as discussed below.

The TPX FB culture may provide valuable samples and novel perspectives for biology. This technique should be applicable for preparing biological samples under various environments, such as lower oxygen levels, physical pressures, ultraviolet exposure and extreme temperatures. The FB culture should also enable the analysis of cell-to-cell interactions with an oxygen gradient and facilitate various enzyme reactions. In this report, we showed two examples of differences between bag and shaking cultures. First, the effects of transferring from colony (static) to liquid shaking can be analyzed using FB. The arresting growth after inoculating ST with cells may be caused by shaking and, thus, shear stress on the cells. We postulate that comparing cell profiles between FB and ST cultures can provide information about the lag phase. Nonetheless, this arrest is typically no issue for the culture efficiency because at least a fraction of living cells rapidly adapts to the new environment, and cell yields usually recover. Second, “biofilms” can form in the bag. No data describes whether biofilms in the bag culture are equivalent to reported biofilms13,14 because the FB culture is performed under no hydrodynamic stresses, and cells may directly absorb oxygen from attachment to the bottom side of the film surface. The formation of “biofilms” also indicated that the culture environment may be inhomogeneous in the bag.

In conclusion, we have presented a useful liquid culture tool for biology and biotechnology. The most important advantage of FB is its simplicity because no equipment for mechanical aeration is required. Thus, FB is probably most suited for small laboratories, including facilities where energy consumption and space must be tightly controlled, such as satellites and space stations. This simple culturing approach provides novel possibilities through appropriate design.

Methods

TPX film bag

TPX film, 50 µm thickness gross type, was obtained from Fuji Chemi Trading Co., Ltd (product name Opulent, https://www.ftcj.co.jp/en_products/lp/release/). Two pieces of the film were stacked, and three sides were heat-sealed to create a bag. The size of the culture space was prepared by sealing the bag with a plastic clip or heat treatment (see Supplementary Video 1) to give either ca. 52 × 74 mm (for E. coli and Bacillus sp.) or ca. 100 × 241 mm (for yeast). The medium volume was 2 mL for 52 × 74 mm bags and 12.5 mL for 100 × 241 mm bags (0.052 mL cm–2 of the bag’s footprint). Film bags were sterilized by autoclaving (121 °C, 1 atm, 20 min).

Strains

An untransformed Escherichia coli DH5α, Rosetta (DE3) strain carrying the empty plasmid pET28a( +) and Komagataella phaffii (X-33) were used as model organisms. Bacillus sp., putative Bacillus amyloliquefacience15, was cultured as a biofilm-forming organism.

Culturing from the suspension-cultured inoculum of E. coli DH5α

Cells were pre-cultured for 5 h (logarithmic growth phase) using 5 mL LB or TB medium in a polypropylene tube (3181–345, 27 mm i.d., Labcon) by liquid shake culturing (37 °C, 180 rpm, shaking diameter, 2.5 cm). Two milliliters of this pre-culture were suspended in 300 mL of each corresponding medium, and 2 mL of this cell resuspension was transferred to a new tube (3181–345) and a TPX film bag. The tube was shaken at 37 °C, 200 rpm, and the TPX film bag was statically set at 37 °C. The turbidity was measured every 1 h by measuring the absorbance at 600 nm (OD600).

Culturing from colonies of E. coli DH5α

For observation of growth from directly inoculated colonies, colonies were developed from a frozen stock by spreading on an LB plate containing 1.5% agar and culturing at 37 °C for 16 h. Three colonies were randomly selected and suspended in 300 mL of LB medium. The turbidity of this suspension was measured as initial turbidity (“Inoc”). Two milliliters of the fresh suspension were transferred to a new tube, 3181–345, and a TPX film bag. The tube was shaken at 200 rpm, and the TPX film bag was placed at 37 °C for 1 h.

Plasmid production by E. coli Rosetta (DE3)

Escherichia coli Rosetta (DE3) cells containing the pET28a( +) plasmid were cultured from colonies using the same conditions described above, except the LB medium contained 50 µg mL–1 kanamycin and chloramphenicol. The cells were harvested from the 1 mL culture by centrifugation at 3000 g and 5 °C for 30 min. The cell pellet was suspended in 200 µL buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl and 10 mM EDTA, pH 7.5), and cells were lysed by adding 200 µL lysis solution (0.2 M NaOH and 1% SDS) followed by mixing 400 µL of neutralization solution (1.25 M potassium acetate and 1.25 M glacial acetic acid). The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 18,000 g, 5 °C for 10 min and treated with RNaseA by adding 1 µL of a 10 µg mL–1 solution stock solution and incubating at 65 °C for 30 min. After extraction using phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) and centrifugation at 18,000 g, 5 °C for 10 min, DNA was precipitated using an equivalent volume of 2-propanol with 0.1 volume of 3 M sodium acetate by centrifugation at 18,000 g, 5 °C for 5 min, and then washed using 70% ethanol. After centrifugation at 18,000 g, 5 °C for 5 min, the pellet was dried under a vacuum and dissolved in an appropriate amount of 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) and 1 mM EDTA･Na2 buffer. The quantity of DNA was measured using a NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd) instrument.

Komagataella phaffii (X-33) culture

Yeast was cultured in Buffered Glycerol-complex Medium (BMGY) and Buffered Methanol-complex Medium (BMMY) media following the User Guide of Pichia Expression Kit provided by Invitrogen, Co. Ltd. Yeast Nitrogen Base (YNB) was prepared with BD Difco YNB without amino acids (BD 291,940). Colonies were inoculated in 30 mL of BMGY medium in 300 mL baffle flasks and cultured at 200 rpm and 37 °C as pre-cultures. After 19 h, the cultured cells were harvested by centrifugation (1500 g, 25 °C for 5 min) and resuspended in BMMY medium to adjust the OD600 to ~ 1.0 to yield a BMMY suspension. Then, 12.5 mL of this BMMY suspension was transferred to a 125 mL baffle flask (1-8773-06, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Co. Ltd), and cells were shaken at 200 rpm as the control. The same volume of the BMMY suspension was transferred to FB. The temperature of both cultures was set to 30 °C. The OD600 was measured after 24 h.

Culturing of Bacillus sp. and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining

The frozen stock of Bacillus sp. was suspended in 100 mL medium (30 g L–1 soy peptone, 10 g L–1 glucose, 1 g L–1 KH2PO4, 0.5 g L–1 MgSO4·7H2O, pH 6) and 2 mL of the suspension was placed into the TPX film bag. The bag was placed statically at 24 °C for 48 h. All edges of the bag were cut, and each divided film was carefully washed with water. The adhered substances were fully stained with the film. The films were placed in a periodic acid solution (1%) for 10 min and washed two times with water. The film was then placed in Schiff’s reagent (191-08441. Wako Co., Ltd) for 10 min. The film was rinsed three times with sodium bisulfite-HCl solution (52 mM sodium bisulfite and 45 mM HCl) for 3 min each and then washed with tap water.

Turbidity

The turbidity of cell suspensions was measured at 600 nm after appropriate dilution with fresh medium (see Supplementary Information 2) using a spectrophotometer (UV1280, Shimadzu Co. Ltd).

Statistics

Significance was determined by a t-test using the “T.TEST” function in Microsoft Excel. The test was conducted as a two-tailed, paired sample Student’s t-test with unequal variances. Standard deviations are shown in the figures.
