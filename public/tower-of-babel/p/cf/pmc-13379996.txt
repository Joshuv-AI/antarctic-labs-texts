Establishment and characterization of a mouse model of cisplatin-induced olfactory aversion
Authors: Haruto Nakano, Yoshihisa Koyama, Koh Shinoda, Shoichi Shimada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chemotherapy-induced sensory alterations, including changes in taste and smell, have been reported as adverse effects of anticancer treatments. However, olfactory aversion has received little attention, and its underlying mechanisms remain unclear, limiting effective preventive or therapeutic strategies. Therefore, chemotherapy-induced olfactory aversion was investigated using cisplatin, a widely used anticancer drug. Three odorants—vanillin, benzaldehyde, and isoamyl acetate—were presented to C57BL/6J mice as conditioned stimuli together with cisplatin-induced visceral malaise (30 μg, intraperitoneal) to induce olfactory aversion. Odor preference was assessed using a two-bottle test. Cisplatin-treated mice exhibited altered preferences for all odor solutions and reduced total fluid intake compared with saline-treated controls. Meanwhile, no olfactory aversion was observed in zinc sulfate-induced anosmic male mice. Notably, female mice showed weaker olfactory aversion than male mice, suggesting a possible sex difference. These results established a cisplatin-induced olfactory-aversion mouse model. No structural abnormalities were observed in the olfactory epithelium of olfactory-aversion model mice following cisplatin treatment. In contrast, c-Fos immunostaining revealed increased neuronal activity in the septal region, whereas c-Fos expression in the lateral septum showed a similar but non-significant trend following vanillin administration to mice with established vanillin aversion. These findings suggest that central neural mechanisms may be associated with cisplatin-induced olfactory aversion, although the involvement of peripheral olfactory processes cannot be excluded. This study provides new insights into the neural correlates of chemotherapy-induced olfactory aversion and may aid the development of future therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

In recent years, alterations in olfactory function have attracted increasing attention as adverse effects associated with cancer chemotherapy. In particular, olfactory aversion—characterized by the development of discomfort or aversion to specific odors—is considered a form of olfactory hypersensitivity to odor stimuli. This effect has been reported in approximately 20% of patients undergoing chemotherapy as a psychologically mediated side effect [[1], [2], [3]]. Although olfactory aversion does not directly affect prognosis, it may markedly reduce the quality of life of patients by diminishing motivation to continue treatment and interfering with daily activities.

The development of food aversion following chemotherapy has been suggested to involve classical conditioning mechanisms [4]. In this framework, nausea and visceral malaise induced by anticancer drugs act as unconditioned stimuli, while concurrently experienced gustatory stimuli serve as conditioned stimuli, resulting in taste aversion. Similarly, olfactory aversion is thought to arise through aversive conditioning between chemotherapy-induced visceral discomfort and specific odors [5]. While taste-aversion learning has been extensively studied, research on olfactory-aversion learning remains limited, and its neurobiological mechanisms are not yet fully understood. Consequently, no established preventive or therapeutic strategies for olfactory aversion currently exist, highlighting the need to elucidate its underlying mechanisms and to develop novel treatment approaches.

In the present study, cisplatin—an anticancer drug widely used in chemotherapy [6,7]—was employed to establish an olfactory-aversion mouse model and to investigate the mechanisms underlying cisplatin-induced olfactory aversion.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of Osaka University (Approval no. 24-085-004) and performed in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARRIVE guidelines. Humane endpoints were applied, and affected mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal administration of pentobarbital sodium (200 mg/kg).

Animals and aversion conditioning induced by cisplatin

A total of 84 C57BL/6J mice (60 males, 24 females; 7–9 weeks old) were purchased from Japan SLC (Shizuoka, Japan) and housed at 23–25 °C with ad libitum access to food and water. After a 1-week acclimation period, the mice were used for experiments at 8–10 weeks of age.

Three odorants—vanillin (Van), benzaldehyde (Bz), and isoamyl acetate (IA)—were dissolved in distilled water (DW) at 0.001% and used as odor solutions. Mice perceive odor solutions via both orthonasal and retronasal olfaction during drinking [8,9]. The concentrations used were below the taste-detection thresholds and detectable only by olfaction [[10], [11], [12]].

All solutions were freshly prepared before testing. Aversive conditioning was induced by intraperitoneal injection of cisplatin (100 μL) concurrently with the presentation of the odor solutions.

Two-bottle test

A two-bottle choice test was used to evaluate aversive conditioning based on changes in fluid intake, as previously described [13]. Mice were given free access to two bottles containing an odor solution and DW (or another odor solution), and bottle positions were alternated daily. Preparation of anosmic mouse models

An anosmic male mouse model was generated as previously described [14]. Under deep anesthesia (medetomidine 0.3 mg/kg, midazolam 4.0 mg/kg, and butorphanol 5.0 mg/kg, i.p.), 5% zinc sulfate (ZnSO4; 10 μL) was instilled into each nostril and the procedure was repeated after 2 min. Anesthesia was reversed with atipamezole (administered intraperitoneally), and the two-bottle test was initiated the following day.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining of the nasal cavity

After the two-bottle test, mice were deeply anesthetized and nasal cavity tissues were collected and immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB; pH 7.4). Tissues were decalcified for 3 days in an EDTA solution (90 g EDTA-2Na·2H2O and 100 g EDTA-4Na in 1 L DW), cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in PB, frozen in powdered dry ice, and stored at −80 °C until use.

Frozen samples were cryosectioned at 20 μm and mounted on MAS-coated slides (Matsunami Glass Ind., Ltd., Osaka, Japan). After drying, sections were stained with Mayer's HE (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan), dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped with Entellan. Images were acquired using a light microscope (BX53; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluorescence staining of free-floating sections

Mice that acquired aversion to the Van solution and control mice were exposed to Van or DW for 10 min before perfusion to reproduce the aversive state. Under deep anesthesia with a three-agent mixture, mice were perfused with 4% PFA. Brains were removed, post-fixed overnight at 4 °C, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in 0.1 M PB, frozen in dry ice, and sectioned at 30 μm. Free-floating sections were stored in PBS at 4 °C.

Sections were washed in PBS, blocked with 0.3% Triton X-100 and 3% bovine serum albumin for 30 min, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-c-Fos antibody (1:1000; Abcam). After washing, sections were incubated for 1 h with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Sections were mounted with PermaFluor (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and observed using a fluorescence microscope (BX53; Olympus). All images were acquired using identical microscope settings. Images were analyzed using ImageJ (version 1.52a; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Images were converted to grayscale, inverted, and thresholds were adjusted individually for each section to compensate for differences in background staining intensity. Quantification was not performed in a blinded manner. Nuclei were defined as c-Fos–positive when they exhibited a round or oval nuclear morphology and fluorescence intensity clearly distinguishable from the background at 10× magnification. Large irregular signals exceeding the typical size of neuronal cell bodies (>20 μm) were excluded from the analysis. Cell counts were performed using all acquired images from each section. The number of c-Fos–positive cells was then quantified in each brain region.

As a negative control, sections were processed in parallel without the primary antibody to assess nonspecific staining. In consideration of animal welfare, negative control experiments were performed using mice from the cisplatin-treated and saline-treated groups exposed to Van (n = 3 per group), rather than all experimental combinations (Supplementary Fig. 1). No detectable signal was observed in these sections, confirming the specificity of the immunostaining.

Statistical analysis

For all two-bottle tests, fluid intake was measured at the cage level, with three mice housed per cage. Accordingly, each cage was treated as the experimental unit for statistical analyses.

Comparisons among four groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. For comparisons between two groups, data were first evaluated for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for equality of variances using Levene's test. As both assumptions were verified, differences were analyzed using an unpaired Student's t-test.

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Student Edition 18 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. Results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

Development of a mouse model for cisplatin-induced olfactory aversion

First, we examined whether cisplatin induces olfactory aversion to three odor solutions (0.001% Van, Bz, and IA) using a two-bottle test (Fig. 1a). Fluid intake was measured at the cage level (three mice per cage), and each cage was treated as the experimental unit for analysis. In cisplatin-treated male mice, daily fluid intake of all odor solutions was significantly reduced compared with saline controls, indicating robust olfactory-aversion learning (Fig. 1b). In contrast, cisplatin-treated female mice showed weaker effects, with a significant difference observed only for Bz (Fig. 1c). Although daily fluid intake of the odor solutions decreased after cisplatin treatment, the reductions were modest and overall fluid intake was higher than in males (Fig. 1d).

These results suggest a possible sex difference in the development of cisplatin-induced olfactory aversion, with female mice showing weaker responses than male mice.

Analysis of olfactory aversion to two odorants

Next, to determine whether cisplatin altered relative odor preference, male mice were presented with pairwise combinations of IA, Bz, and Van and tested using the two-bottle paradigm (Fig. 2a). In cisplatin-treated mice, intake tended to decrease for IA in IA-containing pairs (Van–IA and IA–Bz), whereas saline-treated mice showed similar intake ratios across all combinations (Fig. 2b). Brain regions involved in cisplatin-induced olfactory aversion

In the forebrain, group differences outside the primary olfactory pathway were observed in the septal region, a limbic structure connected with the hippocampus and amygdala (Fig. 4b). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant group effect on c-Fos-positive cell counts in the septal region (Fig. 4c). Notably, the Cis-Van group exhibited the highest number of c-Fos-positive cells in the septal region. Discussion

Cisplatin induced olfactory-aversion learning with possible sex- and odor-dependent differences. cisplatin-treated females exhibited weaker aversive responses, with a significant effect observed only for Bz. In paired presentations, IA appeared to be more readily associated with aversive learning than other odorants. No aversion was observed in cisplatin-treated male mice with olfactory epithelial damage, and no peripheral structural abnormalities were detected, in the olfactory epithelium following cisplatin treatment. Increased c-Fos expression in the septal region may be associated with olfactory-aversion learning. Taken together, these findings suggest that central neural mechanisms may contribute to cisplatin-induced olfactory aversion, although peripheral olfactory mechanisms cannot be completely excluded.

In male mice, cisplatin induced robust olfactory-aversion learning to all odor solutions through associative conditioning between visceral malaise and olfactory cues (Fig. 1b). This aversion was absent in ZnSO4-induced anosmic male mice and was not accompanied by morphological changes in the olfactory epithelium (Fig. 3), suggesting that intact olfactory function is required for olfactory-aversion learning and that structural abnormalities of the olfactory epithelium are unlikely to account for this phenomenon [15]. These findings support the establishment of a novel mouse model of cisplatin-induced olfactory aversion. Repeated exposure to the aversive stimulus may have facilitated long-term memory consolidation [16]. Reports on chemotherapy-related olfactory alterations remain limited and vary depending on assessment methods [17,18].

The cisplatin dose used here does not induce nausea [13] and approximates a typical single clinical dose [19]. Higher doses may reduce water intake and body weight, making two-bottle testing difficult, and neurotoxicity could confound behavioral evaluation. Although simplified, this model may help clarify the neural basis of chemotherapy-induced olfactory aversion.

Cisplatin-treated male mice exhibited robust olfactory-aversion learning, whereas cisplatin-treated females showed weaker and less stable responses (Fig. 1). Previous studies suggest that sex hormones influence odor sensitivity and aversive learning. For example, repeated exposure to benzaldehyde enhances olfactory sensitivity mainly in women of reproductive age and correlates with circulating sex-hormone levels [20,21]. Consistently, the higher drinking ratio for Bz in control females suggests possible sex-dependent physiological responses to odor stimuli. Testosterone has also been implicated in extinction memory in conditioned taste aversion [22], suggesting that hormone-dependent mechanisms may contribute to the observed differences in olfactory-aversion responses. However, the present findings should be considered preliminary, and further studies are required to confirm potential sex differences and their underlying mechanisms.

Natural odors are mixtures rather than single odorants. To better understand chemotherapy-induced olfactory aversion, we examined aversion learning when two odors were presented simultaneously. In saline-treated mice, drinking ratios converged to ∼50% across odor pairs, indicating behavioral neutrality (Fig. 2b). In the Bz–Van pair, some cisplatin-treated mice developed aversion to both odors. Despite their similar chemical structures [23], odor-specific drinking changes indicate that mice could discriminate them, consistent with their large olfactory receptor repertoire and high discrimination ability [24]. Thus, aversion to these similar odors likely reflects non-selective conditioning rather than impaired discrimination.

In contrast, in pairs containing IA, aversion preferentially targeted IA. Because IA differs structurally and perceptually from Bz and Van [25], it may activate distinct receptor patterns. Odorant interactions within mixtures may also influence perception, as odor mixtures are processed through receptor and central interactions rather than simple additive signals [26].

To examine neural activity during the aversive state, cisplatin-treated mice with established Van aversion were exposed to Van 10 min before perfusion fixation. c-Fos expression in the septal region showed a significant overall group effect, with the Cis-Van group showing the highest number of c-Fos–positive cells (Fig. 4). These findings suggest an association between septal neuronal activation and the expression of olfactory aversion. Because the septum is strongly connected with the hippocampus and amygdala, the observed activation may reflect stress-related responses to aversive stimuli [27]. However, the present c-Fos analysis is correlative in nature and does not establish that septal activation is necessary or sufficient for olfactory-aversion learning. Further functional studies will be required to clarify the causal contribution of this region.

Although the Cis-Van group tended to show higher numbers of c-Fos–positive cells in the lateral septum (LS), these differences did not reach statistical significance. The LS integrates cognitive and emotional signals to regulate behavioral responses [28]; therefore, its possible involvement in olfactory aversion warrants further investigation.

Previous studies show that cisplatin elevates serum substance P (SP), which can cross the blood–brain barrier and accumulate in cerebrospinal fluid [29]. SP is associated with nociception, inflammation, and stress responses [30] and is implicated in cisplatin-induced delayed nausea and vomiting [31]. Because the LS receives dense SPergic inputs and shows increased activity under stress [32], SP signaling may represent one possible mechanism linking cisplatin treatment to septal activation. However, neither SP signaling nor stress-related responses were directly evaluated in the present study. Therefore, the potential involvement of these mechanisms in cisplatin-induced olfactory aversion remains speculative and warrants further investigation.

Although often undervalued compared with vision and audition, olfaction is essential for survival, yet the neural basis of odor valence remains unclear. Using a cisplatin-induced olfactory-aversion mouse model, this study investigated mechanisms of olfactory-aversion learning. Understanding olfactory conditioning may provide insight into odor-triggered disorders such as hyperemesis gravidarum and post-traumatic stress disorder and inform conditioning-based therapies, including aversion therapy [33,34].

On the other hand, conditioned taste aversion and conditioned olfactory aversion are both forms of associative learning in which sensory cues are associated with visceral malaise [4,5]. In clinical settings, chemotherapy-induced food aversion is generally elicited by “flavor,” a multisensory perception composed mainly of taste and retronasal olfaction [4,5]. Thus, taste and olfactory aversion are closely related and are likely to share common neural mechanisms.

Rodents perceive aqueous odorants through both orthonasal and retronasal pathways during drinking, enabling odor solutions to serve as effective conditioned stimuli [[8], [9], [10]]. In addition, olfactory and gustatory information converges in brain regions such as the gustatory cortex and limbic system, where integrated processing contributes to learned aversive responses [11].

Our findings demonstrate that odor cues alone, independent of taste, can acquire aversive properties after cisplatin administration. These results suggest that olfactory aversion may represent an important component of the flavor-based food aversion experienced by patients undergoing chemotherapy.

Limitation

A limitation of this study is that human flavor perception involves the integration of taste, smell, texture, and visual cues, whereas the present model focuses primarily on olfactory conditioning [35,36]. In addition, species differences in sensory processing and cognition limit direct extrapolation to humans. Another limitation is that the number of female mice was smaller than that of male mice, which may have reduced statistical power. Female mice also exhibited greater inter-individual variability and delayed onset of aversive behavior compared with males, consistent with our previous findings in a cisplatin-induced conditioned taste aversion model [37]. These observations suggest that sex-specific biological factors, including possible effects of the estrous cycle, may influence chemotherapy-induced olfactory aversion. Further studies are needed to optimize experimental conditions for female mice and to clarify potential sex differences. In addition, some experiments, particularly the c-Fos analysis, were conducted with relatively small sample sizes. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require confirmation in larger cohorts. Furthermore, c-Fos quantification was not performed in a blinded manner, which may have introduced observer bias despite the use of predefined morphological criteria and standardized imaging conditions. Nevertheless, this model provides a useful platform for investigating the mechanisms of chemotherapy-induced olfactory aversion and for identifying potential therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel mouse model of cisplatin-induced olfactory aversion was established. Possible sex differences and septal neuronal involvement in olfactory-aversion learning were also identified. The findings suggest possible sex differences in susceptibility to olfactory aversion and indicate that septal neuronal activation may be associated with the expression of olfactory aversion. Further studies are required to clarify the underlying neural mechanisms and their causal contributions. These findings advance our understanding of olfactory aversion as a psychological adverse effect of chemotherapy and provide a foundation for future research on odor-triggered conditioning and related disorders.
