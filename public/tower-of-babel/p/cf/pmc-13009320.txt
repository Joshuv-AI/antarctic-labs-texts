Microplastic mineralization rate in Fenton reactions depends on polymer type
Authors: Katharina J Neubert, Nina Siebers, Nicolas Brüggemann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite microplastics (MPs) being highly inert pollutants, Fenton‐type reactions—using hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and iron(II) ions (Fe2+)—may effectively initiate chain cleavage and induce mineralization. However, mineralization rates and mechanisms for different MP types at varying Fenton reagent concentrations remain unclear. This study examined the mineralization of four MPs— low‐density polyethylene (LDPE), polypropylene (PP), polystyrene (PS), and polyester (PES)—by measuring CO2 release across varying H2O2 concentrations. Mineralization rates depended on both polymer type and H2O2 concentration. PES showed the highest degree of mineralization, followed by PP, while LDPE and PS exhibited the lowest rates. Increased H2O2 concentrations enhanced CO2 release and accelerated reaction saturation, especially for PES and PP, suggesting efficient mineralization due to elevated hydroxyl radical (OH˙) production. In contrast, PS and LDPE showed no significant increase in mineralization above certain H2O2 levels. Despite visible Fe‐oxide precipitates, scanning electron microscopy did not provide evidence of surface changes associated with mineralization. Contrary to expectations, aromatic PS was less susceptible to Fenton mineralization than aliphatic PP, possibly due to structural factors. The study also emphasizes the importance of radical recombination and scavenging at high OH˙ concentrations, which can lower mineralization efficiency. Non‐integer reaction orders suggest a multi‐step mineralization process influenced by both polymer structure and radical dynamics. These findings underscore the high environmental persistence of MPs, as natural mineralization, such as by fungi utilizing Fenton‐like mechanisms, occurs at even slower rates than those observed under controlled lab conditions.

INTRODUCTION

Plastic debris smaller than 5 mm, known as microplastic (MP), is increasingly entering the environment, particularly the oceans and soils. The widely used synthetic material has grown in production since the 1950s, with 400 million tonnes of plastic produced globally in 2022 (Garside, 2024). About 12.5 million tonnes of plastic is used in agriculture each year, of which a significant portion is likely to fragment into MP in soil over time (Morrison, 2024). Most MP found in soil is composed of polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), or polystyrene (PS) (Fan et al., 2023; Lehmann et al., 2021). They enter the soil through various pathways, for example, sewage sludge and compost application, plastic mulching, littering, tire wear, and atmospheric deposition (Büks & Kaupenjohann, 2020).

Despite their chemically inert character, microbial mineralization of MP has been found in soil. For instance, several bacterial genera (e.g., Bacillus, Rhodococcus, and Pseudomonas) have shown the ability to mineralize PE or PS, though reported mineralization rates are typically low, with mass losses of 3%–6% for conventional and up to 23% for modified plastics in soil within 30 days (Auta et al., 2017; Mohan et al., 2016; Tribedi & Sil, 2013). Similarly, fungi have been reported to modify synthetic polymer structures or to produce surface cracks on PE and polyurethane (Chen et al., 2016; Russell Jonathan et al., 2011; Sangale et al., 2019; Yamada‐Onodera et al., 2001).

The ability of fungi to mineralize MPs has been demonstrated particularly through their utilization of the Fenton reaction (Krueger et al., 2015; Sánchez, 2020; Schlosser, 2021). This reaction was first discovered by H. J. H. Fenton in 1894, when he observed a significant enhancement in the oxidative potential of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in the presence of Fe2+ ions (Fenton, 1894). Later, in 1934, Haber and Weiss (1934) proposed the mechanism behind this process, now known as the Fenton reaction (Equation 1), due to its strong oxidative capabilities to generate hydroxyl radicals (OH˙) (Haber & Weiss, 1934). Since then, the combination of H2O2 and Fe2+ in aqueous solution has been known as Fenton's reagent. Once OH˙ radicals are produced, they undergo a series of propagation reactions, leading to the formation of additional reactive oxygen species (Equations (2), (3), (4), (5)). The Fenton reaction (Equation 1) and the regeneration of Fe3+ ions back to Fe2+ in the presence of superoxide radicals (O2˙−) (Equation 5) culminate in the Haber–Weiss reduction (Equation 6).

Wood‐decaying brown‐rot fungi, for example, naturally utilize the Fenton reaction to compensate for their lack of ligninolytic enzymes required to break down polysaccharides in wood (Shah et al., 2015). The generation of the highly reactive and nonspecific OH˙ radicals during Fenton reaction allow for the depolymerization of long‐chain aliphatic structures by abstracting hydrogen atoms and can also mineralize aromatic hydrocarbons (Palmroth et al., 2006) and organochlorines (Laurent et al., 2012). Ectomycorrhizal fungi, such as Paxillus involutus, switch to protein decomposition as their nitrogen source, using the Fenton reaction to break down solid organic matter during ammonia depletion (Op De Beeck et al., 2018).

However, if fungal‐induced Fenton reactions are to be used for the remediation of soils contaminated with MPs, two key limitations arise: (i) soil carbonates act as radical scavengers (Watts et al., 2005) and (ii) a continuous Fe3+ to Fe2+ reduction system is required, typically via extracellular metabolites such as hydroquinones or reductive enzymes (Baldrian & Brabcová, 2008; Krueger et al., 2015). Furthermore, some fungi (e.g., Pleurotus ostreatus) only mineralize plastics effectively after pre‐treatment such as UV or heat exposure (da Luz et al., 2013, 2014, 2015). Identifying effective fungal strains thus remains challenging.

In addition to biotic systems, the purely chemical Fenton reaction has been applied directly to mineralize MPs. For instance, thermal Fenton treatments mineralize cosmetic PE MPs in water (Hu et al., 2022), while Ortiz et al. (2022) reported weight losses of around 10% for PE, PET, PVC, PP, and expanded PS (EPS) after 7.5 h of treatment. Significant surface changes due to the introduction of oxygenated groups made the MPs more acidic and hydrophilic. The study noted progressive size reduction and more effective oxidation of polymers with aromatic rings (EPS and PET) compared to PP and PVC. In contrast, Tagg et al. (2017) found no significant chemical or size alterations of PE, PP, PVC, and nylon MP, though they suggested that the Fenton reaction could be suitable as a pre‐treatment method for MP analysis in wastewater. Interestingly, Ortiz et al. (2022) utilized harsh reaction conditions (80°C) and Fe(NO3)3 · 9 H2O as the iron source, while Tagg et al. (2017) used ambient conditions and FeSO4. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that while Fenton reaction can mineralize MPs, efficiency varies depending on factors such as polymer type, particle size, reaction conditions (temperature, pH, and reagent concentrations), and treatment duration.

The aim of this study was to demonstrate that, while MP mineralization via the Fenton reaction under laboratory conditions can be monitored through a consistent release of CO2, with reaction kinetics influenced by H2O2 concentration and polymer structure, there was no clear evidence of significant OH˙ radical surface mineralization on MPs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Microplastics

Based on literature data on MP contamination of German agricultural soils (de Souza Machado et al., 2019; Piehl et al., 2018), low‐density polyethylene (LDPE), PP, PS, and polyester (PES) microfibers were considered significant for our study. LDPE, PP, and PS were obtained from Goodfellow Cambridge Limited. LDPE was purchased in powder form (LS543047, maximum particle size 300 µm), whereas PP (PP306312, particle size: 5 mm) and PS (ST316311, particle size: 3–5 mm) were pulverized using a conventional high‐performance kitchen blender (Blendtec Classic 575 with 1560‐W engine, Blendtec) (Figure S1) and sieved to 100–355 µm. The particle size distributions of these granular MP types were measured in duplicate via a laser scattering particle size distribution analyzer (Partica LA‐960, Horiba) by suspending the respective powder in ethanol (Figure S2). The mean diameter sizes for LDPE, PP, and PS were 283 ± 0.3, 353 ± 0.8, and 305 ± 1.6 µm, respectively.

The PES fibers used were commercially available pillow filling material (JYSK Nordic A/S) with 0.74 ± 0.01 mm in diameter, determined with the software tool WinRHIZO (Regent Instruments Inc.) from fiber scans (EPSON, Expression, 12000xl). Fibers of approximately 3 cm in length were further cut to achieve smaller sized fibers in various lengths below 3 cm. The polymer structure was PET, which was determined via Raman spectroscopy (Table S1; Figure S3). The monomeric as well as the polymeric structures of all used MP types are depicted in Figure 1.

The Fenton's reagent consisted of H2O2 solution (30% w/w, Merck) and an FeSO4∙7 H2O solution (Merck). All solutions were prepared with Milli‐Q water.

Experimental set‐up

The CO2 evolution due to MP mineralization was followed online with an infrared laser absorption analyzer (model G2508, Picarro, Inc.), referred to as “Picarro” in the following, using a glass bowl filled with tap water and a 20‐mL glass beaker. The gas inlet and outlet of the Picarro were connected with ¼ in. PTFE tubing to a 10‐cm‐long household sewage pipe (HTM DN50, inner diameter: 50 mm, inner volume: 17.5 mL), closed gastight with an end cap at the top, which served as measurement chamber. In addition, the measurement chamber also had a third piece of ⅛ in. PTFE tubing open to the environment for pressure equilibration. The beaker acted as the reactor, to which 50 mg of the respective MP type and 15 mL of the Fenton's reagent were added. Once the H2O2 and the FeSO4 solution had been added, the beaker was placed in the center of the glass bowl filled with tap water, and the measurement chamber was positioned over the beaker. The water in the glass bowl sealed the chamber, ensuring that the whole system was gastight (Figure S4). The oxidation reaction of the MP took place at room temperature in the dark. The CO2 emissions were measured in duplicate over a period of 4 h from the start of the reaction.

Scanning‐electron microscopic images

The microstructural analysis of the MPs was performed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU‐8000) at 15 keV. The initial MPs (before treatment with Fenton's reagent) and after the Fenton's reaction were placed onto a double‐sided C‐tape and mounted onto an SEM sample holder. Before analysis, the samples were sputter‐coated with a 10‐nm‐thick gold layer to avoid charging effects. At higher magnification, secondary electron images predominately arising from the surface of the sample were used.

RESULTS

CO2 emission patterns during the Fenton‐mediated mineralization of the different MPs

The net release of CO2 (measured CO2 minus blank) confirmed the mineralization capability of the Fenton reaction (Equation 1). With a molar ratio of H2O2 to Fe2+ consistently maintained at 100 for all treatments, H2O2 was in excess relative to the MP species. For treatments F0.2, F0.5, and F1, this corresponded to 130, 260, and 520 mmol H2O2 per gram of MP species, respectively. The reactions were conducted in the dark to prevent the reduction of Fe3+ to Fe2+ by UV radiation (Yu & Kuzyakov, 2021), which could have reduced the reaction rate. The reaction ceased once H2O2 was depleted.

The curves obtained by plotting the net(mCO2‐C) per MP mass (in µg g−1) versus time (in min) displayed a polynomial trend with monotonic increases for treatment F0.2, reaching 349 ± 26, 210 ± 25, 103 ± 13, and 81 ± 35 µg g−1 CO2‐C per MP mass after 240 min for the mineralization of PES, PP, PS, and LDPE, respectively (Figure 2). Different from the mineralization curves of the particulate MP types (Figure 2a–c), the mineralization curve of the fibrous PES (Figure 2d) was notably steeper.

In contrast, the mineralization curves for treatments F0.5 and F1 (Figure 2) exhibited an exponential decay pattern, with an initial rapid increase followed by an asymptotic flattening toward a threshold depending on the MP species and H2O2 concentration. Higher H2O2 concentrations resulted in steeper initial slopes of the curves that flattened earlier than the curves recorded at lower concentration of the Fenton's reagent. In the F0.5 treatment, PES mineralization resulted in the highest CO2‐C release per MP mass with 804 ± 207 µg g− 1 (Figure 2d), while PS, LDPE, and PP showed similar trends after 120 min, with total CO2‐C releases after 240 min of 501 ± 77, 377 ± 26, and 428 ± 84, respectively. In treatment F1, only PP and PES showed significantly higher CO2‐C losses than in the F0.5 treatment. PP mineralization in F1 released 943 ± 48 µg g− 1 CO2‐C, while PES mineralization released even 1177 ± 105 µg g− 1 CO2‐C after 240 min. For LDPE, both F0.5 and F1 curves converged after 120 min, with the F1 curve further rising until the end of the measurement period (437 ± 9 µg g−1 CO2‐C), while the F0.5 curve stabilized at 377 ± 26 µg g− 1 CO2‐C after 240 min (Figure 2a). In contrast, PS mineralization (Figure 2c) in the F1 treatment produced less CO2‐C (358 ± 84 µg g−1) compared to 0.5 M H2O2. The F1 curve gradually reached 358 ± 84 µg g−1 CO2‐C after 240 min, while the F0.5 curve stabilized at 501 ± 77 µg g−1.

The comparison of the net CO2‐C release from MP in the different treatments (Table 2) revealed that mineralization does not increase for all MP types with higher H2O2 concentrations. However, the amount of CO2‐C released from PES fibers after 240 min of treatment with the Fenton's reagent increased progressively with higher H2O2 concentrations, rising from 0.03% to 0.08%, and then to 0.12% of the MP mass with 0.2 M, 0.5 M, and 1 M H2O2 in combination with Fe2+ (100:1), respectively. A similar pattern was observed for the mineralization of PP, where the release of CO2‐C increased progressively with higher H2O2 concentrations, starting at 0.02% of the MP mass with 0.2 M H2O2, then to 0.04% with 0.5 M, and reaching 0.09% with 1 M H2O2. In contrast, PS and LDPE did not show increased mineralization with higher H2O2 concentrations; the CO2‐C release for both types remained around 0.04% with 0.5 M H2O2, with no further CO2‐C release at 1 M H2O2.

Mineralization kinetics

The rate of change of CO2 concentration over time (d(CO2)/dt) in ppm min−1) was calculated from the net release of CO2 (in ppm) (Figure 3). For the F0.5 and F1 treatments, the change in CO2 concentration was highest at the beginning of the reaction and gradually decreased over time. However, in the F0.2 treatment, the CO2 concentration curves did not show any initial increase. The rate of CO2 production remained constant, close to zero, indicating minimal mineralization activity under these conditions. This was particularly evident for the polymer types LDPE, PP, and PS (Figure 3a–c), suggesting that the mineralization of these MPs was negligible with the F0.2 treatment. In contrast, PES exhibited a slight increase in the rate of CO2 production over time. The CO2 concentration for PES showed a gradual upward trend, reaching a maximum rate of 0.48 ± 0.07 ppm min−1 over the measurement period of 240 min.

The F0.5 treatment showed a lower initial CO2 production rate compared to the F1 treatment, reaching maximum rates of 2.76 ± 0.46, 1.55 ± 0.07, and 2.20 ± 1.33 ppm min−1, respectively, for the mineralization of LDPE (Figure 2a), PP (Figure 2b), and PES (Figure 2d), respectively. Only the mineralization of PS (Figure 2c) in the F0.5 treatment showed a higher initial CO2 production rate (2.31 ± 0.6 ppm min−1) than in the F1 treatment. Furthermore, the rate of change in CO2 production in the F0.5 treatment generally showed a less steep decline compared to the F1 treatment.

In the F1 treatment, the initial CO2 production rates were 11.56 ± 0.09 ppm min−1 for PES and 9.63 ± 0.83 ppm min−1 for PP, whereas they were only 4.29 ± 0.45 ppm min−1 for LDPE and 1.90 ± 0.83 ppm min−1 for PS. Notably, the mineralization of PS in the F1 treatment was less efficient than in the F0.5 treatment, reflected in the lower rates of change in CO2 mixing ratio (Figure 3c).

In summary, the mineralization rate for PP increased 4.9‐fold from F0.2 to F0.5 and 6.2‐fold from F0.5 to F1. For PES, the mineralization rate increased 11.2‐fold from F0.2 to F0.5 and 5.4‐fold from F0.5 to F1. In contrast, the mineralization rates of LDPE and PS increased from F0.2 to F0.5 22‐fold for LDPE and 13‐fold for PS. However, from F0.5 to F1, the mineralization rate for LDPE only increased by 1.6‐fold, while the rate for PS decreased by 0.82‐fold.

The reaction orders for the mineralization of PES, PP, and LDPE are listed in Table S3 and were obtained by applying Equations ((14), (15), (16)). Only the factor by which the reaction rate was enhanced from F0.5 to F1 was considered usable, as the reaction curves of F0.2 are not comparable to those of F0.5 and F1. While the reaction orders (n) for the mineralization of PES and PP was around 2.5 (nPES = 2.43, nPP = 2.63), n was 0.64 for LDPE.

Surface structure after the radical attack

Fenton's reagent‐treated MP species (1 M H2O2, 240 min) showed no typical signs of mineralization, such as cracks or holes on their surfaces, under the SEM (Figure 4). Nevertheless, small droplets were observed adhering to the surfaces of the LDPE particles. The PS particles, on the other hand, displayed a roughened texture. Similarly, PES fibers were coated with an additional layer that may have dried. After the MP species had reacted with Fenton's reagent, the MP particles and fibers were covered with a visible orange precipitate.

DISCUSSION

Mineralization efficiency of MPs by the Fenton reaction

The mineralization of MPs was confirmed by the steady net release of CO2 during the reaction. The CO2 evolution was chosen as the primary analytical indicator because it provides direct and quantitative evidence of radical‐induced polymer chain scission and oxidative mineralization, without sample manipulation, particularly in the heterogeneous MP‐Fenton system employed in this study. As MPs were floating at the solution interface, conventional extraction‐based analytical techniques would be limited. Moreover, CO2 represents a polymer‐independent oxidation endpoint, enabling direct comparison of mineralization kinetics among different MP types. The CO2 emission curves for F0.5 and F1 showed typical saturation curves and varying slopes of the curve depending on the H2O2 concentration. The net CO2 release can be considered as the abstraction of carbon atoms from the polymer chains in the form of CO2, possibly due to the generation of OH˙ radicals by the Fenton reaction. The temporal development of the CO2 observed in the release curves indicated that not only more CO2 was generated per MP species at higher H2O2 concentration, but also that the reaction saturated earlier. This is because the reaction between H2O2 and FeSO4 is very fast (exponential increase), and the generation of OH˙ radicals is mainly limited by the depletion of H2O2 (saturation region) (Lin et al., 2021). The Fenton reaction was conducted at pH 2–3, which lies within the optimal acidic range reported for efficient OH˙ radical generation and is consistent with previous studies (Jiang et al., 2010; Krupińska, 2024; Ortiz et al., 2022). The efficiency of the Fenton reaction is strongly pH dependent, as acidic conditions promote Fe solubility and facilitate catalytic cycling between Fe2+ and Fe3+, thereby enhancing OH˙ radical production and subsequent oxidative mineralization of polymer chains. Consequently, at a constant H2O2‐to‐Fe2+ ratio (100:1 in this study), the extent and kinetics of MP mineralization, as reflected by CO2 production, are strongly governed by the initial H2O2 concentration.

The calculated CO2‐C per MP mass varied significantly among the different treatments and types of MPs. PES showed the highest degree of mineralization in all treatments, followed by PP, whereas the mineralization of LDPE and PS with Fenton's reagent was less effective, reflected by the slower release of CO2 compared to PES and PP. In line with this observation, the mineralization of PES and PP, as quantified by the release of CO2, increased with higher H2O2 concentration, indicating more efficient mineralization at presumably higher OH˙ radical concentrations. In contrast, PS and LDPE did not show increased CO2 release with higher H2O2 concentrations, suggesting that the mineralization efficiency for these polymers does not significantly increase beyond a certain H2O2 concentration threshold.

In previous studies, it was reported that polymers with aromatic rings, such as PS or PET, were found to be more prone to oxidation by Fenton's reagent compared to polymers formed of alkane chains like PE or PP (Ortiz et al., 2022; Y. Yang et al., 2022). In our case, we can confirm that the PES fibers consisting of PET showed a good mineralization susceptibility, whereas LDPE had a poor susceptibility to mineralization by the Fenton's reagent. However, PS showed a rather low degree of mineralization in our study, while the alkane PP, contrary to the general assumption, showed a rather good degree of mineralization toward OH˙ radical attack.

Considering the unexpectedly low mineralization efficiency of PS observed in our study, it is worth reconsidering whether measuring only CO2 release is a sufficient indicator of PS mineralization. Other studies examining PS MP mineralization via the Fenton reaction have employed additional analytical methods, which may provide a more comprehensive picture of the mineralization process (Y. Yang et al., 2022). For example, Y. Yang et al. (2022) demonstrated PS mineralization by evaluating weight loss, changes in the oxygen‐to‐carbon (O/C) ratio of treated PS, and identifying mineralization products, such as benzene ring derivatives. These methods offer direct evidence of structural changes and the breakdown of PS, which CO2 measurement alone may fail to capture. Moreover, the study specifically showed that PS treated with a microbe‐driven Fenton reaction had a significantly greater weight loss of 0.6 ± 0.1% after 14 days compared to the radical‐based Fenton reaction alone. This suggests that microbial activity facilitated more substantial alterations to the aromatic structure of PS, pointing to mineralization beyond CO2 release alone. Therefore, measuring CO2 exclusively may not be the most effective approach to validate PS mineralization, as it could overlook other significant changes to the material's structure and composition that alternative methods would reveal.

The reason why LDPE exhibited lower mineralization compared to PES, which showed much higher mineralization, can be explained by the chemical nature of their structures (e.g., alkane vs. polyester) and the reaction mechanism of OH˙ radical attack. Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons with only single bonds between carbon atoms, making them chemically unreactive. They lack functional groups such as double bonds or aromatic rings that can be readily attacked by OH˙ radicals generated in the Fenton reaction. In contrast, polyesters contain ester functional groups (−COO−) that are more reactive toward oxidative attack (Zandieh et al., 2024). In addition, the OH˙ radical is more effective at attacking weaker bonds or bonds adjacent to electron‐withdrawing groups, which are more common in polyesters. Polyesters can form oxygenated functional groups (e.g., hydroxyl and carbonyl) upon reaction with OH˙ radicals. These groups can further facilitate the breakdown of the polymer chain through oxidative cleavage (Zandieh et al., 2024). Alkanes, however, do not readily form such intermediates, making their mineralization less efficient. Furthermore, polyesters, such as PET, contain aromatic rings that can stabilize radical intermediates formed during the Fenton reaction. This stabilization facilitates further oxidative reactions, leading to a more efficient mineralization (Ortiz et al., 2022; Zandieh et al., 2024). Alkanes lack such stabilizing structures, resulting in lower reactivity and slower mineralization rates, as seen for LDPE in our study.

Despite this principal explanation, PP, as an alkane, showed a comparatively good mineralization capacity under attack by OH˙ radicals. OH˙ radicals typically initiate mineralization by abstracting hydrogen atoms from the polymer chains (Calvert, 1986). Unlike LDPE, PP has methyl groups as side chains on every other carbon atom of its main hydrocarbon chain. This creates tertiary carbon sites, which are the most likely sites for hydrogen abstraction by OH˙ radical attack (Sirtori et al., 2014), as they form more stable radicals. In addition, LDPE has a high degree of both short‐ and long‐chain branching (Malpass, 2010), whereas PP is arranged in a more linear structure. The branched structure in LDPE may hinder the mobility of formed radicals, potentially leading to more localized mineralization (Aldas et al., 2018). On the contrary, the more regular structure of PP might allow for greater radical mobility along the polymer chain (Kausch, 2005).

Fenton reaction kinetics

The rate of CO2 production over time (d(CO2)/dt) provided additional insights into the mineralization kinetics. For the F0.5 and F1 treatments, the CO2 production rate was highest at the beginning and then gradually decreased, indicating rapid initial mineralization, followed by a slower phase as reactants were consumed. The F0.2 treatment, however, showed minimal mineralization activity, except for PES. Generally, higher initial CO2 production rates indicate a rapid initial mineralization, likely due to a higher concentration of reactive species. A decrease in the change in CO2 concentration over time (approaching zero) suggests that the reaction is reaching completion or a steady state where the rate of CO2 production, and therefore mineralization, slows down.

The rate of change in CO2 release generally declined less steeply in F0.5 than in F1, indicating a more gradual reaction progress, possibly due to fewer available OH˙ radicals compared to the F1 treatment. While the F0.5 treatment resulted in lower initial CO2 production rates for LDPE, PP, and PES compared to F1, the mineralization of PS showed higher initial rates for F0.5 than for F1.

Although it may seem counterintuitive that a lower H2O2 concentration—and subsequently presumably a lower concentration of OH˙ radicals—can lead to more effective mineralization, several factors can explain this phenomenon. At high radical concentrations, radicals can react with each other instead of the target material, a process known as radical recombination, which reduces their effectiveness and decreases the overall mineralization rate (Chamas et al., 2020). In addition, scavenging effects could have occurred. Additives or impurities in the polymer matrix can act as radical scavengers, neutralizing radicals before they cause further mineralization (McNeill, 1997). These scavenging effects are less pronounced at lower radical concentrations, allowing for more effective mineralization, whereas at high radical concentrations, scavenging effects can become more pronounced, potentially slowing down the overall mineralization process. Radicals may also abstract hydrogen atoms from nearby polymer molecules, thereby stabilizing themselves but not contributing to further mineralization (Yousif & Haddad, 2013). The effectiveness of the mineralization process depends on the balance between productive radical reactions (those that affect mineralization) and nonproductive reactions like scavenging. This balance is influenced by factors such as the concentration of radicals, the presence of oxygen, and the polymer's physical state (Crincoli et al., 2020). High radical concentrations can lead to side reactions or the formation of less reactive intermediates, which may slow down the overall mineralization process (Behnami et al., 2022).

The non‐integer reaction orders of 2.43 for PES, 2.63 for PP, and 0.64 for LDPE suggest a more complex reaction mechanism. In practice, such a result may indicate (i) a reaction with multiple steps or competing pathways (Krenos, 2001), (ii) the involvement of catalysts or inhibitors affecting the rate in a complex manner (Krenos, 2001), or (iii) a reaction that does not follow simple integer‐order kinetics. A multi‐step reaction is often reflected by non‐integer orders when intermediate species are involved. If the intermediate species influence the overall rate, they can cause the observed reaction rate to have a non‐integer order. In our study, CO2 was the final mineralization product due to the OH˙ radical attack on the polymer chains of the MPs. Initially, however, alkyl radicals are formed, which propagate through further abstraction and recombination reactions to continue the breakdown of long polymer chains into smaller carbonyl compounds and ultimately into CO2 (Speight, 2020). Therefore, in reaction media containing Fenton's reagent, other intermediates can be identified, such as aromatic compounds from PS mineralization (B. Liu et al., 2022) or oxygenated groups after the breakdown of PE (Hu et al., 2022). This was also demonstrated in a study that provided an in‐depth analysis of the oxidizing intermediates in Fenton reactions and discussed how their formation and interactions lead to complex kinetics (Meyerstein, 2022).

For practical purposes, the reaction orders of the reaction of PES and PP with Fenton's reagent can be approximated to a reaction order of 2.5 or treated as a mixed‐order reaction, while for LDPE, a reaction order of one appeared to be the closest approximation. However, the exact values provide more precise information about the reaction kinetics and suggest that the reaction mechanism is more complex than a simple elementary reaction. The literature recognizes that kinetic modeling of Fenton and Fenton‐like reactions is complex, as the reaction mechanism often does not fit neatly into integer reaction orders, or two different reaction orders are required to adequately describe the reaction kinetics (Duesterberg & Waite, 2006; Guedes et al., 2003; H. Liu et al., 2007; S. Wang, 2008). For example, the reaction kinetics of Fenton reactions for wastewater decolorization are described as a combined pseudo first‐order process with strong dependence on the initial Fe2+ and Fe3+ concentrations (S. Wang, 2008). Another study concluded that the overall kinetics of the Fenton reaction in cork boiling can be described by a second‐order followed by a zero‐order rate equation (Guedes et al., 2003). In contrast, a fractional order kinetics was identified for a photocatalytic mineralization reaction (C. Wang, 2018).

Microplastic mineralization as indicated by SEM

The SEM images revealed no visible signs of mineralization. Instead, a fine precipitate—most likely iron oxide or iron hydroxide (Equation 7)—was observed on the surfaces of the various MPs. This finding aligns with the study of Tagg et al. (2017), who also reported no significant surface changes in MPs following treatment with Fenton's reagent under ambient conditions, using FeSO4 as the iron source. In contrast, in another study, clear signs of mineralization, such as cracks on the surface of PS MPs, were visible with SEM imaging (B. Liu et al., 2022), but only after intensive treatment involving four cycles of Fenton's reagent (30% H2O2 + 0.2 M Fe2+ solution) combined with peroxymonosulfate for 5 h in each cycle.

CONCLUSION

We demonstrated that OH˙ radicals interacted differently with MPs depending on polymer structure, resulting in complex and polymer‐specific mineralization kinetics, as reflected by the observed differences in CO2 release. The mineralization efficiency of Fenton's reagent was mainly controlled by polymer type and H2O2 concentration. PES and PP showed increased mineralization with higher H2O2 concentrations, whereas PS and LDPE did not benefit from concentrations above 0.5 M, likely due to enhanced radical recombination and scavenging at higher oxidant levels. Non‐integer reaction orders observed for PES, PP, and LDPE further indicate complex mineralization mechanisms. Despite measurable CO2 production, overall MP mineralization efficiencies were extremely low, highlighting the strong persistence of MPs in soil environments. Substantially higher mineralization rates would require harsh conditions, such as elevated temperatures (∼80°C) and acidic pH (∼3), as reported by Ortiz et al. (2022).

Although Fenton‐like reactions can occur naturally in soils through fungal H2O2 production and iron cycling, reagent concentrations and reactivity are far lower than in commonly studied laboratory systems. Given the lack of supporting data for quantitative environmental extrapolation, our results should be interpreted as upper bound estimates under favorable acidic conditions. Overall, hydroxyl‐radical‐driven MP mineralization in soils is expected to proceed at very low rates, largely due to limited radical availability and scavenging by soil components such as organic matter, minerals, and antioxidants, which rapidly neutralize OH˙ radicals before they can attack polymer surfaces.
