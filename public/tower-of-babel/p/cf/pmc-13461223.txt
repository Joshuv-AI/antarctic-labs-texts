Long-term outcomes of peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM): reflux esophagitis and complications including Barrett esophagus and peptic strictures
Authors: Elise M Wessels, Gwen M C Masclee, Barbara A J Bastiaansen, Paul Fockens, Albert J Bredenoord
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPeroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) is an effective and safe treatment for achalasia, but short-term follow-up studies suggest an increased risk of gastroesophageal reflux after treatment. The aim of this study was to evaluate the risk of reflux esophagitis and the long-term complications in achalasia patients more than 5 years after POEM. This prospective cohort study included achalasia patients who underwent POEM between August 2011 and December 2019 and were scheduled for follow-up upper gastrointestinal endoscopy at least five years after treatment. Multivariate regression was used to assess predictive factors for reflux esophagitis after POEM. In total 161 achalasia patients were included of whom 153 completed questionnaires. Median age at follow-up was 56 years and 49.7% were female. Reflux esophagitis was diagnosed in 67 patients (41.6%, 95%-CI 34.0%–49.2%), with 22 of these cases classified as severe (grade C/D, 32.8%). Prior laparoscopic Heller myotomy (OR 0.44, 95%-CI 0.20–0.98) and retreatment with PD (OR 0.36, 95%-CI 0.17–0.75) were associated with lower observed rates of reflux esophagitis. Factors associated with severe reflux esophagitis (grade C/D) included BMI (OR 1.13, 95%-CI 1.04–1.23) and PD after POEM (OR 0.12, 95%-CI 0.02–0.93). Barrett esophagus was observed in five patients (3.1%). Peptic strictures were seen in three patients (1.9%) necessitating dilation and no cases of esophageal adenocarcinoma were identified. Reflux esophagitis remains a common long-term finding in achalasia patients more than 5 years after POEM and is classified as severe in nearly one-third of these cases.

INTRODUCTION

Achalasia is a rare esophageal motility disorder, affecting approximately 0.3–1.63 per 100,000 adults per year.1 Peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) is an effective achalasia treatment, demonstrating high success rates on long-term follow-up with a 5-year clinical success rate of 75%–81%.2,3 In treatment naive achalasia patients POEM shows better long-term efficacy compared to pneumatic dilation (PD), with success rates, defined as an Eckard score ≤ 3, of 81% versus 40% after 5 years of follow-up respectively.3 For PD, a graded approach is recommended to minimize the risk of perforation and to enhance the durability of treatment response; however, repeated dilation series are often required to maintain treatment effect. POEM was found to be non-inferior to laparoscopic Heller myotomy (LHM), with 5 year success rates of 75% after POEM and 71% after LHM.2

During POEM a submucosal tunnel is created towards the esophagogastric junction followed by myotomy of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) and distal part of the esophagus.4 This leads to achalasia symptom improvement by enhancing the emptying of the esophagus. However, the disadvantage of POEM is the risk of gastroesophageal reflux as the myotomy of the LES also affects the natural reflux barrier which normally prevents the back flow of gastric acid into the esophagus. Furthermore, the lack of esophageal peristalsis in achalasia patients hinders the clearance of refluxate which make them even more vulnerable for developing gastroesophageal reflux.

The risk of reflux esophagitis in the short-time after POEM varies across studies with meta-analyses estimating prevalence rates between 13% and 33%.5 Similarly, abnormal acid exposure time during 24-hours pH-impedance measurement and reflux symptoms are common in a significant proportion of patients after POEM. However, these factors are not correlated with each other or with the presence of reflux esophagitis.6,7 In a previous study we showed that reflux esophagitis is present in about half of the achalasia patients within one year after POEM of which 22% is graded as severe reflux esophagitis.8 It is essential to assess long-term prevalence of reflux esophagitis after POEM and to manage it effectively in order to prevent complications of longstanding untreated reflux esophagitis, including peptic strictures, Barrett esophagus and esophageal adenocarcinoma.9

Currently, long-term studies on the risk of reflux esophagitis and the development of complications resulting from reflux esophagitis in achalasia patients after POEM are lacking. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the risk of reflux esophagitis and its long-term complications in achalasia patients more than 5 years after POEM.

METHODS

Study design and patient selection

This prospective inception cohort study was performed at the Amsterdam University Medical Center between February 2024 and August 2025. Patients who had undergone POEM between August 2011 and December 2019 were contacted by telephone to schedule an outpatient clinic consultation as part of standard care. If achalasia symptom recurrence was reported, barium esophagram was performed to assess stasis and, if indicated, high-resolution manometry combined with EndoFLIP was performed to evaluate the distensibility of the lower esophageal sphincter. In case of objectively confirmed recurrence, pneumatic dilation was offered. For patients without objectively confirmed recurrence or with stable symptoms, follow-up upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was discussed to assess the presence of reflux esophagitis and its complications, including Barrett esophagus. Patients scheduled for upper gastrointestinal endoscopy were asked to complete questionnaires prior to upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. Data on other relevant follow-up results between POEM and last upper gastrointestinal endoscopy were retrieved from medical records. For patients who underwent re-POEM, the first POEM procedure was considered as baseline in this study.

All patients aged 18 years or older at the time of the follow-up endoscopy were included when achalasia was diagnosed based on esophageal high-resolution manometry (HRM) and POEM was performed at least 5 years ago. Patients were excluded if they did not undergo upper gastrointestinal endoscopy more than five years after POEM or if they lacked proficiency in either Dutch or English. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before completing questionnaires. The Institutional Review Board concluded that formal medical ethical assessment was not necessary (2023.1019).

Procedures and follow-up

Patients were instructed to follow a liquid diet 3 days before POEM followed by an overnight fast prior to the procedure. POEM was performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation by experienced interventional endoscopists. The procedural steps of POEM and post-procedural care were carried out as previously described.8 After the procedure, patients were discharged with a 2-month prescription of proton pump inhibitors (PPI). PPI was continued if reflux symptoms persisted or if reflux esophagitis was observed during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy performed 3 to 6 months after POEM.

Follow-up upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed more than 5 years after POEM under conscious sedation. A standardized upper gastrointestinal endoscopy protocol was followed, which included inspection of the esophagus, stomach, first part of the duodenum and gastric fundus. The presence and severity of reflux esophagitis was documented. In cases where Barrett esophagus was suspected, biopsies were taken for histopathological evaluation of intestinal metaplasia and dysplasia. Any other abnormalities during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy were also reported.

Questionnaires

Questionnaires were completed either before or shortly after upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, scheduled more than 5 years after POEM. Follow-up time was calculated from the date of POEM to the completion of the questionnaires. The general questionnaire contained questions on age, gender, body mass index, alcohol use (none; 1–7 units per week; >7 units per week), smoking (never; past; current), reflux symptoms (none; occasionally; often) and PPI use. PPI use was classified as appropriate if patients took the PPI once or twice daily before meals. Other questionnaires assessed reflux symptoms measured by gastroesophageal reflux disease questionnaire (GerdQ) and reflux disease questionnaire (RDQ), achalasia symptom severity measured by Eckardt score and achalasia patient reported outcomes questionnaire (APRO) and quality of life measured by achalasia disease specific quality of life questionnaire (achalasia DSQoL).

The GerdQ is a 6-item validated questionnaire to make the diagnosis of gastroesophageal reflux disease more likely. Four positive and two negative predictors of gastroesophageal reflux disease are scored on a four graded Likert scale (0–3). This results in a total score between 0 and 18 where a score of 8 or higher makes the diagnosis of gastroesophageal reflux disease more likely. The RDQ contains twelve questions about the frequency and severity of reflux symptoms using a five graded Likert scale (0–5). The three subscales are heartburn, regurgitation and dyspepsia and the mean of these subscales gives a total score from 0 to 5. Patients have more severe and frequent reflux symptoms when the RDQ is higher. The Eckardt score comprises four items (dysphagia, regurgitation of undigested food, retrosternal pain and weight loss) scored from zero to three making a total score between 0 and 12. An Eckardt score ≤ 3 without retreatment is defined as treatment success and achalasia symptoms are more severe when total Eckardt score is higher. The APRO score is a recently developed symptom severity score. This validated questionnaire consist of eleven questions including dysphagia, regurgitation, retrosternal pain, nocturnal symptoms, self-reported severity of symptoms, dietary modification and weight change. Scores range from 0–47 with higher scores denoting more severe symptoms. The achalasia-DSQoL is a validated 10-item questionnaire with scores ranging between 10 and 31 where lower scores represents better quality of life.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the rate and severity of reflux esophagitis observed during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy performed at least 5 years after POEM. The severity of reflux esophagitis was graded according to the Los Angeles classification and severe reflux esophagitis was defined as grade C/D.10 Secondary outcomes comprised the outcomes of the questionnaires, as outlined above, and rate of complications associated with reflux esophagitis, including peptic strictures, Barrett esophagus and esophageal adenocarcinoma. Barrett esophagus was defined as endoscopically observed Barrett esophagus lesions >1 cm during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, graded according to the Prague classification and confirmed by histological detection of intestinal metaplasia.11

Data extracted from medical records comprised American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA)-score, type of achalasia confirmed by HRM using the Chicago Classification version 3.0,12 treatment prior to POEM, barium column height after 5 minutes and maximum esophageal dilation on timed barium esophagram, sigmoid esophagus, esophageal diverticula, integrated relaxation pressure and the presence of hiatal hernia on HRM and retreatment after POEM. Procedure related variables included length of myotomy (circular, full-thickness and gastric) and intraprocedural adverse events.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics version 28.0. The Wald method was used to calculate 95% confidence intervals for the proportion of patients with reflux esophagitis observed during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. Median values with interquartile range (IQR) were presented for continuous variables with non-normal distributions. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied to compare continuous variables between patients with and without reflux esophagitis. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Variables with P-values below 0.2 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate logistic analysis. Multivariate logistic regression with backward selection was used to identify predictive factors for reflux esophagitis, while forward selection was applied in sensitivity analysis to determine predictors of severe reflux esophagitis (grade C/D). Statistical significance was defined as two-sided P-values below 0.05 in multivariate analysis.

RESULTS

Patient selection

In total 308 patients underwent POEM at our center before December 2019. Of these, 239 were successfully contacted by telephone and had an outpatient clinic consultation for follow-up. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed in 192 patients. The remaining 47 patients did not undergo upper gastrointestinal endoscopy because of several reasons, such as fear of endoscopy, a preference to postpone endoscopy and older age with associated frailty. Of the patients who underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, 31 did not respond to the informed consent letter. This resulted in 161 patients who were included in the study. Of these, 153 completed all questionnaires (Fig. 1).

Patient characteristics

As shown in Table 1, median age prior to POEM was 45 years and 49.7% of the patients were female (n = 80/161). Achalasia type I was diagnosed in 20.5% (n = 33/161), achalasia type II in 53.4% (n = 86/161) and achalasia type III in 9.9% (n = 16/161). In 16.1% of the patients (n = 26/161) the type of achalasia could not be determined, mostly because the initial manometry was performed long before the first description of the achalasia subtypes. Most common prior treatment was PD (n = 97/161, 60.2%), followed by LHM (n = 43/161, 26.7%) and botulinum toxin injections (n = 15/161, 9.3%).

Median length of circular, full-thickness and gastric myotomy was 3.0, 9.0, and 3.5 cm, respectively. Intraprocedural adverse events occurred in 19 patients (11.8%) of which pneumoperitoneum (6.8%) and mucosal injury (3.7%) were the most common reported intraprocedural adverse events.

Reflux esophagitis

Reflux esophagitis was observed in 67 patients (n = 67/161, 41.6%, 95%-CI 34.0%–49.2%) at a median follow-up time of 100 months of which 28 patients had grade A (n = 28/161, 17.4%), 17 had grade B (n = 17/161, 10.6%), 18 had grade C (n = 18/161, 11.2%), and 4 had grade D (n = 4/161, 2.5%) according to the Los Angeles classification (Fig. 2).

Risk of reflux esophagitis during follow-up gastrointestinal endoscopy in achalasia patients more than five years after peroral endoscopic myotomy. Reflux esophagitis is classified according to the Los Angeles classification (grade A 17.4%, n = 28/161; grade B 10.6%, n = 17/161; grade C 11.2%, n = 18/161 and grade D 2.5%, n = 4/161).

Two regression analyses were performed. The first analysis compared the presence of any degree of reflux esophagitis (grade A-D) with the absence of reflux esophagitis. The second analysis compared the absence of or mild reflux esophagitis (none or grade A/B) with the presence of severe reflux esophagitis (grade C/D). Factors which were associated with presence of any degree of reflux esophagitis in univariate analysis were age before POEM, prior LHM, 5-minutes column height on barium esophagram before POEM, time between POEM and follow-up, alcohol use, retreatment with PD or LHM after POEM, Eckardt score, APRO score, total RDQ score and Achalasia-DSQoL score (Tables 1 and 2). Based on clinical relevance, prior LHM, time between POEM and follow-up, alcohol use, retreatment with PD or LHM after POEM, Eckardt score, APRO score, total RDQ score and Achalasia-DSQoL score were considered clinically significant and used for multivariate analysis. According to multivariate logistic regression, both prior LHM (OR 0.44, 95%-CI 0.20–0.98) and retreatment with PD (OR 0.36, 95%-CI 0.17–0.75) were significantly associated with a reduced odds ratio of reflux esophagitis occurring more than five years after POEM (Table 3).

In total, 13.7% of all patients had severe reflux esophagitis (grade C/D). When considering severe reflux esophagitis as outcome in univariate sensitivity analysis, prior LHM, maximum esophageal dilation on barium esophagram, circular length of myotomy, time between POEM and follow-up, BMI, retreatment with PD or POEM, Eckardt score, APRO score, total RDQ score and achalasia-DSQoL score were potential predictive factors (Supplementary Table S1 and S2). Multivariate logistic regression identified BMI (OR 1.13, 95%-CI 1.04–1.23), retreatment with POEM (OR 44.66, 95%-CI 2.97–672.16) and retreatment with PD (OR 0.12, 95%-CI 0.02–0.93) as predictive factors for observing severe reflux esophagitis in patients with a follow-up more than 5 years after POEM (Supplementary Table S3).

As can be seen in Table 4, 25.5% of the patients (n = 14/55) without reflux esophagitis during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy within one year after POEM developed reflux esophagitis more than five years after POEM. Among these, 10.9% had grade A (n = 6/55), 9.1% had grade B (n = 5/55), and 5.5% had grade C (n = 3/55). Patients with reflux esophagitis grade A during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy within 1 year after POEM still had reflux esophagitis grade A in 29.4% (n = 10/34) more than 5 years after POEM and 32.4% experienced worsening of reflux esophagitis of which 14.7% progressed to grade B (n = 5/34), 14.7% to grade C (n = 5/34), and 2.9% to grade D (n = 1/34). Overall, during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy more than 5 years after POEM, 18.8% of the patients (n = 22/117) had clinically significant worsening of reflux esophagitis and 17.9% (n = 21/117) had a clinically significant improvement (patients with none to grade A or grade A to none were not taken into account). Moreover, the risk of reflux esophagitis does not appear to decrease over time, as severe reflux esophagitis was observed in a higher proportion of patients with follow-up of more than 10 years compared to a follow-up between 5 and 10 years (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Subgroup analysis

The distribution of the grade of reflux esophagitis stratified by subgroups can be seen in Supplementary Table S4.

In total 106 patients did not use PPI or stopped PPI before follow-up upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (n = 106/161, 65.8%). Reflux esophagitis was observed in 50 patients (n = 50/106, 47.2%), including grade A in 23 patients (n = 23/106, 21.7%), grade B in 12 patients (n = 12/106, 11.3%), grade C in 12 patients (n = 12/106, 11.3%), and grade D in 3 patients (n = 3/106, 2.9%). PPI was continued at the time of upper gastrointestinal endoscopy in 55 patients. In this subgroup, reflux esophagitis was observed less frequently (n = 17/55, 30.9%). The severity of reflux esophagitis was grade A in 5 patients (n = 5/55, 9.1%), grade B in 5 patients (n = 5/55, 9.1%), grade C in 6 patients (n = 6/55, 10.9%) and grade D in 1 patients (n = 1/55, 1.8%) (Supplementary Table S4).

Complications of reflux esophagitis

Barrett esophagus was suspected in 12 patients during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy more than 5 years after POEM (7.5%). Pathology confirmed the presence of intestinal metaplasia in five patients (3.1%). Dysplasia was absent in all patients. One patient had been diagnosed with Barrett esophagus prior to POEM, while Barrett esophagus was observed in the other patients more than seven years after POEM. All patients with confirmed Barrett esophagus after POEM had concurrent reflux esophagitis during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. A flow chart of patients with suspected and confirmed Barrett esophagus is presented in Fig. 3 and endoscopy pictures of patients with Barrett esophagus are shown in Fig. 4. More characteristics and outcomes of patients with confirmed Barrett esophagus are provided in Supplementary Table S5. No cases of esophageal adenocarcinoma were observed in any of the patients during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy at a median follow-up of 100 months after POEM.

Peptic strictures were observed in 3 patients (1.9%), all of whom had reflux esophagitis observed during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy prior to the development of peptic strictures. All patients underwent controlled radial expansion (CRE) balloon dilation because of dysphagia of whom two had complete remission of dysphagia and one had partial improvement. Detailed characteristics and outcomes of these patients can be found in Supplementary Table S6.

Clinical outcomes

As shown in Table 2, the median time between POEM and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was 100 months (range 60–156 months) and patients had a median age of 56 years at the time of follow-up. Retreatment was performed in 58 patients (36.0%), including PD in 56 patients (34.8%), LHM in 18 patients (11.2%), and re-POEM in 6 patients (3.7%).

Among patients who completed the questionnaires, 73.3% reported reflux symptoms of which 42.9% experienced symptoms occasionally and 30.4% reported often symptoms (Table 2). Reflux symptoms were most frequently experienced at night, or during day and night, while the minority of patients reported symptoms only during the day. Supplementary Fig. S2 illustrates the timing of reflux symptom occurrences, as well as outcomes related to reflux esophagitis and PPI use among patients with and without reflux symptoms. The median Eckardt score was 4 and the median APRO score was 10. Reflux symptoms, measured by GerdQ and RDQ, demonstrated median scores of 8 and 1.1 respectively. The median quality of life score, measured by Achalasia-DSQoL, was 19 (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that reflux esophagitis remains a common long-term finding in achalasia patients more than 5 years after POEM, with 13.7% of patients having severe reflux esophagitis. Furthermore, non-dysplastic Barrett esophagus and peptic strictures were observed in 3.1% and 1.9% of patients respectively, although no cases of dysplastic lesions or esophageal adenocarcinoma were identified. These findings indicate that reflux esophagitis remains a concern in the long term after POEM, highlighting the need for sustained attention, endoscopic monitoring and appropriate management over time.

In a previous study examining the risk of reflux esophagitis within one year after POEM, we observed that 52.0% of the patients had reflux esophagitis during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy of which 22.0% was classified as severe (grade C/D).8 A large multicenter cohort study in Japan reported that reflux esophagitis was present in 64.9% of the patients over a follow-up period ranging from one to five years after POEM and 11.6% of these cases were graded as severe (grade C/D).13 Another retrospective study evaluated the prevalence of reflux esophagitis in 119 patients 5 years after POEM and reported reflux esophagitis in 35.3% of the patients while 29.4% used PPI.14 Furthermore, the retrospective study by Vackova et al (2025) reported that reflux esophagitis was present in 20.2% of the patients after a 6-year follow-up and this was statistically significantly lower compared to the 40.4% prevalence after 3 months. However, compatibility between follow-up time points may be limited as the number of patients differed substantially (473 vs. 99 patients) and the proportion of patients using PPI increased over time (39.2% vs. 55.0%).15 A multicenter randomized controlled trial comparing POEM and LHM also reported a decrease in the risk of reflux esophagitis over time. Three months after POEM, 57% of patients had reflux esophagitis (grade A-D), decreasing to 41% at 5-year follow-up. However, the proportion of patients using daily PPI use increased from 23% at 3-month follow-up to 41% at 5-year follow-up.16 The results of the current prospective study demonstrated that the risk of reflux esophagitis in achalasia patients remained high at 41.6% more than five years after POEM. Of the patients with reflux esophagitis, one third was classified as severe (grade C/D). Similar to our earlier findings, prior LHM was associated with a lower risk of developing reflux esophagitis more than five years after POEM, likely as a result of the fundoplication performed during LHM earlier. Furthermore, retreatment with PD appeared to reduce the risk of reflux esophagitis in the long term after POEM. This may be explained by recurrent tightening of the lower esophageal sphincter or by fibrotic remodeling after POEM, both of which may prevent gastroesophageal reflux but can compromise esophageal emptying. Patients undergoing PD after POEM may therefore represent a population less prone to reflux. Besides, for the development of severe reflux esophagitis (grade C/D), higher BMI and re-POEM were significant risk factors, whereas retreatment with PD was associated with a reduced risk. Although re-POEM was associated with a higher risk of severe reflux esophagitis, the wide confidence interval, which might be related to the relatively low number of patients with severe reflux esophagitis, indicates substantial uncertainty regarding the precise magnitude of the effect. Interestingly, reflux esophagitis observed within one year after POEM did not seem to reflect the presence of reflux esophagitis more than five years later and reflux symptoms were not correlated with the presence of reflux esophagitis at long-term follow-up, suggesting that reflux esophagitis within one year after POEM may not be a reliable indicator of long-term risk and that endoscopic monitoring is warranted at a later stage after POEM as well. The discrepancy between reported symptoms and the presence of reflux esophagitis during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy might be explained by PPI use, non-acid reflux, decreased esophageal clearance or esophageal hypersensitivity.

The risk of reflux esophagitis after POEM is high both at the short term and long term, but is generally responsive to PPI therapy.17 In cases where PPI is ineffective in alleviating reflux symptoms, other potential causes must be further explored, including non-acid reflux (as PPIs reduce gastric acidity but do not prevent the backflow of gastric contents), or other underlying factors such as stasis of food or esophageal hypersensitivity.6,18 Management should be tailored according to the underlying cause. In addition to general lifestyle advices, patients with non-acidic reflux are advised to avoid eating before bedtime and to sleep with the head of the bed slightly elevated.19 Furthermore, a timed barium esophagram can be performed to exclude stasis of food, while a neuromodulator may be considered in patients with esophageal hypersensitivity.20

As fundoplication during LHM is performed to minimize the risk of gastroesophageal reflux after surgery, recent studies have focused on the potential role of a fundoplication performed during POEM (POEM-F). The first reported POEM-F is performed in 2019 by Inoue et al (2019). In POEM-F, the peritoneum cavity is entered after myotomy followed by the creation of a fundoplication by anchoring the stomach to the distal end of the submucosal tunnel.21 Since this initial publication, several reports have described the efficacy and safety of POEM-F. A recent study which reported long-term outcomes of POEM-F compared to POEM without fundoplication concluded that gastroesophageal reflux was significantly less three years after POEM-F compared to POEM without fundoplication. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis, which included seven studies, reported a pooled clinical success rate of 86.2% in alleviating post-POEM gastroesophageal reflux.22 It is important to note that this clinical success rate comprises various outcomes across studies.22 Further high-quality randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these findings.

One of the main concerns regarding complications of longstanding reflux esophagitis after POEM is the potential development of peptic strictures, Barrett esophagus and esophageal adenocarcinoma. Barrett esophagus is characterized by metaplastic replacement of normal esophageal squamous epithelium with columnar cells and is strongly associated with gastroesophageal reflux. It is considered a precursor lesion to esophageal adenocarcinoma, however, the majority of lesions will not progress to esophageal adenocarcinoma.23 Non-dysplastic Barrett esophagus carries a relatively low risk of progression. In contrast, the risk is considerably higher in the presence of dysplasia. The timing of progression from Barrett esophagus to esophageal adenocarcinoma varies widely and is often difficult to ascertain given the considerable delay between the onset of Barrett esophagus and its diagnosis.10 Our study found that the risk of Barrett esophagus confirmed by pathologic observation of intestinal metaplasia was 3.1%. With only five patients presenting with non-dysplastic short length Barrett esophagus and no identified dysplastic lesions, we believe that the risk of progression to esophageal adenocarcinoma is not a major concern five to ten years after POEM.

A systematic review and meta-analysis including 2342 patients described three peptic strictures (1%) and one Barrett esophagus (0.3%) following POEM.24 However, the review did not include a prospective cohort study which reported five peptic strictures (0.8%) and five short segment Barrett esophagus without dysplasia (0.8%) between 2 and 6 years after POEM.25 Furthermore, one case report highlighted the development of Barrett esophagus with esophageal adenocarcinoma in an achalasia patient 4 years after POEM.26 Our study showed a somewhat higher risk of peptic strictures of 1.9% and a considerable higher risk of Barrett esophagus of 3.1% compared to aforementioned studies. The three observed peptic strictures were of clinical relevance as all patients presented with dysphagia and underwent CRE dilation. Among the patients with Barrett esophagus, only short-segment Barrett esophagus were observed without dysplasia and one patient had pre-existing Barrett esophagus prior to POEM. A reason for the difference in percentages between our study and previous studies likely results from our longer follow-up after POEM and the higher proportion of patients with (severe) reflux esophagitis in our study (42% total of which 33% severe in the current study vs. 50% total of which 7% severe25 vs. 4–33% total24), as well as a different patient population.

Follow-up gastrointestinal endoscopy for achalasia patients who have undergone POEM is recommended between 3 and 6 months post-POEM after discontinuing PPI for at least 2–4 weeks.27 Given the lack of long-term data on the development of reflux esophagitis and its complications after POEM, no specific recommendations and standardized intervals for long-term follow-up gastrointestinal endoscopy have been established. Based on the results of this study, we believe that upper gastrointestinal endoscopy remains an important screening method in the long term after POEM as well. Screening may be less frequent or more selective in patients with prior LHM, retreatment with PD after POEM, or with appropriate PPI use regardless of the presence of reflux esophagitis, for example because of reflux symptoms or gastric ulcer prevention. However, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy remains relevant for other achalasia patients, particularly considering that many patients undergoing POEM are relatively young, the absence of early reflux esophagitis does not reliable reflect long-term risk, reflux symptoms do not correlate with the presence of reflux esophagitis and the lack of other adequate diagnostic tests to detect reflux esophagitis. Supplementary Fig. S3 outlines a proposed short-term and long-term follow-up approach for gastroesophageal reflux in achalasia patients after POEM. The findings in this study support the need for further studies evaluating optimal surveillance intervals.

This is the first long-term prospective study on the prevalence and risk factors of reflux esophagitis and its long-term complications in achalasia patients with a follow-up of more than 5 years. Since the introduction of POEM, the POEM technique has evolved over the past several years, a factor that was not accounted for in the study. Nowadays, the length of the myotomy is shorter for patients with achalasia type 1 and 2, which may influence the risk of reflux esophagitis. The length of gastric myotomy is currently limited to two centimeters or less to reduce the risk of reflux esophagitis. Furthermore, the role of selective versus full-thickness myotomy and anterior versus posterior myotomy in the risk of developing reflux esophagitis is not yet fully understood. This study is performed in one country, potentially limiting generalizability, as rates of reflux esophagitis may vary across populations. Comparative data from other countries would be valuable to improve generalizability. Patients were included only if they underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy more than 5 years after POEM which may have resulted in selection bias. However, we believe that the risk of selection bias is limited as patients included in the analysis and those excluded did not differ substantially in terms of baseline characteristics, symptoms, procedure related outcomes or the presence of reflux esophagitis within one year after POEM. The results presented in this study are based on upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. Post-POEM follow-up in our center does not include routine 24-hours pH-impedance testing because acid exposure time may not reliably reflect reflux gastroesophageal reflux disease in achalasia patients. In these patients, elevated acid exposure time is often attributable to stasis of acid food or acid fermentation rather than true reflux.6 A limitation of the study is that PPI was not stopped in every patient prior to upper gastrointestinal endoscopy because of several reasons, such as too much reflux symptoms when stopping PPI and the use of co-medication that required PPI use. However, we expect that when PPIs were stopped in each patient, this only would have led to an even higher risk of reflux esophagitis. Furthermore, adherence to PPI therapy was often suboptimal, as patients often took PPIs only when needed or during meals, stressing the need for improved PPI management. Another limitation is the limited number of events per variable in the multivariate logistic regression for severe reflux esophagitis, potentially increasing the risk of overfitting. Hence, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger cohorts.

It is clear that the risk of reflux esophagitis is high after POEM on both short and long term follow-up. Complications of reflux esophagitis including non-dysplastic Barrett esophagus and peptic strictures are observed in several patients and could potentially be prevented with adequate management of reflux esophagitis. Currently, a standardized protocol for screening for reflux esophagitis and its complications, as well as recommendations for adequate long-term PPI use after POEM, are warranted.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the risk of reflux esophagitis remains high in achalasia patients more than 5 years after POEM. Almost 33% of the reflux esophagitis is classified as severe which might lead to complications such as Barrett esophagus and peptic strictures. These results highlight the importance of ongoing monitoring and optimizing the management of reflux esophagitis after POEM, as well as further technical refinements aimed at reducing the risk of gastroesophageal reflux.
