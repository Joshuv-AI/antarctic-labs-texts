Mitigating future pandemics: the need for socio-behavioural data collection systems and proactive community engagement
Authors: Keenan Duggal, Nina Gobat, Thomas Moran, Thomas Abraham, Daniel Romer, Jim Duggan, Vicky Houssiere, Olivia Tulloch, Wenqing Zhang, Jean-Michel Heraud, Kai Von Harbou
Published: 2026

Globalisation, urbanisation and eroding boundaries between humans and wildlife habitats have collectively contributed to an environment that is increasingly susceptible to the emergence and rapid transmission of respiratory pathogens. Consequently, the frequency of global pandemics has accelerated in recent decades. The COVID-19 pandemic and recent isolated cases of H5N1 influenza (bird influenza) detected in pigs, cows and humans underscore the dynamic, often unpredictable nature of pathogens, and serve as reminders that we must remain alert to the risk of future pandemics.1

In this context, strengthening pandemic control strategies has become an urgent priority. To facilitate this process, the WHO has developed the Health Emergency Preparedness, Response and Resilience (HEPR) framework, which consolidates over 300 recommendations from previous responses to public health emergencies such as COVID-19. This framework provides a comprehensive roadmap for managing emergent health threats.2

While acknowledging the importance of every pillar of HEPR, this commentary focuses on advocating for the proactive adoption and implementation of one critical dimension—Community Protection. This dimension emphasises achieving local-level impact through community-centred approaches to health emergency prevention, preparedness, response and recovery.3 A key component of Community Protection is Risk Communication and Community Engagement (RCCE), which is essential for fostering trust and public uptake of recommended disease control measures.4 Effective RCCE involves integrating data from diverse community sources, maintaining dynamic bidirectional communication streams and actively involving communities in decision-making processes2 3 (figure 1).

Conceptual framework for proactive risk communication and community engagement

While the predominant approach today is to initiate RCCE reactively following the emergence of a public health threat, this approach is suboptimal compared with that of proactive RCCE leveraging pre-existing data systems for the following three reasons:

Firstly, having baseline data about at-risk or affected communities can inform public health agencies on how to most effectively engage with, build trust with and prepare communities prior to a pandemic. Proactive trust-building and tailored communication are crucial, as empirical data suggests that countries with greater interpersonal and governmental trust experienced greater public adoption of public health measures and reduced infection rates during the COVID-19 pandemic.5

Secondly, public knowledge, attitudes and behaviours evolve dynamically during (ie, pandemic fatigue) and after a pandemic.6 These rapid changes are best captured through consistent and repeated waves of data collection, which allow public health agencies to perform the challenging tasks of assessing the impact of their interventions and adapting their strategies in real time.7

Third, proactively established, community-integrated data systems provide up-to-date information that reduces uncertainty for public health organisations, particularly during the early ‘acceleration’ stage of a pandemic when it is especially important to try to control transmission.8 Just as serological data informs transmission models and guides the selection of optimal control measures, social and behavioural data can be used to measure public willingness to comply with different interventions. By integrating these insights, public health agencies can calibrate their responses, tailor communications and foster desired behavioural changes more effectively.

The significant potential benefits of a proactive and dynamic public health communication strategy—particularly one that prioritises community engagement—underscores the urgent need for enhanced global efforts to involve representation from civil society in emergency management governance processes and to establish robust data systems for understanding and collaborating with local communities. Notable examples of such efforts include the National Foundation for Infectious Diseases’s annual survey on ‘Attitudes and Behaviors about Influenza, COVID-19, Respiratory Syncytial Virus, and Pneumococcal Disease’, which provides valuable insights into public attitudes and behaviours.9 Similarly, community-driven initiatives—such as that to map informal urban settlements to generate accurate demographic information—illustrate how grassroots efforts can support public health organisations with actionable information.10 These initiatives ultimately demonstrate the importance and value of integrating community perspectives into pandemic preparedness and response strategies.

In this perspective, we propose that one novel, sustainable, cost-effective and scalable solution to improve proactive data collection is to incorporate a small set of rotating questions into existing national-level health surveys (eg, the NHIS in the USA, HSE in England and the DHS globally). These questions should aim to capture critical social and behavioural variables such as (1) community-specific risk perceptions and knowledge related to respiratory pathogens (eg, influenza), (2) general acceptance and tolerance levels for common pandemic control measures such as masking and social distancing, and (3) trust in health authorities and relevant community leaders. While the implementation of this strategy would require non-negligible financial and labour investments as well as careful execution to guarantee functional, secure data systems and standardisation across different geographic and cultural settings, the integration of socio-behavioural variables into routine data collection would provide public health organisations with key insights needed to design proactive, community-tailored communication strategies. And, by embedding these practices into existing frameworks, public health systems worldwide would be better equipped to anticipate and address the challenges of future pandemics, and therefore promote resilience and equity in health emergency management.
