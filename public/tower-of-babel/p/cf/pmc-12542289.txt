Successful management of neonatal tetanus with autonomic manifestations at a referral hospital in Nigeria: a case report
Authors: Chigozie K Onyeje, Blessing Tochukwu Onyeje
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal tetanus remains a significant cause of preventable neonatal death in developing countries like Nigeria. With case fatality rates approaching 100% in resource-poor settings, improved antenatal tetanus vaccination and clean delivery practices can drastically reduce mortality. Each case of neonatal tetanus highlights major gaps in public health systems, especially where umbilical sepsis and maternal non-vaccination are common. This report presents a critical case of neonatal tetanus in an infant born to an unvaccinated mother managed successfully despite limited resources.Case presentationA 9-day-old term Igbo male presented with high-grade fever, feeding difficulty and intermittent body stiffening triggered by sound or touch, beginning on the seventh day of life. Examination revealed respiratory distress, fever, tachycardia, and multiple muscle spasms. Neonatal tetanus with autonomic dysfunction was diagnosed. He was treated with intravenous diazepam, phenobarbitone, chlorpromazine, and magnesium sulfate infusion. The patient showed marked improvement and was discharged after 34 days of hospitalization.ConclusionThis case underscores the importance of maternal tetanus immunization and clean delivery practices in preventing neonatal tetanus, while demonstrating that timely intervention can lead to favorable outcomes even in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Neonatal tetanus, Autonomic, Magnesium sulphate, Case report

Introduction

Neonatal tetanus is a generalized form of tetanus that affects infants under 28 days old [1]. It is an acute and potentially fatal disease caused by spores of the Gram-positive obligate anaerobic bacterium Clostridium tetani [2, 3]; symptoms are mediated through a potent neurotoxin (tetanospasmin) [4] and usually manifests with compromised suckling in a newborn who previously had good sucking reflex, pronounced irritability, with or without fever, generalized muscle rigidity and painful muscle spasms [5].

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines a confirmed case of neonatal tetanus as a neonate with all three of the following symptomatology: normal feeding and crying over the first 2 days of life; onset of illness between 3 and 28 days of life; and inability to suckle (trismus), followed by stiffening (generalized muscle rigidity) and/or convulsions (muscle spasms) [6].

In 2018, it was reported that approximately 25,000 newborns died from neonatal tetanus globally, with the African Region accounting for majority of these mortalities [7].

Risk factors for neonatal tetanus includes umbilical stump sepsis, use of unsterile equipment to cut the cord, home delivery, application of cow dung and other unsafe traditional practices [8–10]; in addition to other influencing factors such as poverty, lack/poor of maternal education, poor socioeconomic status and adverse environmental circumstances and poor maternal vaccination [11, 12].

The portal of entry for most cases of neonatal tetanus is the devitalized umbilical stump [12, 13]. Other documented sources of infection are ear piercing and infected circumcision sites; and traditional uvulectomy [13, 14]. However, in approximately 20% of tetanus cases generally there is no obvious portal of entry [15].

Poor prognosis for survival is associated with age at presentation less than 7 days [15]; presence of autonomic nervous system dysfunction (such as labile hypertension, unstable heart rate and a variety of arrhythmias); and spasm of respiratory muscles leading to respiratory failure [6, 16, 17].

The diagnosis is clinical and therefore requires a high index of suspicion and adequate knowledge on how tetanus presents by the clinician [3]. Although neonatal meningitis or sepsis may present with some features similar to neonatal tetanus, there is no lock jaw in neonatal meningitis and consciousness is spared in neonatal tetanus [6].

Case management of neonatal tetanus encompasses: eliminating the causative Clostridium tetani, neutralizing circulating toxins, controlling muscle spasms, providing supportive care including respiratory support, managing fluids and nutrition, minimizing patient handling, and preventing recurrence through vaccination. For cases complicated by dysautonomia, characterized by episodes of profound hypertension and tachycardia to bradycardia, arrhythmias, and hypotension, the management of these cardiovascular aberrations includes the administration of combined α and β adrenergic blockers, such as labetolol [18]. Magnesium sulphate has also been successfully used to reduce autonomic disturbance and muscle spasms [19], as magnesium is a presynaptic neuromuscular blocker, blocks catecholamine release from nerves, and reduces the responsiveness of receptors to catecholamines.

Neonatal tetanus is highly preventable through multipronged approaches like vaccination of women of reproductive age with tetanus toxoid-containing vaccines [20]; safe clean delivery services [21] and targeted health education to address cultural practices that increase neonatal tetanus risk [22].

Although, neonatal tetanus has become rare in many developed countries, it is still commonly encountered in underdeveloped regions [23, 24]. The continued high mortality from neonatal tetanus in these resource-limited countries is a great setback to the WHO’s maternal and neonatal tetanus elimination (MNTE) initiative [6, 25].

This case is notable for the rare survival of neonatal tetanus complicated by dysautonomia in a resource-limited setting; an outcome seldom reported in the literature. Neonatal tetanus, particularly when accompanied by autonomic dysfunction such as arrhythmias, temperature instability and labile blood pressure, significantly increases mortality rates [17], especially where access to intensive care is limited. This infant’s survival highlights the potential for favourable outcomes through timely recognition, adaptable care, and effective use of available treatments. This report provides valuable clinical insights into the management of severe tetanus with autonomic involvement in low-resource environments and may inform future care protocols in similar contexts.

Case description

A 9-day-old male neonate (birth weight 2.6 kg), was presented by the parents to Nnamdi Azikiwe University Teaching Hospital, Nnewi with classical signs of neonatal tetanus. He was delivered via spontaneous vaginal delivery at a maternity home, cried at birth, breastfed well, and was discharged in good condition after one day. On day seven, he developed irritability, fever, lip tightening, feeding difficulty, and repeated stiffening of the body especially when touched. By day nine, he was unable to breastfeed, had laboured breathing and became significantly less active. After unsuccessful treatment at a local hospital with unspecified intramuscular injections, the family was referred to our facility where he was admitted and managed in the neonatal ward. The mother had late antenatal booking (at 6 months of pregnancy), received no tetanus toxoid during pregnancy, and applied toothpaste to the umbilical stump for cord care. These factors, along with inadequate immunization history, point to poor maternal immunization and unhygienic cord care as likely contributors to the infection.

Clinical examination

At presentation, the baby was febrile with axillary temperature of 39.7 °C (Tmax = 41.3 °C), moderately dehydrated, tachypneic with respiratory rate of 64 breaths per minute (RRmax=96 breaths/minute) and tachycardic with a heart rate of 174 beats per minute (HRmax= 194 beats/minute). Blood pressure could not be measured due to unavailability of an appropriate cuff at that time. He had lockjaw, facial and generalized muscle rigidity. The baby also demonstrated increased abdominal muscle rigidity and generalized spasms when touched or in response to loud sound. The frequency of generalized spasms was uncountable initially. The umbilical stamp was already detached, there was no sign of periumbilical infection and his skin was not mottled. A diagnosis of Neonatal Tetanus with Autonomic Dysfunction was made at this point with a differential diagnosis of Neonatal Meningitis.

Laboratory investigations

The complete blood count (Table 1) showed markedly elevated total white cell count of 23,150 cells/µL with predominance of neutrophils, a normal haemogram for his age and a blood smear revealed no malaria parasites. Biochemical results revealed slight azotemia (with estimated GFR of 10.6mls/min/1.73m2) but normal electrolytes. Given the unstable clinical state, a lumbar puncture for cerebral spinal fluid analysis was not done.

He was discharged after 34days of in-patient care, in a stable state on oral methocarbamol at 15 mg/kg given once daily. On review 2weeks later, he was well, with normal tone and reflexes, and was feeding well with no reoccurrence of spasms. Oral methocarbamol was stopped and the infant is now growing well with no neurologic sequelae. He was vaccinated as per the immunization schedule for infants and mother adequately counseled to get herself vaccinated for tetanus as per World Health Organization (WHO) recommendation.

Discussion

Resource-constrained settings continue to report high case fatality from neonatal tetanus, due to lack of equipped intensive care facilities and sophisticated management modalities like neuromuscular blockade and invasive ventilation [6, 23, 24]. This high mortality has been reported especially in those with symptom onset within the first week of life and in our case symptoms started on the seventh day of life [12, 16, 26]. Our patient presented with features of autonomic dysfunction (labile heart rate, tachypnea and high temperatures), which has been associated with high mortality in the presence of severe neonatal tetanus [5, 24, 26]. In settings with adequate resources, when the use of benzodiazepines alone fails to control spasms in neonatal tetanus, neuromuscular blockage and ventilation are employed with good outcomes [9]. By controlling spasms of the respiratory muscles, these interventions reduce the risk of mortality from respiratory muscle failure, a leading cause of death among newborns affected by tetanus. However, in the presented case, we has no access to mechanical ventilation, therefore, we used magnesium sulfate infusion alongside diazepam and phenobarbitone and achieved a positive outcome. Magnesium sulfate induces muscular relaxation, vasodilation and a reduction in heart rate, potentially helping to mitigate the autonomic dysfunction that occurs in neonatal tetanus [27–29].

Other components of case management include eliminating Clostridium tetani using broad-spectrum antibiotics, debridement, and disinfecting the umbilical cord with hydrogen peroxide and surgical spirit [5]. While penicillin has been used in the past as first-line antibiotics, metronidazole is the current drug of choice and is associated with decreased fatality rates [30, 31]. Metronidazole was used in this index case.

Administration of intramuscular human antitetanus immunoglobulin (HTIg) or equine antitetanus serum (ATS) is the established strategy for neutralizing circulating tetanus toxin [29, 30]. Since the damage caused by tetanospasmin that has bound to the nervous system is irreversible, treatment focuses mainly on neutralizing the unbound circulating toxin. Our patient was given ATS.

Neonatal tetanus is a highly vaccine-preventable disease; giving pregnant women at least two doses of tetanus toxoid vaccine has been shown to reduce neonatal tetanus mortality by 94% [6, 16]. Protection from tetanus is obtained from the trans-placental transfer of tetanus antibodies from the mother to the fetus. In our case, the mother had zero dose of tetanus toxoid during her pregnancy, leaving her newborn without protective antibodies and at high risk for developing tetanus. Also, given that the delivery happened at a maternity with a trained health worker, we believe the infant most likely got exposed to C. tetani during the process of cord care at home. The mother applied toothpaste to the umbilical stump, which may have provided the enabling environment for Clostridium tetani to thrive.

Additionally, even after complete recovery from tetanus infection, the infant does not develop lasting immunity against the disease, therefore, primary series of tetanus immunization are recommended, and this was started at 6 weeks in the index case.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that neonatal tetanus with autonomic dysfunction can survive in resource-limited settings without access to advanced neonatal intensive care. The successful use of magnesium sulfate alongside diazepam and phenobarbitone demonstrates a viable alternative for managing spasms and autonomic instability when mechanical ventilation is unavailable. Effective supportive care, timely use of antitetanus serum, and appropriate antibiotics like metronidazole, also played critical roles in recovery. This case reinforces the critical importance of maternal tetanus immunization and safe cord care practices in preventing this avoidable yet often fatal disease.
