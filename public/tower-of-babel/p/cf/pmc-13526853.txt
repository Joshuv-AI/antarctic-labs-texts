Symptom-linked disruption of the vaginal Lactobacillus-IL-2/IFN-γ axis in genitourinary syndrome of menopause related symptoms
Authors: Heeyon Kim, Juli Choi, Emmanuel Hitayezu, Leechung Chang, Yun Soo Chung, Jin Kyung Baek, Bo Hyon Yun, Kwang Hyun Cha, Seok Kyo Seo, Ho-Keun Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) is a common disease; however, the underlying mechanisms are unclear. This study aimed to identify the vaginal cytokine and microbiome signatures associated with symptom severity and explore the immune–microbial interactions underlying GSM. Vaginal fluid samples were collected from 66 women (42 controls and 24 patients with GSM). Cytokine levels were measured using a 25-cytokine multiplex assay. Microbiome profiles were obtained by 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing of the same samples; 61 samples passed quality control (37 controls, 24 patients). Most cytokines were downregulated in patients compared with controls, with interleukin (IL)-2, interferon (IFN)-γ, tumor necrosis factor-α, IL-4, and IL-10 showing the greatest reductions. Cytokine levels were negatively correlated with symptom severity, independent of menopausal status. Among post-menopausal women, IL-2 and IFN-γ levels were significantly lower in those with severe symptoms. Microbiome analysis revealed reduced alpha diversity and depletion of the Lactobacillus species, especially L. crispatus and L. iners, with increased levels of intestinal bacteria such as Enterococcus and Bacteroides. Correlation analysis demonstrated a strong positive association between L. crispatus and IL-2 (r = 0.56, p < 0.05) and a weaker trend with IFN-γ. Vaginal cytokine and microbiome profiles were altered in proportion to symptom severity, rather than menopausal status. The IL-2–Lactobacillus crispatus axis appears to help maintain the mucosal immune balance, suggesting its potential diagnostic and therapeutic value for restoring immune–microbial homeostasis in patients with vaginal atrophy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-55963-2.

Introduction

Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) is a prevalent and chronic condition. It affects approximately 27 to 84% of post-menopausal women, as well as younger individuals undergoing anti-hormone therapy or cancer treatments that induce premature ovarian insufficiency1–3. The symptoms of GSM include vaginal dryness, irritation/burning/itching sensation, dyspareunia, dysuria, and post-coital bleeding, which substantially reduce the quality of life of middle-aged women and lead to socioeconomic burden 4.

The primary etiology of GSM is estrogen deficiency, which leads to atrophic changes; however, differences in symptom severity cannot be fully explained by hormone levels alone5. Increasing evidence supports a hormone–microbiome–cytokine axis, such as vaginal epithelium thinning, decreased glycogen levels, reduced Lactobacillus genus dominance, a higher pH, and shifts in the local immune tone6–8. However, the specific host–microbe–immune system interactions that connect bacterial species and cytokines to patient-reported symptoms remain unclear.

Vaginal environment is a complex ecosystem influenced by the host immune system, with numerous cytokines that modulate inflammatory and anti-inflammatory pathways9. Previous studies have revealed changes in the vaginal microenvironment associated with bacterial vaginosis and sexually transmitted infections, describing shifts in regulatory mediators such as Th1, Th2, and Th1710,11. However, symptom-graded changes in cytokine levels in patients with GSM have rarely been examined.

The vaginal microbiome plays a crucial role in maintaining urogenital health. However, previous studies have classified the vaginal microbiome as Lactobacillus-dominant versus non-Lactobacillus states. Although associations of specific species such as Lactobacillus crispatus (L. crispatus) and Lactobacillus iners (L. iners) with features of local inflammatory tone have been described, few studies have examined the association between cytokine levels, particularly IL-2 and IFN-γ levels and the severity of patient-reported symptoms12,13.

Therefore, we integrated multiplex cytokine profiling with 16S rRNA sequencing of vaginal fluid from controls and symptomatic patients and performed a prespecified symptom severity analysis. We hypothesized that symptomatic GSM would be characterized by coordinated reductions in key cytokines; that symptom severity would track community restructuring rather than gross diversity alone; and that specific taxonomic-cytokine relationships, specifically involving the Lactobacillus species, would correlate with a lower symptom burden.

Methods

Study population

This prospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Severance Hospital, Yonsei University College of Medicine (Seoul, South Korea; approval number: #4–2023-1247). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

A total of 66 women aged 20–71 years who visited the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology at Severance Hospital between November 2023 and November 2024 were recruited for this study (Table 1 and Fig. 1). Participants in the symptomatic group were included if they reported GSM-related vulvovaginal symptoms, including vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, irritation/burning/itching sensation, dysuria, or bleeding with sexual activity, as assessed by the composite symptom severity score. Exclusion criteria included active vaginal infection, recent antibiotic use within 4 weeks, current vaginal estrogen or probiotic treatment, pregnancy, and inadequate sample quality. Collection of clinical information and division by severity

Clinical and demographic data were obtained using structured questionnaires and medical records. Patient-reported genitourinary symptoms were assessed across five domains: vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, irritation/burning/itching sensation, dysuria, and bleeding with sexual activity, and rated on a 4-point ordinal scale (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe), using a severity scale adapted from the Nappi RE scale14. A composite symptom severity score was calculated as the sum of scores across all five domains (range: 0–15). Based on this composite score, patients were categorized as follows: control group (asymptomatic, score = 0); Group 1 = mild symptom group (total score ≤ 7); Group 2 = severe symptom group (total score ≥ 8).

Purification of vaginal fluid and storage

To minimize pre-analytical variation, the participants were instructed to avoid intravaginal medications, probiotics, and systemic antibiotics for 24–72 h prior to sampling. During a speculum examination without lubricant, 10 mL of sterile 0.9% saline was instilled into the vaginal vault, gently irrigated along the lateral walls and posterior fornix for 30–60 s, then recovered with the same syringe. Vaginal fluid was collected in BD Vacutainer collection tubes and immediately centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4℃ for 10 min to separate the epidermal pellet for 16 s rRNA sequencing and clear vaginal fluid for the cytokine multiplex. All samples were frozen at -80℃ until use.

Measurement of vaginal cytokine levels using multiple immunoassays

Cytokines present in the vaginal fluid were quantified using two multiplex bead-based immunoassay panels from BioLegend: the LEGENDplex™ Human T helper (Th) Cytokine Panel V02 (741028), which measures 12 cytokines (IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-9, IL-10, IL-13, IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22, interferon [IFN]-γ, and tumor necrosis factor [TNF]-α), and the LEGENDplex™ Human Cytokine Panel 2 V02 (741378), which detects 13 cytokines (thymic stromal lymphopoietin, IL-1α, IL-1β, granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor [GM-CSF], IFN-α2, IL-23, IL-12p40, IL-12p70, IL-15, IL-18, IL-11, IL-27, and IL-33). All assays were performed in duplicate. Vaginal fluid samples were thawed on ice, incubated with capture beads, and stained with detection antibodies at room temperature according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A minimum of 300 beads per analyte were obtained for each sample. Data were analyzed using the LEGENDplex™ Data Analysis Software Suite, a free cloud-based tool (https://legendplex.qognit.com/user/login?next=home). Cytokine (IL-1α, IL-11, IL-12p40, IL-12p70, IL-15, IL-23) concentrations falling below the detection limit were excluded from this study. Data were normalized to those of the control and transformed into log scores.

16S Microbial genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from vaginal swab samples using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA concentration was measured using the ND-1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE) and diluted to 1 ng/µL before amplification. The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341F and 805R and Platinum SuperFi II DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania). Polymerase chain reaction amplification was performed under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s; 35 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s; annealing at 58 °C for 10 s; and extension at 72 °C for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Polymerase chain reaction products were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, Danvers, MA, USA) and quantified using the Qubit dsDNA High-Sensitivity Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Next, the purified amplicons were normalized to 4 nM and pooled in a 2 mL tube for downstream analysis. The pooled library was denatured with 0.2 N NaOH, diluted to 10 pM using a pre-chilled HT1 buffer from the Nextera XT Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and spiked with 10% PhiX library (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed on the MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using the MiSeq Reagent v3 (600 cycles) kit. Raw reads were demultiplexed, quality checked and assembled using the QIIME2 pipeline (version 2024.5)15. The DADA2 workflow was applied for quality trimming, denoising, and removing chimeric polymerase chain reaction artifacts16. Taxonomic classification of the resulting exact amplicon sequence variants was performed using the Greengenes 2 reference database (version 2022.10) with the QIIME2 q2-greengenes plugin17. Samples with sequencing read counts below 6,000 were excluded from the downstream analyses to ensure data quality and reliable taxonomic assignments.

Microbiome data analysis

Analysis of the vaginal microbial community was performed using R software (version 4.5.1) in RStudio. Alpha diversity (Shannon index and the observed ASVs) was computed using the vegan package (v2.6–6), and statistical comparisons between groups were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with the Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Beta diversity was estimated with the phyloseq package and visualized as non-metric multidimensional scaling using the “metaMDS” function in the vegan package. Genus-level relative abundances were visualized as heat maps using the pheatmap package. The taxonomic composition of each severity group was summarized as a pie chart plotted using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.6.1; GraphPad Software, CA, USA), depicting the relative abundance of the most dominant bacterial genera using data exported from the MicrobiomeAnalyst platform18. Additionally, MicrobiomeAnalyst was used to identify differential taxa associated with symptom severity using random forest classification, and discriminant taxa were ranked according to the mean decrease accuracy values.

Statistical analysis

All the cytokine data used in this study were normalized and transformed into log scores. Cytokine data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software v10.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) (https://www.graphpad.com/). Differences in cytokine levels between the control and patient groups were calculated using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test.

This severity stratification was not specified a priori and represents a secondary, exploratory analysis. Cytokine levels were compared using Mann–Whitney U tests (appropriate for small samples, n < 30). Three pairwise comparisons were performed: Control vs. Group1, Control vs. Group2, and Group1 vs. Group2 or Post_0 vs. Post_1, Post_1 vs. Post_2, Post_1 vs. Post_2. P-values were corrected for multiple testing using Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) FDR correction (q < 0.10 threshold). Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated as the primary measure of biological significance. Findings were classified as ‘significant’ if BH-adjusted q < 0.10 or ‘exploratory’ if raw p < 0.05 but q ≥ 0.10. Post-hoc power analysis was conducted to quantify achieved power and Type II error risk (α = 0.05, two-tailed). The effect of symptom severity was analyzed using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparison test. The result of analysis is summarized in Supplementary table S1, 2. Correlation analysis between cytokines and the microbiome was also performed.

Cytokine heatmap graphs were generated using Rstudio (https://cran.rstudio.com/) (R version 4.3.3) with the heatmap package version 1.0.13 (https://cran.rstudio.com/web/packages/pheatmap/index.html). Cytokine biplots were constructed using the Factoextra package version 1.0.7 (https://rpkgs.datanovia.com/factoextra/index.html), and visualized using the ggplot2 package version 4.0.0 (https://rdrr.io/r/utils/install.packages.html).

Results

Characteristics of participants and the study workflow

Vaginal fluid samples were collected from 66 participants (42 controls, 24 patients with GSM) and analyzed for cytokine profiles. Participant baseline characteristics were well-balanced between control and patient groups (Table 1), with no significant differences in age, gravidity, parity, or BMI. This demographic balance was maintained in the cytokine analysis cohort (n = 62; Table 2) and the microbiome analysis cohort (n = 61; Table 3), ensuring that observed differences in immune and microbial profiles were not confounded by demographic variables.

Comparison of vaginal cytokine levels between patients and controls

To investigate the alterations in the vaginal immune milieu according to symptom severity, a multiplex assay of 25 cytokines was performed using flow cytometry. Among these, 19 cytokines with reliable detection ranges were included in subsequent analyses. We categorized these cytokines as 13 proinflammatory (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-9, IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-18, IL-22, GM-CSF, IFN-α2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α) and 6 anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, IL-13, IL-27, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin) for comparison between the control and patient groups.

Heatmap visualization revealed that most cytokines exhibited a decreasing trend in the patient group compared to that in controls (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, when cytokine levels were normalized to z-scores for comparative analysis, both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine levels tended to be lower in the patient group (Fig. 2B, D). Principal coordinate analysis based on the cytokine profiles demonstrated a significant difference in the anti-inflammatory cytokine category between the groups (PERMANOVA, p = 0.029; Fig. 2D). Among individual cytokines, the levels of IL-2, IL-6, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-4, IL-10, and IL-17 were markedly lower in patients than in controls (Fig. 2C, E). To further assess the relationship between cytokine levels and symptom severity, the patient cohort was subdivided into Group 1 (mild symptom; total symptom score ≤ 7) and Group 2 (severe symptom; score ≥ 8). To control the false discovery rate (FDR) across all 57 tests (19 cytokines × 3 comparisons), we applied Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction with a significance threshold of q < 0.10, appropriate for exploratory research where moderate Type I error control is balanced against maintaining statistical power in small sample group (n < 30 per group). No cytokines reached statistical significance after Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (all q > 0.10). However, exploratory signals emerged, particularly in Control vs. Group 2 comparison: IL-27 (p = 0.016, d = 0.89, large effect), IFN-γ (p = 0.020, d = 0.49), IL-1β (p = 0.024, d = 0.60), IL-4 (p = 0.026, d = 0.77), and IL-2 (p = 0.043, d = 0.40). Control vs. Group 1 comparison showed exploratory signals in TNF-α (p = 0.007, d = 0.34) and IL-10 (p = 0.025, d = 0.37). Group 1 vs. Group 2 comparison revealed no cytokine differences at p < 0.05. All findings are exploratory and require validation in larger studies (Supplementary Table S1).

To identify the cytokine factors related to symptom severity, we performed a correlation analysis between cytokine levels and clinical symptom scores and found that most cytokines were negatively correlated with symptom severity. Notably, IFN-α2, IL-27, thymic stromal lymphopoietin, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-4, IL-2, IL-10, and IL-9 displayed significant negative correlations (Fig. 2H). These results indicate that vaginal cytokines—particularly IL-2, TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-10, and IL-27—are closely associated with composite symptom severity score in patients with GSM.

Vaginal microbiome composition in non- and post-menopausal patients and controls

Within the post-menopausal subgroup, cytokine levels were significantly associated with symptom severity, with IL-2 and IFN-γ remaining significantly reduced in the severe symptom group (Post_2) compared to post-menopausal asymptomatic controls (Post_0) (Mann–Whitney, BH-adjusted q < 0.10), suggesting that symptom burden rather than menopausal status alone drives the observed immune alterations. Considering the numerous existing reports indicating that menopausal status substantially affects the vaginal microbiome, this suggests a potential link between menopause, the vaginal microbiome, and immunity. To confirm this, we performed vaginal microbiome analysis. When comparing the microbiomes between patients with GSM and healthy individuals without considering menopausal status, no significant differences were detected, and the microbial communities appeared to be mixed without distinct clustering patterns. (Supplementary Fig. 1A–C). Only differential abundance analysis at the genus level identified several taxa enriched in each group (Supplementary Fig. 1D). However, when stratification was performed based on the menopausal status, we observed increased bacterial diversity in post-menopausal women, with elevated levels of various non-Lactobacillus genera, including Roseburia, Faecalibaculum, Phocaeicola, Akkermansia, Enterococcus, Streptococcus, and others (Fig. 4A). Indeed, GSM was significantly more prevalent among post-menopausal than among non-menopausal women in our cohort, which is consistent with the results of previous epidemiological studies. Alpha diversity analysis (Fig. 4B, C) revealed that microbial diversity, measured by both the Shannon index and observed amplicon sequence variants, was significantly higher in post-menopausal women than in non-menopausal women (p < 0.05), irrespective of disease status. Although beta diversity analysis using non-metric multidimensional scaling ordination based on Bray–Curtis distances (Fig. 4D) showed that the samples did not exhibit clear clustering by disease status when all participants were analyzed together, we observed a modest improvement in the clustering patterns when we restricted the analysis to postmenopausal women and further stratified them by symptom severity (Fig. 4E). Although substantial overlap remained among groups, participants in the severe symptom group (Post_2) exhibited a slight shift in microbial community structure relative to participants with mild or no symptoms (Post_1 and Post_0). However, these differences did not reach statistical significance (PERMANOVA, p = 0.109), suggesting a potential association between symptom severity and vaginal microbial community structure in postmenopausal women. The interpretation of beta diversity analysis results in the non-menopausal group stratified by symptom severity was limited by the sample size and the predominance of asymptomatic participants (Post_0, Supplementary Figure S2). To further elucidate the relationship between GSM severity and microbiome composition, we examined the dominant bacterial species in postmenopausal women stratified by symptom severity (Fig. 4F). In participants with low symptom severity (Post_0 and Post_1), the vaginal microbiome remained predominantly dominated by the Lactobacillus genus, particularly L. iners and L. crispatus. In contrast, participants with severe symptom group (Post_2) exhibited markedly different microbial profiles. The relative abundance of Lactobacillus was dramatically lower, and the microbiome was dominated by Streptococcus anginosus (S. anginosus) and Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis). To identify specific bacterial taxa that could discriminate between symptom severity levels, we performed differential abundance analysis using random forest classification (Fig. 4G). Several Lactobacillus species emerged as protective biomarkers that decreased with increasing symptom severity, including L. iners, L. crispatus, and L. gasseri. Discussion

This study simultaneously analyzed the vaginal microbiome and a comprehensive panel of 19 cytokines using a multiplex immunoassay in women with GSM, including a clinically challenging cohort of patients with cancer who were undergoing chemotherapy and anti-hormonal therapy. Our findings revealed that the pathogenesis of symptomatic GSM involved complex disruption of the microbiome–cytokine axis.

Microbiome shifts: primary driver and symptom-specific restructuring

Consistent with previous reports, post-menopausal status was the primary driver of vaginal microbiome composition and diversity, causing significantly increased microbial diversity compared to that in the non-menopausal state21–23. This hormonal change leads to epithelial atrophy, reduced glycogen deposition, which impairs Lactobacillus colonization and decreases lactic acid production, resulting in elevated vaginal pH and a further decline in mucosal defense24. We confirmed the well-established microbial shift in all patients with GSM, involving a reduction in Lactobacillus abundance, and an increase in gut-derived anaerobic bacteria, such as Streptococcus, Akkermansia, Coprococcus, and Enterococcus. More critically, within clinically classified post-menopausal women, symptom severity was associated with a progressive community shift (β-diversity separation), indicating qualitative changes linked to the burden of disease.

The differential distribution of the Lactobacillus species across the severity groups has important implications. L. crispatus maintains a low vaginal pH (< 4.0) through D-lactic acid production and produces hydrogen peroxide and bacteriocins25,26. L. crispatus-dominated vaginal microbiomes are associated with optimal vaginal health and a lower risk of infection. Women with severe symptoms showed marked Lactobacilli depletion and enrichment of S.anginosus and E. faecalis, a profile characterized by aerobic bacterial overgrowth and inflammatory symptoms27–30. E. faecalis and S. anginosus are opportunistic pathogens that colonize when lactobacilli are depleted31,32. The presence of these organisms in severe disease underscores their potential as local mediators of inflammation and barrier disruption33–35.

The relatively higher abundance of L. acidophilus in severe symptom group is intriguing, as this species is primarily gastrointestinal, and its vaginal protective efficacy appears limited compared to that of native species, such as L. crispatus. Furthermore, the presence of B. pseudocatenulatum among the disease-enriched taxa is noteworthy. Although Bifidobacterium is generally considered beneficial in the gut microbiome, its role in the vaginal environment is unclear and may reflect opportunistic colonization in the absence of adequate Lactobacillus36. Further research is required to clarify the roles of L. acidophilus and B. pseudocatenulatum in GSM.

Association between the immunological signature and vaginal microbiome

Our findings revealed a distinctive immunological signature in symptomatic women, with overall reductions in proinflammatory panels (IL-2, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6) and regulatory/Th2 mediators (IL-10, IL-27, IL-4). Symptom severity declined in a stepwise fashion with TNF-α and IL-2. Together with Lactobacillus loss, this suggests that the loss of microbial support may lead to a diminished local immune response, which could impair epithelial integrity and facilitate the persistence of symptoms and dysbiosis. Lactobacillus is known to interact with epithelial and immune cells, producing metabolites such as lactic acid and bacteriocins, which not only suppress pathogens but may also prime or support the local Th1/Th2 immune axis37,38. For example, S-layer proteins from L. crispatus directly modulate epithelial/innate signaling and dampen inflammatory activation in the lower female reproductive tract, which is consistent with a commensal that shapes a protective immune setpoint rather than sterile quiescence39. Therefore, a coordinated decline in IL-2 and IFN-γ levels suggests that certain commensals may help sustain mucosal Th1 surveillance tone to preserve the epithelium and limit symptoms. IL-2 supports effector T-cell fitness and regulatory T-cell homeostasis, while IFN-γ strengthens epithelial defenses and microbiota containment. Within the Lactobacillus clade, species-level function matters. L. iners is frequently described as a “transitional” species that occupies perturbed vaginal ecosystems and can coexist with dysbiosis; its protective capacity appears context-dependent and generally weaker than that of L. crispatus. This previous literature helps reconcile our finding that L. iners may still correlate positively with IL-2/IFN-γ, whereas the strongest immune association and symptom-favorable axis centers on L. crispatus–IL-240–42. The decline in IL-10 and IL-27 levels indicates weak regulatory control, which may allow pain-related signaling and neuroimmune crosstalk to emerge as a burning/itching sensation and dyspareunia.

The identification of Akkermansia muciniphila as a suppressor of pro-inflammatory IL-6 provides insight into immune-microbiota relationships in postmenopausal women. A. muciniphila is a mucin-degrading bacterium that produces short-chain fatty acids and promotes tolerogenic immune responses, potentially suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6. The strong negative correlation (ρ = -0.639, p < 0.001, q = 0.014) suggests that A. muciniphila abundance is inversely associated with IL-6 levels, indicating a potential biological mechanism linking microbiota composition to immune dysregulation. This relationship achieved formal statistical significance after stringent multiple-testing correction and demonstrated high reproducibility across independent samples (r = 0.88), positioning it as the most robust immune-microbiota association in this cohort. The mechanism may involve A. muciniphila-derived metabolites or microbial-associated molecular patterns that activate tolerogenic pathways. Future studies examining the functional mechanisms linking A. muciniphila colonization to IL-6 suppression, including metabolite profiling and immune cell analysis, are needed to validate this hypothesis and elucidate the underlying biological pathways.

Local neuroimmune alterations as a driver of post-menopausal vaginal symptoms

The coordinated reductions in IL-2, IFN-γ, and Lactobacillus abundance offer a plausible bridge to symptoms via the local neuroimmune network. Chronic vulvar/vaginal pain conditions (e.g., provoked vulvodynia) feature mast cell-rich inflammation, neuroproliferation, and nociceptor sensitization (TRPV1/TRPA1 and NGF pathways), which integrate immune mediators with sensory neuron hyperexcitability43. Notably, lactate itself can modulate nociception; extracellular lactate inhibits TRPV1 channel activity and suppresses Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide release in sensory neurons. One hypothesised mechanism, not tested in the present cross-sectional study, is that Lactobacillus-derived lactate contributes to dampening of TRPV1/CGRP-mediated sensory signalling in healthy mucosa; loss of this metabolite during dysbiosis could therefore unmask or amplify pre-existing nociceptive input. This neuroimmune component would require dedicated mechanistic studies (e.g., DRG neuron stimulation, mast-cell quantification) to be confirmed. Loss of Lactobacillus (and lactic acid), therefore, may remove both a barrier-reinforcing and a neuron-calming input, aligning with our observation that decreases in IL-2/IFN-γ levels—cytokines that sustain the survival and proliferation capacity of effector T cells, tissue surveillance, and microbiota containment—are associated with worse symptoms.

Putting this information together, our findings are consistent with a potential microbiome-immune association involving L. crispatus, lactic acid/commensal-related factors, epithelial and innate immune conditioning, and IL-2/IFN-γ-supported Th1 immune activity. This association may be linked to maintenance of vaginal barrier function and reduced symptom severity. Associations between microbial, epithelial, and immune factors may be influenced by multiple interrelated processes including estrogen deficiency, reduced abundance of L. crispatus, epithelial stress, and altered immune activity, which may together be associated with the co-occurrence of dysbiosis and persistent GSM symptoms such as dryness, burning, itching, and dyspareunia. This integrated interpretation may explainour observed correlations between L. crispatus abundance, IL-2/IFN-γ levels, and composite symptom severity scores and suggests that L. crispatus restoration and IL-2-associated immune activity may represent potential areas for future investigation in GSM symptoms management. Differences in the observed correlations between L. crispatus and L. iners with IL-2/IFN-γ levels may reflect their distinct functional profiles. L. crispatus is reportedly characterized by an ability to produce both D- and L-lactic acid, frequent hydrogen peroxide production, and stable S-layer protein expression, whereas L. iners exhibits reduced lactic acid production potential (only L-lactic acid) and limited hydrogen peroxide production39,44. These functional differences may partly explain the stronger correlation observed only with L. crispatus; however, this interpretation should be made with caution, since microbial functional activity was not directly assessed in this study.

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of our study is that it is a novel in-depth study that simultaneously analyzed the vaginal microbiota and a broad cytokine panel across diverse GSM patient cohorts, including patients with diverse clinical backgrounds such as cancer survivors undergoing chemotherapy or anti-hormonal therapy. Furthermore, the use of a detailed subjective symptom severity scale allowed for clinically relevant analysis of host microbiome–immune interactions.

However, this study has some limitations. First, since this was a cross-sectional study, the observed correlations between microbial and cytokine changes and GSM symptoms indicate an association, not causation, and the lack of functional metagenomic profiling may limit mechanistic insights into the relationship between microbial changes and immune modulation. Second, while the overall sample size was relatively large for this type of detailed molecular analysis, sub-stratification by symptom severity and menopausal status resulted in small and imbalanced subgroups (Post_0 n = 10, Post_1 n = 6, Post_2 n = 10; paired microbiome-cytokine analysis n = 19) , potentially limiting the statistical power. Third, menopausal status was classified using clinical criteria based on menstrual history rather than biochemical confirmation with serum FSH or estradiol. Four patients with GSM symptoms did not meet the menopausal criterion, reflecting the clinical heterogeneity of this condition. Objective clinical measures of vaginal atrophy, including vaginal pH, vaginal maturation index, and physical examination findings, were not systematically collected, which limits the objectivity of patient classification and should be addressed in future studies. In addition, some controls had gynecological diseases such as endometrial polyps or ovarian cysts. Since this observational analysis focuses on the local vaginal cytokine milieu associated with clinical symptoms, the inclusion of cancer survivors and patients under chemotherapy was unavoidable. Cancer survivors may represent a distinct clinical phenotype with treatment-related immune modulation, potentially introducing heterogeneity into the patient group, which should be considered when interpreting our findings.

To address these inherent limitations, in vivo studies based on vaginal microbiome transplantation or longitudinal cohort studies to track changes over time with and without treatment should be conducted in the future. A multiomics approach incorporating metabolomics and proteomics can precisely identify bioactive metabolites produced by the vaginal microbiome that directly interact with and regulate the local cytokine network.

Taken together, the robust correlations we observed among microbial dysbiosis, defined cytokine alterations, and GSM severity indicate that future treatment paradigms should move beyond estrogen replacement therapy alone. Our findings support the development of microbiome- and immune-targeted therapies, including probiotics that promote Lactobacillus recovery and acid production as safe and non-hormonal options for patients in whom hormones are contraindicated. Moreover, IL-18, IL-2, and IFN-γ may serve as actionable biomarkers for patient stratification and for monitoring the disease trajectory and treatment response in clinical trials.

In conclusion, GSM in symptomatic women is characterized by a coordinated reduction in key vaginal cytokines and severity-linked community shifts. Crucially, menopausal status appears to be a key determinant of overall microbial diversity, whereas symptom severity is associated with specific changes in IL-2 and IFN-γ within post-menopausal stratified analyses. These findings suggest that symptom-related immune variation may occur in addition to menopause-associated effects.

This study also highlights a potential microbiome-immune association between L. crispatus and IL-2/IFN-γ levels. Taken together, these findings suggest candidate microbiome-immune biomarkers that may be useful in future studies for the precise management of post-menopausal vaginal symptoms.
