Electroencephalography, behavioural observations, bleed-out and temperature at the point of application in cattle receiving DTS: Diathermic Syncope® at 160–200 kJ
Authors: Alison Small, David McLean, Joel McLean, Andrew Phillips, Dominic Niemeyer, Daniel Driscoll, Ian Jenson, Leisha Hewitt, James Ralph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•EEG, behavioural observation and forehead temperatures were measured in 12 cattle.•DTS induced unconsciousness within a few seconds of energy application.•Forehead temperature did not rise while the cattle were conscious.•Unconscious was maintained during halal neck cutting and bleed-out.•Cattle did not show pain, fear or distress during restraint and energy application.

Introduction

The DTS: Diathermic Syncope® technology uses electromagnetic energy to render an animal unconscious prior to slaughter. The dose of energy applied is carefully controlled to minimise tissue damage and ensure that the animal can recover, such that the technique may be acceptable both for religious slaughter (such as Halal), as well as meeting broader expectations regarding the animal’s state of consciousness at the time of slaughter.

A recent study identified that depilation and skin damage can be observed on the cadaver head after bleed out (Small, Jenson, Fiszon et al., 2025), however this depilation is not evident at the time of removal of the applicator immediately after energy application, and measurements suggest that temperatures capable of causing skin damage on the forehead only develop near the end of the energy application. However, the question remains whether the animal might perceive discomfort or pain from rising forehead skin temperatures before losing consciousness. In addition, empirical observation of the process suggests that a secondary brachiocephalic (or ‘thoracic’) sticking procedure is not required after the transverse neck cut prescribed by Halal tradition. The volume and consistency of the blood flow after the neck cut is one of the important criteria in determining Halal acceptability, as is the presence of a heartbeat during exsanguination. The animal must die as a result of blood loss, not due to any preceding procedure.

The current study therefore had three objectives:•To confirm that the animal loses consciousness before forehead temperature reaches a potentially painful level.•To characterise the bleeding process in terms of Halal acceptability; and•To assess the time to irreversible unconsciousness following DTS application and a transverse neck cut

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

This study was conducted under the authority of the CSIRO ‘Wildlife and Large Animal’ Animal Ethics Committee, reference 2022–12, in accordance with the provisions of the Australian Code for the care and use of animals for scientific purposes (National Health & Medical Research Council, 2013).

Animals

Twelve Australian Angus type steers, liveweight 430–500 kg, were sourced from a single mob presented at the abattoir. They had arrived at the abattoir the preceding week and had been held for 4 days in an adjacent paddock, before being moved into the lairage and held overnight with ad libitum water.

Study design

The study was observational in design, assessing three different energy applications: 200 kJ, 180 kJ and 160 kJ. All animals were processed on a single day in February 2025, in a commercial abattoir in Victora, Australia.

Animal preparation for electroencephalography

Animals were moved individually to a cattle crush in the lairage using low-stress animal handling practices (use of flight zones, voice and a flapper as an arm extension). Once restrained, the hair on the animal’s forehead was clipped (Oster clippers, No. 0 blade) over the zygomatic arch on either side, and on the flat nasal bones, approximately one hand’s breadth forward of the eyes. Low impedance (< 5 kΩ) electrode pads (RedDotMini, 3 M Australia, North Ryde, NSW) were then affixed to the skin using cyanoacrylate (Loctite 454, Loctite Australia, Caringbah, NSW), applied to the margins surrounding the electrode gel.

Data collection

Immediately after preparation, the animal was moved up the lead-in race to the restraint box, which captured the neck and head, raising the chin such that the nasal bones were almost horizontal. EEG leads were attached, and the waveguide applicator (which included temperature monitoring equipment) positioned on the forehead (Fig. 1). After 60 s of EEG data had been collected, DTS energy was applied at a power setting of 18 kW.

The DTS system has been described in Small, Jenson, Fiszon et al. (2025). Briefly, the animal is restrained in a head capture and chin lift device based on that described by Grandin (1992). A waveguide applicator is positioned on the forehead (Fig. 1), and safety shielding secured. The desired power and energy applications are programmed using proprietary software via a control panel, and the generator initiated. The duration of energy application is related to the incident power and the desired total energy ‘package’ to be delivered. The units of power (kW) is shorthand for ‘kJ per second’, so a higher power setting will reduce the time required to reach the desired energy delivery than a lower power setting. The current recommended parameters for DTS application to adult cattle are 16–20 kW power, and total energy between 160 and 200 kJ. 18 kW will deliver 180 kJ in a period of 10 s, while 20 kW will deliver 180 kJ in a period of 9 s. In practice, delivery of energy takes slightly longer, because it takes time for the power to reach the desired level, and some energy is reflected rather than being directed at the target surface. Thus, in the current study, energy application lasted for 11 s for animals receiving 160 kJ, to 13 s for animals receiving 200 kJ.

The aim was to study three animals at each of 200 kJ, 180 kJ and 160 kJ energy applications (at a consistent power setting, reductions in total energy applied reduces the duration of application and reduces the overall heating of tissue). After the initial three animals (200 kJ), adjustments were made to the positioning of the temperature probes to improve consistency of data collection. Three animals were then processed at 180 kJ, a further three at 160 kJ and finally the 200 kJ group was repeated using the remaining three cattle. At the completion of energy application, the efficacy of the stunning process was assessed using established animal-based indicators in line with EFSA recommendations (EFSA, 2004, 2020). Specifically, the presence or absence of a corneal reflex was tested by lightly touching the surface of the eye, and a hand was passed in front of the eye to assess for any tracking or visual response. In addition, the animal was observed for tonic–clonic seizure activity. The combination of a tonic–clonic behavioural pattern, absence of eye tracking, and absence of a corneal reflex was interpreted as indicating a loss of consciousness. Once the assessment of the stunning process had been completed, the waveguide was retracted and the restraint box inverted to present the neck to the authorised Halal slaughterman. The Halal neck cut was performed: a transverse cut across the neck at the level of cervical vertebrae c1-c3, severing both carotid arteries and both jugular veins. Under normal processing conditions, the animal would be tipped out of the restraint box to lie laterally on a bleeding conveyer. However, for the purposes of the study, it was retained in the inverted position to allow continuous observation of the bleed-out. The EEG leads remained in situ, recording data throughout. The bleed-out process was observed to confirm that the major blood vessels, including both carotid arteries, had been effectively severed. Monitoring of animal behaviour continued until visible arterial pulsing had ceased, blood flow had slowed to a trickle, and agonal convulsions were no longer evident. The carcass was then tipped onto the bleed conveyor, shackled and hoisted, and processed for human consumption.

Electroencephalography

A three-channel montage (placed on the left zygomatic arch, right zygomatic arch and ground electrode on the nasal bones) was used to collect EEG data using an Octal BioAmp amplifier, Powerlab and LabChart 8 (ADInstruments, Sydney, Australia) via shielded leads. The sampling rate was 4000 per minute, and a low-pass filter of 30 Hz was applied. Data were collected for 60 s prior to the commencement of energy application, throughout energy application, during box inversion, during cutting of the neck and until the end of bleed-out was confirmed.

The collected data were subsequently analysed offline using LabChart 8 (ADInstruments, Sydney, Australia). From the raw data, Root Mean Square (RMS) power in each 1 s epoch was extracted. The Spectral Analysis Package within LabChart 8 was used to apply Fast Fourier Transformation (FFT), with multiplication using a Hann window in 1-second epochs with a 25 % overlap. Total power (Ptot) in the entire frequency range (0–30 Hz), median frequency (SEF50) and 95 % Spectral Edge frequency (SEF95) were extracted. For each animal the median value of Ptot prior to energy application was calculated and this was used as the baseline value. Baseline normalization was then carried out by transforming data for each 1-s epoch into decibel change from baseline according to the formula: dB=10*log10(value/ baseline), to bring all data sets into a comparable format. These data, RMS data and data for SEF95 and SEF50 were charted and inspected for EEG suppression and epileptiform activity. Time to onset of EEG suppression was estimated based on the time between the start of energy application and first visible drop in SEF95; Time to nadir was estimated based on the time between the start of energy application and the lowest point in SEF95 and duration of suppression was estimate as the time from the onset of EEG suppression until SEF50 and SEF95 began to return towards baseline values.

Temperature at the point of application

The Teflon plate at the applicator tip was modified to include three fiber optic temperature sensors (FISO Technologies, Quebec QC, Canada), such that the sensors would be in contact with the forehead of the animal. Fiber optics enabled continuous, uninterrupted recording of temperature within the applied electromagnetic microwave field. The three probes were placed in a triangular pattern within the Teflon plate, such that one probe was midline, proximal, while the others were left and right lateral towards the poll (Fig. 2). Temperature data were captured and plotted using FISO Commander (FISO Technologies, Quebec QC, Canada) at a sampling rate of one sample every 0.45 s.Fig. 2Diagram of position of applicator and temperature probes on the animal’s forehead. Assessment of behaviours

The DTS control system includes closed-circuit television (CCTV) recording which allows the monitoring of the animal’s head throughout application, and a control panel that shows the progress of energy application, in the form of a chart showing cumulative energy delivered over time. During application, the stun operator called “energy flowing” when the blue line (indicating energy delivered (kJ)) on the chart began to ascend, and “complete” when the line plateaued (i.e. the selected energy had been delivered), and the system had re-set to ‘standby’ (Fig. 3). The stun operator, CCTV output and control panel were videoed using GoPro Hero 5 (GoPro, San Mateo, USA), to allow capture of all events relating to energy application. Assessment of bleed-out

Bleed-out was observed in real time until visible pulsing was no longer observed and blood flow from the arteries slowed to a trickle. The observer recorded the character of the blood flow and the development of any clots in the vessels. A veterinarian with training in assessment of ocular reflexes following stunning checked ocular reflexes intermittently throughout bleed-out, at approximate 30–45 s intervals, until the eye was staring and the pupil dilated. The timing of each check was variable depending on the availability of the veterinarian, and access to the eye: in the inverted position, access was impeded initially by the blood flow across the area. Reflex checking involved touching of the eyelashes, touching of the cornea and subjective assessment of the degree of pupil dilation. The bleed-out phase was also videoed using GoPro Hero 5 (GoPro, San Mateo, USA) cameras, and this footage was annotated offline to capture timings of: start and finish of the Halal cut; reflex checks by the veterinarian; cessation of respiratory movements; blood flow reducing to trickling as opposed to under pressure; and cessation of agonal movements (irregular involuntary movements of the limbs and/or irregular gasping movements).

Fate of carcases

Carcases and offal were processed for human consumption and inspected according to the Australian regulatory requirements.

Results

Behavioural responses to restraint and energy application

The requirement to hold the animal in restraint for approximately 60 s prior to energy application, so that baseline EEG data could be collected, allowed assessment of responses to restraint. After entering the box, two animals (Animal 1 and Animal 10) stepped backwards (back up) away from the neck yoke before putting their head forward to allow capture of the neck. Two animals (Animal 2 and Animal 6) pulled back into the neck yoke and chin lift during baseline EEG data collection: Animal 2 pulled back 2 s after the chin lift had been applied, Animal 6 pulled back 101 s and 114 s after the chin lift had been applied. Animal 7 tossed its head 33 s after the chin lift had been applied, during EEG recording, while Animal 10 vocalised (non-bellowing) between neck capture and application of the chin lift. No instances of leaping, struggling, bellowing or pain face were observed.

On energy application, all animals entered a tonic-clonic epileptiform convulsive state. Onset of the tonic (stiff) phase occurred an average 2.17 s (range 0 - 3 s) after the start of energy application and was followed by the clonic (twitching/jerking) phase, the onset of which occurred an average of 4.08 s (range 1 – 8 s) after the start of energy application (Table 2). Assessment of bleed-out

Blood flow after the Halal cut (which took 3–4 s to perform) was vigorous, the stream of blood arcing out over the head to a distance of between 1 and 1.5 m. Due to the inverted position of the animal, access to the head to assess corneal reflexes was prevented for a period of 30 – 60 s from the start of the neck cut. After the initial high pressure blood flow, blood was still pumping, but at a lesser pressure, running onto the cut surface of the neck and into the drain (the body was still in the inverted position, with the head hanging from the uncut vertebral column and nuchal ligaments), and the veterinarian could access the eye to assess ocular reflexes. Approximately 2 min after the start of the Halal cut, clotting was observed around the cut ends of the carotid arteries, but this was not sufficient to occlude the vessels, and blood continued to flow. Bleed out was considered ‘complete’ when blood flow had slowed to a trickle, and pulsing was no longer evident in the flow. This occurred between 104 and 227 s after the start of the Halal cut (Table 2), with the exception of animal 10, where it was noted that the cut failed to fully sever the right jugular, and large clots formed in both carotid arteries, particular on the right-hand side.

In animals 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 and 8, absence of ocular reflexes with staring eye and dilated pupil (considered to be indicative of death) was confirmed at the first assessment (38 – 75 s after the start of the Halal cut). In animals 4, 6, 9, 10, 11 and 12, the first assessment of ocular reflexes produced a slow palpebral response although the eye was staring and the pupils dilated. Absence of all ocular reflexes was confirmed in these animals between 78 and 149 s after the start of the Halal cut (Table 2), but the actual time of cessation of reflexes would have occurred at some point prior to the confirmation, as reflexes were assessed dependant on the veterinarian availability and occurred at 30–45 s intervals.

Rhythmic respiratory movements ceased between 26 and 96 (mean 70.18) s after the Halal cut, and this was followed by intermittent bouts of agonal convulsions or movements of the limbs. These agonal movements ceased entirely between 171 and 276 (mean 213.36) s after the Halal cut.

Temperature at the point of application

Prior to energy application, temperature recorded at the tip of the applicator was 29.0 - 36.5 °C. This aligned with the ambient temperature for the day as recorded at the Australian Bureau of Meteorology Nilma North station (27 km from the abattoir): min-max 18.1 – 36.4 °C. For animals 1, 2 and 3, temperature increases recorded during and after energy application did not exceed 3 °C, so the positioning of the probes in the Teflon plate were adjusted to ensure that the tips protruded 2 mm from the Teflon plate. Subsequently, the temperature increases at probes 1 and 3 remained at <3 °C, while probe 2 detected greater increases in temperature in animals 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10 and 11. In animals 9 and 12, the temperature increase at probe 2 remained below 3 °C. Subsequent to the study, it was discovered that the particular applicator in use was generating a proximal hotspot, which accounted for the difference between the three probes.

In all animals in which probe 2 indicated a marked increase in temperature, the rapid increase began 8–12 s after the commencement of energy application, and after the onset of the tonic-clonic behavioural response (Table 3 and Fig. 4). In all cases, maximum recorded temperature was reached at the end of or after energy application had been completed and fell rapidly once the waveguide was lifted from the forehead. Electroencephalography

Despite the low-pass filter applied to exclude all frequencies above 30 Hz, evidence of artefact was present, particularly associated with inversion of the restraint box and after the Halal cut when the blood flow was running out of the incision and over the EEG leads (Fig. 5). This interfered with the ability to accurately identify time to nadir and duration of EEG suppression during inversion of the restraint box through to when blood flow is reduced to trickling, although estimates could be made based on qualitative assessment of the data. Onset of EEG suppression was estimated to occur between 0 and 5 s after the start of energy application, nadir was estimated to occur between 20 and 144 s, and suppression was estimated to persist for >96 s after the start of energy application (Table 4). During energy application, there was a sharp drop in EEG power, relative to the median value collected during baseline recording, followed by an increase, and this then fell below baseline after the Halal neck cut (Fig. 6). This pattern was reflected in the [root mean square (RMS)] EEG power data (Fig. 7). Discussion

Behavioural responses to restraint

Under commercial processing conditions, the maximum time that an animal is held in restraint, with the chin lift applied, prior to falling unconscious is 20 s. In the current study, one animal pulled back into the chin lift (which could be considered an ‘escape attempt’) after 2 s of restraint, while two others showed escape behaviours (pull back or head toss) after 33 s and 101 s of restraint. This suggests that under commercial processing conditions, the restraint would be minimally aversive. These findings align with the observations of Grandin (1992) when considering well-designed restraint such as the American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (ASPCA) upright pen for religious slaughter, and contrast with the study by Ewbank et al. (1992), in which the restraint was less well designed, and animals were reluctant to place their heads into the neck capture.

In the current study, the behavioural response to restraint was aligned with crush scores of 1 or 2 according to the system used by Grandin (1993), in which score 1 is “calm, no movement” and score 5 is “rearing, twisting of the body and struggling violently”. The crush score is normally used as a measure of animal temperament (Burrow & Dillon, 1997; Grandin, 1993; Lee et al., 2018), rather than as a means to indicate differences between handling equipment. No further publications evaluating the response to restraint other than a cattle crush or squeeze-chute, which are sufficiently different from the chin-lift head capture restraint used for DTS energy application that the findings bear little relevance to the situation, were found to compare to the findings in the current study.

Behavioural responses to energy application

The principle of DTS is to apply an electromagnetic waveform to the brain. This leads to diathermic heating such that neurotransmission ceases, and induces a generalized epileptiform state in the brain (Rault et al., 2014; Small, Jenson, Phillips et al., 2025; Small et al., 2019). This state is incompatible with consciousness and is physically manifested as tonic-clonic seizures, similar to those that occur as a result of head-only electric stunning (Musk & Johnson, 2024; Zivotofsky, 2023). The tonic-clonic response is considered indicative of unconsciousness by EFSA (European Food Safety Authority) Panel on Animal Health and Welfare (2004), who state that loss of consciousness associated with effective electrical stunning is characterised by “tonic seizure followed by clonic seizures indicative of generalised epilepsy”.

In the current study, loss of consciousness, based on the development of the tonic-clonic response occurred within 3 s of energy application, the tonic phase occurring between 0 and 3 s (mean 1.83 s), progressing to clonic between 1 and 8 s (mean 4.08 s) after the commencement of energy application. These results concur with those of Small, Jenson, Phillips et al. (2025) who reported onset of the tonic-clonic response within 6 s of commencement of energy application. The observed tonic-clonic response was similar to the post-electrical stunning described by Beausoleil et al. (2024); EFSA (European Food Safety Authority) Panel on Animal Health and Welfare (2004); Von Holleben et al. (2010) and Robins et al. (2014) in pulsed ultra-high current electrical stunning.

There was a concern that the behavioural response observed could be ‘absence seizure’ as opposed to unconsciousness. For example, transient immobility and individual oculomotor phenomena such as absence of corneal reflex; open, staring eyes; tonic or clonic upward movement of the eyeball (downward movement of the eyeball does not occur); or rapid blinking can be associated with ‘absence seizure’ (formerly described as “Petit mal epilepsy”), when they occur in the absence of other indicators of generalised epilepsy (Grand mal tonic-clonic seizures) (Bogacz et al., 2000). Behaviourally, during an ‘absence seizure’, the animal may be immobile with eyes open, or flaccid with eyes half-closed (Godschalk et al., 1977; Marescaux et al., 1984; Vergnes et al., 1987), and rhythmic bursts of movement of the eye and eyelids can occur (Bogacz et al., 2000; Gibbs et al., 1943). Following an ‘absence’ attack, there is no post-ictal phase and the animal behaves normally (Cortez et al., 2001; Hayes, 1948). In contrast, DTS energy application results in tonic-clonic full-body convulsive response, with arrhythmic movements of the facial muscles and eyelids. The eye is fixed and staring or may roll down and then return to centre. On recovery there is a period of post-ictal disorientation or catalepsy before the animal returns to normal behaviour (Small, Jenson, Phillips et al., 2025). This aligns with description of generalised (Grand mal) epilepsy (Gibbs et al., 1943; Stanojlovic et al., 2009): behaviourally, the animal is rigidly tonic, progressing rapidly to full body convulsions. The eye may be fixed and staring or may roll up or down. Movements of the facial muscles and eyelids are arrhythmic and random. Following the seizure there is a period of post-ictal disorientation or catalepsy.

In the current study, during the tonic and clonic phases, there was a temporary cessation of respiration. Assessment of the corneal and palpebral reflexes is prevented due to the uncontrolled blinking and twitching of the eyelids and facial muscles in response to the energy application. As described by Small, Jenson, Phillips et al. (2025), rhythmic breathing returned and was present at the time of the Halal cut (in this study, 52–75 s after the start of energy application). The return of breathing indicates a depth of unconsciousness less than that induced by mechanical stunning or electrical stun, but similar to that produced by chemical general anaesthesia, i.e. the animal is in a state of cerebral unconsciousness, but brainstem function is present. In general anaesthesia, loss of rhythmic breathing is an early sign that the animal may be close to irreversible unconsciousness, as cranial nerve X has been affected (King, 1987; Small, Jenson, Phillips et al., 2025). Although brainstem function at the time of the Halal neck cut is indicated by the presence of rhythmic breathing, pain perception is dependent on cerebral cortex function (Cosio, 2020; Sgourdou, 2022). In the current study the corneal response was slow indicating that cranial nerves I and II, i.e., the brainstem, have been affected (King, 1987; Small, Jenson, Phillips et al., 2025), but not completely inactivated.

This was the first attempt to measure EEG during application of DTS. Using a low-pass filter to remove any frequencies above 30 Hz, we reduce the bulk of interference – however artefact relating to physical movement (e.g. inversion of the rotary box, or muscle movements during the tonic-clonic phase) was still present and noticeable in the data. Application of energy resulted in an initial drop in EEG power, indicating a more chaotic, less synchronous discharge of neurones, but this quickly shifted to a high-amplitude, lower frequency (HALF) pattern that is considered to be incompatible with sensibility (Bager et al., 1992; Daly et al., 1988). The increase in amplitude, associated with a drop in spectral edge frequency, particularly SEF50, following DTS energy application concurs with the findings of Rault et al. (2014), Small et al. (2019) and Small, Jenson, Phillips et al. (2025), all of which also observed that there was individual variation in the time taken for the EEG to enter the epileptiform state. The EEG post DTS energy application is similar to that of generalised (Grand mal) epilepsy, which is characterised by continual high-voltage spike-wave and polyspike complexes, with all frequencies elevated, and peak voltage may be seen in the 20–29 Hz range (Gibbs, 1939; Gibbs et al., 1943, 1941; Stanojlovic et al., 2009). The EEG did not show features consistent with absence seizure (Petit mal), which is characterised by bursts of slow spike-and-wave discharges (SWD) in the EEG, with a predominant frequency of around 3–6 Hz (Bogacz et al., 2000; Gibbs, 1939; Gibbs et al., 1943, 1941). Interestingly, the rhythmic movements of the eye and eyelids during absence attack occurs in synchrony with the underlying SWD – the animals subject to DTS did not show rhythmic movements of the eye and eyelids. The predominant frequency of the SWD in petit mal does depend on species (Marescaux et al., 1984), but no data pertaining to cattle have been located. It does appear that smaller animals (e.g. rats) show SWD of 2–11 Hz, while larger animals e.g. baboons and humans show SWD in the 3–5 Hz range, and cats and rhesus monkeys 2.5–4.5 Hz. Thus. it is likely that SWD in cattle would also be around 3 Hz or less.

In the current study, EEG suppression continued for 96 or more seconds, although interpretation of the data after the neck cut is challenged by the presence of blood flowing close to or along the EEG leads, and the presence of agonal convulsions, which occurred intermittently following the cessation of rhythmic breathing.

Assessment of forehead temperature

The temperature measured at the site of energy application remained close to ambient air temperature for the first 8–12 s after the start of application, i.e. until after the animal had entered the clonic phase. There are few studies investigating the nociceptive response to heat in cattle. Machado Filho et al. (1998) identified a nociceptive threshold of 62–70 °C in adult cattle, using a radiant heat source applied to the skin above the hoof, while Veissier et al. (2000) used a laser-based heat source on the legs of calves, which have thinner skin than adult cattle, suggesting a nociceptive threshold of between 45 and 55 °C. Although maximal temperatures did exceed 50 °C in some cases, and the heating associated with DTS energy application can result in tissue damage (Small, Jenson, Fiszon et al., 2025), the animal was unconscious at this point, based on behavioural observations and EEG.

Bleed-out following the transverse neck cut

Neck cutting was delayed in the current study, as compared to what would be expected in commercial slaughter processing (estimated to be 15 – 30 s post the end of energy application), due to the data collection requirements of the study. Nevertheless, neck cutting was performed during the period of EEG suppression, and a crisp corneal response was not detected during bleed-out. A slow corneal response was observed in some animals, which indicates residual brainstem function, the corneal response being mediated via cranial nerves I and II (King, 1987; Small, Jenson, Phillips et al., 2025). Respiratory movements ceased between 26 and 96 s (mean 70.18 s) after the Halal cut, which appears to align with the observations of Neves et al. (2016), who studied cattle undergoing ritual slaughter. In their study, rhythmic breathing was present in 100 % (n = 88) of cattle at 20 s, and in 77 % at 60 s after the neck cut.

The Royal College of Physicians considers brain stem death as confirmation of death (Black et al., 1995), however diagnosis of brain stem death in the context of slaughter is hampered by the persistence of spinal reflexes and agonal convulsions, otherwise termed ‘brain dead associated automatisms’ (Jain & DeGeorgia, 2005; Orban et al., 2015). These reflexes and body movements have been observed to occur for up to three minutes after the thoracic cut (Terlouw et al., 2015).

From a practical perspective, death is usually diagnosed by clinicians based on absence of spontaneous respiration, absence of heart beat and the presence of a staring eye and dilated pupil (Jerlström, 2014). The heart can continue to beat for a period of time after brain stem death (Black et al., 1995; Orban et al., 2015), so although its absence can be confirmatory of death, it is not an indicator of the onset of death. In the current study cessation of respiration after the Halal cut was used as a proxy for the onset of brain stem death, with death subsequently confirmed by the presence of a fixed, staring eye and pupil dilation. Cessation of pulsations in the blood flow was challenging to assess, but loss of blood flow pressure and agonal convulsions could be observed.

In 4 animals, staring eye and dilated pupil had been confirmed prior to cessation of rhythmic breathing, while in the remaining animals, cessation of rhythmic breathing was usually followed by confirmation of death. The actual time from the neck cut to the onset of pupil dilation was not recorded, as timing of assessment of the ocular responses depended on the availability of the attendant veterinarian. In the current study, the presence of ‘staring eye and dilated pupils’ was recorded between 78 and 149 s after the neck cut – a substantially shorter interval than that described by Blackmore (1984), who recorded 415 s to pupil dilation in bulls and 140–435 s in calves.

Prior to brain stem death, the slaughtered animal will enter a state of irreversible unconsciousness, which could be considered to be the ‘onset of death’. A number of studies have been conducted using slaughter without prior stunning in an attempt to estimate the time from the neck cut to a state of unconsciousness. However, there is a challenge in defining what is meant by ‘unconsciousness’, as consciousness is a continuum from fully conscious through increasing depth of consciousness to irreversible unconsciousness, coma and death (Zeman, 2001). The studies pertaining to slaughter without prior stunning have used indicators such as loss of posture as a proxy for the onset of loss of consciousness (Blackmore, 1984; Gibson et al., 2015; Gregory et al., 2010), others have used loss of brainstem reflexes (palpebral reflex, corneal reflex, nociceptive withdrawal) to indicate unconsciousness (Bourguet et al., 2011; Neves et al., 2016), and still others have used various electroencephalographic (EEG) indicators to indicate loss of consciousness (Anil et al., 1995; Bager et al., 1992; Daly et al., 1988; Gregory & Wotton, 1984; Kallweit et al., 1989). Those that study the EEG acknowledge that there is a transitional state between ‘conscious’ and ‘not conscious’, and we cannot to date measure or understand the animal’s experience while in this transitional state.

Under general anaesthesia, the aim of the anaesthetist is to maintain the patient at a level of unconsciousness at which they do not experience the pain associated with the surgical procedure, but above the level of irreversible unconsciousness. Therefore, the depth of anaesthesia required for a surgical procedure is linked to the invasiveness of the procedure (Whyte & Booker, 2003), and the focus is on experience. There is evidence that the thalamus (considered by researchers of slaughter and stunning as the ‘seat of consciousness’) is important in modulating the ability of the animal to experience pain, but under deep sedation/light anaesthesia a breakdown in communication between the thalamus and cortex occurs (Mashour & Alkire, 2013). Therefore, loss of cortical function preceding loss of thalamic function could lead to a loss of the ability to experience the insult applied (surgery, or slaughter), while retaining brainstem function during the transition to irreversible unconsciousness and brain stem death.

Assessment of the bleeding process in terms of acceptability to religious authorities and estimation of the time to irreversible unconsciousness following DTS application and a transverse neck cut

For DTS to be acceptable to the religious authorities, the animal must not enter irreversible unconsciousness as a result of energy application, but as a result of hypoxia associated with blood loss through exsanguination – thus estimation of the time required to lose sufficient blood to achieve irreversible unconsciousness is an important criterion when assessing the welfare of the system. In the current study, the time elapsed from the neck cut to the blood flow being assessed as ‘trickling’ ranged from 104 - 227 (mean 169.5) s. This aligns with the findings of Supratikno et al. (2022) who reported a range of 116 – 293 s for cattle slaughtered without prior stunning, and 104 – 545 s for cattle stunned using a percussive mechanical device prior to slaughter. The limitation in both the current study and that of Supratikno et al. is the subjective nature of assessing ‘trickling’ or ‘not pumping’ when observing exsanguination.

The challenge in interpreting what this means in terms of animal welfare and the experience of the unconscious or transitionally unconscious animal is that cattle possess a potentially viable arterial supply to the brain via the vertebral artery which arises from the common carotid artery low in the neck, travels along the spinal column and connects to the basi-occipital plexus (Baldwin, 1960; Baldwin & Bell, 1963a, 1963b). This anastomosis is considered to be the mechanism by which consciousness can be maintained for longer in cattle subjected to ritual slaughter than in sheep (Blackmore, 1984; Newhook & Blackmore, 1982), particularly where carotid occlusion occurs during exsanguination (Bozzo et al., 2020; Gregory et al., 2010, 2012, 2008). In the absence of carotid occlusion, the neck cut results in a very rapid loss of cerebral blood pressure. Recent calculations indicate that cerebral blood pressure drops to <5 % of baseline, which would result in irreversible unconsciousness, within 10 - 15 s (Hascalovici et al., 2025; Rabkin, 2025). This would suggest that a minimum duration of 96 s of unconsciousness (as indicated by EEG suppression produced by DTS), would allow the animal to be bled to irreversible unconsciousness prior to the animal entering the transitional state associated with return to consciousness, even if the Halal cut did not occur till 70 s after the commencement of energy application. However, in practical terms, to ensure an acceptable level of protection of animal welfare, we would recommend a maximum of 45 s between the commencement of energy application and the Halal cut. The presentation of the animal for the neck cut in the DTS system allows for immediate assessment of the severed vessels, and correction of any mis-cut, mitigating the risk of prolonged perfusion of the brain. Nevertheless, operator instructions should include minimisation of the energy application to neck cut interval, and a back-up mechanical stun device available on the bleed-out area, to be used if there are concerns that consciousness may return – e.g. the return of the righting reflex, or the return of the corneal reflex.

Conclusion

This work has confirmed that the animal did not experience pain or distress during the application of the DTS system at 160–200 kJ and 18 kW, for the purpose of pre-slaughter stunning of cattle. All cattle rapidly entered into a tonic then clonic phase within a few seconds (3 and 8 s, respectively), with multiple behavioural observations and EEG confirming a Grand mal seizure, incompatible with consciousness. While the temperature of the forehead may become high enough to result in a superficial burn, the temperature does not begin to rise until after consciousness is lost.

While the animal is undoubtedly unconscious at the time of the neck cut, welfare of the animal requires that the animal become irreversibly unconscious through the lack of oxygen to the brain (lack of blood flow to the brain) before recovery from stunning can occur. The heart beat was strong and blood loss was rapid, leading to cassation of respiratory activity 28–96 s after the neck cut with a staring eye and dilated pupil being observed as early as 38 s after the neck cut. We recommend that the period between the commencement of energy application and neck cut should be no >45 s.

Animal welfare implications (obligatory)

DTS: Diathermic Syncope® could provide a viable alternative to slaughter without prior stunning in commercial beef processing.

Limitations of the study

Animal numbers were strictly limited by the conditions of the Animal Ethics Approval. The study was conducted in a commercial abattoir, producing beef for human consumption. As such, the surgical procedures required to implant fibre optic thermoprobes that would allow direct measurement of temperature changes within the brain or below the skin; or implanted electrodes for measurement of EEG or ElectroCorticograph, were not feasible: both in terms of animal handling for anaesthesia, and the presence of chemical agent residues in the muscles. Furthermore, a commercial abattoir is not an electrically quiet environment, so although a 30 Hz low-pass filter was applied to the EEG data, electrical interference from the environment may have been present, while electromyelographic interference may also have been present, particularly during the tonic and clonic phases.
