Anteroplacement of the Mucocutaneous Junction and Goblet Cell Expansion: Microanatomic Remodeling of the Human Eyelid Margin
Authors: Xiaowei Yang, Meizhen Zeng, Xulin Zhang, Lingyi Liang, Haotian Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe mucocutaneous junction (MCJ) plays a vital role in maintaining ocular surface health and integrity. However, the corresponding histopathological changes of the MCJ in human eyelids remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the pathological alterations of the MCJ and their relationship with meibomian gland (MG) morphology and advancing age.MethodsTarsal plates (TPs) were obtained from 18 cadaveric donors aged 25 to 90 years. Infrared meibography was performed to evaluate MG morphology. Specimens were processed for histology and immunofluorescence to assess lid margin structures. Quantitative analyses of lid margin parameters, including the MCJ displacement, width of MCJ and lid wiper, goblet cell (GC) density in the lid wiper, and the presence of GCs at the MCJ and MG orifice (MGO) were conducted.ResultsHistological analysis identified four progressive grades of MCJ displacement (0–3) in human eyelids, with the average grade increasing significantly with age. In eyelids with MCJ anteroplacement, palpebral conjunctival GC clusters were predominantly located near the anterior lid margin and occasionally extended into MGOs. The muscular conjunctival area ratio of the MG revealed spatially non-uniform distribution patterns in sagittal TP, and, importantly, correlated with the extent of the MCJ shift, indicating that MCJ anteroplacement involves reorganization of the MG within the TP.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that conjunctivalization, accompanied by palpebral GC expansion at the MCJ and MGO, may be a function of age-related MCJ anteroplacement. MCJ anteroplacement and reorganization of the MG provides a plausible histopathological link among lid margin anatomy, MG morphology, and ocular surface ecology.

The inner eyelid margin constitutes a critical anatomic boundary between keratinized skin and mucosal epithelium, playing a pivotal role in maintaining ocular surface integrity and tear film stability.1 Key subzones of the posterior lid margin including the mucocutaneous junction (MCJ), lid wiper, and the meibomian gland orifice (MGO) collectively contribute to tear film homeostasis and ocular surface health.2,3 Anatomically, the MCJ marks the transition from keratinized skin to non-keratinized conjunctiva and defines the boundary where the tear film meniscus meets the cutaneous epithelium.1,4–6 The MGO is situated distal to the MCJ and opens through the cornified epithelium of the skin. Clinically, the position of MCJ in the lid margin is demarcated by Marx’s line (ML), which stains with vital dyes such as lissamine green and rose bengal, appearing as a line of mucosal staining just posterior to the MCJ.7–9 Because MCJ facilitates the continuous distribution and lipid coating of the preocular tear film with every blink, this region is integral to ocular surface health and proper meibomian gland (MG) function.10–13 It has been proposed that alterations in ML may compromise the radius of curvature of tear menisci by reducing volume or curvature. Disruptions to the lid margin, including abnormalities in the ML/MCJ, might compromise the tear distribution and meibum secretion.10,14

Histopathological alterations in the eyelid margin are active drivers of pathology. In a healthy lid margin, the MCJ is located at the conjunctival side of the MGO. However, impression cytology and in vivo confocal microscopy have shown that the MCJ may migrate anteriorly or posteriorly relative to the MGO with aging and under disease conditions.15–17 Clinically, MCJ retroplacement occurs under conditions such as lid margin keratinization secondary to skin diseases (e.g. Stevens Johnson syndrome),18,19 whereas MCJ anteroplacement is more frequently observed in patients with meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD). Prior studies have reported a strong correlation between ML scores and both meibographic and MG secretion scores.12,20 Moreover, a recent study demonstrated that an irregular lid margin is associated with a higher meiboscore independent of age and sex.21 Nevertheless, the pathogenic mechanisms linking anatomic changes in the lid margin to MG alterations remain poorly understood.

With advancing age, the eyelid margin undergoes progressive structural and functional changes driven by intrinsic biological aging processes and extrinsic environmental factors. These alterations include shifts in ML position, MG atrophy, and irregularity of the lid margin.22 Such progressive age-related deterioration of the lid margin may be amplified in disease states such as dry eye. Previous studies have indicated that aging is a major factor influencing the position and width of ML, with anterior displacement of ML becoming more prevalent in older individual.8,23 However, the histopathological evidence and underlying mechanisms of age-related MCJ migration in humans remain unclear. Understanding these age or disease-dependent morphological and physiological changes of the lid margin is essential for elucidating the pathogenesis of lid margin disorders.

To clarify the pathological alterations of MCJ displacement and corresponding morphological changes of MGs in human eyelids, this study examined the histopathological changes in human tarsal plates (TPs). This study examined the histopathological features of human TPs across different age groups. We specifically evaluated the position and width of the MCJ, the density of conjunctival goblet cells (GCs), and the spatial relationship between the MCJ and MG distribution. By correlating these histological parameters with age-related lid margin abnormalities, this work aims to provide novel insight into the pathological basis for lid margin and MG remodeling during aging.

Materials and Methods

Tissue Acquisition and Processing

The use of human tissue in research conformed to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Review Board of Hainan Eye Hospital of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center (2021-008-01). TP tissues were obtained from 18 fresh Asian descent human donors from the eye bank of Hainan province. All body donors had given informed consent. A total of 39 TPs, including both upper and lower eyelids, were isolated from cadavers (15 men and 3 women) aged from 25 to 90 years old with a mean age of 60.3 years, with the postmortem interval ranged from 1.5 to 6.5 hours (mean = 3.3 hours; Supplementary Table S1). TPs were divided into 2 age groups: <60 years (n = 26 TPs); and ≥60 years (n = 13 TPs). Information regarding the medical history is shown in Supplementary Table S1. TP tissues were carefully dissected from the inner epidermis, ensuring that the procedure avoided piercing the epidermis of eyelids. The removal of TPs was performed by a single skillful eye bank staff member under the standard protocol for procurement of ocular tissues as previously reported.24 The excised TP tissue size was standardized across donors (approximately 2.5 cm × 1 cm for the upper TP and 2.5 cm × 0.5 cm for the lower TP). TPs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) immediately for 24 hours.

Meibographic Analysis of Tarsal Plates Specimens

To improve visualization of MGs, TP specimens were flat mounted in phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Meibography was performed on both the conjunctival and muscular sides of TPs using a Keratograph 5M topographer (OCULUS, Germany) under standardized settings by a single experienced operator and cropped to standardized size. All meibograph images were acquired under standardized conditions with a fixed working distance. To calculate the total MG area, the glandular area in meibographs was outlined manually using the lasso tool. The selected area was then converted into a binary format under a consistent zoom. Quantitative analysis of the gland area was performed using Photoshop CS6 software as we previously described.25,26 MG area was measured from binarized meibographs obtained from both the conjunctival (MG areaconj) and muscular sides (MG areamus). The ratio “MG areamus/conj” was calculated as the MG area measured from the muscular side divided by that from the conjunctival side.

Histology and Immunofluorescence

The central one third of each TP was processed for paraffin sectioning. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS). Immunofluorescence was performed as previously described.26 Sections were blocked in 5% horse serum in PBST (PBS plus 0.5% Triton X-100) for 1 hour and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against the following antigens: Keratin 10 (1:500; Abcam); K13 (1:200; Santa Cruz); Keratin 14 (1:500; Biolegend); and MUC5AC (1:1000; Abcam). After PBS washes, slides were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with fluorophore conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cell nuclei were stained with 4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI).

Anatomic Grading of the Location of MCJ

To characterize the corresponding histological changes associated with MCJ displacement, we performed PAS staining on sagittal sections that included the MGO. Clinically, the location of the MCJ relative to the MGO is classified into four grades based on the ML score11 (Fig. 1B). The grading was: grade 0 = ML runs entirely on the conjunctival side of the meibomian orifices; grade 1 = parts of ML meet the meibomian orifices; grade 2 = ML runs through the meibomian orifices; and grade 3 = ML lies on the skin side of the meibomian orifices. Grading was performed in the central thirds of the upper and lower lid. In this study, the grading of MCJ anatomic displacement (Fig. 1B1), which is based on pathological images, also follows the correlation between ML and MGO as defined in the clinical grading criteria.11 We compared MCJ displacement between the two age groups.

Morphometric Analysis of the Microanatomic Structures in Lid Margin

The following parameters were manually outlined with the lasso tool in Adobe Photoshop CC 2019 software and subsequently quantified: MG length, MG width, lid wiper width, MCJ width, and the distance from the skin epithelium to the MGO. Measurements of lid margin zones were performed according to previously described methods.6 Conjunctival GCs were manually identified and counted using NIH ImageJ software (version 1.53). In sagittal histological sections, MCJ width was defined as the linear extent of the transition zone where non-keratinized conjunctival epithelium converts to cornified epithelium.

Masson's Trichrome Staining, Alcian Blue Staining and Alcian Blue - Periodic Acid Schiff Staining

To evaluate changes in the eyelid in relation to morphological changes in MG, Masson's trichrome (MT) staining was performed on 5-µm paraffin sections of TPs using a commercial Trichrome Stain (Masson) Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. In MT-stained sections, muscle fibers, MGs, and conjunctiva appear red, whereas collagen (indicating fibrosis) stains blue. Images were acquired using a Leica DM4B upright digital microscope. To clarify the mucin profile of the lid margin, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections of TPs were stained with a combination of Alcian blue (AB)-PAS or AB only. AB-PAS staining enables the differentiation between acid mucins and neutral mucins, with blue color showing acid mucins, purple color indicating a mixture of neutral and acid mucins, and magenta color indicating neutral mucins alone. The AB and AB-PAS staining were performed following the standard procedures.27,28

Statistical Analysis

Statistical tests were performed using SPSS version 24.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The data were presented as mean ± SD in continuous variables or frequency (percentage) in categorical variables. Comparisons of the MG area from the conjunctival and muscular sides were performed using paired Student's t-tests. For morphometric parameters between two age groups, mixed-effects linear regression models were used for continuous dependent variables, and mixed-effects logistic regression models were used for binary dependent variables to adjust for eye and donor clustering effects. Associations among variables (MG areamus/conj, MG area, MG length, MCJ width, and extent of MCJ shift) were also assessed using mixed-effects linear regression models. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Grading of the Anatomic Displacement of the MCJ in Human Eyelid Tissues

To characterize the corresponding histological changes associated with MCJ displacement, we performed PAS staining on sagittal sections that included the MGO. Histological analysis revealed that the MCJ interfaced with the keratinized epidermis at the proximal margin of the MGO in the inner eyelid (see Fig. 1A). GCs appearing as interspersed clusters extended from the lid wiper across the palpebral conjunctiva. On the free lid margin, the MCJ area is situated at the posterior border of the MGO and interfaces posteriorly with the lid wiper and palpebral conjunctiva, which is composed of squamous surface epithelium and parakeratinized cells (see Fig. 1A1). The MGO is a small opening on the posterior lid margin, lined by stratified squamous epithelium with a keratinized surface that transitions to MCJ (see Fig. 1A2). Beyond the squamous transition zone, the lid wiper epithelium assumes a conjunctival-like appearance, characterized by interspersed parakeratinized cells of flat to columnar shape at the surface, cuboidal cells, and scattered GCs that form a multilayered elevation (see Fig. 1A3).

Considering that the position of the MCJ is clinically visualized using vital staining, referred to as ML, the grading of MCJ displacement is typically adapted based on the location of ML relative to the MGO (ML score; see Fig. 1B). Based on observations from donors aged 25 to 90 years, 4 progressive grades of MCJ displacement (grades 0–3) were identified with representative images shown in Figure 1B1. Anatomically, in grade 0 (normal), the MCJ was located at the posterior lash line, and the cornified epidermis terminated posterior to the MGO. The suprabasal layers of the epidermal conjunctival transitional zone and the conjunctival epithelium were stained dark red by PAS (dashed line, see Fig. 1B1). Grade 1 was defined by anterior shifting of the epidermal conjunctival transitional zone that did not reach the MGO. In grade 2, this transitional zone extended across the MGO. Grade 3 represented anterior displacement of the transitional zone beyond the MGO, accompanied by scattered conjunctival GCs, suggesting significant GC expansion and hyperplasia at the MCJ and inner lid margin. H&E staining confirmed anterior MCJ shifts in both the upper (see Fig. 1C1) and lower (see Fig. 1D1) eyelids of cadaver tissues. The relative position of the MCJ to the MGO (see Fig. 1E) and MCJ width (see Fig. 1F) did not differ significantly between the upper and lower lids. Our results demonstrate the anatomic basis for the varying degrees of MCJ displacement in both the upper and lower lids.

Characteristics of Lid Margin Microstructures Across Different Age Groups

Quantitative analysis of lid margin structures across age groups (<60 years and ≥60 years) revealed significant age associated changes (see the Table). The presence of MCJ shift was significantly higher in the elderly compared with that in donors under 60 years (P = 0.027). In sagittal sections, MCJ width was slightly narrower in older cohorts (mean, SD = <60 years: 307.536, SD = 134.416; ≥60 years: mean = 224.408, SD = 166.168), whereas lid wiper width significantly widened in the elderly group (mean, SD = <60 years: mean = 1.119, SD = 0.295; ≥60 years: mean = 1.373, SD = 0.345, P = 0.047). GC density in the lid wiper also significantly increased with age (mean, SD = <60 years: mean = 10.431, SD = 8.403; ≥60 years: mean = 33.940, SD = 27.824, P = 0.001). Notably, the presence of ectopic GCs at the MCJ significantly increased in the elderly compared with that in donors under 60 years (P = 0.012), with a parallel increase in GC occurrence in the MGO region. These findings indicate that structural remodeling of lid margin involves the MCJ anteroplacement, lid wiper widening, and GC hyperplasia within MCJ epithelium are more frequently observed in the elderly subjects.

Conjunctivalization and Demarcation Clarity in Lids With MCJ Anteroplacement

To assess palpebral conjunctival GC distribution relative to the MCJ and mucin profile, PAS staining was performed. In lids with MCJ anteroplacement, sporadic GCs were observed within the MGO epithelium of both upper (Fig. 2A1) and lower (Fig. 2B1) lids, extending beyond the normal GC containing lid wiper region (see Figs. 2A, 2B). These ectopic GCs were present not only superficially but also in deeper epithelial layers (see black arrowheads in Figs. 2A1, 2B1). Expanded GCs in cases of MCJ anteroplacement exhibited intense AB and PAS reactivity with the AB and AB-PAS method (Figs. 2C, 2D1), indicating that conjunctival mucin was predominantly a mixture of neutral and acid mucins (see red arrowheads in Fig. 2D1), with only scattered GCs positive for neutral mucin alone (see black arrowheads in Fig. 2D1). It suggests that the hyperplastic GCs in the MGO region are capable of normally secreting both acidic and basic mucins. Co-immunostaining for MUC5AC and Krt14 confirmed the expansion of conjunctival GCs forward to the MCJ and MGO epithelia, along with more conjunctival crypts, in cases of MCJ anteroplacement compared with normal lids (Figs. 2E, 2E1). To further delineate the precise anatomic location of the MCJ, we analyzed the expression of cytokeratin 10 (marker of keratinized epithelium) and cytokeratin 13 (marker of conjunctival epithelium). The boundary between Krt10 and Krt13 positive epithelia defined the MCJ (Fig. 2F). In lids with MCJ anteroplacement, Krt13 was overexpressed and Krt10 expression was reduced in the MGO epithelium (see Fig. 2F1). Co-immunostaining for MUC5AC and Krt13 further confirmed the conjunctivalization and GC hyperplasia within the MGO epithelium in lids with MCJ anteroplacement (Fig. 2G1), whereas conjunctival epithelium does not cover the MGO in lids with normal position of the MCJ (see Fig. 2G). Quantification of palpebral GCs in sagittal sections revealed that in individuals over 60 years of age, GC distribution markedly expanded to the lid wiper and MCJ (Figs. 2H, 2I). GCs in the lid wiper increased in subjects ≥60 years old compared with those <60 years old (Fig. 3). These results suggest that conjunctivalization with GC migration and hyperplasia at the MCJ/MGO is associated with MCJ anteroplacement and advancing age.

MCJ Shift Correlates With the Spatial Distribution of MGs in TP

To investigate whether MCJ displacement is linked to morphological changes in MGs, we analyzed correlations between MCJ location and MG area, spatial distribution, and length. Spatial distribution was assessed by calculating the ratio of MG area from the binarized meibographs (Figs. 4C, 4D1) taken on the conjunctival side (MG areaconj; Figs. 4A, 4B) versus the muscular side (MG areamus) (see Figs. 4A1, 4B1) of TPs. The MG areamus/conj ratio revealed heterogeneous MG distribution in sagittal sections (Figs. 4E, 4F): lower ratios indicated MGs located closer to the conjunctiva, whereas higher ratios indicated proximity to the muscular side. Of note, the extent of MCJ shift was correlated with the MG areamus/conj ratio (β = 0.0004, P = 0.048), indicating that anterior MCJ displacement (shorter conjunctival epithelium - MGO distance) was associated with a shift of MGs within the sagittal tarsal plate (Fig. 4G). Masson's trichrome staining further showed that in cases with severe MCJ anteroplacement (grade = 3), MGs exhibited a posterior shift toward the conjunctival fornix (Fig. 4K). In contrast, MG areamus, length, and MCJ width did not correlate with MCJ location (Figs. 4H–J). Severe gland shortening or atrophy was not consistently accompanied by marked MCJ displacement. Thus, the spatial distribution of MGs correlates with MCJ location, suggesting that MG reorganization may be associated with MCJ displacement (Fig. 4L). We propose that positional reorganization of MGs within the TP may biomechanically influence MCJ placement.

Discussion

The inner eyelid margin is a critical, yet often underappreciated, structure for maintaining tear film stability and ocular surface integrity. Changes in the location of ML along the human eyelid margin are common clinical findings associated with MGD. This study is the first to characterize microanatomic changes of the MCJ in human TP tissues. We explored the anatomic basis of MCJ displacement and its correlation with alterations in lid margin parameters and the spatial distribution of MGs. Our data demonstrate that conjunctivalization and GCs distribution within the MGO epithelium advance with MCJ anteroplacement during aging. Furthermore, the position of MGs within the TP also correlates with MCJ anteroplacement. These findings suggest that MCJ anteroplacement may not merely represent an age-related lid margin disorder, but is also accompanied by a coordinated reorganization of the epithelium in the human inner eyelid margin. In addition, changes in the anatomic position of MCJ were correlated with the spatial arrangement of MGs in the TP, indicating that anatomic remodeling of the entire inner eyelid complex – including the MCJ and MGs – may occur with aging.

In the present study, MCJ anteroplacement in both upper and lower eyelids was occasionally accompanied by conjunctivalization of the MGO epithelium in aged subjects. Our findings pathologically support clinical reports that the anterior positioning of ML advances with age.8,12,14,15,29 This finding is also consistent with a prior meeting abstract describing a relationship between MCJ anteroplacement and conjunctivochalasis at the lower lid margin in aging individuals.23 A similar displacement of MCJ has been observed in the eyelids of aged mice.30 Together, these histological and clinical data support the notion that aging serves as an independent risk factor for anterior MCJ shift in both humans and animal models. Similar migration of the MCJ identified by electron microscopic analysis was also observed in patients with marginal entropion – a rarely reported condition in which there is no significant inversion of the eyelid margin, but the MCJ is displaced anterior to the MGOs, resulting in misdirection of the eyelashes.31 In contrast, although posterior lid margin hyperkeratinization can be present in ectropion, no clear association has been established between ectropion or lid laxity and retroplacement of the MCJ.

We surmised that MCJ anteroplacement may be a key driver of peri-orificial conjunctivalization and GC expansion in human eyelids. The conjunctivalization and GC hyperplasia at the MGO could substantially alter the tribological properties of the lid wiper by increasing mucin secretion in this region. On one hand, reduced epithelial stiffness at the lid margin may diminish shear stress during blinking. On the other hand, increased MUC5AC dominated mucus secretion from the MGO could alter meibum viscosity and disrupt lipid spreading within the tear film. These coupled biomechanical and compositional changes resulting from MCJ anteroplacement may collectively affect the tear film overlying the eyelid margin. Clinically, anterior ML location has been positively correlated with lower tear meniscus height, shorter tear breakup time, oily skin, shorter sleep duration, and contact lens wear.29–32 However, the functional consequences of MGO conjunctivalization and GC expansion on ocular surface health require further clinical investigation.

Previous studies have reported that GCs are typically restricted to the human lid wiper and do not extend into the MCJ epithelium.28 In this study, we observed occasional ectopic colonization and GC hyperplasia within the MCJ and MGO epithelium, suggesting a potential dysregulation of conjunctival epithelial cell fate determination and differentiation. Kinetic studies have postulated that the MCJ region harbors a stem cell pool and transient amplifying cells that migrate toward the fornix to renew the conjunctival epithelium.33–35 In pathological states, epithelial progenitors in the MCJ may become perturbed, leading to ectopic GC differentiation. For example, aging- or inflammation-related signals could bias the differentiation of progenitors – via SPDEF and Wnt signaling – toward the GC lineage at the expense of other epithelial cell types, possibly resulting in GCs distribution at ectopic sites like the MGO.36–39 It is plausible that chronic inflammation driven by advanced age drives both the conjunctivalization and the GC hyperplasia within the epithelium of the MGOs. Conversely, clinical studies have shown that keratinization and destruction of the MCJ in conditions such as Stevens Johnson syndrome lead to GC deficiency, contributing to chronic dry eye and corneal scarring.18 Thus, GC expansion and hyperplasia may be viewed as a consequence of disrupted spatial control over stem cell fate specification, reflecting a breakdown of regional identity that leads to lineage infidelity and epithelial displacement at the MCJ.

In addition, we found that anterior MCJ displacement is not merely a change in histological demarcation but reflects anatomic remodeling of the entire eyelid complex, including the MCJ, MGO, and GCs. Our results demonstrate a structurally inter-related relationship between MCJ location and MG morphology. Notably, anteriorization of ML may serve as a practical clinical predictor of reduced tear break up time (TBUT) when screening for MGD in patients with dry eye.12,40 This finding also suggests that MGs and the MCJ/ML do not function as independent anatomic units; rather, they form a coupled “functional complex” under pathogenic stress. This perspective encourages viewing the inner eyelid margin as an integrated, dynamic structure in which morphological changes in one component can trigger cascading effects. For instance, biomechanical forces such as contact lens wear have been shown to alter MG morphology and lid margin thickness,41–43 and eyelid margin vascularity is an important biomarker for MGD diagnosis and management.44 Additionally, the inner lid margin angle becomes rounded or blunted in association with anterior MCJ displacement. Prior studies have reported that irregular lid margins and higher total lid margin abnormality scores correlate with elevated meiboscores.21,45 As the MCJ/ML serves as the outer margin of tear meniscus, meibum naturally enters the top of the tear meniscus if the MGO opens anterior to the MCJ, but flows directly into the tear film if the MGO opens distal to the MCJ (see Fig. 4L). Therefore, longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether movement of the MGO and spatial rearrangement of MGs lead to functional alterations in meibum delivery and spreading on the tear meniscus.

This study has several limitations. First, the absence of donor dry eye history, eyelid surgery history, inflammatory conditions, and medication use limits our ability to link anatomic findings to clinical disease. Second, wide and arbitrary age cutoffs may obscure subtle age‑related differences across conventional age groups. Additionally, the strong male predominance and scarcity of young donor tissues prevents further explicit assessment of sex‑related variation in eyelid structure and aging. Conjunctival GC numbers are known to be influenced by epithelial turnover in inflammatory and other disease states.46 Varying post‑excision times precluded analysis of the link between lid margin cellular proliferation and the observed pathology. The mechanisms driving age-related MCJ anteroplacement and GCs expansion within the MCJ and MGO, as revealed here, remain unclear and warrant further investigation using animal models.

In conclusion, this study presents histopathological evidence of MCJ displacement in human eyelids. We observed that GCs expansion and hyperplasia within the MCJ epithelium and reorganization of MGs within the TP occur in association with MCJ anteroplacement, suggesting that structural reorganization of the MCJ tend to occur more frequently with age. These findings provide novel insight into the pathophysiological basis of microanatomic remodeling of the lid margin with aging. Further investigation into the molecular drivers of these spatial redistributions may uncover the pathogenic mechanisms underlying age-related ocular surface disorders.
