Developing patient-centered educational material to improve colposcopy adherence
Authors: Rida Qureshi, Naomi Peck, Amanda Compadre, Sarah Badwan, Yu Tao, Jingxia Liu, Lindsay Kuroki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility of implementing the American College of Obstetrics and Gynecology (ACOG) colposcopy pamphlet among patients with low socioeconomic status. We then aimed to create new educational material with input from community stakeholders to effectively improve colposcopy knowledge.MethodsWe conducted a multi-phase pilot study at a university-based colposcopy clinic that serves patients with low income who are under- or uninsured. In Phase 1, 25 new patients assessed the ACOG pamphlet with validated measures of Acceptability, Appropriateness, and Feasibility. In Phase 2, we consulted 12 community stakeholders to create Tailored Education to Advance Cervical Health (TEACH). In Phase 3, 25 new patients completed pre-post knowledge tests alongside viewing TEACH and assessed implementation as in Phase 1. One-tailed Wilcoxon signed rank test was used to compare pre- and posttest knowledge scores.ResultsAmong 50 patients enrolled in Phases 1 and 3, median age was 37.6 (IQR = 19.5) and 74% identified as non-White. 76% of Phase 1 participants found the ACOG pamphlet acceptable, appropriate, and feasible. They suggested elaborating on colposcopy’s steps and clinical indications. In Phase 2, stakeholders improved TEACH’s content and delivery. In Phase 3, 88% of participants found TEACH to be acceptable, appropriate (92%), and feasible (96%). Participants’ understanding of colposcopy significantly increased after reading TEACH (medianpre = 8, medianpost = 11, p = 0.00011).ConclusionsWhen designed with patient and community input, educational materials can positively impact patients’ understanding of colposcopy. TEACH should be tested longitudinally in diverse samples to determine its impact on colposcopy adherence.Keywords: Health literacy, Patient education, Cervical cancer screening, Community engagement, Colposcopy adherence, Gynecological cancer, Participatory research

Introduction

In the United States there are over 13,000 new diagnoses and 4,000 deaths attributed to cervical cancer annually. After years of significant declines in incidence and mortality, attributed to the Papanicolaou (Pap) test and human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine, cervical cancer mortality rates have now stagnated in the U.S. (Siegel et al., 2025). This setback is largely driven by disparities in access to adequate cervical cancer screening, diagnosis, and treatment.

Despite the 2030 World Health Organization’s Cervical Cancer Elimination Initiative advising that 90% of women with abnormal cervical cells receive treatment in every country (Organization, 2020), it is estimated that between 25–75% of American women do not receive proper follow up after an abnormal screen (Barlow et al., 2019, Alimena et al., 2023). These rates almost triple in women with low socioeconomic status (SES) (Rojas et al., 2013), who also face the highest rates of late-stage cervical cancer incidence and mortality (Cohen et al., 2023). To address persistent disparities and prevent cervical cancer, we must develop and implement interventions that mitigate social determinants of health and improve patients’ access to follow-up care, especially colposcopy (Martinez-Gutierrez et al., 2023).

Among patients, lack of knowledge is one of the most commonly reported barriers to colposcopy adherence (Khanna and Phillips, 2001). This remains true in qualitative interviews with colposcopy providers (Compadre et al., 2025). Numerous studies have shown that poor understanding of Pap/HPV testing and colposcopy hinder patients’ ability to not only attend appointments but also provide informed consent for the procedure (Krankl et al., 2011). Educational materials have been shown to improve patients’ understanding of colposcopy (Tomaino-Brunner et al., 1998, Marteau et al., 1996, Byrom et al., 2002,), reduce anxiety related to the procedure (Marteau et al., 1996), and promote adherence to colposcopy appointments (Olbrys, 2011). However, most existing health education interventions for colposcopy have not been tailored to meet the needs of patients with low SES. They are often inaccessible in both content and delivery, containing overly complex language and distributed only clinical settings (Helitzer et al., 2009).

Patients’ perspectives can be meaningfully incorporated into educational materials through the well-researched community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach. According to the CDC’s Principles of Community Engagement, in CBPR, researchers and affected community members collaborate on all aspects of a project to effect meaningful change in health disparities (Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry, 2025). For patients with low SES, CBPR provides an opportunity to serve as partners in an intervention rather than participants, thereby diminishing medical mistrust and ensuring successful, long-term implementation (Holkup et al., 2004). Though participatory interventions to improve colposcopy education are gaining traction (Wen et al., 2023, Batman et al., 2023), none to our knowledge have involved patient and community stakeholders in the development of educational materials. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to assess existing educational materials and create, with patient and community input, a new material that improves knowledge of colposcopy among patients with low SES.

This study took place in three phases in a university-based colposcopy clinic that serves patients with low SES who are either underinsured or have no insurance. The first phase aimed to evaluate patient perspectives on the American College of Obstetrics and Gynecology (ACOG) colposcopy information pamphlet. The second phase then aimed to create new educational material titled “Tailored Education to Advance Cervical Health” (TEACH) in partnership with community stakeholders. Lastly, in the third phase we evaluated the efficacy of TEACH using pre-post knowledge tests. We hypothesized that TEACH would significantly improve patients’ understanding of colposcopy.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a multi-phase, mixed methods study at an urban colposcopy clinic in St. Louis, Missouri where 68.4% of patients have Medicaid, 12.4% are uninsured, and 62% identify as Black. Patients were invited to participate in the study if they were newly referred for colposcopy, ≥21 years of age, and able to give informed consent in English. Exclusion criteria included a history of cervical cancer. Over an 11-month period (10/1/20–8/27/21), 153 out of 229 (67%) newly referred patients arrived for their scheduled colposcopy appointment at the study site. Based on the feasibility of recruiting during our grant funding period, we chose a sample size of 25 for Phases 1 and 3.

In Phase 1, 25 newly-referred patients were shown the three-page ACOG colposcopy pamphlet (Supplement Fig. 1) and then asked to complete a 15–20 min paper survey including the validated Acceptability Implementation Measure, Implementation Appropriateness Measure, and Feasibility Implementation Measure instruments. We also asked open-ended questions assessing how the material could be improved and distributed. Using these results, we created the trifold TEACH brochure. We ensured that its content was written at a Flesch-Kincaid 5th grade reading level using Readability Statistics built into Microsoft Word. Due to logistical time constraints within the clinic workflow, we were unable to administer pre-post knowledge tests during Phase 1; as such, we employed a focus group in the next phase to strengthen the validity of our comparison.

In Phase 2, we partnered with the Washington University Center for Community Health Partnership and Research (CCHPR) to host a Community Engagement (CE) Studio of 12 community stakeholders, defined as local individuals with a vested interest in women’s health and improving colposcopy education. In line with the CDC’s Principles of Community Engagement, (Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry, 2025) a trained group facilitator asked participants open-ended questions about both the ACOG and TEACH materials. These questions were developed by the CCHPR and the principal investigator with the goal of collecting actionable feedback (Supplement Table 2). All responses were manually recorded by the facilitator and forwarded to the research team. As per the CCHPR’s terms, no demographic information was collected from CE Studio participants.

In Phase 3, 25 newly referred patients who did not participate in Phase 1 completed a nine-question pre-test assessing their knowledge of colposcopy. They then viewed the TEACH brochure and completed an identical post-test, as well as the validated surveys (same as Phase 1) to assess acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility. Demographic information was collected in both Phases 1 and 3.

Study recruitment

Eligible patients for Phases 1 and 3 were newly referred to our hospital-based colposcopy clinic following an abnormal cervical cancer screen. Patients were excluded if they had a known history of cervical cancer or prior colposcopy; did not have a cervix; or were deemed unable to provide informed consent due to a language or intellectual barrier. Patients’ medical charts were screened prior to their appointment by the study coordinator for eligibility. Eligible participants were approached via phone call prior to their appointment and then invited to participate at their in-person appointment.

TEACH development

TEACH was developed in two phases: first with input from Phase I participants who viewed the ACOG pamphlet, and second with input from Phase 2 community stakeholders. After Phase 1, we created the foundation of the brochure using Canva and Procreate. Text used in TEACH was adapted from the ACOG pamphlet with incorporated feedback from Phase 1 participants such as the procedure’s duration, importance, expected painfulness, and possibility of a biopsy. After Phase 2, we made key edits to the brochure’s design and content, namely changing its color from purple to teal, creating a digital audio version accessible via QR code, switching from a double-sided handout to a trifold, and adding a resource section about United Way 211. The final brochure is available in Supplement Fig. 2.

Data collection

All study assessments were completed through Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure, HIPAA-compliant, web-based data capture and management tool. For their participation, participants in Phases 1 and 3 were compensated with a $25 gift card, while those in Phase 2 were compensated with a $50 gift card.

To measure implementation, three validated, four-item instruments were used in Phases 1 and 3: Acceptability Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), and Feasibility Intervention Measure (FIM) were used (Weiner et al., 2017). For the AIM, IAM, and FIM, average scores were calculated based on the sum of responses given to a 5-item Likert scale (1 = completely disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neither agree nor disagree, 4 = agree, 5 = completely agree). Participants who endorsed “agree” or “completely agree” for all four acceptability items were considered to have found the material acceptable, and the same operationalization was applied to appropriateness and feasibility.

To measure health literacy, we used the validated Single-Item Literacy Screener (SILS), which asks, “How often do you need someone to help you when you read instructions from your doctor/pharmacy?” (Morris et al., 2006). This uses a 5-item Likert scale (1=Never, 2 = Rarely, 3=Sometimes, 4=Often, 5 = Always), with 1–2 coded as “Adequate” and 3–5 coded as “Limited.” Finally, to assess colposcopy knowledge in Phases 1 and 3, we adapted a nine-item instrument employed by Krankl et al in an urban, publicly insured patient population similar to ours (Krankl et al., 2011). One point was awarded per correct answer given for a maximum score of 12.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 29.0.2.0 and SAS version 9.4 were used to perform all statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics were used to report demographic data from Phases 1 and 3. In our primary analysis, a two-tailed Wilcoxon signed rank test was used to compare the difference between total median pretest and posttest scores. In secondary analysis, we descriptively compared completely correct answers given at pretest vs posttest for each question. “Completely correct” was operationalized as an answer that did not deviate from the answer key (Supplement Table 1). We also descriptively compared acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility scores between Phase 1 (ACOG) and Phase 3 (TEACH).

Results

Phase 1: Patient feedback on ACOG pamphlet

A total of 110 patients were screened. Of the 69 who were eligible for the study, 35 were approached, of whom 25 agreed to participate (Fig. 1). Of note, 34 eligible patients cancelled or missed their colposcopies. 84% (21/25) had a high school graduate or GED level of education or more, and 16% endorsed a limited level of health literacy. The median age was 35.6 (IQR = 15.5), 96% (24/25) had Medicare/Medicaid insurance. 80% (20/25) identified as non-White, with all but one of these patients identifying as Black or African American. Overall, 76% (19/25) of participants found the ACOG pamphlet to be acceptable, appropriate, and feasible. Their average AIM, IAM, and FIM scores were 18.16, 18.04, and 18.72, respectively (Table 1). 44% (11/25) endorsed liking the “Reasons for colposcopy” section best out of all sections in the pamphlet. At the same time, 32% (8/25) indicated that its description of colposcopy could improve, particularly in its explanation of the procedure’s importance, length, and risks. Two patients indicated that the font was too small to read.

72% (18/25) of participants completely agreed that they would be more likely to attend their colposcopy appointment after reading the ACOG pamphlet. When asked how it should be distributed, text was least frequently endorsed (36%, 9/25), whereas mail and waiting room copies were most frequently endorsed (64%, 16/25). One patient felt that the information would be better represented in a trifold brochure rather than a double-sided sheet. All distribution preferences are available in Supplement Table 3b.

Phase 2: Community engagement

Twelve community stakeholders were invited and participated in our community engagement studio. Notes from the studio are included in Supplement Table 2. When asked what they liked about TEACH, stakeholders found it to be visually appealing, straightforward, and conversational in tone. They appreciated that the connection between colposcopy and cervical cancer was clear, and that the headers were phrased as realistic questions that patients might have about the procedure. Several stakeholders were excited to see darker skin tones in TEACH’s anatomical drawings, which they said was uncommon in standard health education materials. They also appreciated that TEACH images focused on the patient’s body, whereas the ACOG pamphlet highlighted the provider.

Regarding improvements, stakeholders wanted statistics to emphasize the prevalence of cervical cancer, as well as information about the cost of the procedure. Stakeholders also felt that next steps following an abnormal biopsy could be more clearly underlined. Finally, when asked about how TEACH should be disseminated, stakeholders recommended that it be digitized and narrated for patients with poor literacy or vision. They suggested increasing accessibility by providing TEACH through patients’ electronic portals and/or community health fairs.

Phase 3: Tailored education to Advance cervical health (TEACH)

After incorporating the feedback from our community stakeholders, we modified TEACH and then sought patient feedback and performed pre-post knowledge tests in our colposcopy clinic. A total of 207 new patients were screened from our university-based colposcopy clinic, of whom 118 were eligible, 48 missed or cancelled their appointments, and ultimately 25 consented to participate (Fig. 1).

The median age of the 25 participants was 38.3 (IQR = 19.6), and 68% (17/25) identified as non-White, with all non-White patients identifying as Black. 16% had a limited level of health literacy, all had a high school level of education or higher, and all had public insurance.

80% (20/25) of participants completely agreed that, after reading TEACH, they would be more likely to attend colposcopy. Regarding TEACH’s implementation, 88% found it acceptable, 92% found it appropriate, and 96% found it feasible. Their average AIM, IAM, and FIM scores were 18.32, 18.36, and 18.76, respectively (Table 1). Two patients noted that TEACH should specify how long to avoid intercourse after colposcopy, and two patients were confused by the difference between “risk factors” and “risks.” When asked how TEACH should be distributed, 68% (17/25) endorsed text while only 24% (5/25) endorsed mail (Supplement Table 3b).

Fig. 2. presents the distribution of pretest scores and posttest scores. TEACH significantly increased patients’ understanding of colposcopy from pretest (Median = 8, IQR = 3) to posttest (Median = 11, IQR = 2); [z = -3.7017, S = 115.5, p = 0.00011]. Fig. 3 presents a question-by-question comparison of the percent of patients who provided completely correct answers at pretest vs posttest. Notable improvements were seen in knowledge of the risks of colposcopy (16% pretest to 56% posttest), body parts examined (20% to 44%), reasons why colposcopy is necessary (52% to 80%), solution applied (80% to 100%), what the provider looks for (76% to 92%), and whether a biopsy is taken (56% to 68%). Supplement Fig. 3 represents a more detailed breakdown of knowledge test responses by incorrect vs correct at pretest vs posttest. Questions 3 (“Body parts examined”), 6 (“Biopsy taken”), 7 (“Purpose of colposcopy”), and 8 (“Risks of colposcopy”) had the highest proportion of incorrect answers at both pre- and posttest (48%, 36%, 12%, and 40% of patients, respectively). For Question 3, fourteen patients (56%) gave partially incorrect answers, with the most common being “uterus and cervix”, followed by “cervix” without mention of the vagina. For Question 6, ten patients (40%) incorrectly said “yes” when asked at posttest if a biopsy is always taken during colposcopy. For Question 7, six patients (24%) had partially or completely incorrect answers at posttest, most commonly because they included “to remove HPV” and/or “to remove uterine cancer” in their answer. Finally, for Question 8, eleven patients (44%) had partially or completely incorrect answers at posttest, with six misinterpreting the meaning of “risks” in the question, three providing only one risk, and two answering “I don’t know.”.

Discussion

TEACH, or Tailored Education to Advance Cervical Health, was developed with input from both colposcopy patients and community members. Using the ACOG pamphlet on colposcopy as a guide, TEACH was designed with an emphasis on the importance of colposcopy for cervical cancer prevention, a breakdown of the procedure with patient-centered images, and improved accessibility with audio narration and resources for patients with unmet social needs. The images in TEACH reflected more diverse skin tones and simplified the depiction of body parts examined during colposcopy.

Through pre-post testing, we demonstrated that new patients’ knowledge about colposcopy significantly increased after reading TEACH. Knowledge increases were largest for key concepts relating to informed consent, including what colposcopy entails, indications for colposcopy, and risks. Like the ACOG pamphlet, TEACH was viewed by patients as highly acceptable, appropriate and feasible to implement. Furthermore, nearly all TEACH participants reported being more likely to follow through with colposcopy after reading the material. Finally, participants preferred digital methods of distribution for TEACH.

Our study offers valuable insights into the role that community-based participatory research can play in cervical cancer screening and prevention. Although prior studies have found mixed evidence for the efficacy of education-alone interventions (Varon et al., 2024, Agide et al., 2018, Bonuck et al., 2023), our findings offer a contrasting perspective. We demonstrate that when educational content is tailored to the specific needs of patients—both in content and accessibility—it can significantly enhance their understanding of colposcopy. Other recent efforts to improve cervical cancer screening in patient populations with poor adherence have similarly found success in tailoring their language to be more culturally sensitive (Musa et al., 2017). Taken together, this suggests that current education materials do not cater to patients with low SES and would benefit from following TEACH’s co-design process.

Conventionally, colposcopy education is given at the time of the procedure, but patients retain little information from these encounter-based educational sessions (Pruitt et al., 2005). They have no opportunity to gain reassurance or clarity leading up to the procedure, leading many to search the Internet for answers and encounter misinformation or “worst case scenarios” (Kohler et al., 2023). TEACH, on the other hand, would be accessible to patients prior to their appointment, either digitally via electronic medical record, text, or email, or physically via mail or waiting rooms. Providing this education prior to the appointment would improve patients’ understanding of not only what colposcopy is, but also why it is important, reducing confusion and anxiety. Within our own colposcopy clinic, we are in the process of integrating TEACH as a dot-phrase in our electronic medical record system, allowing clinicians to send this brochure to patients after an abnormal cervical cancer screen. Though there have been mixed results in the past regarding the impact of informational leaflets on colposcopy-related anxiety (Marteau et al., 1996, Howells et al., 1999), these studies did not develop their materials with patient and community input. Our community stakeholders took comfort in TEACH’s patient-centered tone and simplicity and felt it was acceptable to distribute to patients prior to their colposcopy appointment.

In comparison to other popular community-based interventions (patient navigator programs, community health worker outreach, etc.) (Rees et al., 2018, Percac-Lima et al., 2010, Luckett et al., 2015), tailored educational materials like TEACH are much less resource-intensive, making them more feasible to implement in low-resource settings. Moreover, unlike the ACOG pamphlet, the current TEACH brochure does not require a paid subscription to use, as the research team has made it freely accessible online through SimpleBooklet (Supplement Fig. 2). As we look to expand self-collected HPV screening, it is crucial that our patient education expands as well. By embracing digital formats and co-designing processes, educational materials can better meet the needs of patients with low SES who may newly access cervical cancer screening through self-collected means.

This study is strengthened by the active engagement of stakeholders across all stages of TEACH’s design, development, testing, and implementation. Additional strengths of the study include the integration of key implementation outcomes—acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, and effectiveness—assessed using validated instruments (AIM, IAM, and FIM), alongside a pre-post study design to evaluate the impact of TEACH on patient knowledge. Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. Due to funding and time constraints, we employed a small convenience sample of 25 patients who were recruited at their first colposcopy appointment. These criteria inadvertently excluded patients who may frequently miss appointments due to competing priorities, such as unmet basic needs (Kuroki et al., 2021). Moreover, this brochure is tailored to a largely English-speaking, Black patient population, limiting its generalizability to other racial/ethnic subgroups. Finally, this study lacked a comparator group for knowledge scores, as we were unable to give Phase 1 participants pre-post colposcopy knowledge tests due to logistical constraints in-clinic.

Even after reading TEACH, there were some questions that participants continued to answer incorrectly. Occasionally, this was attributable to the nature of the questionnaire’s wording and paper format; for example, many patients interpreted Question 8 (“List two possible risks of colposcopy”) as asking about risks factors for (e.g., smoking, HPV) or consequences of (e.g., cancer, abnormal Pap) cervical dysplasia. Similarly, some patients only circled one answer for questions that asked for two answers, and vice versa. One common misconception that could not be attributed to the questionnaire was the idea that colposcopy examines the uterus or uterine cancer. Though this likely is due to a simple misunderstanding of anatomy, it raises larger concerns that some patients overestimate the capabilities of colposcopy. Therefore, it may be important for providers to differentiate between the gynecologic cancers that colposcopy can and cannot evaluate.

Alongside refining Krankl et al’s original colposcopy knowledge test (Krankl et al., 2011), future studies should utilize a remote format to improve accessibility and show TEACH to a larger patient sample. They should also deploy TEACH prior to participants’ colposcopy appointment to enable measuring visit adherence as a study outcome. Implementing TEACH as one arm of a randomized controlled trial could further enhance robustness and determine if tailored educational materials are demonstrably superior to usual care with the ACOG pamphlet. A mixed methods approach may also enhance robustness, as qualitative interviews may be better suited than quantitative assessments at capturing the perspectives of diverse patients, particularly non-English speaking ones. In that vein, future iterations of TEACH should be tailored to other under-screened subgroups, particularly those who face barriers to accessing colposcopy.

In conclusion, integrating perspectives from under-resourced communities in the development of colposcopy education can substantially enhance patient understanding of the procedure and willingness to attend. While conventional educational materials are generally well-received, patients and stakeholders were enthusiastic about improving their content and delivery. The TEACH brochure incorporated their suggestions and demonstrated a significant improvement from baseline in patient understanding of key colposcopy concepts. Patients overwhelmingly wanted to receive colposcopy education ahead of their appointment and had clear preferences for method of delivery (e.g. online access). These findings support TEACH as a promising, scalable model for future co-designed educational materials.
