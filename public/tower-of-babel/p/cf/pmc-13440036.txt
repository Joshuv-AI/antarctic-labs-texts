Development and validation of a novel multimodal deep neural network model based on CBC digit parameters and scattergrams for rapid hematolymphoid malignancy classification: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Hongyan Liao, Siyuan Chen, Qiang Meng, Mingxia Tang, Shuyi Hou, Jie Zhao, Yan Liu, Qin Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid screening of hematolymphoid malignancies (HMs) by complete blood cell count (CBC) is crucial for choosing the next appropriate workup and initiating timely treatment for critical cases such as acute promyelocytic leukemia. Commonly presented as leukocytosis and abnormal differential, HMs could be misdiagnosed with diverse etiologies including reactive status of bacterial or viral infection, which also require early diagnosis and treatment to prevent severity, such as sepsis. Conventional workflow based on morphology review for HM screening remains time-consuming, labor-intensive, expertise-dependent and subjective. This study aimed to develop and validate an interpretable and cost-effective artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted model, to enable rapid and accurate prediction of HMs mixed with acute bacterial and viral infection, thus informing proper further workup and timely treatment.MethodsWe retrospectively collected a multicenter dataset containing 4996 CBC records from patients presented with leukocytosis and finally diagnosed of either diseases from six prevalent HMs and acute bacterial and/or acute viral infection for model development and validation. This eight-nomial classification model, on a multimodal basis, integrates the digit parameters and its matching WBC differential scattergram from a single CBC test via a convolutional fusion deep neural network, and was compared with the digit- and scattergram-based unimodal model, as well as the current CBC review rules for performance evaluation.ResultsThe multimodal mDNN-cHM outperformed either unimodal model, as is demonstrated by metrics including precision, recall, F1-score and accuracy (all p < 0.05). The model showed striking diagnostic accuracy in both internal and external validations (the area under the ROC curve [AUC-ROC] all above 0.95, ranging from 0.98–1.00 internally and 0.95–1.00 externally). Further, mDNN-cHM demonstrated a higher prediction rate than the flag prompt by the current CBC review rules.ConclusionsOur study presents a proof-of-concept AI classifier for HMs in a real-time manner, which potentially maximizes the diagnostic value of the low-cost and widely-accessible CBC within 0.5 h after sample receipt. The framework also underscores the potential of interpretable AI in accelerating early disease diagnosis, mitigating human error and enlightening clinical decision-making via CBC, which complements and enhances standard-of-care diagnostics, particularly for urgent and high-risk diseases including leukemia/lymphoma and acute infection.

Background

Hematolymphoid malignancies (HMs) are often associated with peripheral infiltration of neoplastic cell [1–3], particularly in cases of acute and chronic leukemias. Lymphoma cells can also spill into the blood in advanced stages, a condition called “leukemic phase of lymphoma”. Under all these conditions, HMs present as leukocytosis is common, referred to increased white blood cells (WBCs) by complete blood count (CBC). While CBC provides evidence for early screening of suspected HM cases1, the final precise diagnosis usually requires further invasive, advanced and costly checkups, which generally take at least 3 days to be completed [4, 5], and also require experienced cytological experts and well-resourced settings. Besides, the following workup leading to a precise diagnosis and prognosis stratification for a certain HM subtype is divergent. For example, acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) usually require the determination of recurrent genetic abnormalities (e.g., fusions and mutations) by molecular and cytogenetic assays, and for chronic leukemia, detection of BCR::ABL1 and myeloid proliferative neoplasm (MPN)-related driver mutations is essential for diagnosis. In addition, rapid classification is crucial to initiate the proper treatment regimen timely for specific HM subtypes. For example, for acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) that has a high early death (ED) rate of 5 to 17.3% [6, 7], recognition of blasts with featured morphology in blood smear and timely initiation of tretinoin could strikingly lower its early and overall mortality. Similarly, for AML and ALL presented with hyperleukocytosis, timely usage of hydroxycarbamide or cytarabine and glucocorticoids is crucial respectively. Therefore, prompt and accurate classification of the time-sensitive HMs by CBC, if possible, could be of utmost importance for an early prediction, as well as triggering the precise subsequent clinical decision with a higher efficiency and lower cost during the initial presentation.

In real settings, cases of HMs may be presented as markedly neutrophilia, lymphocytosis, monocytosis, eosinophilia and/or basophilia, which could mimic the reactive leukocytosis conditions. Among them, acute bacterial infection can lead to leukemoid reaction (LR), representing a non-malignant increase of immature myeloid precursor cells, and often shows a “left-shifted” picture. Untreated bacterial infections (e.g., sepsis, pneumonia) can be life-threatening and remain major global causes of mortality [8, 9]. Acute viral infection, on the other hand, generally causes lymphocytosis, which may also present as leukocytosis thus could interfere with the differential of HMs. Besides, acute viral infections (e.g., influenza and dengue) are also time-sensitive and needs rapid recognition, as they may require supportive care or specific antivirals. Therefore, in general hematology orientations, differentiating leukocytosis could be challenging encountering cases with diverse neoplastic and reactive conditions, in which classification of the time-sensitive cases is of the utmost priority. Misdiagnosis could lead to detoured workup, delayed life-saving treatment and even unnecessary and harmful chemotherapy.

Although CBC test only may not give the final diagnosis, a fast recognition of the suspected cases (within hours) based on automated CBC and morphological findings is possible. Thus, leveraging CBC in a cost- and time-effective manner may facilitate physicians or laboratory technologists to look beyond the common indices or the next prescribed “rule” for the utmost precise diagnosis. Proof-of-concept studies have reported artificial intelligence (AI) models using digit parameters from CBC and other laboratory tests, enabling the prediction, classification, or diagnosis of certain diseases more rapidly and precisely [10–14]. Also, some AI models developed on numerous images from peripheral blood (PB) or BM smear are able to recognize atypical morphologies. However, these models still show limitations, including deviation from real clinical setting, development on relatively small cohorts, reliance on multiple concurrent or subsequent laboratory tests and lack of external validation to generate reliable predictions.

In our previous work, we initially reported an AI-driven CBC framework to quickly and precisely indicate the susceptibility of APL [15]. Inspired by this, here we propose an innovative multimodal deep neural network (DNN) model, named as mDNN-cHM, aiming to classify six prevalent HMs including acute and chronic leukemia, and leukemia-phase lymphoma, and two reactive statuses associated with acute bacterial and viral infection respectively, which all presented as leukocytosis. By incorporating multimodal information from the parameters in digit form and white blood cell differential (WDF) scattergrams in image form, this model specifically outputs the probabilities of the 8 disease types under a multiclass prediction strategy. Further, a rigorous external validation based on an independent multi-center dataset demonstrated the robustness and generalization ability of mDNN-cHM. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first multimodal and multinomial eight-class AI classifier for leukocytosis that relies solely on the routine, rapid and cost-effective CBC test with an excellent performance. This work indicates that by using single real-world CBC data with previously unrealized multimodality, there is an opportunity to develop a cost-effective pre-screening process that distinguishes conditions showing CBC abnormalities, enabling quicker access to advanced diagnostics and targeted treatments.

Methods

Patient cohort

Based on observations of turn-around-time (TAT) from over 300,000 real-world leukocytosis cases in a tertiary diagnostic hematology laboratory (Additional file 1: Fig. S1), the following eight disease categories were included in our proof-of-concept study: AML excluding acute promyelocytic leukemia (non-APL AML), APL, ALL, chronic phase-chronic myeloid leukemia (CP-CML), breakpoint cluster region::abelson 1 negative myeloproliferative neoplasm (BCR::ABL1- MPN), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), acute bacterial infection, and acute viral infection. These six HMs were selected because they represent the most common and clinically urgent causes of leukocytosis requiring rapid differentiation. Lymphoid malignancy in this study is represented by CLL, a leukemic‑phase lymphoma (i.e., cases with peripheral blood involvement). Patients with history of receiving any disease­modifying therapy before blood sampling were excluded.

Disease diagnosis

The diagnosis of HMs was based on the 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of hematolymphoid tumors [16].

For infection diagnoses, the following criteria were applied. Bacterial infection was confirmed by positive culture from a normally sterile site, or supported by microbiological or molecular detection. Viral infection required detection of viral nucleic acid or antigen, in the absence of concurrent bacterial infection. Patients with mixed bacterial–viral infections were classified as bacterial infection. For patients with concurrent HMs and acute infection, the final diagnosis was assigned based on the dominant clinical presentation at the time of sampling. If infectious symptoms predominated, the case was labeled as infection; if malignancy‑related concerns predominated, the case was labeled as the respective HM category. This approach ensures that our labels reflect the real‑world clinical decision-making scenarios.

To build the mDNN-cHM, all CBC results with both digits and scattergrams were randomly split into a training set and a testing set at a ratio of 4:1 in each fold without any duplication, and a five-fold cross-validation was performed (Fig. 1A, middle panel). The random data division, notably, is based on the patient number rather than the number of CBC results due to the fact that some patients may have multiple and thus unequal numbers of records. In that case, records from the same patient would not be used for training and internal validation simultaneously to prevent from data leakage. Internal validation in each fold was fulfilled by testing on the rest data after the four-fifths been used to train the model.

Ethics statement

All included participants signed an informed consent form. The study protocol was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital of Sichuan University.

Multimodal CBC data set

For each CBC record, twenty-eight digit parameters at initial presentation were retrieved as the digit data, including WBC count, red blood cell count (RBC), platelet count (PLT), percentages and absolute counts of neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, eosinophil, basophil, immature granulocyte (IG) and nucleated red blood cell (NRBC), percentage of blast, percentage of atypical lymphocyte, impedance platelet count, percentage of small red blood cell, hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular Hb content (MCH), mean corpuscular HGB concentration (MCHC), RBC distribution width-coefficient of variation (RDW-CV) and RBC distribution width-standard deviation (RDW-SD). Meanwhile, the matching scattergram from the WDF channel of the automated hematology analyzer (AHA) (Sysmex XN series, Kobe, Japan) was collected as the image data, thus forming the multimodal dataset (Fig. 1B, left panel).

Development of the deep learning algorithm

The step-by-step workflow of the proposed model has been shown in Fig. 1B. The mDNN-cHM starts from two original feature generation operators with each pathway grabs information to produce the digit and image embeddings, respectively. The digit embedding will be fed into the simplified factorization machine [17], which consists of adding and inner product calculations, to model the low order relationship among embeddings. Then, both embeddings will firstly be fed into the hidden layer for further extraction of high order features full of semantic information, and then softmax, the output head to produce category decision. A detailed description of the algorithm development is provided in the Additional file 2: Algorithm development.

Data preprocessing, missing data handling and class imbalance management

To improve the model’s ability to handle data variations during training, numerical parameters were normalized. Scattergrams were loaded and standardized using the MONAI (medical open network for AI) library. The preprocessing pipeline included intensity normalization to the range [0, 1] using ScaleIntensity. For data augmentation, random spatial transformations were applied, including random flipping, random rotation within ± 15 degrees, and random zooming with a scale of 0.9 to 1.1, all composed sequentially using MONAI’s Compose. During testing, no randomness-introducing preprocessing was used to ensure result consistency and comparability.

All images included in training and testing had valid input channels with no missing data. For occasional missing digit values, a zero-filling strategy to maintain a consistent input format during both training and evaluation was executed.

Class imbalance was addressed primarily through weighted performance metrics during evaluation, which weight each class by its population to avoid bias toward dominant classes. For training, cross-entropy loss with L1 regularization was used. Focal loss designed to alleviate class imbalance by down-weighting well-classified examples was also experimentally evaluated; however, this did not yield clear or consistent improvements in the prediction performance. Thus, cross-entropy was retained as the main loss function.

Hyperparameter tuning

To ensure sufficient data volume and computational efficiency, the batch size was set to 60 and the training epoch 500. As a critical parameter in DL training, an initial learning rate of 1e–4 was chosen, which is commonly used to ensure quick convergence of the model. To adjust the learning rate dynamically, a decay factor (gamma = 0.9) was introduced, which gradually reduces the learning rate during training to maintain model stability and accuracy.

Results

Performance comparison of the multimodal mDNN-cHM with models based on unimodal CBC data in HM classification

In our previous work, we initially built a CNN (Convolutional Neural Network)-based model for early APL screening based on CBC WDF scattergram only, i.e., data of single modality. To examine whether the introduction of multimodal data would bring superior performance, we simultaneously built the multimodal mDNN-cHM, together with two unimodal models using the digit parameters and WDF scattergram respectively. As expected, the precision, recall, F1-score and accuracy of both the mDNN-cHM and scattergram-based model were all significantly higher than the digit-based model (all p < 0.0001) in both the 5-fold cross-validation and external validation (Fig. 2). Notably, although mDNN-cHM and the scattergram-based model showed comparable overall performance in the internal validation and precision in the external validation, the former showed significantly higher recall, F1-score and accuracy in the external validation (p < 0.01, p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively). To further evaluate center-specific performance, we stratified the external validation results by individual center. As shown in Additional file 5-6: Fig. S3-S4, the model maintained consistent discriminative ability across all four centers. Considering variations in disease distribution (e.g., fewer rare malignancy cases at the smaller center, CSPH) may exist in real-world clinical practice, this result confirms that the model’s overall performance is not driven by any single center and supports its generalizability across diverse settings.

For better visualization, we embedded the classification results into a much lower two dimensions using t-distributed stochastic neighborhood embedding (t-SNE), during which principal component analysis was used to initialize the embedding space for visualization (fold 3 shown in Fig. 3). Consistent with the confusion matrix, APL and viral infection showed obvious aggregations while staying distant from other diseases. However, BCR::ABL1- MPN and CP-CML overlapped in the feature space, indicating potential misclassifications. Bacterial and viral infection were both located in different regions from all the HMs. Partially due to protocol differences from multiple centers, the distribution pattern from the external validation was not entirely concordant with that of its corresponding internal one. Nonetheless, their aggregation effects were similar, indicating the robustness and generalization of mDNN-cHM in the disease classification.

A fully connected layer was inserted at the end of the convolution sequence to map the learned distributed feature representation to the classification label space and convert it to a prediction score using the softmax function. To use the mDNN-cHM in real clinical scenarios, the highest prediction score associated with one disease type indicated a greater probability of leukocytosis cause from that rather than the rest seven categories (Fig. 3).

Notably, although mDNN-cHM showed overall outstanding metrics reflecting higher sensitivity, accuracy, precision and specificity, there were variances in the classification performance of each disease type. For example, none cases from APL and viral infection were misclassified in the external validation. 4 cases (19.05%) of ALL (n = 21) were misclassified as non-APL AML and 2 cases (9.52%) as BCR::ABL1- MPN, but not other diseases. Major misclassifications of CP-CML (n = 78) involved BCR::ABL1- MPN (7 cases, 8.97%) and bacterial infection (9 cases, 11.54%). Similarly, significant confusion of BCR::ABL1- MPN (n = 106) included bacterial infection (15 cases, 14.15%) and CP-CML (6 cases, 5.66%). Examples of the misclassified cases were displayed with the original CBC results shown in Additional file 7: Fig. S5. Among CP-CML cases misclassified as bacterial infection, two had concurrent acute myocardial infarction, and three were post‑TKI (tyrosine kinase inhibitor) therapy with morphological normalization of the peripheral blood smear. In all five cases, blinded review by expert hematologists confirmed that the morphological features were indistinguishable from reactive neutrophilia, highlighting the inherent diagnostic challenge.

To assess the impact of repeated measurements, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to the first CBC sample per patient (Additional file 8: Table S2 and Additional file 9: Fig. S6). This analysis confirms that our results are not biased by repeated sampling and that the model is robust for first‑time patient evaluation.

A similar pattern of the PR curves of mDNN-cHM was seen in the internal and external validation (Fig. 4), indicating its ability to maintain a high precision while achieving a high recall. In this 8-class clinical classification task, the random chance probability is only 1/8 = 0.125, which imposes a fundamental lower bound on the model’s predicted confidence scores. As has been discussed previously [18], traditional calibration metrics including Expected Calibration Error, Brier score, and reliability diagrams can be misleading for a high-dimensional multi-class task. To formally examine calibration, we applied TemperatureScaling [18], the most widely used and effective post-hoc calibration method for neural networks. The learned temperature parameter was ≈ 0.92, which was close to 1. This result indicates that the model without further adjustment is already well-calibrated.

The digit-only model, relying solely on CBC parameters, yields moderate but stable performance (F1 = 0.71) across both datasets, serving as a robust baseline. Discussion

CBC is one of the most commonly ordered tests in clinical laboratory. CBC results provide evidence for disease screening and checkups, initiating practical diagnostic algorithms and thereby assisting in diagnosis [23]. Building upon emerging evidence positioning the CBC as a critical screening tool, recent advances in machine learning are reshaping the diagnostic landscape [24]. In this study, we innovatively present mDNN-cHM based on the multimodal CBC data, a proof-of-concept model as a classifier for HMs and reactive conditions of leukocytosis with high prevalence or overlapping clinical manifestations. Notably, mDNN-cHM generated consistent results in the external validation on an independent dataset collected from four centers, thus demonstrating robust performance and generalizability.

Previous studies have reported a potential association of abnormal WDF scattergrams and specific conditions, including APL [15], red cell lysis [25], SARS-Covid-2 infection [26], infectious mononucleosis [27], and polyclonal B-cell lymphocytosis [28]. One recent study has focused exclusively on classification of acute leukemia by a model named AI-PAL [12]. However, a potential limitation of these studies may lie in the fact that general hematology orientation also requires differential leukocytosis diagnosis including chronic leukemia and lymphoma (in the form of leukemia). In addition to these more prevalent leukemias, it might be possible to obtain a fast diagnosis of the BCR::ABL1+ (Ph+ ALL and CML) from BCR::ABL1- myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN). As one of the most common lymphoid disorders, CLL could significantly interfere with diagnosing the benign and other malignant conditions [29]. Other studies tended to do a binary task, with one developed an algorithm differentiating myeloid disorders and leukemoid reactions [30], and another one HMs and normal [31], with both relying on digit parameters only. Thus, mDNN-cHM is currently the first AI model designed to distinguish the heterogeneous leukocytosis conditions consisting of both the neoplastic conditions of six prevalent HMs and the reactive statuses (bacterial and viral infections). This work represents an important step towards precise and timely differentiation of diverse leukocytosis etiologies based on routine CBC test only and without further reliance on any other biomarkers.

Our framework is in contrast to previously reported AI models even with newly designed architectures, as they tended to focus only on mining information from parameters of single modality [32–34]. The superiority of mDNN-cHM largely owes to the use of the inherent multimodal data from each single CBC test, which enables the digit and image complement for each other. In detail, the digit parameters provide detailed quantitative data, including normal or abnormal/atypical cell counts and percentages, and RBC/PLT indexes. Meanwhile, as the WBC morphologies and structures vary, the WDF scattergrams may show different patterns of cell distribution, reflecting “qualitative” features and cell heterogeneity. Also, digit-based unimodal models may lack sensitivity to subtle morphological differences (e.g., atypical cells in early phase of infection), and scattergram-based models may not capture the dynamic quantitative information. Thus, mDNN-cHM may classify cases that are not that “standard”. For example, it can predict suspect cases with any parameter in CBC already at a critical value, for example, PLT < 5 × 109/L while the cell distribution remains normal in the WDF scattergram. In addition, APL with a relatively low WBC count may still show scattered points in the blast area on the scattergram that are easily overlooked by interpretation with human eyes but not mDNN-cHM. Therefore, our concept of fusing digit parameters and the “buried” medical images (scattergrams) from a single CBC test as multimodal data may add remarkable contributions to the future AI model development for disease screening, differential and even a final diagnosis.

It is noteworthy that variances in the classification performance of each disease type were observed, possibly due to the disease inherent similarities. For example, CP-CML without showing prominent basophilia and marked leukocytosis can mimic leukemoid reactions including bacterial infections. Indeed, even human experts might misclassify them due to a morphologic ambiguity; for example, the dysplastic neutrophils in CML may resemble infection-related toxic changes. We acknowledge that the model shows some misclassification between CP-CML, BCR::ABL1- MPN, and bacterial infection. However, the current clinical diagnostic pathway mandates BCR::ABL1 testing for any suspected MPN or CML, regardless of model output. Thus, our model functions as an early triage tool that can enhance, rather than replace, the standard diagnostic workup. Additionally, mDNN-cHM itself may be limited by the quality of the training data, which probably should also include the atypical cases. Future application of mDNN-cHM will prioritize recognition of these “stealth” myeloid neoplasms through strategic adding atypical case to the training datasets.

Current CBC review rules typically rely on simplistic heuristics, struggle with nonlinear or multifactorial disease patterns, and carry a substantial risk of false negatives, particularly for malignancies with atypical presentations (e.g., early-stage leukemia, hypogranular APL, or CLL without marked lymphocytosis). In contrast, mDNN-cHM showed a superior recall, which directly translates to a reduced missed-detection rate for high-risk HMs, particularly APL, when a rapid diagnosis is lifesaving. Furthermore, by direct outputting a probability for each disease type included rather than general flags, mDNN-cHM enables prioritized actions for review and report release. Therefore, it can be integrated into the laboratory information system as a module to provide real-time decision suggestions. mDNN-cHM also reduces the inherent differences between observers in manual result interpretation and morphology review, thereby achieving standardization. The fully automated mDNN-cHM offers incomparable scalability for laboratories by remarkably reducing TATs and mitigating staff burdens. Therefore, mDNN-cHM can be configured immediately after CBC outputs and before the against-the-rule–initiated recheck and morphologic review actions.

Our model is intended as an early warning and triage support tool, not a replacement for the full diagnostic workup. By providing real‑time probabilistic predictions, mDNN‑cHM can help prioritize cases for review, particularly in settings with limited hematopathology expertise. This supportive role is especially valuable in resource‑limited or less experienced laboratories, where expert MICM (morphology, immunophenotyping, cytogenetics, and molecular testing) assessment may not be immediately available.

From a technical implementation perspective, our model employs a dual‑path architecture: one path processes WDF scattergrams, and the other handles digit parameters. The overall design is intentionally compact and computationally efficient, with modest resource requirements that make it suitable for deployment even in resource‑limited settings. Accordingly, the model can be packaged as a lightweight containerized application, facilitating compatibility and seamless integration with common laboratory information systems (LIS).

To prohibit the misclassified cases and to generalize it in a more complicated real clinical context, further fine-tuning of the model is warranted. Due to variabilities in hospital scale, data from the external centers were merged as one external dataset. Future refinement would involve more cases from more centers, in consideration of factors associated with the instrument and workflow.

We acknowledge that the eight predefined categories in the current eight‑nomial classification task do not fully capture the complexity of real‑world leukocytosis etiologies. In clinical practice, HMs could be complicated with ongoing infections at initial evaluation of disease presentation. A distinguished percentage of viral infections could develop bacterial infection in clinical course. Besides, leukocytosis could be associated with a wider spectrum of diseases, such as steroid-induced leukocytosis, autoimmune diseases, solid tumor, chronic infection, and other reactive states. Moreover, the model’s ability to identify atypical or early‑stage presentations (e.g., hypogranular APL or AML with normal WBC count) has not been systematically evaluated, as our dataset was restricted to leukocytosis cases (WBC > 10 × 10⁹/L). Therefore, our model currently addresses the question “which of these eight categories is most likely?” rather than a comprehensive diagnostic triage tool. Future work will include an “other leukocytosis” category and evaluate the model in a consecutive, unselected cohort to better assess its real-world performance.

Our study excluded patients who had received disease‑modifying therapy before blood sampling. While this was necessary to establish a clean baseline for model development, it limits applicability to relapsed/refractory patients or those with prior treatment. Future prospective validation should include treated populations to assess model performance in real‑world clinical settings.

Conclusions

To sum, mDNN-cHM establishes unprecedented multimodal fusion of CBC digit parameters and WDF scattergrams as a powerful paradigm for automated leukocytosis classification. Its superior accuracy and generalizability represent a substantial advance toward more efficient hematological diagnostics. With the broad availability of CBC, mDNN-cHM could help guide urgent therapies in settings without cytological expertise, such as in low-resource areas or outside routine working hours. By providing direct, precise and actionable insights, this tool has the potential to transform the current hematology workflow, accelerate life-saving interventions, and ultimately improve patient care. Ongoing refinement and prospective integration into clinical practice are vital next steps that should add more flexibility and accuracy in handling complex and diverse data.
