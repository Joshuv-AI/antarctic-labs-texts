Tolerability of hormonal treatments in acute hepatic porphyria patients
Authors: Andjela Arandjelovic, Charlotte Ross, Gayle Ross
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute hepatic porphyria (AHP) flares have been associated with female hormones and the use of hormonal treatments. Women with AHP are traditionally advised to avoid exogenous oestrogen and progesterone, limiting options for contraception and management of menorrhagia, dysmenorrhoea and menopausal symptoms. Evidence on specific hormonal therapies is limited; a 2003 study found that 25% of women with acute intermittent porphyria (AIP) experienced attacks with contraceptives containing progesterone, oestrogen or both.AimTo update the current understanding of the tolerability of hormonal treatments in women with AHP.MethodsAn anonymous questionnaire was distributed to women with AHP (acute intermittent porphyria, hereditary coproporphyria and variegate porphyria) via hospital records and a national patient support group, capturing prior hormonal therapy use and associated flares.ResultsThirty responses were analysed; 23 participants had used hormonal therapies. Flares were most frequent in hereditary coproporphyria (66%) and less common in variegate porphyria and acute intermittent porphyria (~20%). No flares were reported with hormone replacement therapy, the progesterone‐only pill or progesterone implants. Flares occurred with combined oral contraceptives (46.6%) and progesterone‐containing intrauterine devices (28.5%), particularly drospirenone‐containing pills. Levonorgestrel‐based therapies, including the Mirena IUD, were better tolerated and often improved symptoms.ConclusionsAlthough limited by sample size and retrospective design, this study provides clinically useful data on the tolerability of specific hormonal therapies in women with AHP, supporting personalised prescribing and patient counselling for contraception and symptom management.Keywords: acute hepatic porphyria, hormonal treatment, acute intermittent porphyria, hereditary coproporphyria, variegate porphyria

Introduction

Acute hepatic porphyrias (AHPs) are a group of rare, inherited metabolic disorders caused by genetic defects in the haem biosynthetic pathway. These defects lead to the accumulation of porphyrin precursors, which build up in multiple organs. These precursors are neurotoxic and can cause a range of symptoms, including neuropathic abdominal pain, headaches, nausea, sun sensitivity and fatigue. In some cases, acute attacks can occur, presenting with severe manifestations such as seizures, respiratory failure and, rarely, death. Acute attacks are more frequently observed in females and can be triggered by various factors, including certain medications, stress, fasting and hormonal changes. Porphyrias may be classified as latent or manifest; latent porphyria refers to individuals who carry a pathogenic mutation but remain asymptomatic, whereas manifest porphyria describes individuals who express clinical disease, including neurovisceral attacks.

A potential association between acute attacks and hormonal contraceptive use was first suggested in the 1970s. However, few studies have systematically investigated the effects of specific hormonal contraceptives across different subtypes of AHP. 1 Animal studies have shown that both oestrogen and progesterone can upregulate ALA synthase activity and stimulate haem synthesis, suggesting a possible mechanism by which these hormones may provoke porphyria symptoms. 2 Anecdotal evidence indicates that tolerance to hormonal therapies varies significantly among individuals with porphyria.

Given the wide variability in hormonal treatment options now available, there is a critical need to identify whether specific hormonal agents correlate with symptom flares in various AHP subtypes, including acute intermittent porphyria (AIP), hereditary coproporphyria (HCP) and variegate porphyria (VP).

Currently, the literature in this area is sparse and primarily consists of isolated case reports documenting symptom exacerbation following the use of oestrogen‐ or progesterone‐containing medications. 1 , 2 , 3 The only major survey addressing this issue was conducted in 2003 by a Swedish research group. 4 That study focused exclusively on women with AIP, comparing outcomes between those with manifest disease and those with latent gene carriage. It found that approximately one in four women with manifest AIP experienced an attack after starting oral contraceptives. The authors advised continued caution with all hormonal therapies, although they noted a lower risk associated with intrauterine devices and oestradiol‐containing patches or pills. 4

Our study sought to update and broaden the current understanding by examining the impact of hormonal treatments on all forms of AHP, not just AIP. Our objective was to report flare rates associated with a range of hormonal agents across different porphyria subtypes.

Current guidelines, such as the Cardiff Porphyria Service's safe drug list, generally recommend avoiding all oestrogen‐ and progesterone‐containing therapies in porphyria patients. 5 , 6 , 7 This restricts treatment options for hormone‐related conditions, including menorrhagia, dysmenorrhoea and menopausal symptoms. If our research identifies patterns of safer use, more individualised and evidence‐based guidance could be provided, offering improved quality of life for patients with AHP.

We hypothesised that some hormonal medications might, in fact, be used safely in select patients with AHP. By identifying safer options, we hoped to empower patients with more effective strategies not only to manage their porphyria by stabilising hormonal fluctuations but also to treat other common hormone‐related health concerns, concerns that are often left untreated due to the perceived risks associated with hormone use in this population.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study was conducted using a structured questionnaire. Patients in Australia with a biochemically confirmed diagnosis of AHP, based on plasma, urine or faecal porphyrin testing, and listed on the centre's porphyria registry were invited to participate either in person or via email. To increase numbers, the study was advertised in the Australian Porphyria Association newsletter. Patients were asked whether they had previously experienced symptoms to determine whether they were latent or manifest.

Inclusion criteria

Females, aged 16 years or older. Sixteen was selected as the lower age limit based on the potential for patients to be deemed Gillick competent or mature minors, as individuals in this group may have already experienced porphyria symptoms or menstrual issues and initiated hormonal therapy.

Exclusion criteria

Females under the age of 16 and individuals assigned male at birth who were not receiving female hormone therapy, as they were less likely to have experienced hormone‐related porphyria symptoms.

Given the rarity of AHP, a target sample size of 20–30 participants was considered realistic, with the understanding that recruiting larger numbers might be challenging.

All participants received both written and verbal information about the study. Upon providing informed consent, participants could access the questionnaire through a secure REDCap link or QR code. Paper versions were made available for those who lacked access to compatible digital devices.

Given the exploratory design and observational nature of the data, a formal power calculation was not performed. Data were analysed descriptively using simple summary statistics, including percentages.

Ethics statement

Ethics approval was obtained from the Royal Melbourne Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC 2024.054).

Results

A total of 30 individuals with AHP completed the survey, comprising 12 (40%) with AIP, six (20%) with HCP and 12 (40%) with VP (Table 1). Of all respondents, 23 (76%) reported having used at least one form of hormonal therapy, while seven (24%) had never used hormonal treatments. The age distribution of respondents was as follows: three were aged 16–30 years, two were aged 31–40 years, 12 were aged 41–50 years, four were aged 51–60 years, four were aged 61–70 years, and five were aged over 70 years.

Recall differed across contraceptive and hormonal therapy classes. Among COCP users, 10 of 15 participants recalled at least one brand, most commonly Yaz (n = 3; drospirenone/ethinylestradiol) and Yasmin (n = 2; drospirenone/ethinylestradiol). Other reported COCPs included Microgynon and Levlen (each n = 2; levonorgestrel/ethinylestradiol) and Diane (n = 2; cyproterone acetate/ethinylestradiol), with fewer participants recalling Zoely (n = 1; nomegestrol acetate/estradiol) and Estelle (n = 1; cyproterone acetate/ethinylestradiol). Notably, both participants who reported Microgynon indicated that it did not precipitate a porphyria flare; however, comparative tolerability between COCPs could not be determined, as many participants had trialled multiple formulations and drug‐specific flare data were not systematically captured.

Recall of the progesterone‐only pill was limited (1/2; Primolut, norethisterone), and no participants identified the contraceptive implant that was used (0/1). In contrast, recall of intrauterine contraception was universal, with all participants (6/6) reporting Mirena (levonorgestrel‐releasing intrauterine system). For menopausal hormone therapy, two of five participants recalled a brand (Estradot patches, estradiol; Premarin, conjugated equine oestrogens); neither reported an associated flare.

Flares associated with hormonal treatments

Across all hormonal treatment types, 10 flares were reported among 33 total treatment exposures (30.3%) (Table 3). The COCP was associated with the highest number of reported flares, with eight flares recorded across 18 COCP brands used (44%). In contrast, no flares were reported with the progesterone‐only pill, etonogestrel implant or MHT. Levonorgestrel‐containing IUDs were associated with two flares among seven exposures (29%).

Hormonal treatments reported as tolerable or symptom‐improving

Discussion

This survey provides new insights into the tolerability of commonly used hormonal therapies among individuals with AHP. Despite longstanding concerns regarding the porphyrinogenic potential of exogenous hormones, particularly oestrogen‐containing therapies, our findings suggest that tolerability varies considerably by hormonal treatment type and porphyria subtype. A substantial proportion of respondents in this cohort reported prior hormonal therapy use; however, given the self‐reported design, this likely reflects selection bias and does not permit inference regarding the prevalence of use in the broader AHP population. Nonetheless, these findings underscore the clinical importance of defining safe and effective options for contraception, fertility management and menopausal symptom control in this group.

Gonadal hormones such as oestrogen and progesterone influence porphyria by altering hepatic haem biosynthesis, which is already compromised in AHP. Oestrogen can induce hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes, increasing haem utilisation and reducing negative feedback on ALA synthase 1 (ALAS1), thereby promoting accumulation of neurotoxic haem precursors. 8 Progesterone may also contribute by directly inducing ALAS1, providing a biological basis for the cyclical, premenstrual flares observed in some patients. 9 , 10 However, the porphyrinogenic impact of exogenous hormones is highly dependent on dose, formulation and route of administration.

Consistent with previous literature, COCPs were the hormonal therapy most frequently associated with reported flares. In a Swedish population‐based retrospective study, oral contraceptives were thought to precipitate attacks in 24% of women with previously manifest AIP and were implicated in triggering the first attack in 18%, with combined formulations posing a greater risk than progesterone‐only preparations. 1 Our findings align with this overall pattern, with nearly half of COCP exposures being associated with a flare. Notably, however, the risk in our cohort appeared most pronounced among individuals with HCP, in whom three‐quarters of COCP uses were associated with an attack, rather than among those with AIP as reported previously. This discrepancy may reflect subtype‐specific differences in hepatic enzyme susceptibility or the relative biochemical instability of HCP. In contrast, flare rates among individuals with AIP and VP were lower, though still clinically relevant. Collectively, these findings reinforce existing recommendations to exercise caution when prescribing COCPs in AHP, particularly in patients with HCP.

Although progesterone has historically been regarded as more porphyrinogenic than oestrogen, progesterone‐only contraceptive methods, including the progesterone‐only pill and the etonogestrel implant, were not associated with any reported flares in this cohort. 11 , 12 Together with findings from Andersson et al., this suggests that progesterone‐dominant or oestrogen‐sparing regimens may represent safer contraceptive alternatives for many patients with AHP. 1 Levonorgestrel‐releasing intrauterine devices were also generally well tolerated, though isolated flares occurred in users with AIP and HCP. This variability underscores the heterogeneous clinical expression of porphyria and the necessity of individualised hormonal decision‐making.

MHT was uniquely well tolerated across all porphyria subtypes in this survey, with no respondent reporting a flare, consistent with prior population‐based data. 1 This is clinically significant given the need for safe menopausal symptom management in women with AHP. Despite containing both oestrogen and progesterone, modern MHT regimens typically involve lower, more stable hormone doses and frequently utilise transdermal delivery, thereby avoiding first‐pass hepatic metabolism and cyclical progestin exposure seen with COCPs. 13 These pharmacological characteristics likely contribute to their favourable tolerability profile.

Importantly, emerging evidence from assisted reproduction further supports the concept that hormonal exposure does not uniformly precipitate attacks in AHP. A recent case series examining women with AHP undergoing in vitro fertilisation (IVF) and ovarian stimulation, procedures involving substantial hormonal manipulation, reported no severe porphyria attacks when treatment was carefully planned and monitored in collaboration with porphyria specialists. 14 Similarly, pregnancy, despite sustained physiological elevations in endogenous oestrogen and progesterone, has been reported to be well tolerated in many women with AHP, particularly when disease is stable prior to conception and managed with specialist input. 15 This challenges the assumption that all high‐dose or dynamic hormonal interventions are contraindicated in AHP and reinforces the role of personalised risk assessment and specialist oversight.

The observation that certain hormonal treatments were perceived as helpful or non‐triggering by participants provides valuable real‐world context. Although subjective, these experiences highlight marked interindividual variability in hormonal sensitivity and support the feasibility of tailored hormonal strategies. Some COCP formulations were tolerated by individuals with AIP and VP, while progesterone‐only preparations and MHT demonstrated consistent tolerability across subtypes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The small sample size reflects the rarity of AHP and limits statistical power, while reliance on self‐reported data introduces potential recall bias and misclassification of flares. The self‐referral component further contributes to selection bias, and the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference and detailed assessment of dose–response relationships. In addition, incomplete recall of specific hormonal therapy brands and formulations limited the level of detail in exposure characterisation. Although some participants were able to identify treatments used, this was not consistent across the cohort, limiting the ability to evaluate differences in oestrogen or progesterone dose, route of administration or formulation characteristics, and precluding reliable stratification by older versus newer‐generation therapies. Given that the porphyrinogenic potential of hormonal treatments is likely influenced by dose, pharmacokinetics and hepatic metabolic effects, this represents an important area of uncertainty. Nonetheless, these findings contribute meaningful real‐world data to a limited evidence base and highlight clinically relevant patterns that warrant further prospective investigation with more detailed treatment characterisation.

This study focused specifically on AHP, given their well‐established association with neurovisceral attacks precipitated by hormonal and metabolic triggers. In contrast, the cutaneous porphyrias, such as porphyria cutanea tarda, erythropoietic protoporphyria and X‐linked protoporphyria, are characterised predominantly by photosensitivity rather than acute neurovisceral crises, and the role of hormonal therapies in triggering disease activity is less clearly defined. While oestrogen exposure has been implicated in the pathogenesis of PCT, primarily through effects on hepatic iron metabolism and uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase inhibition, the evidence linking hormonal therapies to acute disease flares in cutaneous subtypes remains limited and mechanistically distinct from AHP. 16 As such, these subtypes were excluded to maintain a clinically homogeneous cohort and to allow more targeted evaluation of hormonally mediated attack risk.

Conclusion

In summary, tolerability of hormonal treatments in AHP is highly variable and influenced by both therapy type and porphyria subtype. COCPs appear to confer the greatest risk of triggering acute attacks, particularly in individuals with HCP, whereas progesterone‐only methods, levonorgestrel‐releasing IUDs and MHT demonstrate more favourable tolerability profiles. Together with emerging evidence from assisted reproductive technologies, these findings support a personalised, carefully monitored approach to hormonal management in AHP and underscore the need for prospective studies to refine clinical guidelines and optimise patient safety.
