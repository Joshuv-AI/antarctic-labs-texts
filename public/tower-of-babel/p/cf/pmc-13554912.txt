Sudden Cardiac Arrest and Takotsubo-Like Cardiomyopathy as the Initial Presentation of Primary Carnitine Deficiency
Authors: Nidhi Talasani, Robert Przybylski, Marc A. Delaney, Akos Berthold, Marta Biderman, Natalie Hauser, Mitchell Cohen, Christopher Jordan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary carnitine deficiency (PCD) is a rare yet treatable disorder of the carnitine cycle causing defective fatty acid oxidation. PCD presents with considerable phenotypic variability, including sudden cardiac death as the initial manifestation.Case SummaryAn 18-year-old female born in India presented with a ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest. Cardiac evaluation was notable for normal anatomy, nonspecific repolarization changes on electrocardiogram, and cardiac magnetic resonance image showing a Takotsubo-like cardiomyopathy phenotype. Genetic testing identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in SLC22A5 (c.43G>T; p.Gly15Trp) consistent with autosomal recessive PCD. History of Presentation

An 18-year-old female presented after a witnessed cardiac arrest. She was sitting in class when she abruptly became unconscious, fell out of her chair, and began to exhibit whole-body tonic-clonic movements. A bystander student felt no pulse and started cardiopulmonary resuscitation promptly. An automated external defibrillator was applied and delivered a shock, after which there was a return of spontaneous circulation.

She was transported by emergency medical services to the emergency department (ED) where she was in sinus rhythm. Her neurologic examination was notable for nonpurposeful movement, and orotracheal examination revealed a 1-cm tongue laceration and blood in the airway. Her initial venous blood gas showed a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.0 with a pCO2 of 78 mm Hg and a lactate of 8.7 mmol/L. She was thus sedated and intubated. The remainder of her physical examination was normal, including a normal cardiac examination and no hepatomegaly. Review of the tracings obtained by the automated external defibrillator showed ventricular fibrillation (VF, Figure 1A) and review of tracings obtained by emergency medical services minutes after successful defibrillation showed short QT and heart rate–corrected QT intervals at 245 ms and 335 ms, respectively (Figure 1B), as well as a short, coupled premature ventricular contraction (PVC; coupling interval 249 ms, Figure 1C). Past Medical History

The patient was born in India. She met major developmental milestones. Her parents denied consanguinity, and she had no significant past medical history indicative of a metabolic condition, including poor feeding, irritability, lethargy, syncope, skeletal muscle weakness, or seizure activity. She also denied a prior history of hypoglycemia, hepatomegaly, elevated liver transaminases, or hyperammonemia during fasting states or childhood illnesses. She participated in sports throughout her childhood with normal exercise capacity compared to her peers and no exertional symptoms.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for a VF arrest in this age group includes cardiomyopathies, cardiac channelopathies, myocarditis, undiagnosed congenital heart disease, toxic ingestion, conditions of inborn errors of metabolism, and idiopathic VF.

Investigations

The initial electrocardiogram in the ED demonstrated sinus tachycardia with inferior lead T-wave inversions and a normal heart rate–corrected QT at 396 ms (Figure 2). Serum electrolytes were normal, and urine toxicology screen was unrevealing. Cardiac telemetry subsequently demonstrated frequent PVCs of 2 distinct morphologies with a coupling interval of ∼300 ms. Given that the PVCs were consistently falling just after the crest of the T-wave at this coupling interval, a lidocaine infusion was started. Initial echocardiogram demonstrated a structurally normal heart with normal coronary artery origins and moderately depressed left ventricular (LV) systolic function (ejection fraction (EF) 35% to 40%) with mild ventricular septal dyskinesis but no significant regional-wall-motion abnormalities detected. There was normal LV wall thickness with no chamber dilation. Subsequently, a cardiac magnetic resonance image was obtained that showed similarly moderately diminished LV systolic function (EF of 42%) but new regional-wall-motion abnormalities with marked hypokinesia of the mid-apical left ventricle and systolic apical ballooning, suggestive of a Takotsubo-like cardiomyopathy phenotype. Genetic testing was performed with a comprehensive 139-gene panel targeting genes associated with cardiac arrhythmias and cardiomyopathies. A homozygous pathogenic variant in SLC22A5 (c.43G>T; p.Gly15Trp) was identified, consistent with a diagnosis of primary carnitine deficiency (PCD). Confirmatory testing revealed a severely depressed serum carnitine level at 5 nmol/mL (reference range 34-78 nmol/mL). No other clinically relevant variants were detected.

Clinical data on the c.43G>T variant remain limited, as it has primarily been identified in newborns detected through newborn screening and in their asymptomatic or presymptomatic mothers with low serum carnitine.1 Functional studies also demonstrate reduced carnitine transport compared with wild type, supporting the pathogenicity of this variant.1

Management

Carnitine supplementation was promptly initiated at a total daily dose of 4,000 mg. Given the uncertain natural history of PCD presenting with cardiac arrest, a secondary prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was recommended but ultimately declined by the patient and family. A repeat echocardiogram demonstrated normalization of LV systolic function (EF of 64%) and resolution of regional-wall-motion abnormalities.

Outcome and Follow-Up

With continued carnitine supplementation, the patient's serum carnitine levels normalized. After normalization of serum carnitine levels, a 7-day Holter monitor demonstrated no PVCs. Counseling included a strong recommendation to wear a medical identification bracelet and convey the new PCD diagnosis to future care providers. Cascade genetic screening for family members was also recommended. Discussion

PCD has an estimated prevalence of approximately 1:120,000 in North America and can lead to a wide range of phenotypes of metabolic decompensation, including periods of hypoketotic hypoglycemia, skeletal myopathy, and a variety of cardiac manifestations.2, 3, 4, 5 Carnitine is an amino acid derivative that is involved in long-chain fatty acids transport across the mitochondrial membrane for beta oxidation. Carnitine is acquired through diet, and the vast majority is stored intracellularly.5 The SLC22A5 gene encodes the OCTN2 carnitine transporter, a sodium-independent organic cation transporter and sodium-dependent carnitine transporter. This transporter is essential for retention of carnitine in cardiac and skeletal muscle.5 In patients with SLC22A5-mediated PCD, severely depleted carnitine stores can lead to cardiac phenotypes including cardiomyopathy, extremely shortened QT interval, and malignant ventricular arrhythmias.2, 3, 4, 5, 6

While PCD is routinely detected on newborn metabolic screening, patients born in areas where newborn metabolic screening is not routinely performed or prior to the advent of such screening will not be detected.6 Thus, PCD should be considered in patients presenting with cardiomyopathy, short QT intervals, or sudden cardiac death; especially if they are from an ethnic background in which the pathogenic SLC22A5 variants are common or if consanguinity is suspected.

Our patient's VF arrest in the setting of severely depressed carnitine levels is consistent with prior reports of malignant ventricular arrhythmias in patients with untreated PCD.2,3 Interestingly, our patient had a short QT interval shortly after her successful defibrillation, and this is congruent with clear evidence that PCD can mimic short QT syndrome.3 However, the significance of this finding is unclear given that her QTc normalized by the time she arrived in the ED, and QT interval dynamics after cardiac arrest and defibrillation are complex.7 In addition, the frequent short-coupled PVCs seen early in the hospitalization course were notable given that short-coupled PVCs have been documented to trigger malignant ventricular arrhythmias in patients with short QT syndrome.8 These findings raise the possibility that short-coupled PVCs are the trigger for malignant ventricular arrhythmias in patients with PCD. In addition, while cardiomyopathic phenotypes of ventricular hypertrophy and dilation have been previously described in patients with PCD, the Takotsubo-like phenotype with transient regional-wall-motion abnormalities and apical ballooning in this case has not been previously reported to the best of our knowledge.4,9

Fortunately, PCD is treatable with carnitine supplementation, and many of the cardiac manifestations of PCD appear reversible.3,4 The largest PCD experience with a focus on cardiac outcomes that has been published to date comes from the Faroe Islands, where the prevalence of PCD is 1:300 people.9 They report on a cohort of 139 patients with PCD followed for a median of >10 years, in which all patients were treated with L-carnitine supplementation. No patient in this cohort suffered a cardiac arrest or malignant ventricular arrhythmia during the study period despite other reports of a high rate of sudden cardiac death among untreated individuals in this population.10 Data regarding recurrence of malignant arrhythmias in PCD on adequate carnitine supplementation is lacking, and the risk of recurrence may not be entirely ameliorated by carnitine supplementation, particularly during times of biological stress. With carnitine supplementation, our patient's ventricular systolic function normalized, and ventricular ectopy was resolved. However, the persistent repolarization abnormalities raise the possibility of residual cardiomyopathic substrate, the significance of which is unclear.

Conclusions

This case highlights PCD secondary to a homozygous SLC22A5 variant as a potentially treatable cause of malignant ventricular arrhythmias that should be considered in patients who did not undergo newborn metabolic screening. Diagnosis of PCD in these patients is critical, as it requires lifelong carnitine supplementation and indicates the need for cascade genetic screening of family members. Data regarding long-term arrhythmic risk, particularly in patients with a history of sudden cardiac death, are lacking, and this is an area in need of future study.
