Comparative effectiveness of two hysteroscopic fixation techniques (suture vs. GyneFix anchor) for levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system: a retrospective study
Authors: Jia Wang, ZhenHui Yang, YingXiang Wang, Jian Gu, XiaoMao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsThe levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system (LNG-IUS) is an effective treatment for adenomyosis; however, its therapeutic benefits are frequently limited by a high expulsion risk. This study aimed to compare the safety and efficacy of two hysteroscopic fixation techniques in patients at high risk for LNG-IUS expulsion.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 42 adenomyosis patients with an increased expulsion risk, underwent hysteroscopic LNG-IUS fixation between 2023 and 2025. The primary outcome was the rate of expulsion at 3 months postoperatively. Data were analyzed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, with between-group comparisons performed via the t-test, χ² test, or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.ResultsAccording to the surgical technique used, patients were allocated to either the suture fixation group (n = 20) or the anchor fixation group (n = 22). No statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics were detected between the two groups. At three months postoperative, the expulsion rate was 10.0% (2/20) in the suture group and 9.1% (2/22) in the anchor group, showing no statistically detectable difference within the limits of the sample. Both groups demonstrated significant improvements in dysmenorrhea (suture group: P = 0.0001; anchor group: P = 0.006). Operative time was significantly shorter in the anchor fixation group(P = 0.04).ConclusionIn this exploratory cohort of patients with adenomyosis at high risk of LNG-IUS expulsion, both hysteroscopic fixation techniques were associated with low early expulsion rates and clinically meaningful relief of dysmenorrhea. However, due to the small sample size and exploratory nature of this study, these findings should be considered preliminary. Introduction

Adenomyosis is a common gynecological disorder characterized by the ectopic invasion of endometrial tissue into the myometrium, leading to diffuse uterine enlargement. It primarily affects women of reproductive age with common clinical manifestations including uterine enlargement (60%), menorrhagia (50%), and progressive dysmenorrhea (30%) [1]. These symptoms substantially reduce quality of life and are linked to significant psychological distress [2]. In recent years, the incidence of adenomyosis in China has risen markedly, with a trend toward earlier onset [3]. Although hysterectomy is considered a radical approach, growing demand for uterine-preserving among young women has accelerated the development of conservative therapeutic strategies [4].

The levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system (LNG-IUS; Mirena®) is a T-shaped device that delivers progestogen locally to antagonize endometrial estrogen effects [5, 6]. This mechanism makes it an effective therapeutic option for women with adenomyosis. making it an effective option for women with AM. However, its clinical utility is often limited by uterine enlargement and cavity distortion. Furthermore, beyond uterine volume alone, the integrity of pelvic floor support is also recognized as a key factor influencing intrauterine device retention [7, 8]. These factors are thought to elevate expulsion risk and thus limit the long-term efficacy of conventional LNG-IUS placement. Reported expulsion rates within the first three months can be as high as 37.5% [9, 10],which leads to therapeutic failure, repeated interventions, patient anxiety and increased healthcare utilization.

To improve device retention, hysteroscopic fixation techniques have been developed. Two promising methods include: [1] suture fixation, first described by Zhu et al. [11] in 2021, which secures the LNG-IUS to the uterine wall using non-absorbable suture under hysteroscopy; and [2] anchor fixation, adapted from the GyneFix® principle [10, 12], which employs a mini-anchor mechanism for fundal attachment while maintaining the hormonal efficacy of the LNG-IUS [13]. Although both techniques aim to prevent expulsion, their comparative safety, efficacy, and practicality remain formally unevaluated.

This study therefore aimed to compare hysteroscopic suture fixation versus anchor fixation for stabilizing the LNG-IUS in patients with adenomyosis and associated uterine enlargement. We focused on procedural and short-term outcomes, including expulsion rates, operative time, technical feasibility, and symptomatic improvement. Our objective is to generate evidence that may inform the integration of these minimally invasive techniques into clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study design

Data for this retrospective study were collected from the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. The present study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (No. II2025-119-01). and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study population

Between June 1, 2023, and March 31, 2025, consecutive patients diagnosed with adenomyosis at our institution were screened for eligibility. Given the exploratory nature of this retrospective analysis and the novelty of the techniques, a formal sample size calculation was not performed a priori; instead, all eligible patients within the specified period were included to maximize available data.

Inclusion criteria

(a) Age > 18 years; (b) Transvaginal ultrasonographic (TVS) confirmation of AM *; (c) Severe dysmenorrhea#; (d) Identification as being at high risk for LNG-IUS expulsion, based on at least one of the following objectively defined criteria: (i) significant uterine enlargement, specifically a uterine volume > 150 mL as estimated by TVS using the standardized prolate ellipsoid formula (volume = 0.523×transverse diameter × anteroposterior diameter×longitudinal diameter); or (ii) a documented history of prior IUD or LNG-IUS expulsion, confirmed by medical records. These criteria were applied uniformly across both groups to ensure consistency in risk stratification; (e) a strong desire for uterine preservation without immediate fertility plans; and (f) no known allergy to levonorgestrel.

Exclusion criteria

(a) Contraindications to hormonal therapy; (b) Active or historical breast cancer; (c) Contraindications to hysteroscopic surgery; (d) Concurrent reproductive tract diseases (malignancies, malformations or acute infections); (e) Severe cardiopulmonary, hepatic, or renal dysfunction.

Operative procedure

Preoperative assessment included physical examination, transvaginal ultrasound, and gynecologic examination to exclude surgical contraindications. Cervical cytology and endometrial sampling were performed when clinically indicated to rule out malignancy. All procedures were performed under intravenous general anesthesia. To minimize allocation bias, the attending surgeon for each case was assigned from two designated, highly experienced hysteroscopic surgeons (Dr. Wang Jia or Dr. Gu Jian) using a random schedule. Both surgeons were highly experienced in hysteroscopic surgery and proficient in both fixation techniques prior to the study, thereby negating any learning curve effect during the study period. The choice of fixation technique (suture vs. anchor) was determined primarily by the real-time availability of the GyneFix® anchor device in the operating room, thereby minimizing selection bias based on patient characteristics or surgeon preference.

Suture fixation technique

Suture fixation was performed under combined hysteroscopic and continuous transabdominal ultrasonographic guidance. Following diagnostic hysteroscopy to assess the uterine cavity and address any concurrent pathology (e.g., endometrial polyps or adhesions), the LNG-IUS was prepared by bundling with a non-absorbable polypropylene suture (e.g., Prolene™ 2 0). Under direct hysteroscopic visualization, the device was positioned at the uterine fundus. The suture needle was then passed through the posterior uterine wall, securing a myometrial bite of approximately 5 mm in depth. The suture was externalized and tied extracorporeally using a knot-pusher, a maneuver confirmed simultaneously by hysteroscopy (visualizing knot apposition at the fundus) and transabdominal ultrasound (confirming the absence of full thickness penetration). This sequence of knot tying was repeated twice more. Finally, hysteroscopic inspection confirmed the secure fundal position of the LNG-IUS, and any excess suture material within the cavity was trimmed (Fig. 1).

Anchor fixation technique

This procedure was also conducted under combined hysteroscopic and continuous transabdominal ultrasound guidance. After initial diagnostic hysteroscopy, the LNG-IUS was secured to the GyneFix® anchor using a non-absorbable polypropylene suture. The anchor-IUS complex was then loaded into its specific delivery sheath. Following the standard GyneFix® insertion protocol, the delivery system was advanced to the uterine fundus. Correct fundal contact was verified by ultrasound. The anchor was then slowly deployed by retracting the sheath, which allowed its arms to penetrate and embed into the myometrium at the fundal midline. Successful intra-myometrial deployment and the absence of uterine perforation were confirmed in real time by transabdominal ultrasound, which visualized the anchor’s echogenic signature within the myometrial layer. Hysteroscopy provided final confirmation of the LNG-IUS’s correct position within the cavity, with its fundal end tethered at the anchor insertion point (Fig. 2).

Data collection

Clinical data were collected from the hospital’s electronic medical records using a structured data-collection form. All data were de-identified. Follow-up assessments were scheduled at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. These were conducted either during scheduled outpatient visits or via structured telephone interviews (Supplementary Table 1). Semi-structured interviews were conducted by a trained research assistant (YX.W) during the scheduled three-month follow-up visits. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Qualitative data analysis followed an inductive thematic analysis framework, conducted systematically in several stages. First, both interviewers (ZH.Y and YX.W) independently familiarized themselves with the transcripts through repeated reading. Second, each researcher independently generated initial codes to capture salient features of the data relevant to patient experiences with the procedure, symptom relief, and side effects. Third, through iterative discussion and comparison, the researchers grouped related codes into potential themes, which were then refined and clearly defined. To enhance analytical rigor, coding and theme development were performed independently by two researchers (ZH.Y and YX.W); any discrepancies in interpretation were resolved through consensus-based discussion, thereby ensuring consistency and credibility of the findings (Supplementary Table 2). Qualitative findings were integrated with quantitative outcomes through a complementary descriptive approach. Specifically, patient-reported experiences regarding symptom relief, procedural acceptability, and side effects were used to contextualize the quantitative measures of dysmenorrhea improvement, expulsion rates, and adverse events. This integration aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the patient experience beyond numerical outcomes alone. All adverse events were actively monitored during the hospital stay and at each scheduled follow-up visit and were documented in a study registry. Complications were specifically monitored and defined as follows: irregular bleeding (patient-reported uterine bleeding occurring outside of expected menstrual cycles or deemed clinically significant by the physician); body weight increase (an unintentional gain of > 5% from baseline body weight recorded at the preoperative visit); and persistent lower abdominal pain (non-cyclic pelvic pain requiring analgesic use or clinical evaluation beyond the immediate postoperative recovery period). For quantitative analysis, they were categorized as present or absent for each patient based on their occurrence during the entire follow‑up period.

The cumulative LNG-IUS expulsion rate at 3 months, calculated as (number of expelled devices/number of patients enrolled) × 100%. This primary end point was chosen because many of the shedding occurred in the first 3 months after the intrauterine device was inserted [9, 10].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). The normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed data are expressed as median and interquartile range and were analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, with between-group comparisons made using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Given the observed numerical imbalance in preoperative dysmenorrhea scores between groups, a direct comparison of postoperative VAS scores was considered potentially confounded. Accordingly, the primary analysis of dysmenorrhea relief was designed to assess within-group changes from baseline for each technique separately, rather than between-group differences. While adjusted analyses (e.g., ANCOVA or linear regression) could theoretically account for baseline imbalance, such models were deemed inappropriate in this exploratory study due to the small sample size, the non-normal distribution of VAS scores, and the risk of overfitting. All tests were two-tailed, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No imputation was performed for missing data, which were minimal and handled by complete-case analysis. Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism.

Outcomes and safety of the fixation procedures

All procedures were successfully completed, with a median follow‑up of 9 months (IQR: 6–18.5). Follow-up data beyond the primary 3-month endpoint were available for 35 patients (83.3%) at 6 months and 24 patients (57.1%) at 12 months, with attrition primarily due to scheduled follow-up not yet due or patients lost to contact. At the 3‑month follow‑up, the expulsion rate was 10.0% (2/20) in the suture group and 9.1% (2/22) in the anchor group. Notably, no additional expulsions were observed in either group beyond the 3-month period among patients with extended follow-up. Furthermore, based on clinical assessments and patient interviews at subsequent visits, symptomatic improvement in dysmenorrhea and menorrhagia appeared to be sustained in the majority of patients who completed longer-term follow-up, although formal statistical analysis was limited by sample size and attrition.

Uterine depth was similar between groups [suture: 9.3 cm (IQR: 8.1–10.0); anchor: 9.0 cm (IQR: 8.0–10.6)]. Operative time was significantly shorter in the anchor group [25 min (IQR: 15–41.5)] than in the suture group [40 min (IQR: 31–50); r = 0.36;95% CI: 0.07 to 0.59, P = 0.04]. Intraoperative bleeding was minimal in both groups and did not require additional hemostasis.

No intraoperative complications (e.g., uterine perforation) occurred. The most common adverse event during follow-up was irregular bleeding, which was reported in 14 of 42 patients (33.3%) overall, with comparable frequencies between the suture (35.0%) and anchor (31.8%) groups (Table 2). A substantial proportion of patients in both groups reported no adverse events. No devices required removal due to adverse events, and the incidence of events did not differ between groups (P > 0.05).

Discussion

This study provides the first comparative analysis of hysteroscopic suture fixation versus Genie-anchored fixation for securing the LNG-IUS in patients with adenomyosis at high risk of expulsion. The principal findings from this exploratory analysis indicate that both techniques were associated with low early expulsion rates in this cohort, coincided with alleviation of menorrhagia and dysmenorrhea, and demonstrated favorable safety profiles. A notable procedural advantage was observed for the anchor method, which required significantly shorter operative time.

Adenomyosis remains a challenging benign condition. Although laparoscopic adenomyomectomy is a common surgical approach, it carries risks of incomplete resection, endometrial injury and disease recurrence. For patients wishing to preserve fertility, however, hysterectomy is not a viable option, underscoring the need for effective conservative strategies [15–17].

The LNG-IUS offers a well-established conservative treatment by continuously releasing levonorgestrel locally, effectively managing menorrhagia, dysmenorrhea, and endometrial hyperplasia [1, 4, 18, 19]. Consequently, it is recommended by several international guidelines [19, 20]. In adenomyosis, however, factors such as uterine enlargement and heavy bleeding substantially increase the risk of device expulsion [20]. This represents a major clinical challenge, with studies reporting expulsion rates as high as 25% over 60 months in this population. The risk is even higher in patients with a history of prior expulsion, with events often occurring within the first 90 days after insertion [19, 21, 22]. These limitations highlight the imperative for stabilization techniques to ensure uninterrupted therapy and improve patient outcomes.

In response, two hysteroscopic fixation methods have been introduced: suture fixation, first described by Zhu et al. [11, 23], and anchor fixation [12, 13, 24], adapted from the GyneFix® principle. The observed expulsion rates for both techniques were lower than historical reports for non-fixed LNG-IUS insertions in similar high-risk populations. However, due to the absence of a non-fixed control group and the small sample size, these findings should be considered descriptive rather than definitive evidence of superiority [19, 22]. In this study, we observed no statistically significant difference in expulsion rates between the methods. However, given the limited power to detect such a difference, this finding should be interpreted as preliminary. It may suggest that, within high-risk adenomyotic uteri, once secure attachment is achieved, other factors could be influential. Once secure attachment is achieved, determinants such as the degree of myometrial infiltration or baseline uterine contractility may outweigh the impact of the specific fixation mechanism itself.

We acknowledge several limitations. The absence of a non-fixed control group precludes definitive causal attribution of the reduced expulsion rate solely to fixation. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size (n = 42) and the low absolute number of expulsion events (n = 4) limit statistical power. Consequently, while the observed expulsion rates were similar, the study is underpowered to detect small or moderate differences between the techniques. Regarding the primary outcome of expulsion, post hoc considerations indicate that with 20 and 22 patients per group and event rates of approximately 10%, the study was only powered to detect extremely large effect sizes (e.g., a difference of > 30% points between groups). To detect a clinically meaningful absolute reduction of 10% (e.g., from 15% to 5%) with 80% power and α = 0.05, a sample size of approximately 300–400 patients per group would be required.Therefore, our results regarding comparative expulsion rates must be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. They represent an absence of a statistically detectable difference within the constraints of our cohort and should not be construed as evidence of equivalence.

Given the low event rate, predictive modeling for expulsion was not feasible. Although expulsion rates did not differ, anchor fixation was associated with a significantly shorter operative time. The anchor technique is designed for ease of use, requiring minimal specific training. In contrast, suture fixation demands proficiency in endoscopic suturing and entails a steeper learning curve. In our experience, surgeons typically achieve consistent operative times after approximately ten supervised sutures-fixation procedures. It should be noted that although random assignment of surgeons helped mitigate operator-related bias, the non-randomized, retrospective design inherently carries risks of selection bias and confounding, which cannot be completely removed.

Regarding symptomatic improvement, the significant reduction in dysmenorrhea observed within each group aligns with the established efficacy of the LNG-IUS [25]. However, given the marked baseline imbalance in VAS scores between cohorts, we refrained from conducting between-group comparisons of pain outcomes.This analytical choice ensures that our efficacy conclusions are not confounded by preoperative differences.The within-group analyses robustly demonstrated significant pain reduction for each fixation technique independently, suggesting that both methods effectively facilitate the therapeutic action of the LNG-IUS.

The exploratory qualitative interviews provided valuable context for the quantitative results. Identified themes, such as perceived acceptability of the procedure, personal impact of dysmenorrhea reduction, and management of side effects such as irregular bleeding, provided a nuanced understanding of the patient experience. These insights directly complement the safety and efficacy data, highlighting the postoperative impact on the patient. Therefore, integrating these findings can better inform clinical practice and provide a more comprehensive evidence base for patient counseling. Understanding patient shared concerns and satisfaction can help clinicians promote shared decision making.

The safety profiles of both techniques were favorable in this study, with no serious intraoperative complications. Postoperative adverse events mainly irregular bleeding, lower abdominal pain and body weight increase were similar between groups, indicating that fixation does not introduce substantial additional risk [26, 27].It is important, however, to contextualize these findings within the inherent risks of each technique.

Suture fixation carries a potential risk of uterine perforation during needle passage and longer-term considerations such as suture irritation. Anchor fixation, while more accessible, introduces possibilities of myometrial trauma, anchor migration or complexity during future removal. Regarding therapeutic efficacy, trends toward uterine volume reduction and hemoglobin increase were observed in both cohorts, though most changes did not reach statistical significance. The isolated finding of significant uterine volume reduction in the anchor group requires considerable caution in interpretation. This result is more likely attributable to the study’s limited sample size, short follow-up, or baseline variability rather than to definitive differential biological effect of the fixation technique itself. The structural remodeling and antiproliferative effects of the LNG-IUS typically require 6 to 12 months to become fully evident. Accordingly, this observation should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, warranting confirmation in larger prospective studies with extended follow-up.

From an economic perspective, both fixation methods may yield long-term cost savings by reducing the need for LNG-IUS reinsertions and potentially avoiding hysterectomy. In terms of direct procedural cost, suture fixation is more economical, utilizing standard suture material versus a proprietary device.

The findings from this comparative study offer direct guidance for managing adenomyosis patients at high risk for LNG-IUS expulsion. Suture fixation is a widely applicable technique that relies on traditional hysteroscopic techniques and the sutures available in most surgical Settings. Conversely, the anchor method depends on the availability of a specific device (GyneFix), which may limit its adoption in certain situations. In addition, the operation time (where anchoring techniques show an advantage) may be highly sensitive to the operator’s familiarity with both methods; The learning curve of hysteroscopic suture may be steeper. Therefore, although our data indicate that both techniques are feasible, the choice in clinical practice may be reasonably guided by the availability of local resources and the proficiency of the surgery.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare these two hysteroscopic fixation techniques for securing the LNG-IUS in high-risk adenomyosis patients. Its primary strengths include the novel, real-world comparison, providing initial evidence for the safety and efficacy of both methods in reducing early expulsion. The finding that fixation may overcome traditional expulsion risk factors also supports its broader integration into personalized conservative pathways. The shorter operative time with the anchor technique is a clear procedural advantage.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the single-center retrospective design and relatively small sample size limit statistical power, particularly for detecting rare adverse events or subtle between-group differences. Beyond these design-related constraints, the follow-up duration also warrants consideration: while the 3-month primary endpoint is appropriate for assessing early expulsion, it remains insufficient to capture the long-term clinical trajectory of adenomyosis management. Given that our study did not evaluate outcomes beyond this initial period, critical longer-term metrics, such as delayed expulsion, the changes in uterine volume, and reintervention rates remain unknown. In addition to concerns about internal validity, the allocation method introduces potential bias. Although the choice of fixation technique was based on device availability rather than patient characteristics, this approach may still introduce unmeasured confounding related to temporal factors or operating room logistics. As a result, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Furthermore, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the single-center setting, where all procedures were performed by two highly experienced hysteroscopic surgeons. Notably, both surgeons had achieved proficiency in suture and anchor fixation techniques prior to the study period, thereby eliminating learning curve effects and ensuring technical consistency. However, this also means that outcomes may differ in less experienced hands or in centers with different patient populations and resource availability. Consequently, the external validity of our results should be interpreted with caution. Taken together, these findings represent a valuable first step, but future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and protocol-defined, serial follow-up assessments are warranted to confirm these preliminary results and comprehensively evaluate long-term retention, safety, and clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

In this exploratory study, both hysteroscopic fixation techniques were associated with favorable short-term outcomes, including low early expulsion rates and symptom improvement, in patients with adenomyosis at high risk of LNG-IUS expulsion. The anchor technique was associated with a significantly shorter operative time, suggesting a practical advantage in procedural efficiency. However, given the study’s retrospective design, limited sample size, and low event rates, these results should be viewed as preliminary. They provide a foundation for future prospective research but do not support definitive conclusions regarding the comparative effectiveness or equivalence of the two techniques.
