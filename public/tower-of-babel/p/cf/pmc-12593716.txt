Impact of physical and psychological stressors on tumor growth and immune responses in breast cancer
Authors: Kory Dees, Kayla Kabir, Roxana Bahani, Christopher Beskow, Matthew Blalock, Ethan Burton, Jessica Kranzlein, Danielle Pierson, Shannon Rice, Marietta Williams, Kari J Dugger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpidemiological studies suggest that heightened psychological stress is associated with poor breast cancer outcomes, while regular physical activity is presumed to improve patient prognosis. These correlations raise the question of whether aerobic exercise might offset the detrimental effects of psychological stress on cancer progression. Neuroendocrine changes during stress are believed to alter anti-tumor immune responses, potentially influencing cancer outcomes.ObjectiveThis study investigated the impact of psychological stress and/or aerobic exercise on tumor growth and anti-tumor immunity in a syngeneic mouse model of breast cancer.MethodsFemale BALB/c mice were subcutaneously injected with EMT6 breast carcinoma cells and assigned to one of four treatment groups: moderate aerobic exercise (Ex), unpredictable chronic mild stress (UCMS), a combination of both (UCMS-Ex), or untreated controls. At study completion, tumor size and immune profiles were assessed.ResultsTumors were consistently smaller in non-stressed, non-exercised control mice, while both aerobic exercise and chronic mild stress, individually or combined, led to larger tumors. Smaller tumors were associated with increased infiltration of T helper and cytotoxic T cells. Exercise enhanced the proliferative capacity of T helper cells and the suppressive function of regulatory T cells, regardless of chronic stress exposure. Although aerobic exercise alone improved cytotoxic T cell activity, this anti-tumor function was diminished when chronic stress was present.ConclusionsIn summary, neither physical activity nor psychological stress reduced tumor growth once tumors were established; instead, both accelerated tumor progression. While exercise alone enhanced EMT6-specific cytotoxic T cell function, this benefit was lost in the presence of chronic stress. Exercise did not mitigate the tumor-promoting effects of stress or restore impaired anti-tumor immunity. However, our findings in sedentary mice with smaller tumors reinforce the established association between increased intra-tumoral T cell presence and reduced tumor growth.Keywords: Breast cancer, EMT6, Aerobic exercise, Psychological stress, Unpredictable chronic mild stress (UCMS), Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), Anti-tumor response, Tumor microenvironment (TME), T cell

Introduction

Bidirectional communication between the neuroendocrine and immune systems plays a central role in maintaining health and shaping disease outcomes. Emerging evidence suggests that lifestyle factors, such as physical activity and psychosocial stress, can influence both tumor progression and anti-tumor immunity through alterations in neuroendocrine signaling [[1], [2], [3]]. These stimuli, or "stressors," activate key neuroendocrine pathways, including the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in the release of catecholamines and glucocorticoids. The magnitude and duration of these signals vary with the intensity and predictability of the stressor and can lead to profound downstream effects on immune cell function, particularly T cell regulation [4]. While the impact of neuroendocrine signaling on immune function has been explored in the context of chronic inflammation, autoimmune disease, and modulation of tumor-immune landscapes; the neuroendocrine signaling induced by physical activity, and its interaction with psychological stress, remains less well defined [[5], [6], [7]]. This study aims to address that gap by investigating how opposing lifestyle factors of exercise and chronic stress affect both cancer cell behavior and anti-tumor immunity in a murine breast cancer model.

Catecholamines, such as epinephrine and norepinephrine, interact with adrenergic receptors expressed on both innate and adaptive immune cells. They act primarily in the acute phase of the stress response via excretion from the adrenal medulla during the initial period of transient stress. This interaction has been shown to promote immune cell mobilization, heightened immunosurveillance, and increased anti-inflammatory effects [8,9]. Additionally, catecholamines can stimulate adrenergic receptors, if present, on tumor cells that can drive cell proliferation [10]. Ultimately, catecholamine release may impact tumor progression both directly, by binding adrenergic receptors on cancer cells, and/or indirectly, by binding to adrenergic receptors on lymphocytes and dendritic cells, affecting their anti-tumor activity [10,11]. Glucocorticoids, such as cortisol, are elevated during the chronic phase of the stress response and can broadly suppress immune function. Although these pathways may contribute, in part, to impaired anti-tumor immunity and could help explain reported links between psychosocial stressors and adverse cancer outcomes [12], they will not be the primary focus of this present study.

Epidemiological data shows associations between high levels of anxiety and/or depression and increased cancer incidence, including breast cancer; however, the effect sizes are modest and may be influenced by publication bias [11,13]. Thus, while stress signaling has been consistently implicated in tumor progression and metastasis [14], further investigation is needed regarding its link to risk.There is ongoing debate in the literature regarding whether exercise acts as a beneficial or detrimental stressor in the context of cancer. On one hand, moderate physical activity has been shown to enhance immune function by reducing chronic activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, increasing the proportion of naïve T cells, decreasing senescent subsets, and improving the CD4/CD8 ratio [15]. In preclinical models, moderate exercise delays early tumor development, particularly in genetic models of breast cancer [16,17]. However, these benefits often diminish once tumors are established, and outcomes vary depending on the specific exercise protocol used. Clinically, exercise is primarily promoted for cancer prevention, with benefits attributed to enhanced immune surveillance, reduced adiposity, and lower systemic inflammation [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. In contrast, evidence supporting exercise as a therapeutic intervention for individuals with existing cancer is less consistent, though it is generally believed to improve cardiopulmonary function and treatment tolerance. Any additional anti-tumor benefits remain under investigation [24].

In this study, we use the EMT6 breast cancer cell line, a non-hormone-sensitive, syngeneic tumor model derived from BALB/c mice, to investigate how physical activity and chronic stress influence T cell–mediated anti-tumor immunity. EMT6 tumors grow locally and rarely form spontaneous metastases despite the presence of disseminated tumor cells in distant organs. This lack of metastatic outgrowth has been attributed to effective clearance by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (Tc). When Tc cells are depleted, these micrometastatic cells can proliferate, highlighting the critical role of T cells in tumor control [25]. Compared to more aggressive models such as 4T1, EMT6-bearing mice exhibit prolonged survival and more robust immune-mediated tumor suppression, making this model particularly well suited to assess how neuroendocrine signaling from lifestyle factors like exercise and chronic stress modulates T cell–dependent tumor responses. Building on this model's suitability for studying T cell–mediated tumor control, we consider how lifestyle interventions, such as exercise, may influence these immune responses and tumor outcomes. While exercise is commonly recommended to manage depression and stress, its effects on tumor progression, particularly in individuals already diagnosed with cancer, remains unclear [26]. Given the known role of beta-2 adrenergic signaling in both stress and immune regulation, our study examines how exercise and chronic stress modulate tumor growth and T cell–mediated immunity through this pathway. Defining these interactions in the context of a cancer diagnosis is essential for developing effective, individualized adjunct therapies.

Methods

Mice

Six to eight-week-old female BALB/c (Taconic Biosciences; Catalog No. BALB-F) were used for all recipient experiments. All mice were maintained in autoclaved microisolator cages and provided with food and water (Teklad Rodent Diet), as needed. All mice were housed in the University of South Alabama animal care facility and under increased observation from veterinary personnel. Facilities were maintained at 70–75 °F (21–24 °C). Mice were terminated using an overdose of chemical anesthetics, ketamine 80 mg/kg: xylazine 10 mg/kg (2-3x the anesthetic dose), to cause a loss of toe pinch, and corneal reflex. Vital tissue/organs were collected per IACUC approval. [IACUC #857151, University of South Alabama]

Tumor model

The EMT6 tumor cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC – CRL-2755). Low passage cells were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for an EMT6 cell stock. A total of 100 μl of EMT6 cell stock (5 × 105 cells) was subcutaneously (s.c.) implanted into the lower right flank of BALB/c mice on day 0. The mice were randomly distributed into one of four treatment groups: 1) no “stress— “sedentary,” 2) moderate aerobic exercise – “exercise,” 3) unpredictable chronic mild stress – “UCMS,” and 4) exercise with UCMS – “exercise-UCMS” on day 1 (Fig. 1). At end of the treatment protocols, tumors were resected and assessed for tumor volume (V). The longest dimension, “length,” of the tumor was identified and measured with calipers. The shortest dimension, “width,” of the tumor was identified, perpendicular to the length, and measured with calipers. Formula V = (width2 ∗ length)/2 was used to calculate tumor volume (V = mm3) [27].

Experimental Design

Wildtype female BALB/c mice were subcutaneously implanted with 5 × 105 EMT6 cells into their lower right flank on day 0. On day 1, recipient mice were randomly assigned to one of four experimental groups: (1) sedentary with no stressors (Sed); (2) moderate aerobic exercise (Ex); (3) unpredictable chronic mild stress (UCMS) or (4) moderate aerobic exercise with unpredictable chronic mild stress (UCMS-Ex). At the end of their respective 24-day experimental protocols, mice were analyzed for tumor growth and tumor immunity. For moderate aerobic exercise (Ex), mice were placed on a treadmill (TM) three times per week for a 45-min session at a speed of 13.5 m/min (0 % grade). For UCMS, mice were exposed to two different minor-intensity stressors daily from days 1–24 (UCMS1 and UCMS2). UCMS1 occurred once daily for 1 h. Potential UCMS1 stressors included predator call, shaking cage, and confinement in a 50 mL tube. UCMS2 took place overnight. Potential UCMS2 stressors included overnight with wet bedding, 24-h daylight, and overnight in a 45° tilted cage. For UCMS-Exercise, mice were placed on a treadmill for 45 min three times per week and exposed to two minor-intensity stressors daily for the duration of the experimental protocol.

Psychological stress model (unpredictable chronic mild Stress—UCMS)

Mice underwent the UCMS mouse model of chronic depression for 24 days according to the published protocol [28]. Briefly, the UCMS model exposes mice to two minor-intensity stressors daily that alternate in an unpredictable pattern. A 1-hr stressor would be administered in the morning (∼9am), and an overnight (O/N) stressor would be applied at 6pm and removed at 6am to adhere to the mouse facility's light/dark cycle. Over 24 days, a 7-day cycle was repeated 3.5 times to assess varying levels of stress. Stressors were applied throughout the circadian period and in an unpredictable manner (Fig. 1). Examples of 1-h minor-intensity daily stressors included: 1) shaking of the cage, 2) predator call, or 3) confinement to a 50 mL tube. Examples of overnight circadian disturbances included: 1) overnight in a 45° tilted cage, 2) wet bedding, or 3) simulated 24-h daylight. Mice were kept on a 12-h day and night schedule with two minor stressors in a 24-h period. Variation in this schedule only took place for the 24-h daylight stressor.

Physical changes of mice to stress were quantified by measuring the state of the coat and body weight 2-3x weekly. At each coat-body check, the head, neck, back, abdomen, tail, forepaws, and hindpaws were assessed. The scoring system went as follows: “0” (good condition) for smooth and shiny fur, “0.5” (moderate) for slightly fluffy fur with spiky patches, and “1” (bad condition) for unkempt fur with slight staining. The total score was calculated from all body parts listed above to obtain a maximum stress score of 7. Mouse weight was also evaluated daily to determine weight loss associated with either tumor progression or stressor treatment. All mice participating in the UCMS protocol averaged ∼3–5 on a 7-point scale throughout the duration of the protocol. Of note, exercised only and sedentary mice maintained a 0–0.5 score on the same 7-point scale.

Physical exercise model

To model moderate, non-aversive physical activity, mice underwent a treadmill running protocol three times per week for four weeks (Fig. 1). Prior to the first week, mice were acclimated to the treadmill with progressive increases in speed and duration. Running was increased from 10.0 m/min for 30 min to 13.5 m/min for 45 min in the initial week of acclimation. For the remainder of the study, exercise bouts were conducted at 13.5 m/min (0 % grade) for 45 min, including warm-up and cool-down periods, totaling ∼60 min. This intensity corresponds to mild-to-moderate aerobic exercise (∼50–75 % VO2 max) and is comparable to brisk walking in humans [[29], [30], [31], [32]]. The protocol was adapted from prior work in inflammatory disease models, where it produced immune-modulatory effects without distress [33,34]. No electrical or mechanical stimuli were used. Mice that did not voluntarily engage in running were excluded. All mice were monitored for health and behavior by trained staff. For groups receiving both exercise and chronic stress (UCMS), mice were exposed to a 1-h stressor in the morning (∼9 a.m.) and exercised in the afternoon (∼3 p.m.), with overnight stressors beginning at 6 p.m. to align with the 12-h light/dark cycle. Sedentary control mice were placed on a stationary treadmill for 10 min to control for novelty and handling and were otherwise housed under identical conditions to exercised mice.

Flow cytometry

The spleen and draining lymph nodes from the tumor were disassociated, and red blood cells were lysed. Cells were fixed in buffered Formalin (Sigma Chemical) and stored at 4 °C until analysis using a Becton Dickinson LSR Benchtop Flow Cytometer with a 488 nm argon-ion laser. A minimum of 30,000 gated events were collected for each sample (n = 6–10 per experimental group). The auto-fluorescence gate was set using an unstained sample. Compensation for each antibody was set with the auto-compensation feature on the LSR by gating on singly labeled samples. All auto-fluorescent samples fell within the first log, with stained samples falling between the first and third logs.

Single-cell suspensions were incubated with Fc block (antiCD16/CD32 antibody for 10 min at 4C, BioLegend, 1:500) and then stained with cell surface–conjugated antibodies. The stained cells were washed and resuspended in 100 mL of cold flow cytometry buffer (1 % BSA, 5 % FBS, 0.1 % sodium azide in PBS; salts from SigmaAldrich). Flow cytometry data were obtained using a LSR Benchtop Flow Cytometer (Becton Dickinson) and analyzed with FlowJo software. The double/aggregated cells were gated out using forward scatter area versus forward scatter width and side scatter area versus side scatter width. No viability dyes were used. Different fluorophores conjugated with the following mAb were used: CD3 (145-2C11), CD45 (30-F11), CD69 (H1.2F3), CD25 (PC61.5), CD4 (GK1.5), FOXP3 (150D), CD8a (53–6.7), PD-1/CD279 (29F.1A12), CTLA4 (CD152), LAG3/CD223 (C9B7W) and CD206 (C068C2, BioLegend). Unless specified otherwise, all the antibodies were used at 1:200 (eBioscience).

T cell subpopulation isolation

To isolate T regulatory cells (Treg; CD4+CD25+), the spleens and lymph nodes of mice were removed, and a “single-cell suspension” was prepared. Red blood cells were lysed using ACK buffer (Fisher Scientific) lysis solution. T helper cells (Th; CD4+CD25−) were isolated from the solution using a “negative selection” magnetic bead separation kit (Miltenyl Biotec, Auburn, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's protocol. Cytotoxic T cells (Tc, CD8+) were depleted from the cell population using biotin-labeled specific monoclonal antibodies. Anti-biotin magnetic beads and an LD magnetic bead column were used to separate the biotin-tagged antibody-labeled cells from the harvested cell population. Negatively isolated CD4+ T cells were then positively selected for CD25 by incubating them with Phycoerythrin (PE)-labeled anti-CD25 antibodies. Anti-PE magnetic beads and the MS column were used to isolate CD4+ CD25+ Treg cells. CD4+CD25− T helper cells were collected from the runoff. The purity of the sorted cells was >90 %.

T cell proliferation assay

Effector CD4+CD25− cells were isolated from each mouse treatment group to be cultured in complete RPMI 1640 (supplemented with glutamine/10 % FBS/1 % Pen-Strep/1 % Pyruvate/50 μM 2-Mercaptoethanol) containing 2 μg/mL anti-CD3 (eBioscience) and 2 μg/mL anti-CD28 (eBioscience). Cells were cultured for 72hr (37 °C, 5 % CO2); tritiated thymidine (3H, 0.5μCi/well; Amersham) was added for the final 18 h. Cultures were analyzed for 3H incorporation via scintillation counting (PHD Harvester, BRANDEL).

T regulatory cell suppression assay

CD4+CD25+ Treg cells were isolated from each mouse treatment group to be cultured with sedentary mouse CD4+CD25− effector cells. To compare suppression function levels between treatment groups (sedentary, exercised, UCMS, and UCMS-Ex), CD4+CD25− effector cells for all suppression assays were derived from sedentary mice with EMT6 cells subcutaneously implanted. Effector and Treg cells were co-cultured in complete RPMI 1640 (supplemented with glutamine/10 % FBS/1 % Pen-Strep/1 % Pyruvate/50 μM 2-Mercaptoethanol) containing 2 μg/mL anti-CD3 (eBioscience) and 2 μg/mL anti-CD28 (eBioscience). Co-cultures were plated at increasing titers of Treg cells, however, the CD4+CD25− effector cells remained constant at 50,000 cells per well. Cells were co-cultured for 72hr (37 °C, 5 % CO2); tritiated thymidine (3H, 0.5μCi/well; Amersham) was added for the final 18 h. Cultures were analyzed for 3H incorporation via scintillation counting (PHD Harvester, BRANDEL).

T cell cytolytic assay

EMT6 cell suspensions were labeled with 10 μM CFSE (Molecular Probes Europe, Leiden, the Netherlands) at 37 °C for 10 min after being washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). CFSE-labeled EMT6 cells were resuspended and cultured for 3 days at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 in the presence or absence of increasing titers of CD8+ Tc cells isolated from each mouse treatment group. The CFSE-labeled EMT6 cells remained constant at 50,000 cells per well in 96-well plates. Cytotoxic T lymphocytes were added at different effector-target ratios. The percentage of survival was calculated as follows: % survival = [absolute no. viable CFSE + target cells (t = x)]/[absolute no. viable CFSE + target cells (t = 0)] x 100.

Reagents and drugs

Formoterol (beta-adrenergic receptor agonist, Millipore Sigma) and ICI-118-151 (β2-adrenergic receptor antagonist, Millipore Sigma) were solubilized in in sterile H2O and adjusted to appropriate working concentrations in complete Waymouth's MB 752/1 Medium with 15 % FBS media for cell count cultures. Epinephrine (adrenergic receptor agonist), propranolol (beta-adrenergic receptor antagonist, Millipore Sigma), Isoprenaline (beta-adrenergic receptor agonist, Tocris), ICI-118551 (beta 2-adrenergic receptor antagonist, Tocris), and procaterol (beta 2-adrenergic receptor agonist, Tocris) were solubilized in sterile H2O and adjusted to appropriate working concentrations in complete Waymouth's MB 752/1 Medium with 15 % FBS media for cell proliferation MTS assays.

EMT6 in vitro culture cell counts

A total of 0.15 × 106 EMT6 cells were plated into a T-25 flask in Weymouth's MB 752/1 Medium with 15 % FBS media as indicated by ATCC protocols (ATCC, CRL-2755). Replicates were plated to allow for duplicate flasks at each timepoint cell count collected. Total cell counts were calculated using a standard hemacytometer. All β-adrenergic receptor treatments (Formoterol and ICI-118-151) were diluted to allow for 100 nM end concentrations during cell culture. Four independent cultures were set up at varying initial cell concentrations, but the trend of β-adrenergic stimulation and inhibition remained consistent.

Cell proliferation assay

The MTS assay was used to assess cell proliferation. Briefly, cells, treated with different drugs, were seeded at a density of either 5000 or 10,000 cells/well in 96-well plates. Each dose was done in triplicate and repeated four times. After incubation with MTS (Promega) for 1–4hrs in 96-well plates, they were read with an absorbance reader at a wavelength of 490 nm at either 24hrs or 48hrs post-treatment.

Statistics and analysis

Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA analysis between treatments as indicated (GraphPad Prism 4). A Tukey's-HSD post-hoc analysis determined which group means differed significantly. To analyze the effects of exercise on sedentary mice directly or the effects of stress on sedentary mice directly, an unpaired Student's T-test was performed. Results are reported as group means ± SEM with significance set at a level of p ≤ 0.05.

Data availability

The data generated or analyzed throughout the study are included in this article and its additional materials.

Results

Exercise and chronic stress increase EMT6 breast cancer growth

To investigate whether moderate aerobic exercise and/or unpredictable chronic mild stress (UCMS) impacts cancer growth, mice were placed in experimental groups for 24 days once subcutaneously implanted with 5 × 105 EMT6 cells in the right flank (Fig. 1). At the end of the stressor treatment protocols, tumors were resected and assessed for growth by using the following: Tumor volume= (L x W2)/2, where L represents tumor length and W represents tumor width. Tumor volumes are reported in cubic millimeters (mm3). Tumor analysis showed increased tumor size of Exercised, UCMS, and UCMS-Ex mice groups as compared to Non-stressed Sedentary mouse tumors (Fig. 2B). The tumor volume of Non-stressed Sedentary, UCMS, Exercised, UCMS-Ex was 93.98 ± 14, 793.75 ± 225, 713.93 ± 215, and 600.41 ± 158 mm3, respectively (p < 0.05, unpaired student t-test). Representative tumors from all mouse treatment groups (Ex, UCMS, and UCMS-Ex) are shown in Fig. 2A. Because UCMS, Exercised, and UCMS-Ex mice exhibited variable but significant body weight reductions compared to non-stressed sedentary controls, tumor volume was normalized to body weight to better reflect tumor burden in relation to each animal's physiological condition. The normalized tumor volume based on percent body weight of Non-stressed Sedentary, UCMS, Exercised, UCMS-Ex was 3.14 ± 1, 5.98 ± 1, 4.25 ± 1, 4.09 ± 1 %, respectively (p < 0.05, unpaired student t-test, N = 6). When tumor growth was normalized to body weight (tumor-to-body weight %), tumors from the exercised groups (Ex and UCMS-Ex) were no longer statistically significantly. In contrast, UCMS tumors remained significantly elevated, suggesting both increased tumor size and significant body weight loss (i.e., cachexia) compared to other groups (Fig. 2C).

Exercise and chronic stress decrease tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes

To determine whether differences in tumor size were associated with changes in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), we analyzed both the quantity and immune profile of infiltrating T cells. The presence of intra-tumoral T cell effectors is a well-established prognostic marker, correlating with improved tumor clearance in patients [35]. Tumors were evaluated using flow cytometry. The change of CD4+ T helper (Th) and CD4+CD25+ T regulatory (Treg) cells within tumors of all treatment groups was statistically decreased when compared to Non-stressed Sedentary mouse tumors. CD4+ Th cells counts normalized to tumor volume for Non-stressed Sedentary, UCMS, Exercised, and UCMS-Ex was 149.27 ± 58, 36.85 ± 8, 64.69 ± 21, 66.92 ± 20, respectively. CD4+CD25+ Treg cell counts normalized to tumor volume for Non-stressed Sedentary, UCMS, Exercised, and UCMS-Ex was 93.04 ± 35, 25.52 ± 6, 36.23 ± 11, 38.18 ± 10, respectively. (unpaired student T tests: non-stressed sedentary compared to exercise, UCMS, and UCMS-Ex, p < 0.05, N = 6) Interestingly, the ratio of T regulatory to T helper cells represented in each tumor was similar in all mouse groups regardless of treatment or total number of infiltrating cells (Fig. 3A). The change in CD8+ cytotoxic T cell (Tc) populations was significantly reduced in tumors from UCMS and Exercised groups compared to non-stressed sedentary controls. CD8+ Tc cells counts normalized to tumor volume for Non-stressed Sedentary, UCMS, Exercised, UCMS-Ex was 1.84 ± 0.8, 0.43 ± 0.1, 0.82 ± 0.3, 1.37 ± 0.7, respectively. CD8+PD1+ Tc cell counts normalized to tumor volume for Non-stressed Sedentary, UCMS, Exercised, and UCMS-Ex was 1.61 ± 0.7, 0.38 ± 0.1, 0.62 ± 0.3, 1.19 ± 0.7, respectively. (unpaired student T tests: non-stressed sedentary compared to exercise and UCMS, p < 0.05, N = 6) In contrast, UCMS-Ex tumors showed improved intra-tumoral Tc cell levels. Additionally, mice exposed to both exercise and chronic stress exhibited a modest, though not statistically significant, increase in the Tc:TcPD-1+ ratio compared to other treatment groups (Fig. 3B).

Exercise and chronic stress increase CD4+ Th cell proliferative function

Given the observed decrease in Th cells within tumors from exercised and stressed mice, we next investigated whether these stressors affected Th cell function. Previous work from our lab and others has shown that exercise can enhance Th cell activation, whereas chronic stress has been associated with dysregulated immune responses, including reduced Th cell activity [34,[36], [37], [38]]. Emerging evidence suggests that both exercise and psychological stress lead to a context-dependent redistribution of immune activity, rather than causing a uniform suppression or enhancement of immune function [20,39]. Interestingly, our findings show that stressor-treated groups exhibited significantly increased CD4+ T helper (Th) cell proliferation compared to sedentary controls, whether the stressor was moderate aerobic exercise on a treadmill or chronic psychological stress. Th cell proliferation as measured by counts per minute (CPM) after culture with H3-tritiated thymidine of Non-stressed Sedentary, UCMS, Exercised, or UCMS-Ex Th cells was 3963.88 ± 1182, 7391.66 ± 1228, 10,560.77 ± 1983, 11,064.11 ± 2058CPM, respectively (unpaired t-test, all treatment group comparisons to non-stressed sedentary controls, plated in triplicate, p < 0.05, N = 6). Notably, cell proliferation was significantly higher in the exercised groups (Exercise and UCMS-Exercised) than in both the UCMS-only and no-stressor groups (Fig. 4A).

Exercise and chronic stress differentially alter T regulatory suppressive function

Because tumors are known to enhance regulatory T (Treg) cell function to promote cancer progression and evade anti-tumor immunity [40], we examined Treg cell function in the context of exercise and/or chronic stress. To assess this, we performed a Treg suppressive assay using effector T helper (Th) cells from sedentary mice and Treg cells isolated from all stressor treatment groups. The results showed that Tregs from exercised mice exhibited significantly increased suppressive function when co-cultured with sedentary effector Th cells. Non-stressed Sedentary Th cell proliferation as measured by counts per minute (CPM) after culture with H3-tritiated thymidine in the presence of Non-stressed Sedentary, UCMS, Exercised, or UCMS-Ex T regulatory cells was 5844.66 ± 578, 9878.1 ± 425, 4522.16 ± 249, 6025.83 ± 607 CPM (counts per minute), respectively. Th cell effector cell proliferation without T regulatory cells was 9079.33 ± 191 (unpaired t-test, exercised Treg cells to non-stressed sedentary Treg controls, plated in triplicate, p < 0.05, N = 6). These findings align with previous studies published showing exercise enhances Treg cell function by activation of adrenergic receptors [29]. Conversely, UCMS Treg cells showed decreased suppressive function when compared to effector-only and sedentary Treg cell controls (unpaired t-test, UCMS Treg cells to Non-stressed Sedentary Treg controls, plated in triplicate, p < 0.01, N = 6). Additionally, UCMS-Exercised Treg cell function was equivalent to the sedentary Treg cell controls, showing neither enhanced nor attenuated function. (Fig. 4B).

CD8+ T cell cytotoxic capability improved with exercise

Because cytotoxic T (Tc) cells play a critical role in controlling and clearing EMT6 murine breast cancer cells [41], we next investigated whether exercise and/or chronic stress affects Tc cell function. To assess this, CFSE-labeled EMT6 cells were co-cultured with Tc cells isolated from mice of each treatment group, and target cell lysis was measured (Fig. 5). The percent of remaining CFSE + EMT6 after culture with Non-stressed Sedentary, UCMS, Exercised, or UCMS-Ex Tc cells at a 1:1 ratio was 19.51 ± 1, 17.01 ± 1, 10.07 ± 0.5, 22.8 ± 2 %, respectively (repeated measures two-way ANOVA, variables of ratio, p = 0.001, and mouse treatment, p = 0.03, interaction, n.s., plated in triplicate, N = 6). There was no significant difference in lytic activity between Tc cells from UCMS and Non-stressed Sedentary mice. However, Tc cells from the UCMS-Exercise group displayed reduced lysis of EMT6 cells.

Tc cells from UCMS-Ex mice showed a decrease in lytic capabilities while Tc cells from exercised mice significantly increased lytic Tc cell function when compared to the no stressor control group. EMT6 cells show enhanced cell proliferation with β2-adrenergic receptor stimulation

To identify, in part, one pathway in which exercise and UCMS could impact EMT6 tumor growth, an EMT6 cell culture was set up with vehicle, β2-adrenergic receptor agonist (formoterol, 100uM), or β2-adrenergic receptor antagonist (ICI-118551, 100uM). Total cells were calculated at seven timepoints to establish a growth curve (Fig. 6A). For the presented data in which EMT6 cells were initially plated at 0.15 × 106 cell/mL, at 84 h, EMT6 cells with vehicle count was 2.71 × 106 cell/mL, EMT6 cells with Formoterol count was 4.26 × 106 cell/mL, and EMT6 cells with ICI-118551 count was 1.0 × 106 cell/mL (repeated measures two-way ANOVA, p < 0.05, plated in duplicate, N = 4). EMT6 cells show enhanced cell proliferation when β2-adrenergic receptors were pharmaceutically stimulated. Conversely, the EMT6 cell growth curve was attenuated when cultured with a β2-adrenergic receptor antagonist.

Discussion

In our studies, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise and unpredictable chronic mild stress (UCMS), individually and combined, enhanced tumor growth in the hormone-insensitive EMT6 murine breast cancer model. We investigated whether exercise could attenuate tumor progression or counteract the pro-tumor effects of chronic stress-induced physiological changes. While “raw tumor weights” suggested no clear benefit of exercise as compared to control or UCMS mice, when accounting for body weight, there was a reduction in “relative tumor burden” when mice underwent an exercise protocol (Ex and UCMS-Ex). In this analysis, exercise and UCMS-exercise mouse tumor burden were no longer significantly different from control mice. These results suggest that exercise may partially mitigate stress-induced tumor progression but does not fully reverse the physiological impact of psychosocial stressors on the TME as demonstrated by the intra-tumoral analysis.

In this study, we observed a significant reduction in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) across all treatment groups [exercise (Ex), unpredictable chronic mild stress (UCMS), and the combined UCMS-Ex] as compared with sedentary controls. This finding is consistent with existing literature linking higher TIL counts to improved prognosis and reduced tumor progression [42]. Both physical and psychosocial stressors were associated with decreased TILs and increased tumor size. When examining intra-tumoral lymphocyte subpopulations, we found that UCMS-Ex treatment restored Th and Treg cell numbers to levels comparable to those in the exercise-only group, although both remained significantly lower than sedentary controls. Importantly, Tc cell counts were higher in exercise-only tumors than in UCMS-only tumors. Notably, UCMS-Ex tumors also exhibited Tc cell levels comparable to sedentary controls, suggesting that exercise may facilitate T cell trafficking into tumors while mitigating the detrimental effects of psychosocial stress. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that exercise enhances T cell redistribution into peripheral tissues and circulation [43], whereas prolonged chronic stress disrupts T cell trafficking and leads to an overall reduction in circulating immune cells [44].

Despite the overall decrease in TILs in tumors from stress-exposed mice (Ex and/or UCMS), we observed differential effects on specific T cell sub-population functions. Exercise-only enhanced the function of helper (Th), regulatory (Treg), and cytotoxic (Tc) T cells, whereas chronic stress had a more variable impact. Specifically, chronic stress significantly enhanced Th cell function, impaired Treg function, and exhibited no change in Tc cell function. Interestingly, the combination of chronic stress and exercise partially restored Th and Treg functions while significantly attenuating Tc cell cytotoxicity relative to the stress-only group.

Although Th function was improved in all treatment groups compared with sedentary controls, this was insufficient to attenuate tumor progression. This outcome may be partly explained by enhanced Treg suppressive activity in exercise mouse groups (Ex and Ex-UCMS) [45,46], which likely limited Th cells’ ability to mount an effective anti-tumor response. The most unexpected finding was in UCMS-only mice, in which Treg cells displayed significantly reduced suppressive capacity while maintaining improved Th cell proliferative capacity. These results suggest some other inflammatory cell subset may play a more critical role in modulating the anti-tumor immune response during chronic stress given tumor progression remained enhanced. Effective anti-tumor immunity depends on effector T cells recognizing and eliminating tumor targets. However, tumors evade this response through several mechanisms, including dysregulation of antigen-presenting cells, formation of vascular barriers that block effector cell homing, and suppression of T cell activity via recruitment and activation of immunosuppressive cells such as regulatory T cells [47,48]. While Treg density has been associated with higher tumor grade in breast cancer, it does not consistently predict patient prognosis, suggesting other immune subsets may play critical roles [40]. Aerobic exercise has been shown to slow tumor progression, though the mechanisms are unclear. One study demonstrated that chronic endurance exercise can reduce intra-tumoral Treg:Tc ratios enhancing anti-tumor immunity [49] underscoring the heightened of importance of Tc cells in exercise-mediated tumor suppression rather than the absence of Treg cells.

Arguably the most striking result from this study was observed in the cytotoxic response: Tc cells from the UCMS-Ex group exhibited a marked reduction in EMT6-specific lytic activity, whereas Tc cells from the exercise-only group demonstrated enhanced cytotoxic function compared to sedentary controls. These findings suggest that while exercise can bolster T cell-mediated anti-tumor responses, chronic stress may override or suppress this benefit when both stimuli are present. Future studies are needed to better elucidate how chronic stress reduces this specific anti-tumor benefit from exercise.

To explore a potential mechanism underlying these results, we examined the role of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and adrenergic receptor (AR) signaling. Moderate aerobic exercise is known to activate the SNS, leading to increased epinephrine secretion [50]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) contains diverse cell types that express varying levels of ARs, which bind epinephrine and norepinephrine and subsequently modulate cellular function. Previous studies have shown that AR activation in tumor cells promotes proliferation, whereas AR blockade suppresses tumor growth [7]. Consistent with these findings, our experiments using the EMT6 cell line (Fig. 6) confirmed that AR signaling contributes to tumor cell proliferation.

Older studies suggest that adrenergic receptor (AR) expression on tumor cells correlates with patient outcomes, with higher AR expression linked to poorer survival [51]. In our study, EMT6 cells demonstrated increased proliferation in response to adrenergic stimulation in vitro, supporting these findings. In contrast, 4T1 cells, a widely used metastatic murine breast cancer model, that expresses both alpha- and beta-ARs, but with non-functional receptors showed attenuated cancer progression and reduced metastasis when mice were exercised [52]. One report proposes that the lack of AR responsiveness in 4T1 cells allows adrenergic signaling to act primarily on stromal cells, mediating anti-tumor effects in response to systemic stimuli such as exercise, highlighting how AR functionality within tumor cells can shape the tumor microenvironment and outcomes. Differential AR expression or activity may thus explain the divergent responses observed between EMT6 and 4T1 models, with EMT6 tumors showing increased growth under adrenergic influence, while 4T1 tumors demonstrate suppressed growth under similar conditions [53]. Additionally, AR signaling in the tumor microenvironment is complex, with effects varying by receptor subtype, expression level, dose, and duration of activation [54], suggesting that the net impact reflects a combination of tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental factors.

Preclinical studies on exercise and breast cancer generally fall into two categories: 1) preventative (exercise before tumor onset) and 2) therapeutic (exercise after tumor establishment). Preventative exercise consistently reduces breast cancer risk, likely through enhanced immune surveillance via immune cell mobilization [36]. Support for this mechanism comes from genetically engineered models (e.g., PyMT), where cancer onset is delayed in a dose- and intensity-dependent manner [16], as well as from studies using exercise protocols before 4T-1 cell inoculation, which show slowed tumor growth [55].

In contrast, exercise as a therapeutic intervention shows more variable outcomes. In spontaneous models like PyMT, early-stage benefits are observed, but effects diminish after several weeks. Inoculated breast cancer models seem to yield inconsistent results, influenced by exercise regimen, tumor location, and cell type. Some studies report enhanced anti-tumor effects when exercise is combined with caloric restriction, chemotherapy, or immune checkpoint blockade [[56], [57], [58]]. These findings suggest that once tumors are established, exercise may require synergy with other treatments to exert therapeutic effects. Proposed mechanisms include improved tumor vascularization, myokine release from muscle, and shifts in immunometabolism that favor anti-tumor immunity [59].

We acknowledge that our experimental design used a subcutaneous injection in the right flank that may not mirror the clinical tumor microenvironment (TME) of a mammary gland. Thus, this data has limitations for its interpretation and direct clinical translation potential [60]. However, there is substantial value in this study that further contributes to understanding the potential variations of physical exercise based on tumor site, tumor cell genetics, and lifestyle factors. Specifically, the proximity of the implanted EMT6 tumor to skeletal muscle suggests increased exposure to skeletal muscle-secreted myokines that support TME changes such as enhanced angiogenesis. These tumor location-specific factors may have played a dominant role in the observed effects in our study. Specifically, physical activity stimulates the secretion of myokines from skeletal muscle, such as VEGF and IL-6. These factors can increase the blood supply to a growing tumor, supporting enhanced tumor growth but also could increase levels of circulating norepinephrine at the tumor site that may not be correlative to a mammary gland tumor implant [61].

While our study employed a treadmill-based, mild-to-moderate intensity “prescribed” exercise protocol, commonly referred to as "forced" exercise, it was carefully designed to minimize distress and mirror a clinically relevant aerobic activity regimen, such as walking 30 min, 3–5 times per week in previously sedentary patients. This approach aligns with established murine protocols designed to avoid distress. Although “forced” treadmill running may raise concerns regarding stress-induced effects, our protocol was optimized to function as eustress and aligns with established murine protocols designed to avoid distress in the literature [32]. Importantly, most preclinical studies demonstrating therapeutic anti-tumor effects with exercise have relied on moderate-to-high intensity treadmill regimens that may not be feasible for cancer patients undergoing treatment. [62,63]. Therefore, despite its limitations, our structured exercise model offers a reproducible and physiologically relevant tool for investigating how exercise influences tumor biology. It provides a controlled platform to dissect the dose-, intensity-, and timing-dependent effects of physical activity, ultimately helping to define exercise regimens that are both feasible and beneficial for cancer patients.

Prior studies suggest that chronic adrenergic stress may induce immune cell metabolic dysfunction within the tumor microenvironment [64]. In this context, exercise may have further exhausted chronically activated adrenergic receptors on immune cells and/or amplified AR-driven proliferation in EMT6 tumor cells. Both possibilities warrant further investigation. Chronic stress is a complex phenomenon, and its physiological response to physical activity may require sustained intervention (typically 1–12 months) to elicit meaningful physiological changes [65]. It is possible that our UCMS-exercise protocol was too short to capture these effects and may be more effective as a preventative rather than therapeutic intervention. This highlights a key challenge in applying physical activity to mitigate cancer-associated depression and supports the need for individualized, time-sensitive exercise strategies.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings suggest that daily, mild-to-moderate aerobic exercise may ameliorate some of the tumor-promoting effects of chronic psychological stress, while adrenergic receptor activation can directly promote cancer cell proliferation and its effects on anti-tumor immunity remain variable and context-dependent. These outcomes highlight the need for deeper investigation into how stress and exercise interact with tumor biology. Moving forward, we will explore how variations in stressor, exercise (dose, intensity, and timing) and tumor (initiation kinetics and anatomical site) affect these outcomes. Given the multifaceted nature of stress responses and exercise physiology, particularly in relation to cancer type and experimental model, future studies using spontaneous tumor models may be better suited to investigate their roles in cancer prevention rather than treatment. Ultimately, identifying key molecular and cellular mediators will be essential to understanding and optimizing exercise- and stress-based interventions in cancer.
