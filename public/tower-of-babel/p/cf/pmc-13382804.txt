Diagnostic accuracy of somatosensory evoked potentials in lumbar spine surgery: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Prasuna L Velur, Vibha Velur, Marvin Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntraoperative somatosensory evoked potential (SSEP) monitoring is widely employed during lumbar spine surgery, yet its diagnostic accuracy remains incompletely characterised for contemporary minimally invasive approaches.ObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of intraoperative SSEP changes for predicting postoperative neurologic deficits and assess whether reversibility differentially influences performance.MethodsSystematic review and meta-analysis (PROSPERO: CRD422026135172) restricted to studies published after the Chang et al. (2021) search cutoff (October 2019 to January 2026); five studies (1017 patients). DerSimonian–Laird random-effects logit models estimated pooled sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR); subgroup analyses by reversibility. Heterogeneity: Cochran Q and I2 with 95% CIs; quality: QUADAS-2.Results: Pooled sensitivity 36.4% (95% CI: 10.7–73.2%); specificity 91.7% (95% CI: 74.6–97.6%); DOR 5.22 (95% CI: 2.17–12.52); I2 = 0.0%. Irreversible changes: DOR 22.12 (95% CI: 3.06–159.7); reversible: DOR 0.69 (95% CI: 0.18–2.66; P = .594). Positive likelihood ratio (LR+) 4.39 (post-test probability 16.9%); negative likelihood ratio (LR−) 0.69 (3.1%). Deeks funnel test P = .819.ConclusionsAcross five contemporary lumbar surgery studies (1017 patients; 2019–2026), SSEP monitoring is highly specific (91.7%) but only moderately sensitive (36.4%) for neurologic deficits. Irreversible changes carry greater diagnostic weight (DOR 22.12; 95% CI: 3.06–159.7) than reversible changes (DOR 0.69; 95% CI: 0.18–2.66). Monitoring modality should match the neural structures at risk; adjunct MEP and EMG are recommended where motor root injury predominates.SignificanceProcedure-specific SSEP diagnostic accuracy estimates for contemporary lumbar surgery support a context-dependent approach; the greater diagnostic weight of irreversible versus reversible changes (DOR 22.12 vs 0.69) has direct intraoperative implications.Keywords: Diagnostic accuracy, Intraoperative neuromonitoring, Lumbar spine surgery, meta-analysis, Neurologic deficit, Somatosensory evoked potentials, Systematic review

Introduction

Lumbar spine surgery for degenerative stenosis and spondylolisthesis carries an inherent risk of iatrogenic neurologic injury; new postoperative deficits occur in 1–13% of patients. (Weinstein et al., 2008; Ghogawala et al., 2016; Hamilton et al., 2011; Katz and Harris, 2008) Intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) encompasses multiple modalities, including somatosensory evoked potential (SSEP) monitoring, motor evoked potentials (MEP), and electromyography (EMG). SSEP monitoring detects impending neural compromise via continuous cortical recording, with a ≥ 50% amplitude reduction or ≥ 10% latency increase conventionally triggering an intraoperative alert. (Gonzalez et al., 2009).

Despite widespread use, the clinical utility of SSEP monitoring in lumbar surgery remains incompletely characterised. Because lumbar surgery endangers individual nerve roots rather than the spinal cord, SSEP may be less sensitive than MEP or EMG for isolated radicular injury, and published IONM guidelines have reached no definitive conclusion on their utility in this setting. (Heary et al., 2014).

The landmark 2021 meta-analysis by Chang et al. (5607 patients) reported SSEP sensitivity of 44% and specificity of 97% (DOR 22.13; 95% CI: 11.30–43.34) for postoperative neurologic deficits, with markedly divergent subgroup performance: reversible changes DOR 13.93, irreversible DOR 57.84. (Chang et al., 2021) However, that analysis predated the widespread adoption of prone transpsoas lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF) and percutaneous endoscopic TLIF (PE-TLIF)—procedures presenting distinct neuromonitoring challenges absent from the Chang et al. (2021) evidence base.

We conducted a systematic review restricted to studies published after the Chang et al. (2021) search cutoff (October 2019 to January 2026), incorporating five contemporary studies (prone transpsoas LLIF, femoral nerve evoked potential [FNEP]-monitored LLIF, PE-TLIF) absent from the prior evidence base, to provide procedure-specific diagnostic accuracy estimates for the era of contemporary lumbar surgical techniques. This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted per PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021). The search was restricted to studies published after the Chang et al. (2021) search cutoff (October 2019) through January 25, 2026, to characterise SSEP diagnostic accuracy in contemporary lumbar spine surgery. The present analysis does not pool with the Chang et al. (2021) dataset; it constitutes a separate analysis of the post-2019 evidence base. PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane were searched using terms combining somatosensory evoked potentials and lumbar spine surgery. After deduplication, 268 retrieved records were screened; 21 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility and 16 were excluded (case reports, reviews, insufficient data, or non-lumbar/non-SSEP procedures), yielding five eligible studies (Fig. 1; see Results 3.1 for full PRISMA flow). Trauma, aneurysm, and tumour surgeries were excluded; multimodal IONM studies contributed SSEP data only. Chen et al. (2021), published in November 2021, falls within the present search window (October 2019 to January 2026) and was therefore eligible for inclusion. It was not captured by Chang et al. (2021), whose search concluded in October 2019.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram. Of 285 records identified across databases, 268 remained after deduplication; five studies (1017 patients) met inclusion criteria after title/abstract screening and full-text review.

Data extraction

For each study, we extracted study design, procedure type, IONM modalities, SSEP alarm criteria, and 2 × 2 contingency data. Procedures included posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF), transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF), lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF), percutaneous endoscopic TLIF (PE-TLIF), and femoral nerve evoked potential (FNEP)-monitored LLIF. The diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) integrates sensitivity and specificity into a single summary statistic. Most studies defined a significant SSEP change as ≥50% amplitude decrease or ≥ 10% latency increase; reversibility (reversible, irreversible, total loss) was recorded when reported. A continuity correction of 0.5 was added to all cells of studies with zero events (Haldane–Anscombe correction). Individual study sensitivity and specificity confidence intervals were computed by the Clopper–Pearson exact binomial method. Study characteristics and outcome data are in Table 1, Table 2.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were implemented in Python (v3.11; NumPy v1.26, SciPy v1.11) using DerSimonian–Laird random-effects models. Separate logit-scale models estimated pooled sensitivity and specificity (continuity correction of 0.5 added to all cells of studies with zero events); a parallel logDOR model estimated the pooled diagnostic odds ratio and subgroup estimates. Pooled sensitivity and specificity estimates and their 95% confidence intervals were derived by back-transforming logit-scale estimates and their standard errors to the probability scale; DOR estimates and confidence intervals were derived by exponentiating the pooled logDOR ±1.96 × SE on the log scale. Fixed-effects and random-effects logDOR models were applied to the full cohort and three SSEP change subgroups (reversible, irreversible/persistent, total loss); post-test probabilities were derived via Fagan nomogram. (DerSimonian and Laird, 1986; Deeks et al., 2005) A bivariate random-effects (Reitsma) model was fitted to generate a summary receiver operating characteristic curve; given k = 5 studies with substantial procedural heterogeneity, individual study operating points are presented instead of a fitted summary curve (Fig. 6). (Reitsma et al., 2005) Study quality was assessed with QUADAS-2 (four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, flow and timing). (Whiting et al., 2011) Heterogeneity was quantified with Q and I2 (threshold: I2 ≥ 50%, per Cochrane Handbook; Deeks et al., 2022); 95% confidence intervals for I2 were estimated using the non-central chi-square method. (Higgins et al., 2003) Given k = 5 included studies, Q-based heterogeneity tests are critically underpowered and I2 point estimates should be interpreted with caution; τ2 estimates are also downwardly biased in small samples. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed for the all-SSEP DOR subgroup. Funnel plot asymmetry was evaluated by Deeks' funnel plot regression (Fig. 8). (Deeks et al., 2005) A two-sided α = 0.05 was pre-specified; logDOR significance was tested by Z-test (Z = logDOR÷SE).

Study registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO International Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD422026135172).

Results

Study characteristics

Database searching (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane) identified 285 records; after deduplication, 268 records were screened by title and abstract. Of 268 screened records, 247 were excluded at title and abstract review; twenty-one full-text articles were then assessed for eligibility and 16 were excluded (case reports [n = 5], reviews [n = 4], insufficient data [n = 4], non-lumbar or non-SSEP procedures [n = 3]), yielding five eligible studies (Fig. 1, PRISMA 2020 flow diagram). The five included studies comprised a total of 1017 patients and were published between 2019 and 2026 (Table 1). Study designs included three retrospective cohort studies (Burkhard et al., Kim et al., Silverstein et al.), one retrospective case series (Chen et al.), and one prospective cohort study (Tohmeh et al.). The surgical procedures represented were posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF; Burkhard et al., 2026), prone transpsoas lateral lumbar interbody fusion (Tohmeh et al., 2022), lateral lumbar interbody fusion with multimodal femoral nerve monitoring (Silverstein et al., 2022), percutaneous endoscopic TLIF (Chen et al., 2021), and standard TLIF (Kim et al., 2019). Sample sizes ranged from 56 evaluable patients (Tohmeh et al.) to 401 (Burkhard et al.). All five studies employed multimodal IONM incorporating at least SSEP and EMG monitoring. Four studies also included MEP monitoring (Tohmeh et al., Silverstein et al., Chen et al., Kim et al.); Burkhard et al. employed SSEP and free-run EMG without MEP. The most common SSEP alarm criterion, applied in three of five studies (Tohmeh et al., Chen et al., Kim et al.), was a ≥ 50% amplitude reduction or ≥ 10% peak latency increase from baseline; Burkhard et al. applied amplitude reduction only (latency criterion was not used), and Silverstein et al. used a qualitative FNEP degradation threshold rather than a fixed numeric criterion.

Outcomes

Across the five included studies, 82 of 1017 patients (8.1%) exhibited significant intraoperative SSEP changes. Postoperative neurologic deficits were documented in 45 patients (4.4%). Of the 82 patients with SSEP changes, 13 (15.9%) also developed postoperative deficits (true positives), while 69 (84.1%) did not (false positives). Of the 45 patients with deficits, 32 (71.1%) had no SSEP change recorded intraoperatively (false negatives). Reversible SSEP changes were reported in three studies (Burkhard et al., Tohmeh et al., Silverstein et al.; n = 50 total events as reported in the source publications), with neurologic deficits occurring in only 2 patients (4.0%). Irreversible or persistent changes were also reported in the same three studies (n = 18 events), with 9 patients (50.0%) developing deficits. Note: the sum of reversible (50) and irreversible (18) events exceeds the total SSEP changes in these three studies (n = 63); this likely reflects patients in whom both reversible and irreversible changes were recorded at different intraoperative time points, as reported in the source publications.

Data analysis

All significant SSEP changes

The pooled sensitivity for predicting postoperative neurologic deficit across all significant SSEP change types was 36.4% (95% CI: 10.7–73.2%) and pooled specificity was 91.7% (95% CI: 74.6–97.6%). Sensitivity varied considerably across individual studies. Kim et al. (2019) reported zero SSEP changes; after continuity correction (0.5 added to all cells), the apparent sensitivity was 4.2%, and this study contributed a logDOR of 3.14 driven by its near-perfect specificity rather than its sensitivity. Among the four studies with measurable SSEP events, sensitivity ranged from 13.6% (Burkhard et al., 2026) to 85.7% (Tohmeh et al., 2022). Specificity ranged from 49.0% (Tohmeh et al.) to 98.9% (Kim et al., 2019). Individual study logDOR values ranged from 0.38 (Chen et al., 2021) to 3.14 (Kim et al., 2019). Under the fixed-effects model, the pooled logDOR was 1.652 (SE 0.447), corresponding to a DOR of 5.22 (95% CI: 2.17–12.52). The random-effects model yielded an identical logDOR of 1.652 (SE 0.447), DOR 5.22 (95% CI: 2.17–12.52; Z = 3.70, P = .0002), as τ2 = 0 (see heterogeneity below).

Heterogeneity was low across all five studies (Q = 1.73, df = 4, P = .785; I2 = 0.0%, 95% CI: 0.0–0.0%), and between-study variance was negligible (τ2 = 0.000). Because Q-based tests are critically underpowered with k = 5, the apparent absence of heterogeneity should not be interpreted as confirming true homogeneity; τ2 estimates are downwardly biased in small samples. Forest plots are presented in Fig. 2, Fig. 3. Pooled logDOR results by subgroup are presented in Table 3. For the all-SSEP and reversible-SSEP subgroups, fixed-effects and random-effects estimates are numerically identical because the DerSimonian–Laird estimator yields τ2 = 0, causing the random-effects model to collapse to the fixed-effects solution; this is an expected mathematical consequence, not an error. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed pooled DOR estimates ranging from 3.76 (95% CI: 1.37–10.38; excluding Burkhard) to 6.94 (95% CI: 2.69–17.90; excluding Chen); no single study reversed the direction of effect. Excluding Tohmeh yielded DOR 4.16 (95% CI: 1.71–10.10; I2 = 0.0%).

Reversible SSEP changes

Among the three studies reporting reversible SSEP changes (Burkhard et al., Tohmeh et al., Silverstein et al.), 50 reversible events were recorded and only 2 patients developed postoperative neurologic deficits (4.0%). The fixed-effects pooled DOR was 0.69 (95% CI: 0.18–2.66) and the random-effects pooled DOR was 0.69 (95% CI: 0.18–2.66; P = .594). No heterogeneity was present in this subgroup (I2 = 0.0%, 95% CI: 0.0–0.0%, Q = 0.85, P = .654; underpowered for k = 3). The forest plot for reversible SSEP changes is presented in Fig. 4.

Forest plot of logDOR—reversible SSEP changes (n = 3 studies). Pooled DOR = 0.69 (95% CI: 0.18–2.66; P = .594); I2 = 0.0% (95% CI: 0.0–0.0%). DOR, diagnostic odds ratio; LLIF, lateral lumbar interbody fusion; SSEP, somatosensory evoked potential.

Irreversible/persistent SSEP changes

Irreversible or persistent SSEP changes demonstrated substantially greater discriminatory ability. Among 18 recorded irreversible events across three studies, 9 patients (50.0%) developed postoperative deficits. The fixed-effects pooled DOR was 14.11 (95% CI: 4.61–43.23) and the random-effects estimate was 22.12 (95% CI: 3.06–159.7), with wider confidence intervals reflecting moderate between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 58.1%, 95% CI: 0.0–84.7%, Q = 4.77, P = .092; k = 3). The pooled DOR of 22.12 was statistically significant (Z = 3.07, P = .002), indicating that persistent intraoperative SSEP signal change is a strong predictor of postoperative neurologic deficit. The forest plot for irreversible SSEP changes is presented in Fig. 5.

Total SSEP signal loss

Total loss of SSEP signal was not consistently reported as a discrete event category across the five included studies. As a result, a separate pooled analysis for total SSEP signal loss could not be performed. This subgroup could not be compared with the prior meta-analysis by Chang et al. (2021), which reported a DOR of 23.91 (95% CI: 7.18–79.65) for total signal loss across 5607 patients. (Chang et al., 2021).

Fig. 6 displays the five individual study operating points with 95% confidence intervals for sensitivity (vertical bars) and 1 − specificity (horizontal bars), and the pooled summary operating point (sensitivity 36.4%, 1 − specificity 8.3%) derived from the DerSimonian–Laird random-effects logit models. Fagan nomogram and post-test probabilities

The Fagan nomogram analysis is summarised in Table 4 and illustrated in Fig. 7. Using the pooled sensitivity (36.4%) and specificity (91.7%) derived from the DerSimonian–Laird random-effects logit models, and a pre-test probability (prevalence) of 4.4% (45 deficits in 1017 patients), the positive likelihood ratio (LR+) was calculated as 4.39 and the negative likelihood ratio (LR−) as 0.69. The presence of a significant intraoperative SSEP change raised the post-test probability of neurologic deficit from 4.4% to 16.9%, representing an approximately four-fold increase. Conversely, the absence of a significant SSEP change reduced the post-test probability to 3.1%, reflecting a modest reduction in post-test probability; the LR − = 0.69 does not substantially rule out deficit and should not be interpreted as conferring strong negative predictive value.

Publication Bias

Funnel plot asymmetry was evaluated by Deeks' funnel plot regression; the funnel plot is presented in Fig. 8. No significant asymmetry was detected (Deeks test P = .819); however, publication bias cannot be excluded given the small number of included studies.

Discussion

Across five contemporary studies (1017 patients; 2019–2026), intraoperative SSEP monitoring is highly specific (91.7%) but only moderately sensitive (36.4%) for postoperative neurologic deficits (DOR 5.22). The lower DOR vs Chang et al. (22.13) reflects the smaller sample, fewer events, and inclusion of high false-positive-rate LLIF cohorts with saphenous SSEP monitoring. The substantially greater diagnostic weight of irreversible changes (DOR 22.12; 95% CI: 3.06–159.7) versus reversible changes (DOR 0.69; 95% CI: 0.18–2.66; P = .594) replicates the fundamental principle that signal persistence—not mere occurrence—is the primary determinant of clinical significance; the ratio of point estimates (approximately 32-fold) carries wide uncertainty that precludes a precise quantification.

Clinically, a normal intraoperative SSEP trace (LR − = 0.69; post-test probability 3.1%) provides only modest probability reduction and does not substantially rule out a deficit (pre-test 4.4% → post-test 3.1%; a clinically meaningful negative test requires LR− < 0.1). Any significant SSEP change (LR + = 4.39; post-test probability 16.9%) warrants investigation. Critically, only persistent changes should prompt escalating intervention: reversible changes normalising before closure carry no statistically significant additional risk (DOR 0.69; P = .594), whereas irreversible changes carry a DOR of 22.12, indicating substantially greater diagnostic discriminability for postoperative deficit.

The inclusion of saphenous SSEP monitoring in LLIF substantially influenced the pooled estimates, accounting for the lower pooled sensitivity (36.4% vs. 44% in Chang et al.) and lower specificity (91.7% vs. 97%). The Tohmeh et al. cohort demonstrated 85.7% sensitivity but only 49.0% specificity, reflecting susceptibility of femoral nerve SSEP signals to non-injury positioning-related events; SSEP amplitude reductions have also been documented with interbody cage placement in the absence of neurological injury (Duncan et al., 2012). These observations underscore that monitoring modality selection must match the neural structures at risk, and that non-neurological technical factors must be considered when interpreting intraoperative signal changes.

Recent evidence further characterises a specific mechanism underlying non-neurological SSEP attenuation in LLIF. Allison et al. (2025) reported that breaking the surgical bed to lower the iliac crest during lateral decubitus LLIF caused downside-leg sciatic nerve distribution SSEP, transcranial motor evoked potential (TcMEP), and triggered MEP (TaMEP) attenuation in 18 of 692 cases (2.6%), correlating with sciatic nerve compression or stretch at the pelvis rather than neural injury. Female sex and BMI ≥30 were the principal risk factors (chi-square p < .001). Critically, none of the 18 patients sustained a postoperative deficit, confirming the benign nature of this positional artefact. (Allison et al., 2025) This finding has direct implications for SSEP interpretation in LLIF: surgeons and neurophysiologists should immediately assess bed position when downside-leg SSEP attenuation occurs in isolation, and partially or completely unbreak the bed as soon as feasible before escalating to neurological intervention. Prompt recognition of this phenomenon would reduce unnecessary false-positive responses and inappropriate surgical interruption.

This meta-analysis complements Chang et al. (2021) by providing procedure-specific diagnostic accuracy estimates for the post-2019 era, with a cohort weighted toward contemporary minimally invasive approaches (prone transpsoas LLIF, FNEP-monitored LLIF, PE-TLIF) absent from the prior evidence base.

Key limitations include: small total adverse event count (45 deficits) limiting subgroup power; small number of included studies (k = 5); an SROC summary curve is not presented owing to unreliable curve-fitting with k = 5 heterogeneous studies (Fig. 6 shows individual study operating points and pooled summary with CI ellipse); procedural and protocol heterogeneity across studies; retrospective design in four of five studies; inability to pool total SSEP signal loss separately; potential conflict-of-interest in Tohmeh et al., which involved a proprietary monitoring device and reported author disclosures (see source publication); non-standard monitoring protocols in Chen et al.; and absence of long-term follow-up to distinguish transient from permanent deficits.

Conclusions

Across five contemporary lumbar spine surgery studies (1017 patients; 2019–2026), intraoperative SSEP monitoring is highly specific (91.7%; 95% CI: 74.6–97.6%) but only moderately sensitive (36.4%; 95% CI: 10.7–73.2%) for predicting postoperative neurologic deficits, with a pooled random-effects DOR of 5.22 (95% CI: 2.17–12.52). The persistence of the SSEP change is the critical determinant of its predictive value: irreversible changes carry substantially greater diagnostic weight (DOR 22.12; 95% CI: 3.06–159.7) than reversible changes (DOR 0.69; 95% CI: 0.18–2.66; P = .594). The lower pooled sensitivity relative to Chang et al. (2021) reflects the inclusion of LLIF cohorts with femoral nerve-targeted SSEP monitoring. These findings support a procedure-specific, context-dependent approach to SSEP monitoring in lumbar spine surgery; adjunct MEP and EMG monitoring are recommended where motor root injury is the predominant risk. Prospective studies with standardised alarm criteria, outcome definitions, and monitoring protocols are required to establish definitive evidence-based guidelines for IONM in lumbar spine surgery.
