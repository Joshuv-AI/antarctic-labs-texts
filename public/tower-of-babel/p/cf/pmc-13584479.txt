Genomic evidence of ecological flexibility and cross-niche CRISPR spacerome targeting phage–plasmid hybrids in Latilactobacillus curvatus
Authors: Ibrahim C. Kurt, Huseyin Guner, Zeynep A. Erdem, Ozge Can, Ismail Gumustop, Ahmet Sirin, Ismail Erol, Enes S. Kotil, Fatih Ortakci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLatilactobacillus curvatus is a lactic acid bacterium with a remarkable ability to persist in diverse niches, including fermented foods and gut. Despite its industrial and potential probiotic relevance, the genomic underpinnings of its cross-niche adaptability remain poorly characterized.MethodsWe conducted a species-contextualized comparative genomic analysis of 53 L. curvatus strains from food and gut isolates. This analysis integrated pangenome structure, metabolic repertoire, CRISPR-Cas immunity profiles, and mobilome analysis. Additionally, binding mode predictions and dynamics simulations were used to evaluate the theoretical binding energies of bacteriocins to the BamA target.ResultsPhylogenomics revealed a polyphyletic population structure, indicating that long-term evolution is not strictly niche-specific. In contrast, genome-wide similarity showed clustering by isolation source, highlighting horizontal gene transfer (HGT) as a plausible contributor to niche adaptation. We identified a highly active mobilome, encompassing diverse plasmids, IS elements, and multiple intact prophages, reflecting high genomic plasticity characteristic of a multihabitat lifestyle. CRISPR-Cas systems were widespread, and analysis of 2,029 spacers revealed a broad immune repertoire targeting mobile genetic elements represented in fermented food, gut, and environmental datasets. We also identified spacer matches to phage-plasmid hybrid-like elements, highlighting the diversity of mobile genetic elements associated with the L. curvatus spacerome.ConclusionOur study reveals genomic features consistent with ecological flexibility in L. curvatus, including high genomic plasticity and a broad CRISPR spacer repertoire. Rather than demonstrating strict niche-specific evolution or a causal mechanism for cross-niche persistence, these findings support the hypothesis that this species has experienced diverse interactions with mobile genetic elements across multiple ecological contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13098-8.

Introduction

Latilactobacillus (L.) curvatus (formerly Lactobacillus curvatus) is a facultatively heterofermentative, rod-shaped lactic acid bacterium commonly exhibiting a characteristic crescent morphology. It has a G + C content of 42–44 mol% and possesses a lysine–D-aspartyl-type peptidoglycan cell wall, with DSM 20019 designated as the type strain. The species grows at 15 °C but not at 45 °C, and certain strains tolerate cold conditions between 2 °C and 4 °C. L. curvatus produces both D- and L-lactic acid isomers and ferments a limited range of carbohydrates, including cellobiose, esculin, gluconate, and melibiose [1].

Originally described as Bacterium curvatum by Troili in 1903 [2], the species underwent several taxonomic revisions—renamed as Lactobacillus curvatus [3, 4] and later reclassified into the genus Latilactobacillus by Zheng et al. [5, 6]. It shares high DNA sequence homology with Latilactobacillus sakei, though the two species differ in arginine hydrolysis and maltose fermentation [7]; differentiation requires species-specific DNA probes [8].

Ecologically, L. curvatus displays a remarkable habitat breadth. It occurs in spontaneously fermented foods such as sauerkraut, sourdough, and raw sausages [9, 10], as well as in poultry and fermented meat products [11, 12]. It is also isolated from dairy matrices, including milk and cheese [13], fermented plant foods such as kimchi [10, 14, 15] and sourdough [16], and other plant-based sources like honey [17]. Its detection in the gastrointestinal tracts or feces of animals consuming plants or grains—including snails [18], chickens [19], and humans [20]—highlights its ability to transition between food-associated and gut environments. Such ubiquity suggests that L. curvatus may exhibit substantial ecological flexibility and considerable intra-species genomic diversity [21].

Beyond its ecological versatility, L. curvatus plays key functional roles in fermentation and health. Strains are frequently employed as starter or adjunct cultures in meat fermentations [9, 10], contributing to acidification and flavor development, and many produce class II bacteriocins such as curvacin A and sakacin P, effective against Listeria monocytogenes and Staphylococcus aureus [22, 23]. The first genome sequence—strain CRL 705 from Argentinean fermented sausage—revealed genes encoding two-component bacteriocins lactocin 705 and AL705 [24]. Moreover, genomic studies uncovered extensive carbohydrate metabolism repertoires, including glucose and trehalose pathways [25], supporting metabolic flexibility across niches.

Recent findings also indicate potential probiotic functions. L. curvatus WiKim38 alleviates dextran sodium sulfate–induced colitis in mice [26], while other strains mitigate obesity and hyperlipidemia [25]. Together, these observations underscore the industrial and biomedical significance of this species.

Despite its prevalence and functional potential, the genomic diversity and ecological adaptation mechanisms of L. curvatus remain insufficiently characterized. Previous comparative studies across a limited number of genomes [21] suggested extensive population heterogeneity, but the evolutionary and ecological dynamics shaping its cross-niche persistence are unclear. In this study, we present a species-contextualized comparative genomics analysis of L. curvatus from food and gut isolates, integrating pangenome structure, metabolic repertoire, and CRISPR–Cas immunity profiles. Our findings provide new insights into its genomic diversity, mobilome composition, and CRISPR-mediated immune history across food- and gut-associated isolates.

Materials and methods

Genome annotation

Whole-genome sequence assemblies of 59 L. curvatus strains were downloaded from NCBI with the accession numbers shown in Table S1. Genome quality was screened via BUSCO (version 5.4.3) [27] with lactobacillales_odb10 lineage. Genome annotations were performed by Prokka (version 1.14.5) using default settings.

Comparative genomics of L. curvatus

The genomic similarity among 53 L. curvatus strains was performed by Jaccard distance via the prabclus package [28] based on presence/absence of genes. The data generated were run by Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) using R (version 4.1.1) [29, 30] to determine the relationship across genomes.

The GFF files were processed using Roary (version 3.13.0) [31] to analyze the pan- and core genomes of L. curvatus [32]. Specifically, the pipeline was configured to produce a multifasta core gene alignment utilizing MAFFT (flags -e -n) and to generate accompanying plots utilizing R (flag -r). To determine pangenome openness of the L. curvatus, micropan package [33] (i.e., 10,000 permutations) to fit Heap’s law was used. Through these computational methods, we gained insights into the genetic architecture and pangenomic variation of L. curvatus.

To infer the evolutionary relationships among the L. curvatus strains, a core genome SNP-based phylogenetic tree was constructed. The core SNP alignment (core.full.aln) was processed using the scripts provided in the Snippy GitHub repository (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy). During this process, recombination events were filtered using Gubbins [34]. The final phylogenetic tree was generated with FastTree 2 [35] using the -gtr and -nt parameters. The resulting tree was visualized and annotated via iTOL [36].

Average nucleotide identity (CDS ANI) of whole genomes were calculated by GET_HOMOLOGUES [37]. dbCAN2 meta server was used to annotate Carbohydrate Active Enzymes (CAZymes) contained by L. curvatus genomes. For CAZyme annotation, we first obtained the CAZy database (v11) and then used HMMER (v3.1b2) to identify relevant domains [38, 39]. CAZyme annotations were filtered in dbCAN2 using default parameters. Isolates were subsequently categorized based on their CAZyme family profiles. Putative bacteriocin-related genes were identified with BAGEL4 [40]. Plasmids were identified via PLSDB [41, 42]. Prophages were detected using PHASTEST [43].

CRISPR

CRISPR analyses were carried out as previously described [44, 45]. Briefly, CRISPR arrays were detected and spacers extracted using CRISPRviz [46]. CRISPR–Cas subtypes were classified with CRISPRCasFinder and CRISPRClassify [47, 48]. Putative protospacers were identified using PAMPredict, which performs BLAST searches against the IMG/VR viral and IMG/PR plasmid databases [49]. Viral elements within the target sequences were annotated with Cenote-Taker3 [50]. Protospacer-flanking sequences were aligned in Geneious, and genome maps and PAM logos were visualized using the same software.

Bacteriocin binding analyses

The binding modes of bacteriocins, derived from 53 L. curvatus genomes, against BamA protein were predicted by co-folding two sequences (BamA-bacteriocin) using Boltz-2 [51]. The predicted BamA-bacteriocin complexes were analyzed for the interaction interface profiles using Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP) [52]. Obtained BamA-bacteriocin complexes were subjected to pKa analysis using Reduce [53] and protonation states were assigned at pH = 7. The orientation of BamA inside the membrane bilayer was predicted using PPM3.0 webserver [54]. Obtained BamA-bacteriocin complexes were inserted into 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) model lipid bilayer using packmol-memgen protocol [55], which is provided through AmberTools25 [56, 57]. The rectangular simulation box consists of protein, bacteriocin, POPC lipids, water, and 0.15 M concentration of NaCl. For water, the TIP3P model was used [58]. The simulation box was energy minimized using sander [57], then obtained relaxed structure was converted into GROMACS with amb2gro script provided in AmberTools25 [56]. The amber19sb force field [59] was used for the protein, solvent, and ions, and lipid21 force field was used for lipids [60].GROMACS version 2024.2 was used for MD simulations [61]. V-rescale thermostat [62] and Parrinello-Rahman barostat [63] were used in production simulations, with 0.1 and 2 ps coupling times. Three independent MD runs were performed as 100 ns; 1000 frames were recorded for each run. Obtained trajectories were used in MM/GBSA calculations, where gmx_MMPBSA tool was used [64].

Results

Genomic characteristics

Fifty-nine L. curvatus genomes were downloaded from NCBI GenBank. All genomes were subjected to BUSCO [27] analysis to determine the completeness of the genomes (Figure S1). Four genomes were excluded from further analysis due to showing below the threshold level (98%) completeness. Two genomes (VRA_2sq_f and VRA_2sq_n) were also excluded because of the wrong taxonomic classification in NCBI which was detected after running the ANI test. Table S1 represents fifty-nine L. curvatus strains isolated from different ecologies such as milk, salami, sourdough, fermented meat and vegetables, pickles, cheese, silage, feces, and starter cultures.

Comparative genomics

A SNP-based phylogenetic tree constructed from 53 genomes revealed that strains isolated from similar ecological niches do not form monophyletic clusters but are instead dispersed across the tree, indicating a polyphyletic distribution (Fig. 1A). This pattern suggests that Latilactobacillus curvatus does not exhibit niche-specific phylogenetic structuring or long-term ecological specialization. Instead, closely related strains can be recovered from markedly different environments, while strains from the same niche are often phylogenetically distant. The position of the reference strain DSM 20019 further illustrates this trend, as it clusters with isolates from multiple, ecologically distinct sources. Overall, the phylogenetic structure reflects a broad environmental distribution and genomic adaptability and suggests that vertical inheritance alone does not explain ecological distribution in this species.

A genomic conservation analysis conducted on fifty-three isolates regarding their pan- and core genomes indicated that 13.68% of all genes were conserved with a 95% BLASTP identity (Fig. 3). Functional annotations

The largest core- and pangenome categories were associated with genes with function unknown (Fig. 4). The second largest pangenome category was related to replication, recombination, and repair-associated genes. Transcription, cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis, and carbohydrate transport and metabolism had similar pan and core genome-related genes as they pertained to functional COG categories. Secondary metabolites biosynthesis, transport and catabolism, signal transduction mechanisms and cell motility had the least number of core- and pangenome associated genes.

Three main clades were identified based on CAZyme composition, suggesting metabolic specializations that may reflect the carbohydrate profiles of their isolation niches. Clade 1, which includes meat-associated strains like MRS6, exhibited a high abundance of Glycoside Hydrolases (GHs). This enrichment may be linked to the breakdown of complex animal-derived glycans or the diverse sugars (e.g., glucose, sucrose) typically added during meat fermentation. In contrast, Clade 2 (the largest group) showed a high ratio of Glycosyltransferases (GTs), which are essential for the biosynthesis of exopolysaccharides—a trait that contributes to biofilm formation in food matrices and supports general environmental resilience (Fig. 5).

Bacteriocins binding affinity calculations

Thirty-eight percent of the L. curvatus genomes analyzed in the current study were predicted to carry 9 unique bacteriocins namely Carnocin_CP52, Sakacin_Q, Sakacin_P_(Sakacin674), Brochocin_C_BrcA, Enterocin_NKR-5–3 A, SakT_alpha, Enterocin_NKR-5-3D, Lactocin_S, and Curvacin_A (Table S5). This study is structured to understand the possible interactions with the studied bacteriocins and BamA. To gain insight, available peptide–BamA complexes were investigated for the interaction sites and the types. Following PDB IDs were considered: 9CS0, 9CS1, 9CS2, 9HG9, 9HGA, 9HE1, 9CO2, 9CNX, 9CNZ, 9CO0, 8QPU, 8Q0G, 8QN4, 8PZU, 8PZV, 8QP5, 8QPV, 8SQA, 8SQB, 7YE4, 7YE6, 8BVQ, 8BNZ, 8ADG, 8ADI, 7R1V, 7R1W, 7P1C, 7TSZ, 7TT0, 7TT1, 7TT2, 7TT3, 7TT4, 7TT5, 7TT6, 7TT7, 7TTC, 7RI4, 7RJ5, 7NRE, 7NRF, 7NRI, 6LYQ, 6LYR, and 6T1W.

The PLIP webserver was utilized to shed light on the binding modes of respective peptides to BamA. Then, the obtained information was used to compare the similarities between the predicted binding modes of the studied bacteriocins and experimental binding modes. Based on PLIP analyses, Brochocin_C_BrcA was found to have similar interactions with BamA as observed in Cyclic peptide CP2 binding in 9CS1. Carnocin_CP52 resembles the binding mode of outer membrane lipoprotein RcsF and interacts with BamA, where similar interactions are observed in 6T1W. The interactions observed in Curvacin_A and BamA complexes obtained in cofolding both sequences using boltz-2 were found to be similar to those observed in BamA–Serine protease EspP chimera complex (PDB ID: 7TSZ). Enterocin_NKR-5–3 A binding mode was similar to Cyclic peptide CP2 binding in 9CS1, which was also observed in the Brochocin_C_BrcA binding. For the Enterocin_NKR-5-3D binding, the residues that were involved in interactions were found to be similar to those observed in PTB2 circular peptide binding to BamA (PDB ID: 9CO2). EspP epsilon binding mode, which is observed in BamA (PDB ID: 8QN4), was similar to the binding mode of Lactocin_S, which was predicted by boltz-2. Sakacin_P binding was very similar to outer membrane lipoprotein RcsF binding in 6T1W structure. Similarities between Sakacin_Q and Cyclic peptide CP3 binding to BamA (PDB ID: 9CS2) were observed, where Cyclic peptide CP3 interacted with the receptor from an internal binding site; however, Sakacin_Q interacted with the receptor from the outer surface. Outer membrane lipoprotein RcsF binding in 6T1W has similar interactions with SakT_alpha, where SakT_alpha is located on the outer surface.

The lowest backbone RMSD values for BamA was observed in Sakacin P as an average of 0.15 ± 0.02 nm (Figure S3). When backbone RMSD values of studied bacteriocins were compared, the lowest average value was observed in Carnocin CP52 with 0.14 ± 0.02 nm (Figure S4). This suggests that Carnocin CP52 is the most rigid bacteriocin in this set, though Sakacin P induces the least structural change in the BamA protein.

The highest backbone RMSD values for BamA was observed in SakT alpha as average of 0.30 ± 0.12 nm (Figure S5). When backbone RMSD values of studied bacteriocins were compared, the highest average value was observed in Brochocin with 0.47 ± 0.10 nm (Figure S6). This suggests Brochocin is the most dynamically unstable molecule, while SakT alpha is the least effective at maintaining BamA’s structural integrity.

The raw MMGBSA scores were divided by the corresponding number of non-hydrogen (heavy) atoms in the bacteriocin ligand. This normalization step yielded a per-heavy-atom binding energy (MMGBSA score/Heavy Atom Count) for each complex, which facilitates a more robust and direct comparison of intrinsic binding affinities among ligands of different sizes. The Efficiency Score in Table 2 serves as a metric for binding energy efficiency, with more negative values indicating better efficiency. The BamA-Enterocin NKR-5-3D system exhibits the lowest energy efficiency score at − 0.271, suggesting it has the most favorable binding energy per heavy atom count among all tested systems (Figure S7). This high efficiency is achieved despite having the lowest Heavy Atom Count (346). Conversely, the BamA-Brochocin C system displays the highest (least favorable) efficiency score at − 0.108 (Figure S8). This indicates that BamA-Brochocin C is the least efficient binder, delivering the lowest favorable MMGBSA score relative to its size (511 heavy atoms). Notably, while BamA-Lactocin S has the most negative MMGBSA score (− 130.08 kcal/mol) (Figure S9) and thus the strongest binding energy, its larger size (504 heavy atoms) results in a less efficient score (− 0.258) compared to the smallest and most efficient binder, BamA-Enterocin NKR-5-3D.

Mobile Genetic Elements (MGEs)

IS Elements

The IS elements detected via ISfinder among L. curvatus gnomes were given in Table S4. A remarkable variety of Insertion Sequence (IS) elements derived from several bacterial species was evident. The L. curvatus screened in the present study did not acquire these elements from a shared ancestor but have instead independently obtained them from their surroundings perhaps via HGT. The species providing these donor elements include Latilactobacillus sakei, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Lacticaseibacillus casei, Lactobacillus delbrueckii, Lactobacillus helveticus, Pediococcus pentosaceus, Leuconostoc mesenteroides, Enterococcus species, and Lactococcus lactis in addition to more distantly related organisms such as Listeria monocytogenes and Staphylococcus aureus.

The IS families that are most frequently identified among L. curvatus genomes screened include IS3 and IS30, with IS6 and IS256 being less common. The high prevalence of these families, especially from L. sakei and L. plantarum, that are conventional starters in meat and vegetable fermentations, indicates that they are common genetic exchange partners with L. curvatus in food environments.

Plasmids

Table S3 shows the plasmids identified in L. curvatus strains evaluated in the present study. The plasmid identified by the accession CP117685.1 is by far the most prevalent, found in the majority of the examined strains. Several other plasmids were often detected in various strains, such as NZ_CP026117.1 (JCM 1096), NZ_AP012546.1 (from Lacticaseibacillus casei), NZ_CP016468.1 (from Latilactobacillus curvatus). Almost all of the plasmids identified were circular, with the exception of the linear plasmid NZ_CP022476.1. The majority of L. curvatus strains possess a distinct assortment of various plasmids. For instance, strain K285 is characterized by the presence of 9 unique plasmids. Strain TMW_1_2270 is identified by having 11 different plasmids. In contrast, strains such as HFS9 and WiKim38 exhibit a simpler profile with just 2 plasmids.

Many strains harbor plasmids that are derived from related bacterial species, suggesting the possibility of horizontal gene transfer. The most frequently encountered foreign plasmids came from Latilactobacillus sakei (e.g., NZ_LT960786.1, NZ_LT960779.1) and Lacticaseibacillus casei (NZ_AP012546.1). Specific groups of plasmids consistently co-occur. A particularly prevalent combination is CP117685.1, NZ_AP012546.1, and NZ_CP026117.1, which can be found in many strains (e.g., 3_8_43, DRC2305, ZJUNIT8).

Prophages

The 53 L. curvatus genomes harbor a robust and diverse reservoir of prophages, with 75 regions identified as “intact” (Table S2). The number of intact prophages per strain varied, with several strains (e.g., ZHA1 and LBAE_E14) carrying up to four distinct integrated viral elements. The GC content of these prophages (mean 40.23%) was slightly shorter than the average host GC content (~ 42%), consistent with their origin from distinct genomic backgrounds and the incomplete amelioration of nucleotide composition following HGT [65].

Crucially, the “most common phage” analysis revealed significant inter-generic diversity. While many prophages were homologous to known Lactobacillus phages (e.g., phig1e, PL_1, and A2), a substantial portion of the reservoir consisted of elements homologous to phages from disparate genera, including PHAGE_Oenoco_phiS13 (found in 8 strains), PHAGE_Lister_2389 (found in 3 strains), and PHAGE_Lactoc_50101. The presence of Oenococcus and Listeria-associated viral signatures suggests a history of HGT occurring in shared ecological niches, such as fermented vegetable matrices and meat processing environments.

CRISPR

CRISPRClassify detected repeat sequences belonging to 49 strains and predicted a total of 71 type II-A, 24 type I-E, and 6 type I-B loci, with sporadic detection of type III-B and II-C repeats [48] (Supplementary File 1). The analysis of the assembled genomes revealed that most strains contained at least one CRISPR–Cas system. Among the 49 analyzed strains, 100% carried a Type II-A system and 30.6% carried a Type I-E system, with 30.6% of the strains harboring both concurrently. CRISPR arrays displayed considerable variation in size, ranging from 1 to 68 spacers, with a total of 2,029 redundant (1,505 unique) spacers detected across the 49 analyzed L. curvatus strains(Supplementary File 2). A total of 11 distinct repeat consensus sequences were identified, several of which were conserved across multiple strains. Notably, the repeat with the consensus sequence GTTTTAGAAGAGTATCAAATCAATGAGTAGTTCAAC was detected in 17 strains, encompassing isolates from dairy, meat, and gut origins. Overall, the pan-genome of L. curvatus is characterized by a high prevalence of CRISPR-Cas systems, with frequent co-occurence of Type II-A and Type I-E types.

To explore the ecological origins of the mobile genetic elements (MGE) targeted by the CRISPR resistome, unique spacers were queried against viral and plasmid sequence databases using PAMPredict. This resulted in 5680 significant (e-value < = 0.009) BLAST hits originating from 596 unique spacers. (Supplementary File 3) The protospacer-flanking motif (PAM) logo generated from all predicted targets revealed an A-rich dominant 5′-NNAAA-3′ consensus motif. (Fig. 6A and Figure S10)

Comparative analysis of spacer–protospacer matches revealed strong ecological structure in the L. curvatus CRISPR landscape. Strains isolated from fermented foods (such as TMW, CIRM-BIA, CRL, and LBAE strains) displayed high-identity spacer hits against fermented-meat, fermented-grain, and dairy-associated viral and plasmid categories. However, because spacers represent historical immune memory and database categories are unevenly sampled, these associations should be interpreted cautiously as signatures of past exposure rather than direct evidence of ongoing niche-specific interactions.

Subtype-level clustering further showed that Type II-A CRISPR–Cas systems accounted for the majority of spacer matches, spanning food, gut, and waste environments. (Fig. 6B) Type II-A spacers exhibited the broadest and highest-identity coverage, particularly within fermented-meat, fermented-dairy, and waste-related categories, aligning with the distribution of food-associated strains. In contrast, Type I-E systems were less frequent and showed narrower targeting, with hits primarily in environment-soil, lab-simulated, and waste datasets, while Type I-B loci were rare and sparsely distributed. Collectively, the strain- and subtype-level data indicate that L. curvatus maintains an extensive CRISPR spacer repertoire dominated by Type II-A systems, with targeting patterns consistent with exposure to MGEs represented across food-, gut-, and environment-associated datasets.

Overall, high-identity protospacers were detected in both viral and plasmid databases, indicating that L. curvatus spacers record past encounters with MGEs represented in diverse environmental and host-associated datasets.

The brewery-derived plasmids SEQ93612 and SEQ38922 both contained protospacers matching spacers from human fecal isolates (IRG 2 and HFS 9) (Fig. 8). In SEQ93612, several phage-related genes, including terminase small subunit, tyrosine recombinase, and MazEF toxin–antitoxin module, alongside DNA polymerase and N-6 DNA methylase genes were detected. The viral contig SEQ14096154, originating from a human-gut metagenome, included protospacers matching a spacer from human gut isolate MGYG-HGUT-00020 and was annotated with characteristic phage structural genes, including tail fiber, tail assembly, lytic transglycosylase, integrase, and P22-type repressor proteins, confirming its classification as a temperate bacteriophage (Fig. 8).

The plasmid SEQ115738, isolated from corn silage, contained protospacers targeted by spacers from ELA214388 and TMW 1.1928. Although primarily plasmid-encoded, this element also contained phage-related genes, including an XRE-family transcriptional regulator and relaxase mobilization nuclease, along with PriCT1 primase, P-type ATPase, and Dps-like DNA-binding protein.

Among all viral and plasmid sequences identified through PAMPredict, two phage genomes—SEQ15405602 and SEQ488270—received the highest number of PAM-verified L. curvatus spacer hits and were therefore examined in detail (Figure S13-14). Discussion

In the present work, we conducted a genome-wide exploration of fifty-three strains of Latilactobacillus curvatus representing fermented foods and gut microbiomes. The genome sizes range between 1.8 and 2.2 Mbp, which falls within the expected range of lactic acid bacteria (i.e., 1.8 to 3.3 Mb) [66–68]. The GC content achieved for L. curvatus (i.e., 42%) is consistent with the low-GC content typically observed in LAB. The number of coding sequences (CDS) differs among strains, indicating genetic variability associated with adaptation to various environments, likely contributed by the accessory genome (for example, food fermentation vs. gut) [69, 70]. Whole-genome sequence analysis determined 45 unique plasmids, with CP117685.1 being the most ubiquitous plasmid found in approximately 89% of strains. L. curvatus possesses a diverse and dynamic plasmidome, which enhances its genetic adaptability and allows it to thrive in multiple environments, particularly under food fermentation conditions. The extensive exchange of plasmids suggests the presence of active horizontal gene transfer networks among communities of lactic acid bacteria [71]. The pangenome of L. curvatus showcases a varied and intricate plasmidome, consisting of a core group of plasmids that are broadly disseminated, along with a sizeable accessory collection that differs among strains and frequently contains plasmids obtained from other species of lactic acid bacteria.

The lack of clustering on clade formation according to the source of isolation in the phylogenetic analysis shows that long-term (core genome) lineages are not exclusively defined by niche/source (i.e., sausage, cheese, or the human gut) during evolutionary development of Latilactobacillus curvatus. This indicates that a strain found in sausages could potentially be more closely related to a strain derived from the human gut than to another strain originating from sausages. Such a pattern indicates an expansive genetic repertoire of L. curvatus, which may facilitate its presence in diverse environments and does not emerge from a specific lineage tied to a single source [8]. However, the open nature of the pangenome indicates that the full genetic repertoire of the species has not yet been captured; thus, drawing definitive conclusions regarding niche specialization or generalist versus specialist lifestyles is preemptive.

The grouping of strains sharing the same origin in the PCoA plot suggests that their genomes are, overall, more alike than those from different origins (Fig. 1B). The clustering seen in the PCoA shows a strong link between the genetic characteristics of the strains and their source of isolation. This serves as clear evidence that the environment significantly influences the genetic composition of L. curvatus populations [72]. Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) provides the most compelling and plausible explanation for this phenomenon. Different lineages of L. curvatus that coexist in the same environment (i.e., cheese vat) can share genes through mechanisms like plasmids, phages, or transposons. These genes may be essential for adapting to that particular niche (i.e., salt tolerance, carbohydrate metabolism, or phage resistance). The phylogenetic tree showcases the core genome SNPs, which mirror the evolutionary history. Conversely, PCoA illustrates variations in the entire genome, encompassing the accessory genome. Genes that are shared or obtained through HGT can lead to strains from the same ecological niche grouping together in PCoA. This perhaps implies that their acquired common characteristics might take precedence over their ancestral differences [73].

Different strains from various lineages, when subjected to the same environmental stresses (i.e., low temperatures or high salinity), might independently acquire comparable mutations in identical genes. Although these mutations do not alter their placement on the phylogenetic tree, they can create more resemblance in their genetic profiles, resulting in clustering in PcoA [74, 75]. When considered collectively, it could perhaps be deduced that the genetic diversity in Latilactobacillus curvatus is mainly influenced by HGT and adaptation to environmental conditions rather than by long-term evolutionary history. This finding is crucial for the food industry, as starter cultures are utilized, since the functional characteristics of a strain such as acidification and flavor production are likely associated with its acquired genes through HGT or selected traits rather than its evolutionary background [21, 76, 77].

The diverse IS profile observed in L. curvatus offers a plausible explanation for the discrepancy between the SNP-based core phylogeny, which reflects vertical inheritance, and the whole-genome PCoA clustering, which is influenced by shared accessory elements among strains from similar ecological niches [74]. While the observed distribution of IS elements and plasmids across niches suggests active gene flow and highlights HGT as a likely contributor to genomic flexibility [78, 79], we acknowledge that these elements may also represent ancestral vestiges rather than contemporary adaptive acquisitions. The current analysis does not definitively establish the timing of these transfers. Future studies utilizing formal tests for phylogenetic incongruence between core genomes and MGE-specific phylogenies are required to distinguish between recent niche-specific acquisitions and stable historical records of the L. curvatus lineage [80]. Consequently, while these shared accessory pools may have practical implications for industrial strain selection, HGT is framed here as a plausible driver of genomic variation rather than definitive molecular proof of active, niche-specific adaptation.

While L. curvatus maintains a core set of carbohydrate metabolism genes, the accessory CAZyme repertoire reveals potential niche-specific adaptations. For example, strains isolated from plant-based matrices (kimchi, sourdough) often harbor CAZymes targeting plant-cell wall components. However, a clear one-to-one correlation between isolation source and CAZyme profile was not always evident, highlighting a broad environmental distribution and genomic repertoire where strains maintain a broad metabolic ‘toolkit’ to facilitate transitions between environments [5, 7, 8, 24, 81, 82]. It is worth mentioning here that CAZyme analysis in the present study is based on genomic potential lacking empirical data on the exact carbohydrate concentrations of the isolation substrates. Future research integrating high-resolution metabolomics with growth assays on niche-specific sugars is necessary to confirm the functional significance of these CAZyme clades and definitively establish the metabolic drivers of L. curvatus niche adaptation.

Twenty out of 53 L. curvatus genomes were predicted to carry unique bacteriocins that have potential antimicrobial activity (Table S5). The primary objective of in silico analyses was to identify which of the novel L. curvatus bacteriocins demonstrated the most promising inhibitory potential against the BamA protein. While bacteriocins are being explored for a wide range of applications, including recent in silico and in vitro studies into their antiviral potential against SARS-CoV-2 and other enveloped viruses [83–85], the focus of the current study was their in silico antibacterial efficacy. To estimate predicted BamA-binding potential of the bacteriocins detected in 20 L. curvatus genomes, we targeted BamA (the core component of the β-barrel assembly machinery, or BAM complex) because the BAM complex is essential and highly conserved across virtually all Gram-negative bacteria. Inhibiting BamA disrupts the proper folding and insertion of vital β-barrel outer membrane proteins (OMPs) into the bacterial outer membrane, which leads to cell envelope stress, loss of membrane integrity, and ultimately, bacterial death. Literature reports have established that certain bacteriocins function by targeting the BamA protein. Specifically, the paper by Matano et al. [86] identified that bacteroidetocins—a class of bacteriocins from Bacteroidetes—bind to and inhibit the essential outer membrane protein BamA. Similarly, the study by Ghequire et al. [87] demonstrated that lectin-like bacteriocins (LlpAs) produced by Pseudomonas species target a surface-exposed loop of BamA, resulting in the inhibition of the β-barrel assembly machinery (BAM complex) and cell death. These findings validate BamA as a critical receptor and target for a distinct mechanism of bacteriocin-mediated killing. The stability and binding energy analyses converged to highlight two exceptional candidates: Enterocin NKR-5-3D and Lactocin S. While Lactocin S exhibited the strongest binding affinity (–130.08 kcal/mol), the normalized “Efficiency Score” revealed Enterocin NKR-5-3D as the most efficient binder (–0.271 kcal/mol/atom). This is a critical distinction; the high efficiency of Enterocin NKR-5-3D, achieved despite it having the lowest heavy atom count, suggests a highly optimized and potent interaction. Conversely, the strong binding of Lactocin S is partially a function of its larger size. These findings suggest that while both are strong leads, Enterocin NKR-5-3D may represent a more “ligand-efficient” scaffold for future therapeutic development. While this study successfully identified promising bacteriocin candidates by cross-referencing predicted binding modes (via PLIP) with experimental structures, these in silico results must be interpreted with caution. The MMGBSA scores and stability metrics provide a robust computational ranking, but they remain predictions. The strong, efficient binding of Enterocin NKR-5-3D and the high affinity of Lactocin S present clear, testable hypotheses. The logical next step is to advance these in silico leads into in vitro validation. Experimental assays, such as bacterial growth inhibition studies using Gram-negative pathogens or biophysical binding assays like surface plasmon resonance (SPR), are required to confirm whether these peptides can effectively target the BamA complex and disrupt bacterial outer membrane biogenesis.

The high prevalence and diversity of intact prophages in L. curvatus provide more than just a descriptive catalog; they offer insight into the species’ “nomadic” lifestyle. Following the framework established by Pei et al. (2021) [88], the maintenance of multiple intact prophages is a hallmark of multihabitat species. These integrated elements serve as significant drivers of genomic plasticity, allowing L. curvatus to rapidly acquire new functional traits as it transitions between niches. From a functional perspective, the persistence of these “intact” regions suggests they are not merely “junk DNA” but may confer fitness advantages to the host. Prophages often encode superinfection exclusion systems, which protect the host bacterium from lysis by related competitive phages in dense environments like the human gut or fermentation vats [89]. Furthermore, the identification of Listeria-associated prophage remnants in meat-isolated strains (e.g., MRS6, SRCM217410) points to a specialized “immunological and metabolic toolkit” acquired through historical co-habitation with other food-borne microbes. This reservoir of uncharacterized viral genetic diversity likely functions as a flexible genetic source, enabling L. curvatus to bridge the gap between disparate environments—from the brewery and meat factory to the animal gastrointestinal tract—by providing pre-existing adaptive mechanisms.

Type II-A is the dominant CRISPR system across food- and gut-associated L. curvatus isolates. The adenine-rich PAM signature of L. curvatus is consistent with biochemically validated PAMs reported for several Lactobacillus type II-A Cas9s, including L. fermentum, L. ceti, Lactobacillus sp. (Lsp2) and L. rhamnosus, which recognize NNAAA, NATGNT, NCAAH(Y > A)B(Y > G) and N(T > V)NTAAW(A > T) motifs in in vitro PAM-library screens [65, 66]. These comparative data support that L. curvatus Cas9 homologs share the same adenine-biased PAM preference, where at least two adenine bases within the PAM are likely critical for target recognition. Collectively, the in-silico and experimental evidence indicate that L. curvatus maintains a canonical type II-A PAM architecture, extending the established A-rich motif pattern observed across Lactobacillus species.

The cross-category distribution of high-identity spacer–protospacer matches indicates that L. curvatus has acquired spacers against MGEs represented in food-, gut-, waste-, and other environment-associated datasets (Fig. 9A, B). One possible explanation is ecological overlap among related MGEs across these niches; alternative explanations include complex historical exposure patterns, homologous elements in distinct habitats, and uneven database representation. Therefore, these data are best interpreted as evidence for a broad and heterogeneous immune repertoire rather than a direct snapshot of ongoing cross-niche circulation.

The presence of L. curvatus spacers mapping to plasmids harboring phage-associated elements suggests that plasmids may act as intermediate vectors of viral genes within the L. curvatus mobilome. Several plasmids carrying protospacers, particularly SEQ93612 and SEQ38922, contained hallmark phage genes such as terminase subunits, tyrosine recombinases, and MazEF toxin–antitoxin modules, indicating past or ongoing phage–plasmid recombination events. These hybrid elements, recovered from brewery environments, may represent prophage remnants integrated into plasmids that persist in microbial populations associated with food fermentation. The coexistence of replication initiation factors, partitioning proteins, and phage structural or recombination genes within the same contigs highlights the fluidity between viral and plasmid gene pools.

The detection of protospacers from L. curvatus strains isolated from human feces, gut, and fermented foods across these elements further underscores their ecological interconnection. The presence of L. curvatus spacers targeting both strictly viral sequences (e.g., SEQ14096154) and plasmids with phage-like modules (e.g., SEQ115738) supports a continuum between phage infection, plasmid mobilization, and environmental persistence. This pattern suggests that L. curvatus populations likely encounter the same mobile genetic elements across distinct habitats, including food fermentation systems, gastrointestinal environments, and waste niches. The observation that multiple plasmid-associated targets carried phage hallmarks suggests that phage–plasmid hybrid-like elements may contribute to the diversity of MGE signatures represented in L. curvatus CRISPR arrays [71]. However, these associations are inferred from sequence annotation and spacer matching and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than direct mechanistic evidence of cross-niche transfer.

Interestingly, the plasmid SEQ115738, which originated from a corn-silage metagenome, was targeted by spacers from a L. curvatus strain isolated from the salmon gut. Because corn silage is commonly used as a feed component in aquaculture, this correspondence may reflect indirect environmental transfer of silage-associated microbial or plasmid DNA into the salmon gut microbiota, providing a plausible route for spacer acquisition [71].

The observation that numerous L. curvatus spacers, including several recurrent ones, map to the same two phage genomes from distant environments (aquatic and insect gut) may partly reflect representation bias within available viral sequence databases. The IMG/VR dataset may include a large number of entries derived from aquatic and insect-associated metagenomes, which could increase the likelihood of identifying matches to phages recovered from those habitats. Alternatively, the phenomenon may indicate that closely related phages occur across different ecological contexts, or that certain viral genomic regions are tightly conserved. The recurrence of spacers at identical protospacer sites among multiple L. curvatus strains could thus result from either true biological convergence—exposure to similar or homologous phages in separate environments—or from the limited diversity and uneven sampling of viral genomes in reference databases. A more comprehensive and balanced viral metagenomic coverage across food, gut, and environmental niches, collected in the future, could provide a more accurate analysis of the CRISPR immunome.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive comparative genomic overview of Latilactobacillus curvatus, highlighting substantial genomic diversity, an open pangenome structure, and a heterogeneous distribution of mobile genetic elements and CRISPR-Cas systems. The lack of clear phylogenetic clustering by isolation source suggests that long-term niche-specific evolutionary trajectories are not strongly reflected at the core genome level. The widespread presence of insertion sequences, plasmids, and prophages underscores the importance of mobile genetic elements in shaping genome architecture. However, their presence alone does not resolve the timing or adaptive significance of horizontal gene transfer events, which may reflect both historical and ongoing processes. Similarly, CRISPR spacer diversity indicates a broad record of past interactions with mobile genetic elements, but should be interpreted as historical immune memory rather than direct evidence of current ecological connectivity. Variation in CAZyme profiles further supports metabolic diversity within the species, although direct links to specific ecological niches remain to be established. Together, these findings suggest that L. curvatus exhibits genomic features consistent with ecological flexibility, but do not provide definitive evidence for a generalist lifestyle or specific adaptive mechanisms. Future work integrating functional assays, environmental metadata, and expanded genome sampling will be essential to clarify the ecological roles and evolutionary dynamics of this species. Such efforts will help place L. curvatus within the broader context of lactic acid bacteria genomics and microbial food systems.
