Cardiac Autonomic Neuropathy and Its Impact on Progression of Diabetic Kidney Disease in Type 1 and Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Kywe Kywe Soe, Dinesh Selvarajah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic kidney disease (DKD) is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease and kidney failure worldwide, with one-third of patients with diabetes developing kidney disease over the course of their lifetime. With no cure for DKD, its management is mainly conservative. Despite advancement in treatments, the rate of DKD progression to kidney failure is unpredictable, varying from months to years in different individuals. Therefore, researchers have been extensively investigating novel risk factors and biomarkers associated with advancement of DKD. One such emerging factor is cardiac autonomic neuropathy (CAN), which is widespread among diabetic population. It has become imperative to assess whether a causal relationship exists between CAN and DKD. This literature review aims to (1) summarize current evidence for the correlation between DKD and CAN in type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus, and (2) outline hypotheses for a possible causal relationship between CAN and DKD. The review covers 28 studies (of which 10 in type 1 diabetes) over the last 3 decades, including well-designed cohort and case-control analytic studies that have clearly demonstrated an association between CAN and the progression of DKD by using kidney parameters such as estimated glomerular filtration rate and urinary albuminuria.Index Words: Diabetic nephropathy, diabetic kidney disease, cardiac autonomic neuropathy

Diabetic Kidney Disease

Approximately 1 in 3 adults with diabetes develops diabetic kidney disease (DKD) over the course of their lifetime,1 and good glycemic control has been shown to prevent the onset and progression of DKD.2,3 The evolution of DKD can be described in 5 stages: hyperfiltration and kidney hypertrophy, clinically silent phase with early histological changes, moderately increased albuminuria with early increase in blood pressure, overt DKD with declining estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and advanced DKD/kidney failure.2 These stages apply to both type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM), although the progress in the later is much less predictable.2

As shown in the United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study, the risk of mortality increases with advancing stages of DKD: 1.4% (no albuminuria), 4.6% (moderately increased albuminuria), and 19.2% (chronic kidney disease [CKD] and kidney failure).4 Despite improvements in managing hyperglycemia and cardiovascular risk factors, DKD prevention remains challenging,5 and many patients still progress to kidney failure.

Early detection and treatment is crucial in effectively managing DKD. Key risk factors include hyperglycemia, hypertension, smoking, male sex, familial tendency, socioeconomic factors, and certain ethnicities (eg, Indo-Asian, African American, Pima Indian).6 For patients with type 1 DM, onset before age 20 is a specific risk factor.6 Once DKD is established, hypertension, poor glycemic control, and proteinuria accelerate progression to kidney failure.2,6

Annual screening is essential, starting at diagnosis for type 2 DM and after 5 years for type 1 DM.2,7 Primary prevention include maintaining hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) below 7% (53 mmol/mol), blood pressure below 130/80 mm Hg, managing dyslipidemia, smoking cessation, weight loss, and regular physical activity.8, 9, 10 Treatment aims to prevent progression from moderately increased albuminuria to severely increased albuminuria and further kidney function decline, while managing risk factors to prevent cardiovascular events, the leading cause of mortality in patients with DKD. However, kidney function decline remains unpredictable. Current research is focused on identifying new risk factors, prognostic factors, and biomarkers for early subclinical DKD.

Cardiac Autonomic Neuropathy

A potential risk factor for DKD is cardiac autonomic neuropathy (CAN), a common microvascular complication of DM.11 The prevalence of CAN in diabetic populations ranges from approximately 20% to 65% in individuals with longstanding diabetes.12 The risk factors for CAN include poor glycemic control, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and obesity.12

Autonomic neuropathy in diabetes progresses in an ascending length-dependent manner. The vagus nerve, which is anatomically the longest autonomic nerve and physiologically mediates 75% of overall parasympathetic activity, is affected early in the natural history of CAN. In early stage of CAN, there is an initial reduction in parasympathetic activity, resulting in relative sympathetic overactivity.13 This increase in sympathetic tone continues until the later stages of CAN, when sympathetic denervation ensues.14,15 Sympathetic predominance or overdrive results in many clinical manifestations of CAN.13 Although the timescale for the progression of CAN is unclear, the stage of sympathetic denervation correlates clinically with postural hypotension.16 Table 1 summarizes the structural and functional changes in different stages of CAN.17 Patients with CAN also have a higher risk of mortality18,19 commonly attributed to sudden cardiac death, possibly related to the abnormal lengthening of QT intervals.20

Diagnosis of CAN

There are several different methods used for assessing CAN ranging from relatively simple bedside tests, for example, heart rate variation (HRV) in the time and frequency domain21, cardiovascular reflex testing at rest, deep breathing, standing, and when performing the Valsalva maneuver22 using O’Brien’s23 or Ewing’s24 protocol to laboratory based assessments such as baroreceptor sensitivity testing. Although multiple abnormalities in different cardiovascular reflex tests are more preferable for the diagnosis of CAN, no single test for CAN is superior to the other.21 The different methods used over the years to diagnose CAN in clinical and research settings were summarized in Table S1 in the Supplementary File.17

For cardiovascular reflex tests, one abnormal test is defined as early CAN. At least 2 abnormal tests are defined as definite CAN and orthostatic hypotension, and 2 abnormal tests are defined as advanced or severe CAN. In addition, it is worth noting that tests of sympathetic cholinergic function such as quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test, thermoregulatory sweat testing, sympathetic skin response, and pupillary autonomic function testing using pupillometry are used for research purposes in the diagnosis of diabetic autonomic neuropathy rather than CAN.

Effect of CAN on DKD

A comprehensive understanding of kidney autonomic nerve anatomy and physiology is essential for discerning the influence of CAN on DKD. The kidney autonomic innervation includes afferent and efferent fibers of the kidney plexus, with the efferent fibers being exclusively sympathetic. This plexus consists of fibers from the celiac plexus, intermesenteric plexus (continuation of coeliac plexus), and lumbar splanchnic nerves, which enter the kidney hilum along with the renal artery and vein (Fig 1).25,26 Parasympathetic innervation via the vagus nerve induces vasodilation and increases blood flow to the afferent arterioles27, whereas sympathetic activation stimulates beta-1-adrenergic receptors in juxtaglomerular cells, activating the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS). RAAS activation results in renal tubular sodium reabsorption, reduced urinary sodium excretion, and decreased renal blood flow because of vasoconstriction.22,28

The effect of CAN on DKD may be due to an imbalance between sympathetic and parasympathetic functions. This narrative review will examine the existing evidence to outline the relationship between CAN and DKD and its effect on albuminuria and kidney function decline as well as identify the possible pathophysiological mechanisms involved.

Search Strategy

A literature search was conducted for studies from January 1993 and December 2024 using the search terms “diabetic nephropathy,” “diabetic kidney disease,” “diabetic autonomic neuropathy,” and “cardiac autonomic neuropathy” on PubMed and Google Scholar. Full-length original articles and their references were examined to gather recent studies on CAN and DKD. Additional articles were identified through reference lists. Studies on CAN in children and adolescents, nontranslated non-English journals and publications were excluded. CAN and Kidney Function Decline in Type 1 and 2 DM

Numerous studies (n = 13) have examined the association between CAN and DKD in type 1 and type 2 DM. All studies used heart rate variability and/or cardiovascular reflex tests as these are relatively quick and simple to perform. Kidney parameters such as creatinine and eGFR were used as outcome measures to assess the progressive kidney function decline in individuals with CAN.

In a prospective 10-year follow-up study29 involving 35 participants with type 1 DM, those with CAN at baseline showed a greater eGFR decline compared with those without CAN (4.46 ± 0.98 vs 0.48 ± 0.73, P < 0.005). Similar findings were observed in 2 other studies32,35that included intensive glucose (additional weekly insulin injection) and blood pressure control (< 140/90 mm Hg). The former study32 reported statistically significant (P < 0.001) increases in serum creatinine over 12 months, whereas the later study35 demonstrated deterioration in kidney function (creatinine clearance reduction ≥8 mL/min) at 1 year was significantly associated (r = 0.30, P = 0.01) with CAN at baseline.

A 14-year study42 with 370 participants with type 1 DM linked baseline CAN to early GFR loss (odds ratio, 4.09; 95% CI, 1.65-10.12; P = 0.002) and incident advanced CKD (hazard ratio [HR], 2.76; 95% CI, 1.44-5.30; P = 0.002), independent of various confounding factors including sex, diabetes duration, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, HbA1c, triglycerides, eGFR, and the presence of moderately increased albuminuria. Over 3.2 years, the PERL study52 (n = 469) also associated CAN with more negative eGFR slopes (−1.15 mL/min/1.73 m2 year), increased odds of rapid kidney function decline (+111%), and increased hazards of experiencing ≥40% eGFR loss (+150%). Despite variations in sample size, follow-up duration and the tests used to diagnose CAN, these studies on type 1 DM indicate that individuals with CAN experienced greater kidney function decline.

Studies on type 2 DM demonstrate a significant and independent association between CAN and rapid decline in kidney function. A 9-year retrospective study22 with 156 participants showed greater eGFR decline (mean change, –10.28%; P = 0.047) in those with definite or severe CAN compared with normal CAN or early-stage CAN at baseline (mean changes, 4.50% and 0.77%, respectively).

Another study26 followed 755 participants with type 2 DM for 9.6 years and found early and definite CAN be linked to higher CKD risks. The annual decline in eGFR in the normal, early, and definite CAN group was 1.61 ± 2.54, 2.61 ± 3.22, and 4.64 ± 5.06 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively (P for trend <0.001). A cohort study41 identified CAN as an independent predictor of eGFR decline over a 2.5-year follow-up (–9.0 ± 17.8% vs –3.3 ± 10.3%, P = 0.009).

Kadoya et al47 found CAN to be independently associated with kidney function decline in individuals with diabetes (HR, 2.40; 95% CI, 1.02-5.60; P = 0.043), but not in those without diabetes, whereas Muramatsu et al50 demonstrated that CAN strongly increased the risk of a 40% eGFR decline (HR, 2.42; 95% CI, 1.54-3.80) from baseline, especially in participants with type 2 DM (n = 831) with severely increased albuminuria during 5.3 years of follow-up. Zeng et al51 used Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes risk stratification and identified reduced HRV as a risk factor for DKD progression (P < 0.01). The ACCORD study52 with 7,973 participants over 4.9 years also found associations between CAN and rapid kidney function decline because of more negative eGFR slopes (–0.34 mL/min/1.73 m2 year), increased odds of rapid kidney function decline (+39%), and increased hazards of experiencing ≥40% eGFR loss (+54%) independent of known DKD risk factors.

In summary, these studies, which encompass a wide age range (with type 2 DM cohorts naturally being older than type 1 DM cohorts) and either match or adjust for key confounding factors such as age, duration of diabetes, HbA1c levels, and blood pressure, demonstrate a significant role of CAN in kidney function decline. However, it is important to note that most of these studies were conducted in the early stages of DKD, raising questions about the role of CAN across different stages of disease severity. The mean range of eGFR in studies extends from 30 to 121 mL/min/1.73 m2 (G1-G3) except one study41 that included a small proportion (1%, 2 out of total 204 participants) of G4 (eGFR 15-29 mL/min/1.73 m2). Additionally, it remains unclear whether the effect of CAN is consistent across different ethnicities, in younger patients (<40) with type 2 DM, different stages of CAN severity or in the context of medication use.

CAN and Albuminuria in Type 1 and Type 2 DM

Research in both type 1 and type 2 DM populations has explored the association between CAN and albuminuria using various methods, such as 24-hour urine collection, early morning urine, and random urine tests.

Early studies30,31 in the 1990s on type 1 DM used 24-hour blood pressure monitoring and cardiovascular reflex tests to assess CAN and day and night urinary albumin-creatinine ratio measurements for albuminuria. One study30 demonstrated that mean nocturnal albuminuria was significantly higher in patients with CAN than in those without CAN (61.4 ± 104.6 [mean ± standard deviation]) vs 16 ± 25.2 μg/min, P < 0.04). Another study31 found that urinary albuminuria correlated with greater autonomic function abnormality (r = 0.69; P < 0.0001). Similarly, a 14-year prospective study36 showed higher rates of albuminuria (P = 0.0016) and greater declines in eGFR in patients with CAN at baseline compared with those without (29 [16.5] mL/min/1.73 m2 vs 11 [9] mL/min/1.73 m2; P = 0.0074).

Another study45 reported that participants with CAN have 7.8% higher albuminuria increase per year (95% CI, 0.50% to 15.63%; P = 0.036) than those without CAN. It also mentioned the associations with kidney failure, all-cause mortality, and significant eGFR decline over 6.1 years (HR, 2.497; P = 0.0254), independent of traditional risk factors such as HbA1c, urinary albumin-creatinine ratio, eGFR, and blood pressure. A retrospective study53 similarly found that participants with definite or early CAN had significantly greater increases in urinary albumin-creatinine ratio over 2 years (HR, 8.61 and 8.35, respectively; both P < 0.05). Additionally, a study39 that assessed CAN using pupillometry reflex tests found that abnormal results were independently associated with the development of moderately increased albuminuria (odds ratio, 4.36; 95% CI, 1.32-14.42; P = 0.016) and retinopathy (odds ratio, 4.83; 95% CI, 1.3-17.98, P = 0.019) over a 12-year follow-up.

In type 2 DM populations, numerous studies (n = 9) over several years have described similar associations between CAN and albuminuria. An early study by Rutter et al33 in 1998 suggested that CAN was an associated factor (P < 0.05), rather than an independent predictor, because of links with diabetic retinopathy, higher body mass index, claudication, alcohol use, and calcium channel blocker usage. However, subsequent studies by Smulders et al34 and Moran et al37 demonstrated associations between albuminuria and CAN. The former study34 showed albuminuria in subjects with impaired glucose tolerance or type 2 diabetes with an odds ratio (95% CI) of 1.19 (1.02-1.39) per point increase in CAN score, and the later study37 (n = 132) also found the independent association between microalbuminuria and CAN (P < 0.0001). Conversely, a study by Takebayashi et al38 found a correlation between CAN and macroalbuminuria (P = 0.0064), but not with normal or microalbuminuria, with a 3-year follow-up.

Other studies40,49 reported that increased CAN severity was associated with higher prevalence of microvascular complications such as retinopathy (P = 0.004),40 nephropathy (P = 0.046),40 detected by moderately increased albuminuria in 24-hour urine collections or eGFR decline and a significantly higher risk of incident DKD (HR, 1.56; 95% CI 1.15-2.12; P = 0.005).49

Strengths of these studies include the use of diverse measurement methods for assessing albuminuria, such as 24-hour urine collection and early morning urine tests, providing comprehensive data. Longitudinal follow-up, including a 14-year prospective study36 and a 6.1-year follow-up study45, enhances the reliability of findings. Adjustments for confounding factors like HbA1c, eGFR, and blood pressure strengthen the validity of conclusions. Investigation of various endpoints beyond albuminuria, including eGFR decline, kidney failure, and microvascular complications, offers a holistic view of CAN's effect on kidney health. Moreover, the use of different diagnostic tests for CAN, such as pupillometry reflex tests and heart rate variability, captures different aspects of autonomic neuropathy.

However, limitations exist within these studies. Variability in whether CAN is an independent predictor or an associated factor leads to inconsistent conclusions and reflects the complexity of the relationships. Differences in study populations, including age ranges, and sample sizes, affect generalizability. Limited consideration of ethnic diversity restricts the applicability of results to broader populations. Moreover, most studies focus on the early stages of DKD, raising questions about CAN's role across all disease stages. These findings underscore the importance of CAN in kidney function decline and its association with albuminuria. However, further research is needed to clarify the causal relationship between CAN and DKD, especially across different stages of disease severity and among diverse populations.

CAN and Kidney Biopsy

A study investigating the relationship between CAN and DKD through kidney biopsy was conducted by Wheelock et al.43 They conducted a cross-sectional substudy involving kidney biopsies of 63 participants with type 2 DM as part of a 6-year clinical trial assessing the reno protective effects of losartan. Findings showed that participants with more advanced renal structural lesions, characterized by greater glomerular basement membrane width (P < 0.05), higher cortical interstitial fractional volume, a higher proportion of globally sclerotic glomeruli (P = 0.01), and lower glomerular filtration surface density and total filtration surface per glomerulus (P < 0.05), exhibited more severe indices of CAN (lower E/I ratio and lower indices of HRV). Although causation was not established, the study indicated an association between DKD histopathology and CAN, suggesting the necessity for follow-up kidney biopsies to validate this hypothesis.

Moreover, a cross-sectional study54 of 165 participants with nondiabetic CKD found that CAN was independently associated with advanced interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (P = 0.045). Despite a low prevalence (9.7%) of CAN in the study, it is 4 times higher than in healthy controls54, underscoring the need for further research.

Parasympathetic Dysfunction

In the early stages of CAN, the parasympathetic system is affected, particularly the vagus nerve. This results in relative sympathetic dominance, causing systolic hypertension, resting tachycardia, and increased cardiac output, which promote glomerular membrane injury with excessive filtration of sodium and albumin.22,32,37,49,55

Sympathetic Overactivity

Excessive sympathetic drive leads to a lack of nocturnal blood pressure drop, resulting in higher intraglomerular pressure at night. This leads to glomerular injury and increased sodium and albumin excretion.29, 30, 31,34,56

Renin–Angiotensin–Aldosterone System Activation

Early compensatory increases in sympathetic tone in response to subclinical parasympathetic denervation activate the intrarenal RAAS, causing renal dysfunction through increased glomerular basement membrane thickness and mesangial matrix expansion because of renal vasoconstriction and ischemia.42,57

Inflammation

Sympathetic nervous system impairment affects the regulation of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines52,58, leading to endothelial inflammation and interstitial fibrosis59 followed by DKD progression.

Sympathetic Neuropathy in Late CAN

In the later stages of CAN, sympathetic neuropathy results in diminished renal sodium excretion, reduced renal blood flow, and decreased eGFR because of renal vasoconstriction and reduction in blood pressure.22,50

Among these mechanisms, the nondipping in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure has been the most thoroughly investigated.30,31,34,60, 61, 62, 63 A recent study64 investigated kidney hemodynamic (GFR and effective kidney plasma flow), using inulin and para-amino Hippurate clearance via the plasma disappearance technique. In individuals with type 1 DM, a higher modified Toronto Clinical Neuropathy score (a clinical assessment tool for peripheral neuropathy) was associated with lower kidney blood flow and greater efferent kidney arteriolar resistance, even after adjusting for age, sex, HbA1c, systolic blood pressure, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and 24-hour urine albumin to creatinine ratio. Interestingly, the study did not identify any association between CAN and kidney blood flow. Based on the proposed mechanisms and the limited mechanistic studies conducted, it is likely that hemodynamic factors are primarily responsible for the increased kidney decline associated with CAN. More robust and better-designed studies using novel imaging techniques are needed to accurately measure kidney blood flow and hemodynamic.

Discussion

Our comprehensive review of 28 studies spanning 3 decades underscores the significant effect of CAN on both the incidence and progression of DKD in individuals with type 1 and type 2 DM. These studies consistently demonstrate that individuals with CAN face heightened risks of DKD, characterized by declining eGFR and increasing urinary albuminuria. Mechanisms proposed to elucidate this relationship include sympathetic overactivity-induced kidney hemodynamic imbalance22,32,42,50,57, lack of nocturnal blood pressure dipping leading to increased glomerular pressure and injury29, 30, 31,34,56, and elevated oxidative stress and proinflammatory cytokine levels associated with CAN58 and described as potential therapeutic targets and biomarkers for DKD.52,65

Moreover, the relative dominance of sympathetic activity because of impaired parasympathetic function may contribute to systolic hypertension, resting tachycardia, and increased cardiac output, further exacerbating glomerular membrane injury and albumin filtration. Persistent systemic hypertension, whether because of inadequate antihypertensive therapy or abnormal physiologic responses, can perpetuate end-organ damage and fibrosis within the kidneys, hastening DKD progression.32

In clinical practice, management of CAN primarily focuses on symptomatic control and preventing disease progression. Despite no existing cure, glycemic control is essential for preventing CAN in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes11, in addition to lifestyle modifications such as weight loss, healthy diet, and exercise as per the 2017 American Diabetes Association recommendation.66 For symptomatic postural hypotension, first-line nonpharmacological measures include patient education, optimized salt and fluid intake, compression stockings, abdominal binders, lower body strength training, moderate recumbent exercise, and physical maneuvers.67 Currently, the 2 drugs primarily used to treat postural hypotension are midodrine and droxidopa, followed by fludrocortisone in nonresponders.21 A key challenge is treating supine hypertension without worsening postural hypotension.

By reducing heart rate and blood pressure through blocking the RAAS, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers have shown improvements in heart rate68, HRV parameters69, and significant dipping (2.4%) in nocturnal systolic blood pressure.70 The effects of metoprolol (beta-blocker) have been shown to improve 24-hour ambulatory HRV parameters and vagal tone71 in patients with type 1 DM and abnormal albuminuria. Furthermore, the roles of vitamin E72 and omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid in treating CAN in type 2 diabetes73,74 were also mentioned in different studies. Given its beneficial effects on glucose homeostasis parameters and lipid profiles75, alpha lipoic acid used as monotherapy76 or in combination with other antioxidants such as acetyl L-carnitine, superoxide dismutase, and vitamin B1277 was also shown to improve autonomic results such as Valsalva maneuver, deep breathing test, and orthostatic hypotension.

Preclinical studies suggest that vagal nerve stimulation might help manage CAN by addressing reduced parasympathetic drive and dominant sympathetic activity. Vagal nerve stimulation has shown benefits in resuscitating animals in cardiac arrest, improving recovery after myocardial infarction, alleviating heart failure and stroke symptoms, and modulating arrhythmias.78 However, vagal nerve stimulation can also cause serious cardiovascular side effects such as bradycardia and cardiac standstill.79, 80, 81 More progressive human studies and clinical trials are needed to determine the efficiency and safety of vagal nerve stimulation in the near future. Despite no specific cure for the symptoms, a patient-centered approach including thorough history taking, physical examination, and review of the medication list of all symptomatic patients remains the mainstay of management for CAN.

Similarly, in DKD, primary prevention remains the most vital part in management strategy. In addition to optimizing glycemic control, lifestyle measures and managing modifiable risk factors to prevent cardiovascular events, treatment goals for DKD are individualized and dependent on each stage with overall aim to prevent progression from moderately increased albuminuria (incipient DKD) to severely increased albuminuria (overt DKD) and preventing progressive decline in kidney function in patients with severely increased albuminuria.

In addition to existing medical management by angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers and statins, newer drugs with positive effects on both cardiovascular and renal outcomes have emerged. Studies such as CREDENCE82, DAPA-CKD83, and EMPA-Kidney84 trials have demonstrated the efficacy of sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors in reducing cardiac mortality, decreasing proteinuria, and slowing disease progression. Meanwhile, positive effect of glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists on kidneys is established by the latest randomized controlled trial, known as the FLOW (Evaluate Renal Function with Semaglutide Once Weekly).85

Novel nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, including finerenone and esaxerenone, are shown to provide reductions in albuminuria with a lower risk of hyperkalemia.86 The efficiency of finerenone in improving kidney and cardiovascular outcomes was shown in the FIDELIO-DKD87 and FIGARO-DKD88 studies that included participants with type 2 DM and albuminuria. Esaxerenone, has been shown to lower albumin excretion89,90, although its long-term kidney and cardiovascular benefits remain unestablished.

The development of these new pharmacological agents signifies improved management and survival rates, enhanced quality of life, and increased longevity for the diabetic population, with a concomitant reduction in cardiovascular and renal complications. Given the multisystem involvement of diabetes, management of CAN and DKD will still require a holistic, multidisciplinary, and individualized approach for each patient in clinical practice.

Despite variations in diagnostic approaches, existing evidence points to a significant role of CAN in DKD progression. The underlying mechanisms may serve as novel targets for future drug development. Early detection and management of CAN could aid in identifying high-risk DKD patients and complement conventional medical therapies. To address knowledge gaps, future research should focus on evaluating and comparing kidney hemodynamic, with advancements in imaging technologies offering promising avenues for analyzing renal perfusion and structural abnormalities in individuals with or without CAN.
