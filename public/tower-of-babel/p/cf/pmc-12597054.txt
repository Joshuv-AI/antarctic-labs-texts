Hemodialysis and high-dose steroids can salvage renal function in cholesterol crystal embolization: Acute kidney injury secondary to microemboli to a solitary kidney during aortic endovascular aneurysm repair
Authors: Tamir E. Bresler, Tyler Wilson, Sonali Sharma, Garo Kalfayan, Arian Gower, Carney Chan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A 66-year-old woman with a solitary right kidney with renal artery stenosis and a 5.0-cm abdominal aortic aneurysm underwent renal artery stenting followed by endovascular aortic aneurysm repair endovascular aneurysm repair 1 month later. She developed rapidly progressive renal failure and recurrent hypertension despite a patent renal stent. A renal biopsy revealed cholesterol crystal emboli with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, supporting the diagnosis of atheroembolic kidney disease. High-dose methylprednisolone followed by a 5-week prednisone taper and temporary hemodialysis led to dialysis independence and blood pressure control within 2 months. Biopsy-driven diagnosis, hemodialysis, and pulse corticosteroid therapy may reverse atheroembolic renal injury, especially in vulnerable patients with a single kidney.

Atheroembolic kidney disease (AEKD) represents a spectrum of cholesterol crystal embolization (CCE) that can complicate any vascular intervention. This infrequent but serious complication after endovascular interventions, with an approximate incidence of 1% to 5% per procedure,1 can demonstrate variable renal outcomes2,3; 30% to % to 55% of patients may require renal replacement therapy, with improvement in only 21% to 28% of patients.4

Case report

Written informed consent for publication of clinical data and images was obtained from the patient and is held on file with the corresponding author. A 66-year-old woman with a history of transient ischemic attack and occlusion of the right basilar artery on lifelong dual antiplatelet therapy, hypertension, chronic kidney disease (CKD), obesity (body mass index of 32.0), and hyperlipidemia was diagnosed with renovascular hypertension (initial serum creatinine [sCr] 2.30 mg/dL). Her hypertension and CKD initially improved with medical therapy (nadir sCr, 1.48); however, she suffered multiple hypertensive emergencies (systolic blood pressure of >220 mm Hg) with worsening renal function (sCr, 2.68) while on maximal medical therapy (carvedilol, chlorthalidone, clonidine patch, doxazosin, hydralazine, losartan, and clonidine as needed). Workup revealed severe right renal artery stenosis (5-mm artery distal to the stenosis), an atrophic left kidney, and an incidental finding of 5.0-cm abdominal aortic aneurysm. After discussion with the patient and her nephrologist, we elected to intervene on the renal artery first given her worsening renal function and impending renal failure. This was to be followed by standard endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) once kidney function improved,5 owing to the patient's obesity, fear of open surgery, and anxiety regarding rupture.

Initial angiography during the intervention confirmed severe stenosis in the right renal artery and no visible flow in the left. Intravascular ultrasound examination demonstrated 99% stenosis, with a 3.5-mm vessel distal to the stenosis. The lesion was treated with a VBX (W. L. Gore & Associates) covered stent for better long-term patency.6 The smallest available VBX in our facility is 6 mm. As such, we predilated the vessel to 4 mm, then underinflated the 6-mm stent, leaving one-third of the stent protruding through the ostium and flaring it proximally to 7 mm (our routine practice to facilitate ease of future access and reduce the risk of ostial restenosis7) (Fig 1, A). Completion angiogram demonstrated brisk flow with <10% residual stenosis. The patient continued her dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin, clopidogrel) perioperatively. The patient then underwent elective EVAR the following month (aortic diameter of 32.1 mm at the proximal landing zone; minimal plaque; small mural thrombus starting ∼22 mm distal to the right renal artery). A Gore Excluder stent graft was used (main body 36 × 14.5 × 140 mm, left contralateral limb 16 × 12 × 120 mm, and right ipsilateral limb 16 × 12 × 100 mm). To avoid damaging or disrupting the renal artery stent, the proximal edge of the EVAR main body was positioned and deployed 6 mm below the renal artery stent. The postdilation balloon was kept strictly distal to the stent. No suprarenal ballooning was performed. The contralateral gate was opened and cannulated without issue (Fig 1, B and C). Intravenous fluids were given for renal protection perioperatively and approximately 100 mL of contrast was used. The patient was discharged home on postoperative day (POD) 1.

The patient was readmitted to the hospital on POD 75 with worsening renal function (sCr, 2.8) and hypertension (systolic blood pressure, 185 mm Hg). The patient was discharged on POD 81 with escalating medical therapy (carvedilol, hydralazine, doxazosin, and clonidine), and subsequently readmitted on POD 104 with a sCr of 3.5 and new-onset edema. Although index of suspicion for restenosis was low, repeat angiography was pursued after a discussion with nephrology (Fig 3); this showed no restenosis. Because the patient experienced worsening renal deterioration (oliguria and sCr of 5.06), other causes of renal failure were considered (Fig 4). Despite the risk of biopsy of a solitary kidney, a percutaneous renal biopsy was performed owing to her worsening renal disease (Fig 5). The findings suggested CCE and AEKD, for which there is no standard therapy.8 Case reports and small case series suggest hemodialysis and high-dose corticosteroid therapy may enhance renal recovery in select cases.2,9 Nephrology initiated hemodialysis, intravenous methylprednisolone 125 mg/day for 3 days, and an oral prednisone taper (60 mg/day, decreased weekly over 5 weeks). Within 2 months, hemodialysis was discontinued, and renal function normalized (sCr, 1.7). Discussion

We suspect the CCE was caused by positioning wires in the aortic arch during EVAR. Although a double hit of cholesterol emboli during index renal artery stenting followed by a dye load during EVAR was considered, the marked improvement in kidney function after renal artery stenting argues against CCE during the initial procedure, and contrast-induced nephropathy has a different clinical course and appearance on biopsy.3 Risk factors for CCE are advanced age (>60 years), widespread erosive atherosclerosis (specifically abdominal aortic), CKD, and other typical risk factors for atherosclerosis (male sex, tobacco use, family history of atherosclerosis, obesity, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes).3 Iatrogenic plaque disruption accounts for 70% of cases, with angiography the most prevalent contributor (80%).10 In patients with a prohibitively high risk of CCE, consideration may be given to open endarterectomy or renal autotransplantation.11,12

Clinically, progressive sCr elevation—frequently accompanied by eosinophilia, hypocomplementemia, or livedo reticularis—should raise suspicion for AEKD, particularly when duplex ultrasound examination or computed tomography angiography fail to identify a stenosis.13 Because noninvasive imaging cannot detect microemboli, biopsy remains the gold standard.14 When extrarenal manifestations of disease occur, a biopsy of the skin or muscle may be sufficient to establish a diagnosis.15

Our patient may have benefited from earlier biopsy, however, a renal biopsy is a high risk procedure in patients with a solitary kidney; as such, we waited until the patient was maximized on medical therapy and an angiogram confirmed normal flow to the kidney. Because there is currently no standard of care to treat CCE, we were not comfortable initiating high-dose corticosteroids or renal replacement therapy without a tissue confirmation of disease. And while these treatments may have helped our patient, only results from a randomized controlled trial can prove the benefit of this regimen.

Novel therapies for CCE include plasmapheresis or low-density lipoprotein apheresis,16 and combination therapy with prostaglandin E1, which causes vasodilation and inhibits platelet aggregation.17 Preventive strategies focus on plaque stabilization with high-intensity statins, blood pressure control, and alternate arterial access sites.18 Long-term outcomes remain guarded; 6-month mortality rates may approach 75% in CCE-related renal failure managed conservatively, with dialysis dependence in ≤80% of survivors.19

Conclusions

CCE and AEKD complicate vascular interventions and pose heightened risks in patients with a solitary kidney. Once diagnosed by biopsy, hemodialysis and high-dose corticosteroids in combination with supportive therapy may help to prevent permanent renal damage.
