Bacopa monnieri biochar derived carbon nanospheres for pendimethalin remediation from water
Authors: Shreya Thakor, Gautam Priyadarshi, Bhakti Patel, Santosh Kumar Sahu, Sherzodbek Tashbaev, Gulomov Gafurjon Shavkatbek Ugli, Esha Rami, Dipak Kumar Sahoo, Ashish Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study reports the rapid synthesis of carbon nanospheres (CNSs) from Bacopa monnieri leaf biochar via microwave irradiation for the removal of the Pendimethalin (PND) herbicide from water. The synthesized CNSs were characterized by SEM-EDS mapping (morphology and elemental distribution), XRD (crystalline structure and elemental composition), FTIR (functional groups), Zeta potential (surface charge), BET (surface area), and BJH (pore volume). Batch-mode adsorption experiments were conducted under various conditions of PND concentration (30 to 70 mg L− 1), pH (2 to 12), contact time (0 to 120 min), and adsorbent dose (2.5 to 20 mg). The optimal removal of PND was 94.02%, achieved in 120 min at pH 10 with a PND concentration of 50 mg L-1 and a CNS dose of 10 mg. The presence of multivalent salt (CaCO3 and MgCO3) enhanced the removal efficiency through strong ionic interactions. Regeneration experiments demonstrated ~ 60% adsorption capacity after the 5th regeneration cycle. Adsorption followed pseudo-second-order kinetics and was fitted to the Langmuir and Freundlich model with a maximum capacity of 63.62 mg g− 1, confirming multi-layered chemosorption and a pore-filling mechanism. Introduction

Pesticides have the potential to harm the environment and human health, with an estimated 300,000 deaths worldwide each year from pesticide exposure [1]. Among pesticides, herbicides such as Pendimethalin (PND) are of particular concern due to their persistence, mobility, and toxic nature. PND, a dinitroaniline herbicide, is widely applied to control weeds in cereal, maize, rice, and legume crops. However, its resistance to degradation and its tendency to leach into soil and water have led to frequent detection in drinking water, baby food, rivers, and groundwater [2, 3]. Aquatic invertebrates and fish are affected by bioaccumulation and biomagnification [4]. It leads to several physiological and endocrine disturbances in animals, including liver and kidney damage, as well as various mutagenic effects [5]. Exposure to PND in human umbilical vein endothelial cells induces dose-dependent cytotoxicity, lysosomal fragility, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, DNA damage, and apoptotic cell death [6].

Conventional treatment methods, including biological degradation, a combination of ozonation and photocatalysis, and electrochemical oxidation, often exhibit limited efficiency or incur high operational costs [7]. Carbon-based nanomaterials have garnered increasing attention as effective adsorbents due to their high specific surface area, mechanical stability, and manageable physical and chemical characteristics [8]. Carbon nanospheres (CNSs) have attracted considerable attention due to their unusual spherical shape, which can increase the vibrational density of activated carbon particles [9]. CNSs have been studied as nanometric carbon shapes. CNSs have been widely used for catalyst encapsulation, drug delivery, and water treatment. CNSs have been synthesized by pyrolysis of silica, polymers, and metal oxides [10]; however, reports on the sustainable synthesis of CNSs from biochar for pesticide removal are limited.

Biochar derived from plant biomass is a low-cost, carbon-rich material with high adsorption potential [11]. Bacopa monnieri, a medicinal herb with high carbon content, has not been explored as a precursor for CNS synthesis. The integration of biochar with microwave-assisted synthesis provides a rapid, energy-efficient route for engineering CNSs.

In this study, CNSs are synthesized using biochar derived from Bacopa monnieri and ferrocene as a catalyst under microwave irradiation. The structural and elemental composition characteristics of CNSs were analyzed using Field Emission Gun Scanning electron microscopy (FEG-SEM) and Energy Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy (EDS) mapping, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and X-ray diffraction (XRD). The surface characterization was confirmed using Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area, pore size & volume, and zeta potential analysis. The efficiency of CNSs for PND removal was evaluated at different concentrations, pH levels, doses, and contact durations. Adsorption kinetics and isotherms were applied to elucidate the mechanism for PND removal. The present work addressed the gap in developing sustainable CNSs derived from biochar and demonstrated their potential as cost-effective adsorbents for pesticide remediation.

Materials & methods

Materials

Bacopa monnieri (family: Scrophulariaceae), commonly known as Brahmi (leaf) powder, was purchased from the local market of Patan, Gujarat. Ferrocene (Fe(C5H5)2), ethanol (C2H5OH), calcium carbonate (CaCO3), potassium chloride (KCl), magnesium carbonate (MgCO3), sodium chloride (NaCl), and zinc sulfate (ZnSO4) were procured as analytical reagent grade chemicals from SRL, India. Pendimethalin (PND) was obtained from NAPOLEON, Parijat Industries (India) Limited. All reagents were used as received without further purification, and deionized water (DIW) was used to prepare solutions and dilutions.

Pyrolysis of biomass

To prepare biochar from Bacopa monnieri leaf powder, 10 g of the powder was placed in a closed container within the muffle furnace heating chamber at 600 ℃ for 2 h. The obtained biochar was then crushed using a mortar and pestle and sieved to obtain uniform-sized particles. To avoid moisture, the sieved biochar is stored in an airtight container.

CNSs synthesis

Biochar derived from Bacopa monnieri (Bramhi) powder was mixed with ferrocene in a 1:0.5 ratio. For each synthesis batch, 150 mg of Bramhi biochar and 75 mg of ferrocene. The mixture was thoroughly homogenized using a vortex mixer and then transferred into a borosilicate test tube with a stopper. The CNSs were synthesized through microwave irradiation for 3 min at 150 °C. The obtained CNS powder was extracted from the test tube and subjected to three cycles of ultrasonication with 60% ethanol to eliminate any unreacted residues and ferrocene. The synthesized CNSs were dried at 60 °C for 6 h and stored in a plastic vial till further application. Multiple identical batches were prepared under the same optimized conditions, and synthesized CNSs were combined and homogenized to obtain sufficient material for all characterization and adsorption experiments.

Characterization of synthesized CNSs

The microstructural, elemental distribution, and compositional characterization of the carbon nanosphere were performed using FEG-SEM (FEI Nova NanoSEM 450) and EDS analysis, respectively. X-ray diffraction (XRD) of these samples was recorded over 3 to 90 specific 2Ɵ values (Advanced D-8 (Bruker, Netherlands) XRD instrument) to assess the crystalline nature of the materials. The functional groups identification of the carbon nanosphere was conducted by FTIR spectra (S6500 Spectrum Instrument (PerkinElmer, USA)) within the range of 550–4000 cm− 1. Surface area analysis of CNSs was determined using N2 isotherm at 77.35 K, performed via a BET surface area analyzer (Anton Paar). The surface charge of the CNSs was measured at pH 7 using a zeta potential analyzer (HORIBA SZ-100) at a conductivity of 0.285 mS/cm and a potential of 3.4 V.

PND removal studies

To evaluate the adsorption performance of CNSs, various PND solutions (30, 40, 50, 60, and 70 mg L− 1) were prepared at a volume of 50 mL. The removal study was conducted in 50 mL of each initial concentration solution (C0), and the results were recorded. Subsequently, 50 mg of adsorbent CNSs was added to each flask. The samples were incubated at 150 rpm for 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. After each 30-minute interval, samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min to separate the CNSs. The residual concentration of PND (Ce) in the supernatant was determined using UV-Vis spectrophotometry at 243 nm wavelength [12]. Effect of ions on CNSs

The effect of ionic solutions on the PND removal efficiency of CNSs was investigated using 1 M salt solutions of NaCl, KCl, MgCO3, ZnSO4, and CaCO3. Each solution was made up to 10 mL with 1 mL of stock PND solution (500 mg L− 1), 8 mL of DIW, and 1 mL of salt solution (1 M). All salt-containing PND solutions were added 10 mg CNSs and agitated for 60 min. To measure the percentage of PND elimination in ionic solution, the CNSs were isolated from the mixes by centrifugation.

Desorption and reusability studies

The regeneration studies were conducted in a 100 mL solution of PND (50 mg L− 1) containing 100 mg of CNSs. The solution was stirred in a shaking incubator for 60 min. Following incubation, the solution was centrifuged for 10 min at 5000 rpm. The supernatant was collected to estimate the residual PND concentration by measuring its absorbance with a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. The CNSs were retained for the desorption study. The recovered CNSs were sonicated for 10 min in 10 mL of ethanol. The solution was centrifuged (at 5000 rpm) for 10 min and analyzed spectrophotometrically to assess the desorbed PND. The CNSs were separated by filtration and then dried in an oven at 60 °C for 2 h. The adsorption-desorption process was repeated for 5 cycles, reducing the CNS quantity in each successive cycle from 100 mg while maintaining the same adsorbed-to-adsorbent ratio.

Results and discussion

Characterization of microwave-synthesized CNSs

FEG-SEM-EDS of CNSs

The structure and morphology of CNSs’ surface were examined at a high magnification (100,000x), FEG-SEM image (with scale bar of 500 nm). A tightly packed morphology composed of circular, nearly spherical particles with dimensions of approximately 100–200 nm is shown in Fig. 2a. The CNS synthesized by pyrolysis and microwave activation processes is strongly supported by the uniform particle shape and size distribution. Their surface’s rough and textured appearance suggests a presence of porosity, as observed in a previous study [22].

XRD for synthesized samples

X-ray diffraction was used to examine CNSs, focusing on their structural characteristics. Figure 3a represents the 2θ scanning range of 3 ° to 90 °, where XRD diffraction peaks were observed [24]. The XRD pattern of synthetic CNSs shows two distinct peaks at 2Ɵ = 26.5˚ and 54.6˚, conforming to the (002) and (004) crystalline planes of graphite, respectively, as described by JCPDS card no. 41-1487. The (100) plane of graphite is identified by a less strong but noticeable peak at around 43.02º. The broad reflections suggest reduced long-range crystallinity, a characteristic commonly observed in carbon materials with nanostructures [25, 26]. A significant peak at 29.52º does not correspond to the typical plane of graphitic carbon. This peak may be caused by residual inorganic elements in the biochar or by interactions with trace elements such as potassium or iron. XRD analysis revealed the presence of mildly graphitic domains within the CNSs, consistent with structural features typically observed in carbon nanomaterials produced via the microwave method [27, 28].

BET surface analysis and zeta potential

The BET isotherms of CNSs, shown in Fig. 4a, correspond to a type IV isotherm with a characteristic hysteresis loop. At low relative pressure (P/Po), a gradual rise in N2 uptake indicates the presence of micropores. At higher P/Po, the sharp increase in adsorption volume reflects the filling of mesopores [31]. The BET surface area of the CNSs was determined to be 54.84 m2/g. Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) pore size distribution analysis conformed to a mesoporous structure (2–50 nm), showing a pore diameter of 3.4 nm and a total pore volume of 0.097 cc/g as illustrated in Fig. 4b. The average pore size ranged from 1.7 to 2.8 nm, further supporting the mesoporous characteristics of the synthesized CNSs, in accordance with the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) classification for mesoporous material [32].

Effect of initial PND concentration and contact time

Figure 5a & b shows a variation of removal efficiency (R%) and adsorption capacity (qe) changes over time for different concentrations, 30 to 70 mg L− 1. All concentrations showed relatively high removal rates; within the first 30 min, most reached above 80%, except for 70 mg L− 1, which was significantly lower. At 60 min, the effectiveness of the removal both increased and stabilized. The 50 mg L− 1 concentration performed best across all concentrations, with an efficiency of almost 94%. Following this, the removal efficiency for most concentrations remained relatively constant, whereas the 40 mg L− 1 solution showed a decrease. The 70 mg L− 1 concentration continuously showed the lowest removal effectiveness in this study. Based on these results, the optimal concentration under the given conditions is 50 mg/L, with the highest and most stable removal efficiency over the specified period. This is because the adsorbate molecules have access to all the active sites [34]. The rapid increase in adsorption capacity within the first 30 min at all concentrations is due to the number of active sites on the absorbent surface and the large concentration gradient. At 30–40 mg L− 1, qe peaks, possibly due to site saturation or partial desorption of the adsorbate, followed by a progressive decrease. However, during the initial phase, qe values continue to increase and stabilize at higher concentrations (50, 60, and 70 mg L− 1), with 70 mg L− 1 reaching the maximum adsorption capacity of approximately 57 mg g− 1. Overall, the graph indicates that the equilibrium qe levels at 50 mg L− 1 and 60 mg L− 1 remain relatively constant. At low concentration, there is limited driving force available to transfer PND onto the adsorbent particles: at high concentrations, the driving force increases in proportion, improving the interaction between the adsorbent’s active site and the PND in the aqueous phase [35].

Effect of pH

The influence of solution pH on PND elimination was assessed over a pH range (2 to 12), and the corresponding results are depicted in Fig. 5c. The CNSs exhibit good removal across the pH range, indicating strong performance in a variety of environmental conditions. With an increasing percentage of pH 2 around 36%, the removal efficiency shows a clear dependent on pH. A significant increase in % PND removal is noted between pH 4 and 6, peaking at almost 93%, suggesting that slightly acidic conditions are ideal for removal. However, there is a significant decrease in removal effectiveness to around 63% at pH 8, suggesting interference or reduced adsorption at this pH. Interestingly, the %R increases again at pH 10, reaching nearly 95%, before slightly decreasing to roughly 92% at pH 12. According to these findings, when the pH rises, adsorption increases because there is less competition for surface sites between PND molecules and water ions, as well as a decrease in positive charge. Most PND adsorption occurs when a less positively charged surface becomes available, as the pH rises [36].

Effect of dose

The influence of adsorbent dosage on removal was investigated using CNS adsorbent dosages ranging from 2.5 to 20 mg in a 10 mL PND (50 mg L− 1) solution. As shown in Fig. 5d, the removal efficiency increased with increasing adsorbent dosage, reaching a maximum of 60.46% at 10 mg. A further increase in dose (15 mg) results in a decrease in removal efficiency to approximately 33%, likely due to particle aggregation or reaching saturation. However, at 20 mg, efficiency increased about 57%, indicating a partial recovery of adsorption capacity. The results suggest that at higher doses, the limited number of PND molecules in the solution or the overlapping of adsorption sites can reduce the efficiency of CNSs [12, 37].

Effect of ionic salts

The influence of ion presence on PND removal was evaluated using various salt solutions, including NaCl, KCl, ZnSO4, MgCO3, and CaCO3, as shown in Fig. 5e. NaCl and KCl, which both contain monovalent cations (Na+ and K+, respectively), showed comparatively moderate removal efficiency among the investigation salts, with %R values of about 71%. With a divalent cation (Zn+) present, ZnSO4 demonstrated a slight rise in removal effectiveness, reaching roughly 74%. Interestingly, the addition of MgCO3 and CaCO3, which contain divalent cations (Mg2+ and Ca2+, respectively), resulted in the highest removal percentages, with removal rates (R) of approximately 91% and 93%, respectively. The CNSs’ removal onto PND solution remained stable up to a specific ion concentration, beyond which fast precipitation occurred, possibly due to increased electrostatic interactions and co-precipitation effects [38, 39]. These outcomes demonstrate the importance of ionic strength and salt composition in determining removal effectiveness, with divalent carbonate salts yielding the best results in this method.

Regeneration and reusability of adsorbent for PND removal

The reusability of the CNSs was examined through five successive adsorption-desorption cycles, as shown in Fig. 5f. For the first cycle, the adsorption and desorption capacities were ~ 62 mg g− 1, which was considered 100% and subsequently decreased with each successive cycle [40]. In the second cycle, adsorption decreased to (~ 63%), but desorption remained high (~ 85%), possibly due to surface blockage. The third cycle showed a minor rebound in adsorption (~ 75%), probably due to surface reactivation following ethanol treatment. In the fourth and fifth cycles, efficiency decreased to ~ 60%, indicating a loss of active sites or material degradation [41]. This cumulative decline, compared to the first cycle, clearly illustrates the gradual reduction in CNSs’ efficiency and highlights the material’s ability to incur losses with continued use.

Adsorption kinetics modelling

Figure 6a–c represents the linear form of PFO, PSO, and IPD. Table 3 presents the linear kinetic parameters for different initial concentrations. In the PSO, a minor increase in adsorption rate was observed at higher concentrations, as indicated by the rate constant k1, which ranged from 0.0211 to 0.0303 min− 1. With a low R2 value (0.7433), poor correlation was found for the linear version of the model. The adsorption of CNSs does not follow PFO kinetics [42]. The PSO model fitted well, with estimated capacities matching experimental values for all concentrations, and all R2 values were ≥ 0.94 (0.9998 at 60 mg g− 1), indicating a good fit. Even though the rate was constant at k2, the minimum was at 60 mg g− 1 (0.0007). The findings were in line with a previous study [43], which shows that the PSO method is commonly used to absorb PND onto CNSs and other adsorbents. According to the IPD, it rises with starting concentrations until it reaches 60 mg g− 1 (Kdiff increase), after which it falls to 70 mg g− 1. At higher concentrations, the intercept (C) increases, indicating a greater boundary-layer effect. At 40 mg g− 1 (R2 = 0.9951), the adsorbate diffusion or transport from the solution phase into the solid phase characterizes the adsorption process by CNSs [44, 45].

Mechanism of PND removal using CNSs

The adsorption was governed via various physical and chemical interaction including pore filling, surface adsorption, π-π stacking, electrostatic interaction, and hydrogen bonding as represented in the probable mechanism (Fig. 8). Hydrogen bonding between oxygen-containing functional groups of CNSs (–OH and C–O) and ionic functional groups on PND enhance molecular binding and stabilizes the adsorbent-adsorbate complex, driving the system towards equilibrium. Electrostatic interactions arising from charge or polarity differences between CNSs and PND are susceptible to solution pH and can amplify electrostatic forces [49]. Furthermore, π-π stacking interactions occur through the connection of aromatic rings in CNSs (confirmed by FTIR) and the aromatic structure of PND molecules. Pore filling and surface adsorption complement these mechanisms. For example, PND molecules diffuse into mesopores on the surface of CNSs and become physically trapped, thereby increasing the adequate adsorption capacity [50]. The combination of heterogeneous surface adsorption, moderate adsorption energies, and chemosorption-influenced kinetics confirms that multiple coexisting mechanisms govern the removal of PND by CNSs.

Cost analysis and practical feasibility

A preliminary cost analysis was conducted to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of the synthesized CNSs for pesticide removal. Bacopa monnieri biomass, used as the carbon precursor, is inexpensive, renewable, and widely available as agricultural and herbal waste, resulting in minimal raw-material costs [51]. Ferrocene was used at half the amount (1:0.5), and its contribution to the overall cost remains moderate compared to that of the conventional activating agent. The microwave-assisted approach significantly reduces time and energy consumption compared to conventional mechanical stirring and heating methods, thereby lowering the cost [52]. In addition, the effectiveness of the adsorbent at low dosage reduces material consumption. The higher regeneration capacity further enhances the cost-effectiveness by reusing the adsorbent and facilitating pollutant recovery. Conclusion

The study investigates the synthesis of carbon nanospheres (CNSs) via microwave-assisted methods for the removal of the PND herbicide from aqueous solutions. The characterization of the synthesized material confirmed the microstructure, surface morphology, elemental distribution and composition, surface functional groups, crystalline nature, and the mesoporous material’s negatively charged nature and high surface area. The batch adsorption study confirmed the optimum (94%) removal at PND concentration of 50 mg g− 1, pH 10, and 10 mg dose. The kinetics and isotherm analysis reveal that chemosorption governs monolayer adsorption with an adsorption capacity of 63.62 mg g− 1. The ionic strength enhanced the removal efficiency in the presence of bivalent (e.g., Ca and Mg). Regeneration studies revealed a gradual decline in adsorption and desorption capacity after each regeneration cycle. Even after the 5th cycle, the adsorption and desorption capacity remain 60%. The higher reusability and regeneration capacity promote a circular ecosystem through PND resource recovery and CNS reuse. Overall, the findings show that CNSs are a promising and effective adsorbent for organic contamination such as PND.
