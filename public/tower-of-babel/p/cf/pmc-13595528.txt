Combined impact of microalbuminuria and obesity on cardiovascular outcomes in hospitalized heart failure patients: a cohort study
Authors: Guo Ai, Qiong Zhou, Yan Huang, Mei Zhai, Xuemei Zhao, Yuhui Zhang, Jian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicroalbuminuria is proven to be an early indicator of poor prognosis in patients with heart failure (HF), while the role of obesity in predicting progression and outcomes of HF is faced with controversies. Evidence probing into the combined effect of microalbuminuria and obesity on cardiovascular outcomes in hospitalized HF patients is still lacking.MethodsConsecutive patients hospitalized for HF were retrospectively enrolled from the Heart Failure Care Unit of Fuwai Hospital. The primary outcome was a composite of cardiovascular death and rehospitalization for heart failure. Patients were classified into four categories based on BMI and UACR: (1) Nonobese/nUACR, (2) Nonobese/hUACR, (3) Obese/nUACR, and (4) Obese/hUACR.ResultsUltimately, 2228 patients with HF were enrolled. Cox regression analysis confirmed that microalbuminuria was independently associated with the primary outcome (HR 1.28, 95%CI 1.01–1.62, p = 0.038). Obesity was independently associated with the primary outcome in main analysis (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.08–1.86, p = 0.013), but the association became insignificant after excluding NT-proBNP from covariates. Using Nonobese/nUACR as reference, Obese/nUACR and Obese/hUACR were related to an equally 61% increase in risk of the primary outcome (HR 1.61, 95% CI 1.1—2.37, p = 0.015; HR 1.61, 95% CI 1.1—2.34, p = 0.014, respectively), higher than Nonobese/hUACR. Component analysis showed that Obese/hUACR was associated with a significantly increased risk of rehospitalization (HR 1.89, 95% CI 1.06–3.4, p = 0.032) but not with cardiovascular death after adjustment. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) demonstrated nonlinear relationships of UACR elevation and risk (p < 0.001). In the subgroup of HFpEF, the association of Obese/hUACR with the primary outcome persisted (HR 3.81, 95% CI 1.87–7.76, p < 0.001, p for interaction = 0.241).ConclusionsOverlapping of obesity and microalbuminuria is relatively common (8.7%) in hospitalized HF patients. The coexistence of obesity and microalbuminuria is an independent risk factor for poor outcomes but in obese patients, the addition of microalbuminuria may not provide incremental prognostic information. The association with primary composite outcome appears to be primarily driven by rehospitalization rather than cardiovascular death. Subgroup findings in HFpEF population may be valuable but still warrant validation. Background

Heart failure (HF) remains to be one of the biggest threats to human health due to aging and the rising prevalence of its comorbidities. Among these comorbidities, the interaction between HF and chronic kidney disease (CKD) draws much attention. CKD is one of the most common comorbidities of HF and closely related to poor prognosis [1]. CKD is an independent risk factor for progression of heart failure, and conversely, cardiac output insufficiency and renal venous congestion contribute to worsening of renal function [2, 3]. The most widely accepted diagnostic criteria for CKD is based on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) or quantification of urinary albumin excretion from Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) [4]. Persistent elevation of urinary albumin-creatinine ratio (UACR) > 30 mg/g for over 3 months is defined as CKD. The degree of albuminuria is further classified into three continuous categories: UACR < 30 mg/g as normal; UACR 30—300 mg/g as microalbuminuria; and UACR > 300 mg/g as macroalbuminuria. Urinary testing of microalbuminuria is becoming increasingly common in HF management. It is an early marker of renal dysfunction and proven to have prognostic significance in patients with HF.

Unlike eGFR, which has been widely utilized for cardiovascular risk assessment and as secondary endpoints for clinical trials regarding cardiometabolic abnormalities, the value of UACR is very much underestimated especially in the context of HF. The prevalence of microalbuminuria among patients with HF is quite high. It is described in a study that approximately 32% of adults with heart failure in the US presented with albuminuria [5]. The prognostic value of UACR in heart failure risk stratification is validated by a large amount of research [6, 7]. UACR is associated with adverse outcomes of heart failure regardless of ejection fraction and independent of other cardiometabolic comorbidities such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [8]. UACR is proven to be a more sensitive marker than eGFR, where it often emerges before the declination of eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 [9]. Additionally, a moderate to large increase in albuminuria is predictive of adverse outcomes even in the setting of eGFR > 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 [1]. Despite all the evidence, current heart failure society guidelines have not recommended the measurement of UACR as a routine test in HF practice. Moreover, little is known about whether there is nonlinear relationship between UACR elevation and cardiovascular outcomes.

The prevalence of overweight (defined as a body mass index, BMI ≥ 25 and < 28 kg/m2) and obesity (BMI > 28 kg/m2) has been rising at an unimaginable speed globally over the past twenty years, spanning across races, genders, and economic status [10]. Obesity is a complex pathophysiological process beyond mere fat accumulation, exerting profound influence on hemodynamics, metabolism, inflammation, and neurohormonal regulation. These alterations accompanying obesity usually promote the development of cardiovascular disease and may contribute to the onset of heart failure [11, 12]. Among patients with established HF, obesity is associated with increased risk of hospitalization, greater symptom burden, and poorer quality of life [13, 14]. Nevertheless, the role of obesity in predicting progression and outcomes of HF is faced with controversies. A phenomenon known as the “obesity paradox” was observed in quite amount of research that overweight and mildly obese patients might have better survival, not only in the setting of HF, but also CKD and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [15, 16]. Moreover, the beneficial effects of weight loss observed in HFpEF were not always consistent in HFrEF [17].

Observational studies displayed an increase in the prevalence of albuminuria among patients with obesity compared to the general population [18]. Analysis of adiposity demonstrated abdominal obesity, in particular, to be a significant independent predictor of incident microalbuminuria, regardless of diabetes status [19, 20]. Secondary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) resulting from obesity facilitates the formation of microalbuminuria and inclination to end-stage kidney disease [21]. Elevated intrarenal resistance may also contribute to the development of microalbuminuria in obese individuals [22, 23].

Our study was dedicated to shedding light on the complex relationship of obesity and microalbuminuria in patients hospitalized for HF. We aimed to 1) describe the prevalence of obesity and microalbuminuria and their overlapping in this cohort; 2) analyze their capability of risk stratification and joint predictive value for adverse outcomes; 3) explore potential nonlinear relationship between UACR and HF prognosis; 4) determine whether there was an interaction between obesity, microalbuminuria, and EF phenotypes.

Methods

Study population and design

This is a single-centre retrospective cohort study. Consecutive patients hospitalized for HF were enrolled from the Heart Failure Care Unit of Fuwai Hospital between December 2016 and September 2022. All patients completed comprehensive history collection, blood tests, echocardiography examination, and medication records. Baseline information was collected uniformly across all patients using standard protocol. The definition of HF was aligned with the Chinese and ESC HF guidelines [24] and all diagnoses were made by a consensus of at least two experienced HF specialists. Diagnostic heterogeneity is common in contemporary HFpEF trials and misclassification may influence the association between obesity, microalbuminuria and outcomes [25]. So we would like to specify that the diagnosis of HFpEF in our cohort was established following the 2021 ESC Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute and Chronic Heart Failure. The diagnostic criteria included: (1) Presence of typical symptoms and/or signs of heart failure (e.g., dyspnea, fatigue, pulmonary rales, lower extremity edema); (2) LVEF ≥ 50%, measured by transthoracic echocardiography using the Simpson biplane method; (3) Objective evidence of cardiac structural and/or functional abnormalities consistent with the presence of left ventricular diastolic dysfunction/elevated left ventricular filling pressures, including: left atrial enlargement (left atrial volume index > 34 mL/m2), left ventricular hypertrophy (left ventricular mass index ≥ 95 g/m2 in women or ≥ 115 g/m2 in men), echocardiographic indicators of diastolic dysfunction (e.g., E/e’ ratio, tricuspid regurgitation velocity), and/or elevated natriuretic peptide levels (NT-proBNP > 125 pg/mL in sinus rhythm or > 365 pg/mL in atrial fibrillation). (4) Systematic exclusion of alternative causes of symptoms, also known as 'HFpEF mimics'. These mimics included: severe valvular heart disease, infiltrative cardiomyopathies, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, pericardial diseases, pre-capillary pulmonary hypertension, high-output heart failure, and primary pulmonary diseases. All diagnoses of HFpEF were independently adjudicated by two experienced HF specialists.

The status of microalbuminuria is assessed based on random spot urine samples which were collected on the second day following admission and UACR was instantly tested and reported by professionals in our laboratory. Notably, the weight of patients was measured upon discharge following standard protocol on condition that a HF professional determined adequate decongestion had been achieved. In doing so, we ensured that the weight collected for analysis accurately reflected dry weight of a patient so as to avoid the potential confounding of volume overload or edema-related weight gain. BMI was defined by the following formula: BMI = Weight (Kg)/Height2 (m2). According to KDIGO, the normal range of UACR is < 30 mg/g, and 30—300 mg/g is defined as microalbuminuria. Diagnosis of obesity was based on baseline BMI following Chinese obesity criteria (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2). For simplification, the term hUACR was used to refer to UACR between 30—300 mg/g (microalbuminuria) and nUACR equaled UACR < 30 mg/g (normal without microalbuminuria). eGFR was calculated using the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation (without a race coefficient), which is based on serum creatinine, age, and sex.

The patients were excluded if (1) age < 18 years; (2) serious comorbidities, such as malignancies, immunological disorders, infective endocarditis; (3) insufficient data on BMI, UACR or important covariables (Fig. 1). After exclusion, 2228 patients were included in the final analysis. They were classified into four categories for further assessment: (1) Nonobese/nUACR, (2) Nonobese/hUACR, (3) Obese/nUACR, and (4) Obese/hUACR.

Flowchart for the detailed inclusion and exclusion process of study subjects

This study complied with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of our hospital (Approval number:2024-ZX035). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study outcomes

Patients were monitored by routine telephone follow-up or clinical visits after enrollment. The primary endpoint was a composite of cardiovascular death (CV death) and rehospitalization for heart failure (HHP). Secondary endpoints included CV death, all-cause mortality (ACM) and HHP, respectively.

Statistical analyses

Categorical values were expressed as absolute numbers (percentage), and continuous variables as median (interquartile range) or mean ± standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. Differences were evaluated for continuous variables by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) if normally distributed or the Mann–Whitney U test as well as the Kruskal–Wallis test if non-normally distributed, and for categorical variables using Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test.

Kaplan–Meier analysis was employed to assess event-free survival across the four groups, followed by log-rank test to determine intergroup differences. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to analyze the association of microalbuminuria and obesity with the outcomes and adjust for potential confounders. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals for individual variables and the overall model. The models included (1) a crude model and (2) an adjusted model. Selection of covariates for multivariable model was based on clinical relevance and univariate analysis. Special attention was paid to variables exhibiting substantial imbalances between groups or reflecting disease severity like NT-proBNP and LVEF. Ultimately age, sex, smoking history, hypertension, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, diabetes mellitus, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), NT-proBNP, LDL, eGFR, and use of ACEI/ARB, MRA, and β-blockers were selected. We adjusted eGFR in the multivariable Cox models to evaluate whether microalbuminuria provides prognostic information independent of eGFR. To explore potential nonlinear relationships, RCS analysis was conducted between UACR and outcomes. We also investigated whether there was an interaction between obesity, microalbuminuria, and EF phenotypes (HFpEF, HFmrEF, HFrEF), presenting P for interaction as well as the HR, 95% CI, and P values stratified by EF phenotypes. Since NT-proBNP has been proven to be a biomarker inversely associated with obesity [26], adjusting for NT-proBNP may alter the observed effect of obesity. So, we conducted sensitivity analysis excluding NT-proBNP from covariates in the fully adjusted Cox model. Two-sided P values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM, USA), R version 4.0.2 (The R Foundation, Austria), and Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, USA) were used for statistical analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 2228 patients hospitalized for heart failure were enrolled in this study. The median age was 59 years old, and 29.4% of the population were women. A total of 484 patients (22%) were diagnosed with obesity, 810 patients (36%) presented with microalbuminuria and 194 (8.7%) with both conditions at baseline (Table 1). Patients scattered across all left ventricular ejection fraction categories, with 10%, 40.4%, 49.5% being HFmrEF, HFpEF and HFrEF, respectively. Most patients (83.7%) were on the medication of β-blockers, 53.1% were treated with ACEI, ARB, or ARNI, and 59.9% with MRA. Notably, no patients were on the medication of SGLT2i in this cohort because of the time interval of recruiting.

Among the patients diagnosed with obesity, microalbuminuria was more prevalent compared with nonobese patients (40.1% versus 35.3%). Patients were subsequently categorized into four groups: Nonobese/nUACR, Nonobese/hUACR, Obese/nUACR, Obese/hUACR (Table 1). The composition of LVEF was similar within the groups with the same microalbuminuria status (Nonobese/nUACR and Obese/nUACR, Nonobese/hUACR and Obese/hUACR). In the two groups with microalbuminuria, the proportion of patients with HFrEF significantly exceeded that of HFpEF. In the Obese/hUACR group, patients with HFrEF were almost twice as many as those with HFpEF. The Obese/hUACR group has the highest percentage of patients with a smoking history compared to the other three groups. Additionally, LVEDD on echocardiography was also the highest in the Obese/hUACR group. NT-proBNP levels were significantly higher in groups with microalbuminuria, and lower in groups with obesity. Interestingly, patients with obesity and/or microalbuminuria showed remarkably preserved or even elevated eGFR, especially the Obese/hUACR group which had the highest eGFR level among the four groups. Medication and comorbidities were also described in each group.

Prognosis

The median duration of the follow-up period was 678 days. During the follow-up, 331 (14.86%) patients died, 268 (11.94%) died of cardiovascular causes, and 163 (7.32%) patients were rehospitalized for HF.

Kaplan–Meier analysis and log-rank test were conducted to compare event-free survival among the four groups (Fig. 2). The results revealed significant differences across the four groups in cardiovascular death, all-cause mortality, rehospitalization for heart failure, and the composite of cardiovascular death and rehospitalization for heart failure (P < 0.05). For the composite of CV death and HHP, the Nonobese/hUACR group showed the worst prognosis, followed by the Obese/hUACR group, while the two groups with no microalbuminuria manifested relatively better prognosis. The same trend can be observed in cardiovascular death and all-cause mortality. Interestingly, when it comes to rehospitalization for heart failure, the trend is quite different than the others, possibly implying diverse contributions to the composite endpoint.

Cox regression analysis was performed to verify the independent prognostic effect of obesity and microalbuminuria for adverse outcomes (Table 2). The results showed that obesity was independently associated with the primary outcome of CV death and HHP (HR 1.42, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.08–1.86, p = 0.013). For the secondary outcomes, obesity was an independent risk factor for HHP (HR 1.71, 95%CI 1.12–2.6, p = 0.013), while the association of obesity with CV death alone or all-cause mortality was not statistically significant. As for microalbuminuria, it was related to an increased risk of the primary outcome both in the crude model and after adjustment (HR 1.28, 95%CI 1.01–1.62, p = 0.038). However, the association of microalbuminuria and secondary outcomes appeared to be statistically insignificant after adjusting for confounders.

Stratified analysis by EF phenotypes (HFpEF, HFmrEF, HFrEF)

We investigated whether there was an interaction between obesity, microalbuminuria, and EF phenotypes (HFpEF, HFmrEF, HFrEF), and conducted a stratified analysis to examine the association between obesity, microalbuminuria, joint grouping, and the primary endpoint. Our cohort comprised 976 patients with HFrEF (primary endpoint incidence 27.7%), 312 patients with HFmrEF (primary endpoint incidence 15.1%), and 874 patients with HFpEF (primary endpoint incidence 11.9%).

As shown in Table 3, there was an interaction between microalbuminuria and EF phenotypes (P for interaction = 0.036), whereas obesity and joint grouping did not show a significant interaction with EF phenotypes (all P for interaction > 0.05). Results of the stratified analysis showed that in the HFpEF subgroup, both obesity and microalbuminuria were independent risk factors for the primary endpoint (obesity: HR 2.11, 95% CI 1.25–3.56, P = 0.005; microalbuminuria: HR 1.89, 95% CI 1.18–3.03, P = 0.008), while these associations were not significant in the HFrEF and HFmrEF subgroups. In the HFpEF subgroup, the risk of primary endpoint was significantly elevated in the Obese/hUACR group (HR 3.81, 95% CI 1.87–7.76, P < 0.001). This association was not significant in the HFrEF and HFmrEF subgroups either.

Sensitivity analysis

Since NT-proBNP has been proven to be a biomarker reflecting HF severity which was also inversely associated with obesity, adjusting for NT-proBNP may alter the observed effect of obesity [26]. So, we conducted sensitivity analysis excluding NT-proBNP from covariates in the fully adjusted Cox model. Table S-2 (Supplementary file) showed that the association between obesity and the primary endpoint became insignificant after adjustment (P > 0.05). Apart from that, no differences in significance were found regarding the associations between obesity or obesity related four-category groups with adverse outcomes, comparing to the original Cox regression model.

Discussion

According to our results, the prevalence of obesity and microalbuminuria was relatively high among hospitalized HF patients, and there was overlap between these two comorbidities. We divided the patients into four groups based on BMI and UACR for survival analysis. The results showed that differences in obesity and microalbuminuria status accurately stratified event-free survival across the four groups. We showed that obesity was an independent risk factor for HF hospitalization, while its association with the primary composite outcome appeared be model-dependent. Microalbuminuria was also an independent predictor for the adverse outcome of HF. Considering both comorbidities, the Obese/hUACR group and Obese/nUACR group were associated with equally highest risk of the primary outcome, which is 61% higher than the reference. Analysis of the secondary endpoints showed that the association between Obese/hUACR and the primary outcome may be primarily driven by an increased risk of rehospitalization rather than cardiovascular death. RCS analysis of UACR revealed a nonlinear association between UACR and the risk of all adverse outcomes. Stratified analysis by EF phenotypes showed that in the HFpEF subgroup, the risk of the primary endpoint was completely different from that observed in the HFrEF and HFmrEF subgroups. Subgroup findings in HFpEF population may be valuable but they still warrant further investigation because obesity and joint grouping did not show significant interaction with EF phenotypes and potential misclassification of HFpEF possibly existed. The study by Candemir et al. is an extraordinary example of of contemporary in-hospital risk assessment research by emphasizing the prognostic importance of hemodynamic parameters like 24-h blood pressure monitoring during hospitalization [27]. We must particularly mention their work for it complementing our study to underline the multifaceted nature of risk assessment in HF. In all, we highlight that under the framework of CKM syndrome, combining renal, metabolic, and hemodynamic indicators may improve prognostic precision for HF.

The interaction between heart and kidney has drawn much attention over the past decades. Microalbuminuria is associated with increased risk of incident heart failure and heart failure progression independent of other comorbidities like T2DM [28]. The prevalence of obesity and CKD has been rising in a parallel style, and since they are both important components of “cardiovascular-kidney–metabolic syndrome”, scientists are particularly interested in their interaction and predisposition to cardiovascular diseases [29]. Evidence from Mendelian randomization studies and large meta-analyses demonstrated obesity, especially visceral adiposity, is an independent risk factor for renal function deterioration and mortality [30–32]. Newer research revealed that the causal relationship between obesity and CKD might be bidirectional, suggesting kidney-derived growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) may mediate weight loss from the hindbrain [33]. Mechanisms mediating this effect are complicated, possibly including obesity-related glomerulopathy (ORG), renal hemodynamic alterations, hypertension, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) activation, and inflammation [34, 35]. The prevalence of obesity and microalbuminuria in our HF cohort aligned with the aforementioned research, remarkably higher than that reported in general population.

An intriguing baseline observation we want to mention is eGFR. Comparing the four groups, we discovered that patients with obesity and/or microalbuminuria showed remarkably preserved or even elevated eGFR, especially the Obese/hUACR group which had the highest eGFR level among the four groups. This seemingly paradoxical finding may be explained by the following aspects. 1) It is likely to reflect the state of glomerular hyperfiltration which is a common hemodynamic adaptation in the early stage of obesity-related kidney disease [36]. The hyperfiltration is not only a direct contributor to the development of microalbuminuria but also results in enhanced clearance of serum creatinine, leading to preserved or even elevated eGFR. 2) Our finding is limited by the inherent drawbacks of creatinine as a marker of renal function. Serum creatinine levels are directly influenced by muscle mass. The high prevalence of sarcopenia in the heart failure population, particularly among obese patients, may result in decreased creatinine generation, further overestimating creatinine-based eGFR [37]. 3) The accuracy of creatinine-based eGFR equations (e.g., CKD-EPI) can be compromised in obese individuals due to variations in body composition [38]. Therefore, the higher eGFR observed may not represent true renal reserve but rather a complex phenotype involving hyperfiltration injury and methodological biases. We also performed interaction analysis to investigate whether the effect of UACR on the primary outcome could be modified by eGFR. Table S-3 (Supplementary file) showed that the interaction between UACR and eGFR was statistically significant (P for interaction < 0.001). These results underscore the unique value of microalbuminuria as an early and critical marker of renal dysfunction. While there is both correlation and distinction between UACR and eGFR, their interaction and impact on prognosis require further investigation.

The methodological consideration regarding adjustment for eGFR in the multivariable Cox model also deserves careful discussion. Given that eGFR and UACR represent interrelated dimensions of renal function, and that declining eGFR may lie on the causal pathway from microalbuminuria to adverse outcomes, adjusting for eGFR may constitute biological overadjustment. Specifically, the estimated effect of microalbuminuria in our fully adjusted models likely represents its ‘direct effect’ independent of eGFR, rather than its ‘total effect’ including the indirect pathway mediated through eGFR decline. This modeling strategy was intentional, as our primary aim was to assess whether microalbuminuria provides incremental prognostic value beyond traditional eGFR assessment. Future studies employing formal mediation analysis could further quantify the proportion of potential mediation.

Unlike microalbuminuria, which has been validated to confer increased risk of cardiovascular death and all-cause mortality, the relationship between obesity and heart failure is more complicated, and there are controversies in different studies. Additionally, the influence of obesity differs in HFpEF and HFrEF [39], still lacking reasonable explanations. Adamson et al. analyzed the association of baseline BMI with outcomes in a subset of patients with HFrEF from DAPA-HF [40]. Results demonstrated that patients with moderate obesity (BMI 30.0—34.9 kg/m2) exhibited the lowest risk of worsening heart failure or cardiovascular death compared with patients with lower or higher BMI at baseline. A meta-analysis of 14 HF trials conducted by Padwal et al. showed similar results that lowest hazard was observed in patients with a BMI of 30.0—34.9, both in the subset of HFpEF and HFrEF [41]. Conversely, research highlighted obesity as a unique phenotype of HFpEF way back in 2017, showing that HFpEF exhibited worse hemodynamic profiles and symptoms when complicated with obesity [42]. Intentional weight loss achieved by drug therapies has demonstrated beneficial effects in patients with HFpEF and obesity, for example GLP-1 RAs. A pooled analysis of the STEP-HFpEF and STEP-HFpEF DM trials showed that Semaglutide achieved significant improvement of exercise tolerability and weight loss across all BMI subgroups as long as they were diagnosed as obese at baseline [43]. To sum up, the aforementioned research introduced an interesting phenomenon called “obesity paradox”. Based on the latest evidence, “obesity paradox” may be attributed to the following factors: reduced risk of hypotension and better tolerability to HF GDMT, early manifestation of HF symptoms and more timely intervention, potential confounders related to high BMI that are not well accounted for like cardiopulmonary function. Besides, low BMI in the setting of HF can be associated with frailty, malnutrition or cachexia. In this regard, we have to mention another intriguing observation from our study. There is discrepancy between the Kaplan–Meier analysis, which showed the poorest event-free survival in the Nonobese/hUACR group, and the multivariable Cox regression, which identified obesity as an independent risk factor for adverse outcomes. This seemingly paradoxical finding warrants further exploration. First, as we already mentioned, the Nonobese group likely included a substantial proportion of patients with malnutrition or even cachexia. Second, after multivariable adjustment for confounders, the direct prognostic role of obesity as a risk factor became apparent. This does not contradict the KM findings which represent the unadjusted ‘real-world’ data. Finally, as with any observational study, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Unmeasured factors such as nutritional status, inflammatory markers, and medication adherence may have influenced our findings. We have to point out that sensitivity analysis showed that the association between obesity and primary outcome became non-significant after excluding NT-proBNP from the covariates. This suggests that the role of obesity as a prognostic factor in our cohort was not robust across different model specifications. To sum up, our results comfirmed that ‘obesity paradox’ may exist in real-world settings. However, the independent prognostic value of obesity in HF is still questionable. Obesity may be associated with worse outcomes in hospitalized HF patients, but this association appears to be model-dependent and warrants further investigation.

Another important finding from our results is that patients with overlapping obesity and microalbuminuria showed significantly worse prognosis than those with none. Previous research on the prognostic effect of overlapping obesity and microalbuminuria is scarce. Nowak et al. revealed that overweight and, particularly, obesity was strongly and independently associated with early progression of autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD), with greater annual decline of total kidney volume and eGFR [44]. The work of Bonnet et al. demonstrated BMI was an independent predictor of arterial hypertension and chronic renal failure among patients with biopsy-validated immunoglobulin A (IgA) nephropathy [45]. Pammer et al. showed that metabolic syndrome and its components including waist circumference were predictors for all-cause mortality and MACE in a CKD cohort [46]. According to our results, categorizing hospitalized HF patients using BMI and UACR accurately stratified the cumulative incidence of primary events. Using the Nonobese/nUACR group as reference, the remaining three groups were all associated with increased risk of the primary outcome, independently from common established risk factors. The Obese/nUACR and Obese/hUACR groups manifested approximately equal hazard, that is, 61% higher than the reference, while the Nonobese/hUACR group was associated with 33% increased risk. These results are quite interesting and offer several aspects to interpret. First, it provided solid evidence for risk stratification of hospitalized HF patients using both BMI and UACR to improve accuracy. Second, the comorbid condition of both microalbuminuria and obesity was identified as a strong independent predictor of adverse outcomes in HF, further underscoring its prognostic importance. Third, it is worth noting that the Obese/nUACR and Obese/hUACR groups have identical HRs and close CIs suggesting in obese patients, the addition of microalbuminuria may not provide incremental prognostic information beyond obesity alone, despite microalbuminuria being an independent predictor for HF prognosis. Possible explanations for this phenomenon are as follows: (1) obesity itself may be a dominant risk factor that it saturates the prognostic signal; (2) microalbuminuria may lie on the causal pathway between obesity and outcomes, rather than being an independent contributor; (3) the relatively small sample size of the Obese/hUACR subgroup (194 people, 8.7% of total cohort) may limit statistical precision.We hope these speculative hypotheses will be validated in future studies.

When analysis HF rehospitalization alone, the situation was slightly different. Regardless of the presence of microalbuminuria, groups with obesity (Obese/hUACR and Obese/nUACR) were associated with significantly increased risk of rehospitalization, while the group with microalbuminuria alone was not. These results strengthened the theory that HF exacerbation symptoms appeared earlier and more pronounced in obese patients. Interestingly, the Obese/hUACR group was associated with a significantly increased risk of HF rehospitalization but not with cardiovascular death. This finding suggests that the observed association between the Obese/hUACR group and the primary composite outcome may be primarily driven by HF rehospitalization rather than cardiovascular death. Several explanations may account for this discrepancy. First, obesity and microalbuminuria may exert stronger effects on non-fatal outcomes such as rehospitalization, which are influenced by hemodynamic alteration, inflammation, and renal function deterioration. Besides, the competing risk of non-cardiovascular death, particularly in obese patients with metabolic complications, could have attenuated the association with cardiovascular death. Regardless of the underlying mechanisms, our findings highlight the importance of obesity intervention and enhanced follow-up for patients with microalbuminuria to reduce the risk of HF rehospitalization and improve quality of life.

Of note, we did not detect statistically significant multiplicative interaction between obesity and microalbuminuria. Several considerations may explain this finding. First, statistical tests for interaction are generally underpowered. Detecting a modest interaction typically requires a substantially larger sample size than detecting main effects. Our sample size may have been insufficient to detect a weak-to-moderate multiplicative interaction between obesity and microalbuminuria. Second, the pathophysiological relationship between obesity and albuminuria may manifest primarily as additive rather than multiplicative interaction for their shared mechanisms such as inflammation, insulin resistance, and glomerular hyperfiltration. Finally, the absence of a significant multiplicative interaction does not negate the value of each risk factor individually, nor does it diminish the clinical utility of combined screening. The key clinical message remains that patients with both obesity and microalbuminuria have poor prognosis, and this high-risk subgroup warrants particular attention in clinical practice.

As mentioned earlier, the association between obesity and prognosis may be influenced by EF phenotypes. Therefore, we conducted a stratified analysis of the association between obesity, microalbuminuria, and the primary endpoint based on heart failure subtypes (HFrEF, HFmrEF, HFpEF). The results showed that in the patients with HFpEF, microalbuminuria were independently associated with the primary endpoint, whereas in the non-HFpEF population, the association was not statistically significant. The same pattern can be found for obesity and Obese/hUACR. However, interaction with EF phenotypes was only significant for microalbuminuria, but not obesity and joint grouping. Obesity is a classic subtype of HFpEF [26]. As mentioned earlier, weight-loss medications have been shown to improve outcomes in patients with HFpEF over the past years. Several studies have also positively confirmed that obesity increased the risk of adverse outcomes in HFpEF patients [47]. Due to the lack of significance in interaction testing, we cannot conclude that the effect of obesity differs by heart failure phenotype based on the present data. The observed pattern may reflect limited statistical power or chance findings. Nevertheless, given the biological plausibility of obesity playing a more prominent role in HFpEF pathophysiology, this hypothesis warrants further investigation in larger, well-powered studies specifically designed to test interaction. Regarding microalbuminuria, Katz et al. confirmed that microalbuminuria was independently associated with myocardial remodeling and adverse outcomes in patients with HFpEF [48]. A large community-based cohort study confirmed that baseline albuminuria significantly increased the risk of new-onset HFpEF, but not HFrEF [49]. After stratification by heart failure type, the joint grouping results revealed that the coexistence of obesity and microalbuminuria was an independent risk factor for adverse outcomes in the HFpEF subgroup, with a 281% increased risk compared to the reference. However, the lack of significance in interaction testing and possibility for residual confounding significantly compromised the validity of these results. The potential misclassification of HFpEF also cannot be neglected. Therefore, these subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution and further investigations are warranted. In summary, our results provide hypothesis-generating evidence that we need to pay more attention to the comorbidity of obesity and microalbuminuria in the HFpEF population.

Our study has some limitations

(1) This is a single-center retrospective study, our findings should be interpreted as associational evidence rather than causal inference. (2) Selection bias is almost unavoidable in retrospective studies, as well as recall bias. We tried our best to enroll patients strictly according to the inclusion criteria consecutively and use objective indicators and endpoints to minimize this bias. (3) None of the patients in our cohort received SGLT2 inhibitors due to the time interval of enrollment. SGLT2 inhibitors are considered cornerstone of contemporary HF management and have significant effects on both microalbuminuria and prognosis. In current clinical practice, with the widespread use of these agents, the associations observed in our cohort may be attenuated. However, it is also worth noting that our study provides a valuable baseline reference for the 'natural history' of the obesity-microalbuminuria phenotype in the pre-SGLT2 inhibitor era. Understanding the prognostic impact of these risk factors in the absence of potent modifying therapies helps contextualize the magnitude of benefit that SGLT2 inhibitors might confer in this high-risk subgroup. Moreover, our findings remain directly relevant to populations who, for various reasons (e.g., contraindications, intolerance, or limited access), are not receiving SGLT2 inhibitors in real-world settings globally. (4) UACR and BMI are inherently continuous and dynamic variables that may fluctuate over time. According to KDIGO criteria, the diagnosis of microalbuminuria requires confirmation over three-month period. However, as a retrospective study, we relied on a single spot urine UACR measurement at the time of hospital admission and were unable to assess the persistence of albuminuria. This single measurement method may be influenced transient factors such as hemodynamic fluctuations and acute HF decompensation, leading to potential misclassification bias. Our findings support the utility of admission UACR for initial risk stratification, while also highlighting the need for confirmatory testing in patients with positive results to establish persistence. (5) The discrepancy about the role of obesity between Kaplan–Meier analysis and multivariable Cox regression may be explained by cachexia, NT-proBNP dilution or residual confounding. Nevertheless, it must be explicitly acknowledged that, as a retrospective study, we did not directly measure cachexia-related parameters, nor did we perform formal analyses to test the role of NT-proBNP in the obesity-outcome relationship. Consequently, the above explanations remain speculative hypotheses rather than empirical conclusions. (6) Our assessment of renal function relied on creatinine-based eGFR, which may be less accurate in individuals with extreme body compositions, such as sarcopenic obesity, and does not measure glomerular filtration rate directly. (7) Given the retrospective nature of our study, systematic confirmation of congestion and exclusion of alternative causes of dyspnea cannot be ensured, which may introduce potential diagnostic heterogeneity for HFpEF. The potential impact of such misclassification on our results is difficult to predict given the heterogeneity of HFpEF mimics. Therefore, the findings in HFpEF subgroup should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Future prospective studies with rigorous diagnostic algorithms, including systematic exclusion of HFpEF mimics, are needed to validate our findings.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first research trying to explore the combined prognostic effect of obesity and microalbuminuria in HF in a large Chinese cohort. In the current study, we displayed the high prevalence of microalbuminuria and obesity and their overlap in hospitalized HF patients. Considering both BMI and UACR accurately stratified event-free survival of HF. Microalbuminuria was an independent predictor for poor prognosis, while the prognostic value of obesity may be model-dependent. The overlap of microalbuminuria and obesity was an independent risk factor for poor outcomes in HF and it appears to be primarily driven by an increased risk of rehospitalization rather than cardiovascular death. Notably, in obese patients the addition of microalbuminuria may not provide incremental prognostic information beyond obesity alone. Proper assessment and early intervention of obesity, microalbuminuria and their overlap may help improve prognosis. Hopefully our work will provide a better understanding of the interplay between HF, CKD, and metabolic disorders and enlighten the individualized management of patients with HF.
