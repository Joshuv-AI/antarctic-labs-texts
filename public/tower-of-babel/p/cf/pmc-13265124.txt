Multidisciplinary Offloading for Healed Diabetic Foot Ulcers: A Prospective Study on Functional Outcomes and Predictors of Recurrence, Amputation, and Mortality
Authors: Chia‐Yu Wang, Chia‐Yuu Koo, Jung‐Hsuan Chang, Ka‐Wai Tam, Yi‐Chun Wu, Shun‐Cheng Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo evaluate the effectiveness of multidisciplinary offloading versus standard care on one‐year diabetic foot ulcer recurrence, amputation, mortality, and functional recovery. In this prospective cohort study, 232 patients with healed diabetic foot ulcers were stratified into a control group (76 patients) or an intervention group (156 patients) receiving offloading modalities ranging from felt padding to custom‐made therapeutic footwear. Assignment was based on shared decision‐making considering biomechanical needs and economic feasibility. Primary outcomes included recurrence, amputation, and mortality. Secondary outcomes assessed quality of life, working ability, and ankle function. The intervention group demonstrated significantly lower recurrence (10.9% vs. 25.0%; p = 0.007) and mortality (3.2% vs. 14.5%; p = 0.004). Multivariable analysis identified offloading as independently protective against recurrence (odds ratio 0.35) and mortality (odds ratio 0.24). Amputation rates did not differ significantly after adjustment. Functionally, the intervention group achieved superior recovery in quality of life, working ability, and ankle scores (p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis indicated that customized therapeutic footwear yielded the lowest complication rates and highest patient satisfaction. Multidisciplinary offloading significantly reduces recurrence and mortality while restoring physical function. Although financial barriers influence device selection, customized therapeutic footwear offers the optimal balance of biomechanical protection and functional outcomes.

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) represent a global health challenge, affecting approximately one‐third of individuals with diabetes over their lifetime [1, 2, 3] With the global diabetic population projected to reach 852 million by 2045 [4] the burden of these complications is expected to surge. Despite advances in medical management, these ulcers remain the leading cause of non‐traumatic lower extremity amputations and carry a 5‐year mortality rate of 50%, a prognosis that rivals many malignancies [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. The burden of the disease extends beyond the acute wound, as the recurrence rates remain persistently high even after successful closure. While recurrence is driven by established risk factors such as peripheral neuropathy, arterial disease, and renal insufficiency [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14] this cycle of ulceration, healing, and recurrence suggests that addressing the underlying systemic risk factors alone is insufficient for long‐term limb preservation.

To alleviate these risks, the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF) advocates a comprehensive multidisciplinary care model integrating glycemic control, vascular assessment, and infection management [15, 16, 17]. Within this framework, IWGDF guidelines emphasize preventive strategies including risk stratification, routine foot surveillance, patient and provider education, appropriate footwear, and modification of reversible risk factors [3, 18]. Among these measures, the routine use of appropriate footwear and offloading interfaces is identified as the most critical modifiable factor for preventing recurrent mechanical trauma [3, 18].

Despite strong guideline support, the real‐world implementation of offloading strategies remains highly variable globally [19, 20]. This implementation gap persists across both high‐income and resource‐limited settings, largely driven by a disconnect between clinical guidelines and routine practice [21]. Secondary prevention options utilized in daily practice range widely from simple adhesive felt padding and prefabricated insoles to fully customized therapeutic footwear [2]. Although specialized footwear is generally recommended for high‐risk patients, selecting the optimal modality requires balancing biomechanical efficacy and economic feasibility. Consequently, longitudinal comparative data evaluating the clinical and functional outcomes of different offloading strategies remain limited.

To address these gaps, this study employed a standardized multidisciplinary care platform to evaluate the real‐world effectiveness of different offloading interventions for the 12‐month secondary prevention of diabetic foot complications. The primary objective was to assess the impact of offloading intervention versus standard care on one‐year rates of ulcer recurrence, amputation, and mortality in patients with healed DFUs. Secondary objectives included exploring clinical trends and functional outcomes associated with specific offloading modalities, ranging from basic padding to custom therapeutic footwear, to comprehensively assess the balance between biomechanical protection and daily usability, thereby providing practical evidence to support guideline implementation.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This open‐label, prospective non‐randomized cohort study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Taiwan. We consecutively recruited all patients presenting to the wound care center who met the inclusion criteria between March 2017 and February 2020. The study evaluated offloading interventions for the secondary prevention of diabetic foot complications. Patients with confirmed healed DFUs were recruited and followed for 12 months, with the final follow‐up concluding on February 28, 2021. Eligible participants were aged 20 to 80 years, cognitively capable of providing consent, and had a documented DFU history. To ensure biomechanical relevance, patients with isolated dorsal ulcers were excluded, as these lesions are typically caused by direct friction rather than weight‐bearing stress [2]. Further exclusions included immunosuppression, active cancer, pregnancy, Wagner grade 5 ulcers or cognitive impairments precluding effective communication. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the study hospital, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Integrated Care Protocol and Multidisciplinary Intervention

The protocol integrated a general survey, multidisciplinary intervention, and five standardized care bundles. Initial evaluation assessed systemic and wound status via standard laboratory panels, radiographic imaging, and microbiological sampling to rule out osteomyelitis. Subsequent multidisciplinary care involved vascular surgery, endocrinology, nutrition, rehabilitation, infectious disease, and social work teams to provide comprehensive support for systemic comorbidities.

Treatment was delivered through five functional bundles comprising non‐surgical management; surgical interventions ranging from debridement to vascular bypass; a structured seven‐step education program (removal, cleaning, debridement, selection, dressing, education, re‐visit); an offloading intervention bundle utilizing specific devices (e.g., therapeutic footwear or custom insoles) and functional rehabilitation; and outcome surveillance involving systematic monitoring. Further details are illustrated in Supplementary Digital Content 1.

Shared Decision‐Making and Offloading Assignment

Patients were stratified into two groups via a shared decision‐making process involving a plastic surgeon and occupational therapist (OT), grounded in objective biomechanical data and patient‐specific factors. The OT performed foot pressure evaluations using electronic pedobarography (Ortho‐tech; Dr. Foot Technology Co. Ltd., Yilan, Taiwan) to quantify peak plantar pressure during standing and walking, serving as the basis for personalized strategy design. The team discussed trade‐offs regarding biomechanical protection, out‐of‐pocket cost, and lifestyle requirements to determine the most appropriate offloading strategy. Group 1 comprised patients who declined specific offloading due to personal or economic reasons. Group 2 included patients who agreed to use offloading devices. All included patients (n = 232) maintained their initially selected offloading strategy throughout the entire follow‐up period without switching groups.

Classification of Offloading Modalities

Group 2 was stratified into four subgroups (2A–2D) representing progressively greater offloading capacity, customization, and cost (Supplementary Digital Content 2). Group 2A received a wool adhesive felt pressure‐relief pad (Hapla, Singapore) combined with walk shoes (Baldur, Taiwan) [WFP + WS]. This approach provided localized offloading by applying the felt pad directly to the healed DFU site for targeted decompression, supported by a simple sandal‐style shoe. Group 2B used prefabricated offloading insoles (Dr. Foot, Taiwan) [POLI], which were factory‐produced standardized insoles without customization for foot morphology or pressure distribution, selected solely according to foot size. Group 2C received custom‐made insoles (Dr. Foot, Taiwan) [CMI] fabricated from a mould of the patient's foot to address individualized plantar pressure patterns; these insoles were fitted into standard footwear. Group 2D was prescribed therapeutic footwear (Dr. Foot, Taiwan) [TF], representing the most comprehensive intervention, consisting of a fully customized insole combined with a custom‐moulded shoe that fully enclosed the foot. Both components were fabricated based on a custom mould of the patient's foot to provide maximal offloading and protection.

Outcome Assessment

Primary outcomes were defined as the incidence of ulcer recurrence, amputation (including both minor (toe/ray) and major (above‐ankle) amputations), and all‐cause mortality during the 12‐month follow‐up period. Ulcer recurrence was defined as the development of any new full‐thickness skin defect on the ipsilateral foot (the same foot as the initial index ulcer) following complete epithelialization. These clinical assessments were performed by the treating plastic surgery team during scheduled outpatient follow‐up visits. All suspected recurrence events were systematically verified and documented using standardized photographic records to minimize observer bias. Incidences of amputation and mortality were confirmed via electronic medical records.

The secondary outcomes were the functional outcomes comprising Quality of Life (QOL), Working Ability (WA), and the Freiburg Ankle Score (FAS) assessed using questionnaires. Patients who lost ambulatory capacity during the follow‐up period were excluded from these functional assessments. In this study, the FAS was utilized to monitor the potential biomechanical impact of the offloading devices on lower extremity mobility. The evaluation encompassed pain, instability, functional impairment, gait, ankle circumference, range of motion in dorsiflexion and plantarflexion, and muscle strength (standing on tiptoes) (Supplementary Digital Content 3) [22]. QOL and WA assessments were evaluated using structured questionnaires adapted from the WHOQOL User Manual (2012 revision) and Taiwan's 2010 Physical Fitness Guidelines to ensure content relevance and patient comprehension in our local cohort (Supplementary Digital Content 4) [23, 24]. All functional assessments were performed by a single OT at four specific time points: Baseline (defined as the time of initiation of the protocol), 3, 6, and 12 months. In instances of absence, a second experienced therapist followed the same standardized protocol to complete the assessment. To evaluate feasibility, the primary barriers preventing patients in Group 1 from utilizing the recommended interventions were documented. Additionally, for patients in Group 2, overall patient satisfaction with the offloading device was assessed at the 12‐month follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Baseline differences were evaluated using Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Chi‐square or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables.

Primary outcomes were analysed in the intention‐to‐treat (ITT) population. To identify independent predictors of prevention failure, a two‐stage regression approach was applied. Due to zero‐event subgroups in the amputation and mortality analyses, merging of Smoking levels (Current vs. Non/Ex‐smoker) and Wagner grades (Grades I–III vs. Grades IV) was used to ensure model stability. Variables with p < 0.1 in univariable analysis or with significant baseline imbalance were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model using backward stepwise selection based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Secondary functional outcomes were analysed only including patients with complete 12‐month follow‐up. Longitudinal changes were assessed using repeated‐measures ANOVA to compare recovery patterns. Between‐group differences at baseline and 12 months were evaluated using independent‐samples t‐tests, and within‐group changes using paired‐samples t‐tests. Subgroup comparisons among intervention modalities were performed using one‐way ANOVA with post hoc analysis to compare 12‐month functional outcomes and patient satisfaction. Analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with statistical significance defined as a two‐tailed p < 0.05.

Results

Patient Data and Baseline Characteristics

Between March 2017 and February 2020, a total of 250 patients with healed DFUs were assessed. Of these, 18 patients were excluded prior to enrollment: 7 declined to participate or were lost to contact, 4 were ineligible due to non‐ambulatory status, 3 had pure dorsal foot ulcers, and 4 met other ineligibility criteria. The remaining 232 patients were stratified into two arms: the non‐intervention control group (Group 1, n = 76) and the offloading intervention group (Group 2, n = 156). The intervention group was further categorized into four subgroups: Group 2A (WFP + WS, n = 52), Group 2B (POLI, n = 41), Group 2C (CMI, n = 43), and Group 2D (TF, n = 20). During the follow‐up period, 31 patients (Group 1: n = 15; Group 2: n = 16) were excluded from the secondary functional analysis due to mortality or incomplete assessment data. Therefore, 201 patients (Group 1: n = 61; Group 2: n = 140) completed 12‐month follow‐up (Figure 1).

Incidence and Risk Factors of Recurrence

Ulcer recurrence occurred in 36 patients, with significantly lower incidence in the intervention group (17/156, 10.9%) compared to the control group (19/76, 25.0%; p = 0.007). Univariate analysis identified Group (p = 0.007), advanced CKD (p < 0.001), PAOD (p = 0.004), DM treatment (p = 0.001), and Wagner grade (p = 0.016) as significant (Supplementary Digital Content 5). After adjusting for these factors and baseline characteristics that showed significant differences (Age and advanced CKD) in the final model (AIC = 171.36; retaining Group, Age, DM treatment, advanced CKD, PAOD), offloading remained independently protective (OR 0.35; 95% CI: 0.15–0.81; p = 0.014), while advanced CKD (OR 2.65; 95% CI: 1.12–6.39; p = 0.027) and PAOD (OR 3.69; 95% CI: 1.25–13.79; p = 0.030) were identified as independent risk factors.

Incidence and Risk Factors for Amputation

Amputation occurred in 21 patients. A lower rate is observed in the intervention group (10/156, 6.4%) compared to the control group (11/76, 14.5%; p = 0.050). Univariate analysis indicated that advanced CKD (p = 0.001) and higher Wagner grades (p = 0.005) were significantly associated with adverse outcomes (Supplementary Digital Content 6). A trend toward reduced amputation risk was observed in the intervention group during unadjusted analysis (OR 0.40; p = 0.050). However, this association was not significant after adjusting for Age, advanced CKD, and Wagner grade (OR 0.51; 95% CI: 0.19–1.37; p = 0.179). The multivariate model identified advanced CKD (OR 3.10; 95% CI: 1.11–9.21; p = 0.034) and Wagner Grade 4 (OR 2.77; 95% CI: 1.03–7.75; p = 0.045) as significant independent predictors of amputation. The final model retained Sex, Hypertension, Smoking, advanced CKD, and Wagner grade as the most relevant factors contributing to the model (AIC = 125.1).

Incidence and Risk Factors for Mortality

Mortality involved 16 deaths, with significantly fewer deaths observed in Group 2 (5/156, 3.2%) compared to Group 1 (11/76, 14.5%; p = 0.004). Based on the merged categories for zero‐event subgroups, univariate analysis identified Group (OR 0.20; p = 0.004), advanced CKD (p = 0.004), and Wagner grade (p = 0.004) as significant (Supplementary Digital Content 7). After adjustment for potential confounders, the final multivariate model (AIC = 98.98), which retained Group, HbA1c, smoking status, advanced CKD, and Wagner grade, demonstrated that the offloading intervention remained independently protective. Patients in Group 2 had a significantly reduced risk of mortality (OR 0.24; 95% CI: 0.07–0.75; p = 0.017), whereas advanced CKD (OR 3.85; 95% CI: 1.28–13.12; p = 0.021) and advanced Wagner grades (OR 4.08; 95% CI: 1.36–13.83; p = 0.015) were independent risk factors.

Descriptive Subgroup Analysis of Clinical Events

To further evaluate the impact of specific interventions on the primary outcomes, a descriptive subgroup analysis was conducted within Group 2 (Supplementary Digital Content 8). Consistent with the overall protective trend, Group 2D (TF) demonstrated the numerically lowest rates across all three primary endpoints: recurrence, amputation, and mortality.

Secondary Outcome: Functional Outcome Assessment

QOL, WA, and the FAS were evaluated at four intervals: Baseline, 3, 6, and 12 months. Baseline scores for all metrics were comparable between the control and intervention groups (p > 0.05). Quality of Life

Longitudinal analysis revealed that both the control and intervention groups achieved significant improvements in QOL from baseline to 12 months (p < 0.001 for both). However, improvement over time was significantly greater in the intervention group than in the control group (p < 0.001). By the 12‐month endpoint, Group 2 achieved significantly higher QOL scores than Group 1 (13.09 vs. 11.18, p < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis at 12 months showed patients receiving TF (Group 2D) reported the highest QOL scores (16.95), which were significantly superior to all other intervention subgroups (p < 0.001).

Working Ability

Similar to QOL, WA scores improved significantly in both groups (p < 0.001). However, improvement over time was significantly greater in the intervention group, which achieved higher final scores than the control group at 12 months (15.05 vs. 12.57, p < 0.001).

Among intervention modalities, post hoc analysis at 12 months showed that patients receiving TF achieved significantly higher WA scores (18.74) than those in Group 2A, 2B, and 2C (p < 0.001 for all).

Freiburg Ankle Score

While the intervention group showed significant improvement in FAS from baseline to 12 months (+14.62, p < 0.001), the control group showed no statistically significant improvement (+1.67, p = 0.257). This difference in recovery patterns was statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating that skin closure alone does not equate to functional restoration; ankle function remained unchanged in the absence of offloading intervention. In the subgroup analysis, Group 2D achieved the highest score (65.74) at 12 months and was significantly superior to Group 2B (p = 0.027).

Satisfaction Assessment and Barriers to Insole Use

Within the intervention group, the overall mean satisfaction score was 3.53 ± 0.76 (Supplementary Digital Content 11). Satisfaction levels differed significantly across offloading modalities (p < 0.001). Post hoc analysis indicated that Group 2D achieved the highest satisfaction score (4.63 ± 0.68), which was significantly superior to all other subgroups (p < 0.001). Furthermore, prefabricated (Group 2B) and custom insoles (Group 2C) yielded significantly higher satisfaction than simple felt padding (Group 2A, 3.02 ± 0.50; p < 0.05) (Supplementary Digital Content 10).

Regarding the barriers to adherence in the control group (Group 1), financial burden was the most frequently cited barrier to adherence (n = 38) (Supplementary Digital Content 11). Secondary barriers included a perceived lack of need due to limited walking distances (n = 16) and reluctance to change footwear style (n = 13). Physical limitations, such as the inability to put on shoes independently, and general unwillingness were less common.

Discussion

The high recurrence of foot ulcers remains a major clinical challenge, highlighting that a healed DFU is more accurately viewed as a state of remission rather than a definitive cure [19]. With recent evidence showing persistently high recurrence rates of approximately 42% at 1 year, 58% at 3 years, and 65% at 5 years, there is a critical need for structured surveillance and preventive offloading after wound closure [2, 15, 19, 25]. Our study provides real‐world evidence validating that multidisciplinary offloading significantly improves prognosis by independently reducing recurrence and mortality even after adjustment for confounders, thereby reinforcing the critical need to translate international guidelines into everyday clinical practice. Furthermore, patients receiving offloading interventions achieved better functional recovery, with sustained improvements in QOL, WA, and FAS. Although amputation risk was not statistically reduced in multivariate analysis, the descriptive subgroup data suggests a trend toward more favourable clinical outcomes and higher patient satisfaction with fully customized TF (Group 2D).

The recurrence of DFU is often driven by repetitive mechanical stress on insensitive tissue [26]. Our multivariate analysis showed that offloading intervention reduced the risk of recurrence by approximately 65%, which aligns with biomechanical principles indicating that redistribution of peak plantar pressure facilitates maturation of scar tissue at healed site [27]. We further identified advanced CKD and PAOD as independent risk factors for recurrence (OR 3.69 and 2.65, respectively). Although these systemic comorbidities are often irreversible, their strong association with recurrence highlights the necessity of aggressive mechanical offloading in high‐risk patients. In individuals with compromised perfusion and healing capacity, tolerance for weight‐bearing is limited, rendering external protection through insoles or TF a critical limb‐preserving strategy.

Although offloading was associated with a significant reduction in ulcer recurrence, the reduction in amputation risk did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment. This pattern suggests that the mechanisms underlying ulcer recurrence and amputation may differ. Ulcer recurrence is largely attributable to biomechanical stress, whereas amputation reflects a more complex interplay of ischemia, infection, and systemic comorbidities, including digital gangrene related to PAOD [28, 29]. As offloading devices act by redistributing plantar pressure, they do not address vascular patency or the progression of arterial occlusion. Supporting this interpretation, advanced CKD and higher Wagner grades were independently associated with amputation in the multivariable model. These findings indicate that while offloading effectively reduces pressure‐related ulcer recurrence, it may be insufficient to prevent limb loss once tissue viability is compromised by advanced ischemia or deep infection [30].

The reduction in mortality observed in the intervention group (OR 0.24) likely reflects multifactorial influences beyond mechanical protection alone. Although TF does not directly prevent cardiovascular death, adherence to offloading protocols may serve as a surrogate marker of health literacy and engagement in multidisciplinary care [31]. Patients willing to wear therapeutic footwear are often more engaged in glycemic control and lifestyle modifications. Furthermore, recurrence prevention reduces the risk of severe infection and hospitalization, both of which are associated with systemic inflammation [32], a recognized contributor to mortality [33]. However, residual confounding must be acknowledged, as the intervention group was younger and had a lower prevalence of advanced CKD. Consistent with prior studies, advanced CKD and advanced Wagner grades remained independent predictors of mortality, reflecting the impact of systemic vascular disease on survival [34, 35].

When contextualizing our findings, the overall 1‐year ulcer recurrence rate in our study appears lower than the commonly cited population‐level estimates of approximately 40% to 42% reported in recent comprehensive reviews [19, 36]. However, our data align closely with recent studies evaluating patients managed within dedicated multidisciplinary care settings. For instance, Bouly et al. reported a reduced 1‐year recurrence rate of 21.1% under optimized multidisciplinary follow‐up [37], while Hicks et al. reported an incidence of 30.6% within a multidisciplinary limb preservation service [38]. Furthermore, the research by Dargis et al. demonstrated that a multidisciplinary approach (incorporating 3‐month follow‐ups and specialty footwear) significantly halved recurrence rates (30.4% vs. 58.4%) over a two‐year period when compared to standard care [39]. The comparatively low recurrence incidence observed in our cohort is likely attributable to our structured multidisciplinary approach and regular 3‐month follow‐up intervals, which facilitated earlier detection of pre‐ulcerative lesions and reinforced patient adherence. Furthermore, our follow‐up was limited to 12 months, and because the risk of DFU recurrence increases over time, longer follow‐up period would likely result in higher recurrence rates [2, 19].

Subgroup analysis showed that Group 2D consistently exhibited the lowest event rates. Researchers suggest that optimally designed TF can reduce ulcer recurrence by at least 30% compared with standard footwear through the reduction of mechanical stress [40]. The observed trend toward lower recurrence and mortality in our TF group is consistent with this expectation, highlighting the clinical value of combining multidisciplinary care and customized footwear in preventing severe complications.

Beyond limb preservation, our study highlights the impact of offloading on functional restoration. Longitudinal analysis revealed that while both groups achieved significant improvements in QOL and WA during the post‐healing surveillance period, the control group showed no statistically significant improvement in ankle function over 12 months, despite receiving comprehensive multidisciplinary care. This finding suggests that while standard care is effective in resolving tissue defects and relieving pain, it does not restore physical function or correct underlying biomechanical deficits, leaving patients at risk for ongoing gait instability and limited ROM [2, 19, 36, 41]. In contrast, the intervention group demonstrated a significant restoration of ankle function which resulted in a marked difference in outcomes between the two groups over time. This show that offloading intervention, integrated with multidisciplinary care, protects the skin and accommodates biomechanical deficits [19, 42], thereby preserving the physical mobility necessary for vocational capacity and overall well‐being.

Subgroup analysis demonstrated a trend of efficacy where Group 2D consistently produced better outcomes across all functional outcomes. Notably, Group 2D was the only modality to achieve both QOL and WA significantly higher than all other subgroups, suggesting that the stability and protection provided by full customized TF are essential for patients returning to the workforce [3, 43, 44]. This objective functional data aligns with patient‐reported satisfaction. Satisfaction scores increased progressively from simple felt padding (Group 2A) to TF (Group 2D, 4.63/5), indicating that comprehensive offloading footwear provides better comfort and acceptance than simple felt pads.

Despite these clinical benefits, substantial economic barriers to adherence were identified. In the control group, financial burden was the most frequently cited reason for declining offloading intervention. While simpler and less costly modalities such as felt pads (Group 2A) were associated with lower satisfaction, TF (Group 2D) yielded the highest satisfaction yet remained cost prohibitive. This creates a gap in access, where the most effective treatment is accessible only to patients who can afford it [45].

Viewing DFU as a marker of multi‐organ disease, integrated care has been estimated to prevent up to 75% of DFUs and significantly reduce lower‐extremity amputations [6, 46, 47] However, real‐world care for patients with DFUs is often fragmented across multiple specialties and facilities, contributing to persistently high recurrence, amputation, and mortality rates [13, 46, 47, 48] To address this gap, the integrated care model within our wound care center emphasizes coordinated multidisciplinary management, and its effectiveness is reflected in the significant improvements in QOL and WA observed even in Group 1.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its nonrandomized design. First, allocation of offloading devices was influenced by patient preference and economic capability, introducing potential selection bias, as patients with greater financial resources were more likely to access advanced modalities such as custom insoles or TF. Such financial capacity may also correlate with better healthcare access and health literacy, potentially contributing to more favourable outcomes. Second, the open‐label design and visible nature of the offloading devices precluded assessor blinding, introducing potential observer bias. However, to minimize this bias, all clinical endpoints were verified using standardized photographs and electronic medical records. Third, relatively small subgroup sample sizes limit statistical power, while the one‐year follow‐up precludes assessment of long‐term durability. Additionally, data collection for this cohort concluded in February 2021 upon the completion of our pre‐planned follow‐up period. Consequently, there is a chronological gap between the study's conclusion and this publication. Although this timeframe might not reflect the most recent advancements in offloading materials, the concepts of integrated care and offloading evaluated in our study remain highly applicable to current clinical practice. Finally, as a single‐center study, our findings may be influenced by local cultural attitudes toward footwear and characteristics of the Taiwanese healthcare system, thereby limiting generalizability. Larger multicenter studies are needed to validate these results across diverse settings.

Conclusion

Within a multidisciplinary care framework, offloading interventions constitute a cornerstone of secondary prevention for DFUs, significantly reducing recurrence and mortality while improving functional recovery. These real‐world findings reinforce IWGDF recommendations supporting comprehensive offloading strategies [19, 49]. Although mechanical protection alone cannot reverse advanced systemic vascular disease or fully prevent amputation, offloading intervention remains particularly critical for patients with high‐risk comorbidities such as advanced CKD and PAOD to disrupt the cycle of ulcer recurrence. Accordingly, these results underscore the importance of routine offloading interventions, as all evaluated modalities conferred meaningful benefits compared with no intervention. Importantly, greater offloading comprehensiveness was associated with a trend toward lower recurrence, most notably in the TF group (Group 2D). Customized TF, incorporating individualized design, appears to provide the optimal balance of biomechanical protection and patient satisfaction and may represent the most effective strategy for optimizing long‐term outcomes.
