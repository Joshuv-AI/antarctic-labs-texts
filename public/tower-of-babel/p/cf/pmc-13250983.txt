Technoeconomic and Environmental Assessments of CO2 Methanation and Liquefaction for Climate Mitigation
Authors: Changgwon Choe, Jiwon Gu, Junaid Haider, Seunghyun Cheon, Anthony Paul Roskilly, Muhammad Abdul Qyyum, Hankwon Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate change driven by significant anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions necessitates accelerating the decarbonization of the energy system. The power-to-X strategy has emerged as a promising pathway to convert renewable electricity into carbon-neutral energy carriers. Synthetic natural gas is an attractive solution due to its compatibility with existing natural gas infrastructures and widespread end-use applications. In this work, a technoeconomic and environmental analysis of liquefied synthetic natural gas production via CO2 methanation is conducted. The analysis focuses on evaluating the economic feasibility and environmental impact of the methane derived from green H2 and captured CO2 in multiple renewable electricity supply scenarios as well as future cost-reduction pathways. Such feasibility indicators are systematically assessed in different renewable electricity supply scenarios to investigate the climate mitigation potential. The assessment is conducted within a cradle-to-gate system boundary covering the production, liquefaction, and storage of synthetic natural gas, with conventional fossil-based natural gas used as the baseline for comparison. The results demonstrate that power-to-methane can show a wide cost range (76.0 to 109.0 $ GJ–1), which is dependent on electricity prices. Further, it can achieve competitive environmental performances (approximately 0.015 tonCO2-eq GJ–1) and represents a feasible alternative to fossil-based natural gas under favorable renewable electricity integration. This study highlights the potential role of synthetic natural gas as a sustainable energy carrier for long-term climate mitigation.

Introduction

Greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from anthropogenic activities are a major driver of global warming and climate change. Since the Industrial Revolution, the concentrations of carbon dioxide (CO2) have increased to 421 ppm from 280 ppm due to anthropogenic GHG emissions, and these phenomena have resulted in a rise of 1.5 °C in Earth’s temperature. To address this challenge, governments and industries are pursuing strategies to reduce emissions and achieve net-zero targets by 2050. Fossil-fuel consumption remains a major contributor to global emissions due to the increasing demand for energy. Natural gas plays an important role in the global energy system because of its relatively lower emissions compared with other fossil fuels and its extensive infrastructure for production, transportation, and end-use. However, continued reliance on natural gas still results in significant CO2 emissions. Therefore, alternative low-carbon pathways that maintain compatibility with existing natural gas infrastructure are required for a sustainable energy transition.

The Power-to-X (PtX) concept is a well-established platform for the flexible utilization of renewable electricity generated by a mismatch of supply and demand, called surplus electricity. Although renewable energy is a limitless source of clean energy, its production is highly dependent on time and season, which makes PtX development critically necessary. PtX technology is a viable pathway for CO2 conversion to valuable molecules and provides decarbonized alternatives to fossil-fuel-based energy resources. , The representative technology as a renewable electricity carrier is water electrolysis, which is the electrochemical decomposition of water. Water electrolysis technology provides green hydrogen (H2) production using renewable electricity. This green H2 can be integrated with captured CO2 to produce synthetic fuels such as CO2 methanation. A few industrial corporations such as Audi in Germany and Store&Go in Germany have demonstrated pilot plants of the methanation on the MW scale. Despite being a well-developed and demonstrated process, CO2 methanation technology still has a technology readiness level (TRL) of 6–7 due to economic challenges and scale-up of the electrolyzer and methanation unit. , Global industrial corporations have developed several water electrolysis technologies, such as alkaline water electrolysis (AWE), polymer electrolyte membrane water electrolysis (PEM WE), and solid oxide electrolyte water electrolysis (SOE), based on their electrolyte properties and modes of operation. AWE and PEM WE are the most mature water electrolysis techniques in a few decades (TRL of 9), whereas SOE is in the demonstration stage with a TRL of 7. It is, however, noted that SOE presents high electrical efficiency for H2O decomposition, which means a low electrical energy requirement since it operates at a high temperature (more than 700 °C), leading to fast reaction rates. Further, SOE is suitable for integrating large-scale exothermic reactions, as it is capable of waste heat integration. A detailed schematic representation and technical parameters are summarized in the Supporting Information (see Figure S1 and Table S1).

H2 is an important element in the universe to be utilized in the chemical industry; however, recent large-scale H2 production relies strongly on fossil-fuel reforming processes, particularly steam methane reforming processes causing huge amounts of GHG emissions. Nevertheless, utilizing CO2 and green H2 can help carbon emission reduction by integrating carbon capture from a point source or the atmosphere. , There are a lot of molecules catalytically produced from PtX technology (such as methane, methanol, ammonia, etc.), , but methane is, as aforementioned, a primary component of natural gas. The Sabatier reaction, which is a thermodynamically stable reaction for methane synthesis using the CO2 hydrogenation principle over Ni-based catalysts, outlines the multiple chances to accelerate a low-carbon energy society. , As a result, CO2 methanation based on the Sabatier reaction has been extensively investigated as a promising route for renewable methane production. Numerous studies have evaluated the technoeconomic and environmental performance of synthetic methane systems with different CO2 sources and hydrogen supply pathways. Tregambi et al. assessed the technoeconomic performance of synthetic methane production via CO2 methanation using direct air capture and renewable H2. Depending on solar availability, the levelized cost of methane ranged from 4.9 to 8.2 € kg–1 in Abu Dhabi and from 7.7 to 12.0 € kg–1 in Benevento, indicating that economic incentives or further optimization are needed for viability. Lv et al. assessed the technoeconomic potential of integrating carbon capture and methanation for a 1000 MWe coal-fired power plant. Compared to the conventional process, the integrated carbon capture and methanation approach reduced coal consumption by two-thirds and CO2 emissions by 44% while achieving a competitive methane production cost of 443.26 € ton–1 with waste heat recovery and carbon pricing. Ahn et al. conducted an integrated technoeconomic and life cycle assessments of the catalytic depolymerization of waste polyethylene into drop-in fuels and identified optimal operating conditions that achieved a minimum selling price of approximately 1.8 $ GGE–1 and significant greenhouse gas reductions compared with conventional gasoline. Salomone et al. conducted a comprehensive process simulation and energy analysis of a power-to-gas system integrated with a real waste incinerator. The study evaluated six configurations combining different water electrolysis and CO2 capture technologies, demonstrating that thermal integration between methanation and capture units significantly enhances energy efficiency (up to 46.7%) and reduces CO2 avoidance energy consumption, down to 40.5 MJ kg–1. Yun et al. conducted a system-level technoeconomic and life cycle assessment of sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) production from lignocellulosic biomass with the coproduction of value-added chemicals. Their results showed that integrating chemical coproducts improved process economics by 2.7% while achieving significant greenhouse gas reduction of 15.75 kg CO2-eq kg–1 SAF. Otsuki et al. analyzed the technoeconomic feasibility of integrating CO2 capture, water electrolysis, and methanation in Japan’s energy system using a linear programming model. The study found that a toluene-based H2 carrier and domestic methanation could reduce system costs, and policy instruments like carbon pricing would be essential to improve the competitiveness of synthetic methane production. Dincă et al. investigated the integration of power-to-methane with carbon capture (P2M-CC) into cement production as a strategy to reduce CO2 emissions. Three modernization scenarios were analyzed, replacing fossil-based natural gas with synthetic methane derived from green hydrogen and captured CO2. Results showed a drastic reduction in CO2 emissions from 789 to 85 kg ton–1 cement but also revealed significant capital cost increases. Among the scenarios, the ideal scenario delivered the best environmental and economic performance due to the use of surplus wind power, achieving a cement cost of 71 € ton–1 and a net present value (NPV) of 2609 million euros. However, this reported CO2 reduction should be interpreted with caution, as the synthetic methane would ultimately release CO2 upon combustion, and the apparent emission reduction is strongly dependent on the assumed system boundaries and accounting approach.

In this study, we present an integrated technoeconomic and environmental assessment of liquefied synthetic natural gas (SNG) production derived from green H2 and captured CO2, sourced by SOE and renewable electricity. Despite growing interest in power-to-methane systems, comprehensive assessments integrating liquefaction, renewable electricity variability, and climate mitigation potential have remained limited. In particular, the combined technoeconomic and environmental evaluation of liquefied SNG in different renewable electricity supply scenarios has not been sufficiently addressed in the literature. To address this gap, this work evaluates both the unit cost and life cycle emissions of liquefied SNG, providing an integrated assessment of its feasibility as an alternative to conventional natural gas. In contrast to previous studies focusing on CO2 methanation alone, this work integrates methane liquefaction, renewable electricity scenarios, and future economic projections to provide a more comprehensive assessment of SNG production and further evaluate cost-reduction pathways driven by technological learning and scale-up effects. By linking economic feasibility to environmental performance, the results offer insights relevant to climate policy alignment and investment prioritization in the emerging green gas sector.

Process Design and Simulation

The process design and simulation of liquefied SNG production from CO2 hydrogenation and methanation are performed. The overall schematic diagram of cryogenic-based SNG production is depicted in Figure .

Overall schematic diagram of cryogenic-based synthetic natural gas production. (A) Green H2 produced by water electrolysis with renewable electricity, (B) external CO2 supply, (C) chemical reactions (CO2 methanation), (D) The cryogenic processes for CO2 separation, and (E) methane liquefaction for producing a high purity of methane and recyclable raw materials.

The process is divided into five portions: green H2 production, external CO2 supply, Sabatier reaction for methane synthesis, CO2 separation using cryogenic technology, and methane liquefaction. The water electrolysis enables the decomposition of water into gases H2 and oxygen (O2) by an electrical energy input (Figure A). Then, produced H2 is reacted with captured CO2 (Figure B), as in eqs –: 1 2H2O(l)→2H2(g)+O2(g) 2 CO2(g)+4H2(g)↔CH4(g)+2H2O(g) 3 CO(g)+3H2(g)↔CH4(g)+H2O(g) 4 CO2(g)+H2(g)↔CO(g)+H2O(g) As green H2 and CO2 are consumed with a H2/CO2 molar ratio of 4 for methane production in the Sabatier reactor, storing large amounts of renewable electricity is facilitated (Figure C). However, unreacted elements of H2 and CO2 remaining at equilibrium should be removed for recycling and to obtain a high purity product. Because such a component has a large gap of dew point, it is favorable for it to be separated by temperature control (bpH2 < bpmethane < bpCO2 ); therefore, product mixtures including H2 and CO2 are separated with a cryogenic process operating below the boiling point of CO22 2. By the phase separation, CO2 is recycled to feed the stream of methanation (Figure D). Lastly, the methane is liquefied to achieve a high purity of methane and enables transportation to distributors in liquid form (Figure E). Furthermore, the distribution of methane from the producing well to the end user might be favored by well-established natural gas infrastructures.

The SNG production and liquefaction processes are simulated using Aspen Hysys v11, which is a commercial chemical process design simulator. The chemical reaction of methane synthesis is predicted based on chemical reaction kinetics in the form of the Langmuir–Hinshelwood kinetic model reported by Xu et al. over Ni/MgAl2O4 catalysis. Furthermore, the thermodynamic properties of all elements in both methane synthesis and liquefaction are predicted by the Peng–Robinson equation of state for the binary interaction coefficient. Temperature and pressure dependencies of the thermodynamic properties were determined using the built-in Aspen Hysys thermodynamic correlations. The methanation reactor was modeled using a plug flow reactor with kinetic rate expressions adopted from previously published literature (see the Supporting Information). Therefore, the reaction behavior was governed by the kinetic model rather than by thermodynamic equilibrium constraints.

For gas phase components, the Peng-Robinson equation of state is suitable due to its extensive database of physical properties over wide temperature and pressure ranges. Figure presents the detailed process flow diagram selected from the conceptual pathways illustrated in Figure C–E, including methanation reaction with the steam Rankine cycle (SRC) , cryogenic CO2 separation, and methane liquefaction (Figure ). , Detailed information on the process is introduced in Table . The feedstocks of green H2 and CO2 are mixed and then fed into the CO2 methanation reactors after preheating to 350 °C through the heat exchanger. The highly exothermic reaction takes place during methane synthesis through a series of reactors; the SRC, therefore, makes it possible to recover energy with electricity generation. The product stream from the reactors is separated into water in a knockout drum. Cryogenic CO2 separation (Figure ) is introduced to recover unreacted CO2 while producing purified SNG. Mixed refrigerants (CH4 and C3H8) are compressed through a two-staged compression and interstage cooling system, which first exchanges their heat in a cryogenic exchanger (CHX-1) and then delivers cold energy to the SNG after expansion. This reduces the SNG stream temperature up to −59.0 °C, which then charges to the component separator where CO2 is separated from SNG. For the liquefaction of SNG, another refrigeration system (three-staged compression and cooling) is introduced, which utilizes N2, CH4, and C3H8 as mixed refrigerants. This cryogenic system provides dual benefits: it reduces the SNG temperature up to its boiling point (−161 °C) and purifies it further by removing other impurities and unreacted gases. The remaining streams leaving the cryogenic systems are then combined and recuperate their heat before being recycled. Technoeconomic Analysis

The technoeconomic analysis of liquefied SNG production is performed based on the process design and simulation results. The cost estimation of liquefied SNG production is mainly described in this section, and first, capital cost and operational cost are classified. Capital cost is a one-time expenditure for equipment purchase, installation, construction, and acquisition, and operational cost is an ongoing expenditure incurred from the normal day-by-day running of the process. For the capital cost estimation, the purchased equipment cost of each main unit process should be calculated first, and the appropriate equipment costs are estimated on the basis of the equipment scale of the process. Based on the purchased equipment costs estimated for each piece of equipment, the total capital investment is calculated using the method reported by Peters and Timmerhaus. The total capital investment is the sum of fixed capital investment and working capital. Fixed capital investment is the capital required to manufacture and construct plant facilities, divided into direct costs and indirect costs. Direct costs include the equipment installation, piping, instrumentation, etc., while indirect costs consist of construction expenses, legal expenses, contingency, and others. In addition, working capital is expenditure that is necessary for the plant start-up and operation. For the fixed capital investment of water electrolysis, methanation, and liquefaction, economic factors based on purchased equipment cost are covered. Life Cycle Assessment

Life cycle assessment (LCA) is a powerful tool for environmental impact quantification at the whole life cycle stage of a process or service. In this work, LCA aims to investigate the effects on the environment of liquefied SNG production based on the International Organization of Standardization (ISO) guideline ISO 14040/14044. , ISO 14040 describes the general principles and guidelines of LCA, and ISO 14044 covers its detailed recommendations and requirements. According to the guidance, the procedure of LCA involves four steps: goal and scope definition, life cycle inventory analysis, life cycle impact assessment, and life cycle interpretation. The first phase, goal and scope definition, covers the decision of detailed information on LCA work such as goal, system boundary, functional unit, and environmental impact category. To demonstrate its environmental sustainability, the system boundary is selected as cradle-to-gate, and the functional unit is 1 GJ of liquefied SNG production. In addition, the six environmental categories are quantified to identify which phase is the most influential on the total environmental impacts, including climate change, ozone depletion, particulate matter, acidification, ozone formation, and fossil resource scarcity. The next step, life cycle inventory, involves collecting certain input/output data based on the system boundary defined in the first step. The life cycle inventory, including mass and energy flows consumed in green H2 production using SOE, CO2 methanation for SNG production, cryogenic carbon capture for CO2 separation, and SNG liquefaction process, is shown in Tables S9–S13. LCA was performed using SimaPro (version 9.4, PRé Sustainability) with the Ecoinvent database version 3.8 (cutoff by classification). The collected inventory in the second phase is translated into specific environmental impacts based on characterization factors. In this study, the ReCiPe 2016 life cycle impact assessment (LCIA) method was applied at the midpoint level to estimate the environmental impacts. , A midpoint indicator is used to translate the inventory data to a specific environmental impact, e.g., the climate change impact can be obtained based on the climate change characterization factor of global warming potential (GWP). In the last stage, certain results of LCA have to be drawn, and they should be compared with the initial objective decided in the first phase. Moreover, a summary of the results can be provided to provide directions and recommendations for decision-making.

Results and Discussion

Liquefied Synthetic Natural Gas Cost

Cost estimation of liquefied SNG can enable the comparison of economic competitiveness in detail for H2 production, methanation, and methane liquefaction phases. We first calculate the base cost of liquefied SNG with a 60 $ MWh–1 electricity price, and the distribution of cost breakdown is presented in Figure A. The H2 production cost from SOE water electrolysis is estimated to be 7.4 $ kg–1 with the required water electrolysis system scale of 68 MW. For the H2 production cost using SOE, the capital cost of water electrolysis and electricity cost, as expected, present a large portion in the total cost because SOE is still under development, leading to an increase in system cost compared with other electrolyzers. The estimated cost of liquefied SNG is 87.0 $ GJ–1, which has a large proportion of reactant cost (H2) and O&M cost, making up 90% of the cost. Further, the sensitivity analysis is provided with respect to the cost parameters in Figure B. The potential cost variations due to sensitive impact factors can be a significant factor in revealing the chance for SNG cost reduction. Therefore, large fluctuations of more than 10% of the SNG cost are implemented on the interest rate, project lifetime, stream factor, electrolysis system cost, and electricity price. In particular, the biggest variation appears at the electricity price change from 76.0 to 109.0 $ GJ–1, which means that electricity cost has a significant impact on the overall production cost of liquefied SNG. Hence, this reveals that the liquefied SNG cost strongly depends on the electricity prices, which affect operating costs. Utilizing renewable electricity for green H2 production with water electrolysis and methanation processes can provide a great opportunity for carbon reduction compared to conventional natural gas. However, the high dependency on geometry and varying renewable electricity prices by country result in wide cost variations of liquefied SNG. According to the type of renewable energy sources, estimated liquefied SNG costs are presented in Figure C. Given that hydroelectric and onshore wind electricity are integrated, liquefied SNG can be cost-competitive compared with the current renewable SNG cost. However, hydroelectric industries have recently faced issues with rising capital costs and the destruction of forests for the construction of dams. Solar photovoltaics (solar PV) has been further considered as a mature renewable energy source that numerous countries currently focus on. Therefore, on/offshore wind and solar energy will become a large amount of energy generation sources due to their expected high maturity in the future.

Environmental Potential of Liquefied Natural Gas

Environmental impact analysis using LCA is carried out for the liquefied SNG production from a quantitative perspective. The results of representative environmental damages of climate change, ozone depletion, fine particulate matter formation, acidification, ozone formation, and fossil resource scarcity are shown in Figure A in percentages by classifying various phases of green H2 production, methanation, CO2 separation, and SNG liquefaction. For all cases of environmental characterization, water electrolysis presents the largest share of total environmental impact due to the significant consumption of electricity for green H2 production. The individual contributions of each environmental impact category are reported in Table S15. Note that energy consumption is highly associated with electrolyzer efficiency and renewable sources. For the uncertainty based on the electrolyzer efficiency, Monte Carlo simulation was used to quantify the environmental impact in terms of climate change for onshore wind and solar PV electricity, as presented in Figure B. Respective impact ranges are 0.014–0.015 and 0.036–0.040 tonCO2-eq GJ–1 for onshore wind and solar PV, respectively. Due to the high carbon intensity of solar PV, it has a greater possibility of environmental damage than onshore wind. The carbon intensity of renewables and nonrenewables is a well-known factor that makes an impact on the total GHG emissions. Therefore, scenarios derived based on electricity suppliers and electrolyzer efficiency were explored to investigate the variability in CO2 emissions (Figure C). The CO2 emissions of the base case are 0.015 and 0.040 tonCO2-eq GJ–1 for onshore wind and solar PV, respectively. With an increase in electrolyzer efficiency, the CO2 emissions are, understandably, reduced by 8.8% due to the lower energy requirement. Renewable electricity from on/offshore wind and solar PV can give a chance to bring environmental feasibility compared with fossil LNG production (approximately 11-fold). Further, liquefied SNG production coupled with the Canadian grid mix can be lower in CO2 emissions than fossil-based production as well. Therefore, it is true that liquefied SNG production is more environmentally sustainable than fossil LNG production when integrated with renewables. Besides, enhancing electrolyzer efficiency can be a considerable approach to minimizing CO2 emissions.

Economic and Environmental Outlook

The methanation process with water electrolysis technology is still far from being commercialized because it is not profitable compared to fossil LNG production. As presented in Figure A, the green H2 production cost from water electrolysis is a major contributor to the total liquefied SNG cost. On the other hand, the carbon avoidance costs of liquefied SNG can be significantly saved in the future since future electrolyzers and renewables can bring down green H2 cost, leading to a fair cost reduction for liquefied SNG. The respective carbon avoidance costs for climate mitigation are 538.1–1099.9, 302.3–709.9, and 210.4–569.15 $ tonCO2 –1 for the pessimistic, base, and optimistic cases, respectively. Furthermore, onshore wind will become a mature renewable source of the lowest carbon avoidance cost to reach environmental sustainability. The largest cost reduction is represented by solar PV. Therefore, it can be concluded that wind and solar energy have considerable potential for climate mitigation coupled with liquefied SNG production, due to its sustainability and carbon reduction compared to conventional LNG.

Feasibility of Climate Mitigation

The LCA results demonstrate the environmental benefits of liquefied SNG in terms of reduced life cycle emissions, while the technoeconomic analysis evaluates its economic viability, together supporting its potential as a sustainable alternative to fossil-based LNG. The world consumed 39,413 TWh of natural gas in 2022, with the largest consumer being North America (27.9%), followed by the Asia Pacific (23.0%), the Middle East and Africa (18.3%), and other regions (Figure A). The details of countries consuming large amounts of LNG, more than 10% of each region, are presented in Figure A. The USA consumed 8812 TWh of natural gas, with combustion of 1689 TWh for electricity generation. , In addition, Russia and China are also primary consumers of natural gas, consuming 4080 and 3757 TWh, respectively. In order to substitute SNG for fossil-based natural gas, the domestic generation and supply of renewable electricity are of critical necessity for SNG production. To satisfy the natural gas demand for each country, extensive renewable capacity is indispensable, as shown in Figure B. The USA is the prime contributor to global natural gas consumption, but an increase in the renewable energy capacity is the primary mission to accelerate and enable renewable natural gas to be used instead of fossil-based gas (Figure B). If the replacement of all conventional natural gas by renewable SNG is realized in the future, reductions of CO2 emissions can be achieved considering onshore and solar PV integration for water electrolysis for green H2 production, CO2 methanation, and its liquefaction, as shown in Figure C. Conclusions

The integrative potential of liquefied SNG production via CO2 methanation and subsequent cryogenic liquefaction, sourced by solid oxide electrolysis (SOE) and renewable electricity, is systematically evaluated to assess its current feasibility and future scalability to meet decarbonized energy demands. CO2 hydrogenation to SNG is an exothermic process, and thus, two steam Rankine cycles are integrated into the system to recover thermal energy, enabling the generation of approximately 1.97 MW of power. The produced SNG undergoes cryogenic upgrading and liquefaction, requiring 0.072 kWh kgSNG–1 and 0.53 kWh kgSNG–1 for upgrading and liquefaction, respectively. The liquefied SNG maintains industrial-grade purity to ensure compatibility with existing infrastructure for transportation and distribution. A technoeconomic evaluation of the integrated process indicates that the estimated production cost of liquefied SNG is 87.0 $ GJ–1, with H2 production and associated operation and maintenance accounting for nearly 90% of the total cost. Sensitivity analysis confirms that electricity price is a dominant factor influencing cost variability, with production costs ranging from 76.0 to 109.0 $ GJ–1 depending on the electricity rate. Among renewable sources, onshore and offshore wind and solar PV are projected to dominate the energy supply mix due to their expected cost and technological maturity. Environmental assessment results indicate a considerable reduction in life cycle greenhouse gas emissions with improving electrolyzer efficiency, leading to 8.8% lower emissions. Specifically, climate change impacts are quantified as 0.014–0.015 tonCO2-eq GJ–1 for onshore wind and 0.036–0.040 tonCO2-eq GJ–1 for solar PV. Scenario analysis further reveals that the carbon avoidance cost for climate mitigation ranges from 538.1 to 1099.9 $ tonCO2 –1 (pessimistic), from 302.3 to 709.9 $ tonCO2 –1 (base), and from 210.4 to 569.2 $ tonCO2 –1 (optimistic). In conclusion, this study presents a comprehensive technoeconomic and environmental framework for sustainable liquefied SNG production. The integration of SOE-based green H2 production offers a promising pathway to reduce both cost and emissions, particularly in future scenarios of improved electrolyzer performance and renewable electricity supply. When powered by low-carbon sources such as onshore wind and solar PV, liquefied SNG demonstrates strong potential as a viable and environmentally feasible alternative to fossil-based natural gas, offering a strategic direction for future climate mitigation efforts.
