Stroke coordinators perceived roles, responsibilities, & resources: Florida Stroke Registry insights
Authors: Joy Sessa, Lauri Bishop, Carolina M. Gutierrez, Hannah Gardener, Judith Krigman, Christina Ampie, Valynncea Butler, Ashley Bolling, Farya Fakoori, Tara Hylton, Evie Sobczak, Negar Asdaghi, Tatjana Rundek, Jose G. Romano, Gillian Gordon Perue, Florida Stroke Registry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOptimization of stroke care plays a critical role in post-stroke outcomes. The stroke coordinator role includes leadership of stroke care teams, education of staff, patients, and emergency personnel, and stroke care quality improvement initiatives. The current study aimed to characterize Florida stroke coordinator responsibilities, stroke coordinators roles, and resources available and job satisfaction through a statewide hospital survey.MethodsWe report on a 20-item Stroke Coordinator Roles and Responsibility Survey that was administered to stroke coordinators from participating Florida hospitals. Topics included description of stroke center (i.e., size and type); experience of stroke coordinator; roles/responsibilities (data abstraction details, number of sites covered, description of additional duties); resources available; adequacy of training; satisfaction with compensation. We characterized various roles and resources available to coordinators and examined their variations by center type, size, and additionally by years in current position using the Chi square test.ResultsSurveys were deployed to 148 stroke coordinators covering 180 stroke hospitals from August to December 2023. A total of 55 responses (response rate 37 %) were available for analysis from 25 comprehensive/thrombectomy capable and 24 primary stroke centers with some coordinators covering multiple sites. Stroke coordinators who have been in their current role >5 years were more likely to work in large (63 % vs 29 %, p = 0.02), comprehensive (75 % vs 33 % p = 0.03) stroke centers. Coordinators reported spending on average 45 to 60 min per chart for data abstraction, which was unrelated to center type (p = 0.57), size (p = 0.99) or coordinator experience (p = 0.81). The longer the stroke coordinator was in their role (>5 years vs. <5 years) the more likely they had additional duties (>2 additional duties, 69 % vs 49 %, p = 0.04), and less likely to have extra resources (88 % vs 67 % p = 0.05). The majority (61 %) of stroke coordinators indicated dissatisfaction with their training and compensation.ConclusionsStroke coordinators face challenges with chart abstraction workload, fulfilling multiple roles, and limited available resources. Dissatisfaction is present with perceptions of inadequate training and compensation. Organizations should focus on addressing these concerns to stabilize and strengthen the stroke coordinator role, enhancing the quality and performance of the stroke program.

Introduction

Stroke continues to present significant concerns for public health within the United States and beyond (Tsao et al., 2023). Although there are known effective treatments for both ischemic stroke and hemorrhagic stroke, these are time dependent, with patients receiving earlier treatment exhibiting better outcomes(Powers et al., 2019; Asdaghi et al., 2023). To treat stroke effectively, stroke systems of care must be optimized. Optimization of stroke systems of care could reduce stroke-related deaths by 2–3 % annually(Adeoye et al., 2019). Effective stroke systems of care include a broad range of stakeholders. Among them, stroke coordinators are the key members of stroke care teams. The role of the stroke coordinator has been shown to be pivotal in fostering stroke program excellence, is associated with a lower length of stay(Purvis et al., 2018), increased delivery of evidence-based care (Purvis et al., 2018; Cadilhac et al., 2013), improved stroke education compliance(Malfitano et al., 2013), and higher likelihood of a home discharge disposition (Purvis et al., 2018). While the positive impact of stroke coordinators on quality of care has been established, there is a paucity of evidence outlining the current roles, responsibilities, and resources available to stroke coordinators in the stroke system of care.

In the United States, the stroke coordinator primarily focuses on the stroke team operations; program leadership; education of staff, patients, community, and emergency medical service personnel; and the oversight of stroke quality improvement initiatives(Aroor et al., 2024). Typically, qualifications for assuming the stroke coordinator role include licensing as a registered nurse or a physical therapist. Requirements regarding an academic nursing degree vary from one healthcare system to another, with most requiring a bachelor’s or master’s degree as the minimum degree required for the role.

Stroke center levels

In the United States, stroke program certification levels are dictated by certifying bodies, e.g. The Joint Commission or Det Norske Veritas. Although the terms differ slightly across certifying organizations, there are usually four different levels of certification ranging from acute stroke ready to comprehensive stroke centers (DUSENBURY et al., 2023). Acute stroke ready may be a small hospitals or free-standing emergency departments that are capable of doing computerized topography imaging for administering thrombolytic treatments. They may transfer all stroke patients out for inpatient care or may keep a limited number of strokes at the facility, depending on what services are available at the site. Primary stroke centers typically treat with acute thrombolytic therapy and keep all ischemic stroke patients that do not have a large vessel occlusion and may keep small, uncomplicated bleeds. Availability of neurocritical care, neuroendovascular or neurosurgical treatment services varies widely across primary stroke centers and if offering these services, the primary certification level does not carry volume or 24/7 availability requirements. However, most primary stroke centers in Florida are transferring out all stroke patients with acute hemorrhage or who require neuroendovascular services. Thrombectomy-capable stroke centers must meet minimum thrombolytic and thrombectomy case volumes annually but are not required to provide comprehensive neurosurgical services, transferring out complex intracranial hemorrhages. At the comprehensive stroke center certification level, centers must meet volume requirements for thrombolytic, thrombectomy, and aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage treatments on an annual basis. They are expected to provide access to advanced neurocritical care and neurovascular physicians, as well as access to complex neurosurgical care, 24 h a day, 7 days a week. As the level of certification increases from acute stroke ready to comprehensive stroke center, the amount of data monitoring and collection that is required increases substantially to ensure effective program monitoring.

Stroke coordinator roles

As the complexity of stroke treatments that are offered continues to expand, there is an increased need for the stroke coordinator role to also expand in order to complete data abstraction, interpretation, and reporting requirements. While data is an important program component, there are many other key roles that must be managed by the stroke coordinator including ongoing workflow and process improvement initiatives, assisting with interdisciplinary team collaborations, coordinating the various team meetings, and ensuring that the program is running smoothly (Dusenbury, 2023 #719). Community education on stroke risk factors, signs/symptoms, and interfacing with stroke patients and their support systems is often the responsibility of the stroke coordinator. Stroke education sessions for pre-hospital personnel are often coordinated and provided by the stroke coordinator in the absence of a dedicated hospital personnel that liaise with Emergency Medical Service. In certain hospitals, an additional role exists within the stroke program structure, of the stroke navigator. Stroke navigators may be tasked with delivering targeted stroke education directly to patients and facilitating patient transitions across the continuum of care. In instances where a dedicated stroke navigator is not available, stroke coordinators assume responsibility for these functions, alongside their ongoing duties related to routine data collection and reporting of stroke metrics. This arrangement highlights the diverse scope of responsibilities managed by stroke coordinators and underscores their integral role in both quality improvement initiatives and patient engagement within stroke care systems.

Recommendations for adequate program structure, essential roles, and appropriate program resources at the acute stroke-ready, primary, thrombectomy-capable, and comprehensive levels of stroke program certification have been proposed (Dusenbury et al., 2023). The recommendations address program structure by level of certification but offer little in terms of recommendations based on the size or patient volume of specific programs. Both factors are key components that may affect efficiency of stroke coordinators. To date, it is unclear how extensively these existing recommendations have been adopted by stroke programs, so exploring stroke coordinators’ perceptions related to the level of support provided to them is essential to understanding what further guidance may need to be proposed. Descriptions of stroke coordinator workload, actual program resources, data abstraction workload, and perceptions of adequate stroke coordinator role training, job satisfaction and compensation are needed to further guide stroke program staffing and resource recommendations.

The primary aim of this study is to identify the standard tasks of a stroke coordinator, the scope of the stroke coordinator role within a stroke program, and the reported resources or support available within the stroke coordinator role. Additionally, this study examines the average length of training that Florida stroke coordinators are offered to prepare for their role and their current job satisfaction. By matching survey results with volume and certification data, variations between different stroke program sizes and certification levels were analyzed.

Methods

Research design

The Florida Stroke Registry is a state funded program focused on advancing stroke care in Florida by measuring the quality of care, tracking stroke related metrics, benchmarking these measures against statewide and national goals, and then disseminating these results to participating Florida hospitals. The data are used to drive policy, advocate advances in stroke care and to drive quality improvement. In so doing the Florida Stroke Registry has improved stroke outcomes in Florida over the past 13 years (Asdaghi et al., 2016). The details of the Florida Stroke Registry scope and methodology(Sacco et al., 2017) have been published (Marulanda et al., 2023). Florida Stroke Registry maintains Institutional Review Board approval from our local institutional review board (ID 20,120,987; CR00012124).

The Florida Stroke Registry examined the state's stroke infrastructure as part of the quality improvement efforts of the Florida Paul Coverdell Acute Stroke Program in the form of the Hospital Inventory Survey which includes a unique subsection for Stroke Coordinators Roles and responsibilities. Appendix 1 provides a map of all participating hospitals at the time of the survey. Appendix 2, a timeline of the survey development and distribution of the hospital inventory survey particularly the specific questions of the Stroke Coordinator tab. For this study, we report on the stroke coordinator section of the Florida Stroke Registry Hospital Inventory Survey.

The Stroke Coordinator Roles and Responsibilities section included 20 items detailing resources available to stroke coordinators; adequacy of training; satisfaction with compensation the stroke coordinator (i.e., time in role, total experience as a stroke coordinator, data abstraction details, number of sites covered, description of additional duties; see page 7 of Appendix 3 which provides a list of all the FSR hospital inventory Survey questions). The Survey was pilot tested by three independent stroke coordinators who rated the questions on readability, relevance, accessibility, and ease of completion of the survey. After two formal beta testing sessions and feedback, the survey was put into production mode. The Florida Stroke Registry Hospital-Inventory Survey was built in REDCap and included prefilled identification fields and skip-logic to allow ease of completion. For August - December 2023, the Florida Stroke Registry deployed its Hospital-Inventory Survey to 180 stroke hospitals participating in the across the state of Florida. Each Stroke Coordinator or Quality Director received a secure, unique survey link via direct email. In addition to individual email links provided, the study was presented at live events (the Florida Stroke Registry’s annual meeting and a separate stroke coordinator webinar) to enhance recruitment and reduce risk of participation bias. All participants were supported by a Florida Stroke Registry coordinator who provided a telephone call to assist with any technical difficulties in completing the survey. Responses were tabulated by center certification level and size (stroke patient volume/year). The time (in years) stroke coordinator reported being in the role was also used to evaluate role turn-over. Survey responses were coded with unique identifiers and kept in password-protected files on secure servers restricted to access by Florida Stroke Registry key personnel only. Individual hospital identities were blinded in data files used for data analysis. This study follows the STROBE (von Elm et al., 2007) reporting format which can be found in supplemental tables.

Study population/data cleaning

The affiliated stroke coordinator for each of the Florida Stroke Registry 180 participating stroke centers were invited to participate via a unique link. During data cleaning, duplicate responses were removed by selecting the most complete response. Any incomplete or otherwise duplicate response was removed. Surveys were linked to individual hospitals so that a stroke coordinator who covers multiple hospitals can complete a separate survey for each center without these being considered duplicated. Those centers covered by one stroke coordinator are linked in the dataset to allow for ease of completion. The final response rate was assessed as the number of responses divided by the total number of hospitals. Stroke coordinators may cover multiple hospital sites, such that the final denominator for stroke coordinators is 148.

Variables

Primary variables of interest included center type, center size (as measured by volume of stroke discharges over the past year), and stroke coordinator years in current position. Responses to primary variables of interest were aggregated and dichotomized for analysis. Center type was dichotomized into ‘Acute Stroke Ready/Primary’ or ‘Thrombectomy Capable/Comprehensive’ Stroke Centers. Center size was dichotomized into either ‘Small’ (<501 stroke discharges per year) or ‘Large’ Center (>500 stroke discharges per year). These center size categories were based on the scientific statement by DUSENBURY et al. (2023) that recommended additional data abstraction 1.0 full-time equivalent personnel support for stroke patient discharges > 500 patients per year. Due to the low number of survey responses, it was necessary to look at the responses in larger groups to avoid fragmented data. Repeating the study with a larger number of participants would allow for more detailed analysis of other center sizes. The time in current stroke coordinator role was dichotomized into <5 years or >5 years. Additionally, variables surrounding the diverse duties of the stroke coordinator, including the different types of abstractions completed, time required for a single chart abstraction, whether assistance is provided for abstraction, the number of stroke-related duties (i.e. data abstraction, stroke educator, stroke navigator) for which the stroke coordinator is responsible, the number of non-stroke related duties, and whether the stroke coordinator has additional duties unrelated to stroke at the institution (i.e. managing stroke, sepsis, and chest pain certification programs concurrently) were extracted from the survey results. Variables pertaining to the adequacy of available resources, including orientation, available assistance, and compensation provided to the stroke coordinators were also evaluated. Survey responses indicating a neutral response were included in the negative/disagree category for questions pertaining to compensation, orientation, and program resources as several stroke coordinators expressed concerns about retribution for negative responses, even though they were assured of anonymity of responses by the Florida Stroke Registry member administering the survey.

Data analysis

Summary statistics were used to characterize data related to hospital size and stroke center type. Chi Square tests were used to characterize the types and amounts of diverse stroke coordinator roles by center type, by center size, and by years in current position. Chi square analyses were also used to examine the resources available to stroke coordinators by center type, center size, and by years in current role. To further explore the significant relationships identified by chi square analyses, additional logistic regression models were used that adjusted for stroke center size, to evaluate the effects of time in current position 〈 5 years vs 〉 5 years on its impact on additional assigned tasks, adequacy of resources provided, and job satisfaction.

Data availability statement

The data that supports the findings of this study is available upon a written request from the corresponding author and requires approval by the Florida Stroke Registry publications committee. Data from the Florida Stroke Registry Hospital-Inventory Survey will be made available to qualified investigators, who make a formal request to the Florida Stroke Registry at flstrokeregistry@miami.edu.

Stroke coordinators report, on average, 45 to 60 min per chart for data abstraction (66 %), which is independent of center type (p = 0.57), size (p = 0.99) or coordinator experience (p = 0.81). We asked stroke coordinators how many separate databases they abstract for with the following results: 45 % of participants abstract for 4 different databases with 18 % reporting abstraction for 3 different databases, 8 % abstracting for 2 databases, and 12 % abstracting for one database. The majority (61 %) of stroke coordinators indicated dissatisfaction with stroke coordinator role training with 72 % reporting less than a month of stroke coordinator training and 28 % reporting 2–6 months of training, regardless of role longevity or size/complexity of stroke center managed. Factors statistically associated with dissatisfaction in compensation for their role include years of experience in the role (75 % dissatisfied >5 years vs. 59 % <5 years, p = 0.09); hospital size (84 % dissatisfied coordinators from large hospitals vs 55 % from small hospitals p = 0.05); and center type (72 % Comprehensive Stroke Center vs. 50 % Primary Stroke center p = 0.09), though these were not all statistically significant. Discussion

This study shines a spotlight on the realities of the role of the stroke coordinator in the stroke system of care: revealing a heavy burden of data abstraction, simultaneous responsibilities for reporting and abstracting data for 4 different data sets, conducting 100 % abstraction of every stroke case with each abstracting lasting 45–60 min on average. This heavy abstraction burden is exacerbated by increasing demands to fulfill multiple roles (stroke education, leadership, and coordinating for multiple hospitals or multiple certification programs (acute myocardial infarctions, sepsis, orthopedic total joint). These challenges, in the context of limited resources (>50 %) and dissatisfaction with compensation among 60 % of respondents is of concern to the Florida Stroke Registry and may contribute to reduced time in the role of Stroke coordinator with the majority of coordinators (71 %) being in the role for <5 years in Florida.

Several studies discussed the role of the stroke coordinator and their impact on the stroke system of care (Purvis et al., 2021; Purvis et al., 2018) but to our knowledge these studies did not address or include an analysis of process of stroke data abstraction. Data abstraction is a critical part of the stroke system of care, as it collates patient level data into actionable metrics that drive systems of care. These metrics are heavily relied on by the Centers for Medicare Services and Stroke Certification bodies to validate the care provided by stroke centers and ultimately improve performance over time (Madeline Feldmeier et al., 2024; Dusenbury et al., 2023). As previously mentioned, results of our study indicated data abstraction from a single chart requires approximately 45–60 min, and most stroke coordinators abstract data for at least 4 databases. If these numbers are multiplied by the volume of patients per hospital/year, we can better understand the task that data abstraction represents. Some hospitals outsource this role to a third-party vendor, but among participants of the Florida Stroke Registry Hospital-Inventory Survey, 75 % of stroke coordinators report no additional personnel or support for this critical task. The abstraction burden for stroke coordinators is therefore substantial, regardless of hospital size or designation. This uniform expectation of 100 % data abstraction by the stroke coordinator does not account for the increased chart complexity at comprehensive stroke centers, which often handle more acute, comorbid, and procedural cases. In addition to abstraction duties, 69 % of coordinators reported managing more than two other roles, ranging from oversight of non-clinical quality metrics to direct clinical support. These additional responsibilities, layered atop an already demanding data burden, highlight a disconnect between the scope of the role and the structural support provided.

Performing the multifocal tasks of the stroke coordinator role requires definition of adequate resources, staffing, and program structure. Clear delineation of the necessary roles and the resources required to adequately perform complex stroke program management will allow healthcare leaders to make appropriate recommendations for optimal stroke program staffing and resourcing. Support needs for the stroke coordinator role may vary across entities due to differences in patient volume, acuity, certification level, and other factors, but the current literature offers little guidance in terms of adequate program support based on size and complexity of the stroke programs.

In addition to the task of data abstraction, nearly half of coordinators (45 %) fulfill multiple other tasks; some cover other clinical duties, others provide coordination services for other unrelated programs or focus only on stroke but do so for multiple hospitals. The reasons that drive these decisions to have stroke coordinators pursue multiple tasks cannot be delineated from this survey. A recent review of Stroke Coordinator role in Australia (Tara Purvis et al., 2022) also demonstrated similar findings, including the perception that stroke coordinators have too many responsibilities, insufficient time to perform their role and limited staff allocation. They suggest that a lack of awareness about the details of the stroke coordinator job function at the executive/managerial level and limited funding to support their role are the major barriers to fulfilling these tasks.

Consistent with the literature, our study demonstrates a low satisfaction of stroke coordinators with their compensation, particularly for stroke coordinators at larger centers or those who have been in the role for greater than 5 years. Most stroke coordinators in this survey were in the role for < 5 years suggesting that compensation may be related to lower rates of longevity in the role.

Longevity in the stroke coordinator role also emerged as a point of concern. The majority of respondents had been in their position for fewer than five years, suggesting high turnover or relatively recent role development in many institutions. While some larger centers have introduced support roles such as stroke navigators to assist with patient-facing and transitional care responsibilities, this resource is not consistently available across all institutions. Compounding these challenges is the reported lack of adequate orientation or structured onboarding, with most coordinators noting insufficient preparation for the complexity and breadth of their duties. Given the multifaceted nature of the stroke coordinator role—which spans clinical coordination, data abstraction, regulatory compliance, quality improvement—robust onboarding and continued professional development should be prioritized to promote role sustainability and reduce burnout.

Stroke coordinators also expressed concern about inadequate preparation for a very complex role, with 72 % reporting a month or less of formal training. Reduced Coordinator time in the role, whatever the cause, leads to instability within the stroke program and a loss of consistent quality improvement work. When there are gaps in stroke coordinator coverage, information may not get adequately communicated to the incoming new coordinator, requiring relearning or redoing work—an inefficient practice.

Florida hospitals are high performing centers with improvements in overall defect free care (9 % in 2010 to 88 % in 2023, increasing percentages of all eligible stroke patients treated within 45 min of arrival (6 % in 2010 vs 84 % in 2023), and increasing volumes of mechanical thrombectomies performed (2 % in 2010 vs 14 % 2023)(Marulanda et al., 2023). These outstanding stroke systems of care metrics maybe at risk if the current job satisfaction concerns among stroke coordinators are not addressed.

The strength of the Florida Stroke Registry Hospital Inventory survey includes the ability to analyze stroke coordinator survey responses in relation to hospital size and stroke certification level, providing an additional layer of data and providing context to these results. Limitations include limited generalizability beyond the State of Florida and a low response rate (37 %) among stroke coordinators, but this is comparable to other stroke coordinator studies reported in the literature (Purvis et al., 2018).

Another important limitation of our study is the lack of granular data regarding the time stroke coordinators dedicate to education and leadership responsibilities, as well as insufficient information about whether coordinators serve in part-time or full-time capacities. Replicating this study with a larger, more diverse group of stroke centers while simultaneously including these additional details in the hospital inventory survey may yield additional insights. Further research is warranted to better assess the necessary levels of appropriate stroke program support.

Conclusions

Our data reveals existing challenges within the stroke coordinator role: abstraction workload, fulfilling multiple roles, sub-optimal training for the role, and dissatisfaction with compensation, especially given the highly specialized complexity of the role. This presents an opportunity for hospital leadership and healthcare regulators and policymakers to address this in a more systematic manner. Ideally, coordinator compensation and additional staff support for data abstraction and stroke education routine tasks could be determined based on stroke center size/patient volume, the level of stroke certification, and number of additional stroke coordinator responsibilities.
