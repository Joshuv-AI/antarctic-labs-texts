Segmental Snare Traction Technique for Helix‐Fixed Leadless Pacemaker Implantation Across a Repaired Tricuspid Annulus
Authors: Yuya Nakamura, Yoshikazu Suzuki, Yumi Aiyama, Kanji Miki, Erina Kunimoto, Toshiro Shinke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHelix‐fixed leadless pacemaker delivery can be challenging after tricuspid annuloplasty with a rigid ring. An 80‐year‐old woman with prior mitral valve repair, tricuspid annuloplasty (26‐mm MC ring), and Maze III surgery presented with syncope due to atrial fibrillation with a slow ventricular response. Standard maneuvers failed to advance the Aveir VR delivery system across the repaired annulus, and simple distal snare traction crossed the ring but could not secure a stable septal trajectory. A three‐step segmental snare traction technique enabled controlled annular crossing and inferior septal fixation with preserved tricuspid valve function at 1 year.Keywords: atrial fibrillation, helix‐fixed system, leadless pacemaker, maze surgery, snare technique, syncope, tricuspid annuloplasty

Introduction

Leadless right ventricular (RV) pacemakers avoid device pockets and transvenous leads and reduce related complications compared with conventional systems [1]. After tricuspid valve surgery, however, prostheses or annuloplasty rings can restrict the catheter angle and narrow the functional orifice, making leadless pacemaker (LP) delivery difficult. Snare‐assisted implantation has been reported as a bailout strategy in patients with enlarged right atria or unfavorable venous anatomy [2]. More recently, we described the Segmental Snare Traction Technique (SSTT), a three‐step snaring approach for helix‐fixed LP systems in complex right hearts [3]. Here we report a case of Aveir VR LP implantation across a rigid tricuspid annuloplasty ring after mitral valve repair and Maze III surgery, in which SSTT enabled inferior septal fixation after failed standard maneuvers and distal snare traction.

Case Report

An 80‐year‐old woman with permanent atrial fibrillation presented with recurrent syncope and a fall. Ten years earlier, she had undergone mitral valve repair, tricuspid annuloplasty with a 26‐mm rigid MC ring, and Maze III surgery. On admission, the surface electrocardiogram showed atrial fibrillation with a slow ventricular response. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function without significant tricuspid regurgitation. Because the patient wished to avoid a device pocket and transvenous leads, a helix‐fixed LP system (Aveir VR; Abbott) was selected. The device was introduced via a 27‐Fr femoral venous sheath using the manufacturer's delivery catheter and valve bypass tool. A 35‐mm single‐loop snare was prepared in advance as a bailout device. Standard full‐flexion maneuvers with gentle forward advancement were repeated but failed each time to traverse the repaired tricuspid annulus, and the catheter tended to align toward the RV free wall. To minimize stress on the rigid ring, more forceful manipulation was avoided. We next attempted a simple distal snare traction maneuver. The snare loop was tightened around the LP tip to increase catheter curvature and push the device toward the valve. Although this maneuver allowed the system to cross the tricuspid annulus, the trajectory still favored the free wall, and a stable septal path could not be obtained. SSTT was then applied. The snare catheter was advanced alongside the delivery system, and distal traction on the LP tip created a tighter, more controllable curve that allowed the assembly to ride along the atrial edge of the annuloplasty ring and enter the RV cavity (Figure 1A). The snare was subsequently repositioned to the midshaft of the catheter. Traction at this segment elongated the catheter, improved forward support, and allowed controlled advancement while avoiding a free‐wall trajectory (Figure 1B). Finally, proximal traction near the docking cap gently rotated the entire system toward the inferior RV septum, resulting in stable contact with the inferior septal myocardium and permitting standard stability testing and helical fixation (Figure 1C). At this point, contrast injection through the protective sleeve under biplane fluoroscopy (anteroposterior and left anterior oblique views) confirmed fluoroscopic findings consistent with septal positioning. Throughout the procedure, biplane fluoroscopy was used to monitor docking integrity, snare position, and interaction with the annuloplasty ring and tricuspid leaflets and to avoid excessive tension on the repaired valve (Supplementary Video 1). Electrical parameters at implantation were satisfactory, with a capture threshold of 0.25 V at 0.4 ms, R‐wave amplitude of 10.5 mV, and lead impedance of 740 Ω. There were no vascular complications or pericardial effusion. Pre‐discharge device interrogation and transthoracic echocardiography showed stable electrical parameters, preserved left ventricular function, no new tricuspid regurgitation, and no evidence of device‐valve interference. The patient was discharged on day 3. At 1‐year follow‐up, she remained free from syncope and heart failure symptoms. Pacing parameters were stable, left ventricular function was preserved, and there was no evidence of device‐related tricuspid valve dysfunction.

Discussion

This case shows that a simple snare maneuver can be insufficient in the presence of a rigid tricuspid annuloplasty ring, whereas a structured, segmental approach can provide a safe septal trajectory for helix‐fixed LP implantation. Standard delivery maneuvers failed to cross the repaired annulus, and simple distal snare traction, although helpful for annular crossing, still favored a free‐wall trajectory and did not yield a stable septal path. In contrast, SSTT, by assigning distinct roles to distal, midshaft, and proximal traction, allowed controlled annular crossing and redirection of the system toward the inferior RV septum (Figure 2). Snare‐assisted LP implantation has been reported in patients with markedly abnormal right atrial or venous anatomy, but reports remain limited for Aveir implantation across a surgically repaired tricuspid annulus. Our initial report of SSTT formalized a three‐segment snaring strategy for helix‐fixed systems, and the present case extends this approach to a surgically repaired tricuspid annulus supported by a rigid ring. In this setting, excessive force or uncontrolled catheter deflection may increase the risk of annular or leaflet injury, so stepwise adjustment of curvature, support, and orientation under careful fluoroscopic guidance is essential. When standard maneuvers and simple snare traction do not provide a stable septal trajectory in patients with prior tricuspid surgery or markedly distorted right heart geometry, SSTT may be a useful option. Although this report describes a single case, the mechanical principle of segmental traction, which sequentially modifies catheter curvature, support, and orientation, may be applicable to other anatomies in which the delivery system repeatedly aligns toward the RV free wall after annular crossing. Although long‐term data on helix‐fixed LPs implanted across repaired tricuspid annuli are limited, potential concerns include chronic device–valve interaction, progression of tricuspid regurgitation, or device instability related to altered catheter trajectory. The favorable one‐year clinical, electrical, and echocardiographic outcomes in this case support the feasibility of this strategy when performed with meticulous technique.

Conclusion

In a patient with prior mitral valve repair, tricuspid annuloplasty with a rigid ring, Maze III surgery, standard maneuvers, and simple distal snare traction did not provide a stable septal path for helix‐fixed LP implantation across the repaired tricuspid annulus and tended to direct the system toward the RV free wall. Application of the three‐step SSTT allowed controlled annular crossing and stable helical fixation at the inferior RV septum while preserving tricuspid valve function at one year. SSTT is a practical bailout option for helix‐fixed leadless pacing in surgically modified tricuspid valves.

Limitations

Escalation may be considered when standard advancement would require excessive forward pressure on the annuloplasty ring, proceeding stepwise from simple distal snare traction to SSTT. Because these strategies rely on snare traction, careful fluoroscopic monitoring of the protective sleeve and docking interface is essential. Although the standard release sequence was completed without difficulty in this case, snare traction could theoretically compromise docking integrity or impede normal release. Longer‐term follow‐up is needed to better define late device–valve interaction after implantation across a repaired tricuspid annulus.
