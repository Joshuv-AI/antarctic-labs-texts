Design, Synthesis and Evaluation of Anticancer Potential of Novel Pyrrolopyrazines Against Glioblastoma: Mechanistic Details and In Vivo Safety Analysis
Authors: Ayşe Usta, Burak Kuzu, Meltem Tan‐Uygun, Veysel Yüksek, M. Edip Akyol, Nurettin Menges
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA protocol through propargyl moiety on pyrrole ring cyclization with ammonium acetate was demonstrated. With this protocol, 11 pyrrolo[1,2‐a]pyrazine derivatives were synthesized, and their antiproliferative activity against the U87MG glioblastoma cell line was evaluated. Among the synthesized compounds, a naphthyl‐substituted derivative (3g) exhibited the most potent in vitro activity with an IC50 value of 60 µM, and showed no antiproliferative effect against human dermal fibroblast (HDF) cells up to 100 μM. Gene expression analysis of U87MG cells treated with 3g revealed a significant upregulation of key genes involved in apoptosis (CASP8, CASP9, CASP3, BAX, BCL2), autophagy (ATG3, ATG5), necroptosis (RIPK1), and DNA damage repair (PARP1) suggesting a multifaceted cell death mechanism. Furthermore, the in vivo safety profile of compound 3g was assessed in an animal model by evaluating renal (BUN, creatinine) and hepatic (AST, ALT, ALP) biochemical parameters. No significant adverse effects on renal function were observed across the tested doses (IC50, IC50 × 2, and IC50 × 3). While AST and ALT levels remained stable, ALP levels showed a dose‐dependent modulation. These findings highlight the potential of 3g as a promising anticancer agent for glioblastoma, warranting further investigation into its detailed mechanisms of action and long‐term safety.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GB) is the most common and most aggressive form of primary brain tumor in adults and contributes to a high social and medical burden due to its incurable nature and significant neurological morbidity [1]. It represents approximately 57% of all gliomas and 48% of all primary central nervous system cancers [2]. Patients with GB typically present with symptoms such as confusion, memory loss, focal or progressive neurological issues, personality changes, headaches, or seizures. These symptoms often appear and progress rapidly in many cases [3, 4]. The presence of certain biomarkers expressed in GB can provide prognostic and diagnostic potential [5].

Despite ongoing research, there has not been an improvement in survival in GB [1]. The current standard of care for GB patients includes surgical resection, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy. The primary drug used in the treatment of GB is the chemotherapeutic agent temozolomide [6, 7]. Although TMZ continues to be the primary chemotherapeutic agent for GB, approximately half of the patients develop resistance to treatment [8].

The nitrosourea derivative, carmustine, another alkylating agent, has been used both at the initial diagnosis of glioma and at tumor recurrence, administered either intravenously or via wafer implantation [9]. It is placed at the site of surgical debulking, and the drug is slowly released [10, 11]. However, several complications have been reported with the use of carmustine wafers, including infection, local edema [12, 13, 14], and the development of symptomatic cysts, which have resulted in significant morbidity [13, 15, 16]. GB, such as all solid tumors, requires tumor angiogenesis for sustained growth and progression [17, 18], which revealed that bevacizumab inhibits VEGF‐mediated endothelial cell proliferation and angiogenesis. When considering the initiation of bevacizumab in patients with recurrent GB, it is crucial to balance the potential survival benefits with the risk of severe side effects [19]. These limitations provide a significant obstacle to achieving effective cancer treatment.

Nitrogen‐containing heterocyclic compounds have shown many promising biological activities [20, 21], and our research group was reported some of them [22, 23, 24, 25]. One of the ring members for fused heterocyclic compounds is pyrrole, which is commonly found in natural products and pharmaceutical compounds due to its significant biological activities and different applications [26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Among the fused nitrogen‐containing rings, pyrrolo[1,2‐a]pyrazine has been used as a core scaffold due to its broad pharmacological function range [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. Structural modifications of pyrrolo[1,2‐a]pyrazines have led to the development of clinically used drugs for the treatment of lung [38], prostate [39, 40], breast cancer [41], tuberculosis [42], glycogen synthase kinase‐3β (GSK‐3) [43], and protein kinase B1 inhibitors [44], its antiproliferative effects against U87 and CML cell lines [45].

We previously published a series of 1,3‐disubstituted pyrrolo[1,2‐a]pyrazine compounds that had significant antiproliferative action against the T98G glioma cell line [46]. The structure–activity relationship (SAR) investigation of these compounds demonstrated that the kind and position of substituents had a considerable impact on biological activity. Among the investigated analogues, the cyclohexyl‐substituted derivative had the most antiproliferative impact, lowering cell viability by 31.3%. In addition, 2‐naphthyl‐substituted derivative also showed a good antiproliferative effect decreasing the cell viability down to 42%. This increased activity might be due to their bulky group in the same position, which might highlight the log P value. Building on our earlier discoveries, which showed considerable antiproliferative action in cyclohexyl and 2‐naphthyl‐substituted derivatives, the current work intends to synthesize new pyrrolo[1,2‐a]pyrazine analogues with comparable alkyl, bulky, and some electronic substituents at the same location. These molecules will be tested for anticancer efficacy, followed by gene expression analysis to determine probable underlying molecular pathways. Furthermore, in vivo investigations focusing on the liver and kidney were undertaken to achieve a comprehensive evaluation of the biological behavior and safety profile of the most promising candidate under physiological conditions. The liver constitutes the principal site of drug metabolism and biotransformation, where enzymatic systems critically regulate metabolic fate, bioavailability, and the generation of potentially reactive intermediates. Concurrently, the kidney plays a fundamental role in drug elimination and systemic clearance and is particularly vulnerable to toxicity arising from compound accumulation. In this context, hepatic function was evaluated through the determination of serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP), which serve as established indicators of hepatocellular integrity and biliary function. Renal function was assessed by measuring blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (CREA) levels, widely recognized markers of glomerular filtration and renal clearance [47, 48]. Collectively, these biochemical parameters offer sensitive and mechanistically informative insights into organ‐specific responses, potential adverse effects, and overall systemic safety, thereby substantiating the rationale and in vivo relevance of the investigations presented in this study.

Results and Discussion

Chemistry

2‐Acylpyrrole derivatives (1a–k) were commercially obtained and utilized without additional purification. The N‐propargylated‐2‐acylpyrroles (2a–k) were produced by alkylating 1a–k with propargyl bromide in the presence of potassium carbonate (K₂CO₃) at room temperature. Compounds 2a–k were refluxed with cesium carbonate in isopropanol for 30 min before proceeding with the cyclization procedure. TLC confirmed intermediate allene production; thus, ammonium acetate (NH₄OAc) was added to the reaction mixture. The final pyrrolo[1,2‐a]pyrazine derivatives (3a–k) were produced in good to exceptional yields, ranging from 33% to 98% (Scheme 1).

Synthesis of pyrrolo[1,2‐a]pyrazines 3a–k.

A proposed mechanism for the cyclization is shown in Scheme 2. In the first step, the propargyl unit in 2a–k is converted into an allene using cesium carbonate as the base. In the second step, the nucleophilic nitrogen atom of NH₃ (generated in situ from NH₄OAc) attacks the carbonyl group of 4a–k to form the imine intermediate 5a–k. The subsequent attack of this imine nitrogen atom on the central carbon atom of the allene unit facilitates the formation of the heterocyclic ring. Finally, a 1,3‐H shift affords the desired products 3a–k.

Biological Results

GB U87MG Cell Line

Compounds 3a, 3b, 3c, 3e, 3h, 3i, 3j, and 3k showed no appreciable antiproliferative (p < 0.05) action against U87 GB cells, with IC₅₀ values over 100 µM. Even at the highest tested dose (100 µM), these chemicals did not impede 50% of cell viability. Although these derivatives showed a dose‐dependent decrease in cell viability, the investigation focused on three drugs with significantly lower IC50 values. Figure 1 shows that there is not promising antiproliferative potential with low micromolar range.

MTT assay results of candidate molecules applied to the U87MG cell line at 72 h, showing the cytotoxicity profiles of molecules 3a, 3b, 3c, 3e, 3h, 3i, 3j, and 3k. All data were expressed as mean ± SD. Different letters (a–d) on the columns show a statistical difference (p < 0.05).

Overall, among all candidate molecules, compounds 3d, 3f, and 3g exhibited statistically significant cytotoxic effects at the lowest tested concentrations compared with the control group (p < 0.05) in the U87MG GB cell line. Among these compounds, 3g displayed the lowest IC50 value, indicating the highest cytotoxic potency within the series. Consistent with these findings, MTT assay results identified compound 3g as the most effective molecule at the lowest concentration, supporting its selection for subsequent in vitro and in vivo applications. To evaluate the cytotoxic selectivity of the compounds, their effects were additionally assessed in a normal human dermal fibroblast (HDF) cell line. Statistical analysis demonstrated that compound 3f induced a significant decrease in cell viability at concentrations of 60 and 75 μM compared with the control group (p < 0.05). However, the extent of cytotoxicity observed for compound 3f in HDF cells was markedly lower than that detected in the U87MG GB cells, suggesting a reduced sensitivity of normal cells. In contrast, compounds 3d and 3g did not exhibit statistically significant cytotoxic effects on HDF cells at any tested concentration (p > 0.05). Overall, these results indicate that compound 3g displays selective cytotoxic activity against GB cells while showing no toxicity toward normal human cells up to 100 μM. A SAR evaluation of the synthesized pyrrolopyrazine derivatives (3a–k) highlights the influence of steric and electronic properties on biological activity. Derivatives bearing small to moderately sized alkyl substituents (3a–c) exhibited weak activity, suggesting that simple hydrophobic groups do not substantially contribute to target interaction. Interestingly, the cyclopropyl‐substituted compound 3d displayed an improved antiproliferative effect (IC50 = 100 µM) compared with other aliphatic analogues. This suggests that the cyclopropyl group, with its unique balance of compact size, conformational rigidity, and electron‐donating character through σ‐hyperconjugation, may contribute positively to molecular recognition and binding affinity [49]. Compound 3g, incorporating a naphthyl moiety, exhibited the strongest activity (IC50 = 60 µM), highlighting the beneficial role of extended aromatic systems in enhancing target engagement, potentially through π–π stacking or hydrophobic interactions. On the other hand, derivatives bearing CF₃ groups (3h and 3j) showed reduced activity, indicating that strong electron‐withdrawing substituents may not favorably impact binding. Compounds with methoxy groups (3i and 3k) demonstrated a moderate antiproliferative effect, suggesting a limited but potentially stabilizing contribution from electron‐donating groups. Overall, the data indicate that conformationally constrained substituents such as cyclopropyl and extended aromatic frameworks significantly influence biological activity within this scaffold, whereas electronic effects alone are not sufficient to drive potent inhibition (Figure 3).

Antiproliferative effects of selected compounds against U87MG and HDF cell lines.

With all antiproliferative attempts, molecule 3g, with an IC50 value of 60 µM, was chosen as the leading contender for further investigations. Hence, because of its strong antiproliferative effect, 3g was selected for both gene expression analysis and in vivo studies.

Gene Expression Studies With Selected Compound

In the present study, gene expression analyses were performed exclusively using the U87MG GB cell line. While gene expression analyses were restricted to tumor cells, the evaluation of safety and systemic toxicity was addressed through complementary in vivo experiments. Specifically, renal and hepatic toxicity assessments were conducted in healthy experimental rats, providing supportive evidence regarding the biocompatibility and safety profile of the selected compound. This integrated in vitro–in vivo approach enabled the investigation of anticancer‐related molecular responses in glioblastoma cells while simultaneously assessing potential off‐target toxicity in normal cells and tissues.

To investigate the potential regulatory effects of the newly synthesized molecule 3g on cell death mechanisms, we evaluated the mRNA expression levels of key genes involved in apoptosis, autophagy, and necroptosis pathways. The expression profiles were assessed in both control and treatment groups (N = 6 per group), and statistically significant differences were observed across multiple targets (Figure 4).

The mRNA transcript levels of apototic, autophagic, and RIPK1 and PARP1 in the U87MG cell line at the 48th h. All data were expressed as mean ± SD. Significant differences between control and treatment samples are indicated with an “*” (p < 0.05). SD, standard deviation.

A substantial upregulation of CASP8 and CASP9 was detected in the treatment group (4.51 ± 1.36 and 5.98 ± 2.74‐fold increase, respectively), indicating activation of both extrinsic and intrinsic apoptotic pathways. Similarly, CASP3 expression was significantly elevated (2.66 ± 1.15‐fold; p = 0.012), supporting the execution of apoptosis following initiator caspase activation.

It is well‐known that expressions of the BCL2 family genes reveal a dual regulatory pattern. While anti‐apoptotic BCL2 was upregulated (5.40 ± 2.59‐fold), the pro‐apoptotic BAX gene showed a markedly higher increase (7.05 ± 3.47‐fold). Consequently, the BAX/BCL2 ratio, a critical determinant of mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), was significantly elevated (1.30 ± 0.03 vs. 1.00 ± 0.02 in controls), strongly indicating a shift towards apoptotic signaling.

Autophagy‐related markers ATG3 and ATG5 also demonstrated a significant induction in the treatment group (3.14 ± 1.36 and 3.39 ± 1.56‐fold, respectively), suggesting a potential activation of autophagic processes, which may act in coordination or competition with apoptosis under the influence of compound 3g.

In addition to those data, RIPK1, a central mediator of necroptosis, showed a significant increase (5.80 ± 2.80‐fold; p = 0.002), implying possible engagement of programmed necrotic pathways. On the other hand, the increased expression of PARP1 (5.12 ± 2.37‐fold), which is a key player in DNA repair and cell death decision‐making, supports a state of heightened cellular stress and may contribute to parthanatos‐like cell death under excessive activation.

These findings collectively suggest that compound 3g induces a multifaceted cell death response involving apoptosis, autophagy, and necroptosis cell deaths. The concurrent upregulation of both initiator and effector caspases, increased BAX/BCL2 ratio, and activation of autophagic and necroptotic markers indicates that the compound 3g exerts a broad pro‐death effect, possibly through mitochondrial dysfunction and intracellular stress pathways. Such a profile supports the compound's potential as an antiproliferative or anticancer agent, consistent with reports that structurally related heterocycles, including fused pyridazine and pyrrole derivatives, can modulate cell death in various malignancies [50].

Further mechanistic studies are warranted to dissect the interplay between these pathways and to determine whether compound 3g preferentially triggers apoptosis, autophagy‐dependent cell death, or a necroptotic phenotype in specific cellular contexts.

Evaluation of Biochemical Parameters for Pharmacokinetic Studies

We sought to determine the potential in vivo impact of molecule 3g on renal function in an animal model by meticulously analyzing key biochemical markers, specifically BUN and creatinine. These parameters are well‐established indicators reflecting the glomerular filtration rate and the overall health status of the kidneys. Furthermore, the BUN/creatinine ratio serves as a clinically relevant index in the differential diagnosis of various forms of azotemia, including prerenal, renal, and postrenal etiologies [51]. Our findings revealed that the administration of the molecule 3g across a range of concentrations, including the IC50 dose, a two‐fold increase, and a three‐fold increase, did not elicit statistically significant alterations in BUN or creatinine levels when compared with the control group (Figure 5). Similarly, the BUN/creatinine ratios remained statistically comparable across all treatment groups. These results suggest that within the timeframe of this study, the synthesized molecule did not induce any discernible acute or subacute impairment of the glomerular filtration capacity, a critical aspect of renal function. This absence of significant nephrotoxic effects is particularly noteworthy, especially considering that certain heterocyclic compounds reported in the literature have been associated with renal toxicity [52, 53, 54]. The observed stability in BUN and creatinine levels, along with the unchanged BUN/creatinine ratio, implies that the molecule 3g did not provoke conditions leading to elevated nitrogenous waste products in the blood due to dehydration, direct kidney damage, or urinary tract obstruction. This preliminary indication of a favorable renal safety profile for the molecule 3g is encouraging for its potential therapeutic development. However, we acknowledge the limitations of our study, primarily its acute‐to‐subacute timeframe.

The in vivo effects of the molecule 3g on hepatic function were evaluated in an animal model by analyzing key biochemical markers. AST levels remained at 119.00 ± 40.74 U/L in the control group and showed no statistically significant changes across the IC50 dose group (84.16 ± 13.57 U/L), the two‐fold dose group (100.00 ± 31.06 U/L), and the three‐fold dose group (108.33 ± 27.89 U/L) (Figure 6). Similarly, ALT levels, which were 52.33 ± 16.66 U/L in the control group, did not exhibit significant alterations in the IC50 dose group (37.50 ± 4.96 U/L), the two‐fold dose group (47.83 ± 20.74 U/L), or the three‐fold dose group (44.33 ± 11.92 U/L). These stable AST and ALT values across all treatment groups suggest that the molecule did not induce significant hepatocellular damage within the tested dose range.

Liver function test results obtained from study groups. All data were expressed as mean ± SD. Different letters (a–b) show a statistical difference (p < 0.05). SD, standard deviation.

However, ALP levels, an indicator of biliary function, showed a more complex response. Compared with the control group's ALP level of 341.50 ± 80.90 U/L, the IC50 dose group exhibited a statistically significant decrease to 281.50 ± 70.50 U/L. The two‐fold dose group showed a numerical but non‐significant increase to 374.16 ± 149.68 U/L. Notably, the three‐fold dose group demonstrated a statistically significant increase in ALP to 456.50 ± 94.55 U/L. This dose‐dependent modulation of ALP, with a decrease at the lower concentration and an increase at the higher concentration, warrants further investigation into the molecule's potential interactions with the biliary system, as altered ALP levels can indicate various conditions affecting bile flow and liver health [55].

This chemical has a positive hepatic safety profile at IC50 and IC50 × 2 dosages; however, the considerable increase in ALP levels shown at the IC50 × 3 dose shows that larger exposures may have detrimental effects on the hepatobiliary system. These data suggest that structural optimization or dose‐limiting methods may be required to reduce these effects. While the chemical shows promise as a therapeutic candidate, careful consideration of dose‐dependent toxicity and target organ safety is required before further development.

Conclusion

We have concluded our previous cyclization protocol for 11 different pyrrolo[1,2‐a]pyrazines derivatives. Different substituents, including electronic and steric effects, were considered. Initial in vitro screening against the U87MG glioblastoma cell line identified compound 3g, bearing a naphthyl group, as exhibiting the most potent antiproliferative activity with an IC50 value of 60 µM. Subsequent gene expression studies with 3g revealed a multifaceted cell death response, characterized by the upregulation of both intrinsic (CASP9, BAX) and extrinsic (CASP8) apoptotic pathways, activation of the executioner CASP3, an increased BAX/BCL2 ratio, and the induction of autophagy‐related genes (ATG3, ATG5). Furthermore, the upregulation of necroptosis (RIPK1) and parthanatos (PARP1) markers suggested the engagement of multiple programmed cell death mechanisms. In vivo evaluation of this hit compound in an animal model, through the assessment of renal (BUN, creatinine) and hepatic (AST, ALT, ALP) biochemical parameters, indicated a promising safety profile at the tested doses, with no significant adverse effects on renal function. While hepatocellular integrity was maintained, a dose‐dependent modulation of ALP levels suggested a potential interaction with the biliary system at higher concentrations. These integrated findings underscore the potential of naphthyl‐substituted pyrrolopyrazine 3g as a promising anticancer agent, warranting further in‐depth mechanistic studies to fully elucidate its therapeutic potential and long‐term safety profile, alongside exploration of its efficacy in more complex in vivo models and eventual translation towards clinical applications.

Chemistry

General Remarks

All reagents were used as purchased from commercial suppliers without further purification. Proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectra (1H NMR) were recorded on an instrument 400 MHz, and chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (ppm) downfield from TMS, using CDCl3 as an internal standard. Proton‐decoupled carbon nuclear magnetic resonance spectra (13C NMR) were recorded on an instrument 100 MHz. Column chromatography was performed on silica gel (60‐mesh). TLC was carried out on 0.2 mm silica gel 60 F254 analytical aluminum plates. High‐resolution mass spectra were recorded on a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive mass spectrometer using the ESI method. High‐performance liquid chromatography analyses were performed using an Agilent 1100 system. Melting points were measured using a melting point apparatus. All spectroscopic analyses were conducted at the Science Research and Application Center of Van Yuzuncu Yil University.

General Procedure for the Preparation of Compounds 2a–k

To a stirred mixture of 2‐acylpyrroles 1a–k (1 mmol) in 10 cm3 DMF was added K2CO3 (2 mmol) at 0°C. The reaction mixture was stirred at 0°C for 20 min, and then propargyl bromide (1.4 mmol) in 5 cm3 DMF was added to the reaction flask dropwise over a period of 10 min. After stirring for 16 h at room temperature, the resulting mixture was diluted with 20 cm3 water and extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 20 cm3). The combined extracts were washed with brine (4 × 10 cm3), dried with MgSO4, and filtered. The solvent was evaporated, and the crude product was purified over silica gel eluting with hexane/EtOAc (5:1) to give 2a–k [46].

General Procedure for the Synthesis of the Compounds 3a–k

To a stirred mixture of N‐propargylated‐2‐acylpyrroles 2a–k (1 mmol) in 20 cm3 isopropanol was added Cs2CO3 (1 mmol) at room temperature. The reaction mixture was then heated at reflux for 0.5 h. Ammonium acetate (30 mmol) was added to the reaction flask and refluxed continued for 96 h, the resulting mixture was evaporated, and the crude product was diluted with 20 cm3 water and extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 20 cm3). The combined extracts were dried with MgSO4 and filtered. The solvent was evaporated, and the crude product was purified over silica gel eluting with hexane/EtOAc (5:1) to give 3a–k [46].

Biology

Cell Culture

This study was performed at the Cell Culture and Molecular Biology Laboratory within the Biochemistry Department of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine at Van Yuzuncu Yil University. Human Glioblastoma epithelial brain cells, U87MG (ATCC HTB‐14) and non‐cancerous (healthy) HDF cells (Human Dermal Fibroblasts, Atcc Pcs‐201‐012) from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, USA), were used as the material in the study. The U87MG cells were reproduced in in vitro conditions through regular passages inside the DMEM High Glucose (Gibco, DMEM; New York, USA) growth medium with 10% FBS (Capricorn, FBS‐11A; Ebsdorfergrund, Germany), 1% L‐Glutamine (Capricorn, GLN B; Ebsdorfergrund, Germany), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Capricorn, PS‐B; Ebsdorfergrund, Germany), 5% CO2, and 95% moisture by incubation (Esco CelCulture CO2; Changi, Singapore) at 37°C.

Preparation of Molecular Solutions for Application in Cell Culture

Stock solutions of the synthesized compounds were prepared in DMSO at a concentration of 0.02 M. Final working concentrations were achieved by dilution with cell culture medium, ensuring that the DMSO content remained below the non‐toxic threshold (< 0.5%).

Cell Viability Assays

In cell culture assays, the half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) defined as the concentration required to inhibit 50% of cell proliferation—serves as a key metric for identifying the most potent active compound. Among candidate molecules demonstrating cytotoxic effects, those exhibiting the lowest IC₅₀ values are prioritized. To assess 48‐h IC₅₀ values, cell viability was evaluated using the MTT assay (3‐[4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl]‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide) [56]. For this purpose, 8 × 10³ cells per well were seeded into 96‐well plates and incubated at 37°C in a CO₂‐enriched atmosphere for 24 h. Cells were then exposed to varying concentrations of the test compounds (0, 10, 15, 25, 50, 75, and 100 µM). Each concentration was applied to four replicate wells containing U87MG glioblastoma cells for a duration of 72 h. Following treatment, a medium containing 10% MTT reagent (M5655; Sigma Aldrich) was added to each well, and plates were incubated for an additional 3 h at 37°C. Formazan crystals formed by metabolically active cells were solubilized, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA reader (Biochrom, Anthos Zenyth 200). Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel. Cell viability was normalized to untreated controls, set as 100%. The percentage of viable cells was calculated according to the formula: cell viability (%) = [A570 sample/A570 control] × 100 [57]. Molecules exhibiting the lowest IC₅₀ values were identified as potential drug candidates based on MTT assay outcomes.

Reverse Transcription‐Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‐qPCR) analysis

The mRNA expression levels of key genes encoding proteins involved in programmed cell death pathways were determined using real‐time quantitative PCR (qPCR) for the identified molecule. The study was conducted in two groups: control and IC₅₀ groups. For each group, 10⁶ cells were seeded into 25 cm² flasks. Cells were allowed to adhere to the surface for 24 h. After adhesion, the cells were treated with the defined IC₅₀ concentration (60 µM) of the compound and incubated. While IC₅₀ values were determined based on cell viability measured by the MTT assay after 72 h of treatment, gene expression analyses were performed at 48 h in IC₅₀‐treated cells to capture early apoptotic, necrotic, and autophagic signaling events before extensive cell death [58]. At the end of the treatment period, cells were detached from the flask surface using trypsinization. The collected cells were centrifuged, and the medium was discarded. The cells were washed with sterile PBS, then centrifuged again, and the supernatant was removed.

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using the Trizol reagent‐chloroform method [59]. The quantity and purity of the obtained RNA were measured using a spectrophotometer (BioDrop; Biochrom). Following appropriate RNA concentration and purity measurements, cDNA synthesis was carried out using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (ABT cDNA kit, Turkey). The mRNA transcription levels of target genes, whose primer sequences are provided in Table 1, were determined using the synthesized cDNA.

The RT‐qPCR reaction was performed using a Rotor‐Gene Q (Qiagen, Germany) instrument. To determine gene expression patterns, transcription levels of major genes involved in autophagic and necrotic cell death pathways, such as ATG5, ATG3, and RIPK1, as well as genes associated with the apoptotic pathway, including BCL2, BAX, CASP8, CASP9, and CASP3, were analyzed. Additionally, the transcription level of PARP1, a DNA damage marker, was determined. Beta‐actin (Actb) was used as a housekeeping gene for normalization in the expression analyses. SYBR Green master mix (ENZO Life Science, cat: ENZ‐NUC104‐0200) was used for amplification detection. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the cycle threshold (Ct) value was determined at the beginning of the logarithmic phase of amplification. Gene expression was quantified using the 2 –ΔΔCt method [60]. Differences between groups were evaluated by comparing fold changes in expression relative to the control group.

In Vivo Cytotoxicity Studies

In this study, the toxicity of the most promising candidate molecule, determined after in vitro testing, was investigated in healthy rats, along with the biochemical changes induced by the candidate following administration. The dosage administered to the rats was determined based on the concentration derived from the cancer cell line. The aim was to determine safe exposure ranges before moving on to higher systemic doses, which is important as it can guide initial toxicity assessments and pharmacodynamic modeling. It can provide a starting criterion for predicting the in vivo effect [61, 62, 63]. Due to the difficulty in establishing an in vivo glioblastoma model and limited project budget, only toxicity studies in healthy animals were conducted.

In the experiment, 24 male Wistar albino rats weighing between 250 and 300 g were used. The animals were kept in controlled rooms at a constant temperature of 24°C with a 12‐h light/dark cycle (07:00–19:00 light/dark). They had unrestricted access to water and standard food. After being allowed to acclimate to the environment for 1 week in their cages, the animals began the experiments. All animal experiments were conducted at the Experimental Medicine Application and Research Center of Van Yuzuncu Yil University.

Wistar albino rats were randomly divided into four groups, with six rats in each group. The experimental groups were organized as shown below (Table 3).

Collection of Blood Samples

After the 30‐day experimental period, under ketamine anesthesia, blood was collected via cardiac puncture from the left ventricle of the rats into gel‐coated glass serum tubes. The blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at +4°C, and the separated serum samples were transferred into Eppendorf tubes. The serum samples were stored at –80°C in a deep freezer until biochemical analyses were performed. On the day of analysis, the serum samples were transported in foam boxes containing ice packs to the Laboratory of Internal Medicine, Department of Clinical Sciences, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Van Yuzuncu Yil University for biochemical analysis.

Biochemical Analyses

In cases of liver and kidney tissue damage, significant increases are generally observed in certain specific parameters. In this study, the activities of AST, ALT, and ALP were analyzed as markers of potential liver damage, while serum creatinine, BUN, and the BUN/creatinine ratio were analyzed as indicators of potential kidney damage.

The measurements of ALT, AST, ALP, creatinine, BUN, and BUN/creatinine in serum samples were performed using commercial kits on an auto‐analyzer (Fuji Dri‐Chem NX500V, Vietnam). The obtained data were statistically analyzed.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. All variables were found to be normally distributed (p > 0.05). For the gene expression analysis involving two independent groups, an independent samples t‐test was conducted to compare group means. For the biochemical analyses involving more than two groups, one‐way ANOVA was applied, followed by Bonferroni post‐hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. All results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
