GlycoRNA, a novel RNA modification
Authors: Binbin Hu, Tianyu Ma, Dongli Zhou, Juan Jiao, Xiangwei Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA plays a variety of critical roles in cellular environments, including serving as a structural molecule, messenger molecule, translational carrier, and effector molecule. During the execution of these functions, RNA undergoes diverse post-transcriptional chemical modifications, which are essential for sustaining life processes. Glycosylation, a key biological modification, was traditionally thought to occur only in lipids and proteins. However, recent studies have revealed that it can also take place on RNA, giving rise to what is termed glycosylated RNA (glycoRNA). This article explores the mechanisms underlying the formation and function of glycoRNA, detection methods, extracellular binding proteins, and key enzymatic systems involved. Furthermore, it highlights the potential of glycoRNA research to provide new insights for precision cancer therapy.Keywords: GlycoRNA, Cancer, Biomarker, RNA glycosylation

Introduction

The cell surface, as the central interface for interactions between cells and the external environment, serves as a critical hub for biological regulation. Through molecular mechanisms such as ligand recognition, signal transduction, and cascade initiation, it plays a pivotal role in essential physiological processes like intercellular communication and immune surveillance [1]. Traditionally, glycosylation modifications were believed to occur only on proteins or lipids. Extensive research has demonstrated that glycoproteins play key roles in various biological functions. For instance, clinically used tumor biomarkers such as PSA (prostate-specific antigen), CA125 (cancer antigen 125), CA19-9 (carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9), and AFP (alpha-fetoprotein) are widely employed in cancer screening and diagnosis [2]. However, the discovery of acp3U has expanded the frontiers of RNA modification research [3], revealing that RNA can also serve as a third target molecule for glycosylation, in addition to proteins and lipids. Conventionally, RNA biology and glycobiology were considered two unrelated fields—the former primarily confined to the nucleus and cytoplasm, while the latter localized to the endoplasmic reticulum-Golgi system. GlycoRNAs are small non-coding RNAs primarily localized in the cytoplasm. These small non-coding RNAs include small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs), small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), Piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), and small temporal RNAs (stRNAs), among others. The unique feature of glycoRNAs lies in their N-glycan structures, which are rich in sialic acid and fucose components [4]. Notably, these glycosylated RNAs have been confirmed to exist on the cell surface, suggesting their potential involvement in intercellular communication and immune recognition processes [4]. Their emergence adds a new layer of complexity to the mechanisms of molecular interactions within and outside the cell. However, the specific pathways remain unclear. It is speculated that the process may resemble the mechanism of protein N-glycosylation and may even require the participation of some of the same enzymes [5]. GlycoRNAs can bind to two types of sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectins (Siglecs) [6]. They are considered potential ligands for the Siglec family. Zhang et al. found that ribonucleic acids enhance neutrophil recruitment to inflammatory sites in vivo by interacting with P-selectin on endothelial cells, and their expression and function depend on the Sidt gene [7].

GlycoRNAs have been detected in multiple human cell lines, and studies have shown that these cell surface-associated glycoRNAs share homology with disease-associated small RNAs. This significant discovery may provide new insights into the mechanisms linking tumors and glycoRNAs, thereby opening novel avenues for cancer therapy.

Discovery of extracellular membrane-associated RNA and glycorna

Although the study of nucleic acids (including RNA and DNA) has predominantly focused on intracellular activities, the role of RNA in extracellular spaces was documented decades ago [8, 9]. Eukaryotic cells are enclosed by a semi-permeable lipid bilayer membrane and contain various internal membrane-bound organelles. While some small molecules can passively diffuse across membranes, most molecules—regardless of size—require active transport mechanisms for transmembrane movement. In recent years, advances in high-sensitivity technologies have expanded the scope of research. Extracellular RNA (exRNA) broadly refers to all RNA found outside the cell membrane, primarily consisting of small RNAs that are speculated to influence diverse processes, from immune cell activation to cancer progression [10]. Databases such as the exRNA Atlas [11] have begun cataloging extracellular RNA populations across different species and bodily fluids, while the wide variety of RNA types detected in extracellular environments has prompted questions about their functions. Thus, this field opens new research pathways for understanding hidden aspects of RNA biology. The Flynn’s team discovered glycoRNA molecules localized on the cell membrane. This groundbreaking study revealed that sialylated and fucosylated glycans can conjugate with non-coding RNAs to form glycoRNAs. The group demonstrated that these sialylated glycoRNAs can bind to Siglec receptors, a family of sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectins. The 14 members of this receptor family are implicated in immune checkpoint regulation [12, 13] and tumor immune evasion [14]. Further validating the phenomenon of membrane-associated RNA, Huang et al. identified the membrane localization of MALAT1, a long non-coding RNA closely linked to metastasis, in EL4 cell lines using a proprietary surface sequencing technique. They termed these membrane-associated extracellular RNAs maxRNA. Their study also found that antisense oligonucleotide-mediated knockdown of monocyte membrane-localized FNDC3B and CTSS RNA fragments significantly reduced cellular adhesion to endothelial cells [15]. Given the critical role of these genes in cellular remodeling, this discovery holds substantial research significance. In recent years, RNA biology has experienced explosive growth. With technological advancements, an increasing number of reversible RNA modifications have been uncovered. These findings have fundamentally transformed our understanding of the cell membrane: once viewed as a mosaic structure composed of lipids, cholesterol, proteins, and carbohydrates—later expanded to include glycolipids and glycoproteins—the membrane must now also accommodate glycoRNA as a novel component.

While this field is expected to continue advancing, current discoveries raise numerous new questions. The precise molecular mechanism by which glycoRNAs associate with the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane remains unclear. One possibility is direct RNA-membrane contact (supported by multiple studies [16]), while another involves protein-mediated anchoring. Elucidating the membrane attachment mechanisms of these cell-surface RNA ligands will provide crucial insights into their biophysical properties and regulatory functions.

Detection methods

There is an urgent need to develop advanced techniques for the detection and analysis of glycoRNAs. Ren et al. [17] developed an imaging technology called dual-recognition fluorescence resonance energy transfer (drFRET), which enables the visualization of representative glycosylated RNAs in small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) derived from various cancer cell lines and clinical serum samples. Using drFRET, they further elucidated the interaction between glycosylated RNAs and Siglec-10, Siglec-11, and P-selectin. Xie et al. [3] reported RNA-specific periodate oxidation and aldehyde labeling (rPAL), a more sensitive method for the enrichment, isolation, and characterization of glycoRNAs. Crucially, they were the first to identify the previously unknown linkage between RNA and N-glycans. This technique leverages the unique reactivity of 1,2-diols in sialic acids, where periodate oxidation generates aldehyde groups that form stable oxime bonds with aminooxy-functionalized solid-phase supports, enabling specific labeling of glycoRNAs. Combined with high-sensitivity mass spectrometry, their study proposed that 3-(3-amino-3-carboxypropyl)uridine (acp3U) serves as a key nucleotide anchoring site for glycan attachment(Fig. 1). As a highly conserved modified uridine in bacterial and mammalian tRNAs, acp3U has been shown to enhance tRNA thermostability and play a significant role in cellular physiology [18]. Ma et al. [19] developed a method called sialic acid aptamer and RNA in situ hybridization-mediated proximity ligation assay (ARPLA), which achieves high-sensitivity and high-selectivity visualization of glycoRNAs at the single-cell level. This technique employs dual recognition of glycans and RNA to trigger an in situ ligation reaction, followed by rolling circle amplification of complementary DNA and signal output via fluorescently labeled oligonucleotides. Using ARPLA, they discovered that glycoRNAs undergo intracellular trafficking via SNARE protein-mediated secretory exocytosis. These advancements in detection methodologies provide powerful tools for uncovering the biological roles of glycoRNAs and their potential implications in disease mechanisms.

RNA-binding proteins

An emerging feature of glycoRNA biology is that these surface interactions require coordination with cell surface RNA-binding proteins (csRBPs). Even before the recent discovery of cell surface glycosylated RNAs, studies had demonstrated the existence of RNA-RBP interactions in extracellular spaces. Several well-documented examples can be found in extracellular vesicles (EVs), including exosomes and microvesicles [20]. Similar to intracellular RNA-RBP interactions, extracellular RNA-RBP associations also depend on specific sequence contexts. Many RBPs detected on the cell surface—such as nucleolin, enolase, La protein, U5 SNRNP200, DDX21, hnRNPU, and NPM1—lack known transmembrane domains [21]. The findings from Perr et al. demonstrate that these cell-surface RBPs (csRBPs) can precisely assemble into well-defined nanoclusters enriched with multiple RBPs and glycoRNAs, and that these clustered structures can be disassembled by the addition of extracellular RNase [21]. In various cell types, csRBPs have been observed forming specialized functional domains on the extracellular surface. Their clustering with glycoRNAs may further enhance interactions with immunoregulatory receptors, providing the spatial organization necessary for precise immune recognition and response [21]. The arrangement of these glycoRNA-csRBP complexes on the cell surface is not only critical for immune regulation but may also broadly influence membrane structure and function. Currently, deciphering the regulatory mechanisms of these interactions and their implications in diseases such as cancer remains a key research focus.

Regulation of glycosylated RNA modifications

The biosynthetic pathway of glycosylated RNA (glycoRNA) remains poorly understood. Flynn et al. provided the first evidence that N-glycans on small RNA molecules are synthesized via the endoplasmic reticulum-Golgi pathway, a process dependent on the oligosaccharyltransferase (OST) complex [4]. This discovery directly links glycoRNA to the N-linked glycosylation machinery – distinct from O-GlcNAc modification catalyzed by O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT).

Notably, the phenomenon of RNA N-glycosylation challenges conventional understanding, suggesting the existence of undiscovered mechanisms enabling glycans to bind non-protein substrates. While early studies on protein glycosylation described the initiation of O-glycans in the Golgi apparatus mediated by GALNTs or sialyltransferases [22], the role of these enzymes in RNA glycosylation has not been experimentally validated.

Discussion

With advancements in biotechnology, the understanding of RNA has expanded from its role as an “effector molecule in protein synthesis” to encompass previously unknown functions, including the catalytic activity of ribozymes [23], gene regulation by non-coding RNAs, and intercellular communication mediated by extracellular RNAs in exosomes. RNA modifications serve as critical regulatory factors that respond to metabolic and cellular stress [24], influencing the translation of stress-related proteins and thereby conferring survival advantages to cells. glycoRNA is a class of RNA molecules modified with glycans that can be detected in various cell types [7, 19]. The discovery of glycan modifications on RNA has attracted widespread attention, marking the extension of glycosylation from proteins and lipids to a new molecular domain. In cancer, glycoRNAs may participate in multiple stages of tumorigenesis and progression. Recent studies utilizing sialic acid aptamers and RNA in situ hybridization-mediated proximity ligation assay (ARPLA) revealed that the levels of glycoRNAs on the surface of breast cancer cell lines are inversely correlated with tumor malignancy and metastatic potential [19]. Specifically, compared to non-tumorigenic mammary cells, malignant and metastatic breast cancer cells exhibit a progressive reduction in glycoRNA signals, suggesting that downregulation of glycoRNA expression may be associated with increased tumor aggressiveness. The Krishnan team [25] extended Flynn’s perspective by proposing a role for extracellular secretion of glycoRNAs in cancer. Ren et al. [17] used the drFRET imaging technique to confirm the specific interactions between five representative glycoRNAs and Siglec-10, Siglec-11, and P-selectin. They found that the loss of N-linked glycans, Neu5Ac, or glycoRNAs significantly impaired sEV internalization, suggesting that glycoRNAs may regulate sEV uptake through cell-surface glycan receptors. Sharma et al. [26] used metabolic labeling techniques and discovered that glycoRNAs primarily exist as intraluminal cargo within exosome vesicles. Additionally, the authors found that exosomal glycoRNAs can be transferred to untreated recipient cells, revealing their role in intercellular RNA communication. Inhibition of exosome biogenesis leads to intracellular accumulation of glycoRNAs, while blocking protein glycosylation transfer reduces glycoRNA sorting into exosomes.

Despite numerous hypotheses regarding the functions of extracellular RNAs (exRNAs), their precise biological roles remain poorly understood. When exploring the functions of exRNAs, several intriguing questions must be considered: How do they systematically and precisely exit cells (how does RNA cross the cell membrane? ); How do they maintain sufficient stability extracellularly to exert their functions (why aren’t exRNAs rapidly degraded? ); And how do they find molecular partners to fulfill their roles (what functional groups must RNA possess to play an active role in extracellular biology? ). Additionally, the localization of phenotypic observations presents challenges—the release of exRNAs may have a greater impact on donor cells than on recipient cells [10].

Notably, glycoRNAs exhibit glycosylation patterns similar to those of glycoproteins synthesized via the endoplasmic reticulum-Golgi pathway, raising new questions: Do glycoRNAs share the same glycosylation pathways as glycolipids and glycoproteins? As mentioned earlier, Flynn et al. found that OST is involved in the biosynthesis of glycoRNA-associated glycans, rather than being catalyzed by the conventional O-GlcNAc transferase. These complexities, coupled with the inherent technical challenges in exRNA research, make it difficult to rapidly establish their functions. Nevertheless, clear examples of exRNAs demonstrating reproducible biological functions can still be identified in existing studies.

Several critical questions remain. If both glycosylated and non-glycosylated RNAs coexist on the cell surface, non-glycosylated RNAs might interact with different receptors than glycoRNAs. Alternatively, non-glycosylated RNAs may be more prone to degradation, thereby altering intercellular interaction patterns. Precisely elucidating the molecular requirements for glycan modifications in the presentation of RNA on cell surfaces would be a highly valuable research direction. Given the potential importance of glycoRNAs in various biological processes, future studies should explore their developmental trajectories across different tissues and organs.
