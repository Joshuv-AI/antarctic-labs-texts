Evaluation of color matching of single-shade resin composites with different background shades: an in vitro study
Authors: Fikret Yılmaz, Melek Taş Çinkılıç, Mücahit Enes Çinkılıç
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractContemporary single-shade resin composites claim to match all 16 VITA Classical shades using a single universal shade, simplifying clinical procedures and reducing technique sensitivity. This study aims to comparatively evaluate the color-matching potential of various single-shade resin composites against different background shades simulated using conventional resin composites. Four single-shade resin composites—Omnichroma, Zenchroma, Clearfil Majesty ES-2 Universal, and Vittra APS Unique—were evaluated against three conventional background shades (A1, A2, A3; G-aenial, GC). Color measurements were conducted using a spectrophotometer, and color differences (ΔE₀₀) were calculated according to the CIEDE2000 formula. Visual assessments were performed by independent observers, and CAP-I and CAP-V values were determined accordingly. Statistical analysis was carried out using a two-way and three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) at a significance level of α = 0.05. Material type and background shade had a significant effect on both CAP-I and CAP-V values (p < 0.05). ΔE₀₀ values tended to increase from A1 to A3 in all composites. Zenchroma exhibited the lowest CAP-V at A1, while Omnichroma showed the highest at A3. The color-matching potential of the tested materials depends on both the type of composite and the background shade. Among the materials studied, Vittra APS Unique demonstrated the highest color-matching potential and the most noticeable shade adaptation. These findings may guide material selection in clinical practice. The color-matching performance of single-shade composites depends on the material and background shade, directly influencing material selection in esthetic restorations.Keywords: Single-shade resin composites, Shade matching, Color adjustment potential (CAP)Subject terms: Health care, Materials science, Medical research

Introduction

Resin-based composites are widely used in restorative dentistry. A key factor in ensuring patient satisfaction in restorative treatments is the structural and optical compatibility of the composite restoration with both the adjacent teeth and the overall tooth structure. To achieve a natural appearance, multi-layered composites with different colors and opacities are often combined. However, multilayer restorative procedures require precise shade selection and significant clinical expertise, making treatment more complex, increasing chair time, and raising costs1,2.

The ability of a composite to blend with the surrounding tooth structure is known as its color adjustment potential (CAP). Additionally, a composite’s translucency allows it to adapt to the remaining tooth tissue3. Based on these principles, various restorative materials have been developed to reduce technical sensitivity and facilitate esthetic restorations4.

Recently, the concept of “one-shade” or “single-shade” composite resins has emerged, referring to resin-based composites designed to aesthetically match all tooth shades using only one universal shade5. These materials rely on broad shade compatibility and are intended to blend seamlessly into the surrounding tooth structure, regardless of tooth color2. This shade adaptation is based on an optical phenomenon known as color matching or color assimilation.

Omnichroma gained attention as the first single-shade composite resin introduced to the market, simplifying aesthetic dental restorations by eliminating the need for shade selection6. Following this development, a new generation of single-shade composite materials with varying chemical compositions, optical properties, and pricing has been introduced. However, the aesthetic success and clinical performance of these materials remain subjects of ongoing debate7. Most recent studies have assessed the shade-matching ability of single-shade composites using a uniform background color, without comprehensively addressing the influence of different background shades on this process8. Additionally, the majority of existing research has relied solely on instrumental assessments, such as spectrophotometric measurements, without validating these findings through visual evaluation methods5.

Moreover, existing research tends to focus on individual materials, and there is a lack of sufficient independent laboratory studies that directly compare single-shade composites from different commercial brands. Therefore, identifying the differences in shade matching among commercially available single-shade composites, as well as determining their effectiveness in various clinical scenarios, is of both scientific and clinical significance.

The aim of this study is to comparatively evaluate the shade-matching ability of single-shade composite resin materials from different commercial brands when used for the repair of previously placed esthetic restorations made with conventional (multi-shade) composites, and when applied over different background shades. In this context, the study seeks to determine which single-shade composite material provides the best color integration across diverse clinical scenarios.

The null hypothesis of this study is that there will be no difference in the color-matching potential between single-shade resin composites and conventional resin composites, and that different background colors will not influence their color-matching ability.

Power analysis was conducted using Minitab 16.0, based on the study by Zhu et al.9. With a 5% Type I error and 80% statistical power, the minimum required sample size was calculated as four per group. To account for potential data loss, five samples (n = 5) were included in each group.

In this study, two different types of samples were prepared: single and dual samples. All samples were fabricated using a specially designed silicone mold. The single samples were disc-shaped, measuring 10 mm in diameter and 4 mm in height, and were produced for each material included in the study (n = 5).

Dual samples were prepared using the specially designed silicone mold shown in (Fig. 1). These samples consisted of an outer ring measuring Ø10 × 4 mm, created from three different shades of the conventional composite. The outer ring featured a central cavity of Ø6 × 2 mm, which was restored using four different single-shade resin composites. Instrumental evaluation

A portable clinical spectrophotometer (Vita Easyshade V; Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) was used to measure the color of the samples against a white background. The observer angle was set at 2°, and a D65 illuminator was used during the measurements. Color readings were taken from the center of each sample, and the device was calibrated before each measurement.

The measured L’, a’, and b’ values were recorded. L’ represents the lightness and darkness of the color, with a perfect black having an L’ value of 0 and a perfect white having an L’ value of 100. The a’ and b’ values indicate color intensity, where the a’ axis ranges from redness (positive a’) to greenness (negative a’), and the b’ axis ranges from yellowness (positive b’) to blueness (negative b’).

Visual evaluation

Visual color assessments were conducted by three dental experts with superior color discrimination ability, as defined by ISO/TR 28642:2016. Under D65 illumination and using a 0°/45° viewing geometry, the observers performed blind visual evaluations of all specimens, which were placed in a random order on neutral gray paper.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS (version 26; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, United States). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation. The Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that the data followed a normal distribution (p > 0.01).

A two-way and three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the effects of material type, background color, and application method on ΔE00, CAP-I, and CAP-V values. When a significant difference was identified, Tukey’s HSD test was used for multiple comparisons, as the assumption of homogeneity of variance was met. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level set at α = 0.05.

Discussion

This study evaluated the color-matching potential of four different single-shade resin composites—Omnichroma, Zenchroma, Clearfil Majesty ES-2 Universal, and Vittra APS Unique—against different background colors. The results indicated that both material type and background color significantly affected the final esthetic appearance of the restorations. Similar studies in the literature also report substantial variation in the color adaptation potential of different materials. For instance, Truong et al. found that the optical behavior of single-shade resin composites was highly dependent on the color of the underlying tooth structure12. Likewise, Ismail and Paravina observed that the adaptation of single-shade composites to tooth surfaces of different shades varied significantly based on the material used13.

The findings of this study underscore the importance of careful material selection and consideration of clinical conditions when using single-shade resin composites. The consistency of these results with existing literature contributes to a better understanding of the clinical performance of these materials. Given the demonstrated impact of material type and background color on color harmony, the null hypothesis proposed in this study was rejected.

Despite their advantages, the clinical effectiveness of single-shade composites remains controversial. While some studies report that these materials can match a broad range of shades, others indicate limitations in certain hues. Notably, the background color of the restoration and the optical transmittance of the composite material are the primary factors influencing the final color match. In this context, instrumental and visual assessment methods are employed to objectively evaluate color differences. Spectrophotometers are particularly valuable in color analysis, as they can detect subtle shade variations that may be imperceptible to the human eye14.

In this study, a VITA Easyshade V (VITA Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) spectrophotometer with a 5 mm diameter probe was used to objectively measure color differences. According to previous studies, VITA Easyshade has demonstrated reliability and accuracy rates exceeding 90%14. Furthermore, recent research confirms that VITA Easyshade provides stable and reproducible color measurements under varied lighting conditions, making it a valuable tool for dentists in restoration evaluation15. Additionally, this device has been reported to be highly precise, particularly in assessing monochromatic resin composites16. These findings support the use of instrumental measurement methods as a reliable aid in clinical decision-making for esthetic restorations.

The CIEDE2000 (ΔE₀₀) formula was chosen for evaluating color differences in this study. Compared to the traditional CIELAB (ΔEₐb) system, ΔE₀₀ provides more clinically relevant results, as it better represents the human eye’s perception of color differences17.Miranda et al. demonstrated that ΔE₀₀ could detect small color differences more accurately when assessing the adaptation of monochromatic composites to background colors6. Similarly, Koi et al. reported that CIEDE2000 provides more reliable results in determining color differences between restorative materials and natural tooth structures compared to the traditional CIELAB formula18. As a result, CIEDE2000 is regarded as one of the most reliable and precise methods for evaluating the esthetic fit of restorations in dentistry.

In this study, the ΔESİNGLE values for single-layer samples ranged between 3.22 and 14.49, while the ΔEDUAL values for paired samples and unrestored teeth ranged between 2.88 and 11.09. The fact that the ΔEDUAL value is lower than the ΔESINGLE value supports the role of color harmony and CAP effect in aesthetic restorations. The lower ΔEDUAL values suggest that these materials successfully achieve color integrationin esthetic dentistry5.

The findings of this study confirm that Color Adjustment Potential (CAP) indices (CAP-I and CAP-V) vary depending on material type and background color, leading to differences in esthetic outcomes among composite resin restorations19. Spectrophotometric analyses revealed that the majority of the tested composite resin materials exhibited positive CAP-I and CAP-V values, indicating an enhanced ability for optical adaptation to the surrounding dental tissue13.

The findings of this study demonstrated that these materials exhibited variable performance in both visual assessments (CAP-V) and instrumental evaluations (CAP-I). There is no universal standard for CAP values in achieving effective color change. However, Pereira et al. proposed a CAP threshold of 0.20, defined as a 20% reduction in ΔE₀₀ values or visual grading in dual-layer specimens compared to single-layer specimens20. Apart from CAP, the threshold value of ΔE₀₀ also plays a critical role in evaluating color match. While Paravina et al. suggested an acceptability threshold of ΔE₀₀ ≤ 1.8 units, it should be noted that this value may vary depending on measuring device, illuminant conditions, and background color10,21–23. Considering that most groups in this study were visually evaluated as acceptable (score ≤ 2), a slightly higher ΔE₀₀ threshold than 1.8 appears more appropriate for clinical relevance.

In terms of CAP-I, Vittra APS Unique exhibited the highest values (above 0.20 across all background colors), indicating the best and most effective color adaptation. This may be attributed to its high translucency (light transmittance) and low opacity, which allow for enhanced light scattering and a more uniform shade match with surrounding tooth tissue. Similar studies in the literature also indicate that composites with high translucency levels achieve better color adaptation18. Additionally, Rosa et al. reported that Vittra APS Unique demonstrated the best color matching ability among single-shade composites, successfully blending with natural tooth color, even in large restorations24.

The fact that Omnichroma achieves the highest CAP-V value, especially with the A3 background color, indicates that the material provides a visually distinct color change. However, in terms of CAP-I, the best results have been achieved with Vittra APS Unique and Clearfil Majesty ES-2 Universal. This success of Omnichroma is based on the structural color technology known as “Smart Chromatic Technology”. This technology is based on the principle that spherical filling particles with a diameter of 260 nm reflect certain wavelengths of light and adapt to the surrounding tooth color25. However, the inability of this adaptation to maintain its stability when the background color changes drastically can limit color matching in certain clinical situations. As a matter of fact, current studies report that Omnichroma provides higher color harmony in light tones and more limited color harmony in dark tones18,26.

Zenchroma exhibited the lowest CAP-I values and demonstrated less effective color compatibility compared to the other materials, particularly on light-shade backgrounds. This may be attributed to its lower translucency level and the optical properties of its filler particles. A study by Forabosco and Checchi investigated the color matching potential of various single-shade composite sand found that Zenchroma had a lower matching capacity compared to other materials27. This difference may stem from variations in the material’s ability to reflect and absorb light, affecting its overall shade adaptation performance.

Among the four single-shade composites evaluated, Vittra APS Unique demonstrated the highest color matching potential, while Omnichroma performed well in specific background colors. However, the lower color adaptation capacity of Zenchroma should be carefully considered when selecting materials for clinical applications.

This study was limited to only three background shades; therefore, the generalizability of the findings across the full spectrum of natural tooth colors is constrained. The specimens used did not fully replicate the structure of natural teeth, and color measurements were conducted under standardized laboratory conditions. As a result, the influence of clinical variables such as the light source, patient positioning, and biological factors could not be considered. On the other hand, the VITA Easyshade V spectrophotometer employed in this study is primarily designed—according to the manufacturer’s instructions—for measuring the color of natural teeth and dental porcelains. In this context, its application for evaluating the color of composite resin discs may represent a methodological limitation. Nonetheless, there are studies in the literature that have utilized this device in a similar manner for assessing the color of composite specimens28–30. However, under laboratory conditions, the use of alternative devices capable of providing more precise spectral analysis—particularly for uniform artificial samples—may improve measurement accuracy and offer an alternative approach for future investigations. Furthermore, the in vitro nature of the study limits the direct applicability of the results to clinical practice. To enhance clinical relevance, future studies should include a broader range of background colors and be conducted under in vivo conditions.

Conclusion

This study examined the color matching ability of single-shade resin composites against different background colors and demonstrated that both material type and background color play a critical role in determining color adaptation potential.

Among the tested materials, Vittra APS Unique exhibited the highest color fidelity, while Omnichroma showed better adaptation, particularly on darker backgrounds. In contrast, Zenchroma demonstrated lower color-matching performance, especially on lighter backgrounds.

These findings highlight the importance of considering background color when selecting single-shade composites for clinical applications. To further validate these results, in vivo studies involving larger patient groups are recommended for a more comprehensive evaluation of their clinical applicability.
