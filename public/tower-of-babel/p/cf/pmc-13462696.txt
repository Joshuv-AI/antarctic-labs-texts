Hybrid deep learning and ES-MDA for pressure transient inversion in radial composite reservoirs
Authors: Yaser Abdollahfard, Ali Hamzei, Ali Asghar Shokoohi, Mohammad Ahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate characterization of reservoir heterogeneity is important for improving reservoir management and production forecasting. This study presents a hybrid workflow for permeability estimation in structured radial composite reservoirs using deep learning (DL) and the Ensemble Smoother with Multiple Data Assimilation (ES-MDA). Prior to DL modeling, a quantitative sensitivity and identifiability analysis was performed to evaluate the information content of single-well pressure-transient data. The analysis examined the effects of inner-zone permeability, mobility ratio, and composite radius on pressure-derivative responses, showing that the dominant sensitivities are associated with near-well radial-composite parameters, while sensitivity decreases with increasing radial distance from the well. Two DL architectures—a convolutional neural network (CNN) and a fully connected (FC) network—were evaluated using synthetic pressure-derivative responses generated from radial composite reservoir realizations. The objective of the proposed framework is not to reconstruct arbitrary geological permeability fields, but rather to infer permeability distributions constrained by radial composite reservoir behavior. Both models achieved high prediction accuracy under noise-free conditions, while the CNN demonstrated greater robustness when Gaussian noise was added to the pressure-derivative inputs during testing. For history matching (HM), two initialization strategies were investigated: (i) random radial-composite initialization and (ii) AI-informed initialization derived from CNN predictions. Ten ensemble realizations were generated using ± 5% perturbations, followed by 20 ES-MDA assimilation steps with a constant inflation factor of α = 20. The AI-informed initialization achieved faster convergence and lower final pressure-derivative misfit than the random initialization. Overall, the proposed framework provides an efficient and physically consistent workflow for permeability estimation and history matching in structured radial composite reservoirs.

Introduction

Well testing is a fundamental technique in reservoir engineering used to evaluate subsurface formation properties and behavior. By measuring the pressure response of a well to controlled changes in flow rate, engineers can infer key reservoir characteristics, including permeability, skin factor, and boundary conditions. Pressure Transient Analysis (PTA) involves analyzing these pressure responses over time to gain insights into the reservoir’s dynamic behavior. Common types of well tests include drawdown tests, where the well is produced at a constant rate, and buildup tests, where the well is shut in after production to observe pressure recovery. The interpretation of PTA data often utilizes analytical models and type-curve matching to estimate reservoir parameters. Advancements in PTA have led to more accurate and efficient methods for reservoir characterization, aiding in the optimization of hydrocarbon recovery and reservoir management strategies1.

Radial composite reservoirs are characterized by concentric regions with distinct permeability and storativity properties, separated by a discontinuity or transition zone. This model effectively represents reservoirs undergoing enhanced oil recovery processes, such as steam injection or CO₂ flooding, where a front or transition zone forms between the injected fluid and the native reservoir fluid. In these systems, the inner region typically exhibits higher permeability, while the outer region has lower permeability, leading to a radial flow pattern towards the wellbore. The pressure transient behavior in such reservoirs is influenced by factors like mobility ratio, storativity ratio, and the presence of a skin effect at the discontinuity. Analyzing PTA data in radial composite reservoirs is crucial for accurate reservoir characterization and optimizing production strategies2,3.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has significantly advanced well test interpretation by automating and enhancing the analysis of PTA data. Traditional methods often rely on manual interpretation and predefined models, which can be time-consuming and prone to human error. AI techniques, particularly machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), have been employed to address these challenges (1). Over the past decade, the application of AI in well test interpretation has focused on automating model identification and parameter estimation1.

The application of AI in well test interpretation has gained increasing attention over the past decade. Early efforts focused on using neural networks to automate model identification and parameter estimation. Li et al. (2020) applied a one-dimensional convolutional neural network(CNN) to automate well-test interpretation in radial composite reservoirs, successfully estimating mobility ratios, storativity ratios, composite radius, and skin parameters directly from transient pressure data4. Wang et al. (2019) introduced long short-term memory (LSTM) networks for analyzing raw pressure-transient sequences, demonstrating that sequence learning can capture flow-regime dynamics and estimate reservoir parameters robustly, even under noisy conditions5. Dong et al. (2022) developed a DL workflow using convolutional networks to classify reservoir types and identify pressure behaviors, showing improved diagnostic consistency compared with expert type-curve matching6. Xue et al. (2022) proposed a fully data-driven framework for pressure-transient analysis of water-drive reservoirs, automating model selection and parameter estimation without manual derivative inspection7. Freites et al. (2023) employed unsupervised ML combined with dynamic time warping to cluster derivative curves, enabling objective recognition of flow regimes in naturally fractured reservoirs8. Pandey et al. (2022) integrated metaheuristic optimization algorithms with neural networks to improve model identification and parameter inversion, achieving accurate results under noisy derivative data9. Yang et al. (2022) combined syntactic pattern recognition with ML for reservoir model identification, offering automated diagnosis and pre-estimation of parameters from derivative plots10. Liu et al. (2023) extended the CNN-based interpretation approach to circular reservoirs, confirming that deep-learning architectures can generalize across different reservoir geometries11. Abdalla et al. (2024) compared CNN-LSTM models with tree-based learners, showing that while deep networks improve dynamic modeling, ensemble tree methods often outperform them in generalization for real-field pressure-transient analysis12. Starikov et al. (2024) investigated time-lapse PTA using automated feature extraction and pattern recognition, enabling monitoring of reservoir property changes across repeated well tests13.

Recent advances in history matching (HM) for heterogeneous reservoirs have focused on addressing three fundamental challenges: strong nonlinearity between model parameters and observations, high-dimensional parameter spaces, and limited information content of available data. To overcome these challenges, a combination of ensemble-based data assimilation, iterative smoothing techniques, and ML–assisted workflows has been increasingly adopted in recent years.

ES-MDA has become one of the most widely used ensemble-based methods for HM due to its robustness and computational efficiency. By assimilating observational data gradually over multiple assimilation steps with inflated data covariance, ES-MDA mitigates filter divergence and improves convergence in nonlinear inverse problems. Numerous studies have demonstrated its successful application for integrating production data, pressure transient responses, and seismic attributes in heterogeneous reservoirs, particularly in radial and layered systems where parameter contrasts are pronounced14–16.

Building upon ensemble smoothers, Iterative Ensemble Smoother (IES) and its hybrid variants introduce iterative updates and optimization-inspired formulations to improve convergence in highly nonlinear settings. IES methods reformulate HM as a sequence of approximate minimization problems, allowing for better handling of complex parameter interactions while maintaining ensemble-based uncertainty quantification. Recent developments have emphasized hybrid formulations that balance computational cost and inversion accuracy, making IES suitable for large-scale reservoir models with thousands of uncertain parameters17–19.

In parallel, ML–based approaches have gained increasing attention as surrogate models or preprocessing tools in HM workflows. DL models, including convolutional and FC neural networks, have been employed to extract diagnostic features from pressure and pressure-derivative data, classify flow regimes, and generate rapid approximations of reservoir properties. While these approaches significantly reduce computational cost, recent studies emphasize that their predictive reliability strongly depends on the physical consistency of the training data and their ability to generalize beyond idealized, noise-free conditions7,20,21.

More recently, advanced parameterization and physics-informed learning techniques have been proposed to bridge the gap between purely data-driven models and physics-based simulators. Methods such as physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) and neural operators aim to embed governing flow equations directly into the learning process, improving generalization and reducing the required training data. These approaches have shown promising results in representing complex spatial heterogeneity while preserving physical consistency, particularly when combined with ensemble-based data assimilation frameworks22–24.

Despite these advances, recovering spatially distributed permeability fields from limited well-test data remains fundamentally ill-posed and non-unique. Consequently, recent research increasingly emphasizes hybrid workflows that combine physics-based constraints, ensemble uncertainty quantification, and data-driven feature extraction. Within this context, the present study positions DL as a complementary tool for generating physically plausible initial permeability ensembles, which are subsequently refined through ES-MDA, rather than as a standalone inversion method. These recent developments underscore the trend towards integrating advanced computational techniques and ML methodologies in HM processes. By leveraging these innovations, reservoir engineers can achieve more accurate and efficient model calibrations, leading to improved reservoir management and optimization strategies.

In recent years, DL approaches have demonstrated significant potential in extracting complex patterns from pressure transient data, providing rapid and automated estimation of permeability distributions. By integrating CNNs with ensemble-based HM techniques, such as the ES-MDA, it is possible to reduce prediction errors and obtain history-matched permeability fields efficiently.

This study aims to leverage synthetic well-test data generated from a radial composite reservoir model to develop a DL framework capable of predicting two-dimensional permeability distributions. Furthermore, the integration of ES-MDA facilitates iterative refinement of initial predictions, ensuring that the inferred permeability maps are consistent with pressure derivative observations. The proposed methodology addresses the limitations of traditional PTA, offering a robust, data-driven approach for enhanced reservoir characterization and management. However, the effectiveness of such hybrid approaches strongly depends on the suitability of the neural network architecture and the quality of the initial permeability ensemble, which motivates the methodology proposed in this study.

Methodology

The methodology of this study is designed to systematically investigate the relationship between pressure transient responses and reservoir permeability in a radial composite system. The proposed workflow integrates synthetic reservoir modeling, pressure transient simulation, permeability estimation, and physics-based HM using the ES-MDA. A central feature of the methodology is the use of two alternative approaches for defining the initial permeability model prior to data assimilation, allowing a direct assessment of the impact of prior information on the history-matching process.

To establish a reliable foundation for permeability inference, idealized radial composite reservoir models were first constructed and simulated under controlled and noise-free conditions. These models were used to generate pressure and pressure-derivative responses corresponding to a wide range of permeability contrasts and inner-zone radii. This synthetic dataset served as the basis for understanding the fundamental relationship between pressure-transient behavior and spatial permeability variations in radial composite systems.

Two distinct approaches were then employed to define the initial permeability estimate. In the first approach, the initial model corresponds to a randomly generated radial composite reservoir. In this case, the reservoir structure (inner and outer zones) is preserved, but the permeability values and geometric parameters are randomly sampled within physically realistic bounds. This approach represents a physics-consistent but uninformed prior and serves as a baseline initialization for ES-MDA.

In the second approach, an AI-based model was used to generate an informed initial permeability estimate. A deep neural network (DNN), trained on the idealized synthetic pressure-derivative dataset, was employed to predict the spatial permeability distribution directly from pressure-transient data. The resulting DNN-based permeability field was then used as the mean of the initial ensemble, with controlled stochastic perturbations applied to ensure sufficient ensemble diversity.

Regardless of the initialization strategy, the resulting permeability ensembles were subsequently refined using ES-MDA. The ES-MDA framework iteratively assimilates pressure-derivative observations by updating the ensemble of permeability fields through repeated forward simulations of the radial composite reservoir model. This process reduces the mismatch between simulated and observed pressure derivatives while maintaining consistency with the governing flow physics.

By comparing the random radial composite initialization and the AI-based initialization within a unified ES-MDA framework, the proposed methodology enables a systematic evaluation of convergence behavior, misfit reduction, and robustness of the history-matched permeability estimates. The complete workflow is implemented in a stepwise manner and is illustrated in Fig. 1, which summarizes the progression from synthetic data generation to initial model construction and final HM.

Base reservoir model description

A synthetic reservoir model was developed using ECLIPSE 100 to generate pressure transient data representative of a radial composite system. The reservoir was discretized on a Cartesian grid of 201 × 201 × 1 cells, representing a single-layer formation with a thickness of 50 ft at a depth of 8000 ft. Porosity was uniformly set at 0.15. The reservoir fluid was modeled as a slightly compressible single-phase oil with a density of 50 lb/ft³ and viscosity of 1.5 cP at a reference pressure of 5000 psi.

To accurately capture radial flow behavior, grid spacing in both X and Y directions increased logarithmically from the central well. This design provides high resolution near the wellbore, where pressure changes are most rapid, while gradually coarsening toward the outer boundary, optimizing both accuracy and computational efficiency. The logarithmic variation of the grid spacing is illustrated in Fig. 2.

A single production well was placed at the center of the grid (I = 101, J = 101) and completed across the full formation thickness with a radius of 0.67 ft and skin of 3. The well operated under a constant production rate of 1000 STB/D. Synthetic pressure data were generated using logarithmically increasing time steps ranging from 1 × 10⁻⁵ days to approximately 5 days, capturing both early- and late-time reservoir responses.

The well-test duration was selected such that the pressure transient fully propagates across the inner zone and enters the outer zone, while remaining well within the external reservoir boundary. This design ensures that the pressure response reflects the permeability contrast between the two radial zones only, without contamination from boundary effects.

The reservoir was conceptualized as a radial composite system with two distinct permeability zones, as shown in Fig. 3. This conceptual design highlights the inner low-permeability zone and the surrounding outer zone with higher permeability, which are key for analyzing pressure transient behavior in composite reservoirs. On the left, the actual radial size of the composite region is represented, highlighting the inner and outer zones with different permeability. On the right, the heat map of the permeability matrix provides a numerical visualization of the same configuration, where higher and lower permeability contrasts are clearly observed. This dual representation, based on logarithmic grid sizing, facilitates the understanding of spatial heterogeneity and serves as the input framework for subsequent numerical simulations and history-matching analysis. In the results section, the permeability heat map will also be examined, which is equivalent to this representation.

It should be noted that the permeability heterogeneity considered in this work is intentionally restricted to a two-zone radial composite structure. This configuration represents a simplified but physically meaningful form of heterogeneity frequently used in pressure transient analysis, rather than complex stochastic geological heterogeneity. The objective of this study is to evaluate the feasibility of DL models for recovering structured permeability contrasts from limited well-test data.

Generation and simulation of radial composite reservoir realizations

A total of 1,000 realizations of the radial composite reservoir were generated to capture the effects of heterogeneity. Each realization consists of two distinct permeability zones: an inner low-permeability region and an outer higher-permeability region. The inner zone permeability was randomly varied between 100 and 200 mD, while the outer zone permeability ranged from 1.66 to 10 times the inner zone. The radius of the inner zone was randomly assigned between 1000 and 2000 ft. This approach produces diverse reservoir configurations, enabling assessment of how permeability contrasts and inner-zone size influence pressure transient behavior.

Following the generation of the realizations, each reservoir model was simulated under the constant production scenario outlined in Sects. 2 − 1 using ECLIPSE 100. For every realization, pressure transient data were extracted, forming a comprehensive dataset that captures the variability of reservoir responses across different configurations.

To normalize the pressure data, the recorded pressures were divided by the production rate of 1000 STB/D, ensuring consistent comparison across all realizations. Subsequently, the pressure derivative (dP/dlnt) for each normalized dataset was computed using numerical differentiation according to follow25:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${(\frac{{dp}}{{d\ln t}})_i}= - {t_i}\frac{{{p_{i+1}} - {p_i}}}{{{t_{i+1}} - {t_i}}}$$\end{document}

In this study, only pressure-derivative signals were used as inputs to the DL models. Pressure derivatives are widely recognized in PTA for their enhanced diagnostic capability, as they clearly highlight flow regimes and reduce the influence of constant pressure offsets, datum uncertainty, and wellbore storage effects. This representation facilitates learning invariant and physically meaningful features across different realizations.

Although absolute pressure data contains valuable information related to average reservoir permeability and skin factor, it was intentionally excluded in this work to focus on derivative-based flow regime information and to reduce sensitivity to initial pressure conditions.

To evaluate the robustness of the proposed DL models under realistic measurement conditions, noise-perturbation experiments were conducted on the test dataset. While the neural networks were trained using noise-free synthetic data to ensure stable learning of fundamental pressure–permeability relationships, two testing scenarios were considered. In the first scenario, the models were evaluated using noise-free pressure derivative data. In the second scenario, zero-mean Gaussian noise with standard deviations corresponding to 3–10% of the signal magnitude was added to the pressure derivative curves. This approach mimics common sources of uncertainty in field well-test data, such as gauge noise, rate fluctuations, and operational disturbances, allowing assessment of model robustness without compromising training stability.

Sensitivity and identifiability analysis

A structured simulation-based sensitivity and identifiability analysis was performed to quantify the influence of key radial composite reservoir parameters on pressure-transient responses. The objective of this analysis is to move beyond qualitative interpretation and provide a quantitative measure of parameter importance and identifiability in a single-well inverse problem.

Three controlling parameters were investigated: inner-zone permeability, permeability contrast ratio between inner and outer zones, and inner-zone radius. These parameters were selected due to their primary role in governing pressure propagation in radial composite systems.

To evaluate parameter influence, a set of controlled numerical experiments was designed. In this framework, 9 simulation cases were generated by systematically perturbing each parameter while keeping the remaining parameters fixed. Specifically, three cases were assigned to variations in inner-zone permeability, three cases to permeability contrast ratio, and three cases to inner-zone radius (See Table 1).

For each case, pressure responses were extracted from the reservoir simulator and transformed into pressure-derivative curves in logarithmic time space. These derivative curves provide enhanced visibility of flow regimes and reduce the influence of absolute pressure offsets, making them suitable for sensitivity assessment.

DNN for permeability prediction

In this study, two DNN architectures, namely a CNN and a FC network, were developed to map pressure-derivative time-series data from well tests to two-dimensional permeability distributions (See Fig. 4). The dataset was randomly split into 80% for training and 20% for testing. Both models were trained using the Adam optimizer with the mean squared error (MSE) as the loss function. Training was performed with a batch size of 32 for a maximum of 1000 epochs, while convergence was controlled using an early stopping strategy with a patience of 30 epochs and adaptive learning-rate reduction with a factor of 0.5 and a patience of 10 epochs. Model training was carried out on a personal workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7 (6th generation) CPU and a 4 GB GPU.

CNN-based architecture

CNNs are widely used in engineering applications because they can efficiently extract hierarchical features using convolutional kernels. This makes them particularly effective for handling sequential data such as pressure derivative responses in well testing, where short-term variations contain critical information about reservoir heterogeneity26,27.

The first model employs a 1D CNN, which is particularly suited for sequential data such as well test derivative pressure signals. The network begins with a sequence of Conv1D layers followed by pooling operations to extract local temporal features from the pressure derivative response. The flattened feature maps are then connected to dense layers, and finally reshaped into the desired two-dimensional output corresponding to the reservoir permeability map. The CNN’s ability to exploit local correlations and hierarchical feature extraction makes it efficient in learning pressure derivative patterns that reflect permeability variations in the reservoir. FC-based architecture

FC networks, also known as Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs), provide a direct and flexible approach for mapping input features to output targets. Each neuron in a dense layer is connected to every neuron in the previous layer, allowing the network to learn complex nonlinear functions. Although FC models typically require a larger number of parameters compared to CNNs, they can serve as a baseline for regression tasks where the relationships between input and output are highly nonlinear but not necessarily local27. In reservoir engineering, FC models have been successfully applied to predict petrophysical properties and perform inverse modeling tasks28.

The second model adopts a FC design, also known as a MLP. The input sequence is flattened into a vector and passed through a series of dense layers with increasing dimensionality, ultimately mapped into the target 2D permeability grid. Unlike CNNs, the FC model treats all inputs globally and does not explicitly utilize local temporal dependencies. However, its flexibility in nonlinear regression provides a strong baseline for comparison. The details of the architecture is summarized in Table 3.

Model performance evaluation

It is essential to define the criteria for assessing model performance. In this study, several statistical metrics—including root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and R-squared (R²) were employed to evaluate the models. Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive view of the models’ accuracy and predictive capability. Specifically, RMSE and MAE quantify the average magnitude of prediction errors, with RMSE placing greater emphasis on larger deviations, while MAPE expresses errors as percentages, enabling scale-independent comparisons. R² represents the fraction of variance explained by the model, serving as an indicator of its overall goodness-of-fit.

The RMSE quantifies the discrepancy between the network’s predictions and the target values, with lower RMSE indicating better model performance. History matching using ES-MDA

To improve the accuracy of permeability estimation and ensure consistency with observed well-test data, HM was performed using the ES-MDA. ES-MDA is an ensemble-based method that iteratively updates reservoir model parameters by assimilating observed pressure and pressure-derivative data while explicitly quantifying uncertainty. Compared to conventional gradient-based history-matching techniques, ES-MDA efficiently handles nonlinear and high-dimensional problems by updating all ensemble members simultaneously over multiple assimilation steps, reducing the risk of filter inbreeding and improving convergence behavior14,16,33.

In this study, two distinct strategies were considered for generating the initial permeability ensemble prior to ES-MDA HM:

Random Initialization: The initial guess was constructed as a radial composite reservoir with a prescribed inner radius. Permeability values in the inner and outer zones were sampled randomly within physically plausible bounds. This approach represents a conventional, uninformed ensemble generation strategy that does not rely on prior information from the observed well-test data.AI-based Initialization: An initial permeability field was inferred from data using an AI model, such as a DNN trained on prior reservoir data. This approach introduces an informed prior into the history-matching process.

Results and discussion

In this section, the performance of the proposed deep learning models for estimating permeability distributions from well-test pressure-derivative data is presented within a workflow that integrates parameter identifiability analysis, data-driven permeability prediction, and ES-MDA-based history matching. First, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the identifiability of radial composite reservoir parameters, which led to reformulating the inverse problem as a low-dimensional estimation of inner permeability, permeability contrast, and inner-zone radius. Based on this formulation, two deep learning models, a CNN and a fully connected (FC) network, were developed to map pressure-derivative responses to permeability fields. Their performance was evaluated using standard metrics (R², RMSE, MAE, MAPE) under both noise-free and noisy conditions to assess accuracy and robustness. Cross-plots and error analyses are also used to compare predicted and reference permeability distributions. Finally, the CNN-based predictions are used to initialize ES-MDA history matching, enabling comparison with random initialization in terms of convergence behavior and misfit reduction.

Clarification of the inverse problem formulation

To avoid misinterpretation of the inversion task, it is emphasized that the proposed framework does not attempt to reconstruct a fully unconstrained high-dimensional permeability field. Instead, the inverse problem is formulated as a low-dimensional parameter inference problem under a structured radial composite reservoir assumption.

In this formulation, the spatial permeability distribution is governed by a limited set of physically meaningful parameters, including inner-zone permeability, permeability contrast, and composite radius. Therefore, the deep learning model is trained to learn the mapping between pressure-derivative responses and these constrained permeability structures rather than arbitrary heterogeneous fields.

This formulation inherently reduces the effective dimensionality of the inverse problem and improves the stability and identifiability of the solution space.

The results are summarized in four complementary figures, including pressure-derivative response curves, sensitivity maps, and parameter identifiability ranking.

This behavior indicates that most of the identifiable information is concentrated near the wellbore, where pressure gradients are strongest, whereas far-field properties are weakly constrained.

DNN evaluation for permeability prediction

To assess the performance of the proposed DL models in predicting reservoir permeability, two architectures were employed: a CNN-based and a FC-based model. Both models were trained using well-test data as input and the corresponding permeability distributions as output. The evaluation metrics included the coefficient of determination R², RMSE, MAE, and MAPE, calculated for both training and testing datasets.

To evaluate the robustness of the proposed DL models under more realistic conditions, additional experiments were conducted by contaminating the test well-test derivative signals with Gaussian noise. The comparative results are summarized in Tables 4 and 5. Both CNN-based and FC-based models achieve high accuracy on noise-free test data (R² ≈ 0.980 for mean values), with relatively low RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. However, their performance under noisy inputs diverges significantly. The CNN-based model maintains strong predictive capability, with only a modest reduction in mean R² (0.9464 ± 0.0553) and moderate increases in RMSE, MAE, and MAPE, indicating robust behavior across individual realizations. In contrast, the FC-based model experiences a substantial drop in mean R² (0.6574 ± 0.8928) and dramatic increases in error metrics under noisy conditions, with high variability among realizations as reflected in the large standard deviations. These results demonstrate that convolutional architectures are considerably more robust to noise in pressure-derivative signals, making them more suitable for practical applications where measurement noise and operational uncertainties are unavoidable, whereas FC models are more sensitive to input perturbations despite performing slightly better on noise-free data.

History matching using ES-MDA

In the HM stage, the initial permeability distributions—generated either by random perturbations of a hypothetical radial composite reservoir (Fig. 13a) or by a DL-based prediction (Fig. 13b)—were iteratively updated using the ES-MDA to improve agreement between simulated and observed pressure derivatives. The ES-MDA procedure aimed to minimize the mismatch between field responses and model predictions while maintaining geological consistency. By applying 20 assimilation steps with an inflation factor of =20 and introducing ± 5% perturbations to generate 10 ensemble members, the method dynamically adjusted the permeability fields toward the observed reservoir behavior, providing a more accurate representation of the heterogeneous and composite structure.

Figure 13 presents the evolution of the RMSE of the pressure derivative for the 10 ensemble members as well as the ensemble mean across the 20 assimilation steps. In both initialization strategies, the most significant reduction in misfit occurs within the first few assimilation steps, indicating that ES-MDA rapidly incorporates the observed data into the model early in the process. However, a clear distinction emerges between the two approaches: the final misfit in the random-initialized ensemble (Fig. 13a) remains higher than that obtained with the AI-based initial guess (Fig. 13b). This difference demonstrates the advantage of embedding informed prior knowledge: the AI-based initialization provides a closer starting point to the true permeability distribution, which accelerates convergence and reduces residual discrepancies. Conversely, the random initialization requires larger corrections during early iterations, resulting in a higher overall misfit even after 20 assimilation steps.

The observed behavior highlights key characteristics of ES-MDA: (i) the majority of misfit reduction occurs in the initial iterations, (ii) later steps primarily serve to fine-tune the ensemble and stabilize the solution, preventing over-adjustment, and (iii) the choice of the initial ensemble significantly influences the efficiency and accuracy of the history-matching process. Overall, these results indicate that AI-informed initialization not only accelerates convergence but also yields a more reliable and lower-misfit representation of the reservoir, whereas the purely random approach, while capable of achieving acceptable convergence, remains less efficient and produces slightly higher final errors.

Limitations

It is important to acknowledge several limitations associated with the proposed framework. The inversion of single-well pressure transient analysis (PTA) data into a two-dimensional permeability field is inherently non-unique, as multiple permeability configurations can produce similar pressure responses, particularly at distances far from the observation well. As a result, the reconstructed permeability maps should be interpreted as plausible realizations rather than unique or exact representations of the true reservoir.

The predictive capability of PTA data is strongest in the near-wellbore region, while sensitivity progressively decreases with radial distance, leading to increased uncertainty in poorly constrained regions. To partially mitigate this ill-posedness, the inverse problem in this study is constrained by assuming a radial composite reservoir structure with physically bounded parameters, including inner-zone permeability, permeability contrast, and composite radius. Within this reduced parameter space, the deep learning model provides a physics-informed approximation that is subsequently refined using the ES-MDA framework. Nevertheless, the spatial resolution of the inferred permeability field remains fundamentally limited by the decreasing sensitivity of pressure data with distance.

In addition, the deep learning model is trained on a finite number of synthetic realizations, predicting a high-dimensional output (40,401 grid cells). Although the effective dimensionality is reduced through the imposed radial composite structure, the size and diversity of the training dataset may still limit generalization beyond the simulated scenarios. Moreover, the CNN architecture does not include an explicit spatial decoder, meaning that spatial coherence is learned implicitly through convolutional feature extraction rather than explicitly enforced.

Finally, the noise model adopted in this study is based on additive Gaussian perturbations applied to pressure-derivative data. While this provides a practical and commonly used approximation for measurement uncertainty, real field data may exhibit more complex, correlated, or non-Gaussian noise characteristics. Future work should consider incorporating more realistic noise models, integrating absolute pressure data alongside derivatives, and extending the framework to more complex geological settings beyond the radial composite assumption.

Conclusions

In this study, a hybrid workflow combining deep learning and the Ensemble Smoother with Multiple Data Assimilation (ES-MDA) was developed for reconstructing spatial permeability distributions from single-well pressure transient data within a constrained radial composite reservoir framework. Two deep learning architectures, namely CNN and fully connected (FC) networks, were evaluated as surrogate models for mapping pressure-derivative responses to permeability fields. The CNN was selected for the subsequent history matching stage due to its improved robustness to noisy inputs, while both models demonstrated comparable performance under noise-free conditions.

To address the inverse problem, two initialization strategies were investigated: (i) random generation of permeability ensembles based on radial composite assumptions, and (ii) AI-informed initialization derived from CNN predictions. Each ensemble consisted of 10 realizations generated through ± 5% perturbations, and ES-MDA was applied over 20 assimilation steps using an inflation factor of α = 20. Results show that both strategies reduce the mismatch between simulated and observed pressure derivatives; however, the AI-informed initialization provides a more consistent starting point, leading to faster convergence and lower final residuals.

From an identifiability perspective, the sensitivity analysis indicates that model response is strongly governed by a limited set of radial composite parameters, while spatial resolution decreases away from the wellbore. This confirms that the problem can be more appropriately interpreted as a low-dimensional parameter inference problem rather than a fully unconstrained high-dimensional field reconstruction.

Ensemble-based uncertainty analysis further shows that both approaches reproduce the main reservoir heterogeneity, while preserving non-uniqueness inherent in the inverse problem. The AI-based prior does not reduce uncertainty artificially but guides the ensemble toward dynamically consistent regions of the parameter space.

Overall, the proposed hybrid framework provides a physically consistent and computationally efficient approach for constrained reservoir characterization from single-well pressure transient data, while highlighting the importance of informed priors in ensemble-based history matching.
