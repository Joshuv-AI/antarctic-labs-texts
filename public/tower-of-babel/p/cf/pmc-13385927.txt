Geochemical mechanisms of fluorine migration and transformation during deep geological sequestration of high-salinity coal mine water in the ordos basin
Authors: Qiaohui Che, Yidan Bu, Song Du, Zhan Yang, Yanjun Liu, Yan Ding, Rui Wang, Xiaojun Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep geological sequestration is an emerging strategy for managing high-salinity coal mine water. However, the migration behavior of fluoride (F−) during sequestration poses significant risks to groundwater safety and system stability. Elucidating the mechanisms of F− migration and transformation is therefore critical for ensuring the long-term safety of sequestration projects. In this study, static batch experiments were conducted to investigate F− migration and retention in sequestration formations with different lithologies. Three representative rock types were collected from the Liujiagou Formation (fine sandstone–muddy sandstone and fine sandstone–mudstone) and the Shiqianfeng Formation (medium sandstone–mudstone) in the Ordos Basin. The experiments were carried out under simulated formation temperature (55 C) using synthetic high-salinity water with varying initial F− concentrations (20–150 mg/L) and pH conditions (2.0–10.0). Adsorption kinetics and isotherms were analyzed using pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, and Freundlich models, while water–rock interaction mechanisms were evaluated through ionic ratio analysis, chloro-alkaline indices, and sodium adsorption ratio. Results showed that F− removal suggests dominance of chemical adsorption, following a rapid–slow–equilibrium pattern, and was well described by the pseudo-second-order kinetic model and Freundlich isotherm (R2 > 0.999). F− migration was likely governed by coupled processes of cation exchange, competitive adsorption, and mineral precipitation–dissolution. Fine sandstone–mudstone exhibited the strongest F− fixation capacity (462.7 mg/kg) due to synergistic adsorption and precipitation. Sensitivity analysis identified pH as the most influential factor. These findings provide a experimental basis for predicting F− pollution and support the safe implementation of deep geological sequestration in high-salinity coal mine water management.Keywords: Deep geological sequestration, Fluoride migration, High-salinity mine water, Geochemical mechanism, Adsorption mechanism, Ordos basinSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

In response to the national “Dual Carbon” strategy and the demand for green development, the safe disposal and resource utilization of high-salinity coal mine water1 play an important role in promoting the clean and low-carbon transformation of the coal industry2. Currently, evaporation pond technology3 is widely applied, but it suffers from inherent limitations such as leakage risk, extensive land occupation, and insufficient stability4. In addition, the crystalline salt produced has limited resource value5, which restricts the sustainable development of this technology. Deep geological sequestration relies on the natural barrier function of geological formations6. It can effectively isolate high-salinity water from the biosphere7 and is regarded as an important pathway for achieving zero discharge of mine wastewater and supporting carbon neutrality goals8.

However, the complex ionic composition and extremely high salinity of high-salinity coal mine water pose significant challenges to the long-term safety of deep geological sequestration. Among various pollutants, F− is particularly critical due to its high toxicity, strong solubility, and pronounced geochemical activity. Under prolonged water–rock interactions, it is prone to migration and enrichment. This may induce chemical clogging within pore spaces of sequestration formations, reducing sequestration efficiency. Moreover, in the event of leakage, it may also pose serious risks to groundwater environments, human health, and ecosystems. Previous studies have primarily focused on fluoride contamination in shallow groundwater systems9–11 or on surface water treatment technologies12, with limited attention to the deep geological sequestration environment. Several investigations have explored mine water injection techniques13, but systematic analyses of fluoride migration and transformation under reservoir conditions, specifically regarding the coupled effects of competitive adsorption, cation exchange, and mineral precipitation—remain poorly constrained. Moreover, the controlling factors governing fluoride behavior in high-temperature, high-pressure rock pore environments have not been systematically elucidated. In contrast to previous studies that focused on either single mechanisms or shallow hydrogeological settings, the present study investigates the coupled multi-mechanism controls on fluoride migration under simulated deep sequestration conditions using representative reservoir lithologies from the Ordos Basin.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study selected a typical coal mining area in the Ordos Basin as the case study, from which three representative rock types were collected from the Liujiagou Formation and the Shiqianfeng Formation. Through static batch adsorption experiments combined with geochemical mechanism analysis, this work aims to clarify the migration and transformation patterns of fluoride during deep geological sequestration of high-salinity coal mine water, elucidate the coupling effects of competitive adsorption, cation exchange, and mineral precipitation on fluoride behavior, and evaluate the sensitivity of key factors, including initial F− concentration, pH, and clay content, on F− removal performance. The findings are expected to provide a scientific basis for the safe and large-scale implementation of deep geological sequestration technology in China, supporting the national dual-carbon strategy and the sustainable development of mining areas.

Study area

The study area is situated in the northern Yimeng Uplift of the Ordos Basin, covering an area of approximately 123 km2 (Fig. 1a). Tectonically, this region is characterized as a gently dipping monocline with continuous stratigraphic development and favorable sealing conditions, rendering it suitable for deep geological sequestration14–16.

Stratigraphic structure division map of the Ordos Basin16.

The target sequestration strata consist of the Triassic Liujiagou Formation and the Permian Shiqianfeng Formation. The Liujiagou Formation (depth interval: 1626–2026 m) comprises fluvial facies deposits17, predominantly gray-white medium- to fine-grained sandstones interbedded with purplish-red mudstones, with well-developed large-scale cross-bedding. The overlying Shiqianfeng Formation (2026–2181 m) represents delta-front to shallow-lacustrine facies deposits, consisting of interbedded gray-green medium sandstones and dark gray mudstones.

Core samples were collected from these two formations for experimental analysis: fine sandstone–muddy sandstone (Sample M1, 1830–1831 m) and fine sandstone–mudstone (Sample M2, 1915–1916 m) from the Liujiagou Formation, and medium sandstone–mudstone (Sample M3, 2090–2091 m) from the Shiqianfeng Formation (Fig. 1b).

Materials and methods

Materials

Rock samples

Three types of representative core samples from the Liujiagou Formation and the Shiqianfeng Formation in a mining area of the Ordos Basin were selected as adsorbents18. The adopted lithologies included fine sandstone–muddy sandstone (M1), fine sandstone–mudstone (M2), and medium sandstone–mudstone (M3), with the corresponding sampling depths of 1830–1831, 1915–1916, and 2090–2091 m, respectively. Prior to experimentation, the rock samples were crushed, ground, and sieved through a 120-mesh sieve. The physical characteristics of the rock samples are presented in Table 1.

High-salinity water solution

The high-salinity water solution used in the experiments was prepared using NaF, NaCl, Na2SO4, NaHCO3, CaCl2, KCl, and MgCl2 (AR ≥ 99.8%) dissolved in deionized water (18.20 MΩ∙cm), based on the analytical results of the on-site water samples. The measured F− concentration in the collected high-salinity water reached 49 mg/L. Accordingly, four synthetic high-salinity water solutions with different initial F− concentrations19 (20, 50, 100, and 150 mg/L) were prepared for the experiment, These concentrations comprehensively cover the real range of fluoride ion concentrations.

Experimental design

For each type of rock sample (M1–M3), eight subsamples of 15 g were weighed and placed in 250 mL conical flasks. Subsequently, 150 mL of the baseline high-salinity water solution was added to each flask. The suspensions were subjected to continuous oscillation at the formation temperature (55 °C) and a constant speed of 195 rpm. Supernatant samples were collected at 1, 5, 20, and 40 min, and at 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40, 44, and 48 h. After centrifugation, ion chromatography was employed to determine the concentrations of major anions and cations (K+, Ca2+, Na+, Mg2+, F−, Cl−, SO42-) in the solutions, while HCO3- and CO32- were determined by titration (Fig. 2). The detection limits for F−, Cl−, SO42-, Na+, K+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ were 0.02–0.10 mg/L, The analytical precision, expressed as relative standard deviation (RSD), was within ± 5% for all measurements based on replicate analyses (n = 3).Calibration curves were established using five standard concentrations, with correlation coefficients (R2) > 0.999 for all analytes. Blank corrections and duplicate analyses (one duplicate per ten samples) were applied to ensure data quality.

Experimental flowchart.

Based on the actual conditions of deep geological storage of high-salinity water, the pH of the injected high-salinity water ranges from 6 to 8.8, and the F- concentration ranges from 6.85 to 53.08 mg/L. In this study, 24 sets of static batch experiments were designed to investigate the geochemical effects and influencing factors of fluoride ions during the migration of formation fluids in the storage process. The specific experimental conditions (Table 2) and objectives are as follows: (1) Adsorption kinetics and water–rock interaction experiments were performed by reacting high-salinity water with an initial F− concentration of 50 mg/L at pH 6.5 using three types of rock powders with different clay contents until adsorption equilibrium was achieved. (2) Adsorption thermodynamic experiments were performed using high-salinity water with initial F− concentrations of 20, 50, 100, and 150 mg/L at pH 6.5, which reacted with the three rock powders until equilibrium was reached. (3) Influencing factor experiments involved high-salinity water with initial F− concentrations of 20, 50, 100, and 150 mg/L at pH 6.5, as well as high-salinity water with an initial F− concentration of 50 mg/L at pH values of 2.0, 4.0, 8.0, and 10.0. These were reacted with three rock powders of different clay contents until adsorption equilibrium was attained.

Analysis

Adsorption kinetics model

To elucidate the rate-controlling mechanisms of adsorption20, the pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order kinetic models were applied to fit the experimental data for the M1-4, M2-4, and M3-4 systems. The calculation formulas are respectively ln(Qe-Qt) = ln(Qe)-K1t and t/Qt = 1/K2Qe2 + t/Qe (Table 3). The pseudo-first-order model is generally applicable to diffusion-controlled physical adsorption processes21, whereas the pseudo-second-order model is more suitable for adsorption dominated by chemical interactions22. By comparing the goodness of fit of the two models and the consistency between calculated and measured values, the dominant adsorption mechanism in high-salinity water–rock systems was identified23,24.

Adsorption thermodynamic model

Adsorption thermodynamic models were employed to characterize the equilibrium behavior and interaction mechanisms between adsorbents and adsorbates25. In this study, the Henry, Langmuir, and Freundlich models26 (Table 4) were used to fit the experimental data from the M1–3–1, M1–3–4, M1–3–7, and M1–3–8 systems to evaluate adsorption equilibrium characteristics and underlying mechanisms. The Henry model describes the linear partitioning behavior, the Langmuir model assumes monolayer adsorption on homogeneous surfaces27, and the Freundlich model is applicable to multi-layer adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces25. By assessing the applicability of each model, information regarding the uniformity of the adsorption sites, nature of the adsorption forces, and adsorption strength can be inferred28,29.

Discrimination of water–rock interaction mechanism

To further elucidate the geochemical processes governing adsorption, parameters including ionic ratios30, chloro-alkaline indices, and the sodium adsorption ratio (SAR)31 were utilized for a comprehensive analysis to discriminate the dominant chemical reaction mechanisms during F− migration and transformation32. The calculation formulas are respectively CAI-I = Cl−-(Na+ + K+) / Cl−, CAI-II = Cl−-(Na+ + K+) / (HCO3− + SO42−) and SAR = Na+ / [(Mg2+ + Ca2+) / 2]1/2 (Table 5). Quantitatively calculated ionic milliequivalent concentration ratios were adopted to qualitatively identify the trends of ion sources and infer mineral dissolution–precipitation33. Chloro-alkaline indices were applied to determine the direction of cation exchange34, where the negative values of both CAI-I and CAI-II indicated forward cation exchange, while the positive values indicated reverse cation exchange35. The SAR36 was adopted to characterize the intensity of cation exchange, where SAR < 10 indicated weak adsorption, SAR values of 10–18 suggested alternate adsorption, and SAR ≥ 18 denoted significant adsorption37.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity of F− removal to initial F− concentration, pH, and clay content was evaluated using the unit gradient change rate (ΔV)38 , calculated as ΔV = Δη / Δfactor, where Δη is the change in removal efficiency (%) and Δfactor is the change in the corresponding influencing factor (initial F− concentration in mg/L, pH in units, or clay content in wt%). This normalization enables direct comparison of sensitivity across factors with different units.

Results and discussion

Theoretical model and adsorption mechanism

Adsorption kinetics process

Based on the M1-4, M2-4, and M3-4 experiments, the equilibrium adsorption capacities of the fine sandstone–mudstone, medium sandstone–mudstone, and fine sandstone–muddy sandstone systems were 462.7, 440.5, and 421.4 mg/kg, respectively. The F− adsorption process exhibited a three-stage rapid–slow–equilibrium pattern (Fig. 3). During the initial reaction stage (0–20 min), the adsorption rate was extremely rapid, primarily attributed to the abundance of active sites on clay mineral surfaces39. For all three lithologies, more than 97% of the equilibrium adsorption capacity was attained at this stage: the adsorption capacities at 20 min reached 410.2, 450.6, and 428.3 mg/kg for the fine sandstone–muddy sandstone, fine sandstone–mudstone, and medium sandstone–mudstone systems, respectively. This indicates that F− can be rapidly immobilized during the early sequestration phase, thereby reducing the short-term leakage risk. In the intermediate stage (20–1200 min) , the adsorption rate decreased markedly, which was attributed to the progressive depletion of available adsorption sites. In the later stage, with adsorption capacities stabilizing after 48 h. The pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order kinetic models were applied to fit the experimental data from the M1-4, M2-4, and M3-4 systems to elucidate the adsorption rate-controlling mechanisms. The fitting results are presented in Table 6 and Fig. 4. The coefficients of determination (R2) for the pseudo-second-order model exceeded 0.999 for all three lithologies, significantly outperforming those of the pseudo-first-order model (R2 < 0.38). Furthermore, the calculated equilibrium adsorption capacities were highly consistent with the measured values. These results demonstrate that F− adsorption conformed to the pseudo-second-order kinetic model, which is consistent with the adsorption rate was primarily governed by chemical adsorption rather than physical diffusion40. This finding is consistent with the observed kinetic behavior, characterized by rapid adsorption in the early stage followed by slower adsorption in the mid-to-late stages, suggesting that fixation reactions dominated by chemical bonding occurred at the rock particle surfaces41.

Adsorption thermodynamic model

The Henry, Langmuir, and Freundlich models were employed to fit the experimental data from the M1–3–1, M1–3–4, M1–3–7, and M1–3–8 systems, with the fitting results presented in Table 7 and Fig. 5. The Freundlich model exhibited the highest fitting performance (R2 > 0.92), suggesting that F− adsorption on the surfaces of the three rock types was characterized by multi-layer adsorption on heterogeneous media42. In this model, KF reflects the adsorption capacity, whereas n−1 represents the nonlinearity of the adsorption isotherm and the intensity of the adsorption process43. The fitted parameters ranged from KF = 1.683–3.979 and n−1 = 3.509–3.953, indicating that the adsorption capacity decreased in the order of fine sandstone–mudstone > medium sandstone–mudstone > fine sandstone–muddy sandstone44, and that F− adsorption in all systems did not follow the typical favorable adsorption pattern29. In contrast, the Langmuir model resulted in anomalous negative values for the maximum adsorption capacity, indicating that this model was not suitable for describing the present system. The negative Langmuir parameters indicate that the model is invalid for these systems, likely because adsorption does not saturate within the experimental concentration range. Although the Henry model exhibited linear correlations (R2 > 0.92), its overall goodness of fit was relatively poor, further demonstrating that the adsorption process was intrinsically non-linear. Typically, a Freundlich exponent n−1 < 1 indicates favorable adsorption. Although the obtained n−1 values exceed unity, the positive KF values and the high R2 indicate that adsorption is still favorable under the experimental conditions, while the high nonlinearity reflects the strong heterogeneity of the rock surfaces.

Reaction mechanisms and coupling effects during water–rock interactions

Based on the ionic concentration data obtained from the M1-4, M2-4, and M3-4 experiments, the migration and transformation of F− in high-salinity water–rock systems were identified to be governed by the coupled effects of three core mechanisms: cation exchange, competitive adsorption, and mineral precipitation–dissolution. These mechanisms collectively determined the ultimate fate and long-term stability of fluorine by modifying interfacial properties, competing for adsorption sites, and forming bulk-phase precipitates, respectively.

Cation exchange

Cation exchange is the primary driving force for the evolution of the geochemical environment within the system. The calculations indicated that the chloro-alkaline indices (CAI-I and CAI-II) for the M1-4, M2-4, and M3-4 systems were all positive, demonstrating the occurrence of pronounced reverse cation exchange in high-salinity water–rock systems. Specifically, Na+ and K+ in the high-salinity solution exchanged with Ca2+ and Mg2+ on rock surfaces, releasing the latter into the aqueous phase. The SAR values ranged from 166 to 180, indicating extremely intense cation exchange, far exceeding that observed in typical groundwater environments45. Consequently, this process has a dual effect. On the one hand, the continuous release of Ca2+ provides an essential ionic source for subsequent fluorine mineral precipitation46. On the other hand, the accumulation of Na+ on rock surfaces alters the interfacial electrical properties and structure of the electrical double layer, thereby influencing the adsorption behavior of subsequent anions47.

Competitive adsorption

Anion competition directly affects the instantaneous adsorption capacity of F−. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the variations in F− concentration (Δ[F−]) exhibited a significant negative correlation with Δ[Cl− + HCO3−], confirming the presence of competitive adsorption between F− and Cl−, HCO3− in high-salinity water–rock systems. Although F− generally exhibits a stronger affinity for mineral surfaces than Cl− and HCO3−, the latter anions at extremely high concentrations effectively compete for and occupy a large proportion of non-specific adsorption sites under high ionic strength conditions48. This mechanism also explains the superior performance of the Freundlich model compared with the Langmuir model, as the energy heterogeneity of adsorption sites was partly attributable to competitive occupation by different anions.

Mineral precipitation-dissolution

As shown in Fig. 7, γ(Na+)/γ(Cl−) < 1 indicates that in addition to halite dissolution, Na+ in the system extensively participates in reverse cation exchange49, resulting in a relatively lower concentration. The ratio γ(Ca2+)/γ(SO42−) > 1 suggests that Ca2+ originates not only from gypsum dissolution but also from carbonate dissolution and reverse cation exchange50. Similarly, γ(Ca2+ + Mg2+)/γ(HCO3− + SO42−) > 1 demonstrates that Ca2+ and Mg2+ are mainly derived from carbonate and gypsum dissolution as well as reverse cation exchange, whereas HCO3− and SO42− predominantly originate from carbonate rocks and gypsum51. Moreover, γ(Ca2+)/γ(F−) > 1, and the calculated ion product (Q = 1.35 × 10−6) far exceeds the solubility product of fluorite (Ksp = 3.45 × 10−11)52, suggests potential for fluorite precipitation.

Multi-mechanism coupling effect on F− evolution

According to the F− concentration variation patterns (Fig. 8), the migration and transformation of F− in high-salinity water–rock systems represent a dynamic process controlled by the coupling of multiple mechanisms, with the dominant reaction pathway exhibiting a pronounced dependence on reservoir lithology.

Variations in the F− concentration.

The fine sandstone–mudstone system was characterized by a relatively high clay content (22.30%), which can facilitate the instantaneous adsorption and fixation of substantial amounts of F− during the rapid adsorption stage53. During the mineral precipitation–cation exchange coupling stage, reverse cation exchange dominated F− mineral precipitation. Although interference from competitive adsorption by Cl− and HCO3− occurred during the 600–1200 min interval, the strong fixation capacity of this system resulted in only minor fluctuations in F− concentration, after which the water–rock system gradually stabilized. In contrast, the fine sandstone–muddy sandstone system exhibited a lower clay content (10.80%), leading to limited adsorption sites and consequently a shorter duration and lower capacity for surface chemical adsorption during the rapid adsorption stage. During the mineral precipitation–cation exchange coupling stage, the extremely high SAR values indicated that the intense reverse exchange continuously supplied Ca2+ for CaF2 formation. However, owing to the lower specific surface area, the generated precipitates readily reached “surface adsorption saturation”54. The reversible redissolution of precipitates occurred during periods such as 120–240 min, causing F− concentration rebound and sustained dynamic fluctuations. Subsequently, the amplitude of F− concentration variation decreased, and the system approached a relatively stable equilibrium. The medium sandstone–mudstone system exhibited the most complex kinetic behavior, characterized by pronounced hysteresis. During the mineral precipitation–cation exchange coupling stage, CaF2 precipitation was the dominant mechanism. During the 1200–1440 min interval, an anomalous increase in the F− concentration was observed, which was attributed to the heterogeneous nucleation and growth rates of precipitates on different mineral surfaces55. This resulted in a transient condition in which the precipitation rate was lower than the competitive adsorption rate during specific time periods19.

In summary, the migration and transformation of F− in high-salinity water–rock systems were not governed by a single mechanism but by the coupled effects of competitive adsorption, cation exchange, and mineral precipitation. F− removal in the fine sandstone–mudstone system primarily relied on the synergistic action of adsorption and mineral precipitation. The medium sandstone–mudstone system was dependent mainly on mineral precipitation but exhibited relatively weak fluorine fixation. The fine sandstone–muddy sandstone system also depended on precipitation but exhibited poor stability.

With increasing initial F− concentration, the sensitivity of F− removal decreased. The maximum unit gradient change rate was 0.20%/mg/L, indicating a higher sensitivity in the low concentration range of 20–50 mg/L. In the 50–100 and 100–150 mg/L ranges, the sensitivity declined substantially, with the unit gradient change rates decreasing to 0.05 and 0.03%/mg/L, respectively. This suggests that when the initial F− concentration exceeds 50 mg/L, active adsorption sites in the high-salinity water–rock system could approach saturation, resulting in limited variation in the removal efficiency.

The F− removal rate exhibited a V-shaped response to pH. Under acidic to neutral conditions (pH ≤ 6.5), the unit gradient change rate decreased from 1.22%/pH to 0.34%/pH. Under alkaline conditions (pH > 6.5), the removal rate declined markedly, and the unit gradient change rate became negative, with an absolute value reaching 2.78%/pH. This indicates that under strongly alkaline conditions, F− removal is highly sensitive to pH variation and that such conditions are unfavorable for fluoride binding57.

For clay content ranging from 8.04 to 9.74%, the unit gradient change rate was 0.83%/wt%, indicating strong sensitivity. As the clay content increased to 10.97%, the unit gradient change rate decreased to 0.33%/wt%. This suggests that under low clay content conditions, moderate increases in clay content effectively enhance the adsorption or co-precipitation capacity. However, beyond a certain threshold, the sensitivity diminishes, likely due to constraints imposed by kinetic processes or mass transfer limitations.

Based on the absolute values of the maximum unit gradient change rate (Fig. 9), the sensitivity of influencing factors decreased in the order of pH (2.78) > clay content (0.83) > initial F− concentration (0.20). Accordingly, under deep geological sequestration conditions for high-salinity coal mine water, pH was identified as the most sensitive factor, with an optimal range of 6.5–8.0, followed by clay content in rocks, with an optimal range of 9.74–10.97%. In contrast, the initial F− concentration was the least sensitive factor, with an optimal range of 50–100 mg/L.

It should be noted that direct mineralogical evidence (e.g., XRD, SEM‑EDS) for competitive adsorption and fluorite precipitation is lacking; future studies will include solid‑phase characterization to validate these mechanisms.

Implications for field application

The optimal pH range (6.5–8.0) and clay content (9.74–10.97%) identified in this study can serve as reference indicators for site selection and injection condition control in deep geological sequestration projects. Among the three lithologies, fine sandstone–mudstone exhibits the strongest F− fixation capacity and is therefore recommended as the preferred sequestration lithology. The high sensitivity of F− removal to pH suggests that field monitoring should prioritize pH control. However, direct extrapolation of these laboratory findings to field scale requires validation under dynamic flow conditions using intact core samples.

Conclusions

By integrating static hydrochemical experimental data with geochemical mechanism analysis, the migration and transformation behavior of F− during the deep geological sequestration of high-salinity coal mine water and the coupling effects among controlling mechanisms were elucidated, and the sensitivity of key influencing factors was evaluated. The main conclusions are as follows:

The adsorption of F− during the deep geological sequestration of high-salinity water was a complex process suggest dominated by multi-layer chemical interactions on heterogeneous surfaces. The adsorption process exhibited a distinct rapid–slow–equilibrium pattern and was effectively described by the pseudo-second-order kinetic and Freundlich thermodynamic models.

The adsorption of F− during the deep geological sequestration of high-salinity water was a complex process suggest dominated by multi-layer chemical interactions on heterogeneous surfaces. The adsorption process exhibited a distinct rapid–slow–equilibrium pattern and was effectively described by the pseudo-second-order kinetic and Freundlich thermodynamic models.

The migration and transformation of F − in high-salinity water–rock systems were governed by the coupled effects of competitive adsorption, cation exchange, and mineral precipitation. Specifically, F − removal in the fine sandstone–mudstone system depended on the synergistic action of adsorption and mineral precipitation, whereas F − removal in the medium sandstone–mudstone and fine sandstone–muddy sandstone systems was mainly controlled by mineral precipitation. The former showed relatively weak fluorine fixation, whereas the latter exhibited poor stability.

Under deep geological sequestration conditions for high-salinity coal mine water, the sensitivity of environmental factors affecting F − removal decreased in the order of pH > clay content in rocks > initial F − concentration. The optimal conditions for F − removal involved a pH range of 6.5–8.0, a clay content of 9.74–10.97%, and an initial F − concentration of 50–100 mg/L.
