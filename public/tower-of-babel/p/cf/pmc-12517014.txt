Additional arm-hand boost therapy (AHA-BOOST) in an inpatient rehabilitation setting during the subacute phase after stroke: protocol for a randomised controlled trial including a clinical, process and health economic evaluation
Authors: Liesel Cornelis, Lisa Cruycke, Sarah Meyer, Ann De Smedt, Maaike Fobelets, Marc Michielsen, Maaiken Vander Plaetse, Koen Putman, Geert Verheyden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionA considerable number of patients present with an upper limb impairment after stroke, which has a significant impact on daily life activities and quality of life. Currently, based on a Cochrane review, there is no high-quality evidence for any of the upper limb interventions used in routine practice. Therefore, the Jessa Hospital developed an additional arm-hand boost programme (AHA-BOOST), a novel, stratified intervention that integrates neurophysiological knowledge with an early start to optimise poststroke upper limb recovery.Methods and analysisThis multicentre randomised controlled trial with parallel design compares AHA-BOOST with a dose-matched programme for the lower limbs. Both interventions are in addition to usual care and consist of a combination of group sessions (1 hour per day, 5 times per week) and individualised therapy (two 30 min sessions per week) provided over 4 weeks. In total, 80 subacute stroke patients will be recruited from five rehabilitation centres in Belgium and randomised to one of both interventions. Patients will be followed up until 12 months post stroke to support a cost-utility analysis and assess long-term clinical effects of the programme. Various outcomes are collected at different time points: preintervention, immediately post intervention, 3 months post intervention, 6 months post stroke and 12 months post stroke. The Action Research Arm Test is the clinical primary outcome. The secondary outcomes are: Fugl-Meyer assessment—upper extremity, Stroke Upper Limb Capacity Scale, Barthel Index, modified Rankin Scale, Stroke Impact Scale and EuroQoL 5D-5L (EQ-5D-5L). The clinical analysis includes a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) analysis for comparing clinical changes between both groups and a subgroup analysis to explore the effects in different types of stroke survivors. Additionally, a process evaluation, guided by the Medical Research Council Framework, will assess key aspects of the implementation, including delivery (structures and resources), dose, reach, fidelity, adaptations, quality, mechanisms of change and the context. Both quantitative and qualitative data will be collected using the following tools during the intervention: therapy diaries for each session; video recordings of AHA-BOOST group sessions and after the intervention using: interviews with patients, therapists and management. The qualitative data will be assessed using content and thematic analysis and the quantitative data with descriptive statistics. Ultimately, in addition to the EQ-5D-5L, direct and indirect healthcare costs are collected for the health economic evaluation. A cost-utility analysis will be performed with a time horizon of 12 months post stroke comparing both therapies, and a Markov model will assess the cost-effectiveness of AHA-BOOST on a lifetime horizon. To test the robustness of the results, sensitivity and scenario analysis will be performed.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been obtained by different ethical committees (UZ/KU Leuven (central committee), UZ Gent, Gasthuiszusters Antwerpen and Maria Middelares). The study will be performed in accordance with the protocol, current International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) and Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) guidelines, and applicable regulatory requirements. The current approved protocol is version 5, dated 7 May 2025. Protocol amendments will be submitted to all involved ethical committees. The study results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and stakeholder reports.Trial registration numberNCT06517251.Keywords: Stroke, Randomized Controlled Trial, Inpatients, REHABILITATION MEDICINE, HEALTH ECONOMICS, Implementation Science

Introduction

Stroke is a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide, often resulting in upper limb impairments that significantly affect daily functioning and quality of life.1 Despite receiving standard rehabilitation, more than 40% of stroke survivors continue to experience upper limb deficits at 6 months post stroke and later.2 These lasting impairments create both a substantial clinical challenge and a major driver of long-term healthcare costs. With over 1.7 million new stroke cases annually in Europe, projections show stroke costs increasing to €86 billion by 2040.3,5 Since the level of disability following stroke is one of the main cost drivers, effective rehabilitation strategies have become essential for both patient outcomes and healthcare sustainability, highlighting the critical need to investigate non-conventional interventions to enhance upper limb recovery.6

A comprehensive network meta-analysis by Saikaley et al7 evaluated over 50 non-conventional interventions aimed to improve upper limb recovery after stroke. Among these, modified constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT) showed the highest probability of being the most effective.7 However, despite these findings, the Cochrane review by Pollock et al8 reported only moderate-quality evidence for CIMT and concluded that no intervention currently used as routine practice is supported by high-quality evidence.8 In addition, implementing CIMT as standard care faces several barriers: it requires intensive therapeutic resources and appears suitable only for a specific subgroup of stroke survivors.9

To address this treatment gap and provide effective intervention for a broader population of stroke survivors, the arm-hand boost programme (AHA-BOOST) was developed. The programme’s innovation lies in three key aspects that directly target limitations in current practice. First, it adopts a stratified approach based on the Jessa Sint-Ursula (JSU) diagram, which categorises patients into three groups according to their arm-hand function and relearning potential.10 Unlike the restrictive criteria used for CIMT therapy, the AHA-BOOST programme specifically targets the large middle group of patients with moderate to mild upper limb impairments. Second, the programme capitalises on early initiation during the critical window of heightened neuroplasticity when patients are most responsive to sensorimotor training.11,13 Third, it integrates current neurophysiological knowledge into a focused, high-dose, high-intensity protocol designed to optimally stimulate recovery for this specific patient population.9 To evaluate the clinical effects of this novel intervention, the first aim of this study is to perform an adequately powered, high-quality randomised controlled trial (RCT).

However, even when interventions like CIMT demonstrate effectiveness in RCTs, effect sizes alone do not offer policymakers insights into how an intervention can be replicated in their unique context or whether trial outcomes could be reproduced.14 This challenge is particularly relevant for complex interventions that require specific expertise, resources and organisational structures. Therefore, incorporating a process evaluation into the AHA-BOOST trial can provide valuable insights into fidelity, implementation quality, causal mechanisms and contextual factors influencing outcome variations.15 It can also help explain why the intervention fails unexpectedly or has unanticipated consequences and how it can be optimised.16 To strengthen the evidence of the AHA-BOOST programme and to give policymakers a complete insight into the process of implementation, a complete process evaluation of the AHA-BOOST programme is the second aim of this study.

In addition to understanding implementation processes, policymakers also require economic evidence to make informed decisions about resource allocation and reimbursement. The systematic review of Craig et al17 included 21 studies that performed an economic evaluation in stroke rehabilitation, in which only one study evaluated a specific stroke rehabilitation intervention (ie, the AVERT trial), the others evaluated stroke rehabilitation services (eg, early-supported discharge service, community-based rehabilitation, home-based rehabilitation).17 The costing of the economic evaluations was often limited to short-term resource use and the costs of the delivery of the intervention and neglected the societal perspective.17 It is crucial to consider the societal perspective when conducting a health economic evaluation, as it encompasses all relevant viewpoints.18 Evaluations that focus solely on direct costs risk being biased and may fail to capture the full benefits of interventions for society. Additionally, excluding the societal perspective can lead to suboptimal resource allocation, potentially resulting in significant losses in overall societal welfare.18 Therefore, a final aim of this study is to conduct a health economic evaluation alongside the AHA-BOOST clinical trial to contribute to the current literature and to enable policymakers to make an informed decision regarding the implementation of the AHA-BOOST programme in standard-of-care and its reimbursement.

Summarised, the overall aim of this phase III trial is to perform a clinical evaluation, accompanied by a process evaluation and health economic evaluation, of the AHA-BOOST programme.

Methods

Study design

The AHA-BOOST trial is an assessor-blinded phase III RCT with a parallel-group design (see figure 1). In total, 80 patients will be randomised to either the experimental group (AHA-BOOST) (n=40) or the dose-matched control group (L-BOOST) (n=40). Randomisation will be stratified for centre. The study is approved by the ethics committee research of the University Hospital Leuven (UZ/KU Leuven) and local ethics committees of the different clinical sites (rehabilitation centre RevArte, general hospital Maria Middelares and university hospital Gent) and conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.

Patient selection and recruitment

Patients are recruited from different inpatient stroke rehabilitation centres in Belgium: (1) rehabilitation centre K7 of university hospital Gent, (2) general hospital Maria Middelares, (3) rehabilitation centre Revales, (4) rehabilitation campus Pellenberg of university hospital Leuven and (5) rehabilitation centre RevArte.

The following inclusion criteria are used: maximally 5 months after a first-ever unilateral, supratentorial stroke (ischaemic or haemorrhagic); minimally 18 years old; a residual inpatient stay of minimally 4 weeks; the ability to sit independently; motor impairment in the upper limb defined based on the JSU diagram,10 which stratifies patients into recovery groups based on the Fugl-Meyer Assessment of the Upper Extremity (FMA-UE). Patients are eligible for inclusion if a score of 8–17 is achieved on stage 2 (volitional movement within synergies) of part A or a score of <8 on stage 2, part A, in combination with a score >6 on stage 5 (hand) of part C. Patients are excluded based on the following criteria: severe communication deficits defined as a score of <24 out of 32 on the ‘visual sentence comprehension’ subscale of the Comprehensive Aphasia Test; 19 severe cognitive deficits defined as a score of <18 out of 30 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment.11 20

Screening for eligibility is performed by healthcare staff as potential participants undergo inpatient rehabilitation at the clinical study sites. If a patient is found to be eligible, healthcare staff provide them with trial information. Afterwards, the blinded assessors contact the patients to provide additional information and answer questions and to sign the informed consent (online supplemental file A).

Sample size calculation

A power calculation was executed based on our primary research question, namely, the evaluation of the short-term clinical effect of the AHA-BOOST programme versus the L-BOOST programme on arm-hand capacity, measured by the action research arm test (ARAT) (see the Clinical evaluation section). In the proof-of-concept RCT,11 the mean change in ARAT scores between preintervention and post intervention was 7.8 points higher in the AHA-BOOST group compared with the control group.11 The SD of change from preintervention to post intervention was 11.7 points for the ARAT scale.11 With 74 patients, the comparison between the experimental and the control group for change from preintervention to post intervention will have 80% power to detect an average difference of at least 7.8 points at the 5% two-sided level of significance, assuming a SD of at most 11.7 points. To account for an expected dropout of at most 8%, 80 patients will be recruited.21

Randomisation and blinding

To ensure the rigour of the study, the following randomisation procedures have been established: the included patients (n=80) are randomly assigned to one of two groups, namely, the experimental group ‘AHA-BOOST’ (n=40) or the control group ‘L-BOOST’ (n=40). Patients are stratified for study site to balance the distribution of patients between the two treatment groups over the different study sites. Allocation concealment is used. Randomisation lists were prepared in advance and randomisation is performed by a member of the study team who is not involved in patient contact and data collection. A block size of four is used. To ensure objectivity and prevent bias, the assessors will be blinded for treatment allocation.

Trial treatment

Both interventions (AHA-BOOST and L-BOOST) consist of an equal amount of additional therapy: 24 hours delivered over 4 weeks, 5 days a week, in the inpatient rehabilitation setting. In both groups, these 24 hours are a combination of 20 hours of BOOST group sessions and 4 hours of individualised therapy under minimal supervision. Interventions are delivered by trained therapists at the clinical sites using an interdisciplinary approach, with physiotherapists and occupational therapists collaborating towards shared treatment goals for each participant. While individual sessions are conducted by a single therapist at a time, the disciplines alternate and maintain continuity through structured communication.

Experimental intervention

The experimental group (n=40) receives the AHA-BOOST programme (24 hours), consisting of a combination of 20 1-hour group sessions and 4 hours of individual technology-supported therapy provided over 4 weeks.

A participant receives one AHA-BOOST group session a day, 5 days a week, with a maximum of two patients per therapist. These sessions are focused on four key aspects: neurophysiology, sequences of reaching and grasping, de-weighting of the arm and orientation of the hand towards objects. The different exercises are tailored to the individual patient, based on ongoing assessment (Model of Bobath Clinical Practice), discussion within the group of therapists and individual treatment goals of the patient.22 The material used in a BOOST group session includes standard rehabilitation material such as different sizes of balls, cones, hoops, beads and daily objects. Additionally, participants exercise two times 30 min per week with a technology-supported device, focusing on finger and hand rehabilitation. Different upper limb rehabilitation devices can be used, such as the AMADEO (Tyromotion, Austria) or E-LINK (Biometrics, UK). In general, a combination of passive mobilisation, assistive therapy and gaming is provided. A therapist is present throughout each session to provide feedback. The Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist was completed to provide a standardised representation of the AHA-BOOST programme23 (online supplemental file B).

The quality of the AHA-BOOST programme is monitored during the study using various methods: (1) video recordings of two randomly assigned AHA-BOOST group sessions per patient will be reviewed by specialised therapists; (2) therapy diaries documenting the various exercises, including starting positions, type of exercises and an estimation of the total active time; (3) regularly scheduled sessions within a community of practice for the therapists to address questions and provide feedback on the implementation process and (4) semistructured interviews with participants and therapists to explore the level of satisfaction, acceptability, perceived benefits, relevance and suggestions for improvement of the programme. Additionally, treating therapists record patients’ attendance at each therapy session. Study therapy sessions are incorporated into the usual care treatment schemes of the patients to enhance fidelity.

Control intervention

The dose-matched control group (n=40) receives the L-BOOST programme in the inpatient rehabilitation setting. This programme consists of a combination of 20 1-hour group sessions and a personalised two times 30 min self-exercise programme per week, for 4 weeks.

The L-BOOST intervention consists of a programme of strengthening exercises for the lower limbs and general reconditioning. During the group sessions, circuit-class training is performed according to a standardised written protocol: 20 min of cycling on a sitting bike using the THERA-Trainer tigo (Thera-Trainer, Germany) or alike, 20 min of strengthening exercises for muscles around hip and knee (eg,sit-to-stand training), 10 min of knee exercises using quadriceps bench in free swing mode and 10 min of leg press exercises using the Minivector (Easytech, Italy) or alike. Again, a gradual increase in levels of difficulty is provided. Additionally, each patient receives a self-exercise programme for the rehabilitation of the lower limbs (balance; strengthening of foot, knee and hip muscles; walking exercises), which is executed two times 30 min per week under minimal supervision of a therapist. None of the exercises involves the use of the upper limb. The TIDieR checklist was completed to provide a standardised representation of the L-BOOST programme23 (online supplemental file C).

The quality of the L-BOOST programme is monitored using a therapy diary, recording the presence of the patient in each of the sessions and any deviations from the protocol. The study sessions are also incorporated into the usual care treatment of the patients to improve fidelity.

Usual care

During the intervention, patients receive the study therapy (AHA-BOOST or L-BOOST) in addition to their conventional usual care programme. Throughout the study, the usual care programme remains unchanged. Usual care is defined as the standard practices, treatments and interventions used in the different rehabilitation centres. It consists of different therapies such as physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, neuropsychology and sports therapy and is tailored to the needs of each participant based on stroke severity and treatment goals. To monitor potential variability, a structured diary is used. This records the type, frequency and duration of the different usual care interventions and documents the content of occupational and physiotherapy throughout the 4-week intervention period. The diary is completed by the healthcare staff and is developed by the research team based on expert opinions.

To prevent contamination of the experimental therapy in the control group, therapists trained in AHA-BOOST will not be permitted to deliver conventional therapy to any of the study participants for the duration of their involvement in the study.

Adverse events

The patients are asked to report any adverse event to the healthcare staff. These incidents are documented in an adverse events log, specifying the severity of the event as well as an estimation of the causality to the study interventions. When an adverse event directly or indirectly related to the study participation is reported and additional care is required, the associated costs will be covered by the study’s insurance. A procedure has been established that outlines the various steps to be followed in reporting adverse events to the appropriate regulatory committees. Based on the proof-of-concept RCT, the risk of adverse events is estimated to be low.11

Patient and public involvement

People with lived experience offered valuable feedback to the study team on the protocol, including the feasibility of the therapy, the administration load of the study and the patient participation process. They also reviewed and provided input on the different study documents that patients are required to complete during the study. Adjustments were made based on this feedback. In addition, an expert panel was created and will remain involved throughout the full course of this study. This panel includes a number of people with stroke.

Clinical evaluation

This first work package aims to explore the clinical effects of the AHA-BOOST programme on various levels of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) immediately post intervention and after follow-up.24 Therefore, patients are tested at four different time points: (T1) preintervention; (T2) immediately post intervention; (T3) 3 months post intervention and (T4) 12 months post stroke (figure 1). The assessors are blinded to treatment allocation and receive an assessor training package to standardise clinical assessment. The results from the clinical evaluation will be published in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines for RCTs.25

Outcome measurements

Different outcome measurements are collected and shown in figure 2. The primary outcome is the ARAT, recommended in stroke trials, measuring arm-hand capacity at ICF activity level.26 27 This test consists of 19 items, divided into four subcategories (grasp, grip, pinch and gross movement), and evaluates both proximal and distal motor function. Each item will be scored on a four-point ordinal scale (0, 1, 2, 3) with a maximum of 57, indicating good upper limb performance.28 The primary endpoint is the postintervention time point, that is, after 4 weeks of additional therapy.

At baseline, patients’ demographics (eg, age, gender, hand dominance) and stroke-related data (eg, stroke timing, location and type) are documented. Additionally, various factors such as cognition, neglect and motivation are collected to explore their impact on different outcome measures. These variables were selected based on the prior literature and clinical expertise to ensure that all known potential confounding factors were considered. Motivation will be monitored before intervention, mid-intervention and after 4 weeks of intervention, using the Motivation In Stroke Patients for Rehabilitation Scale (MORE-scale).35

Data collection

The clinical data are collected by two trained, blinded assessors at four different time points (figure 2). During data collection, a standardised case report form is completed. This form includes researchers’ notes, contextual information and the results of the standardised clinical tests and questionnaires. The demographic and stroke-related data are collected by the clinical sites. In order to ensure data quality, this information is verified and, when necessary, clarified with the patient by the blinded assessors. To minimise missing data, weekly meetings are held with the individuals responsible for this aspect of data collection.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses will be conducted according to a predefined Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP), developed in collaboration with a senior biostatistician from the KU Leuven. The SAP will be finalised and approved prior to database lock and unblinding.

The clinical data will be analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.26.0. An intention-to-treat analysis will be performed, followed by an exploratory sensitivity analysis where patients within the lowest quartile of compliance (total duration of therapy in the intervention period) will be excluded. For both analyses, normality will first be assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test to determine whether assumptions for parametric analysis are satisfied. The main analysis will compare the change from baseline in ARAT between patients in the experimental and control group. Change from baseline will be estimated with a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) model, with time and treatment as main effects and with time by treatment interaction. The model will be corrected for the stratification variable centre. All observed longitudinal measurements from patients initially included in the trial, irrespective of whether patients prematurely dropped out, will be included in the analysis. Inferences will be valid under the assumption that dropout is missing at random, meaning that the probability of missingness may depend on observed data but not on unobserved data. Sensitivity analyses using multiple imputations will be conducted to study the robustness of the results under realistic deviations from the missingness at random assumption. The same process will be used for the secondary outcome measures. Finally, subgroup analyses will be conducted to explore the effects of the interventions in different types of stroke survivors, based on cognition, neglect and motivation. If there appears to be different effects in different subgroups, this will be investigated using interaction or trend tests rather than the statistical significance of the result for the individual subgroup.

Data collection

Depending on the objective, different data collection methods will be applied. As discussed above, the first objective is to explore if the AHA-BOOST programme was delivered as intended. In order to explore this objective, structured diaries (PE2) are used to assess the content of the therapy, the number and type of exercises given, the length of therapy, etc. Semistructured interviews (PE2 and PE3) are used to assess the quality of the therapy experienced by the patients and therapists. Therapists will also be interviewed to discuss the adaptations that were made to the rehabilitation therapy (PE3). The semistructured interview guides were developed by the research team, based on the literature and expert opinions.36,38 In order to get more insight into the content of the intervention therapy, 80 therapy sessions of 1 hour will be videotaped and analysed with an observation scheme by clinicians from the Jessa Hospital who developed the AHA-BOOST programme and provided training to trial therapists (PE2). This observation scheme was developed by the research team, discussed with experts and tested by expert therapists during rehabilitation sessions.

The second objective is to understand whether, how and why the therapy had an impact by exploring patients’ and healthcare providers’ perspectives of the programme. The clinical effectiveness of the AHA-BOOST trial can express the impact on the clinical parameters of these patients. Semistructured interviews with patients and AHA-BOOST therapists will be used to understand the perspectives of the causal mechanisms of the therapy and to gather suggestions on how to roll out the therapy (PE2 and PE3). The videotapes (PE2), as described above, will be analysed to identify and understand the variations in outcomes and different contexts.

The third objective is to explore the context and the interaction between the programme and the real-world context by exploring the facilitators and barriers of the implementation and sustainability of the AHA-BOOST programme, including recommendations for future implementation. Semistructured interviews with patients, AHA-BOOST therapists and management will be performed to explore those facilitators and barriers regarding the AHA-BOOST programme (PE2 and PE3). The aim is to interview 12 interviewees per respondent group (patients, AHA-BOOST therapists and management staff). Approximately 48 interviews will be conducted, although the final numbers will be determined by data saturation.39 Interviews will be preferably conducted face-to-face, depending on the availability of the interviewees; interviews can also be conducted online. Interviews will be audio recorded, transcribed verbatim. Throughout the entire trial period, communities of practice will be organised in the respective rehabilitation centres, allowing AHA-BOOST therapists to interact with Jessa therapists, who are experts in delivering the AHA-BOOST programme. During these sessions, facilitators and barriers will be identified and collected through field notes.

The fourth objective is to explore the transferability of results and the sustainability of the programme, by exploring stakeholders’ (patient, AHA-BOOST therapists, local PI, principal physiotherapist and principal occupational therapist) perceived experiences and impact. The latter will include a quantitative approach on the data and a full health economic evaluation (as described in the Health economic evaluation section) to assess the reimbursement of the therapy. Semistructured interviews with patients, treating physicians and the management will be performed to explore their expectations regarding the sustainability of the programme (PE2 and PE3).

The last objective is to assess and refine the logic model if needed. At the completion of the data analysis, all results from the process evaluation components (PE1–PE3) will be systematically reviewed and synthesised to evaluate the validity and accuracy of the original logic model. The structured diaries, semistructured interviews, videotaped sessions and field notes from communities of practice will be analysed collectively to determine whether the assumed causal pathways, contextual factors and mechanisms of change align with the observed implementation. If discrepancies are identified, the logic model will be refined to better reflect the actual processes and relationships discovered during the evaluation.

Data analysis

The integration of quantitative and qualitative data will follow a convergent parallel mixed methods design, where both data types are collected concurrently and integrated during interpretation.

Data analysis is performed by the blinded assessor from the Vrije Universiteit Brussel, who is responsible for the process evaluation. This researcher is guided by an expert in qualitative research. To ensure rigour and reduce bias, two researchers will independently code qualitative data, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus.

Quantitative data, collected during the trial monitoring and by using structured diaries (PE2), will be analysed using R statistical software. Descriptive statistics (mean, SD, frequencies, percentages) will be used to report implementation fidelity measures including therapy session frequency, duration, content adherence and protocol deviations.

Qualitative data will be analysed using content analysis (a deductive analysis based on the defined indicators) and thematic analysis (an inductive analysis where themes will be generated from the data by the researchers using a generic qualitative approach).40 Initial deductive content analysis will be guided by predefined theoretical frameworks: the Theory of Reasoned Action for patient interviews (examining attitudes, subjective norms and behavioural intentions) and the Theory of Planned Behaviour for therapist and management interviews (examining attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioural control and implementation intentions). Subsequently, inductive thematic analysis using Braun and Clarke’s approach will identify emergent themes not captured by the theoretical frameworks.40

All interviews will be analysed using NVivo V.1.7 software. Data saturation will be assessed continuously, with additional interviews conducted if needed. Member checking will be performed with therapists and management participants to validate key findings.

Systematic triangulation will manage the complexity of multiple data sources: (1) methodological triangulation will compare objective fidelity measures from diaries with subjective fidelity perceptions from interviews to validate implementation quality; (2) data source triangulation will compare perspectives across patient, therapist and management groups using structured comparison approaches; (3) investigator triangulation will involve independent coding of transcripts by two researchers with consensus meetings to resolve discrepancies; (4) theoretical triangulation will apply the research team’s multidisciplinary expertise (rehabilitation and implementation science) to interpret findings from different theoretical perspectives. Integration meetings will be held during data collection and analysis to systematically combine quantitative fidelity findings with qualitative contextual explanations.

Health economic evaluation

To estimate the cost-effectiveness of the AHA-BOOST programme a trial-based cost-utility analysis, comparing costs and outcomes of the AHA-BOOST group and L-BOOST group, with a time horizon at 1 year post stroke will be performed, supplemented with a Markov model to estimate the cost-effectiveness over a lifetime horizon.

Cost-utility analysis

This evaluation will incorporate costs and health-related QoL during the trial and follow-up period, adopting healthcare and societal perspectives. At the cost site, the evaluation will focus on the direct medical and non-medical costs and on indirect costs depending on the perspective. The direct medical costs encompass all costs for treatment and follow-up from the healthcare perspective and all out-of-pocket payments by the patient. Direct non-medical costs include transport costs and home care help, whereas indirect costs include productivity loss (eg, the number of days away from work). The productivity losses of the patients and productivity losses due to informal care will be valued using the human capital approach.41 The EQ-5D-5L questionnaire will be used to derive utilities based on the Belgian tariffs.34 42 In the AHA-BOOST trial, data collection concerning the costs and resource use occurs during the inpatient stay (HE1), at discharge (HE2), 6 months post stroke (HE3) and 12 months post stroke (HE4) (figure 1). QoL data are collected during the timepoints of the clinical trial (T1, T2, T3 and T4) (figure 2). Therefore, the time horizon used for this cost-utility analysis is from the first additional therapy session up until 12 months post stroke.

Data collection

Data on resource use are collected through invoices from the rehabilitation centres (HE1), diaries (HE2) and questionnaires (HE3 and HE4) (figure 2). Health-related QoL data are collected through the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire at T1, T2, T3 and T4 (figure 2).34 To document the resource utilisation and costs of patients residing in the rehabilitation centre, invoices are gathered from the respective rehabilitation centres of these patients. After discharge, each patient fills out a healthcare utilisation diary up until 6 months post stroke (HE2). The following types of healthcare resource use are documented: primary care visits (regular visits as well as out of hours), emergency visits, inpatient stays, outpatient services, medication use, transport costs and the estimation of amount of informal care. At 6 months post stroke (HE3), the following data are collected by completing a questionnaire: home modifications, special equipment and aids and socioeconomic status. Between 6 months and 12 months post stroke, a prompt is provided for the patients in order to document their healthcare uses during those 6 months. At 12 months post stroke (HE4), the patients complete the last questionnaire. In this questionnaire, the same data will be collected as in the diary and 6 months post stroke questionnaire supplemented with days away from work and social benefits (eg, sickness leave payments). All the data collection instruments are developed by the research team, based on literature, expert and patient opinions.43 In order to estimate the costs corresponding to the resource use of the patients several tariff lists will be used: national standardised public tariffs lists, ambulatory tariffs lists, national pharmaceutical price lists, national cost per working hour and transportation costs.44 45

Data analysis

The cost-effectiveness of the intervention will be expressed in incremental cost per quality-adjusted life years (QALY), based on the trial data with a time horizon of 1 year. The incremental cost per QALY will be calculated as a ratio of (Expected Cost Experimental – Expected Cost Control)/(Expected Outcome Experimental – Expected Outcome Control). The robustness of the results will be analysed by probabilistic sensitivity analyses and scenario analysis on the cost as well as on the outcome. Several subgroup analyses will also be conducted according to specific patient subgroups, which will be categorised based on age, socioeconomic status, stroke severity and total rehabilitation dose. Tornado diagrams will be used to measure the impact of individual components in healthcare utilisation. Probabilistic sensitivity analyses by bootstrapping with replacement will be employed to test the robustness of the results, using a minimum of 1000 iterations to obtain 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles of the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio distribution. Discounting of the costs is not needed because all costs occur within 1 year. A lot of resource use will be collected during this trial; therefore, a large proportion of patients may have at least one item of missing data. A preferred approach to deal with this missing data is imputation, which will be used during data analysis of the AHA-BOOST trial data.46 R will be used as a statistical programme. The analysis will be performed in accordance with the Belgian health economic analysis guidelines.47 The analysis will be reported based on the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards reporting guidance.48

Markov model

The conventional practice guidelines for cost-effectiveness analysis suggest employing a time horizon extensive enough to encompass all costs and outcomes, particularly for interventions addressing chronic conditions, often recommending a lifetime horizon.49 50 Kim et al similarly advocate for using a lifetime horizon in the base case analysis, ensuring all consequences are captured far into the future.49 The International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) task force states that the absence of long-term follow-up data should not justify the failure to extend the time horizon to a relevant duration.50 Therefore, as our trial only follows patients for up to 12 months post stroke, a Markov model will be developed, defining the health states in chronic stroke, to estimate the cost-effectiveness of the AHA-BOOST programme over a lifetime horizon. Overall, authors agree that health economic evaluations, based on an analytic decision model, should be used in order to evaluate new health programmes, in our case AHA-BOOST. Historically, Markov models were used in epidemiological and clinical studies.51 Due to the improvement of computers and the availability of specific software, Markov models are now frequently adopted in the healthcare setting. The strength of using Markov models in health economic evaluations is that the model takes into account the use of resources and the outcomes.49 50

Data collection

Estimations on resource use and utilities for each health state are derived from the international peer-reviewed literature. Special attention is paid to the transferability of the data to the Flemish healthcare context. This will be done by validation checks within the research consortium and advisory board.

Data analysis

Similar to the 1 year model, probabilistic sensitivity analyses will be used to account for uncertainty around the input parameters. R will be used as a statistical programme to develop the model, to calculate the incremental cost per QALY and to perform the probability sensitivity analysis.

Discussion

This study wants to investigate for subacute stroke patients if the AHA-BOOST programme compared to the dose-matched L-BOOST programme is beneficial for enhancing arm-hand activity and QoL post-treatment and long term, is cost-effective and can be implemented in other rehabilitation centres.

At the start of this study, we hypothesise that: (1) AHA-BOOST will result in a significantly greater improvement in arm-hand activity post-intervention, at follow-up and 12 months post stroke compared to control therapy (L-BOOST); (2) AHA-BOOST will result in a significantly greater improvement in upper limb function, performance, independence and activity of daily living and participation post-intervention, at follow up and 12 months post stroke; (3) Investing in 24 hours of extra arm-hand therapy for subacute stroke patient in the inpatient rehabilitation setting is cost-effective.

By creating the study protocol, different core elements of evidence-based research are used.52 Initially, the protocol is based on consensus-based core recommendations, previous high-quality research and our proof-of-concept, a pilot RCT conducted at the Jessa Hospital.11 53 54 In the following step, the protocol was refined based on therapists’ experience and patients’ feedback and preferences. This close collaboration between scientific researchers, clinical experts and patients is seen as a first strength of this project. Another strength of the general AHA-BOOST project is that it covers the required phases suggested by the MRC framework. This study builds further on earlier research, including development and first assessment of the intervention, and will deliver outcome for the next phase, namely, evaluation of the intervention, incorporating outcome, economic and process evaluation.10 11 A third strength is the extensive clinical evaluation based on the different domains of the ICF. In this way, a holistic view of the patients is created considering difficulties in motor function, activities of daily living and participation in everyday life. The inclusion of a comprehensive process evaluation, guided by the updated MRC framework, is another strength.14 Rehabilitation of the upper limb after stroke is a complex process and research in this field is equally challenging.8 Even when positive outcomes are observed, they may not always be transferable to other rehabilitation settings due to implementation challenges.14 Therefore, this process evaluation is a valuable contribution to the literature. Lastly, by performing a health economic evaluation with a societal perspective, there will be strong evidence concerning the cost-effectiveness of the AHA-BOOST programme, which can help policymakers to make an informed decision regarding the further implementation of the AHA-BOOST programme.

On the other hand, we are aware of some limitations. First, only five rehabilitation centres are involved in patient recruitment. This can possibly impact the generalisability of the result and the replicability of the AHA-BOOST programme. By performing a process evaluation, insights will be given into all the mechanisms of change in the intervention that impact the outcome measures.15 Thanks to those insights, generalisability and transferability of the results will be made possible. Second, the variability in usual care in the rehabilitation centres can possibly impact the clinical outcomes and the overall perception of patients (eg, programme intensity). Therefore, the total hours of multidisciplinary therapy are documented as well as the content of physiotherapy and occupational therapy. Patients will also be interviewed concerning the overall perception of the intervention, and these results can be compared between both centres. They may also identify if this is a confounding factor. Lastly, by using therapy for the lower limb as a control therapy, a positive outcome in favour of AHA-BOOST for the primary outcome measure may potentially be overestimated. By comparing the results of the process evaluation and the clinical evaluation, a possible overestimation can be revealed and explained.

With this study, we hope to make a meaningful impact on the future rehabilitation of subacute stroke patients. By providing therapists with specific expertise in AHA-BOOST and creating a realization blueprint and roadmap for implementing this, we hope that AHA-BOOST can be used in the daily organization of different rehabilitation centres in Belgium and beyond.
