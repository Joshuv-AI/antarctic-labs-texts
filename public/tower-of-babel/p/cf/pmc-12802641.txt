Transcatheter Valve-In-Valve Implantation in Patients With Fluoroscopically Invisible Tricuspid Valve Prostheses
Authors: Dzhem Farandzha, Nikolay Stoyanov, Vasil Velchev
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTricuspid bioprostheses are prone to structural valve degeneration, often requiring complex interventions. Surgical reoperation carries high morbidity and mortality, whereas transcatheter valve-in-valve implantation offers a safer alternative. However, when the tricuspid prosthesis is fluoroscopically invisible, accurate valve positioning becomes challenging. This case series presents 3 such patients successfully treated using a novel adjunctive technique. A coronary guidewire was positioned in the right coronary artery as an anatomical marker for the atrioventricular groove. Balloon pre-dilatation was then performed to delineate the bioprosthetic valve's position, guiding precise deployment of the new valve. All procedures utilized Myval prostheses and were completed without major complications. Post-procedural echocardiography confirmed optimal valve function, low transvalvular gradients, and no residual regurgitation. This reproducible approach of combining RCA guidewire marking with balloon pre-dilatation offers an accurate, low-risk solution for managing fluoroscopically invisible tricuspid prostheses during valve-in-valve implantation.Key words: case series, fluoroscopically invisible, tricuspid valve prostheses, valve-in-valve, radiolucent

Structural valve degeneration of tricuspid prostheses represents a significant clinical challenge, often necessitating complex diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. The degeneration is characterized by irreversible intrinsic modifications in the prosthetic valve, including progressive wear, pannus formation, fibrosis, calcification, and distortion or fracture of supporting structures such as stents and struts. Compared with mechanical prostheses, bioprosthetic valves are more susceptible to structural failure.1 The primary anatomical locations involved in bioprosthetic degeneration are the leaflets and stents, where calcific deposits may cause leaflet wear and disruption, ultimately compromising valvular function.1

Traditional surgical interventions for tricuspid valve dysfunction carry high morbidity and mortality, particularly in reoperative settings.2 Transcatheter valve-in-valve implantation has emerged as a viable, less invasive alternative.3 However, there are few studies on situations in which the surgical tricuspid bioprosthesis is fluoroscopically undetectable, making precise valve-in-valve positioning difficult. In this case series, we describe 3 patients with radiolucent prostheses and significant tricuspid valve failure. We present a novel method that uses a right coronary artery (RCA) guidewire as a marker to help with alignment during balloon predilatation. The management of this complex condition is guided by the sharing of clinical information, procedural methods, and results.

Case Series

Three elderly patients who were assessed at University Hospital St Anna (Sofia, Bulgaria) between February 2022 and August 2024 are included in this case series. All three patients presented with chief complaints of shortness of breath and fatigue, and a history of surgical tricuspid valve replacement. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed severe valve dysfunction in all three patients. Due to high operative risk, the patients were evaluated as possible candidates for transcatheter valve-in-valve implantation. During their diagnostic work-up, which included coronary angiography, radiographic imaging (including computed tomography) failed to optimally visualize the prostheses, posing a challenge in performing valve-in-valve implantation. Furthermore, the commonly used valve-in-valve apps (software applications) were not helpful, as they lacked specific information for the bioprosthetic valve sizes used in our patients.4 Thus, in all 3 patients, valve sizing was guided by a combination of TTE, surgical records, and prosthesis label information, given the suboptimal visualization on CT. Although contrast-enhanced CT was performed in each patient, it was not electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated, which likely contributed to motion artifacts and inadequate assessment of the prosthetic valve structure. This limitation is particularly evident in the tricuspid position, where image quality is often more affected by right-sided chamber motion and complex anatomy. As such, prosthesis sizing relied primarily on known surgical valve types and sizes (Labcor 31, 33, and 31 mm), confirmed through available operative reports and TTE measurements of annular dimensions. This approach, although less precise than ECG-gated CT, was sufficient in these patients and may be appropriate when high-quality CT imaging is not available or interpretable.

Technique Description

The procedure includes radial artery access for RCA guidewire placement and femoral vein access for valve deployment. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) may be intentionally avoided to allow the use of conscious sedation and local anesthesia, minimizing patient discomfort and the risks associated with general anesthesia. To identify the atrioventricular groove, a coronary guidewire is first inserted into the RCA. The deteriorated bioprosthesis is then predilated with a balloon. Although it may result in an apparent misalignment of the transcatheter valve with the bioprosthetic plane during deployment, a stiff wire in the right or left pulmonary artery facilitates balloon and valve delivery. Alternatively, a preformed wire, such as the Safari wire (Boston Scientific), can be placed in the right ventricle. Such wires may require further reshaping by adding a proximal curve before placement to ensure optimal positioning and stability during valve deployment.

To facilitate accurate deployment, the tricuspid valve ring is marked by aligning the RCA guidewire with the waist of the balloon using the right anterior oblique view during balloon predilatation. After position confirmation, the valvuloplasty balloon is removed, leaving the RCA guidewire aligned with the plane of the prosthesis ring. Then, at a ratio of 20:80, a new balloon-expandable valve is implanted, with 20% in the right atrium and 80% in the right ventricle across the annulus. Although it is not usually necessary in tricuspid valve-in-valve procedures, rapid pacing may be employed to stabilize the valve during deployment.

The combination of RCA guidewire placement and balloon dilatation for prosthesis marking provides a reproducible and effective strategy to overcome the challenges of poor prosthetic valve visualization, ensuring precise positioning and successful valve-in-valve implantation. The practical implementation and versatility of this technique in actual clinical settings are demonstrated by the following 3 cases.

Patient 1

A 60-year-old male patient presented with shortness of breath, fatigue, and peripheral edema. The patient had undergone a coronary artery bypass grafting (left internal mammary artery-to-left anterior descending artery) and dual bioprosthetic valve replacements (23-mm Labcor for the aortic and 31-mm Labcor for the tricuspid) despite his age 7 years ago. Severe tricuspid valve regurgitation and restenosis were detected on TTE (mean gradient: 11 mm Hg). Neither prosthesis could be visualized during diagnostic angiography, prompting the performance of a CT scan to prepare for a possible valve-in-valve procedure. Although the latter provided some visualization of the prosthetic valve and confirmed the proximity of the RCA to the tricuspid annulus, it was not ECG-gated, which likely contributed to suboptimal image quality for valve sizing (Figure 1A). Therefore, valve sizing was primarily based on surgical records, prosthesis label information, and TTE.

The procedure was planned to be performed by placing a coronary guidewire in the RCA (Figure 1B). A 9F CPS catheter (Abbott Laboratories) was used to cannulate the coronary sinus to position a left ventricular cardiac resynchronization therapy lead as a temporary electrode in the anterolateral vein for rapid pacing. This was done to avoid crossing the tricuspid valve and trapping the pacemaker electrode between the surgically implanted valve and the transcatheter valve to be implanted. Predilatation was performed with a 25-mm Mammoth balloon dilatation catheter (Meril Life Sciences), and the position of the valve was determined (Figures 1C and 1D, Videos 1 and 2). A tricuspid valve-in-valve procedure was performed with a 27.5-mm Myval (Meril Life Sciences) (Figures 2A and 2B, Video 3).

Postimplantation right ventriculography and TTE confirmed normal valve function, with a mean transvalvular gradient of 4 mm Hg and no residual tricuspid regurgitation (Video 4). Additionally, invasive hemodynamic measurements demonstrated the restoration of favorable hemodynamics after valve-in-valve implantation (Figures 2C and 2D). The patient was discharged 3 days after the procedure, and significant symptom improvement was noted during follow-up visits.

Patient 2

A 73-year-old woman with a history of tricuspid valve replacement (33-mm Labcor) for Ebstein anomaly 10 years prior presented with worsening dyspnea and peripheral edema. Severe prosthetic valve dysfunction with significant regurgitation and stenosis was observed on TTE. The preprocedural peak and mean transvalvular gradients were 27 and 12 mm Hg, respectively. Radiographic imaging failed to visualize the tricuspid prosthesis (Figure 3A, Video 5), and an invasive gradient was also noted (Figure 3B).

(B) Preprocedural hemodynamic measurements in the right atrium (yellow tracing) and right ventricle (red tracing). A diastolic gradient is clearly seen (dashed area).

Transcatheter valve-in-valve implantation with a 32-mm Myval was planned. Rapid pacing was deemed unnecessary. Nevertheless, as previously described, a coronary guidewire was placed in the RCA in preparation for balloon dilatation (Figures 4A and 4B). Predilatation was performed using a 28-mm Mammoth balloon dilatation catheter. The procedure was successfully completed, with no residual regurgitation (Figure 4C, Video 6).

Postprocedural TTE showed a peak-to-mean transvalvular gradient of 9/4 mm Hg and confirmed the absence of tricuspid regurgitation. The day after the intervention, a sinoatrial block was detected on the surface ECG. Given the need to avoid crossing the newly implanted valve, a single-chamber pacemaker was placed in atrial single-chamber pacing mode (Figure 4D). Significant symptomatic improvement was noted at follow-up.

Patient 3

A 68-year-old woman with severe stenosis and regurgitation of a tricuspid valve prosthesis 5 years after tricuspid valve replacement and correction of a patent foramen ovale presented with severe right-sided heart failure. The mean tricuspid gradient on TTE was 15 mm Hg. A tricuspid balloon valvuloplasty using a 26-mm Inoue balloon (Toray Industries) was performed as a bridge to a transcatheter tricuspid valve-in-valve procedure. During the initial intervention, the tricuspid prosthesis (31-mm Labcor) was not able to be visualized, requiring multiple attempts to position the balloon correctly (Figure 5, Video 7).

One month later, the valve-in-valve procedure was conducted using the previously described RCA guidewire and balloon predilatation method. A 28-mm Mammoth balloon was inflated, and once its waist was visible (Figure 6A), the angiographic projection angle was changed so that the RCA guidewire was approximately overlaying the waist of the balloon (Figure 6B). Next, the transcatheter balloon-expandable valve (30.5-mm Myval) was slowly implanted while the RCA guidewire marked its landing zone (Figure 6C).

Postprocedural right ventriculography showed no tricuspid regurgitation (Figure 6D, Video 8). The mean transvalvular gradient was 5 mm Hg on TTE, and no tricuspid regurgitation was detected.

Discussion

This case series highlights the difficulties in treating tricuspid valve prosthesis malfunction, especially the radiographic invisibility of bioprosthetic valves during transcatheter valve-in-valve implantation. This invisibility poses a substantial obstacle to the accurate positioning and deployment of the new valve, potentially compromising procedural success and patient outcomes. To address this concern, a combination of RCA guidewire placement and balloon dilatation for prosthesis marking was used, resulting in effective valve-in-valve deployment in all 3 cases.

Bioprosthetic heart valves often require reintervention, given their susceptibility to structural valve deterioration.5 Such valves in the aorta have an average longevity of 7.8 years.6 A meta-analysis by Rizzoli et al7 estimated the deterioration rate of bioprosthetic tricuspid valves at 1.7% per patient-year, underscoring concerns about long-term valve function.7 Managing tricuspid bioprosthetic valve dysfunction requires a multifaceted approach, including medical therapy, surgical intervention, and transcatheter procedures.

Repeat tricuspid valve surgery is often considered, but it carries greater procedural risk than initial valve replacement, particularly in patients with advanced age and significant comorbidities, with an in-hospital mortality rate exceeding 13%.2,8 Adhesions from previous surgeries further complicate subsequent procedures, increasing the risk of damaging critical structures.8 Additionally, redo surgery in an isolated tricuspid valve is reported to result in 8.5% of patients developing new atrial fibrillation, 18.1% requiring permanent pacemaker implantation, and 0.6% experiencing stroke.9 Transcatheter valve-in-valve procedure presents a viable alternative to surgery, as it has a low risk of postoperative complications and better early survival outcomes.10,11 However, in the cases we have described, the lack of radiopaque landmarks on fluoroscopy necessitated additional measures to ensure proper valve positioning and function.

The placement of an RCA guidewire, a method not previously documented in the literature, was an essential anatomical reference point in each of our 3 patients. Its purpose was to designate the tricuspid valve ring consistently. Notably, the placement of the coronary guidewire required only a negligible amount of contrast, particularly in the absence of significant coronary artery disease, making it a low-risk addition to the procedure. Balloon dilatation was carried out after guidewire placement, primarily to improve prosthesis visualization and facilitate valve sizing. The precise location of the valve was determined by modifying the angiographic angle so that the RCA guidewire was aligned above the waist of the balloon. This alignment allowed the team to remove the balloon after pre-dilatation while leaving the RCA guidewire in place as a continuous marker for accurate transcatheter valve deployment.

In our case series, TEE was intentionally omitted to allow for local anesthesia and conscious sedation, given the patients' advanced age, comorbidities, and frailty. Although we recognize that TEE provides real-time visualization of the tricuspid prosthesis, facilitates accurate valve deployment, and allows immediate assessment of valve function without the need for contrast, the trade-off was deemed acceptable to reduce procedural invasiveness. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that TEE, particularly with newer multiplane and three-dimensional modalities, could have significantly enhanced procedural precision in these fluoroscopically challenging cases.

Regarding the RCA guidewire technique, standard workhorse coronary wires were used in all 3 patients as safer options. However, wires with extended radiopaque tips such as the Fielder XT (Asahi Intecc), with a 16-cm radiopaque segment, may offer superior visualization over standard wires with short (3 cm) markers. This refinement may further increase accuracy and reduce contrast use in patients with renal impairment.

Rapid ventricular pacing was used selectively across the 3 cases. In patient 1, pacing was initiated via a temporary coronary sinus lead to minimize valve motion during balloon inflation and transcatheter valve deployment. This decision was driven by initial concerns regarding the risk of malalignment or migration of the transcatheter valve, especially given the absence of fluoroscopic landmarks and the need for precise positioning. Furthermore, placing the pacing lead in the coronary sinus was preferred to avoid traversing the existing tricuspid prosthesis and risking entrapment during valve deployment. However, as our procedural experience with the RCA guidewire and balloon predilatation technique grew, the perceived need for rapid pacing diminished. In subsequent patients, the procedures were performed successfully without pacing, reinforcing the notion that, unlike aortic valve procedures, rapid pacing is generally not required during tricuspid valve-in-valve implantation.3 The lower pressure gradients and flow velocities in the right heart, combined with the stability provided by a carefully positioned guidewire and balloon, reduce the likelihood of valve displacement during deployment. Nonetheless, rapid pacing remains a tool that may be selectively employed depending on patient anatomy, rhythm, and operator preference.

In this case series, we highlight a promising method to manage the difficulties presented by radiolucent bioprostheses during tricuspid valve-in-valve procedures. There are few studies on supplemental techniques for poor visualization, despite many current publications focusing on valve design and delivery systems. The lack of radiopaque landmarks was originally noted by Calvert et al,12 who used a technique including TEE and general anesthesia; to overcome this issue. In another series by Tzifa et al,13 prestenting of the tricuspid bioprosthesis was performed before implantation of the transcatheter valve because of the relatively short stent frame of the latter.13 Alternatively, rapid pacing was suggested to be necessary when prestenting was not conducted.

Aurigemma et al14 reported a case in which a valve-in-valve procedure was performed in a degenerated tricuspid bioprosthesis that was invisible on fluoroscopy. To overcome this, the investigators employed a combination of bilateral jugular vein approach, transapical delivery system, and injection of contrast into the right atrium during deployment to ensure precise positioning. This technique was effective, as indicated by the absence of regurgitation and stenosis; however, it required another vascular access and TEE guidance. Colucci et al15 also encountered a patient with a degenerated prosthesis not visible on fluoroscopy. Their successful outcome relied on meticulous preprocedural planning with CT to accurately reconstruct the tricuspid prosthetic annulus and size the transcatheter valve. Intraprocedurally, they used TEE, balloon valvuloplasty, and injection of contrast into the right atrium to achieve correct valve positioning.

To the best of our knowledge, no prior reports have described an RCA guidewire as an anatomical marker in conjunction with balloon predilatation for prosthesis localization. Further studies are warranted to evaluate these techniques’ broader applicability and effectiveness in diverse patient populations. Additionally, advancements in imaging technology and the development of more radiopaque valve materials might reduce the need for such adjunctive techniques.
