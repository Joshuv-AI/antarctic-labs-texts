Insulin Resistance and Beta Cell Function in Hepatogenous Diabetes
Authors: Kirandeep Kaur, Anand Sharma, Gourab Bhaduri, Ch Jagapathi Babu, Aneesa A. Mirza, Yogesh Bahurupi, Kalyani Sridharan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The present study aimed to examine the change in beta cell function and insulin resistance (IR) over 6 months in patients with liver cirrhosis (LC) and hepatogenous diabetes (HD) and to assess their correlation with liver disease severity.Methods:One hundred and seventy-three patients with LC without a prior history/risk factors of type 2 diabetes mellitus and with non-diabetic fasting blood glucose and glycated haemoglobin were subjected to a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Those with 2-h plasma glucose ≥140 mg/dl were categorised as having HD. Plasma insulin and glucose were analysed during OGTT. Indices for IR and beta cell function, such as Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), oral glucose sensitivity index – 3 h (OGIS-3h) and insulinogenic index (IGI) were examined at baseline and repeated after a 6-month follow-up.Results:HD was detected in 65.8% (114/173) of the study cohort. Eighty-nine patients were recruited to the study, and 40 were available for follow-up. The majority (89.8%) were males with a mean body mass index of 23 ± 4.1 kg/m2. Alcohol was the most common cause of LC (76.4%), and most (84.3%) belonged to Child–Turcot–Pugh (CTP) classes B and C. Plasma fasting insulin and HOMA-IR values were significantly higher in higher CTP classes and worsened at the 6-month follow-up. Peripheral insulin sensitivity measured by OGIS-3h was significantly worse in patients with a worse Model for End-Stage Liver Disease value at baseline but did not change during follow-up. Beta cell function measured by IGI was neither different across CTP classes at baseline nor changed during follow-up.Conclusion:Peripheral hyperinsulinaemia and IR, rather than beta cell dysfunction, predominate in patients with LC and glucose abnormalities on OGTT and worsen with worsening liver dysfunction.

INTRODUCTION

Glucose abnormalities in liver cirrhosis (LC) are common. Patients with LC can have low fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and erroneous glycated haemoglobin due to impaired glucose output and increased red cell turnover. This necessitates performing an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) for the detection of glycaemic abnormalities in LC.[1] Diabetes mellitus (DM) in LC can be of two types: type 2 DM (T2DM) or hepatogenous diabetes (HD). DM occurring as a direct consequence of liver dysfunction is called hepatogenous diabetes (HD). Although the concept was described in the 1960s, this term was first used in the 1990s.[2] HD differs from T2DM in that it is typically not associated with metabolic risk factors or micro- and macrovascular complications. It is more closely associated with liver dysfunction.[3] Despite these differences, it may be difficult to distinguish the two in a given subject.[4]

The pathophysiology of HD is less well studied but is believed to involve insulin resistance (IR) and beta cell dysfunction.[567] IR in LC occurs due to several factors, including decreased skeletal muscle mass, hyperammonaemia, oxidative stress and altered gut microbiota.[8910] Hyperinsulinaemia due to IR, pancreatic congestion seen in advanced cirrhosis and oxidative stress further lead to impaired beta cell function.[111213] The relative contributions of IR and beta cell dysfunction to the glycaemic abnormalities and their progression with time in LC are not well studied. The present study aimed to examine the change in beta cell function and IR over 6 months in patients with LC and HD and to assess their correlation with liver disease severity.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study participants

This observational prospective cohort study was conducted in the Department of Endocrinology and Gastroenterology at a tertiary care centre in North India. The study duration was 18 months, from July 2021 to December 2022. Participants were selected from the outdoor liver clinic of the Department of Gastroenterology.

Adults (age >18 years) with LC were screened for inclusion in the study after obtaining written informed consent. Cirrhosis was defined based on standard clinical, biochemical, radiological, endoscopic or histological criteria. Patients with FPG ≥126 mg/dl or glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% were excluded due to the possibility of pre-existing T2DM. Among those with non-diabetic FPG and HbA1c, a 75-g OGTT was performed, and HD was defined as 2-h plasma glucose (2h-PG) ≥140 mg/dl. There is currently a lack of uniform diagnostic criteria to define HD, so a cut-off of ≥140 mg/dl was used to define HD in this study to include both impaired glucose tolerance (HD-IGT: 2h-PG = 140–199 mg/dl) and HD-DM (2h-PG ≥200 mg/dl) detected after OGTT as per the American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria for the diagnosis of T2DM.[14] Study subjects who had alcohol intake in the 8 weeks before screening, subjects with DM onset before the onset of liver disease or those with a prior history of T2DM or who were taking insulin/oral anti-diabetic drugs, those with Metabolic dysfunction-Associated Fatty liver disease (MAFLD) as an aetiology of cirrhosis, those with a history of steroid use for >3 weeks or those with anaemia (haemoglobin <9 g/dl), a past history of chronic pancreatitis, a diagnosis of chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, acute variceal haemorrhage, hepatic encephalopathy, morbid obesity (body mass index [BMI] >35 kg/m2), untreated chronic hepatitis C or hepatocellular carcinoma were excluded from the study.

The primary objective of the study was to assess the change in beta cell function as measured by the oral glucose insulin sensitivity index – 3 h (OGIS-3h) at 6 months of follow-up in patients with alcoholic cirrhosis and HD. The secondary objectives of the study were to assess the change in IR as measured by the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) at 6 months of follow-up, the change in the area under the curve (AUC) for insulin and glucose during OGTT at 6 months of follow-up and the correlation between beta cell function and the severity of liver dysfunction in patients with alcoholic cirrhosis and HD.

Assessment of baseline parameters, insulin resistance and beta cell function

All recruited study participants were assessed at baseline with a predefined study proforma. Baseline demographic and clinical data were recorded. Neuropathy was screened using the 10-g Semmes Weinstein monofilament test. Retinopathy assessment was conducted with an indirect ophthalmoscope after dilating the pupils with tropicamide. The severity of liver disease was assessed by the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) and Child–Turcot–Pugh (CTP) grading. Based on the latter, patients were categorised based on increasing liver disease severity from CTP class A through CTP class C.

After an overnight fast, all the participants underwent an extended 3-h OGTT in the Department of Endocrinology. Venous samples for plasma glucose (PG) and serum insulin were drawn at baseline and at 30, 60, 120 and 180 min after an oral 75-g glucose load. PG was measured immediately by the glucose oxidase method using an automated analyser. Samples for insulin were allowed to clot, centrifuged, stored at −20°C and analysed in batches. Serum insulin was measured by the chemiluminescence method using the ADVIA Centaur XP immunoassay system (Siemens, Germany).

IR was calculated using the mathematical models – OGIS-3h and HOMA -IR. OGIS-3h predicts glucose clearance using glucose and insulin values derived from OGTT and has been validated against the hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp method. It was calculated using available online software (http://webmet.pd.cnr.it/ogis/index.php).[15] Higher OGIS-3h values indicate better insulin sensitivity. HOMA-IR value for predicting hepatic IR was calculated using baseline serum insulin and FPG values, using the formula: (Fasting serum insulin (IU/L) × FPG (mg/dl)/405).[16] Higher HOMA-IR values indicate greater IR. The AUCs for glucose and insulin were calculated using the trapezoidal rule from PG and serum insulin at 0, 30, 60, 120 and 180 min, respectively.

Beta cell function was estimated by the insulinogenic index (IGI). IGI is an index derived from OGTT and estimates the early-phase insulin secretion from the pancreatic beta cell. It has been shown to correlate well with the acute insulin response derived from an intravenous glucose bolus. IGI was calculated from the following formula: IGI = (I30 − I0)/(G30 − G0).[17]

Follow-up

Participants were followed for 6 months, and at the end of follow-up, a repeat extended 3-h OGTT was performed, and parameters including OGIS-3h, HOMA-IR, AUC for glucose, AUC for insulin and IGI values were recalculated. None of the participants required any oral anti-diabetic drugs or insulin. Lifestyle modification was advised to all the patients diagnosed with DM after OGTT. All patients were advised a calorie intake of 30 kcal/kg/day, with a protein intake of 1.2 g/kg/day.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power 3.1 software. For two dependent means, using an effect size of 0.5, that is, Cohen’s effect size medium convention, and assuming an alpha error of 0.05 and a study power of 80%, the required sample size was calculated to be around 34. After assuming a 20% loss to follow-up, the estimated sample size was 43.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM, Armonk, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Normality of the data was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. The paired t-test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test were used to compare paired variables for parametric and nonparametric data, respectively. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for continuous variables in more than two groups. The Pearson or Spearman correlation test was used to analyse the correlation between two normally or non-normally distributed parameters, respectively. A P value of <0.05 was taken as statistically significant.

Ethical aspects

Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Institute Ethics Committee of AIIMS Rishikesh (vide letter number AIIMS/IEC/21/396 dated 16 July 2021). Written informed consent was obtained for participation in the study and the use of patient data for research and educational purposes. The study procedures adhered to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki 1964 and its subsequent revisions.

RESULTS

From July 2021 to December 2022, a total of 203 individuals with LC were screened. Thirty subjects had FPG ≥126 mg/dl or HbA1c ≥6.5% and were excluded from the study. Of the remaining 173 subjects with non-DM FPG (<126 mg/dl) and HbA1c (<6.5%), a 75-g OGTT showed a 2h-PG <140 mg/dl in 59 (34.1%) subjects (normal glucose tolerance group), 140–199 mg/dl in 80 (46.24%) subjects (HD-IGT group) and ≥200 mg/dl in 34 (19.65%) (HD-DM group) subjects. The latter two were categorised into the HD group (n = 114, 65.89%). Of the 114 subjects, 25 had to be excluded from the study due to death, loss to follow-up or new diagnoses of malignancy after screening. Eighty-nine individuals met the inclusion criteria and were available for recruitment after providing informed consent. These participants underwent baseline testing and were followed for 6 months, after which 40 participants were available for repeat testing after excluding mortality and those lost to follow-up [Figure 1].

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort

The mean age of the participants was 47 ± 10.02 years, and 89.88% were males. The majority of the patients had a lean phenotype, with a mean BMI of 23 ± 4.12 kg/m2. Alcohol intake was the predominant aetiology (76.4%) of LC. Fourteen (15.7%) patients were in CTP class A, 47 (52.8%) in CTP class B and 28 (31.5%) in CTP class C. Among the participants, 25.8% had a history of variceal bleeding and 22.5% had hepatic encephalopathy in the past. None of the patients had an onset of DM before the diagnosis of liver disease [Table 1].

Follow-up characteristics of the study cohort

Among the 89 patients recruited to the study, on follow-up at 6 months, 29 died and 20 were lost to follow-up. Therefore, 40 patients were available at the 6-month follow-up. Patients who died (vs those who completed the study vs those who were lost to follow-up) had worse baseline liver function (mean MELD value: 17.48 ± 5.79 vs 13.21 ± 5.49 vs 13.45 ± 3.36, respectively; one-way ANOVA P = 0.003) and lower insulin sensitivity (OGIS-3h: 344.79 ± 61.26 vs 375.97 ± 71.17 vs 426.20 ± 87.65, respectively; one-way ANOVA P < 0.001) [Table 3]. Patients who died also had a higher HOMA-IR value (3.72 ± 3.02 vs 2.71 ± 1.44 vs 2.63 ± 2.16, respectively; one-way ANOVA P = 0.126), but this was not statistically significant. IGI was similar across the three groups. The patients who were lost to follow-up had similar MELD, HOMA-IR and IGI values as compared to those who completed the study [Table 3].

DISCUSSION

In our study, 65.8% of the participants with LC and non-DM FPG and HbA1c were found to have HD (both IGT and DM) when screened with a 75-g OGTT. There was significant hyperinsulinaemia and worsening IR across the CTP classes at baseline and in the whole cohort on follow-up. Peripheral insulin sensitivity estimated by the OGIS index was worse in patients with worse MELD values at baseline but was not different in the whole cohort on follow-up. Beta cell function estimated by IGI was neither different across the CTP classes at baseline nor in the cohort on follow-up.

HD in LC is common, and its prevalence has ranged from 20% to 70% in the literature.[471819] In our study, the prevalence of HD was 65.8%, including HD-IGT (46.2%) and HD-DM (19.6%). The strict exclusion criteria in our study ensured that the diabetes detected on OGTT was most likely to be HD rather than T2DM. We included in our cohort any dysglycaemia on OGTT and categorised it further as HD-IGT and HD-DM. The reason for including both IGT and DM in our cohort is twofold: First, because we intended to study the pathophysiological alterations in IR and beta cell function, we believed these changes would begin right from IGT and progress to DM. Second, any level of dysglycaemia has been shown to have prognostic significance, as demonstrated in multiple prior studies. The prevalence of HD has been shown to correlate with liver disease severity, as seen in the study by Jeon et al.[20] Therefore, the higher prevalence of HD seen in our cohort could also be because of the higher number of CTP class B and C patients recruited.

The pathophysiology of HD is not clearly delineated but is believed to involve both IR and poor beta cell function. Basal plasma insulin levels in LC were increased in our study, and the levels further increased on follow-up. The fasting peripheral hyperinsulinaemia has been attributed to several reasons, including impaired insulin clearance by the liver in LC, escape of liver degradation because of porto-systemic shunting, increased secretion by the pancreatic beta cells or impaired feedback regulation of insulin secretion.[2122] Therefore, peripheral hyperinsulinaemia in LC does not necessarily imply increased insulin secretion. Kruszynska et al.[22] found that c-peptide levels were increased twofold, whereas insulin levels were increased four- to sixfold in patients with LC as compared to controls, suggesting that both beta cell hypersecretion and impaired insulin clearance contributed to the high fasting insulin levels. Greco et al.,[23] however, showed that hepatic insulin extraction was not reduced in LC and that hyperinsulinaemia was entirely because of beta cell hypersecretion.

The fasting hyperinsulinaemia in our patients was accompanied by normal fasting blood glucose levels and high HOMA-IR values. This suggests the involvement of IR in HD, which has been shown to occur early on in several studies.[72124] IR in HD could be because of decreased peripheral utilisation of glucose or increased endogenous hepatic glucose production or both. Petrides et al.[11] and Nielsen et al.[24] in clamp studies in patients with LC showed that it is peripheral non-oxidative glucose disposal was significantly impaired in LC, whereas endogenous hepatic glucose release was unaltered. Cavallo-Perin et al.[21] further showed that the defect of insulin action at peripheral tissues was likely a combined receptor and post-receptor defect. In our study, OGIS, a marker of peripheral insulin sensitivity, was not different across the CTP classes at baseline. However, in the group of patients with worse MELD values at baseline (and who had died), the OGIS value was significantly worse as compared to those with better MELD values (and who could complete the study). This shows the association of insulin sensitivity with liver dysfunction. On follow-up, however, OGIS did not change, probably because the survivors had a similar MELD value at follow-up as at baseline, suggesting no progression of liver dysfunction. This finding is at variance with Grancini et al.,7] who did not find any association of insulin sensitivity with liver dysfunction.

Insulin secretion in patients with LC has been shown to be increased,[22] normal[24] or decreased.[25] Grancini et al.[26] in their study on patients with LC found that beta cell dysfunction drove the transition from IGT to DM[7] and also the regression of dysglycaemia after liver transplantation. Studies showed that early-phase insulin secretion on OGTT was blunted with a delayed peak attained on OGTT as compared to normal subjects.[822] In our study, we assessed beta cell function using an OGTT measure (IGI) and found that it was not different across the CTP classes, nor did it change on follow-up. We found that the peak insulin attained was at 120 min following the glucose load, both at baseline and at follow-up, which was later than that conventionally found in non-diabetic normal subjects.[22] Although, without a control group of normal subjects, it is not possible to comment on whether this represents a blunted or delayed early-phase insulin response, beta cell function did not change over follow-up in our study. This is at variance with the finding of Grancini et al.[7]; however, it is in accordance with Nielsen et al.,[24] who found normal beta cell function in LC. The latter, however, had not followed patients over time. It is possible that because our cohort predominantly consisted of patients with IGT there was little beta cell dysfunction. However, when analysed between the HD-IGT and HD-DM subgroups, IGI was worse in the HD-DM subgroup at both baseline and follow-up, highlighting that beta cell dysfunction was the hallmark of worsening glycaemic status. However, because our follow-up MELD values were similar to baseline values, there was possibly no change in beta cell function on follow-up in the whole cohort.

There are not many studies that have examined the sequence of pathophysiological defects that drive the glycaemic abnormalities in LC. Most studies, including ours, agree that IR is an early and invariable finding in HD.[68] Beta cell dysfunction likely happens later and drives the transition from glucose intolerance to DM. An interesting hypothesis for the development of IR in HD is that it is because of insulin itself. Peripheral hyperinsulinaemia, which develops early on, induces IR by causing a defect in non-oxidative glucose disposal at peripheral tissues, primarily in skeletal muscles.[6]

Notwithstanding the pathophysiology of HD, the entity is associated with higher mortality and liver-related outcomes. In our cohort, the mortality rate was around 32% (29/89 patients; 21 had HD-IGT and 8 had HD-DM) over 6 months of follow-up, and all the patients died due to liver-related complications. Even subclinical abnormalities on OGTT in patients with HD and LC have prognostic implications for patients and are important to detect.[2728]

Strengths and limitations

This study is one of the few to address longitudinal changes in insulin indices and beta cell function in patients with cirrhosis. Our study also has some limitations. First, although our strict clinical criteria ensured that the glycaemic abnormalities diagnosed on OGTT in our patients were likely HD and not T2DM, due to the cross-sectional nature of our study, it is possible that some patients could be newly diagnosed with T2DM or prediabetes. Second, the study had a limited follow-up duration of 6 months, which might limit adequate changes in glycaemic parameters from accruing. Third, due to the COVID-19 pandemic in the initial period of recruitment, there was a >20% loss to follow-up, leading to attrition bias. Furthermore, there was high mortality due to the recruitment of predominantly CTP class B and C patients, resulting in a small follow-up cohort with a survivor bias. The follow-up cohort had similar MELD values at follow-up as at baseline. Together, these factors could result in the study outcomes being biased, and the results of the study should be cautiously interpreted with these in mind. Fourth, we used OGTT for calculating IR and beta cell function. Although not as accurate as clamp to assess these parameters, especially beta cell function, it is more physiological, and in our study, we were able to demonstrate the importance of performing an OGTT to pick up subclinical glucose abnormalities in LC. Furthermore, although IR and beta cell indices derived from OGTT provide useful research insights, these measures are not part of routine standard-of-care evaluation in patients with chronic liver disease. Fifth, most of our cohort were male and had alcoholic cirrhosis, and the results, therefore, are generalisable only to this cohort. Sixth, although all the study participants were given a uniform dietary advice, adherence to the diet was not measured in the study, and this could have affected the outcomes. Lastly, although the MAFLD terminology is retained for protocol consistency, the findings should be interpreted in the context of the current metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease framework.

CONCLUSION

This study showed a high prevalence of glycaemic abnormalities on OGTT in patients with LC and non-diabetic FPG and HbA1c. Hyperinsulinaemia and IR, rather than beta cell dysfunction, predominate in patients with subclinical glucose abnormalities in LC and worsen with worsening liver function. These findings need reconfirmation in a larger, adequately powered study with a longer follow-up.
