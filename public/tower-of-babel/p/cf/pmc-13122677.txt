Paraneoplastic Brachial Amyotrophic Diplegia and Encephalitis With Ma2-IgG During Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Use for Mesothelioma: A Case Report
Authors: Sanem Pinar Uysal, Shervin Badihian, Karlo Toljan, Nirmal Andrapalliyal, James Stevenson, Jonathan Lee, Amy Kunchok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) improve cancer survival but can cause neurologic immune-related adverse events. Ma2-IgG–associated paraneoplastic neurologic syndrome typically presents with central nervous system involvement in patients with testicular or lung cancers. We present a case of paraneoplastic brachial amyotrophic diplegia (BAD) associated with Ma2-IgG which developed after ICI.Case ReportThis is a case report of an 86-year-old man with pleural mesothelioma treated with nivolumab and ipilimumab, who presented with progressive, asymmetric weakness of his upper extremities after 5 cycles of ICI. Examination did not reveal associated sensory deficits. Blood creatine kinase level was normal and acetylcholine receptor antibody testing result was negative. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed findings consistent with bilateral frontal temporal lobe encephalitis, but without concurrent cervical cord lesions. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed mild pleocytosis and oligoclonal bands. Ma2-IgG was present in the cerebrospinal fluid (>1:32) and serum (>1:3200). Electrodiagnostic testing revealed diffuse chronic innervation and active denervation changes involving the upper extremities raising concern for a motor neuron disorder. He was diagnosed with having paraneoplastic BAD and encephalitis. A progressive course ensued, despite initial transient improvement with escalation of immunosuppression.ConclusionParaneoplastic BAD is an uncommon paraneoplastic phenomenon that can occur with Ma2-IgG in the setting of ICI use and atypical tumors.Keywords: Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Onconeural antigens, Paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes, Motor neuronopathy, Limbic encephalitis, Case report

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) improve cancer survival but can cause neurologic immune-related adverse events (irAEs), sometimes linked to paraneoplastic antibodies because of ICI-induced autoimmunity.1 Ma2-IgG–associated paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes (PNS) most often present with limbic, diencephalic, or brainstem encephalitis and are typically associated with testicular cancers in younger males.2 Peripheral nervous system involvement is rare.

We report a case of Ma2-IgG–associated motor neuronopathy in the setting of ICI for pleural mesothelioma.

Case Presentation

An 86-year-old man with pleural mesothelioma (diagnosed 8 months prior, previously treated with pemetrexed and carboplatin) and nephritis as an irAE from ICI (nivolumab and ipilimumab) developed mild right upper extremity (RUE) weakness, progressing to left upper extremity (LUE) weakness and diplegia after 5 cycles of nivolumab and ipilimumab. Before the neurologic complications, he had tolerated ICI with improved subjective symptoms (chest tightness and breathing capacity) and interval improvement in the nodular pleural thickening findings and mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathies.

Neurologic examination revealed a left lower visual field quadrantanopia (longstanding from remote traumatic brain injury) and restricted left eye horizontal movement. LUE strength was impaired with deltoid 1 of 5, biceps 1 of 5, and wrist flexors 2 of 5 on Medical Research Council muscle strength scale and no movement in other muscles. RUE strength was impaired proximally (deltoid 3/5, biceps 4/5, triceps 4/5) only. Reflexes were absent on the left and diminished on the RUE. Results of lower extremity, sensory, and cerebellar examination were normal. Montreal Cognitive Assessment was 23 of 30, with deficits in visuospatial, naming, attention, abstraction, and delayed recall.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed T2 and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery hyperintensities involving bilateral insula and mesial temporal lobes with bilateral amygdala enhancement (Fig. 1A–F). Cervical MRI revealed mild degenerative changes but no cord lesions.

Initial magnetic resonance imaging axial FLAIR images demonstrated area of encephalomalacia and gliosis because of remote traumatic injury (A, marked by arrows) and FLAIR hyperintensity in the bilateral mesial temporal, insular, and left greater than right orbitofrontal areas (B–D, marked by arrows). There was associated contrast enhancement of involving the bilateral amygdala (E is postcontrast axial T1, F is postcontrast coronal T1, marked by arrows, and an artery is marked by asterisk). FLAIR, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery

Serum studies were unrevealing. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed evidence of intrathecal inflammation with oligoclonal bands present. Ma2-IgG was positive in the CSF (>1:32, nanoliter scale immunoassay) and serum (>1:3200, nanoliter scale immunoassay). Testicular ultrasound result was negative (Supplementary Table 1).

Electrodiagnostic studies of the RUE performed within 3 weeks of onset revealed normal nerve conduction study results (Supplementary Table 2). Electromyography revealed active denervation in the C5 myotome and cervical paraspinals, with chronic reinnervation across C5 to T1 myotomes (Table 1A).

He was diagnosed with ICI-associated paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis and brachial amyotrophic diplegia (BAD). The ICI was discontinued, and he received intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g daily, 5 d), followed by oral prednisone taper. Because of lack of considerable improvement at 2 months, 2 g/kg intravenous immunoglobulin was administered. Nevertheless, the LUE weakness persisted and the RUE weakness progressed. Intravenous cyclophosphamide (750 mg/m2) was added. A repeat brain MRI revealed interval improvement in fluid-attenuated inversion recovery hyperintensities and contrast enhancement. Contrasted cervical and thoracic MRI results remained unremarkable.

Nerve conduction study of the LUE and lower extremities at 4 months after presentation revealed reduced motor response amplitudes in the left median and ulnar nerves (Supplementary Table 3) while studies of the left lower extremity and thoracic paraspinal muscles showed no abnormalities. Electromyography demonstrated severe active denervation and chronic reinnervation across left C5 to T1 myotomes (Table 1B).

Despite treatment, the RUE weakness worsened. He ultimately transitioned to hospice care because of cancer progression. The timeline of events is summarized in Supplementary Figure 1.

Discussion

Ma1 and Ma2 are intracellular neuronal proteins with Ma1 also being expressed in germ cells of the testis. Ma1-IgG and Ma2-IgG–associated PNS is typically found with germ cell testicular tumors and less frequently NSCLC and breast cancer.2 The immunopathogenesis of Ma2-IgG–associated PNS is demonstrated in Figure 2.

Postulated immunopathogenesis of Ma2-IgG paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes. During tumor cell apoptosis, onco-neural antigens are released. These antigens are taken up, processed, and presented by APCs through MHC molecules. MHC class I molecules present antigens to naive CD8+ T cells, whereas MHC class II molecules present antigens to CD4+ T cells, which subsequently activate B cells. This process leads to the generation of tumor-specific cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and plasma cells that produce onco-neural antibodies. CD8+ T cells, once activated, recognize antigens presented on tumor cell surfaces through MHC I molecules through their TCRs, proliferating and launching an attack on the tumor. The immune response can be down-regulated by immune checkpoints, including the interaction of PD-1 with its ligand, PD-L1, and CTLA-4. PD-1 inhibits T-cell activity within the tumor microenvironment, whereas CTLA-4 primarily regulates the early stages of T-cell activation in the lymph nodes by outcompeting the co-stimulatory receptor CD28 for binding to B7 molecules on APCs. ICIs targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 block these inhibitory checkpoints enhancing the immune system's ability to target and eliminate tumors. APC, antigen-presenting cell; CTLA-4, cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4; ICI, immune checkpoint inhibitor; MHC, major histocompatibility complex; PD-1, programmed cell death protein 1; PD-L1, programmed death-ligand 1; TCR, T-cell receptor.

Our patient developed progressive asymmetric upper extremity weakness without sensory changes or upper motor neuron signs, localizing to the peripheral nervous system. Peripheral nervous system involvement associated with Ma2-IgG is rare but has been reported to involve radiculopathy, neuropathy, or neuronopathy.3 In this case, normal creatine kinase and unremarkable cervical MRI made myopathy and polyradiculopathy unlikely, respectively. Diffuse electrodiagnostic findings in the absence of cervical MRI abnormalities and lack of sensory deficits supported a motor neuron disorder than polyradiculopathy.

The constellation of cancer, subacute bilateral upper extremity weakness, Ma2-IgG positivity, and partial clinical stabilization with immunosuppression suggested paraneoplastic BAD. Also known as “flail arm syndrome,” BAD is a variant of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis with asymmetric proximal/distal upper extremity weakness.4 Features distinguishing paraneoplastic BAD from neurodegenerative variants include additional neurologic symptoms (such as ataxia and cranial neuropathies), inflammatory CSF, neuronal antibodies, and cancer.5

This case is unique as mesothelioma is an uncommon cancer associated with Ma2-IgG PNS, potentially unmasked by ICI therapy. ICIs enhance antitumor responses but can lead to irAEs, including the unmasking of paraneoplastic autoimmunity in cancers not otherwise often associated with PNS.1 Motor neuronopathy is also an unusual phenotype for this antibody. A review of 108 PNS cases with Ma2-IgG identified 10 motor neuronopathy cases with variable presentations, including pure upper/lower motor neuron involvement or a combination of both.3 Paraneoplastic BAD outcomes vary, with most cases stabilizing at best.3,5 Ma2-IgG–associated PNS often responds minimally to immunosuppression.2

Paraneoplastic BAD should be considered in patients with cancer with progressive asymmetric upper extremity weakness. ICI therapy may unmask PNS in tumors not typically linked to paraneoplastic autoimmunity.
