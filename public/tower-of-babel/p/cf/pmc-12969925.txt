CT-based hepatic and splenic extracellular volume fraction and iodine washout rate for noninvasive assessment of portal hypertension: a preliminary study
Authors: Jinkui Li, Long Gao, Baoyuan Wang, Qing Yang, Xiaoyue Zhang, Junqiang Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn cirrhotic patients, the severity of portal hypertension (PH) directly correlates with the risk of decompensation. The hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) is critical for staging and prognostic prediction of PH, but it is invasive. Non-invasive assessment of PH remains an unmet clinical need. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether multiphasic computed tomography (CT)-derived extracellular volume fraction (ECV) of the liver and spleen, and the liver iodine washout rate (IWR), could serve as noninvasive biomarkers for clinically significant PH (CSPH, defined as HVPG ≥ 10 mmHg) and severe PH (defined as HVPG ≥ 16 mmHg).MethodsThis retrospective study included 127 participants: 76 cirrhotic patients with CSPH and 51 control individuals with normal liver (as determined by clinical data, laboratory tests and imaging). All participants underwent contrast-enhanced CT. Pearson correlations between CT parameters and HVPG were analyzed. The diagnostic performance of these parameters in differentiating patients with CSPH from control individuals, and non-severe PH (HVPG < 16 mmHg) from severe PH was assessed using the areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsThe CT parameters significantly differed between CSPH patients and control individuals (P < 0.05). ECVliver, ECVspleen, and IWRliver were correlated with HVPG (r = 0.29, 0.41, and − 0.45, respectively; all P < 0.05). Both ECVspleen and IWRliver independently predicted PH severity (P < 0.05), and showed excellent diagnostic accuracy for CSPH (AUC = 0.981 and 0.953, respectively), outperforming ECVliver (AUC = 0.706, P < 0.001). For distinguishing non-severe PH from severe PH, ECVspleen and IWRliver achieved AUC values of 0.718 and 0.722, respectively, with their combination improving diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.832, sensitivity = 90.00%, and specificity = 69.44%).ConclusionThe CT parameters ECVspleen and IWRliver can serve as promising noninvasive tools for assessing PH severity. Further validation in broader PH cohorts is warranted.Keywords: Cirrhosis, Contrast-enhanced CT, Extracellular volume fraction, Iodine washout rate, Portal hypertension

Background

Portal hypertension (PH) drives the pathophysiology of complications in cirrhosis, including acute variceal hemorrhage, refractory ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and portal hypertensive gastroenteropathy. The hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) measurement is considered the reference standard for quantitatively assessing portal pressure [1, 2]. Clinically significant PH (CSPH), defined as HVPG ≥ 10 mmHg, is a critical threshold that considerably accelerates the risk of decompensation events [2, 3]. Progressive elevation of HVPG correlates with deteriorating outcomes. For example, HVPG ≥ 12 mmHg predicts a heightened risk of variceal bleeding [4], whereas severe PH (defined as HVPG ≥ 16 mmHg) is strongly linked to mortality, treatment resistance, and poor postoperative prognosis [5–8]. Although HVPG measurement is valuable for assessing PH and predicting prognosis, it is underutilized in clinical practice due to its invasiveness, cost, and limited availability outside specialized centers. This gap in accessibility underscores the need for noninvasive biomarkers to stratify PH severity.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) is a commonly used imaging technique to evaluate cirrhosis-related complications, including PH and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [9–11]. Equilibrium-phase CT imaging enables quantification of extracellular volume fraction (ECV) [12–14]. Emerging evidence suggests that quantifying liver ECV(ECVliver) using extracellular contrast agent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or contrast-enhanced CT correlates well with the staging of liver fibrosis [14–18] and has the potential for assessing PH-related complications such as esophageal varices [13, 19]. ECV also holds promise for the noninvasive evaluation of PH. Notably, an experimental animal study using MRI-derived ECV has demonstrated associations between ECVliver and PH severity [18]. A Recent study has demonstrated that ECV of the spleen (ECVspleen), derived from T1 mapping, can effectively differentiate patients with severe PH and healthy volunteers [20]. Nevertheless, the clinical utility of CT-based ECV for PH evaluation remains unvalidated.

Moreover, multiphasic CT provides insights into intrahepatic hemodynamics through iodine washout rate (IWR) analysis. IWR, a parameter validated for liver fibrosis assessment, quantifies temporal contrast dynamics between the portal venous phase (PVP) and the delayed phase (DP) [16, 21]. As intrahepatic hemodynamic alterations often occur in patients with PH, liver IWR (IWRliver) would allow the assessment of PH severity, which is closely associated with the degree of liver fibrosis. To date, no studies have explored CT-derived IWR in this context.

In light of the aforementioned findings, we hypothesized that the CT-based ECV and IWR could evaluate PH. Consequently, this study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of ECVliver, ECVspleen, and IWRliver derived from contrast-enhanced CT in assessing CSPH (HVPG ≥ 10 mmHg) and severe PH (HVPG ≥ 16 mmHg) in patients with cirrhosis.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (LDYYLL-2025-125), adhering to ethical guidelines. The requirement for patient consent was waived due to the retrospective design of this study. Consecutive cirrhotic patients with CSPH confirmed by HVPG measurements for specified clinical indications (e.g., variceal bleeding) were admitted to our hospital from January 2018 to December 2022. The diagnosis of liver cirrhosis was based on a combination of clinical data, laboratory tests, and imaging. All patients underwent multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT, including non-contrast imaging, within 2 weeks before HVPG measurement. The interval between CT and HVPG was 4.26 ± 3.61 days (range: 0–14 days). The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with a history of transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) placement, splenectomy, partial splenic embolization, liver transplantation, portal or splenic thrombosis, and those with HCC. A total of 76 patients were included (49 males and 27 females; aged 22–73 years: mean 52.34 years). During the same period, 51 individuals (25 males and 26 females; aged 22–78 years: mean 51.39 years) with normal liver function tests, who had undergone contrast-enhanced CT for nonhepatic indications and with normal liver findings on CT, were enrolled in the control group (Fig. 1; Table 1). All control individuals were free of chronic liver disease, excessive alcohol consumption, and metabolic syndrome, and exhibited no evidence of hepatic or splenic hemodynamic abnormalities.

Clinical data and laboratory markers within 3 days before TIPS implantation were retrieved from the institutional medical information system, which included the etiology of cirrhosis, platelet count (PLT), hematocrit (HCT), and hepatic biochemical data (serum albumin, serum total bilirubin, serum aspartate transaminase, alanine aminotransferase, and international normalized ratio). FIB-4 was evaluated according to published formula [22].

Image acquisition

All patients underwent multiphasic liver CT scans consisting of pre-contrast, arterial, portal, and DP images on a Siemens Healthcare system (Sensation 64 CT and SOMATOM Definition Flash CT, Forchheim, Germany), using the following parameters: tube voltage of 120 kVp, automatic tube-current modulation, slice thickness of 1.00 mm, matrix of 512 × 512, gantry rotation speed of 0.5 s, pitch of 0.6, and detector configuration of 64 × 0.625/128 × 0.6 mm. An intravenous, nonionic contrast agent (volume, 80–100 mL; contrast, iopromide, 370 mg I/mL; Bayer Health-Care, Guangzhou, China) was administered at a rate of 3.0–4.0 mL/s, followed by a 20-mL saline injection. Whole liver scans were obtained at approximately 25–30 s (arterial phase), 60–70 s (PVP), and 180 s (DP) after initiating the contrast material injection.

Transjugular HVPG measurement

Transjugular HVPG measurement was conducted post-CT scan, following the standard protocol [23], by an experienced interventional radiologist (with 20 years of experience). A 5-French balloon-tipped catheter was guided into the right or median hepatic vein by puncturing the right jugular vein to measure the free hepatic venous pressure (FHVP). Thereafter, the wedged hepatic venous pressure (WHVP) was measured by inflating the balloon catheter. HVPG was calculated by subtracting FHVP from WHVP. Based on HVPG measurements, patients were categorized into 2 groups: severe PH (HVPG ≥ 16 mmHg, n = 40) and non-severe PH (HVPG < 16 mmHg, n = 36). Invasive HVPG measurements were omitted for control individuals defined to have normal portal pressure (< 5 mmHg).

Image analysis

Two radiologists (with 6 and 10 years of experience, respectively), blinded to the clinical data or HVPG results, independently performed image measurements. Eight regions of interest (ROIs) were delineated based on Couinaud’s classification within the liver parenchyma and 3 ROIs were placed within the spleen parenchyma at the level of splenic hilum. Each ROI measured approximately 200 mm2 and was carefully positioned to avoid intrahepatic vessels, biliary structures, focal lesions and organ boundaries. For the abdominal aorta, ROIs were drawn as large as possible within the lumen, avoiding the vessel wall and atherosclerotic plaques, using the first porta hepatis and splenic hilum as anatomical reference points. Initially, ROIs were placed on the DP images and then copied-pasted onto the matching sections of the pre-contrast images and/or PVP images, with appropriate placement correction if needed (Fig. 2). Each segment was measured 3 times to ensure consistency and reproducibility, and the average value of these measurements was calculated to represent the CT attenuation. The average values of the ROIs in the liver and spleen were used as the final measurements. Subsequently, the absolute contrast enhancement (ΔHU) of the liver, spleen and the abdominal aorta was calculated by subtracting the unenhanced attenuation values from contrast-enhanced values during PVP or DP, respectively. ECVliver, ECVspleen, and IWRliver were calculated using the following equations, as described in a previous study [16]:

ECVliver = ΔHUliver−DP /ΔHUaorta−DP × (100-Hematocrit [%])

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to assess interobserver agreement, and the mean values of their measurements were used for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0) and MedCalc software (version 22.009). Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), and compared using the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as absolute numbers (percentages) and analyzed using the χ2 test. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Inter-observer reliability for CT parameters was evaluated using ICC, and categorized as follows: poor (< 0.40), moderate to good (0.40–0.75), and excellent (>0.75). Correlations between CT parameters, laboratory variables and HVPG were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r). Univariate linear and logistic regression analyses were performed to predict HVPG and severe PH, respectively. Variables with P < 0.1 in the univariate analyses were included in multivariate models. Diagnostic performances of noninvasive parameters were assessed by plotting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the curve (AUC) to discriminate between CSPH patients and control individuals, and between severe and non-severe PH. Optimal cutoff values were determined by maximizing the Youden index. Sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Comparisons of AUCs were performed using the DeLong test [24]. A two-tailed P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Discussion

This study was novel in its investigation of the feasibility of using CT parameters—ECVliver, ECVspleen, and IWRliver—for evaluating CSPH. The results revealed significant correlations between these CT parameters and invasively measured HVPG in patients with cirrhosis. ECVspleen and IWRliver proved to be independent predictors of HVPG and demonstrated excellent diagnostic performances in distinguishing patients with CSPH from control individuals. However, ECVliver exhibited relatively limited predictive values. Furthermore, both IWRliver and ECVspleen were identified as risk factors for severe PH (HVPG ≥ 16mmHg). These findings support the utility of ECVspleen and IWRliver for PH evaluation. This approach moves beyond conventional anatomic imaging by providing quantifiable metrics that mirror the core pathophysiological processes of PH. Both ECV and IWR are readily quantifiable parameters that can be retrospectively derived from contrast-enhanced CT scans, particularly in the context of liver conditions such as cirrhosis. The computational pipeline for ECV/IWR quantification demonstrates high reproducibility (inter-observer ICC > 0.87). Adopting this dual-screening strategy could further enhance the cost-effectiveness of CT in the management of liver disease.

Previous studies have primarily focused on ECV for the noninvasive assessment of liver fibrosis [14–18] and esophageal varices [13, 19, 25]. A histopathologic study demonstrated a correlation between hepatic ECV and portal pressure in an animal model [18]. Notably, ECV was associated with cirrhosis severity and decompensation events, such as ascites and variceal bleeding in patients with cirrhosis [26, 27]. Our study findings aligned with these associations, emphasizing the relevance of ECVliver to HVPG. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between patients with and without severe PH, suggesting that ECVliver was not an effective indicator for evaluating the progression of PH. The increased ECVliver in cirrhotic liver can be explained by deposition of extracellular matrix and fibrosis in the extracellular interstitial compartment [9, 16]. However, extrahepatic factors such as splanchnic vasodilation and hyperdynamic circulation, which continue to elevate portal pressure without impacting ECVliver.

As the ECVliver has been extensively studied, we focused on the ECVspleen, a parameter less explored in previous studies [20, 24, 26]. Our study demonstrated significant correlations between ECVspleen and HVPG (r = 0.41, P < 0.05), indicating its potential utility in assessing CSPH. Notably, ECVspleen values were significantly increased in cirrhotic patients with CSPH compared with control individuals. Moreover, ECVspleen demonstrated superior diagnostic performance, suggesting its potential as a reliable marker for distinguishing cirrhotic patients with CSPH from control individuals. These observations aligned with recent prospective studies that used direct HVPG (the difference between portal venous pressure and free inferior vena cava pressure) and portal vein pressure as the reference standard, based on a MR-mapping-derived ECV measurements [20]. Further, another study reported significantly higher ECVspleen values in patients with CSPH compared to those without CSPH [26], supporting our results. Compared with MRI-based ECV, CT-based ECV provides a more accessible, cost-effective, and rapid means for quantifying extracellular volume. Although ECVspleen measured through hepatocyte-specific contrast MRI effectively identifies CSPH [28], the clinical utility of CT-based ECVspleen for PH evaluation requires further research and validation. This approach is particularly advantageous in patients with contraindications to MRI. Our results demonstrated that the diagnostic performance of ECVspleen for assessing PH was superior to that of ECVliver. This discrepancy is not coincidental but is rooted in the distinct pathophysiological responses of the liver and spleen to chronically elevated portal pressure. Within the liver, ECVliver primarily quantifies the deposition of collagen and the expansion of the extracellular matrix, which is the fundamental driver of the initial increase in intrahepatic resistance [9, 16]. However, in cirrhosis, splenic haemodynamics are characterized both by congestion and tissue hyperplasia. There is a notable increase in the fraction of red pulp, leading to an expansion of the sinusoidal space, which constitutes a component of ECV [29, 30]. The resulting splenomegaly is followed by an increase in splenic blood flow, which actively contributes to the portal venous congestion [31]. Therefore, ECVspleen effectively integrates the cumulative effects of PH-induced structural alterations, including sinusoidal congestion, follicular hyperplasia, and collagen deposition [31, 32]. This makes ECVspleen a more robust and sensitive biomarker for the hemodynamic severity of PH.

Another CT-based parameter, IWRliver, exhibits promising results in assessing liver fibrosis [16]. Our study further expanded on this investigation, revealing inverse correlation between IWRliver and HVPG (r = -0.45, P < 0.05). As HVPG was closely associated with the degree of hepatic fibrosis. Patients with CSPH exhibited significantly lower IWRliver compared with control individuals, and IWRliver demonstrated exceptional accuracy in identifying CSPH. It can be explained by a dual-path mechanism:1) In patients with cirrhosis, increased intrahepatic vascular resistance reduces peak iodine uptake, decreasing iodine delivery during PVP; 2) Concurrently, the extensive fibrosis expands the extracellular-extravascular space and creates a diffusion barrier. This causes iodine contrast to become ‘trapped’ within the interstitial compartment, profoundly delaying its washout during the DP [9, 21]. Consequently, a lower IWRliver value is not merely an imaging observation but a direct reflection of the dysfunctional diffusion and retention kinetics that are intrinsic to the hemodynamic severity of PH.

More intriguingly, our results demonstrated that both ECVspleen and IWRliver are not only independent predictors of CSPH but also exhibit significant correlations with severe PH (HVPG ≥ 16 mmHg). Patients with severe PH demonstrated significantly higher ECVspleen and lower IWRliver values. Although individual parameters exhibited moderate discriminatory ability, combining these two CT parameters improved diagnostic accuracy, as evidenced by an increased AUC of 0.832. The synergistic power of combining ECVspleen and IWRliver lies in their ability to capture complementary aspects of the disease process. While ECVspleen reflects the systemic sequelae of long-standing PH, IWRliver probes the primary intrahepatic dysfunction in microvascular hemodynamics. This multi-parametric approach offers a more holistic, pathophysiologically-grounded assessment. Moreover, the high sensitivity of 90.00% underlined the capability of the combined index to accurately identify patients with severe PH. By leveraging routine contrast-enhanced CT scans, this method could potentially facilitate widespread screening for severe PH, obviating the need for invasive HVPG measurements in certain clinical scenarios. It could also be invaluable for monitoring disease progression or response to pharmacotherapy in longitudinal studies.

In this study, PLT effectively distinguished CSPH from healthy controls, underscoring its value as a widely available screening marker for advanced liver disease. This discriminative ability is mechanistically rooted in PH-induced splenic congestion, leading to splenomegaly, hypersplenism and platelet sequestration [34, 35]. In this context, PLT serves as a functional surrogate for the morphological change of splenic enlargement. However, in our multivariate model that incorporated direct quantitative CT markers of tissue remodeling, PLT was not an independent predictor of HVPG severity. This discrepancy may be explained by the fact that thrombocytopenia is influenced by multiple factors beyond splenomegaly, limiting its specificity as a direct biomarker for PH [36]. Spleen volume, as a well-established anatomical marker of PH [37], was not quantitatively analyzed in our study. Nevertheless, the diagnostic performance of PLT indirectly highlights the central pathophysiological role of the spleen in CSPH. This understanding further rationalizes our investigation into more direct, tissue-level markers of splenic involvement. We propose that the pathophysiological information reflected by PLT is effectively encapsulated and refined by imaging biomarkers such as ECVspleen, which quantifies the underlying tissue changes (e.g., congestion, fibrosis) that cause splenomegaly and hypersplenism. Thus, ECVspleen may provide a more fundamental and less confounded assessment of the portal hypertensive state.

Furthermore, we incorporated the FIB-4 index into our analysis to address potential confounding by hepatic fibrosis. Notably, the FIB-4 index itself was not significantly associated with CSPH in our cohort. This observation provides crucial insight into the distinct pathophysiological pathways reflected by different non-invasive markers. While the FIB-4 index is a well-validated estimator of hepatic fibrosis [22], our results suggest that in this specific population of patients with advanced chronic liver disease, the burden of PH may not be solely dictated by the degree of architectural fibrosis as captured by FIB-4. Other factors, such as intrahepatic vascular tone and dynamic vascular changes, likely play a more dominant role in maintaining portal pressure at this late disease stage. Importantly, this finding strengthens the specificity of our primary results. Therefore, our CT-derived parameters may offer a more direct and dynamic window into the hemodynamic alterations of PH, rather than merely reflecting the underlying fibrotic substrate.

This study had several limitations. First, inherent constraints of the single-center retrospective design and the selection bias introduced by the composition of our control group comprising cirrhotic patients without CSPH. These factors restrict the generalizability of our results across all stages of PH and necessitate verification in future studies that include the appropriate control group. Second, the absence of correlation between the CT parameters and HVPG values below 10 mmHg limits the broader applicability of our results. Third, HVPG was not measured in the control subjects for ethical and clinical reasons. Fourth, the lack of a validation cohort restricts the ability to confirm the findings universally. Thus, further studies with larger sample sizes across various stages of PH are essential for validating the accuracy and utility of ECVliver, ECVspleen, and IWRliver. Finally, beyond the scope of this study, the clinical application of our CT protocol must consider the potential risk of contrast-induced nephropathy in patients with pre-existing renal impairment.

Conclusions

ECVspleen and IWRliver, derived from routine contrast-enhanced CT, demonstrated potential as noninvasive biomarkers for risk stratification of PH, whereas ECVliver exhibited relatively limited predictive utility. Further investigations, ideally incorporating a broader range of portal pressure (including HVPG < 10 mmHg), are warranted to validate the clinical applicability of these parameters.
