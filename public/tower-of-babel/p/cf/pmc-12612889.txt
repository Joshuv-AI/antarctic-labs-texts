Tuning of Optoelectronic Properties in Oligo(phenyleneethynylene)-Based Cocrystals through Modulation of Charge-Transfer Interactions
Authors: Sumayya Kuttiyullathil, Sanal Vasantha Sudhakaran, David Stephen Arputharaj, Mohamed Hussien, Reji Thomas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Organic multicomponent cocrystals with semiconducting properties have gained wide interest due to their potential in solid-state optoelectronic applications. Herein, we report the design and synthesis of an oligo­(phenyleneethynylene) (OPE) derivative and its cocrystals with 2,3,5,6-tetrachloroterephthalonitrile (TCPN) and 1,2,4,5-tetracyanobenzene (TCNB) to explore the optical properties and charge-transfer interactions. The optical property of the charge-transfer cocrystals was dependent on the in-crystal geometry of luminophore OPE and charge-transfer interactions. The study also explored the role of molecular packing and intermolecular interactions on photoluminescence properties in the crystalline state of OPE, OPE–TCPN, and OPE–TCNB using structural data obtained from single-crystal X-ray diffraction. The theoretical investigation of the molecular assemblies demonstrated that the mixed-stacked systems allow a broader range of tunability in electronic properties compared to single-component systems. Combining different molecules (TCPN and TCNB) of complementary electronic structures of OPE, we could modulate parameters such as the band gap, charge carrier mobility, and energy levels more precisely.

Introduction

Organic multicomponent cocrystals of π-conjugated molecules with semiconducting properties gained paramount interest owing to their scope for various solid-state optoelectronic applications. , Depending on the carrier transport, various cocrystalline systems found applications in field-effect transistors (FETs), , photovoltaic cells, photoconductivity, ferroelectricity, magneto electricity, etc. To obtain crystalline systems with tailor-made optoelectronic properties, cocrystal engineering was developed as a tool where the subtle control on molecular packing and weak intermolecular interactions is crucial. − Although several efforts have been made toward cocrystal engineering, considerable attention has been received after the organic charge-transfer (CT) cocrystal, tetrathiafulvalene and 7,7,8,8-tetracyanoquinodimethane (TTF–TCNQ), was found to conduct over a wide range of temperatures. In general, the organic CT cocrystals possess an alternating molecular stacking of electron-rich donor (D) and electron-deficient acceptor (A) molecules with evident charge transfer from the donor to acceptor. The possibility for the control over donor and acceptor partner molecules in cocrystals endows crystal systems with the advantage of tunable optical and electronic properties such as stimulus-responsive photoluminescence, , optical waveguide behavior, , photoconductivity, , and semiconducting properties etc. ,

The significance of CT cocrystallization lies in its scope for tunability of the optical and electronic properties that can be accomplished by varying the strength of charge-transfer interaction realized by the changing the donor/acceptor components. − In addition to the difference in the molecular component, the molecular packing, stoichiometry of the cocrystal components, and polymorphism were also found to be influential in electronic structure of the cocrystals. − Nowadays, there are several reports on modulation of optoelectronic properties of CT cocrystals achieved by varying the donor or acceptor component by maintaining one of the components fixed. , A report by Jiang et al. demonstrated the fluorescence tuning by modulation of charge transfer and π–π interaction in different cocrystals prepared from carbazole derivatives with tetracyanobenzene (TCNB). The cocrystals formed by chalcone derivatives and phenanthrene derivatives with TCNB have also shown fluorescence switching by modulating the charge-transfer interactions through molecular packing. A recent report demonstrated the thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) in cocrystals formed by stilbene and tetracyanobenzene (TCNB), wherein CT mediates in the reduction of the singlet–triplet energy gap. Some of the recent studies also explored variation of charge carrier mobility in charge-transfer cocrystals influenced by CT, molecular arrangement, and intermolecular interactions. , Most of the reports on exploration of optoelectronic properties of charge-transfer cocrystals have been carried out on derivatives of polyaromatic hydrocarbons or heterocyclic systems such as phenothiazine, carbazole, etc. Although there are some reports on CT cocrystals of phenylenevinylene-based linear π-conjugated molecules as donors in charge-transfer cocrystals, the reports on oligo­(phenyleneethynylene) OPE derivatives as the donor component in CT cocrystals are limited. One of the reports on OPE-based donor–acceptor systems demonstrated the hypsochromic emission switching by controlling the conformation of the OPE backbone through noncovalent interaction originating from a covalently bonded fluorinated benzyl ester.

Oligo­(phenyleneethynylene) systems are well explored for their intriguing optoelectronics properties in their solid and solution states. The solution and solid-state luminescent properties of various OPE systems were previously explored in conjunction with structural tunability achieved by free rotation of π-systems. − The free rotation of phenyl groups in OPE about the triple bond provides a scope for various degrees of planarity and different molecular packings in the crystalline state. Hence, it can be envisaged that the insertion of OPE as a partner in CT cocrystals could give rise to diverse packing modes and charge-transfer interactions arising from varying degrees of planarity, facilitating the modulation of optoelectronic properties.

Herein, we investigate the tuning of solid-state photoluminescence properties of a propyloxy-substituted OPE and its cocrystals with acceptors 2,3,5,6-tetrachloroterephthalonitrile (TCPN) and 1,2,4,5-tetracyanobenzene (TCNB) (see Scheme ). The molecule TCNB with tetracyano substitution is a strong electron acceptor, whereas TCPN, a less explored acceptor, is expected to show moderate electron affinity compared to TCNB. Moreover, the TCPN with chloro substitution is likely to form different halogen contacts with OPE on cocrystallization. The cocrystallization of OPE with acceptor moieties of different electronic structures and intermolecular interaction possibilities can lead to diverse optoelectronic properties. The study probes the impact of molecular structure, packing, and intermolecular charge transfer on photoluminescence properties of OPE–TCPN and OPE–TCNB cocrystals. The influence of single-component and mixed-stacking cocrystal structures on charge-transfer properties in OPE systems is also investigated. By combining different molecules with complementary electronic structures, it becomes possible to modulate parameters, such as band gap, charge carrier mobility, and energy levels, more precisely. The comparison of mobility parameters of OPE-based cocrystals to that of similar systems reported in the literature pointed out the improved electron mobility of the OPE–TCNB cocrystal. − By elucidating the fundamental principles governing charge transfer in single-component and mixed-stacking cocrystal organic semiconductors, this work aims to provide insights into strategies for enhancing the performance and functionality of organic electronic devices.

Experimental Section

All chemicals and solvents required for the synthesis and photophysical studies were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Alfa Aesar, and TCI and used without further purification. The 1H NMR spectra and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance 400 MHz NMR spectrometer operating at a temperature of 25 °C. The UV–visible absorption spectra were recorded on Agilent Technologies Carey Bio50 (solution) and Carey 5000 UV–visible absorption spectrometers. Fluorescence spectra were obtained using Horiba-Jobin-Yvon Fluorolog-3–11 and Edinburgh FLS 1000 Spectrofluorimeters. The quantum yield was measured on Edinburgh FLS 1000 instrument attached with an integrating sphere. The time-resolved fluorescence lifetime experiments were performed on a time-correlated single photon counting system (TCSPC) Fluorocube (Horiba-Jobin-Yvon). The single-crystal X-ray data were collected from Bruker Kappa Apex (OPE) and Bruker D8 Venture (OPE–TCPN and OPE–TCNB) single-crystal X-ray diffractometers, and mass spectra of the compound OPE were recorded on a Waters Xevo G2 XF QToF mass spectrometer.

Results and Discussion

The fluorophore 4,4′-((2,5-dipropoxy-1,4-phenylene)­bis­(ethyne-2,1-diyl))­dibenzaldehyde (OPE) was synthesized by Sonogashira coupling between 1,4-diethynyl-2,5-dipropoxybenzene and 4-bromobenzaldehyde. ,, Scheme S1 shows the detailed synthetic route toward the synthesis of compound OPE. The compound OPE was characterized by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy (see Figures S1–S10), mass spectrometry, and single-crystal X-ray diffraction (see Table S1). The crystals of pristine OPE and cocrystals of OPE with 2,3,5,6-tetrachloroterephthalonitrile (TCPN) and 1,2,4,5-tetracyanobenzene (TCNB) were obtained by slow evaporation from dilute solutions in toluene.

Solution-State Photophysical Properties of OPE

To explore the solution-state photophysical properties of OPE, the UV–visible spectrum of the compound was recorded from a solution in tetrahydrofuran (6 μM) (see Figure , black curve). The spectrum showed two major bands centered at 328 and 392 nm corresponding to π to π* and n to π* transitions, respectively. The solution-state photoluminescence spectrum of OPE is recorded from the same solution showing a broad band with a maximum at 440 nm and shoulders at 465 and 407 nm, respectively (see Figure , blue line). The solution state transient spectrum of photoluminescence of OPE was fitted with a single exponential decay (2.32 ns) indicating that the single emitting species contributes to the emission in the solution state (see Figure S11).

Solid-State Photophysical Properties of OPE and Cocrystals

The solid-state UV–visible absorption spectra were recorded for crystalline sample OPE and its cocrystals with TCPN and TCNB. All of the spectra showed different features with multiple maxima and shoulder bands (see Figure a). The spectrum corresponding to the crystalline OPE showed a maximum centered at 423 nm with a shoulder band at 336 nm. Cocrystal OPE–TCPN showed a broad spectrum with maximum ranging from 347 to 412 nm. The cocrystal of OPE–TCNB also showed a broad absorption with multiple bands ranging from 218 to 546 nm. The broad absorption with multiple bands observed in the cocrystals can be attributed to charge-transfer interactions in the solid state. To explore the photoluminescence properties of crystalline samples of OPE, OPE–TCPN, and OPE–TCNB, the photoluminescence spectra were recorded at room temperature by excitation at 400 nm. The compound OPE showed a red-shifted emission in the solid state compared to that recorded from dilute solution in THF with a maximum at 524 nm (see Figure , red curve).

Crystal Structure Analysis and Structure Property Correlation

To obtain an insight into the photoluminescence properties of OPE, OPE–TCPN, and OPE–TCNB, a detailed analysis of molecular packing and intermolecular interaction is conducted. Since the molecular alignment and weak intermolecular interaction determine the optical properties of the molecules in the solid state, the information drawn from the crystal structure can precisely predict the origin of the observed photoluminescence properties of these samples. The molecule OPE crystallized in the monoclinic, C2/c space group with half molecules in the asymmetric unit, whereas the cocrystals OPE–TCPN and OPE–TCNB crystallized in monoclinic, P21/c, and triclinic, P-1, space groups, respectively. The structure of OPE–TCPN showed half molecules of OPE and full molecules of TCPN in the asymmetric unit, while the asymmetric unit of OPE–TCNB contains a half molecule of both OPE and TCNB (see Figure S12 and Table S1). To improve the structural analysis beyond routine refinement, Hirshfeld Atom Refinement (HAR) was performed for both OPE–TCPN and OPE–TCNB cocrystals using the HARt module in Olex2. This approach resulted in significant enhancements in refinement quality, including reduced R 1 and wR 2 values, as well as more accurate modeling of anisotropic displacement parameters (ADPs) for hydrogen atoms. The HAR-refined electron density maps also provided clearer insights into noncovalent interactions, which are critical to the stability and charge-transfer efficiency of the cocrystals.

A detailed analysis of molecular packing can give input into the emission characteristics of luminogen OPE in various crystalline environments. The molecule OPE adopted different conformations in response to the crystalline environment and intermolecular interaction present. In pure OPE crystals, the molecules showed an intramolecular twist about the terminal phenyl rings resulting in a twist angle of 26.8°, whereas the out-of-plane twist in OPE is increased in the OPE–TCPN cocrystal with an angle of 48.4°. The conformation of OPE was nearly planar in OPE–TCNB with a twist angle of 7.3°. It is noteworthy that the molecule OPE in its pristine crystalline form adopts a slipped stacking along the long molecular axis and an X-type stacking crossing at the middle phenyl ring (see Figure a and b). The slip stacked molecular pairs of OPE comprise a J-aggregate giving rise to red-shifted photoluminescence in the solid state compared to the solution state. The crossed arrangement in OPE can contribute to an X-type aggregate which was characterized by improved quantum yield. The OPE molecules in the OPE–TCPN cocrystal did not show any close OPE–OPE interaction (see Figure c). The OPE molecules along the c-axis showed a mixed stack with the TCPN molecule giving rise to the inter OPE spacing of 7.24 Å and TCPN to OPE distance of 3.64 Å. The large inter OPE spacing is indicative of the absence of dipolar coupling between OPE molecules in OPE–TCPN. Figure d shows the important molecular packing features present in OPE–TCNB. Similar to pristine OPE crystals, the OPE molecules in the OPE–TCNB crystal form a J-aggregate pair with a slip along the long molecular axis and an interplanar distance of 3.32 Å. Furthermore, the molecules OPE and TCNB form a mixed stack along the a-axis with a OPE–TCNB distance of 3.40 Å. From the molecular packing analysis, it is evident that the planar structure along with J-aggregate formation and charge-transfer interaction between OPE (donor) and TCNB (acceptor) leads to red-shifted emission in the OPE–TCNB crystal (Figure d and e). The observed low quantum yields in OPE–TCPN and OPE–TCNB can be attributed to the charge-transfer interactions present in these cocrystals. In OPE–TCPN, the OPE molecules are well separated with large molecular twists causing emission at shorter wavelengths compared to pristine OPE crystals. From the analysis of in-crystal geometry of OPE molecules, it is evident that the molecular planarity plays a crucial role in deciding the photoluminescence maximum in the crystalline state. The OPE–TCPN with maximum twist in the OPE molecule showed blue-shifted emission compared to that of the pristine OPE crystals, while the OPE–TCNB system showed red-shifted emission with nearly planar OPE molecules in the cocrystal. A similar observation of planarity-dependent emission switching was observed in a fluorinated benzyl ester-functionalized OPE derivative, wherein the noncovalent interaction between OPE and fluorinated benzyl ester led to nonplanar conformation of the OPE backbone resulting in blue-shifted emission.

Analysis of Intermolecular Interactions

Molecule OPE and its cocrystals OPE–TCPN and OPE–TCNB have showed various types of nonbonding interactions in the crystalline state. The major category of weak intermolecular interactions in OPE and its cocrystals includes C–H···O and π–π stacking (see Tables S2 and S3). The crystals of OPE form a pair of C–H···O interactions along with π–π stacking involving two terminal phenyl rings and two middle phenyl rings of OPE molecules (Figure S13).

The cocrystal OPE–TCPN also showed various nonbonding interactions, including C–H···O and π–π stacking. Additionally, the cocrystals showed a pair of intermolecular chlorine contact with the oxygen atom of the aldehyde group in OPE and the nitrogen atom of the cyano group in TCPN (Figure S14). In the cocrystals, OPE and TCPN formed a stack, wherein each OPE molecule is separated by two TCPN molecules as seen in Figure c. In addition, the TCPN molecules form a zigzag arrangement through chlorine–oxygen contacts. The cocrystal OPE–TCNB showed various C–H···N interactions in a single molecular layer with an average H···N distance of 2.55 Å, while a C–H···O interaction is formed between the aldehyde group of OPE and the TCNB molecule (see Figure S15). The molecular layers stack through various π–π interactions involving the triple bond of OPE and the phenyl ring on TCNB. As seen in Figure S15b, OPE molecules form a slipped arrangement, linked though π–π stacking interactions between the sides of the terminal phenyl rings of the OPE molecules. The interaction energies for the π···π-stacked donor–acceptor (D–A) dimer in the OPE–TCPN and OPE–TCNB cocrystals, calculated at the B3LYP/6-31 + G­(d,p) level and corrected for basic set superposition error (BSSE), are −3.12 and −0.22 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating a strong π···π stacking interaction. In contrast, the π···π interaction energy for the OPE crystal structure is found to be −0.12 kcal/mol.

Hirshfeld Surface Analysis

Analysis of the Hirshfeld surface (dnorm) and 2D fingerprint plots provide insights into the noncovalent interactions present in the crystal packing of OPE, OPE–TCPN, and OPE–TCNB. In the OPE crystal structure, prominent large red spots (see Figure ) on the dnorm surface signify strong C–H···O hydrogen bonds, whereas large red spots indicate Cl···N and Cl···O interactions in OPE–TCPN and O···H interactions in OPE–TCNB cocrystals, underscoring their stronger interaction strength. Conversely, weak π···π stacking and C–H···π hydrogen bonds in all the studied structures are depicted by large white and blue regions on the dnorm surface, suggesting weak interactions at van der Waals distances. In the 2D fingerprint plot, sharp spikes represent the significant contribution of C–H···O interactions in OPE (14.9% contribution), followed by π···π stacking interactions (6.7% contribution), while in OPE–TCPN and OPE–TCNB, the contribution of π···π stacking interactions is 7.6% and 10.6%, respectively.

Optical and Orbital Analysis

The electronic structure and charge-transfer dynamics of OPE, OPE–TCPN, and OPE–TCNB were investigated by using density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations at the B3LYP/6-31 + G­(d,p) level. For pristine OPE, the HOMO is delocalized across the central phenyleneethynylene backbone, while the LUMO resides predominantly on the terminal aldehyde-substituted phenyl rings (Figure S16a). This intramolecular charge-transfer (ICT) character aligns with the observed redshift in solid-state emission (524 nm) compared to solution (440 nm), as the rigid crystalline environment restricts rotational freedom, enhancing conjugation and stabilizing the excited state. In contrast, the cocrystals exhibit distinct HOMO–LUMO distributions: for OPE–TCPN, the HOMO localizes on the OPE donor, and the LUMO on the TCPN acceptor (Figure S16b), while OPE–TCNB shows similar donor–acceptor separation with a stronger orbital overlap due to the planarized OPE conformation (Figure S16c). This spatial separation of Frontier orbitals in cocrystals facilitates intermolecular charge transfer (ICT), as evidenced by the broadened absorption spectra (Figure a) and red-shifted emission in OPE–TCNB (618 nm). The calculated band gaps for OPE (3.00 eV), OPE–TCPN (2.65 eV), and OPE–TCNB (2.59 eV) correlate well with experimental values derived from absorption edges, confirming the role of acceptor strength (TCNB > TCPN) in reducing the effective band gap through enhanced CT interactions.

The ground-state dipole moment in cocrystals, oriented from OPE (donor) to the acceptor, further corroborates the CT character. For OPE–TCNB, the larger dipole moment (2.38 D) compared to OPE–TCPN (0.51 D) reflects the stronger electron-withdrawing ability of TCNB, consistent with its lower LUMO energy (−3.59 eV vs −3.19 eV for TCPN). This aligns with the significant redshift in OPE–TCNB emission, where CT interactions dominate the excited-state relaxation.

The broader absorption bands in cocrystals (Figure a) arise from overlapping π–π transitions of OPE and CT transitions between the donor and acceptor. The weak CT band in OPE–TCPN (∼468 nm) and the pronounced CT feature in OPE–TCNB (∼478 nm) correlate with their respective orbital overlaps and stacking geometries. The TD-DFT calculations show excellent agreement with experimental UV–visible and emission data for OPE and its cocrystals. The calculated absorption maxima (OPE: 462 nm, OPE–TCPN: 468 nm, OPE–TCNB: 478 nm) (Figure S17) closely match the observed redshifts in the solid-state spectra. Moreover, the HOMO–LUMO separation across donor–acceptor moieties and decreasing band gaps confirm the increasing charge-transfer nature, especially in OPE–TCNB. These results validate the computational model and support the interpretation of the CT-driven optical behavior. Although the TD-DFT calculations reproduce the key experimental absorption bands and afford important information regarding the orbital localization and charge-transfer nature of the transitions, this approach has some limitations. In particular, standard hybrid functionals might underestimate excitation energies of the charge-transfer type, and our cluster-based models do not account well for charge polarization and long-range crystal-field-effects in the solid state. In addition, vibronic coupling and spin–orbit couplings have not been taken into account; these effects could contribute to other photophysical decay channels. The nearly planar OPE conformation in OPE–TCNB (twist angle = 7.3°) enhances the π-orbital overlap with TCNB, promoting stronger electronic coupling (J-aggregate-like stacking), whereas the twisted OPE–TCPN structure (48.4°) limits dipolar coupling, resulting in weaker CT interactions.

Notably, the reduced photoluminescence quantum yield (ΦF) in cocrystals OPE–TCNB and OPE–TCPN (0.02 and 0.06) arises from competing nonradiative pathways. Although the strong π–π interactions in both these systems (3.35 and 3.39 Å) facilitate exciton delocalization, they also enhance vibronic coupling, accelerating nonradiative decay. Conversely, the comparatively larger intermolecular spacing between OPE molecules in pristine crystals (3.73 Å) minimizes the exciton quenching with improved ΦF (0.29). The low quantum efficiency in cocrystalline forms suggests that the CT state provides an alternative pathway for the excited molecule to lose energy, making the system less emissive. These findings are consistent with prior studies on CT cocrystals, where interfacial dipole alignment and stacking geometry critically govern the emission efficiency.

Charge Mobility

The ionic properties of the three molecules OPE, OPE–TCPN, and OPE–TCNB reveal distinct charge transport behaviors based on their ionization potentials, electron affinities, and extraction potentials. OPE exhibits moderately high ionization and low electron affinity values, favoring p-type conduction with limited n-type potential, making it suitable for applications in which hole transport is desired. OPE–TCPN, with a slightly lower ionization potential and increased electron affinity compared to OPE, suggests efficient electron transport, which could support n-type conduction efficiently. This nature was further enhanced in that OPE–TCNB displays the highest electron affinity and extraction potential of the three, indicating strong n-type behavior as it readily accepts and stabilizes added electrons. The potential application of charge transfer in optoelectronic devices can be assessed by evaluating electron (t e) and hole (t h) transfer integrals. Two pathways, direct and superexchange mechanisms, facilitate the transfer of electrons and holes. In the mixed-stack cocrystals OPE–TCPN and OPE–TCNB, the superexchange mechanism is notably significant. The single component OPE crystallizes in the centrosymmetric C2/c space group along the crystallographic a–c plane, and OPE–TCPN and OPE–TCNB were crystallized in the centrosymmetric P21/c and P-1 space group with a mixed stacking arrangement along the crystallographic a–b plane. Due to the centrosymmetric crystal packing with mixed stacking along a specific crystallographic axis, calculating superexchange charge-transfer integrals via the energy-splitting method is feasible.

The OPE-based cocrystals show remarkable advantages over a wide variety of benchmark charge-transfer (CT) systems, DPTTA, DBTTF, DBPP, Perylene–TCNQ, Coronene–F4TCNQ, and CN-TFPA-based derivatives such as 4M-DSB-CN-TFPA and 2MDCS-CN-TFPA. Most notably, OPE–TCNB has a spectacular electron mobility of 2.51 cm2/V·s, far exceeding that of DPTTA–F2TCNQ (0.47 cm2/V·s) and 4M-DSB-CN-TFPA (0.067 cm2/V·s), several orders of magnitude greater than that of Coronene–F4TCNQ (1.56 × 10–3 cm2/V·s) and Perylene–TCNQ (2.1 × 10–5 cm2/V·s). Strong n-type performance is also delivered by OPE–TCPN (μe = 0.54 cm2/V·s), confirming the potential of the OPE scaffold as a tunable unipolar semiconducting platform. Unlike many of these systems, which require complex extended π-frameworks or suffer from limited stacking interactions, the OPE cocrystals are formed via simple cocrystallization, offering both high performance and synthetic accessibility. This highlights the effectiveness of supramolecular design in achieving high electron mobility and polarity control, positioning the OPE series as a novel and practical alternative for next-generation organic electronics. It should be noted that charge carrier mobilities of ∼10–3–10–1 cm2 V–1 s–1 are commonly regarded as moderate for organic semiconductors and those exceeding 1 cm2 V–1 s–1 for high performance in field-effect transistors. The calculated electron mobility of the OPE–TCNB cocrystal (2.51 cm2 V–1 s–1) thus falls within the regime of high performance, holding promise for room-temperature OFET devices and solar cells provided that desirable film orientation and low defect densities can be obtained. With the lower hole mobility of the pristine OPE (0.108 cm2 V–1 s–1) and lower quantum efficiency of the cocrystals, it may be inferred that these systems are less fit for emissive optoelectronic devices. Overall, the calculations suggest that charge-transport characteristics are promising for device integration, even though the photoluminescence efficiency remains a limiting factor.

The present investigation demonstrates the tuning of optoelectronic properties by modulation of charge-transfer interactions and molecular packing by cocrystallization of OPE with TCPN and TCNB (see Scheme ). While pristine OPE displays moderate emission alongside hole transport, its cocrystals with TCPN and TCNB show n-type conduction. Among the cocrystals, OPE–TCNB performs best on account of the higher charge-transfer interactions and better molecular stacking. The present investigation demonstrates the promise of supramolecular design toward precise tuning of emission and charge mobility in organic semiconductors. The tunability of photoluminescence achieved by modulating the charge-transfer interactions in OPE cocrystals has potential for the fabrication of tunable optoelectronic devices. However, the limitation of the present systems is the low quantum yield on crystallization due to intermolecular charge transfer, where this could be surpassed by modifying the strength of donor–acceptor interaction through a suitable selection acceptor moiety.

Conclusion

In summary, we designed and synthesized oligophenyleneethynylene (OPE) and cocrystallized it with 2,3,5,6-tetrachloroterephthalonitrile (TCPN) and 1,2,4,5-tetracyanobenzene (TCNB). In contrast to the emission recorded from a dilute solution in THF, which had a lifetime of 2.32 ns and a quantum yield of 0.63, the compound OPE displayed a red-shifted emission in the solid form, with a maximum at 524 nm, a lifetime of 1.30 ns, and a quantum yield of 0.29. In comparison to pure OPE crystals, the cocrystals of OPE with TCPN exhibited a slightly blue-shifted emission with a lifetime of 5.95 ns and a quantum yield of 0.06, while the cocrystal of OPE–TCNB exhibited a red-shifted emission with a lifetime of 2.68 ns and a quantum yield of 0.02. Using structural information from single-crystal X-ray diffraction, the study also explored the role of molecular packing and intermolecular interactions in the crystalline states of OPE, OPE–TCPN, and OPE–TCNB. The study shows that the in-crystal geometry of the luminophore OPE and the intermolecular interactions, in particular, the π–π stacking in the crystalline state, have a profound connection to the optical properties of these charge-transfer cocrystals. Moreover, the research highlights the advantage of mixed-stacked systems in offering greater tunability of electronic properties, such as band gap and charge carrier mobility, compared to single-component systems. These findings underscore the potential of these cocrystals in advancing the field of organic semiconductors through precise modulation of their optoelectronic characteristics by controlling the charge-transfer interaction and molecular packing.
