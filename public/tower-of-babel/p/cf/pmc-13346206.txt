SERENA-6 and the regulatory limits of radiologic progression in modern oncology
Authors: Saeed Rafii, Humaid O Al-Shamsi, Adam Brufsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe emergence of real-time molecular monitoring has challenged traditional oncology paradigms, where radiologic progression has historically represented the primary trigger for therapeutic intervention. The SERENA-6 clinical trial introduced a novel treatment approach by adapting therapy based on ctDNA-detected molecular progression before radiologic progression in patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer with emergent ESR1 mutations. The trial has prompted important discussion regarding the integration of molecular biomarkers into treatment decision-making and regulatory frameworks.MethodsWe reviewed the ODAC deliberations, regulatory discussions, published data, and expert perspectives surrounding SERENA-6 to examine the clinical, scientific, and regulatory implications of ctDNA-guided treatment adaptation and molecularly adaptive trial designs.ResultsThe SERENA-6 trial demonstrated significant improvements in progression-free survival, durable disease control, and quality of life outcomes. However, important considerations remain regarding the optimal timing of treatment adaptation, evidence required to support molecularly guided switching strategies, interpretation of crossover and survival outcomes, and the regulatory evaluation of adaptive precision oncology trials.ConclusionAs oncology transitions toward biologically driven treatment strategies, new clinical and regulatory frameworks are needed to define when molecular alterations should become actionable. SERENA-6 challenges the traditional reliance on radiologic progression as the sole trigger for therapeutic intervention and provides important lessons for the future development and evaluation of molecularly adaptive precision oncology strategies beyond breast cancer.

Introduction

On 30 April 2026, the FDA’s Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee (ODAC) voted 6–3 against recommending approval of camizestrant in combination with a CDK4/6 inhibitor for patients with hormone receptor (HR)-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer who develop emergent ESR1 mutations during first-line endocrine-based therapy. The decision resulted in divided views within the breast oncology community.

SERENA-6 represents a departure from conventional trial design. In patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer receiving first-line aromatase inhibitor (AI) plus CDK4/6 inhibition, treatment was adapted based on the emergence of ESR1 mutations detected in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), prior to radiologic progression. The result was a substantial improvement in median progression-free survival (mPFS) (HR 0.44; 95% CI, 0.31–0.60; P < 0.0001) [1]. Additionally, 30.4% of patients who were switched to camizestrant had durable disease control at 30 months, compared with 2.7% of those who remained on AI therapy [2], and a clinically meaningful delay in deterioration of quality of life. In contemporary breast oncology, hazard ratios of this magnitude have supported multiple practice-changing approvals.

However, the majority of ODAC members voted against approval not because of a lack of efficacy of camizestrant, but on the grounds that the trial did not demonstrate whether switching at molecular detection is superior to switching at radiologic progression.

At the heart of the controversy is whether molecular progression detected before radiologic progression represents a clinically actionable state that justifies treatment adaptation. More broadly, the SERENA-6 debate challenges the long-standing reliance on radiologic progression as the principal trigger for therapeutic change and raises important questions about whether current regulatory frameworks are sufficiently adapted to evaluate ctDNA-guided precision oncology strategies. This discussion is particularly relevant in the era of real-time molecular monitoring, where emerging resistance mechanisms can now be identified months before conventional imaging demonstrates overt disease progression.

Notably, the FDA itself granted Breakthrough Therapy Designation to camizestrant in May 2025 on the basis of the SERENA-6 interim data, formally acknowledging that the drug may demonstrate substantial improvement over available therapies in a life-threatening condition [2]. The discrepancy between this designation and a subsequent 6–3 vote against approval warrants critical scrutiny.

The inevitability of disease progression

ESR1 mutations are not passive biomarkers, but the dominant mechanism of acquired resistance to aromatase inhibition in HR-positive metastatic breast cancer [3, 4]. Their emergence reflects ligand-independent estrogen receptor signaling and loss of dependence on estrogen deprivation. Patients who develop ESR1 mutations are destined to experience disease progression on AI-based therapy. Importantly, ESR1 mutations become detectable in ctDNA months before radiologic progression [5]. This interval represents a phase during which resistant clones are actively expanding while disease remains anatomically below conventional imaging thresholds.

This molecular-to-clinical lag is precisely the therapeutic window that SERENA-6 was designed to exploit. Waiting for the scan to confirm what the mutation has confirmed only means to allow clonal expansion of a resistance mechanism that will increasingly govern subsequent aggressive disease behavior.

Rapid molecular progression and the risk of endocrine resistance

One of the most underappreciated findings from SERENA-6 was the behavior of the control arm following ESR1 emergence. Approximately 40% of patients continuing AI therapy experienced radiologic progression within six months of ESR1 mutation detection [1]. A similar pattern was observed in the control arm of the PADA-1 trial, in which a substantial proportion of patients developed radiologic progressive disease within six months of ESR1 mutation emergence, underscoring the reproducibility of this observation across independent datasets [5].

The speed of progression after molecular resistance emerges strongly suggests that, for a substantial subgroup of patients, endocrine therapy had already entered a state of functional futility at the molecular level. This observation invites an important conceptual parallel with the established European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) definition of primary endocrine resistance, which defines progression within six months of endocrine therapy initiation in the metastatic setting as evidence of endocrine futility [6].

Applying the same spirit of the ESMO definition, that the speed of progression relative to treatment initiation determines therapeutic futility, rapid radiological progression occurring within six months of ESR1 mutation detection on ctDNA in a significant proportion of the SERENA-6 control arm constitutes an equivalent acquired endocrine resistance state. We propose the term acquired molecular resistance with rapid phenotypic conversion to describe this clinical entity: patients with a confirmed ESR1 mutation on ctDNA who develop radiological progression within six months of molecular detection while continuing AI therapy. This is a prospectively identifiable and clinically actionable subgroup.

With nearly 40% of patients falling into this category, the argument that patients can simply be switched later at radiologic progression becomes considerably weaker. By the time of radiological progression, increased tumor burden, symptomatic deterioration, and loss of endocrine sensitivity may necessitate earlier transition toward chemotherapy or antibody drug conjugate-based treatment strategies. For this subgroup, the debate is not between early versus late camizestrant, but between pre-emptive endocrine intervention and endocrine resistance.

The concept of radiologic progression as the optimal trigger for treatment adaptation

The ODAC discussion also revealed a deeper methodological tension that increasingly confronts modern oncology trials. Historically, radiologic progression has been favored because it is measurable, reproducible, and standardized through RECIST criteria [7]. However, RECIST was never designed to identify resistance biology. It captures anatomical consequences of resistance after sufficient clonal expansion has already occurred. Tumors do not become resistant when scans change; scans change because tumors have already become resistant. This distinction becomes increasingly important as oncology transitions into an era of real-time molecular monitoring. SERENA-6 effectively asks whether a validated and targetable resistance mechanism should become actionable when it emerges biologically, rather than when its downstream anatomical consequences become visible radiographically.

This concept has regulatory precedent for approvals. Maintenance avelumab after platinum-based chemotherapy in urothelial carcinoma was approved based on the JAVELIN Bladder 100 trial in the absence of radiologic progression [8]. Maintenance PARP inhibitors following response to platinum chemotherapy in ovarian cancer are approved in patients without radiological disease progression [9]. Minimal residual disease-guided escalation strategies in hematologic malignancies similarly act on molecular evidence before overt relapse [10].

Crossover, quality of life, and PFS2

Several criticisms raised during ODAC deliberations deserve careful consideration. The absence of crossover leaves unresolved the question of whether early camizestrant is superior to camizestrant introduced at progression. However, the closest available evidence on the early-versus-late question comes from PADA-1, which supports early switching. In PADA-1, 68% of control arm patients who crossed over to fulvestrant plus palbociclib at radiological progression achieved a median subsequent PFS of just 3.5 months [5], directly contradicting the ODAC-implied strategy of “wait and switch.” The FDA agreed with the SERENA-6 trial design prospectively. Demanding that the trial answer a question it was not powered or designed to address is not a consistent regulatory standard.

Additional points were also raised including quality-of-life analyses were limited by declining questionnaire completion rates over time, and the time-to-deterioration analysis was exploratory rather than pre-specified in the original statistical analysis plan. PFS2 lacked blinded independent central review. These are legitimate scientific observations. At data cutoff 3, median PFS2 was 25.7 months with camizestrant versus 19.1 months with AI (HR 0.63; 95% CI 0.46–0.86; p = 0.00373) [2]. Chemotherapy-free survival also favoured camizestrant (HR 0.64), and the time to first subsequent therapy (TFST) was 17.0 versus 9.6 months (HR 0.43) [2]. These endpoints collectively capture benefit that reaches beyond the primary PFS signal.

Theoretical concerns also asked regarding clonal dynamics, specifically whether suppressing ESR1-mutant clones could allow expansion of alternative resistant populations previously constrained by AI therapy. This is a genuine biological question that merits investigation in translational sub-studies [11]. However, this concern is speculative in the context of SERENA-6’s actual results.

Overall survival in the modern therapeutic landscape

The absence of mature overall survival (OS) data was a major focus of ODAC deliberations. Yet OS in contemporary HR-positive metastatic breast cancer is increasingly confounded by sequential exposure to multiple active therapies, including PI3K inhibitors, AKT inhibitors, oral SERDs, and antibody drug conjugates [12, 13]. In such settings, OS may no longer cleanly isolate the contribution of earlier interventions. This is precisely why endpoints such as PFS, PFS2, chemotherapy-free survival, and quality of life have assumed increasing importance in metastatic breast cancer trials.

The absence of a regulatory framework for molecularly adaptive trials

Demanding definitive sequencing superiority before approval may also impose a regulatory burden that becomes increasingly impractical in precision oncology. A crossover design capable of resolving early-versus-late switching would require substantially larger sample sizes, longer follow-up, greater financial investment, and recruitment of patients into a rapidly evolving therapeutic landscape. Such a high regulatory threshold for biomarker-guided intervention, and absence of a clearly defined framework for evaluating ctDNA-guided adaptive trials in solid tumors will risk development of novel precision oncology precisely when molecular oncology is becoming increasingly sophisticated.

As the technology to identify molecular resistance in real time is already available, a regulatory paradigm is are increasingly needed to integrate such technology into therapeutic decision-making.

SERENA-6 should not be the last trial of its kind; it should be the one that catalyses the development of that framework.

Conclusion

SERENA-6 met its primary endpoint, quality of life is preserved, overall survival shows no detriment, and the safety profile is manageable with appropriate monitoring.

SERENA-6 challenges the established assumption that radiologic progression must remain the mandatory threshold at which intervention becomes justified, an assumption that is increasingly at odds with our modern understanding of the biology of molecular resistance, the capabilities of real-time ctDNA monitoring, and the precedents already established across multiple regulatory approvals that do not require prior radiologic progression as a condition of therapeutic action.

SERENA-6 teaches us that we are approaching an era where visible disease on the scan should not be the only determinant of intervention, particularly when an actionable molecular event will lead to a poorer outcome for our patients.
