Enhanced humidity sensing properties of Ta2O5 and ITO doped rutile-TiO2 porous ceramics
Authors: Jurimart Wongsricha, Kaniknun Sreejivungsa, Noppakorn Thanamoon, Viyada Harnchana, Pornjuk Srepusharawoot, Nutthakritta Phromviyo, Wirat Jarernboon, Prasit Thongbai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we investigated the humidity sensing properties of TiO2-based ceramics doped with tantalum pentoxide (Ta2O5) and indium tin oxide (ITO). Pure TiO2, 1%Ta-doped TiO2 (1%TTO), 1%ITO-doped TiO2 (1%ISTO), and 1%(Ta2O5 + ITO) co-doped TiO2 (1%ISTTO) ceramic samples were obtained by sintering at 1200 °C for 3 h. The rutile phase was observed in all samples. The lattice parameters of the single and co-doped samples were larger than those of pure TiO2, confirming the substitution of dopants. Porosity was observed in all ceramics. The mean grain sizes of all doped samples were significantly reduced compared to undoped TiO2. A homogeneous element dispersion was observed in the 1%TTO and 1%ISTTO ceramics, while segregation particles of related In-rich elements was observed in the 1%ISTO ceramic. Giant dielectric properties were not achieved in any samples due to the porosity. Nevertheless, excluding the undoped TiO2, the dielectric properties of all porous ceramics varied significantly with changes in humidity. The 1%ISTTO ceramic demonstrated superior humidity sensing properties, including a low maximum hysteresis error of 3.6% at 102 Hz. In contrast, the 1% TTO and 1% ISTO ceramics showed higher maximum hysteresis errors of 7.2% and 19.8%, respectively. Notably, the response and recovery times were 7.05 ± 0.18 and 2.48 ± 0.39 min, respectively, with good repeatability. This improvement is likely due to the synergistic effect of oxygen vacancies and TaTi· defects on the surface, enhancing the humidity sensing properties of the 1% ISTTO ceramic, coupled with its optimal microstructure due to its lowest porosity and grain size.Keywords: Rutile TiO2, Giant dielectric oxide, Humidity sensor, Hysteresis errorSubject terms: Materials science, Physics

Introduction

TiO2 can exhibit notable giant dielectric properties (GDPs) when co-doped with acceptor dopants (e.g., Ag + , Ni2+, Zn2+, Mg2+, Sc3+, Ga3+, Al3+, and In3+1–9) and donor dopants (e.g., Ta5+, Nb5+, and Sb5+1,3,8,10–12), which represent a significant area of application in capacitors1,5,12,13. The dielectric permittivity (εʹ) can be significantly enhanced (> 103), while maintaining a low loss tangent (tanδ < 0.1)1,10,11. Moreover, εʹ is stable with temperature10,11,13,14, making TiO2-based materials more suitable than CaCu3Ti4O12 (CCTO) and related ACu3Ti4O12 (ACTO) compounds, CuO, and NiO-based materials15–19. The GDPs of co-doped TiO2 arise from defects1,4,6–11, known as electron-pinned defect-dipoles (EPDDs)1,7,9. Additionally, surface barrier layer capacitor2,9,11,20,21 and internal barrier layer capacitor (IBLC)6,9,10,13,21 are also important factors. All of these contribute to the enhanced dielectric properties observed at ambient temperature9,22.

The capacitive humidity sensing capabilities of dielectric oxides are especially noteworthy. Capacitive humidity sensors (CHSs) operate by detecting changes in capacitance (Cp) resulting from variations in environmental relative humidity (RH). These changes are primarily due to the adsorption and desorption of water molecules on the surfaces or within the pores of the sensor materials16,23,24. The performance of oxide-based capacitive sensors is primarily influenced by factors such as the surface area, porosity, and intrinsic properties of the oxide used23–25. Different kinds of CHSs, including capacitive15,16,23,26,27, gravimetric28, thermal24, and resistive29,30 sensors, have been developed. Among these, CHSs have been the focus of extensive research. Ceramic oxides are often preferred over organic polymers31 and electrolytes32 for use in CHSs due to their superior thermal, chemical, and mechanical stability23,33. Research on the dielectric properties of CCTO23,34, ACTO15,35, Al2O336, (Sr,Ba)TiO337, CuO16, SnO2-based38, and TiO2-based26,27,33 oxides has demonstrated their effectiveness as CHSs, which are advantageous due to their simple capacitor structure. Furthermore, the Cp can be easily amplified using oscillator circuits23, enhancing the performance of sensors.

Recent research has focused on improving the sensitivity, response time, and stability of humidity sensors15,23,29,32–35. However, enhancing selectivity remains challenging. Selectivity is crucial for accurate humidity measurement without interference from other factors. Strategies to improve selectivity include using selective materials, functionalizing sensor surfaces with specific chemical groups, and employing advanced fabrication techniques to create highly porous structures24,25,32,39,40. Implementing these strategies can significantly enhance the selectivity, accuracy, and reliability of humidity sensors in various applications.

The development of Nb2O5-doped TiO2-based materials has been explored by co-doping with In2O3 and Na2CO3. Notably, the co-doped TiO2 ceramics (denoted as INTO for In2O3 + Nb2O5 and NNTO for Na2CO3 + Nb2O5) exhibited significant humidity-sensing properties26,27. Sensitivities of 229.22 pF/%RH and 102.6 pF/%RH at 102 Hz were achieved for INTO and NNTO, respectively. However, the maximum hysteresis errors for these materials were relatively high, at 7.3% and 4.2%, respectively, likely due to their dense ceramic microstructures. The humidity-sensing properties of porous co-doped TiO2 bulk materials have not been investigated. For CCTO-based materials15,23, the maximum hysteresis errors ranged from 9.2 to 20.1%. Given that TiO2 is abundant, low-cost, non-toxic, and a simple oxide, TiO2-based materials present a compelling alternative to CCTO-based oxides. Compared to humidity sensors made from 2D materials and polymers29,31,41,42, TiO2-based sensors offer advantages such as superior chemical stability and high melting points, allowing them to be heated to remove residual impurities for extended use43.

Previous studies have shown that Ta2O5-doped TiO2 materials, fabricated in various forms, demonstrate interesting dielectric responses and notable photocatalytic activity, indicating potential for a range of applications44,45. Unfortunately, the use of Ta2O5-doped TiO2 in humidity sensors has not been reported. Given that Ta5+ has the same ionic size as Nb5+ but with an additional electron shell, it may cause a larger distortion around the Ti3+ site in the Ta2O5-doped TiO2, potentially providing stronger interactions with H2O molecules in the air compared to Nb2O5-doped TiO2.

This study aims to address the gap in research on the humidity-sensing properties of porous bulk TiO2-based materials and to investigate Ta2O5-doped TiO2-based materials for this application. We aim to fabricate porous bulk ceramics and enhance the humidity-sensing properties of Ta2O5-doped TiO2-based materials. Therefore, the scope of this work is to study the humidity sensing properties of Ta2O5 + indium tin oxide (ITO) co-doped TiO2 ceramics by limiting the doping concentration to 1% only since a high concentration of co-dopants degrades the dielectric response in this material system46. This investigation was compared with undoped TiO2, Ta2O5-doped TiO2, and ITO-doped TiO2 ceramics. The incorporation of Sn ions into TiO2 can effectively lower the sintering temperature47. Phase compositions and microstructural analyses were conducted, revealing that the co-doped TiO2 exhibited promising humidity-sensing performance. The dielectric mechanisms underlying these properties are discussed in detail.

Experimental details

In this study, 1%Ta-doped TiO2 (1%TTO), 1%ITO-doped TiO2 (1%ISTO), and 1%(Ta2O5 + ITO) co-doped TiO2 (1%ISTTO) ceramics were prepared via the wet ball-milling method48. Raw materials consisted of high–purity starting materials: TiO2 (99.9%, Sigma–Aldrich), Ta2O5 (99%, Sigma–Aldrich), and In2O3:SnO2 (ISO) with a ratio of 95:5 wt% (99.99%, Nanostructured & Amorphous Materials, Inc.). These powders were mixed in ethanol for 24 h, employing a ball milling method. ZrO2 balls served as the milling media. Then, ZrO2 balls were separated from the starting powders and the ethanol was evaporated in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h. All resulting mixed powders were pressed under uniaxial pressure into pellets without calcination. These pellets underwent sintering at 1200 °C for 3 h. The sintered pure TiO2 was also fabricated under the same conditions.

The ceramic density was determined using the Archimedes method. Structural and microstructural analyses were performed using X-ray diffraction (XRD, PANalytical EMPYREAN) with Rietveld refinement, and focused ion beam-field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, FEI QUANTA 450) combined with SEM elemental mapping. For dielectric measurements, the outer surface layers of the as-sintered ceramics were removed to minimize the impact of the insulating surface layer. Silver paint was used to fabricate the electrodes, which were then annealed at 600 °C for 0.5 h. The dielectric properties were measured across a frequency range of 102–106 Hz at a root mean square voltage of 0.5 V using an impedance analyzer (KEYSIGHT E4990A).

For investigation of the effect humidity on the dielectric properties, the dielectric properties were evaluated at room temperature in an environmental chamber (ESPEC, SH-222), assessing their sensitivity to RH levels ranging from 30 to 95% RH. The hysteresis loops of Cp values were determined using a KEYSIGHT E4990A impedance analyzer within an SH–222 environmental chamber set at 25 °C and 102 Hz. The SH–222 was programmed to gradually raise the RH and maintain it at each increment for a predetermined time. Samples were secured in stationary holders within the chamber. To assess hysteresis errors, measurements were conducted by initially increasing the RH from 30 to 95%, then decreasing it back to 30%. The RH was adjusted in 5% increments, with an 8 min stabilization period before recording Cp values to ensure stable humidity. The RH increase rate was 4%/min. Actual RH was calibrated at the sample holder positions using a standard commercial humidity sensor, which had a fluctuation of ± 0.3% RH. Consequently, the RH fluctuation was approximately ± 0.3% for each step.

The response and recovery times were assessed in sealed conical flask with saturated solutions of LiCl (theoretical 11% RH, referred to as solution–A) and KNO3 (theoretical 96% RH, referred to as solution–B). Standard commercial humidity sensors recorded the exact %RH, which was noted as 30% RH for flask–A and 95% RH for flask–B. The probe of the commercial humidity sensor was placed alongside the sample in the sealed containers for all measurement steps. The RS PRO precision RLC Meter (RLC–6100) measured the response and recovery times by placing samples in the containers. The RLC–6100 program continuously measured Cp values at 0.47 s intervals. The procedure was as follows: the sample was placed in flask–A for 30 min to reach equilibrium at a low RH, with Cp values being continuously recorded. Then, the sample was transferred from flask–A to flask–B by changing the conical flask, while Cp values were recorded to assess the response time. Finally, the sample was returned to flask–A, with Cp values recorded throughout to examine the recovery time. Several rounds of loop measurements were conducted with varying holding times at 95% RH to reach equilibrium at a high RH. Response time is the duration to reach 90% of the total change during adsorption, and recovery time is the duration to reach 90% of the total change during desorption20,23,33. To ensure accuracy, the hysteresis error (γH) must be considered, calculated as γH=±ΔHmax/2FFS, where ΔHmax is the maximum Cp difference at identical RH levels during adsorption and desorption cycles, and FFS is the full scale of the Cp difference over the entire RH range tested20,23,26,33.

Results and discussion

Figure 1 illustrates the XRD patterns of sintered 1%TTO, 1%ISTO, and 1%ISTTO ceramics compared to TiO2. All patterns reveal the crystalline phases present in each sample, with distinct peaks corresponding to different crystallographic planes. The sintered TiO2 pattern serves as a reference for the pure material, showing characteristic peaks of the rutile TiO2 phase with a tetragonal structure (JCPDS 21–1276)5,7,11. Additionally, the rutile phase is confirmed by the highest peak at 2θ = 27.5°, while the primary peak of the anatase phase appears at around 2θ = 25.3°49,50. The 1%TTO sample exhibits a similar pattern, indicating that Ta doping ions likely substitute into the rutile crystal structure. However, the 1%ISTO pattern includes additional peaks, indicating the presence of an impurity phase, In2TiO5, due to the large ionic radius of In3+ compared to the Ti4+ host ion. This observation corresponds with findings in existing literature1,46. In contrast, the 1%ISTTO sample, co-doped with both Ta and ITO, displays only the main phase of rutile TiO2. This suggests that Ta5+ doping ions can enhance the incorporation of In3+ into the rutile structure, consistent with previous studies1,46.

Figure 2 shows the Rietveld refinement of all sintered ceramics. The structure parameters obtained from the refinement data are listed in Table 1. The Rietveld refinements for these ceramics demonstrate good fit quality, with slight variations in Rexp(%), Rp(%), Rwp(%), and GOF among the samples. These variations reflect differences in how well the model captures the observed data for each sample. It is evident that the lattice parameters of the 1%TTO and 1%ISTTO ceramics are significantly larger than those of the undoped TiO2, while the lattice parameters of the 1%ISTO are only slightly larger. This suggests that the dopants in the 1%TTO and 1%ISTTO ceramics can substitute into the rutile structure, resulting in an increase in lattice parameters due to the larger ionic radius of the doping ions compared to the host Ti4+ ions1,5. In contrast, the dopants in the 1%ISTO ceramic are less likely to integrate into the rutile structure, as indicated by the presence of a second phase of In2TiO5 in the XRD pattern of the 1%ISTO ceramic.

Figure 3 represents the SEM images of surface morphologies of TiO2, 1%ISTO, 1%TTO, and 1%ISTTO ceramics sintered at 1200 °C for 3 h. Doping TiO2 with ISO and Ta has remarkable effects on the microstructural evolution. As shown in Fig. 3a, pure TiO2 exhibits the largest grains (3.0 ± 2.4 μm) and highest density (96%), while the doped samples show progressively smaller grains and lower densities, as shown in Fig. 3b–d. According to Table 1 and Fig. 4, the mean grain size and relative densities of the 1%ISTO, 1%TTO, and 1%ISTTO ceramics are significantly lower than those of TiO2, with the 1%TTO and 1%ISTTO ceramics having much smaller grain sizes. This result indicates that Ta5+ doping ions inhibited grain growth in TiO2, a common observation in TiO2-based materials doped with Ta5+21. As shown in the insets of Fig. 3, a narrow grain size distribution is observed in all samples, indicating no abnormal grain growth during the sintering process. A careful inspection of Fig. 3b reveals small, irregular grains in the 1%ISTO ceramic, likely second-phase particles of In2TiO5, as confirmed by the XRD pattern in Fig. 1.

Grain size and relative density of all sintered samples.

The SEM–EDS mapping technique was performed to confirm the presence of second-phase particles of In2TiO5 in the 1%ISTO ceramic, as well as the dispersion of dopants and primary elements in the ceramic microstructure. As illustrated in Fig. 5, clear segregations of In and Sn elements are observed in the mapping images of the 1%ISTO ceramic, which are consistent with the XRD and SEM results. Notably, the dopant elements in the 1%TTO and 1%ISTTO ceramics are homogeneously dispersed in the microstructures, corresponding to the disappearance of the secondary phase in the XRD patterns of these two samples.

SEM–EDS mapping images of (a) 1%TTO, (b) 1%ISTO, and (c) 1%ISTTO ceramics.

This work focused on the variation in dielectric properties under a wide range of humidity conditions to study the potential applications of TiO2-based materials in CHSs. First, the εʹ and tanδ of all sintered porous ceramics were measured at 25 °C and in the frequency range of 102–106 Hz. As demonstrated in Fig. 6, within the frequency range of 103–106 Hz, the GDPs were not achieved in any of the samples. Furthermore, as illustrated in the inset, the tanδ of doped samples is large. A high εʹ of 104 was observed in the low–frequency range for the 1%ISTTO ceramic. According to the first–principles calculation, as demonstrated in Fig. S1 of the supplementary information, the EPDD was not favorable in the 1%ISTTO ceramic. Consequently, free electrons could not be tightly confined in the designed defect clusters, resulting in a high tanδ value. Although the IBLC structure in the 1%ISTTO ceramic is unlikely to be dominant due to its porous microstructure, if the EPDDs could be created, the GDPs might be observed. Unfortunately, both EPDDs and IBLC are not dominant, leading to a low dielectric response. Only the SBLC effect was dominant in the low–frequency range, resulting in a high εʹ in the frequency range below 103 Hz. Overall, the results indicate that excellent GDPs, suitable for capacitor applications, were not obtained in any of the samples12. However, particularly in the 1%TTO and 1%ISTTO ceramics, porous ceramics may offer interesting humidity sensing properties.

Dielectric permittivity (εʹ) at 25 °C in the frequency range of 102–106 Hz; inset illustrates loss tangent (tanδ).

To investigate the potential applications of sintered porous ceramics in CHSs, the dielectric properties were tested at different relative humidity levels and frequency at 102 Hz. It was found that the dielectric properties of undoped TiO2 ceramics did not significantly change with humidity, whereas those of doped TiO2 ceramics did. As shown in Fig. 7a–c, the Cp at 102 Hz of the 1%ISTO, 1%TTO, and 1%ISTTO ceramics significantly increased as the relative humidity rose from 30 to 95% RH. This indicates that the dielectric properties of these ceramics are sensitive to humidity, particularly at lower frequencies. This observation suggests a promising potential for these ceramics in CHS applications. In previous works22,46,48, we reported that the Cp of dense ISTTO ceramics and other dense TiO2-based materials remained stable under varying humidity. Therefore, the porosity of these ceramic samples may be a crucial factor contributing to the response of Cp to humidity. The γH quantifies the difference between the adsorption and desorption processes24,25,31. For the 1%ISTO sample, the maximum γH is quite large at 19.8%, indicating a significant difference between adsorption and desorption Cp values, which is not ideal for humidity sensing applications. For the 1%TTO sample, the maximum γH is 7.2%, lower than that of 1%ISTO, but still too high compared to other humidity sensing oxide materials. For the 1%ISTTO sample, the maximum γH is as low as 3.7%, suggesting its potential as a promising oxide material for CHS applications. The observed hysteresis suggests that these ceramics have different degrees of reversibility in their response to humidity changes. The lower hysteresis error in 1%ISTTO indicates a more stable dielectric response to humidity cycling23–25,33,37, making it potentially more suitable for applications requiring consistent performance under varying humidity conditions. The results suggest that the combination of IST and TTO in the 1%ISTTO ceramic improves the stability of the humidity response compared to the individual ISTO and TTO ceramics.

To further investigate the potential application of 1%ISTTO ceramic in CHSs, it is essential to study its response and recovery times, which are critical factors in determining suitable materials for such applications. Additionally, the repeatability of CHSs in response and recovery with humidity variations is crucial. According to the definition of response and recovery times of CHSs, the states of equilibrium at low and high RH must be confirmed. Note that only the 1%ISTTO ceramic was selected for detailed examination due to its lowest γH. Initially, we maintained the environment at 30% RH, continuously recording Cp values, as shown in Fig. 8a. The results indicate that Cp values remained stable from the start up to 1800s (30 min), with a Cp of ~ 0.15 nF, marking the state of equilibrium at low RH. Subsequently, the humidity of the environment was rapidly increased from 30 to 95% RH. To explore the state of equilibrium at high RH, we extended the measurement duration for each round to 10, 30, 50, and 60 min, continuously recording Cp values. As displayed in Fig. 8b, for the first round (R1 loop), when the humidity environment rapidly changed from 30 to 95% RH (by changing the closed flask) and holding for 10 min, the Cp dramatically increased, reaching approximately 1.42 nF. Then, the humidity environment was immediately changed from 95 to 30% RH, and Cp rapidly decreased to a saturated value of ~ 0.15 nF. In this round (R1), the equilibrium level at high RH may not have been reached. Next, when equilibrium at low RH was reached, the second round (R2 loop) was performed. It is likely that the equilibrium level at high RH began to be reached. Clearly, the equilibrium level was observed in R3 and R4 loops with holding times of 50 and 60 min, reaching ~ 1.58 nF. At this stage, the response and recovery times could be calculated. The averaged response and recovery times were found to be 7.05 ± 0.18 min and 2.48 ± 0.39 min, respectively. As demonstrated in Fig. 9, the comparative study of humidity sensing properties shows that the 1%ISTTO ceramic is one of the most promising oxide materials for CHSs due to its fast response and recovery times and low γH.

(a) Cp value at 30% RH (25 °C and 1 kHz) and (b) repeatability of response and recovery times of 1%ISTTO ceramics (25 °C and 1 kHz) in different relative humidities between 30 and 95%RH.

The change in Cp value with humidity can be attributed to porosity and the effects of the surface layer. Usually, the characteristics of sintered ceramics—such as surface area, intergranular pores, intragranular pores within individual grains, necks between grains, grain boundaries, and grain boundary pores—are known to act as receptors for H2O molecules51. According to the relative density and SEM images, a porous structure is evident. Therefore, the porous microstructure in the 1%ISTO, 1%TTO, and 1%ISTTO ceramics can be considered a significant factor contributing to the observed variation in Cp in response to humidity. The humidity sensing properties might be affected by the chemical composition and defects on the surface. Typically, the formation of Ti3+ in the TiO2 structure results from the substitution of + 5 ions, such as Ta5+ or Nb5+. In many sensing oxide materials, the presence of abundant crystal defects, particularly VO··, is a critical factor for enhancing sensitivity52–54, offering more advantages compared to polymers or metals. These VO··, acting as donor defects within metal oxides, serve as active sites for the adsorption of target molecules (e.g., gas or H2O in air), thereby boosting sensitivity. However, as displayed in Fig. 5, segregation of In element was observed in the 1%ISTO ceramic, indicating that In3+ ions did not substitute into the TiO2 structure. The relative densities of the 1%ISTO and 1%ISTTO ceramics are nearly the same in value. Thus, the enhanced sensing properties of the 1%ISTTO ceramic are attributed to the presence of VO··. Simultaneously, TaTi· defects resulting from the substitution of Ta5+ ions into Ti4+ can also react with H2O molecules via an electrostatic field, separating the absorbed H2O molecules into protons (H+) hydroxide (OH-) ions26,27,33,38:

In this scenario, the Ta5+ doping ions play a crucial role in the sensing process by creating active centers. The generated OHO·, OH-, and H+ interact with the point defects InTi′ and TaTi·. This results in the formation of complex dipoles, e.g., OHO·-OH-, InTi′-H+, InTi′-OHO·, and TaTi·-OH-. These complex dipoles can reorient under an applied electric field, enhancing the Cp value through orientation (dipolar) polarization. From this discussion, it is evident that the induced defects (VO·· and TaTi·) can act as detectors for H2O molecules (humidity) or active centers through chemical absorption. A low concentration of dopants in the 1%ISTTO ceramic produced a low content of defects. As humidity increases, more water molecules are absorbed, forming a continuous layer of H2O, resulting in proton transfers via the Grotthuss chain reaction55: H2O+H3O+→H3O++H2O, giving rise to an enhanced Cp. Combining these two mechanisms, each playing a significant role at different humidity levels, may explain the non-linear increase in the Cp value of 1%ISTTO ceramic with rising RH levels. According to Eq. (1), TaTi· defects can be created in both the 1%TTO and 1%ISTTO ceramics, a lower γH of the 1%ISTTO ceramic can be described by higher porosity (lower relative density) of the 1%ISTTO ceramic. Therefore, the significantly improved sensing properties of the 1%ISTTO ceramic were attributed to the synergistic effect of defects and its optimal microstructure.

Conclusion

This study underscores the importance of advanced ceramic materials in the development of highly sensitive and reliable sensors. Through careful experimentation with TiO2-based ceramics modified by tantalum and indium tin oxide (ITO) doping, it is evident that such modifications can significantly enhance the material properties and performance of CHSs. The findings from Rietveld refinement, which indicated increased lattice parameters in doped samples, along with homogeneous elemental dispersion as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy, suggest improved material structures as a result of doping. The standout performance of the 1%(Ta + ITO) co-doped TiO2 (1%ISTTO) sample, which demonstrated superior humidity sensing capabilities, low hysteresis error, and satisfactory response and recovery times, highlights the effectiveness of co-doping strategies. Furthermore, the variation in dielectric properties in response to changes in humidity validates the utility of this approach in practical sensor applications. The significantly enhanced humidity sensing properties were attributed to the synergistic effect of oxygen vacancies and TaTi· defect coupled with its optimal microstructure due to its lowest porosity and grain size. Overall, the enhancements observed in TiO2-based sensors through isovalent and tantalum doping provide a promising pathway for the advancement of sensor technology, advocating for further research and development in this area.
