Exploring crystallized and fluid intelligence in down syndrome using graph theory
Authors: Hefziba Lifshitz, Shlomit Shnitzer-Meirovich, Meny Koslovsky, Roi Yozevitch
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis cross-sectional study examined the cognitive performance of crystallized intelligence (Gc) and fluid intelligence (Gf) in 340 individuals, comparing adults (aged 22-45) to adolescents (aged 16-21) in two groups of etiologies. Down syndrome (DS) and non-specific intellectual disability (NSID). The aim was to estimate whether their cognitive performance reflected accelerated, stable, or continuous trajectories. Participants were assessed using the Vocabulary, Similarities, Block Design, and Raven Matrix tests. ANOVA analysis indicated that adults exhibited higher scores than adolescents on three of the crystallized and fluid intelligence tests, with similar trends observed in the Raven Matrix test, thus supporting the Compensation Age Theory. Participants with NSID exhibited higher scores in Vocabulary than participants with DS. Participants with DS exhibited higher scores in Block Design and Raven than participants with NSID. There was no difference between the groups in Similarities, suggesting that the verbal ability of individuals with DS is not so impaired relative to participants with NSID. Graph analysis demonstrated divergent Gc-Gf networks between the two groups of etiologies. The DS etiology revealed more coherent connections between crystallized and fluid intelligence, especially in adulthood, compared to the diffuse and absent connections seen in adults with NSID. Thus, the relative strength in Similarities and the more coherent Gc-Gf interconnections in the DS etiology suggested a more coherent and not-so-impaired profile in a clear diagnostic etiology such as DS, especially in adulthood, compared to unclear genetic etiologies such as NSID. The findings hold educational implications for adults with ID with and without Down syndrome at least until their 40’s as a time for growth and development, perhaps serving as a protective factor against possible cognitive decline in the future.Subject terms: Psychology, Computational science

Introduction

In recent years, individuals with intellectual disability (ID), including those with Non-Specific Intellectual-Disability (NSID) and Down Syndrome (DS), are experiencing increased longevity, with some reaching advanced ages beyond 80 years1,2. This extended lifespan has prompted critical inquiries about their ’cognitive trajectories’ - a term denoting the patterns of cognitive development - how they evolve, peak, and potentially decline over time. Our study delves into these trajectories, particularly focusing on how two core cognitive abilities-crystallized intelligence, the accumulation and use of knowledge, and fluid intelligence, the ability to solve novel problems-unfold and interact in adolescence and adulthood in individuals with NSID and DS.

Crystallized and fluid intelligence in intellectual disability

McGrew3 re-defined the Horn-Cattell model4 of crystallized and fluid intelligence. Crystallized intelligence (Gc) is the person’s acquired knowledge of a specific culture’s language, information, and concepts. It is considered a “maintained” ability that continues increasing into one’s 60’s and 70’s and then begins to decline5. The Vocabulary and Similarities sub-scales of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale (WAIS) IQ test6 may serve as indices of crystallized intelligence7.

Fluid intelligence (Gf) refers to the capacity for problem-solving and logical reasoning and is considered one of the most important features associated with various cognitive abilities8. Gf is considered a “vulnerable” ability that peaks in one’s early 20’s and then declines9,10. Despite the evidence that Gc and Gf are perceived as distinct intelligence components11, the question arises of whether and how neuro-biological processes and educational experiences work together to shape Gf and Gc’s interaction and development over the course of a lifespan12,13.

In a typical population, the assumption is that fluid abilities support or constrain the acquisition and expression of crystallized abilities14. The authors examined the intercorrelations between crystalized and fluid and intellectual in a sample of 291 participants ages 6-89 and found that intellectual abilities are undifferentiated in childhood; undergo differentiation and diversification during maturation, show middle correlations between 18-40, leading to a multifaceted ability structure that remains largely invariant during adulthood (45-60); and become undifferentiated again (dedifferentiation) during senescence15. The explanation is that neurobiological and cultural factors contribute to varying in life periods and ability domains14,15.

In a population of typical healthy adults, fluid intelligence (Gf) was found to be associated with the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of the brain, critical for executive function, problem-solving, and working memory, as well as the inferior and superior parietal lobes which are associated with spatial processing and working memory, the anterior cingulate gyrus, etc.16,17. Crystallized intelligence (Gc) is associated with a different region13 - the left lateral prefrontal cortex which is involved in language and knowledge retrieval, the temporal lobe, specifically the left hemisphere, which is associated with long-term memory and language comprehension, and the parietal lobes involving sensory information and the integration of information from different modalities. The multiple regions associated with both types of intelligence might explain the individual differences in goal-directed behaviors in the performance of both types of intelligence13,18,19.

The link between neuro-anatomical brain structures and cognitive outcomes was examined also in special populations such as patients with mild traumatic brain injury (MTBI) for the purpose of ramifications and enhancing cognitive abilities such as learning, memory and executive function13.

Measurement of the relationships between Gc/Gf among individuals with low cognitive levels is challenging20. The questions posed are which type of intelligence - Gc or Gf - determines the individual’s cognitive abilities throughout the lifecycle, whether they interact together and whether Gc/Gf correlations would be found in the same age periods in a population with ID as in a typical population.

It has been documented that adults with DS exhibit a β-amyloid (Aβ) burden prior to dementia diagnosis21. FMRI studies can help elucidate the abnormalities of the DS phenotype and aid in finding biomarkers of neurodegeneration at an early age in order to prepare for advanced age.

In a neuroimaging study22 the authors administered the KBIT-2 and verbal fluency tests to adults with DS and found increased activity in areas involving the frontal and temporal lobes and left cerebellum anterior lobe.

Our study aimed to dissect trajectories and correlations, specifically examining how crystallized and fluid intelligence behave and interact in individuals with NSID and DS during two pivotal life stages: adolescence and adulthood.

Trajectories of intellectual development across the lifespan

Research has identified three developmental trajectories of intelligence for individuals with neuro-cognitive disorders spanning from adolescence into adulthood23,24. These trajectories build upon established theories of intelligence that suggest a general pattern in the broader population: a rise in intelligence until around age 20, followed by a period of stability, and then a decline commencing in one’s 60’s9,25.

The accelerated aging trajectory is anchored in the Cognitive Reserve Theory26, which posnormally occurring differences in how people process tasks might provide differential reserve against age-related changes. Individuals with intellectual disability have lower intelligence by definition. They typically do not achieve high education levels or occupational status, and they tend to participate in fewer intellectually-stimulating leisure activities. The accelerated trajectory predicts restricted intelligence development among individuals with/without DS before their 20’s, stability thereafter, and accelerated decline in their mid-30’s or 40’s. Studies conducted in the 1960s, when the life expectancy of individuals with ID was shorter than today27 reported an age-associated decline in episodic memory and new learning, with these individuals developing signs of dementia in their 30s, 40s, or thereabouts27,28.

The stable development The cognitive reserve hypothesis would predict that people with intellectual disability should be substantially at higher risk of dementia29. However, lower-than-expected rates of dementia or Alzheimer’s disease were found among adults with ID compared to the general population. Thus, question the applicability of cognitive reserve theory for this population29–31. In the professional literature, there is a disagreement concerning the age of beginning of Alzheimer’s type dementia in populations with and without Down syndrome. In 1995, the cumulative incidence of Alzheimer’s disease in the general non-ID population was estimated to be as high as 47% by age 70, increasing to 49% by age 9032. Zigman et al.29 found that the incidence of significant cognitive decline rose from less than 2% at age 50 to 52% at age 88 for adults with NSID, whereas decline in adults with DS increased from less than 4% at age 50 to 67% at age 72. The aforementioned references pertain to the age of onset for cognitive deterioration and dementia. The subsequent studies demonstrate cognitive stability even in adults with Down syndrome.

Facon33 showed that adults with ID with and without DS (ages 20 to 54 years) demonstrated a similar evolution of crystallized and fluid intelligence to that of the general population as age increased. In a cross-sectional study the cognitive performance of crystallized/fluid intelligence in adults with NSID and with typical development in four age cohorts (30-39; 40-49; 50-59; 60+) was examined34. Their findings indicated stability of both groups in Vocabulary along the age cohorts, while lower scores in Matrix were found only in the 50-59 cohorts of both groups. In Block Design, participants with typical development exhibited lower scores in the 50-59 age cohort whereas participants with NSID exhibited lower scores only in the 60-69 cohort, indicating more preserved fluid scores. A longitudinal study35 on individuals with DS found stability from ages 30 to 50 on the British Picture Vocabulary Scale36 - crystalized test and only a small non-significant drop (0.99) from ages 21 to 50 on the preschool version of the Wechsler. The authors theorized that receptive and expressive abilities served as protective factors against decline in memory in this population. However, there are also individuals differences29.

Thus, the stabilized trajectory predicts that the intelligence of individuals with NSID and DS reaches its peak in their 20’s, after which there is an asymptote and the onset of decline occurs later (50-60+).

The third trajectory, the Continuous Trajectory, anticipates that intelligence in adults with ID may continue to develop into their late 40’s, reach a plateau, and then begin to diminish in their 60’s. Another study37 examined crystalized and fluid trajectories among adolescents (16-21) and adults (21-45) with NSID versus their peers with typical development (TD) in the same age groups. The performance of the participants with TD was higher than that of participants with ID in all the IQ tests, which is self-explanatory. Both groups exhibited the same age trajectories in the crystalized tests: the adults exhibited higher scores in Vocabulary and Similarities than the adolescents, which is supported in the literature5,9,38. Different fluid trajectories were demonstrated between the two populations: adolescents and adults with typical development exhibited the same scores in Block Design and Raven, indicating stability until the age of 40. However, individuals with ID exhibited different trends where the scores were higher among adults vs. adolescents. Similar patterns have been found in individuals with DS with same tests39, suggesting that chronological age may be a more significant factor in cognitive ability for adults with ID than previously understood, perhaps leading to continued cognitive development due to maturity and life experiences40.

Our study expands upon prior research by examining these trajectories in larger sample sizes and exploring potential differences in the developmental paths of crystallized and fluid intelligence between individuals with NSID and those with DS. Furthermore, it seeks to determine whether distinctive patterns of interconnected networks between crystallized and fluid intelligence emerge between adolescents and adults, with and without DS.

Cognitive profiles in non-specific intellectual disability and down syndrome

Intellectual disability (ID) encompasses a range of cognitive impairments that affect intellectual functioning, specifically an IQ <70-75 and adaptive behavior, often leading to challenges in understanding, reasoning, problem-solving, planning, abstract thinking, learning from experience, and academic learning20,41. Despite these challenges, individuals with ID can exhibit strengths in specific cognitive areas such as vocabulary, verbal abstraction, visual discrimination, and memory-skills typically associated with crystallized intelligence42. Etiology is conceptualized as a multi-factorial construct composed of four risk factors (biomedical, social, behavioral and educational) that are entangled with each other. Over 50% of individuals with ID have more than one causal risk factor43. The larger group within the population with ID are those with unknown etiology. Their Non-Specific Intellectual Disabilities (NSID) can result from a wide array of genetic variations, with as many as 1829 gene mutations44 potentially contributing to the condition44. Chromosomal disorders, genetic syndromes, congenital brain malformations, neurodegenerative diseases, congenital infections, inborn errors of metabolism, and birth injury are the most commonly identified causes of intellectual disability, which is expressed in individual differences in their cognitive phenotypes45.

Down syndrome (DS), a well-known genetic disorder occurring in approximately 1 in 800 live births, is characterized by an extra copy of chromosome 2146. This genetic anomaly leads to a distinctive neuro-psychological profile marked by challenges in language processing, morpho-syntax, verbal working memory, and explicit verbal long-term memory47–49. This cognitive pattern is reflected in distinct brain development, including reduced volumes in the frontal, temporal, and cerebellar regions50,51.

Studies indicate that individuals with DS exhibit deficit in verbal tasks but have relatively preserved visual-spatial abilities49,52. However, there have been other findings in the research53. The authors of Ref.54 categorized individuals with DS into three cognitive clusters based on their abilities: one group demonstrating stronger nonverbal than verbal skills, another with the opposite pattern, and a third group showing relatively balanced higher abilities in both domains.

This nuanced understanding of the cognitive profiles in NSID and DS underscores the complexity of ID and highlights the importance of tailored educational and therapeutic approaches to support the varied needs of these individuals.

Our contribution

This study provides new insights into the intellectual development of individuals with ID, both with and without DS. We have carefully examined and compared cognitive performance across different age groups and etiologies, employing a range of intelligence measures. The study includes both theoretical and practical contributions, as outlined below:

Theoretical contribution: We demonstrated that adults with ID, irrespective of the presence of DS, exhibit relatively higher performance in both crystallized (Vocabulary, Similarities) and fluid intelligence (Block Design, Raven Matrices) compared to adolescents with ID. Even with its limitation as a cross-sectional study, our findings suggest a continuous trajectory of cognitive development in these populations which might support the Compensation Age Theory40.

Method

The sample size was determined a priori by using the G*Power software. For two-way MANOVA with the test parameters (low effect size =0.05, α error =0.05, power =0.90 and number of variables =3), the total sample size required is 178 participants. In addition, hierarchical regression analyzes were performed in the current study. For hierarchical regression analyses (Linear multiple regression: Fixed model, R2 increase) with test parameters (small effect size =0.05, α error =0.05, power =0.90 number of tested predictors =3 and total number of predictors =4), the total sample size required is 288 participants. To increase the power and sensitivity, the sample comprised 340 participants (147 males and 193 females).

Participants (N=340) were adolescents and adults with NSID (76%; N=261) and with DS (24%;N=81). The distinction between the two groups of etiologies was based on the report of the staff and their diagnosis as it was written in their personal files (see Sect. "Study limitations and future directions"). Of the sample N=261 were adolescents (CA = 16-21; 75%; N = 121; 25%; N=33 for the NSID and down respectively) and adults (CA = 30-45; N = 186; 75%; N=140; 25%; N=46 for the NSID and down respectively). IQ ranged between mild and moderate ID (M = 59.54, SD = 7.67; range 43-75), with no statistical differences in the level of IQ between the two groups of etiologies and age cohorts. IQ was calculated according to the following cognitive tests: Vocabulary, Similarities, and Block Design (WAIS IIIHEB)6.

IQ calculations

The general IQ was obtained from participants’ personal files. However, since the tests were not evaluated at the same period across all participants, we decided to measure IQ for the purpose of this study. The IQ calculation was conducted using the Wechsler Abbreviated Intelligence Scale (WASI)55. The WASI is a procedure developed by Wechsler to derive an IQ score without administering the complete battery; it has three sub-scales: Vocabulary, Similarities, and Block Design. Nevertheless, these subscales are identical to those of the original WAIS. Consequently, we utilize the three assessments from the Hebrew version (Wechsler IIIHEB, 2001). The WASI serves as a tool for calculating IQ based on three assessments, utilized in the typical developed (TD) population, and has been validated for its sensitivity to the brain’s biological condition in neurological patients56 and in individuals with intellectual disabilities39,57.

The above three sub-scales of the Hebrew intelligence test for adults (WAIS-IIIHEB; Wechsler 2001)6 were administered to the adolescents (16-21) and adults (22-40) with NSID and DS. Based on the results, IQ was calculated by the chief Psychologist of the Disability Division, the Israel Ministry of Welfare and Social affairs (see procedure section there).

Graph analysis

Graph theory, traditionally utilized in fields like computer science and network analysis, has recently gained traction in cognitive neuroscience and psychology, particularly in the area of network neuroscience. Its application to the study of intelligence in individuals with intellectual disabilities (ID) offers a fresh perspective on analyzing complex cognitive networks. This approach is demonstrated in Ref.61, where graph theory was employed to investigate cognitive processes by analyzing the relationships between different cognitive tasks, shedding light on network structures underlying intellectual functioning.

In graph theory, relationships and interactions are depicted through nodes (representing variables) and edges (the connections between these variables). This framework provides a clear and structured way to conceptualize and analyze relationships between variables, using graphical representations that make complex interactions easier to interpret and understand. In our analysis, the nodes represent the four cognitive tests: Raven, Similarities, Vocabulary, and Block Design. The edges represent the partial correlations between these tests, which measure the relationship between two variables while controlling for the influence of other variables. An undirected network is used since the partial correlations reflect symmetric relationships, meaning the connection between two tests is bidirectional.

Graph analysis offers the ability to quantify the importance of each node and connection within the network through centrality indices such as strength and closeness. The strength of a node is the sum of the weights of the edges connected to it, indicating how strongly the node is connected to its neighbors. Ethical considerations and procedure

Authorizations, including the experimental protocol, were approved by the Bar Ilan University Ethics Committee and the Israel Ministries of Education and Welfare. Informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians/parents of participants with intellectual disabilities (ID). The study aims and procedures were explained to participants with ID, who signed an adapted informed consent form for participation in the research.

Like the typical population participating in research, our participants with ID were rewarded with either payment or a gift based on their choice. They were informed that they could leave the study at any time without losing their reward.

The battery of tests was administered individually, at schools for adolescents, or in residential and employment settings for adults. For half of the participants (from both etiologies), the three WAIS-IIIHEB subtests were administered first in one session (approximately one hour), followed by the Raven Matrices after a 20-minute break (lasting approximately 45 minutes). For the other half of the participants, the order was reversed: the Raven was administered first, followed by the three WAIS-IIIHEB subtests after a 20-minute break.

To further illustrate the partial correlations between the cognitive tests, we also present heatmaps (Fig. 1 that visualize these relationships across four different groups: adolescents with DS, adolescents with NSID, adults with DS, and adults with NSID. The heatmaps provide a detailed view of the correlation structure, highlighting the relative strength of the connections between the tests. Darker colors represent stronger correlations, while lighter colors indicate weaker correlations. The visual representation of these correlations complements the network graphs by offering a clearer comparison across the different groups.

For adolescents with NSID, the Block Design test, which measures fluid intelligence, emerged as a central node with moderate connections to crystallized intelligence tests (Vocabulary and Similarities) and weaker connections to the Raven test. However, in adults with NSID, the Raven test became the central node, connected to Block Design and Vocabulary, but the overall coherence of the network diminished in adulthood, indicating a less integrated cognitive structure.

In contrast, individuals with DS exhibited more integrated and cohesive cognitive networks. Both adolescents and adults with DS showed Block Design and Vocabulary as central nodes with strong connections to Similarities, suggesting a strong interaction between crystallized and fluid intelligence. This integration remained consistent and even strengthened into adulthood, implying a more stable cognitive profile.

Discussion

Our discussion centers on three critical aspects: the developmental trajectories of crystallized and fluid intelligence from adolescence through adulthood in individuals with Non-Specific Intellectual Disability (NSID) and Down Syndrome (DS), the distinctive characteristics inherent to each group of etiology, and the varying connectivity patterns between crystallized and fluid intelligence across the two distinct age brackets.

Trajectories of intelligence among adolescents and adults with ID

Cross-sectional research may suffer from cohort effects, whereas longitudinal research may suffer from “retest effects.” However, Kaufman9,63 found the same trajectories of crystallized and fluid intelligence using cross-sectional and longitudinal methods in a typically developing population. Our research confirms that people between the ages of 30 and 45 who have intellectual disability, such as NSID and DS, exhibit higher levels of crystallized and fluid intelligence than teenagers aged 16 to 21. The pattern remained similar across three intelligence measures: Vocabulary, Similarities, and Block Design. Although the Raven Matrix did not achieve statistical significance, its results were consistent with the above tests (see the explanation below). Based on the Kauffman-Salthouse assertion9,63, interpreting the results of this cross-sectional study comprised an indication or a continuous developmental trajectory of cognitive abilities in both people with and without DS. Regression analysis further supports this finding, which indicates a significant contribution (β =0.15–0.24***) of the developmental progression (chronological age) to the cognitive capacities. Research on the general population63,64 suggests that peaks in various cognitive skills, such as Vocabulary and Information, occur later in life, around 60–70 years, while abilities like Similarities peak around 30–40 years. Our findings supported these patterns, indicating that crystallized intelligence may continue to develop into mid-adulthood. Fluid intelligence, however, is traditionally more susceptible to decline with age in a typical population, with decline in reasoning and Block Design starting around 20–30 years of age63,64. Our study’s adult participants with NSID and even those with DS demonstrated higher Block Design scores compared to adolescents challenging the decline or stability seen in fluid intelligence measures in the general population37. Our results align with previous research showing that adults with ID tend to perform better cognitively than adolescents with ID. For example:

Numminen et al.65 found higher performance of adults with ID (MCA = 49.88 years) to children with TD (MCA= 5.50 years) who were matched according to the CPM Raven’s (1986) in crystalized tasks asserting that age i.e. maturity and life experience contribute to the performance of crystalized abilities of adults with ID. Lifshitz et al.66 found that adults with ID (CA= 30–45) with/without DS) gained more from dynamic assessment training and improved their analogical reasoning tasks (a fluid task) than did adolescents (CA =16–21) with ID. Chen et al.37 found higher scores of the Raven and Block design among adults with ID (CA = 20–45) compared to adolescents with ID (16–21). The same trends were found for adolescents and with DS. Adults with ID with and without DS exhibit higher scores in the phonological loop and in vernal executive function tests67 than the adolescents with ID68. The observed cognitive trends of higher scores among adults in fluid intelligence may be explained by a combination of behavioral and neurological factors. The Compensation Age Theory40 suggests that life experience and maturity could enable adults with ID with/without DS to acquire new cognitive skills that were previously absent from their behavioral repertoire. Our findings are cross-sectional but may be an indication for cognitive growth, at least in the above crystallized and fluid skills at the ages of 20–45.

In typical population brain imaging demonstrated that brain maturation, including the frontal lobe and prefrontal cortex, appears to reach its peak in late adolescence69. Apparently, in individuals with ID, brain maturation processes more slowly initially and reaching fuller maturation later, in adulthood. This may possibly extend their period of cognitive development beyond middle adulthood. Caution should be exercised and further brain research is sorely needed to validate this assertion.

As for the Raven Matrix, this test is identified as a complex visual-spatial analogical reasoning task12 which may place a greater cognitive demand on individuals with ID, as indicated by the higher standard deviation in performance across study groups. Hartshorne and Germain64 state that, in the general population, reasoning peaks at around 25 years of age.

Unlike in the Block design, in the Raven test our adults with ID exhibited the same scores as did the adolescents. Our findings correlated with37, whose study found similar scores among Israeli TD adolescents and adults, indicating a stabilized trajectory but not decline. It should be noted that the sample in this test was smaller since it was administered to all the participants.

Our findings are particularly noteworthy, especially in the context of adults with DS. It has been documented that adults with DS are prone to early cognitive decline associated with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) neuropathology and β-amyloid (Aβ) deposition, which can begin as early as young adulthood or middle age70.

However, there is disagreement as to gauging the age at which decline begins. Cases of individuals with ID, even those with DS, who reached their 70s and 80s without cognitive decline31,71. Other studies have pointed to age 58 as the onset of decline, especially among adults with Down syndrome1 while others have pointed to younger ages21. Yet, genetic and brain studies do suggest that the notion of a compensation mechanism with increasing age may be of particular relevance for adults with DS72 syndrome73,74.

Genes that are overexpressed in Down syndrome (APP, DSCAM, MNB/DYRK1A, RCAN1) may lead to greater cognitive delays in childhood, but paradoxically, in adulthood, they produce proteins critical for neuron and synapse growth, development, and maintenance and may participate in molecular cascades supporting neuronal compensation72.

Our study, however, focused on adolescents and adults with DS until the age of 45, which is beyond the controversial debate about the age of onset of decline, at least from a behavioral point of view. Thus, despite the limitations of this being a cross-sectional study, our findings joined the above-mentioned studies in maintaining that the adult period holds potential of growth and development of adults with NSID and with DS. As a consequence of maturity and life experience, these individuals can reach a level of cognitive functioning previously absent from their behavioral repertoire (see practical recommendations).

Etiological differences in cognitive development

Our study specifically focused on comparative cognitive performances across two distinct groups: those with mixed etiologies and DS. The investigation revealed a nuanced portrait of cognitive strengths and vulnerabilities.

In crystallized intelligence assessments, such as the Vocabulary test, which measures accumulated knowledge and language skills, participants with NSID demonstrated relatively higher scores than their peers with DS (the scores of both groups are two S.D below the mean). In contrast, DS individuals showed relatively higher fluid intelligence scores, in Block Design and particularly in the Raven test. This suggests that participants with DS have a relatively stronger ability to cope with spatial reasoning challenges compared to their peers with NSID.

Both Block Design and Raven tests are considered fluid intelligence. Block Design aims to examine the mental image, rotate, combine, and reason about visual information. The test-taker uses hand movements to rearrange blocks that have various color patterns on different sides to match a pattern9,25.

The Raven Progressive Matrices test, designed to assess complex visual-spatial analogical reasoning, offered additional insights12. Although this test, intended for adults, posed a substantial challenge to our participants, those with DS solved a significant percentage/number of the items, surpassing expectations and highlighting a relatively strength in fluid intelligence within this group compared to their peers with NSID.

Our results support other studies’ findings indicating that individuals with DS perform better in fluid tasks such as Corsi Block, Raven test, and visuo-spatial working memory compared to those with other types of disability such as William syndrome, ID with mixed etiologies and high-functioning ASD48,75.

Surprisingly there was no significant performance discrepancy in Similarities between the two groups. Our findings correlated with those of Ref.47 who found the same trends among children and early adolescents with DS (CA = 7–17). These outcomes suggest that verbal abilities in individuals with DS depend on the type of task. The Vocabulary test measures semantic information so it is not different from the Similarities test in that regard. However, Vocabulary is not generally considered a measure of verbal concept formation, the Similarities test assess verbal abstract reasoning and inductive reasoning. Similarities is considered to be a process of thematic taxonomic categorization7 involving verbal reasoning skills; Thus, verbal deficit of individuals with DS may not be one piece as compromised as traditionally thought. This revelation invites a reevaluation of cognitive competencies in DS, particularly in the realm of verbal and abstract reasoning

As stated, in typical populations, crystallized intelligence (Gc)-which includes vocabulary and similarities-is processed in the left lateral prefrontal cortex and the temporal lobes. The left lateral prefrontal cortex is associated with language, knowledge retrieval, and problem-solving, while the temporal lobes are linked to long-term memory and understanding language. Fluid intelligence (Block Design and Raven) is predicted as processed in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, which is associated with executive functions and problem-solving, as well as in the parietal lobes involved in spatial processing and working memory. The disparate strengths in crystallized and fluid intelligence between the two groups of etiologies might hint at the possibility of unique brain region and distinct neural mechanisms underlying cognitive skills in NSID and DS alongside a common region (Similarities) that should be examined in comprehensive neuroimaging research.

Connections of crystallized/fluid intelligence according to graph analysis

Using graph theory for statistical analysis, we built two separate networks of the crystallized and fluid tests, one for participants with NSID and one for participants with DS, to map the interacting constructs between them. The findings indicated different Gc-Gf network correlations between the two groups of etiologies in the two age cohorts. For participants with DS, Block Design-a Gf test-and Vocabulary-a Gc test-emerged as the primary nodes, interconnected during both adolescence and adulthood. In adulthood (20-45), the two tests exhibited comparable strength correlations to Similarities, which bridges the two types of intelligence9,12,76. The interconnections between Gc-Gf (represented in our study by four tests) are consistent with the correlations (r=0.04–0.05) between the two types of intelligence found in the typical population12,14, mainly at ages 22-45. Higher correlations between the two types of intelligence suggest that participants who display higher scores in one type of intelligence also show higher scores in the other type12,14. These findings may serve as an indication of a more integrative profile in which Gf-Gc operates as a unified network in both age cohorts in the DS etiology (Fig. 1).

This was not the case for the NSID group which exhibited distinct Gf-Gc connections. In adolescence, Block Design remained the central node, displaying medium connections to the two crystallized tests in the structure of a triangle. Block Design exhibited connections to the Raven test, which is placed at the edge of the triangle. As stated both Raven and Blcok Design are fluid oriented. However, Block Design is considered as a performance test as it measures construction of Block according to visual spatial models. The Raven is considered as a more abstract test as it examines analogical reasoning. As demonstrating by the graph analysis this test took a side, and in both etiologies it is connected only to one or two nodes.

In adulthood, the Raven test took precedence as the main node and was connected to Block Design on one side and to Vocabulary on the other side, but the coherent structure of the interconnections between the four tests disappeared. All correlation figures below 0.2 were omitted.

An isolated network does not necessarily imply impaired node processes61 but rather indicates weak connections between them or even lack thereof, especially in adulthood.

Given the correlations between brain regions and cognitive outcomes in the DS etiology found by Ref.22, our findings may suggest more coherent interconnected networks between different brain regions in the DS etiology: the left-frontal superior orbital and the left-frontal middle orbital lobes, activated during Matrices performance, are interconnected to the left middle occipital and the left frontal orbital lobes, which are activated during Vocabulary or Similarities performance in this genetic syndrome. In contrast, according to the graph analysis, our group with NSID exhibited a diffuse network between the above crystallized and fluid tests. This trend can be attributed to the heterogeneity of genetic and environmental etiologies (1828 gene variations) in this group as a whole and even between the age cohorts, since the design of our study was cross-sectional. Does the lack of connections between the cognitive tests in the NSID group reflect weak interconnections between their brain region networks? fMRI and genetic studies are needed to validate the interpretation.

Behavioral and genetic studies can serve as explanations for the more coherent Gc-Gf connections in the DS etiology. In support of the Environmental Enrichment Theory77, an increase in life expectancy, improvement in medical and health care conditions, and an increase in educational, vocational, and cognitively leisure activities for adults with DS all serve to strengthen their brain plasticity as expressed in their Gc-Gf connections.

In the DS etiology, a third chromosome 21 adds an extra copy of over 200 protein-coding genes, which may cause the neuro-cognitive deficit in this etiology. However, in this etiology, the abnormal genetic mutation is expressed on only one chromosome. Scientists78,79 have recently introduced a new mouse model, Ts66Yah, which exhibits memory difficulties and deficient behavioral traits but to a lesser degree of severity compared to the characteristics observed in the previous mouse model. The authors assert that the potential for treating ID in the context of DS delves into reshaping our understanding of disability. Bathelt et al.23 used fMRI to examine age-related changes in functional brain connectivity across young, middle-aged and older adults with ASD (CA=30-73 years) compared to age-matched controls with typical development. Surprisingly, they found less age decline in brain function in the visual network connectivity of adults with ASD compared to their controls, beginning from their 30s through their 50s and into their 70s.

One might argue that autism and ID represent two distinct etiologies (social communication and behavior deficits in ASD and intellectual functioning/adaptive behavior deficits in ID). However, scientists found that genes associated with ASD are often associated with ID80–82. Similar to them23 our findings may indicate relative strength in brain connectivity among adults with a clear diagnostic etiology such as DS. fMRI is needed to confirm this claim.

Conclusions

Our research findings emphasized a significant and ongoing trend of higher scores in crystallized and fluid intelligence in adulthood compared to adolescence for individuals with Down Syndrome (DS) and Non-Specific Intellectual Disability (NSID), even with study’s limitation as cross-sectional. It appears that fluid intelligence reaches its peak later in individuals with intellectual disability (ID), both with and without DS, compared to those with typical development (TD).

The relatively higher performance in specific cognitive tasks and more organized connection patterns observed in participants with DS, as well as the lack of differences in Similarities between them and their peers with NSID - a crystallized intelligence test - might reflect a less impaired phenotype than previously thought and more cognitive and neurological assets, especially in adulthood, compared to their peers with NSID. This suggests a need for longitudinal study to confirm our claims.

Study limitations and future directions

Our sample included 261 participants with NSID and only 79 with DS. A small sample size is common in clinical populations due to difficulties in recruiting adults with DS who comprise only 10% of the population with ID83.

It is possible that these participants performed differently than other persons in the same age cohort, which might hinder generalization. Furthermore, generalization should be approached with caution since there is variability within each of the two groups of etiologies: not all individuals with ID function at the same cognitive level or exhibit the same pattern of strengths and weaknesses. This variability may be due to individual differences in brain structure, function, or connectivity. Differences between our adolescent and adult participants might also stem from differences in education/curriculum, social experiences, or occupational demands of the two age cohorts. Future research using a larger sample of participants with NSID, especially those with DS, would help validate our findings and rule out significant results due to chance. Furthermore, the cause of the cognitive impairment or the etiology of our participants with NSID was unknown, as was the level of chromosome 21 triplication (full/partial/mosaicism). Therefore, differences between DS and NSID in terms of verbal vs. visuospatial cognitive abilities could be attributed to various etiological factors associated with an unknown cause of ID or DS. Future research should provide more data about the genetic causes of NSID and DS etiology. Longitudinal studies on the cognitive trajectories of individuals with ID from adolescence to adulthood would help validate our results.

Future research should include a broad range of crystallized and fluid intelligence tests, as well as a battery of working and explicit memory tests. This study focused on intellectual development patterns of individuals with and without DS from adolescence to adulthood. Extending the age range to other life periods (childhood, middle age, late adulthood) could elucidate the transformation of their crystallized and fluid intelligence over the life cycle and the unique profile of each etiology. The basic cognitive level of our participants ranged from mild to moderate ID (IQ = 50-75). It is recommended to examine the cognitive development patterns of individuals with more severe ID and other etiologies to find suitable measures for this population. Our study indicated a more coherent cognitive profile with a relatively strong connection network between Gc and Gf. fMRI studies are needed to examine whether this coherent network is also expressed between crystallized and fluid regions in the brain.

Practical recommendations

Our study employed a cross-sectional design and suggested that cognitive growth in individuals with ID, with or without DS, may not end in their 20s as it does in the typical population (at least for fluid intelligence), thus supporting the Compensation Age Theory40. These conclusions offer a more hopeful outlook for families and staff working with the ID population at both younger ages and in adulthood. Policymakers and administrators of community residences and vocational centers for adults with and without Down syndrome should be introduced to the Compensation Age Theory and the potential for cognitive development in young and middle adulthood.

Kim &Kim84 showed that adults with ID (ages 20–70; IQ 40–70) could benefit from “focused cognitive interventions”84 aimed at improving specific cognitive skills that tend to decline with age, such as verbal abstraction skills and analogical reasoning. Implementing cognitive intervention programs in residential or employment settings for adults and older individuals with ID is recommended. Post-Secondary Education (PSE) university programs have improved the crystallized and fluid intelligence of adults (ages 28–45) with ID (IQ 50–75; Ref.85). Therefore, PSE for adults with ID should be an integral part of the service offerings for adults with ID, anchored in government legislation and support, as seen in the US (Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) 2004) and the UK (Equality Act 2010). Enhancing the cognitive functioning of adults with ID may also contribute to their adaptive behavior skills, as correlations have been found between the g factor and adaptive behavior.

The Cognitive Activity Theory86,87 suggests that lifestyle factors, such as participation in cognitive leisure activities, are associated with current cognitive functioning and reducing the risk of cognitive decline leading to Alzheimer’s disease87 in the typical population. Lifshitz et al.68 supported these trends in a population with and without DS. Participation in cognitive activities during midlife (age 20+) can mitigate health and ADL deterioration39. The Cognitive Reserve Theory88 defines cognitive reserve as “a property of the brain that allows for cognitive performance that is better than expected given the degree of life-course-related brain changes.” Therefore, young and middle-aged adults with ID, especially those with DS who are more prone to developing Alzheimer’s-type dementia, should be encouraged to engage in leisure activities and challenging employment that involve greater cognitive stimulation. This can help strengthen their cognitive reserve and potentially protect against future cognitive decline.

Finally, on the basis of the connection between fluid abilities and Similarities in the DS etiology, we propose that fluid tasks such as analogies could serve as a lever for the development of crystalline abilities in this etiology.
