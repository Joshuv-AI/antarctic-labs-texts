The impact of climate change on mental health in vulnerable groups: a systematic review
Authors: Ridha Mahmood, Philippa Clery, Justin C. Yang, Liying Cao, Jennifer Dykxhoorn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate change events may disproportionately affect vulnerable groups, exacerbating existing socioeconomic, racial, and cultural inequalities. This systematic review summarises the evidence on the mental health impacts of climate change events on vulnerable populations: (1) low socioeconomic status groups; (2) minoritised ethnic or racial groups; (3) Indigenous groups; and (4) housing-insecure groups.MethodsWe searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science for studies published before 17 July 2023 (PROSPERO: CRD42023442489). Key search terms included (1) climate change events; (2) mental health; and (3) vulnerable groups. Titles/abstracts and full texts were screened for inclusion, followed by quality assessment and narrative synthesis.ResultsOf the 1,197 articles retrieved, 32 met inclusion criteria. Most studies (n = 27) examined acute weather events, with few exploring sub-acute weather events (n = 2), gradual environmental changes (n = 2), or combined events (n = 2). Studies predominantly examined low socioeconomic groups (n = 26) and minoritised ethnic or racial groups (n = 12), with limited research on Indigenous groups (n = 2) or housing-insecure groups (n = 1). The most common mental health outcomes were post-traumatic symptoms/disorder (n = 15), depressive disorder (n = 12), general mental health (n = 8), anxiety disorder (n = 6), and psychological distress (n = 6). 25 of the studies originated from high-income countries, 7 were from middle-income countries, and none from low-income countries. Study quality varied: 7 rated low, 16 medium, and 9 high.Eighteen studies found worse mental health outcomes in vulnerable groups following climate change exposure compared to other populations, five studies found mixed results, and nine found no evidence of worse outcomes in vulnerable groups.ConclusionsVulnerable groups generally experienced worse mental health outcomes following climate change events compared to other populations, though some studies found mixed or null effects. There were critical evidence gaps, including limited high-quality evidence on sub-acute and gradual climate change events, Indigenous or housing-insecure groups, and low-and-middle-income countries. The wide range of mental health outcomes assessed makes direct comparisons challenging. Addressing these gaps is critical to understanding the interplay between existing social inequalities and the mental health impact of climate change, as well as informing effective public health policies in the context of increasing frequency and severity of climate change events globally.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03497-z.

Introduction

Climate change, once regarded as a distant threat, is now recognised as the largest threat to global health in the twenty-first century, fundamentally reshaping our world [1–3]. Climate change has intensified the frequency and severity of weather events, including acute weather events like hurricanes, floods, wildfires, or heat/cold waves; sub-acute events like droughts; and gradual environmental changes like rising temperatures, increasing sea levels, or permanently altered ecosystems [4].

The adverse effects of environmental changes on physical health are well documented, such as increased exposure to infectious and respiratory diseases and nearly 12.6 million preventable deaths annually [5]. A growing body of literature has also found links between exposure to climate change events and increased mental health problems [5], including increases in psychiatric hospitalisation during heat waves [1], decreased emotional well-being during droughts [6], and elevated rates of mental disorders including anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress [4]. These climate change events are predicted to increase in frequency and intensity, potentially adding to the burden of mental health problems [7].

Two pathways linking climate change events to mental health problems have been hypothesised, including direct impact due to experiences of trauma and threats to safety during weather events [8, 9] or indirect impacts due to damaged infrastructure, reducing economic activity, and loss of belonging to the community impacted by climate events [10, 11]. Eco-anxiety has also been identified as a potential indirect pathway between climate change events and mental health as it includes negative cognitive, emotional, and behavioural responses to worsening environmental conditions [12, 13].

The burden of climate change events is not equally distributed, and disproportionately impacts historically marginalised and vulnerable populations, who also have the fewest resources to respond and recover from the impacts [14]. Indigenous groups, for example, are more likely to live in ecologically sensitive areas like coastlines, mountains, or arctic regions which are highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change [15, 16]. Housing-insecure and homeless groups also face greater exposure to extreme temperatures due to inadequate shelter [17]. Socioeconomically disadvantaged groups struggle to respond swiftly to catastrophic weather events and have less access to resources to rebuild and recover after a natural disaster [18, 19]. Although these populations generally contribute the least to the climate crisis, they may suffer its greatest consequences [20]. This further highlights that climate change is an issue of both health equity and justice [21]. With both climate change and mental health conditions estimated to rise in the coming years [9], it is important to explore whether existing inequalities compound the challenges that disadvantaged populations may experience.

This disproportionate burden of climate change events among vulnerable groups amplifies the risk for populations already facing a high burden of mental health problems related to income, housing, discrimination, displacement, and inequality [22, 23].

Reviews have only recently begun exploring the climate change-related mental health experiences of vulnerable groups, mainly focusing on gender [24], age [25], pre-existing mental disorders [26], chronic disabilities [27], and specific occupations (e.g., farmers) [28]. However, the experiences of many other potentially vulnerable populations remain overlooked, such as those who are economically disadvantaged, homeless or housing-insecure, or exposed to systemic racism and/or other discrimination [29, 30]. For this review, we conceptualized vulnerable populations as groups occupying a low socioeconomic position, minoritised ethnic or racial groups, Indigenous groups, or those facing housing insecurity or homelessness.

Most current reviews on the health impact of climate change focus solely on acute weather events [29, 31]. While it is plausible that the direct and indirect pathways used to explain mental health outcomes of acute weather events may also apply to sub-acute and gradual climate change events, these have not been explored as in a systematic review [8, 32]. Additionally, studies commonly combine climate change-related mental health and physical health as part of broader “health outcomes” [31] which can make it difficult to discern their unique impacts. Studies which measure mental health as a separate construct mainly examine clinical disorders such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [8] or severe mental illness [33] but neglect to also consider measures of general mental wellbeing. By focusing solely on the most severe mental health conditions, current literature may be overlooking the diverse range of mental health outcomes experienced by vulnerable populations [34]. Furthermore, it is difficult to infer population-level impacts as most studies involving vulnerable groups have limited participants and employ qualitative methods [33]. A large-scale quantification of mental health in vulnerable populations exposed to climate change is lacking [35].

This systematic review summarises evidence on the impacts of acute, sub-acute, and gradual climate change on the mental health outcomes of vulnerable populations, drawing on both outcomes reported within these populations and comparisons with other populations.

Methods

This systematic review used the PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome) framework to develop the research question and followed the Preferred Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses’ (PRISMA) guidelines [36]. We pre-registered the study protocol on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) [37].

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed with a research librarian at University College London, consisting of keywords, text terms, and subject headings capturing the core three concepts of this review: (1) climate change events; (2) mental health; and (3) vulnerable populations. The search strategy was informed by preliminary searches, including titles and abstracts that had been identified by the study authors as relevant to the review (Appendix 1).

Climate change events

We adapted Palinkas and Wong’s ​ [4]​ framework to identify and organise climate change exposures: (a) acute weather events like hurricanes, floods, wildfires, and heat/cold waves; (b) sub-acute events like droughts; and (c) gradual changes like global temperature increases or decreases, sea level rises, or permanently altered environments. We included an additional category for studies which included multiple climate change events.

Mental health outcomes

We used a broad definition of mental health, including general mental health, psychological distress, and symptoms or diagnosis of mental disorders like depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress, or severe mental illness. We included studies which measured mental health using self-report symptoms and diagnoses, standardised diagnostic tools, or psychiatric hospitalisations.

Vulnerable populations

We focused on four vulnerable populations: (1) low socioeconomic status groups; (2) minoritised ethnic or racial groups; (3) Indigenous groups; and (4) housing-insecure or homeless groups.

Other populations (comparators)

We defined other populations as (1) the general population, (2) groups with differing levels of vulnerability (e.g., higher- versus lower-income groups, racial or ethnic minority versus majority populations), and (3) groups unexposed to climate change–related events. Studies without explicit comparator groups were also included if they reported mental health outcomes within vulnerable populations exposed to climate change-related events.

We searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, Scopus and Web of Science on 17th July 2023 (Appendix 1). We excluded studies which were not published in English and any research done on non-human populations but did not place restrictions on the date of publication or geographic location (Table 1). Screening and selection criteria

After searches were conducted, results were exported to EndNote, de-duplicated, then imported into Rayyan [38], a systematic review screening platform. The titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers (RM and LC). Full-text articles were then retrieved and reviewed (RM and LC). We calculated Cohen’s kappa (κ) to assess the level of agreement between the two reviewers, and any disagreements were discussed with a third independent reviewer to reach consensus (PC).

Data extraction and synthesis

We extracted detailed information from each study in our standardised data extraction table, including authorship, publication year, study title, study design, duration, setting, sample characteristics, vulnerable groups, comparators/controls, climate exposure, mental health outcomes and measures, effect measures, confounders, and key findings.

We grouped articles by climate change exposure and vulnerable group for narrative synthesis. We assessed each study to identify if the evidence indicated that the mental health impact in vulnerable groups exposed to climate change events was greater, equivalent, or less than the impact observed in other populations. Due to heterogeneity in study methodology, exposures, outcomes, and vulnerable groups, a meta-analysis was deemed inappropriate for this review.

Quality appraisal

We used the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools [39] to assess the quality of cross-sectional and cohort studies. For the included case-crossover study, we used the checklist for case–control studies, given the methodological similarities [39]. RM and LC independently assessed each study against the appraisal checklists, each item in the checklist was marked yes (met criteria), no (did not meet criteria), unclear (insufficient information), or not applicable. Based on these assessments, each study was given an overall quality score of low, medium, or high, with any disagreements resolved by discussion with PC.

Results

Our searches retrieved 2,179 articles and 1,197 articles were included in title and abstract screening following de-duplication. 52 articles were included in full-text screening, and 32 were included in the final synthesis (Fig. 1). Study characteristics

Twenty-one studies were cross-sectional [40–60], 10 were cohort studies [61–70], and one was a case-crossover study [71], with data collection periods spanning from 1 month to 16 years (Fig. 2). Studies were published between 2009 to 2023, with the highest number of articles published in 2013. The median sample size was 558 (interquartile range: 387–1,610).Fig. 2Number of studies by the three core concepts and study design

Most of the studies (n = 26) explored acute weather events [41–45, 47–53, 55, 56, 58–64, 66–70], two studies explored sub-acute weather events [57, 65], and two studies explored gradual environmental changes [40, 71]. There were two studies which used a combined climate event exposure [46, 54] (Fig. 2).

A range of mental health outcomes were studied, and several articles included multiple mental health outcomes. Eight studies looking at mental health problems [40, 45, 48, 51, 52, 54, 65, 71], six examined psychological distress [42, 60, 61, 66–68], two measured psychiatric symptoms [40, 64], one study measured perceived stress [67], and one study explored probable mental illness [58]. Several studies focused on specific psychiatric conditions: major depressive disorder (n = 12) [41–44, 47, 53, 54, 56, 57, 59, 62, 63], post-traumatic stress symptoms/disorder (n = 14) [41–44, 46, 47, 49, 55, 59, 61, 63, 67, 68, 70], generalised anxiety disorder (n = 6) [42–44, 47, 54, 59], severe mental illness (n = 2) [42, 58], mood disorders (n = 1) [50], or complicated grief (n = 1) [69]. Mental health outcomes were measured using self-report symptoms and diagnoses (n = 4) [40, 51, 52, 69], standardised diagnostic tools (n = 27) [41–50, 53–68, 70], or psychiatric hospitalisations (n = 1) [71].

Twenty-six studies focused on the low SES group [40–44, 46–48, 50–52, 54, 56–62, 64, 65, 67–71], 12 studies focused on minoritised ethnic or racial groups [41, 43, 45, 49, 51, 53, 55, 58, 61, 66, 69, 70], two studies focused on Indigenous groups [47, 63], and one study focused on the housing-insecure group [62] (Table 2). Quality appraisal

Two reviewers (RM, LC) appraised the quality of all included studies, and discrepancies were discussed with a third reviewer (PC) to achieve consensus (n = 3). Although discrepancies were limited to three studies, they occurred across multiple checklist items within these appraisals, which may have contributed to the slight overall agreement (κ = 0.136, p = 0.001).

Sixteen studies were rated medium, nine were rated high, and six were rated low (Table 3). Overall association between climate change events and mental health in vulnerable groups

Of the 32 included studies, 18 studies reported significant adverse mental health outcomes for vulnerable populations exposed to climate change events [40–44, 47–51, 53, 55, 59, 60, 62, 63, 67, 68]. Five studies produced mixed results [58, 61, 64, 70, 71], while nine studies found no significant disparities in mental health outcomes of climate change for vulnerable groups [45, 46, 52, 54, 56, 57, 65, 66, 69].

Acute climate change events

We identified 26 studies which focused on acute climate change events: tropical cyclones (including hurricanes, cyclones, typhoons, or major storms; n = 20), floods (n = 4), and wildfires (n = 2).

Tropical cyclones

Twenty studies explored the mental health impact of tropical cyclones in three vulnerable populations: low SES groups, minoritised ethnic or racial groups, and Indigenous groups.

Tropical cyclones & SES

Eight studies found worse mental health outcomes among low SES groups following tropical cyclone exposure [41, 42, 44, 48, 50, 59, 67, 68], two studies found mixed results [64, 66], and five studies did not find evidence of an effect [45, 56, 58, 61, 69]​.

Studies exploring mental health following tropical cyclones found evidence of worse mental health outcomes in low SES groups, including higher rates of mental health problems [42], perceived mental health problems [48], psychological distress [42, 67], post-traumatic stress disorder/symptoms [68], depression [41], anxiety [44, 59], and SMI [42] than rates in higher SES groups. Different variables were used to estimate low SES, including income [41, 42, 48, 68], financial strain [44], or wealth indices [50]. One study found that people who experienced more hurricane-related stressors had worse mental health outcomes than those who faced fewer stressors, regardless of SES, but that high SES before the hurricane reduced the number of stressors experienced [67]. The studies which found associations between SES and post-tropical cyclone mental health outcomes, were mostly of medium quality, including six cross-sectional studies [41, 42, 44, 48, 50, 59] and two cohort studies [68].

Two studies found mixed evidence for the differential impact of a cyclone in low SES groups [64, 66]. Brown et al. (2010) found that some measures of SES predicted more psychiatric symptoms following a cyclone, including owning fewer consumer durables like TVs, washing machines, or bicycles before the cyclone; worse housing services; and lower educational attainment, while other variables like housing quality or insurance status were not associated with these symptoms [64]. Lowe and Rhodes [66]’s study of female survivors of Hurricane Katrina classified most low-income mothers as “resilient” with low psychological distress levels pre-tropical cyclone. While this group experienced an increase in distress levels immediately following the cyclone, distress returned to pre-cyclone levels, and this group had lower rates of PTSD at 1 year and 3 years post-tropical cyclone compared to other groups [66]. The two cohort studies [64, 66] that reported mixed evidence, were mostly of medium quality.

Five studies did not find evidence for a difference in mental health outcomes following a cyclone among low SES groups compared to other populations: mental health problems [45], or post-traumatic stress disorder or symptoms [61], major depressive disorder [56], or complicated grief [69]. For example, Joseph et al. [56] did not find differences in the odds of major depressive disorder by education or neighbourhood poverty [56], and Shear et al. [69] found no association between rates of complicated grief across income, education, or health insurance status [69]. In their unadjusted analysis, Sastry & VanLandingham [58] showed that economically disadvantaged individuals had higher rates of mental illness than less disadvantaged individuals, however, these associations were attenuated following adjustment for confounders [58]. The three cross-sectional [45, 56, 58] and two cohort studies [61, 69] that found no evidence for an effect of SES on post-tropical cyclone mental health outcomes, were of medium to high quality.

Tropical cyclones & ethnic or racial groups

Ten studies explored impacts of tropical cyclones on mental health by ethnic or racial group [41, 45, 49, 53, 55, 61, 66, 68–70]. Out of these, six studies reported worse post-tropical cyclone mental health outcomes in minoritised ethnic or racial groups compared to their counterparts ​ [41, 45, 49, 53, 55, 68], two studies found mixed findings [61, 70], and two studies did not find evidence of an effect [66, 69].

Ali et al. [53] found that rates of depression were higher in African American groups following Hurricane Katrina than other racial groups and Pham et al. [45] found higher levels of depressive symptoms in Vietnamese Americans compared to other ethnic groups [53]. Three studies found that African American, non-Hispanic Black, and other non-White individuals were likely to develop post-traumatic stress symptoms or disorders following a tropical cyclone when compared to White individuals [41]. Of the studies which found ethnic or racial differences in mental health outcomes following tropical cyclones, three were high quality [41, 45, 53] and three were medium quality [49, 55, 68].

Raker et al. [61] found higher rates of psychological distress and post-traumatic stress symptoms in Black individuals compared to non-Black individuals in the first year after a tropical cyclone, but this difference did not persist over the 12 years of follow-up, and no long-term racial disparities in mental health outcomes were detected [61]. In a cohort study of low-income mothers exposed to cyclones, Lowe et al. [70] found that non-Hispanic Black individuals had higher post-traumatic stress symptom scores but also higher post-traumatic growth scores when compared to other ethnic and racial groups [70]. These studies were assessed as medium and low quality, respectively [61, 70].

Two post-tropical cyclone studies did not detect ethnic or racial differences in levels of psychological distress [66] or complicated grief [69] following a tropical cyclone. These studies were assessed to be medium and low quality, respectively.

Tropical cyclones & indigenous groups

We found one study which explored the impact of tropical cyclones on the mental health of Indigenous groups. Chen et al. [63] explored rates of post-traumatic stress symptoms in elderly Aboriginal People in Taiwan, describing patterns of attenuating post-traumatic stress disorder in the 12 months following a cyclone, with increasing levels of depression over the same period. However, these rates were not compared to other ethnic or racial groups, and limitations in confounding control and information about the statistical analysis further preclude drawing conclusions from this finding about possible disproportionate burden of mental health in this sub-population [63].

Floods

Four papers explored the mental health impact of floods, focusing on low SES groups, ethnic or racial groups, Indigenous groups, and housing-insecure groups.

Floods & SES

Of the studies examining the mental health impact of floods in low SES groups, two studies found associations between SES and adverse mental health outcomes [47, 51], one study reported mixed findings [62], and one study did not find evidence of an effect [52]. Two post-flood studies found evidence of more mental health problems in those facing financial difficulties compared to those without financial problems, including higher rates of depression [47, 51], anxiety [47], and post-traumatic stress disorder [47, 51]. A study by Carías et al. [62] found mixed findings, where individuals living in poverty who had experienced a flood in the past year had higher depression scores than the general population, but there was no difference in depression scores if the flood had occurred more than a year before [62]. Finally, Clemens et al. [52] did not find differences in the emotional impacts of a flood between participants from socioeconomically disadvantaged versus advantaged areas [52].

Both studies which found evidence for worse mental health outcomes in those with low SES were both cross-sectional with high [47] and medium [51] quality. The study which found mixed evidence used a cohort design and was rated as medium quality [62], and the study which did not find an association was cross-sectional and rated as low quality, due to limited information provided on the study subjects, settings, statistical analysis, and no confounding control [52].

Floods & ethnic or racial group

We identified one study which looked at the differential impact by ethnic group, which found that Hispanic participants exposed to floods were more likely to report mental health problems than non-Hispanic participants [51]. This study used a cross-sectional design and was assessed as medium quality.

Floods & indigenous groups

Matthews et al. [47] found that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander participants were more likely to report probable anxiety and probable depression compared to non-Indigenous participants [47]. This cross-sectional study was rated as high quality.

Floods & housing-insecure groups

There was one study which explored the impact of floods on the mental health of housing-insecure groups. Carías et al. [62] found that participants living in informal settlements/slums, characterised as dense and poorly constructed housing with low access to basic amenities and poor drainage, had a larger increase in depression scores compared to the sample mean [62]. This cohort study was rated as medium quality.

Wildfires

There were two studies which explored the mental health impact of wildfires on low SES groups [43, 60] and one also explored minoritised ethnic or racial groups [43].

Wildfires & SES

Two studies explored the impact of wildfire exposure on mental health by SES group, both finding evidence of worse mental health outcomes in low SES groups compared to other populations following a wildfire [43, 60]. Silveira et al. (2021) found elevated traumatic distress, depression and anxiety symptom scores in low SES groups [43] while Eisenman et al. [60] showed that high-income individuals were less likely to report clinically significant psychological distress compared to low-income individuals [60]. These studies were both cross-sectional and assessed to be medium [43] and high [60] quality.

Wildfires & ethnic or racial group

One study found mixed evidence of a differential impact of wildfires on mental health by ethnic group [43]. Participants of mixed or other ethnic backgrounds had greater traumatic distress, depression and anxiety symptom scores than other minoritised groups, but high depression and anxiety scores were also found in Caucasian groups. This cross-sectional study was rated as medium quality.

Sub-acute climate change events

Droughts & SES

The two studies which explored sub-acute climate change events focused on the impact of droughts in low SES groups. Neither study found evidence of worse mental health outcomes in low SES groups after drought exposure [57, 65]. In a study of farmers in drought-affected areas, Viswanathan et al. [57] detected slightly higher depression scores in low SES groups but did not detect a statistical difference in mean scores [57]. Powers et al.'s [65] study did not detect differences in mental health outcomes among low-income women exposed and not exposed to a drought [65]. The cross-sectional study [57] was rated as medium quality, and the cohort study [65] was rated as high quality.

Gradual environmental changes

Long-term heat exposure & SES

We identified two studies that explored gradual environmental changes, both focused on long-term heat exposure in low SES groups, finding mixed results [40, 71]. A case-crossover study explored socioeconomic disparities in vulnerability to heat exposure, finding evidence of higher rates in cities with lower income. Lower middle-income cities had higher psychiatric hospitalisation rates than upper middle-income cities and high-income cities, although there was overlap in the variance estimate with high-income cities [71]. Yuan et al. [40] found that individuals with lower monthly income reported more severe psychological symptoms including low mood and emotional irritability compared to those with higher income, although they did not find significant differences by education level [40]. The case-crossover study was assessed as medium quality, as it lacked information on confounding control [71], and the cross-sectional study was assessed as low quality, also lacking information on confounding, having unclear inclusion criteria, and not sufficient information on outcome measurement [40].

Combined climate change exposures

Combined events & low SES

We found two studies which explored combined climate change exposures in low SES groups [46, 54]. Powers et al. [54] assessed women with financial difficulty living in exceptional circumstance areas, defined as areas experiencing drought, flood or wildfire, and found no evidence of worse mental health compared to low-income women living in non-exceptional circumstance areas [54]. Patrick et al. [46] investigated individuals with a direct experience of bushfires, floods, or heat waves, and did not find differences in rates of post-traumatic stress by area-level [46]. Both studies were cross-sectional and rated as high [54] and medium quality [46].

Discussion

We identified 32 studies exploring disproportionate impact of climate change events on the mental health of vulnerable populations. Eighteen studies found worse mental health outcomes in vulnerable groups compared to other population groups following climate change events, five studies found mixed evidence, and nine studies did not find evidence of differential outcomes.

Meaning of the findings

Most studies in this review found some evidence that climate change exposures act as risk amplifiers in vulnerable groups, resulting in worse mental health outcomes. This supports theories hypothesising that climate change may exacerbate existing disparities across socioeconomic, ethnic, racial, or cultural group [20, 72, 73]. While this review identified studies which quantified a disproportionate burden of climate events on the mental health of vulnerable groups, several found positive outcomes, including higher levels of post-traumatic growth in low-income mothers [70] and reduction in psychological distress to below pre-disaster levels in the year following a tropical cyclone [66].

Climate change exaggerates disparities among vulnerable populations through three major ways: increased exposure to climate events, increased susceptibility to damage caused by climate events, and decreased ability to cope with and recover after climate events. Vulnerable populations may be disproportionately exposed to climate events. For example, individuals belonging to low SES groups may live in areas with higher disaster risk [74], exposing them to increased physical threats and psychological harm. Minoritised ethnic or racial groups may also be more likely to live in areas more vulnerable to climate-change-related disasters [75]. Historic discriminatory housing policies and residential segregation has resulted in high concentrations of minoritised groups in areas with outdated infrastructure, insufficient weather warning systems, and limited public investment, which may put them at increased risk of facing physical hazards during climate events [8, 75–77]. Housing-insecure and homeless individuals may also be disproportionately impacted by extreme temperature events with limited access to adequate heating/cooling, or public cooling centres [75]. Moreover, during extreme weather events, public health announcements might lack the ability to reach populations experiencing housing precarity [78].

Vulnerable population may be more likely to lose their income due to climate events or may lose access to the land that their income depends on. For example, low SES individuals may be more likely to be employed in climate-sensitive occupations like agriculture, fisheries, or tourism [79]. These threats to one’s livelihood have been found to be associated with significant mental health consequences such as anxiety and depression [80, 81].

Indigenous groups living in close relationships with their environment and its resources may feel the impact of climate change events acutely, particularly if climate change events make ancestral lands less habitable and force the displacement of Indigenous communities [82, 83], invoking painful memories of historic exclusions and displacement, inducing stress and anxiety in affected groups [9, 84]. Losing access to land may drive the loss of traditional ways of life, cultural practices, and community support systems, which may limit the ability to engage in meaningful rituals [75]. For example, Indigenous elders in Australia report psychological distress connected to the inability to collecting shells to create traditional art [9].

Low SES groups and housing-insecure groups may have fewer financial, material, or social resources to evacuate swiftly in a catastrophic event and may face additional challenges relocating and recovering after a climate event [30, 75]. This prolonged exposure to climate change events and delayed recovery from climate change events may impact mental health in the medium-term, although there was some evidence that the disproportionate impact of climate events on mental health in vulnerable groups may attenuate a year following the climate change event [56, 61, 62, 70]​.

Limitations of the existing evidence

While most of the literature included in this review found evidence of disproportionate burden of mental health problems in vulnerable groups following climate change events, some of the studies found mixed or null results. This heterogeneity may be due to the wide range of climate exposures included which may have distinct relationships with mental health outcomes, the measures used to estimate vulnerability, and the choice of mental health outcomes. It is plausible that different climate change events may have a differential impact on mental health. For example, the two studies we identified that looked at the mental health impact of floods in low SES groups consistently found worse mental health outcomes [47, 51], the two studies we identified on the impact of drought did not find evidence of disparities by SES [57, 65]. The findings of disparities in the mental health impact of climate event by SES depended on the measure used to estimate SES. Studies which explored income consistently found evidence of a worse outcomes in low-income groups, while studies measuring education [40, 69] or insurance status [64] found mixed or no evidence of disparities by SES. This review also included a wide range of mental health outcomes, from stress and psychological distress to diagnoses of depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, or severe mental illnesses. There may be distinct pathways between climate change exposures and different mental health outcomes, so consistent outcome measurement is needed to better estimate the associations.

More than 80% of the studies focused on acute climate change events, and we identified limited studies which explored sub-acute or gradual climate change exposures. Further research on the medium- and long-term climate change exposures like sea level rise and permanently altered ecosystems are needed to understand the mental health impact of the full spectrum of climate change exposures. With the increasing occurrence of sub-acute events and accelerated progression of environmental changes [85], there is an urgent need to shift current research attention towards these other climate change exposures [29]. We also did not identify studies which looked at repeated exposure to climate change events, which may pose unique challenges compared to singular events.

An important gap in the literature is that nearly 80% of the studies we identified were from high-income countries and there were no studies from low-income countries, despite evidence indicating that low-middle income countries experience more frequent and severe climate change events, with fewer resources for recovery [86, 87].

Most of the evidence summarised in this review was from cross-sectional studies, with ten cohort studies, and one case-crossover study. Study quality ranged, with nine of high quality, sixteen of medium quality, and seven of low quality. The primary methodological flaw for cross-sectional studies was failure to account for possible confounders. There were few cohort studies, one was rated as high quality, while five were medium, and four were low quality, largely due to lack of clarity around the measurement of outcomes at baseline, and insufficient information on follow-up time and losses to follow-up.

Strengths of this review

Our review adds valuable evidence to the existing knowledge base regarding mental health experiences of vulnerable populations exposed to climate change, expanding on previous research focused on acute climate events [31]. We followed best-practice recommendations for this review, including pre-registering our protocol on PROSPERO, adhering to the PECO framework, and reporting our findings according to the PRISMA guidelines. Title and abstract screening, full-text screening, and quality appraisal were conducted by two independent reviewers, and discussed with a third reviewer to ensure a rigorous review. Our inclusive definition of mental health outcomes, including self-reported measures and symptom scales in addition to diagnostic information was important to capture a more comprehensive view of mental health in historically marginalised and vulnerable groups which face additional barriers to mental health care [88].

Limitations of this review

There are several limitations to note. Our review prioritised exploring the evidence for four vulnerable groups: low SES groups, minoritised ethnic or racial groups, Indigenous groups, and housing-insecure groups, which moves past previous reviews which concentrated on age [25], gender [24], pre-existing mental disorders [26], disability [27], chronic illness, or specific occupations [28], when considering vulnerability. While our review uses a more expansive definition, it does not fully capture the experiences of vulnerability and did not explicitly consider the intersectional nature of vulnerability. For example, many minoritised ethnic groups or Indigenous groups were disproportionately represented in low SES groups and are more likely to face housing insecurity due to systemic discrimination which limits education, employment, and social opportunities [89–91]. Future research which adopts an intersectional lens could provide a more comprehensive view on how pre-existing vulnerabilities are impacted by climate change [92].

Additionally, as this review was limited to English-language articles, it may have excluded a number of articles from low- and middle-income countries, where climate-related impacts on vulnerable populations are often most acute [86, 87]. Moreover, four articles included in this review used data from a sample of low-income mothers who had survived Hurricane Katrina in the United States [66–68, 70], which may have skewed the findings from this study. Given the rapid pace of research in this area, our research includes studies published up to July 17, 2023, and we were not able to update the searches further; therefore, more recent evidence has not been captured. As interest in the field of mental health and climate change continues to grow, the body of evidence is expanding rapidly. Finally, we used the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools for quality appraisal [39]. As there is no tool specific to case-crossover studies, we applied the checklist for case–control studies, which may have introduced inconsistencies in appraising that study.

Conclusion

The mental health of vulnerable populations is disproportionately impacted by climate change events. The current evidence suggests that low socioeconomic status groups, minoritised ethnic or racial groups, Indigenous groups, and housing-insecure groups exhibit worse mental health outcomes following climate change events compared to less vulnerable groups. While the evidence suggests that the mental health impact of climate change events may be worse for vulnerable groups, more research is needed to better understand this differential impact. We found few studies focused on Indigenous groups or housing insecure groups, limited research exploring longer-term climate events, and no studies from low-income countries. With the frequency and intensity of climate change events predicted to rise in the coming years, there is an urgent need to consider underlying social inequalities and prioritise the health of vulnerable communities.
