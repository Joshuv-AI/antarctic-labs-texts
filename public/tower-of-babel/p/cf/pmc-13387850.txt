Post-Acute Sequelae of COVID-19 Across 15 Major Health Domains and 67 Diseases in Individuals With Depressive Disorder: A Korean Nationwide Cohort Study
Authors: Jaehyeong Cho, Selin Woo, Jaeyu Park, Yejun Son, Hyesu Jo, Hayeon Lee, Ho Geol Woo, Hanseul Cho, Guillaume Fond, Laurent Boyer, Yerin Hwang, Dong Keon Yon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals with depressive disorder often face barriers in accessing healthcare; however, research on the acute and post-acute sequelae of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) (PASC) in this population remains limited. We aimed to investigate the risk of PASC in individuals with depressive disorder.MethodsWe used a longitudinal, large-scale, population-based cohort from South Korea, including individuals aged ≥ 20 years with depressive disorder and confirmed severe acute respiratory syndrome-coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection between January 1, 2020 and December 31, 2022. Individuals were followed from the date of COVID-19 diagnosis (index date) until the occurrence of each outcome, which included 15 major health domains and 67 specific diseases defined based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10) codes. To reduce bias, we applied 1:1 propensity score matching and estimated the adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using Cox proportional hazards regression. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on COVID-19 severity (mild vs. moderate-to-severe), vaccination status, and time-dependent risk patterns (< 3, 3–6, 6–12, and ≥ 12 months).ResultsThe study included 722,088 individuals with depressive disorder (mean age, 44.10 [standard deviation, 11.80] years; 61.65% female). In individuals with depressive disorder, SARS-CoV-2 infection was associated with an elevated risk of postacute sequelae across 14 of 15 major health domains, including infections (aHR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.24–1.30), respiratory disorders (aHR, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.49–1.59), and circulatory/respiratory symptoms (aHR, 1.34; 95% CI, 1.31–1.37). Among 67 specific conditions, 35 showed significantly elevated risks, including malnutrition (aHR, 1.31; 1.13–1.53), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (aHR, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.49–1.59), asthma (aHR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.54–1.68), and vascular intestinal disorders (aHR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.03–1.85). The risks peaked within the first 3 months postinfection and gradually declined over time. Severe COVID-19 was associated with increased risks compared to mild cases, particularly for blood disorders (aHR, 3.14; 95% CI, 2.84–3.48), and self-harm (aHR, 14.83; 95% CI, 6.52–33.71).ConclusionIn individuals with depressive disorder, SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated with a higher risk of developing post-acute sequelae across nearly all health domains and more than half of the specific conditions. Targeted surveillance and tailored intervention strategies are needed to mitigate long-term health consequences.

INTRODUCTION

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic was an unprecedented global crisis, resulting in an estimated 3.39 billion infections1 and 13.3–16.6 million excess deaths2 worldwide between 2020 and 2021. However, as global vaccination coverage has increased and the acute phase of the pandemic has subsided, global concern has shifted from severe acute respiratory syndrome-coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection to its diverse sequelae.3 Furthermore, COVID-19 is now recognized as a multi-organ disease with diverse clinical manifestations, highlighting the growing concern over its persistent symptoms and long-term complications.4

However, previous studies investigating the post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC) have been limited to specific organ systems5 or individual diseases,6 without fully considering the multi-organ nature of COVID-19. For instance, individuals with depressive disorder remain underrepresented in research on the long-term effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection, despite their heightened vulnerability due to difficulties in self-care and limited access to healthcare. This vulnerability may have been further exacerbated during the pandemic, as individuals with depressive disorder are less likely to receive timely medical intervention following SARS-CoV-2 infection.7 In addition, most previous studies have primarily focused on mental health outcomes, with limited attention given to the physiological health outcomes associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection.8

Therefore, we investigated a broad spectrum of PASCs across 15 major health domains and 67 specific conditions among individuals with depressive disorder, using a population-based longitudinal dataset representative of South Korea (the K-COV-N cohort). This approach provides a comprehensive understanding of how COVID-19 affects individuals with depressive disorder, who are particularly vulnerable to the challenges posed by the pandemic.

METHODS

Data source

This longitudinal, propensity score-matched cohort analysis utilized the K-COV-N cohort, which is a large-scale, nationwide dataset representative of South Korea. The universal health insurance system of South Korea ensures comprehensive coverage of the entire population and provides a well-structured foundation for longitudinal research. The K-COV-N cohort, developed through collaboration between the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) in South Korea, Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA), and Statistics Korea, includes extensive health data from January 1, 2018 to December 31, 2022 of individuals aged ≥ 19 years.9 This dataset integrates information from national health screenings, prescription records, and related health outcomes from NHIS, mortality data from Statistics Korea, and SARS-CoV-2 test results from KDCA. The cohort, designed as a long-term tracking system supported by a unique single-payer insurance model, enables comprehensive follow-up across hospitals and healthcare facilities; it captures health events, including deaths, occurring outside medical institutions.

Study design and participants

The K-COV-N cohort initially included individuals aged ≥ 19 years (n = 18,873,703) between 2018 and 2022 (Fig. 1A). Depressive disorder was identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10) codes for major depressive disorder with single episode (F32), major depressive disorder with recurrent episode (F33), or dysthymia (F34.1) between 2018 and 2019 (look-back period).10 The index date was defined as the date of SARS-CoV-2 infection, while the end date was determined by the earliest occurrence of the following: 1) date of death, 2) first diagnosis of each outcome, or 3) December 31, 2022.11 To ensure the robustness of the analysis, individuals meeting any of the following criteria were excluded: 1) those with prior diagnoses of the studied health outcomes or death before the index date, and 2) those with missing values in covariates (Fig. 1B). The look-back period for assessing prior diagnostic history extended from 2018 to the index date, while the follow-up period spanned from 2020 to 2022.

Exposure

The primary exposure in this study was the SARS-CoV-2 infection status among patients with depressive disorder. SARS-CoV-2 infection was verified through real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction testing of nasal or pharyngeal swabs, following protocols established by the KDCA and World Health Organization (WHO).12

Additional details on exposure are as follows: 1) The severity of COVID-19 was categorized into three levels (none, mild, and moderate-to-severe) based on each individual's clinical condition. Moderate-to-severe cases were defined by the need for intensive care admission or medical interventions such as oxygen therapy, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, renal replacement therapy, or cardiopulmonary resuscitation; 2) PASC was defined as all health-related outcomes occurring 30 days or more after SARS-CoV-2 infection; and 3) For time attenuation analysis, the post-infection period was divided into four intervals: within 3, 3–6, 6–12, and beyond 12 months after infection.

Outcomes

This study examined the primary outcomes across 15 major health domains and secondary outcomes encompassing 67 specific diseases, classified according to ICD-10 codes.1314 Outcomes were defined as new-onset events occurring after the index date (SARS-CoV-2 infection). The 15 major health domains included the following: 1) infection, 2) blood disorders, 3) endocrine diseases, 4) mental disorders, 5) self-harm, 6) neurological diseases, 7) cardiovascular diseases, 8) respiratory diseases, 9) digestive diseases, 10) skin diseases, 11) genitourinary diseases, 12) circulatory and respiratory symptoms, 13) digestive and abdominal symptoms, 14) eye and ear diseases, and 15) musculoskeletal diseases. A comprehensive list of the specific conditions within each category is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Covariates

The demographic characteristics of the study participants were obtained from the National Health Insurance Database. These included sex, age group (20–39, 40–59, and ≥ 60 years), household income level (low [0–39th percentile], middle [40–79th percentile], and high [80–100th percentile]), and region of residence (urban or rural).9

Body mass index (BMI) was categorized according to the Asia-Pacific guidelines as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (23.0–24.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 25.0 kg/m2).15 Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was classified as < 60, 60–89, and ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m2, with an additional category for unknown values. Blood pressure was defined as either normal (systolic < 140 mmHg and diastolic < 90 mmHg) or elevated (systolic ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg), with unknown values categorized separately. Fasting blood glucose levels were grouped into < 100 mg/dL, ≥ 100 mg/dL, and unknown.16 All biochemical measurements were based on fasting serum samples obtained during national health examinations. Missing covariate values were addressed using the missing indicator method.

Additionally, medical history variables included the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), treatment for diabetes and hyperlipidemia, hypertension medication use, smoking status (never, former, and current smoker), weekly alcohol consumption frequency (< 1 day, 1–2 days, 3–4 days, ≥ 5 days, and unknown), and aerobic physical activity level. Aerobic physical activity was classified as sufficient or insufficient, with sufficient activity defined as at least 600 minutes per week in metabolic equivalent task scores, following the WHO physical activity guidelines.15

Propensity score matching

To enhance the reliability of the primary outcomes and improve generalizability, exposure-driven propensity score matching was conducted to compare individuals with and without SARS-CoV-2 infection among patients with depressive disorder. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model and adjusted for covariates, including age, sex, region of residence, household income, BMI, CCI, history of medication use (diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension), blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, GFR, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and aerobic physical activity. A 1:1 matched cohort was constructed using the greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.001 standard deviations (SDs).17 The quality of matching was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an SMD < 0.1 indicating a minimal imbalance between the groups.17

Statistical analysis

This study examines the exposure of COVID-19, while the outcomes included 15 major health domains and secondary outcomes encompassing 67 specific diseases. The index date for patients with COVID-19 was the date of SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis, whereas for non-infected individuals, it was set as the date matched using propensity score matching. The follow-up period was extended from the index date to December 31, 2022. Patients with depressive disorders were defined as those diagnosed during the look-back period (2018–2019).

This study used the Cox proportional hazards regression model to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to analyze the association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and 67 diseases categorized into 15 major health domains among patients with depressive disorder. Additionally, to minimize residual confounding, the multivariate model included variables such as age, sex, region of residence, household income, CCI, medication history, BMI, blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, GFR, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and aerobic physical activity. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying for COVID-19 severity (mild and moderate-to-severe), time attenuation (< 3, 3–6, 6–12, and over 12 months), and vaccination status (none, 1, and ≥ 2 times). All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), with two-sided P values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

It was approved by the Kyung Hee University Institutional Review Board (KHSIRB-23-241[EA]), the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA), and the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS; KDCA-NHIS-2024-1-280). In accordance with the approval conditions, anonymized administrative data were used, and informed consent from participants was waived.

RESULTS

The K-COV-N cohort initially included 18,873,703 individuals aged over 19 years in South Korea from 2018 to 2022. Subsequently, individuals with depressive disorder were selected and participants meeting the exclusion criteria were removed. After 1:1 propensity score matching, the final sample included 722,088 individuals, divided equally into two groups: 361,044 with SARS-CoV-2 infection and 361,044 without. The mean age was 44.10 years (SD, 11.80) in both groups, and 61.65% of participants were female. The propensity score-matched cohorts were well balanced in terms of baseline characteristics, with standardized mean difference values below 0.1, indicating no significant imbalance (Table 1).

Among patients with depressive disorder, those with SARS-CoV-2 infection had a higher risk of developing post-acute sequelae across 14 of the 15 major health domains (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 2). The following domains showed a significantly increased risk in individuals with COVID-19: infection (aHR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.24–1.30), blood disorders (aHR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.05–1.14), endocrine disorders (aHR, 1.07; 95% CI, 1.05–1.10), mental disorders (aHR, 1.07; 95% CI, 1.01–1.14), neurological disorders (aHR, 1.05; 95% CI, 1.02–1.08), cardiovascular diseases (aHR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.07–1.14), respiratory diseases (aHR, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.49–1.59), digestive diseases (aHR, 1.14; 95% CI, 1.09–1.19), skin diseases (aHR, 1.15; 95% CI, 1.12–1.18), genitourinary diseases (aHR, 1.19; 95% CI, 1.16–1.22), circulatory and respiratory symptoms (aHR, 1.34; 95% CI, 1.31–1.37), digestive and abdominal symptoms (aHR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.13–1.18), injuries (aHR, 1.15; 95% CI, 1.12–1.18), eye and ear diseases (aHR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.09–1.15), and musculoskeletal diseases (aHR, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.10–1.17).

Of 67 post-acute sequelae, 35 were significantly elevated in individuals infected with SARS-CoV-2. The conditions with the highest aHRs included malnutrition (aHR, 1.31; 95% CI, 1.13–1.53), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (aHR, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.49–1.59), asthma (aHR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.54–1.68), and vascular intestinal disorders (aHR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.03–1.85). Notably, digestive diseases, eye and ear diseases, and musculoskeletal diseases were the health domains with the most affected subtypes showing an increased risk.

Subgroup analyses based on the severity of SARS-CoV-2 infection are shown in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 3. Among the patients with depressive disorder who experienced mild COVID-19, more than half of the health domains (11 out of 15) showed elevated aHRs. In individuals with moderate-to-severe infections, an increased risk was observed across all 15 health domains. Notably, moderate-to-severe SARS-CoV-2 infection was associated with markedly elevated risks for several outcomes, including blood disorders (aHR, 3.14; 95% CI, 2.84–3.48) and self-harm (aHR, 14.83; 95% CI, 6.52–33.71). Furthermore, most specific conditions within each major health domain followed a similar pattern, with individuals with moderate-to-severe COVID-19 showing a higher risk than those with mild infection (Supplementary Table 4). Conditions with particularly elevated risks were shown in malnutrition (aHR, 4.48; 95% CI, 3.16–6.33), substance use disorder (aHR, 3.80; 95% CI, 2.33–6.20), epilepsy/seizures (aHR, 3.82; 95% CI, 3.21–4.55), cardiomyopathy and myocarditis (aHR, 6.93; 95% CI, 4.33–11.11), and appendicitis (aHR, 11.83; 95% CI, 10.04–13.93).

The association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and the 15 major health domains among patients with depressive disorder was most pronounced within the first 3 months following the index date. Over time, the aHRs either decreased or remained stable, indicating a time-attenuated or persistent pattern (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 5). Vaccinated individuals exhibited a lower risk of PASC across most health domains than nonvaccinated individuals.

To assess the robustness of our findings for the case definition of depressive disorders, we conducted two sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table 7). First, when patients with dysthymia (F34.1) were excluded, the association between pre-existing depressive disorders and post-acute COVID-19 outcomes remained largely unchanged. Second, when depressive disorders were defined as having ≥ 2 depression-related claims within a 12-month period before COVID-19 diagnosis, the results remained consistent with the main analysis. These findings suggest that the primary results were not materially affected by the operational definitions of depressive disorders.

DISCUSSION

Among patients with depressive disorder, we examined the association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and the incidence of PASC using the K-COV-N cohort (n = 722,088). First, in individuals with depressive disorder, SARS-CoV-2 infection was associated with a higher risk of developing post-acute sequelae across 14 of the 15 major health domains, with the greatest risks observed for infections, respiratory disorders, and circulatory and respiratory symptoms. Second, among the 67 specific post-acute sequelae examined, 35 showed a significantly increased risk following SARS-CoV-2 infection in individuals with depressive disorder, particularly within the digestive, musculoskeletal, and eye and ear disease categories. Third, the risk of PASC was the highest within the first 3 months following infection, with a gradual decline or stabilization over time. Fourth, the impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection varied by severity, with individuals with depressive disorder who experienced moderate-to-severe COVID-19 showing elevated risks across nearly all health domains compared to those with mild infection. Notably, moderate-to-severe infections were associated with a higher risk of blood disorders and self-harm. Fifth, the most specific conditions also showed greater risks among individuals with depressive disorder who experienced moderate-to-severe COVID-19. Substantial associations were observed among malnutrition, substance use disorders, epilepsy/seizures, cardiomyopathy and myocarditis, and appendicitis. Lastly, individuals with depressive disorder who received vaccination showed a lower risk of PASC than those who were not.

As COVID-19 emerged as a global pandemic, several studies have investigated the diverse spectrum of PASC.518192021 However, these studies were often limited, primarily focusing on specific health outcomes (e.g., neurologic,18 cardiovascular,59 and renal function19), involving small sample sizes (n = 154,068,18 153,760,5 134,56519 vs. n = 722,088 of our study), addressing the general population51819 or individuals with broad mental illness without focusing on specific conditions such as depressive disorder.21 In addition, although some studies examined a wide range of clinical outcomes,2223 they were conducted in the general population22 or focused on hospitalized individuals.23 In contrast, this study uniquely investigates the large spectrum of PASC specifically in individuals with pre-existing depressive disorder, offering a novel and comprehensive perspective.

Moreover, prior research involving individuals with depressive disorder has primarily focused on worsening24 or new onset of depressive disorder,6 the development of other mental health conditions,24 susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection rather than the resulting complications,25 or associated demographic and healthcare-related factors after SARS-CoV-2 infection.26 However, previous studies examining individuals with underlying conditions, such as diabetes mellitus,27 and inflammatory rheumatic diseases,28 have shown that investigating the PASC in specific vulnerable subgroups can yield meaningful and clinically relevant findings. In line with this evidence, our study provides novel and academically valuable insights into the association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and the risk of PASC among individuals with depressive disorder using a large-scale, nationwide population-based cohort with a substantial and representative sample size (before matching, n = 8,632,221).

The results of our study may be largely influenced by the substantial barriers to healthcare access faced by individuals with depressive disorder, including unmet medical needs, limited access to timely interventions, and poorer treatment outcomes, which may contribute to their heightened vulnerability to PASC.29 Additionally, the pandemic itself may have amplified psychological distress, further exacerbating physiological vulnerability.30 These factors may compound the effects of the infection and lead to a greater burden of post-acute outcomes.

The temporal pattern of our results may reflect acute inflammatory responses and immune dysregulation triggered by the initial viral infection,31 which tend to subside over time. As the body gradually recovers and homeostasis is restored, the likelihood of new complications or worsening symptoms decreases. In addition, moderate-to-severe COVID-19 is often accompanied by intense systemic inflammation and greater physiological stress, which can contribute to multi-organ dysfunction and long-term health deterioration.32 Notably, aggressive treatments such as mechanical ventilation in moderate-to-severe cases may increase the risk of complications.33

First, reinforcement of the global pharmacovigilance system is required. While certain antidepressants, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, have been associated with reduced hospitalization and mortality among patients with COVID-19,34 other medications such as benzodiazepines may exert immunosuppressive effects.35 Notably, given the substantial increase in the prescription of these medications during the pandemic,26 continued research and close monitoring of individuals receiving them is warranted.

Moreover, considering the potential for SARS-CoV-2 infection to cause diverse complications, personalized and community-based management strategies are particularly important for individuals with depressive disorder. For example, the United States strengthened its mental health care system during the pandemic by institutionalizing telepsychiatry, implementing Crisis Intervention Teams, and expanding peer specialist training programs.36 These efforts may contribute to more effective management of individuals with depressive disorder through tailored vaccination approaches and sustained mental health follow-ups.

Despite the strengths of this study in identifying an increased risk of PASC among individuals with depressive disorder, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, depressive disorders and health outcomes were identified using ICD-10 codes from insurance claims data provided by the NHIS in South Korea. As claims data are primarily collected for billing purposes rather than clinical research, they may not capture all clinical diagnoses and may lack detailed information on the treating clinician’s specialty or treatment details, such as antidepressant use or duration. Consequently, this approach may lead to underreporting or misclassification of depressive disorders.37 Nevertheless, this claims-based definition has been widely applied in previous large-scale epidemiological studies.3839 To address concerns regarding potential diagnostic misclassification, we additionally conducted sensitivity analyses using more restrictive definitions of depressive disorders, which required at least two depression-related claims within a 12-month period and excluded cases with dysthymia (F34.1). The results of these analyses (Supplementary Table 7) were highly consistent with the main findings, supporting the robustness of our conclusions and suggesting that the observed associations were not driven by case-definition artifacts. Second, because the study was conducted on an Asian population, the generalizability of the findings to other racial and ethnic groups may be limited. Nevertheless, the use of a propensity score-matched design allows for a more accurate comparison between individuals with and without SARS-CoV-2 infection by accounting for various baseline characteristics. Third, the database lacked detailed information on certain socioeconomic indicators and other social determinants of health, leading to residual confounding. Fourth, undiagnosed or asymptomatic COVID-19 cases, particularly those that did not undergo PCR testing, may have led to exposure misclassification and underestimation of the true effect. However, in South Korea, the government covers the full cost of all PCR tests; therefore, bias regarding undiagnosed COVID-19 cases is likely to be minimal. Fifth, the observational design of the study limited the ability to draw definitive causal inferences. Nonetheless, in the context of a pandemic, observational studies allow for timely, large-scale analyses in real-world settings, enabling the identification of risk factors and outcomes across diverse populations.

This study examined the association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and risk of developing PASC in individuals with depressive disorder. In individuals with depressive disorder, SARS-CoV-2 infection was associated with a higher risk of developing post-acute sequelae across 14 of the 15 major health domains. Among the 67 specific conditions analyzed, 35 showed a significantly elevated risk in the infected group. The risk of PASC was most pronounced during the first 3 months after SARS-CoV-2 infection, and vaccination was associated with a significantly reduced risk. Additionally, the impact of infection varied according to severity, with more moderate-to-severe cases showing greater risks. These findings emphasize the heightened vulnerability of individuals with depressive disorder to PASC and highlight the importance of targeted monitoring and intervention strategies.
