Vasopressin stimulation testing in hospitalized foals
Authors: Myriah Albrecht, Javier Perez, David Wong, Nimet Browne, Teresa Burns, Maddie Edwards Stevens, Katarzyna Dembek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal gland axis (HPAA) dysfunction impairs survival and can be diagnosed with an arginine vasopressin (AVP) stimulation test.Hypothesis/ObjectivesAdrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and cortisol response to an AVP stimulation test will be attenuated in hospitalized foals compared to healthy foals and associated with severity of disease.AnimalsFifteen healthy and 45 hospitalized foals: 19 septic, 26 sick non-septic (SNS).MethodsA prospective, observational case–control design was utilized to assess HPAA function and compare corticotropin-releasing hormone, ACTH, and cortisol concentrations at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 90 min after administration of AVP (5 IU). Adrenocorticotropic hormone and cortisol fold increase were calculated at 15 and 30 min compared to baseline.ResultsAdrenocorticotropic hormone and cortisol concentrations increased at 15 and 30 min after AVP administration in all groups (P < .05). Adrenocorticotropic hormone concentration was lower in septic (112; 95% CI, 86.3-181.8 pg/mL) and SNS (102; 95% CI, 82.8-150 pg/mL) compared to healthy foals (179; 95% CI, 135.6-275.8 pg/mL) 15 min after AVP administration (P < .05). Fold increase in ACTH was lower in septic (3.3; 95% CI, 2.3-5.6) compared to SNS (3.7; 95% CI, 3.5-6.1) and healthy foals (7; 95% CI, 5.7-9.5; P < .01). Fold increase in cortisol was lower in septic (2; 95% CI, 1.8-8.3) compared to healthy foals (4.3; 95% CI, 2.8-9.4; P < .05).Conclusions and clinical importanceHypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal gland axis dysfunction, evidenced by ACTH and cortisol responses to an AVP stimulation test, appears linked to disease severity and is a complication of illness. An AVP stimulation test might help guide therapy in ill foals.

Introduction

Endocrine dysfunction, particularly adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and cortisol responses, is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to prognosis and treatment outcomes in hospitalized foals. Survival in critically ill neonates depends on the integrity of homeostatic systems, including the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (HPAA). 1–4 Sepsis and endotoxemia stimulate the HPAA, resulting in increased concentrations of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), arginine vasopressin (AVP), ACTH, and cortisol.5 Neonatal HPAA function differs from adult horses, with maturation occurring days before parturition and continuing through the first weeks of life.6 Such differences negatively affect the foal’s ability to respond to stress and illness.

Elevations in cortisol concentrations during times of illness are critical for stress adaptation.7 However, transient, reduced cortisol production and activity relative to increased demand during illness are documented in several species, including neonatal foals.6,7 This phenomenon is termed relative adrenal insufficiency, critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency (CIRCI), or ACTH:cortisol imbalance.8

Critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency results from transient suppression of one or more components of the HPAA (CRH, ACTH, or cortisol secretion), reduced sensitivity of target tissues, depletion of adrenocortical cortisol synthetic capacity, diminished responsiveness due to peripheral tissue resistance, or impaired cortisol metabolism.6,9 The precise mechanisms remain unclear although likely involve a multitude of factors including direct HPAA damage, suppression by medications, or immune or inflammatory-mediated suppression.6

Adrenocorticotropic hormone stimulation is the current gold standard method although studies show poor reliability and performance in critically ill foals.6,10 Additionally, an ACTH stimulation test only evaluates the adrenal component of the HPAA.6 A CRH stimulation test assesses higher HPAA levels, and while historically not recommended due to poor sensitivity in humans, recent studies suggest it might reliably evaluate ACTH and cortisol responses in foals.6,10

Arginine vasopressin also stimulates ACTH release and is the primary adrenocorticotropin-releasing hormone in horses, indicating its potential to assess HPAA function more effectively.11–13 The HPAA response in healthy neonatal foals to AVP stimulation tests has been investigated and includes rise in ACTH and cortisol.14 An AVP dose of 5 international units (IU) administered IV is recommended for assessment of both pituitary and adrenal responses in neonatal foals.14 Currently, there are no studies examining the pituitary and adrenocortical responses to an AVP stimulation test in hospitalized foals, and further investigation is needed. Therefore, the goal of this study was to determine CRH, ACTH, and cortisol response to 5 IU of AVP in hospitalized and healthy foals. We hypothesized that ACTH and cortisol response to administration of exogenous AVP will be attenuated in hospitalized foals compared to healthy foals. Additionally, we aimed to establish cut-off values for ACTH and cortisol responses (fold increase) to identify foals with HPAA dysfunction and suspected CIRCI.

Materials and methods

Animals

Sixty neonatal foals of both sexes and any breed < 7 days of age were included in the study between December 2023 and June 2025. Enrolled foals were categorized as healthy, sick non-septic (SNS), or septic. All hospitalized foals underwent treatment for their respective conditions throughout the study period. Foals were excluded if exogenous corticosteroids were administered before or during the study period.

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and the Veterinary Clinical Research Advisory Committee at all respective institutions. Owner consent was obtained before inclusion in the study.

Study design

A prospective, observational case–control design was used to evaluate systemic CRH, ACTH, and cortisol response to IV administration of 5 IU of AVP. The dosage of AVP was determined based on previously published data.14

Sample collection

Enrollment into the study was obtained within 24 h of hospital admission. Stabilization was prioritized for any critically ill foals before initiating vasopressin stimulation. Five IU of AVP was diluted in 20 mL of 0.9% sterile saline, then administered IV over 5 min. A baseline blood sample was obtained before AVP administration, and serial blood samples were collected at 15, 30, 60, and 90 min after administration.

Data analysis

Data were not normally distributed as tested for normality with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. A Friedman’s 2-way repeated measures analysis of variance test with Student–Newman–Keuls post-hoc test was used to compare CRH, ACTH, and cortisol concentrations between time points as well as between groups. A fold increase was defined as the ratio of an increased concentration of ACTH at 15 min and of cortisol at 30 min compared to baseline concentrations, as previously described.14 Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test was utilized to compare the differences in fold increase in ACTH and cortisol concentrations after AVP stimulation between groups of foals. The lower limit of the 95% CI of ACTH and cortisol fold increase in healthy foals were used to define ACTH insufficiency (<5.7) and cortisol insufficiency (<2.8). Lastly, univariate logistical regression analysis was utilized to predict sepsis based on the presence of ACTH and cortisol insufficiency. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism and IBM SPSS Statistical Software (SPSS and GraphPad Software, Inc [version 7.0b], La Jolla, California).

Results

Study sample

A total of 60 foals were included in the study. Breeds included Quarter Horses (n = 28), Warmbloods (n = 10), Thoroughbreds (n = 14), Arabians (n = 3), Saddlebreds (n = 3), and Gypsy Vanners (n = 2) < 7 days of age. Of the 60 foals, 15 were healthy and 45 were hospitalized. Further categorizing our hospitalized group, 19 were considered septic, and 26 were considered SNS. No difference was noted in age between groups (P > .05; Table 1). Median sepsis score was 2.5 (range 0-7) for healthy foals, 7 (range 0-11) for SNS foals, and 15 (range 6-28) for septic foals (P < .05). Diagnoses for the SNS group included lameness, meconium impaction, failure of transfer of passive immunity, mild neonatal maladjustment syndrome, and foals born from dystocia or premature placental separation delivery but were not critically ill. All foals in the healthy group survived to discharge, and 43/45 foals in the hospitalized group survived to discharge. Slightly paler mucous membranes as well as a slight decrease in heart rate were transiently observed 5-10 min after AVP administration as previously reported.14

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that an AVP stimulation test can be utilized to assess HPAA function in healthy and hospitalized neonatal foals. In this study, ACTH response to AVP was significantly lower in hospitalized foals compared to healthy controls, suggesting impaired pituitary responsiveness and HPAA dysfunction within this hospitalized study sample. This blunted response might indicate altered pituitary response secondary to impaired receptor signaling, desensitization, or disruption in signaling or response due to underlying disease. Inflammatory cytokines and lipopolysaccharide can impair HPAA function.15 This is a recognized mechanism in critically ill human patients and is a suspected, and likely, similar process in septic foals.1 Further investigation is needed to understand the mechanism for this finding as well as to determine whether this attenuated response correlates with disease severity, prognosis, or response to treatment.

Cortisol response to AVP stimulation (fold increase) was lower in septic foals compared to healthy foals, indicating that both ACTH and cortisol responses are altered in critical illness. In human medicine, CIRCI is often diagnosed by subnormal response in cortisol release after administration of exogenous ACTH.16 Cortisol response to ACTH stimulation was decreased and associated with sepsis and multiple organ dysfunction in previous studies and is also supported by the current study.3,17–19 These results suggest that while the adrenal cortex retains some capacity for cortisol production, its response might be blunted in severe illness and potentially compounded by biologically inactive ACTH released from the pituitary.

In addition to the observed difference in cortisol fold increase, logistic regression analysis demonstrated that cortisol response was a stronger predictor of sepsis than ACTH response. Specifically, foals with cortisol insufficiency, defined as a ≤ 2.8-fold increase in cortisol from baseline, were 3.3 times more likely to be septic compared to foals with a cortisol response > 2.8-fold. In contrast, no statistically significant association was found between ACTH insufficiency and sepsis outcome in the regression model. This suggests that cortisol response is a clinically relevant indicator of HPAA dysfunction in hospitalized foals.

Our study findings underscore the potential role of impaired adrenal output in the pathophysiology of illness in foals. While complex, the apparent dissociation between ACTH and cortisol dynamics warrants further investigation to clarify underlying mechanisms and clinical relevance. Several hypotheses might explain this finding. The adrenal glands might be unable to mount an appropriate cortisol response due to adrenal and pituitary immaturity, low ACTH concentration, lower ACTH biological activity, adrenal hypo-responsiveness due to prolonged illness, or alterations of ACTH receptors in the adrenal cortex.6,18 Secondly, while bacterial components and host pro-inflammatory cytokines can initiate HPAA response and stimulation of cortisol synthesis and secretion, these same factors might also paradoxically suppress HPAA function during times of overwhelming infection or excessive host response.6,15 Furthermore, cortisol is not stored, and therefore once utilized, synthesis must occur for additional secretion. Cortisol synthesis is under enzymatic control for conversion with numerous steps involved in its formation. Illness might alter cortisol synthesis, metabolism, or increase cortisol clearance.6 Presently, there is no data on cortisol metabolism or metabolites in foals or horses.

In contrast to previous studies reporting elevated baseline ACTH concentrations in sick foals, there was no difference in baseline concentrations of ACTH between groups in our study.18,20 Potential explanations for this discrepancy include differences in timing of sample collection relative to disease onset or initiation of treatment, disease severity, classification of foal groups, as well as sample size and statistical power. The absence of differences in ACTH is most likely attributable to mild disease severity, as not all of the foals in this study were critically ill (low sepsis score) and only 2 foals did not survive.

No change in plasma CRH was noted between groups or over time after administration of AVP. In equids, AVP acts primarily at the anterior pituitary via V1b receptors to potentiate ACTH release and appears to have minimal direct stimulation of hypothalamic CRH secretion. This finding has also been supported by previous studies and further confirms that AVP appears to exert minimal influence on hypothalamic CRH release in neonatal foals.14 Additionally, because CRH secretion is predominantly into hypothalamic-hypophyseal portal vessels and not into systemic circulation, obtaining accurate measurements presents a methodological challenge.6

Adrenal insufficiency can be further categorized into primary, secondary, or tertiary. Primary adrenal insufficiency, also known as cortisol insufficiency, refers to dysfunction at the level of the adrenal gland. Secondary adrenal insufficiency, also known as ACTH insufficiency, results from dysfunction at the level of the pituitary. Lastly, tertiary adrenal insufficiency refers to dysfunction at the level of the hypothalamus.7 Utilizing the lower end of the 95% CI for fold increase response in healthy foals, we defined ACTH insufficiency as < 5.7-fold increase from baseline and cortisol insufficiency as < 2.8-fold increase from baseline within our population. Using this definition, evidence of ACTH insufficiency was identified in 70%-78% of SNS and septic foals, while 50%-63.2% demonstrated cortisol insufficiency. These values represent initial reference points based on our dataset and require additional investigations to further validate. When assessing both cortisol and ACTH responses to AVP stimulation together, septic and SNS foals showed signs of adrenal and pituitary dysfunction. However, none of the healthy foals exhibited any indications of HPAA dysfunction based on the same criteria. This suggests that our protocol might provide a more accurate method for evaluating HPAA integrity in foals compared to methods that only assess adrenal response. Our findings demonstrate that ACTH and cortisol insufficiency are prevalent in hospitalized foals and might require targeted treatment with hormonal replacement therapy.

Accurate differentiation of CIRCI is important as the pathophysiology of adrenal insufficiency and therapeutic strategies can vary according to etiology.7 Various treatments for CIRCI have been suggested and typically involve the use of corticosteroids with hydrocortisone being utilized most commonly. Human studies demonstrate reduced duration of septic shock and intensive care unit hospitalization after administration of corticosteroids to CIRCI patients although effects on mortality rate are variable. Corticosteroids are associated with potential adverse effects including hyperglycemia, neuromuscular weakness, and increased risk of infection.21 In equids, therapy with hydrocortisone or other corticosteroids have been utilized although not critically evaluated.6 Overall, treatment for CIRCI in both human and veterinary medicine remains controversial. Accurate diagnosis of HPAA dysfunction is a critical first step in identifying CIRCI. Additionally, establishing this diagnosis provides the necessary foundation for guiding therapy, monitoring response to such therapies, and minimizing adverse effects.

Not only does investigation of the HPAA provide insight into the diagnosis and treatment of CIRCI, but an altered response might also be useful to predict sepsis. Early identification and institution of therapies are key to improving outcome in septic foals. However, means of identifying septic foals such as positive blood culture results or weighted sepsis scores have low sensitivity and specificity.16,22–31 In our study groups, foals with cortisol insufficiency were 3.3 times more likely to be septic. This finding along with other clinical and clinicopathological findings might be useful to identify septic foals earlier, implement therapy accordingly, and better inform owners about the potential financial investment and care commitment required for successful treatment.

In septic humans, CIRCI is frequent and is negatively correlated with survival.32–37 Although previous studies report a poor prognosis in foals with CIRCI, no association with death was observed in our study.20 This is attributable to the relatively mild disease severity of the enrolled cases. Additionally, survival rates were high (95%). Therefore, no attempt was made to detect significant associations or trends with non-survival, as there were only 2 non-survivors and a lack of severely ill foals within our study sample.

Limitations to this study include a low number of ill foals, low number of septic foals, and mild severity of illness. Secondly, while there was no difference in age among groups within our study, age-matched foals within each group should be considered for future studies, as maturation of the HPAA is highly variable during the first days of life. Thirdly, initial stabilization, including fluid therapy, was prioritized before vasopressin administration. While this approach was necessary for patient welfare, such interventions might influence endogenous vasopressin dynamics and hydration status, potentially affecting measured responses to stimulation.

In conclusion, our study has revealed that evaluating both the ACTH and cortisol response to an AVP stimulation test offers insight into HPAA dysfunction in hospitalized foals. However, this assessment is limited to the pituitary and adrenal responsiveness to AVP stimulation and does not evaluate peripheral tissue cortisol responses or alterations in cortisol metabolism and clearance.
