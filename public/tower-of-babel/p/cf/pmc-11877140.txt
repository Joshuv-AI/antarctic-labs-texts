CircMALAT1 promotes the proliferation and metastasis of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma via the miR-512-5p/VCAM1 axis
Authors: Meixia Zhang, Mingyan He, Liangliang Bai, Fan Du, Yingping Xie, Bimin Li, Yuming Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCircular RNAs play a pivotal role in the progression of various cancers. In our previous study, we observed high expression of the circRNA MALAT1 (cMALAT1) in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) cells co-incubated with activated hepatic stellate cells. This study is designed to explore the roles of cMALAT1 and the underlying mechanisms in ICC. We find that cMALAT1 significantly facilitates the progression of ICC both in vitro and in vivo. The binding between cMALAT1 and miR-512-5p is subsequently confirmed through RNA pull-down experiments. As anticipated, the application of miR-512-5p mimics noticeably reverses the cMALAT1 overexpression-induced malignant phenotypes of ICC cells. Furthermore, VCAM1 is identified as a downstream gene of the cMALAT1/miR-512-5p axis. Importantly, silencing of VCAM1 not only effectively suppresses the malignant phenotypes of ICC cells but also significantly impairs the functions of cMALAT1. Our study reveals that cMALAT1 promotes the progression of ICC by competitively binding to VCAM1 mRNA with miR-512-5p, leading to the upregulation of VCAM1 expression and the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Keywords: circular MALAT1, ICC, miR-512-5p, VCAM1, EMT

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), the second most diagnosed primary liver cancer after hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), is considered a universally fatal malignancy, accounting for approximately 3% of all gastrointestinal malignancies [ 1, 2]. The 5-year survival rate of ICC is only 10%–40% [3]. Owing to the hidden onset of this cancer, the efficacy of surgery (mainly in the early stages of ICC) is not very promising [4]. The primary reasons for treatment failure in ICC patients undergoing surgical intervention are local recurrence and distant metastasis, which are attributed to the different genetic background-induced individual differences. Therefore, exploring new biomarkers related to the treatment and prognosis of ICC is significantly beneficial for identifying new strategies for the treatment of ICC.

Circular RNAs, a class of non-coding RNAs, have been mistakenly recognized as the by-products of splicing for a long period of time [ 4, 5]. They are abundantly expressed: circRNA expression is much higher than that of their linear host RNAs in fibroblasts [6]; diverse: circRNAs with varying exon-intron combinations are different [7]; stable: circRNAs always lack 5′-3′ polarity and a polyadenylated tail, which contributes to stability [8]; and conserved: circRNAs are reported to be more conserved than other RNA types [9]. These features indicate that there may be a variety of potential bio-functions and clinical settings [10]. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have indicated that circRNAs are closely involved in the regulation of multiple diseases, mainly through the transcriptional or post-transcriptional regulation of genes [ 11, 12]. More importantly, circRNAs are differentially expressed in tumor tissues and are involved in various aspects of tumorigenesis and development [13], at least partially. CircRNAs can function as microRNA (miRNA) sponges, enabling them to bind target miRNAs and inhibit their activity via a mechanism named “miRNA sponging”. As reported, miRNAs are involved in the transcription of mRNAs and contribute to cell proliferation, mobility, death, and differentiation [14]. For example, ciRS-7 (also known as CDR1), the most prominent circRNA, can serve as a miR-7 sponge and bind to miR-7 to inhibit its activity, resulting in upregulated expression level of miR-7-targeted downstream transcripts [15]. In papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), CircFAT1 is highly expressed and can strongly decrease the expression of miR-873, thus promoting zinc finger E-box binding homeobox 1 ( ZEB1) expression and eventually facilitating the malignant progression of PTC [16]. However, few studies have investigated the role of circRNAs in ICC.

It has been reported that there is apparent mesenchymal and fibrous tissue deposition in the malignant process of ICC [17], and activated hepatic stellate cells (aHSCs) are major contributors to mesenchymal and fiber formation in ICC [18]. Moreover, our previous work indicated that aHSCs regulate the RORα/Wnt/β-catenin axis via miR-1246 to promote cell proliferation, metastasis, and process of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [19]. More interestingly, our research demonstrated that circMALAT1 (cMALAT1) is highly expressed in ICC cells co-incubated with aHSCs and that highly expressed cMALAT1 promotes the proliferation and metastasis of ICC cells, indicating that cMALAT1 may play a role in the regulation of ICC progression.

Materials and Methods

Cell culture and transfection

The human cholangiocarcinoma cell lines HCCC9810 and HUCCT1 were supplied by Procell (Wuhan, China) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (61870036; Gibco, Carlsbad, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 15070063; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) and 1% Pen-strep (PB180120; Procell) at 37°C and a CO 2 concentration of 5%.

The plasmids of short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting cMALAT1 (Lenti-cMALAT1) and its shRNA control (Lenti-control) were constructed by Hippo Bio (Huzhou, China), and separately transfected into lentivirus using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668-019; Invitrogen) according to the instructions. The constructed virus was then collected (1 × 10 8 TU/mL), incubated with HCCC9810 cells at 37°C in an incubator with 5% CO2, and screened with puromycin (1 μg/mL). The silenced plasmids of cMALAT1 (siR-cMALAT1) or VCAM1 (siR- VCAM1) targeted by small-interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and negative control siRNA (siR-NC), the over-expression plasmids of pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro vector (Invitrogen) cloned with cMALAT1, and VCAM1 sequences, and miR-512-5p mimics or negative control mimics (NC mimics) were purchased from Hippo Bio. When the cell confluence reached 70%, HUCCT1 and HUCCC9810 cells were transfected with the above plasmids utilizing lipofectamine 2000 and adding 50 μL Opti-MEM (31985-070; Gibco) for 24 h, respectively. Post transfection, the efficiency of transfection was determined using western blot. The sequences of information were as follows:

siR-NC sense: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGAGUCACGUTT-3′

siR-NC antisense: 5′-ACGUGACUCGUUCGGAGAATT-3′

siR-cMALAT1 sense: 5′-GUAUUUUUAGAAACUUUGUCU-3′

siR-cMALAT1 antisense: 5′-AGACAAAGUUUCUAAAAAUAC-3′

VCAM1 siRNA sense: 5′-CCAGAUAGAUAGUCCACUGAA-3′

VCAM1 siRNA antisense: 5′-UUCAGUGGACUAUCUAUCUGG-3′

NC mimcis: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGAGUCACGUTT-3′

hsa-miR-512-5p mimics sense: 5′-CACUCAGCCUUGAGGGCACUUUC-3′

hsa-miR-512-5p mimics antisense: 5′-AAGUGCCCUCAAGGCUGAGUGUU-3′

Methyl tetrazolium (MTT) assay

MTT assay was performed to determine cell proliferation. Briefly, cells (100 μL) were added to 96-well plates at 5 × 10 3 per well, and the plate was incubated overnight at 37°C. The medium was subsequently removed, and 50 μL of MTT solution (475989, 1 mg/mL; Sigma, St Louis, USA) was added to each well. After that, the plate was incubated at 37°C for up to 3 h. Then, 150 μL of DMSO was added to each well to facilitate the dissolution of the purple crystals. Finally, a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, USA) was used to read the absorbance at a wavelength of 570 nm.

Transwell experiments

To assess the migration and invasion abilities of the cells, a transwell assay was performed. A 100-μL cell suspension (1 × 10 4 cells/mL) was added to the transwell chamber (CLS3396; Millipore, Billerica, USA), and then, the lower chamber was filled with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were fixed with methyl alcohol for 15 min and subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution at room temperature for 30 min. The migration of the cells was subsequently monitored, and images were obtained at ×100 magnification via a microscope (Olympus IX73; Olympus Corp, Tokyo, Japan). For the cell invasion experiments, the Transwell filters were precoated with Matrigel (354234; Corning, New York, USA) before the cells were seeded. All other steps were the same as those in the migration experiment.

Colony formation assay

Five hundred cells were added to the 6-well plate to be cultured for two continuous weeks, and the medium was changed every 3 days. The cells were subsequently subjected to washing with PBS, followed by fixation with 0.8 mL of methyl alcohol for 20 min. After that, the cells were stained with crystal violet solution (0.8 mL, C0121; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min. Ultimately, the colonies with a cell count greater than 50 cells were counted under an optical microscope (Olympus IX73).

Western blot analysis

The cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer on ice to extract the protein. A BCA protein assay kit (P0012S; Beyotime) was subsequently used to quantify the concentration of total protein in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The western blotting experimental procedures were described in relevant previous research [20]. Finally, a chemiluminescence kit (20148; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) was used to visualize the protein bands, with β-actin used as a loading control, and the antibody information is presented in Table 1.

Xenograft mouse model

Twelve 4-week-old nude mice (20 g, female) were obtained from SLACK Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The temperature range in the animal facility was maintained at 22–24°C, with a humidity level of 40%–60%. Xenografts were established via the subcutaneous injection of 5 × 10 6 HCCC9810 cells stably expressing cMALAT1 (Lenti-cMALAT1, n = 6) or control cells (Lenti-control, n = 6) into the nude mouse right flank. Ten days after subcutaneous injection, the tumor size was determined via precision micrometer calipers until the volume of the largest tumors reached approximately 1500 mm 3. The tumor volume (mm 3) was calculated via the following equation: V = (a × b × c)/2, where a, b and c represent the three diameters of the tumor. Five weeks after treatment, the mice were humanely euthanized, and the tumors were surgically excised and weighed in strict adherence to international ethical standards and the principles outlined in the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanchang University (IACUC) and approved by the IACUC (Approval No. 2020--124).

TdT-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay

A TUNEL assay was used to assess the effect of cMALAT1 on cell apoptosis in xenografts. The tumor tissues were processed for embedding in paraffin wax, with a precise slice thickness of 5 μm. The slices were deparaffinized in xylene and hydrated in ethanol on different gradients. Then, 100 μL of proteinase K solution (20 μg/mL) was added for permeability. The FITC solution was prepared following the instructions provided in the TUNEL kit (G1501-50T; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), incubated at 37°C for 1 h in the dark, and then washed with PBS. Upon nuclear staining with DAPI solution, the sections were sealed and imaged promptly via a fluorescence microscope.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay

To detect the expression of cMALAT1 in xenografts, tumor tissue slices were treated with citrate buffer (G1202; Servicebio) or 3% H 2O 2. The slices were subsequently blocked with 3% BSA, and the primary antibody was subsequently incubated with the samples overnight at 4°C. Next, the sections were incubated with secondary antibody at 37°C for 30 min. Staining and restaining were completed with DAB (G1212; Servicebio) and hematoxylin (G1004; Servicebio), respectively. Finally, images were captured with a microscope.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

The tissue samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde overnight. The fixed tissue samples were dehydrated sequentially in ethanol solutions of different concentrations, usually 70% ethanol, 95% ethanol, and 100% ethanol. The samples were dissolved in paraffin at 55°C–60°C for sectioning. Thin slices of tissue were placed on slides and dehydrated through ethanol, after which the tissue was saturated with paraffin. The nuclei were stained first with hematoxylin solution, and then the cytoplasm was stained with eosin solution. After staining, the sections were dehydrated in different concentrations of ethanol solution and then made transparent in a mixture of benzene and hyaluronic acid. Finally, the sections were covered with coverslips and sealed with a sealer. The prepared slides were placed under a microscope to observe the tissue morphology and structure.

Biotin-coupled probe pull-down assay

Through a database of circular RNAs ( https://circinteractome.nia.nih.gov), the downstream target of cMALAT1 was predicted. Next, a biotin-coupled probe pull-down assay was performed to validate the results obtained. MiR-512-5p mimic-overexpressing HCCC9810 cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde at 37°C for 10 min. The cells were then lysed and subjected to sonication. Following centrifugation, 50 μL of the supernatant was taken as the input, and the remaining portion was incubated with the biotin-labeled probe against the miR-512-5p mimics or negative control at 25°C for 4 h. The complexes of the miR-512-5p mimics were then captured overnight with streptavidin-coupled Dynabeads (Invitrogen). The following day, the miR-512-5p mimic complexes were incubated with the relevant RIP wash buffer, which contained proteinase K, for a specified period. Finally, the expression of cMALAT1 was evaluated by real-time reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR).

qRT-PCR

The total RNA was extracted and subjected to treatment with RNase R (R7092L; Beyotime) before cDNA synthesis was performed via previously described procedures [21]. The relative expression of cMALAT1 was subsequently assessed via qRT-PCR via an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Forster City, USA). The 2 –ΔΔCt method was used to calculate the relative expressions of genes, and β-actin served as an internal control. Table 2 presents information of the primers used.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To further determine the mechanism by which miR-512-5p regulates VCAM1 expression, we conducted a dual-luciferase reporter experiment. First, we used TargetScan (v8.0; targetscan.org) to predict the binding site of miR-512-5p in the 3′UTR of VCAM1 mRNA. Then, the sequences of the wild-type and mutant binding sites were synthesized and subcloned and inserted into the pGLO vector (Tsingke, Beijing, China) to generate the wild-type pGLO-VCAM1 3′UTR (pGLO-VCAM1-WT) and the mutant pGLO-VCAM1 3′UTR (pGLO-VCAM1-MT) luciferase reporter plasmids, respectively. The mutant was formed by reverse complementation of the sequences predicted to bind to hsa-miR-512-5p. The reconstructed vectors mentioned above were then cotransfected separately with the miR-512-5p overexpression mimic plasmid and control mimics into HCCC9810 cells via Lipofectamine 2000. Finally, the luciferase activity was evaluated via a luciferase assay kit (Promega, Madison, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed via GraphPad Prism 6.0 and are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted to assess differences among multiple groups via one-way ANOVA, whereas for comparisons of two groups, the t test was employed. P values were used for the statistical significance threshold. P ​< 0.05 is denoted as * and P ​< 0.01 as **.

Results

cMALAT1 regulates the malignant phenotype of ICC in vitro and in vivo

cMALAT1 serves as a biomarker for cancer diagnosis and is highly expressed in clinical hepatocellular carcinoma samples [ 22, 23]. To verify the function of cMALAT1 in ICC, siRNA and overexpression plasmids targeting cMALAT1 were synthesized and constructed, respectively. Two intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) cell lines, HUCCT1 and HCCC9810, were selected for this study. The relative expression of cMALAT1 in HUCCT1 and HCCC9810 cells was evaluated ( Figure 1A), revealing higher expression in HUCCT1 cells than in HCCC9810 cells. Consequently, we knocked down cMALAT1 in HUCCT1 cells and overexpressed it in HCCC9810 cells to obtain more obvious and intuitive experimental results. The efficiency of cMALAT1 knockdown and overexpression was subsequently verified in HCCC9810 and HUCCT1 cells, respectively ( Figure 1B). The data from the MTT, transwell, and colony formation experiments confirmed that overexpression of cMALAT1 dramatically promoted cell proliferation, invasion, migration, and colony formation, whereas its knockdown showed opposite effects ( Figure 1C–E). EMT is a process that is strongly implicated in the invasion and migration of tumor cells, and key biomarkers of EMT include E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and vimentin [ 24, 25]. Additionally, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)-2 and 9 are endopeptidases linked to cancer and are known for their capacity to degrade the extracellular matrix, thus promoting tumor invasion [26]. Overexpression of cMALAT1 resulted in the downregulation of E-cadherin expression and the upregulation of N-cadherin, vimentin, MMP2, and MMP9 expressions in ICC cells, and the opposite results were observed with the knockdown of cMALAT1 ( Figure 1F). Similarly, the growth of xenograft tumors was significantly greater in the cMALAT1-overexpressing group than in the control group ( Figure 2A). Ki67 is a marker of tumor proliferation and is commonly used to assess the proliferative status of tumor cells [27]. TUNEL and IHC assays revealed that overexpression of cMALAT1 inhibited apoptosis ( Figure 2B) and significantly increased the expression of Ki67 in tumor tissues ( Figure 2C). The results of HE staining demonstrated that overexpression of cMALAT1 resulted in high inflammatory infiltration of the tumor tissue ( Figure 2D). Moreover, we analyzed cMALAT1 expression in clinical samples and its correlation with clinical features via TCGA ( https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) ( Table 3). These findings indicate that cMALAT1 functions as an oncogene, contributing to tumor growth.

In summary, the results of both the cellular and animal experiments consistently confirmed that cMALAT1 facilitates proliferation and migration and inhibits apoptosis in ICC.

cMALAT1 reverses the anti-cancer effect of miR-512-5p in ICC cells

Circular RNAs (circRNAs), important noncoding RNAs in various human cancers, play crucial roles in a broad range of human malignancies by functioning as molecular sponges for miRNAs, which competitively bind to miRNA-binding sites, thereby blocking the expressions of their respective target mRNAs [ 28, 29]. Circ-MALAT1 (cMALAT1) has been reported to regulate the malignant phenotype of colorectal cancer (CRC) via the miR-506-3p/KAT6B axis [ 30, 31]. However, the role of cMALAT1 in ICC remains poorly understood. To explore whether cMALAT1 performs similar functions in the ICC, the circular RNA interactome database ( https://circinteractome.nia.nih.gov/) was used to predict the miRNAs that bind to cMALAT1. Notably, cMALAT1 and miR-512-5p strongly bind to each other ( Figure 3A), and this binding relationship was confirmed by biotin-coupled miRNA pull-down experiments and qPCR ( Figure 3B). To further confirm that cMALAT1 binds to miR-512-5p, the binding site identified in cMALAT1 was mutated to perform dual-luciferase reporter and RNA pull-down experiments ( Figure 3C). To reveal the role of miR-512-5p in regulating ICC progression and the effect of cMALAT1 on miR-512-5p action, miR-512-5p mimics and cMALAT1 were transfected into ICC cells. As demonstrated by the experimental results, miR-512-5p inhibited the cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and colony formation capabilities of ICC cells, while this tumor-suppressive effect was significantly attenuated by cMALAT1 ( Figure 3D–F). Furthermore, cMALAT1 significantly reversed the inhibition of EMT by miR-512-5p ( Figure 3G). In conclusion, our results preliminarily confirmed that cMALAT1 could significantly reverse the suppressive influence of miR-512-5p on the malignant phenotype of ICC cells.

cMALAT1 promotes the malignant progression of ICC through the miR-512-5p/VCAM1 axis

VCAM1, as a cell adhesion molecule, has been implicated in the progression of several types of cancers [32]. It was found to be closely associated with ICC growth and metastasis and could serve as a promoter of ICC [33]. It has been reported that VCAM1 can bind to miR-98-5p in HUVECs and accelerate the injury of endothelial cells [34]. However, the underlying mechanism by which miR-512-5p affects VCAM1 in ICC is unclear. Based on our basic research, in this study, the potential binding site of miR-512-5p and VCAM1 was predicted via the Target Scan Human database, which revealed that the binding site is located in the 3772-3778 region of the 3′UTR of VCAM1 ( Figure 4A). The role of VCAM1 in HCCC9810 cells was explored via gain- and loss-of-function experiments. VCAM1 was overexpressed or silenced in HCCC9810 cells. The effects of silenced and overexpressed VCAM1 were verified by qRT-PCR and western blotting experiments ( Figure 4B,C). We observed a significant reduction in the proliferation, colony formation, invasion and migration of HCCC9810 cells in the siR-VCAM1 group, while VCAM1 overexpression reversed these effects ( Figure 4D–F). Furthermore, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of VCAM1 indicated that VCAM1 is closely associated with the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway ( Supplementary Figure S1). As demonstrated in Figure 4G, the expressions of EMT- and PI3K/AKT pathway-related proteins were significantly inhibited by siR-VCAM1 in HCCC9810 cells, and overexpression of VCAM1 produced the opposite result. These results revealed that VCAM1 effectively promoted the malignant phenotype of HCCC9810 cells.

The results obtained from the qRT-PCR assay confirmed that the expression of VCAM1 was inhibited by the miR-512-5p mimic and was increased by the overexpression of cMALAT1. More importantly, the miR-512-5p mimics attenuated the effects caused by the overexpression of cMALAT1 ( Figure 5A). Similarly, the same results were obtained by western blot analysis ( Figure 5B). The findings from the dual-luciferase reporter assays subsequently revealed that miR-512-5p significantly affected the luciferase activity of VCAM1-Wt but had no effect on VCAM1-Mut ( Figure 5C), employing a method delineated in the Supplementary Figure S2. More importantly, luciferase assay further revealed that the miR-512-5p mimics inhibited the expression of VCAM1-Wt, whereas cMALAT1 reversed the inhibitory effect of the miR-512-5p mimics ( Figure 5D). In summary, the results of this study implied that cMALAT1 might promote the malignant phenotype of ICC cells via the upregulation of VCAM1 expression.

To validate this conclusion, VCAM1 was knocked down by siRNAs in HCCC9810 cells overexpressing cMALAT1. Not surprisingly, subsequent cellular function experiments confirmed that there was a positive relationship between cMALAT1 and VCAM1 ( Figure 6A–C). Furthermore, the reduction in E-cadherin and the upregulation of N-cadherin, MMP-2, MMP-9, p-PI3K, p-AKT, and vimentin, which were caused by cMALAT1, were reversed after the knockdown of VCAM1 ( Figure 6D). These findings revealed that cMALAT1 promoted the malignant progression of ICC through the miR-512-5p/VCAM1 axis.

Discussion

The circRNA/miRNA/mRNA network is a classical mode by which circRNAs regulate cancer progression [35]. MiR-195 was confirmed to interact with circPVT1, and circPVT1 silencing inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of thyroid cancer cells and suppresses the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [36]. The upregulation of circ_100338 increases the expression of ZEB1 mRNA, a direct target of miR-141-3p, which increases HCC cell proliferation in vitro and in vivo [37]. Circ_0002395 is a competing endogenous RNA that sponges miR-548c-3p, thereby promoting PDK1 expression and aerobic glycolysis, and ultimately resulting in the proliferation of pancreatic adenocarcinoma cells [38]. CircATP13A1 promotes cell proliferation and metastasis and inhibits cell apoptosis in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) via the miR-186/miR-326/HMGA2 axis, acting as an oncogene to promote PDAC development [39]. Furthermore, the expressions of circRNAs vary in different tumor cells, suggesting their potential as diagnostic and prognostic markers [40]. In our previous work, we confirmed that activated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) promote the proliferation and metastasis of HCC cells and facilitate the EMT process by targeting the RORα/Wnt/β-catenin axis via miR-1246 [19]. In addition, the expression of circMALAT1 (cMALAT1) in ICC cells cocultured with HSCs was significantly upregulated. In this study, cMALAT1 overexpression promoted the capacity of ICC cells to proliferate, migrate, invade, and colony formation, whereas cMALAT1 silencing had the opposite effect on those phenotypes, further confirming that cMALAT1 is deeply involved in the malignant progression of ICC. Moreover, this conclusion was confirmed in vivo.

One crucial function of circRNAs is to act as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) capable of interacting with target miRNAs; thus, we speculated that cMALAT1 may act as a miRNA sponge and then upregulate downstream genes by binding to certain miRNAs, thus regulating the malignant progression of ICC. This study revealed that cMALAT1 is able to bind to miR-512-5p, which was subsequently confirmed via RNA pull-down experiments. More importantly, the application of miR-512-5p significantly reversed the cMALAT1 overexpression-induced malignant phenotypes of ICC cells. Previous studies have reported that circRANGAP1 facilitates tumorigenesis and development by sponging miR-512-5p to upregulate SOD2 expression in non-small cell lung cancer [41]. It has also been shown that miR-512-5p can influence resistance to osimertinib in lung adenocarcinoma cells [42] and resistance to paclitaxel in non-small cell lung cancer [43]. In addition, Li et al. [44] demonstrated that cisplatin resistance is regulated by the circ-LIMK1/miR-512-5p/HMGA1 axis in lung adenocarcinoma. These findings reveal that miR-512-5p plays an anticancer role in cancer progression, which is consistent with our results.

Although cMALAT1 has been preliminarily shown to target miR-512-5p, thereby regulating the progression of ICC, the downstream genes involved have not yet been identified. The potential binding site of miR-512-5p on the 3′UTR (3772–3778) of VCAM1 has been predicted via the TargetScan Human database. Surprisingly, in our study, VCAM1 knockdown significantly suppressed the cell proliferation, colony formation, invasion, and migration abilities of HCCC9810 cells, whereas VCAM1 overexpression had the opposite effect. These results indicate that VCAM1 might be the downstream transcript of miR-512-5p. For further validation, rescue experiments confirmed that miR-512-5p could bind to VCAM1, and this finding was confirmed by subsequent luciferase reporter experiments. More importantly, knockdown of VCAM1 abrogated the cMALAT1-promoted malignant phenotypes of ICC cells, further indicating that VCAM1 is involved in cMALAT1-mediated ICC progression.

As a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily, VCAM1 is a potent adhesion molecule triggered by tumor necrosis factor and interleukin-1. VCAM1 contributes to the inflammatory response by mediating leukocyte‒endothelial cell adhesion and signal transduction, as exemplified by arteritis, atherosclerosis, and rheumatoid arthritis [45]. Mounting research has suggested that VCAM1 can bind to VLA4, the ligand of VCAM1, which is helpful for the accumulation of VCAM1, which subsequently phosphorylates the downstream Ezrin. Phosphorylated Ezrin was found to promote the proliferation and metastasis of breast cancer cells through the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by binding to the AKT promoter [ 46– 48]. Similarly, binding between VCAM1 and VLA4 facilitates the peritoneal metastasis of ovarian cancer, which is a significant risk factor leading to poor prognosis [49]. Interestingly, we also found that VCAM1 is involved in the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Given that the competitive binding of cMALAT1 to miR-512-5p results in the upregulation of VCAM1 expression, we preliminarily speculated that the accumulation of VCAM1 may trigger Ezrin phosphorylation, and ultimately activates the PI3K/AKT signaling. Moreover, VLA4 may also be involved, and this issue should be further explored in the future.

In summary, this work revealed a novel mechanism by which the interaction of the cMALAT1/miR-512-5p/VCAM1 network regulates the progression of ICC, as shown in Figure 7. Mechanistically, cMALAT1 competitively binds to VCAM1 mRNA with miR-512-5p, which leads to the upregulation of VCAM1 expression and the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway to promote the malignant progression of ICC, providing novel insight into the antitumor therapy of ICC.
