Giant kelp (Macrocystis pyrifera) gametophytes grow endophytically in red macroalgae: Implications for kelp restoration
Authors: Tamzin Gannon, Wouter Visch, Christopher P. Burridge, Adam Smolenski, Sharee McCammon, Ellen Gunn, Jeffrey T. Wright, Catriona L. Hurd, Scott Bennett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiant kelp (Macrocystis pyrifera) forests have declined by 95% in Australia since the 1970s due to climate change. Despite expanding restoration efforts, long‐term recovery is still lacking. Factors influencing recruitment by the microscopic gametophyte phase may represent a key mechanism underpinning the successful recovery and restoration of M. pyrifera. In the northern hemisphere, kelp gametophytes grow epiphytically and endophytically in red macroalgae and may represent an important “propagule bank” for kelp gametophyte recruitment. Our goal was to determine if M. pyrifera recruitment might depend on endo‐ or epiphytic gametophyte growth with understory red macroalgae on reefs in Tasmania, Australia. We first examined the composition and biomass of understory macroalgae living under kelp forests in Tasmania. We then tested if M. pyrifera gametophytes were located epiphytically or endophytically within specific red macroalgal hosts of 13 common species. Digital polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with M. pyrifera primers found M. pyrifera gametophytes in eight species of red macroalgae (Ballia callitricha, Gloiocladia halymenioides, Halopeltis australis, Phacelocarpus pepperocarpos, Plocamium angustum, Plocamium dilatatumi, Ptilonia australasica, and Ptilonia subulifera), with Ballia callitricha being the most common host (66.7% positive detections). By sterilizing the surface of host tissue, the detection of M. pyrifera DNA was consistent with endophytic occurrence of kelp gametophytes within the red macroalgal hosts but did not exclude the possibility that they also live epiphytically. These results suggest that red macroalgae may provide an important propagule bank for M. pyrifera gametophytes, and maintaining healthy understory assemblages may facilitate recovery and restoration efforts of M. pyrifera.

INTRODUCTION

Marine macroalgae are ecologically important in temperate marine systems, creating habitat, providing food to higher trophic levels, and supporting diverse benthic and pelagic communities (Bennett et al., 2016; Pessarrodona et al., 2022; Steneck et al., 2002). However, as the climate changes, macroalgae are being impacted by a suite of pressures including ocean warming and marine heatwaves, changes in nutrient regimes, and herbivore overgrazing (Smale, 2020; Wernberg et al., 2016). Among macroalgae, temperate kelp forests (order Laminariales) are critical foundation species that cover approximately one third of the world's coastlines (Jayathilake & Costello, 2021) and have undergone extensive declines in abundance and distribution in recent decades (Krumhansl et al., 2016; Wernberg et al., 2019).

In response to their decline, kelp forest recovery and restoration efforts are growing globally (Eger et al., 2023; Layton et al., 2020). Through these efforts, significant advances in active restoration have been made, with techniques ranging from transplantation of reproductive adults (Bennett, Wernberg, Joy, et al., 2015; Vergés et al., 2020) to the deployment of substrates such as gravel (Fredriksen et al., 2020) and twine (Ling et al., 2025), seeded with microscopic gametophytes that enable practical and scalable restoration opportunities under a variety of conditions. Despite progress in out‐planting methods, less attention has been given to the conditions necessary for the persistence of these restoration efforts. The success of restoration efforts, and indeed natural kelp recovery, is contingent on reestablishing self‐sustaining populations that are capable of successful reproduction and recruitment, to (1) infill the forest, (2) expand beyond replanted areas, and (3) persist beyond the lifespan of a single cohort. Achieving these outcomes depends on the availability of suitable substrates for kelp gametophytes to settle on following spore release. Ideally, these substrates support the establishment of a propagule bank, analogous to a terrestrial seed bank, where gametophytes can accumulate over time until environmental conditions become favorable for fertilization and growth. Currently, critical knowledge gaps remain about the identity of host substrates and the longevity of viable propagule banks in the wild (Veenhof et al., 2022).

Kelp gametophytes have been recorded living on and within a variety of substrata, including rocks, shells, bryzoans, and other macroalgae (Akita et al., 2020; Bringloe et al., 2018; Funano, 1969; Garbary et al., 1999a). Of particular interest, kelp gametophytes have been recorded living endophytically within cell walls of red macroalgae (Rhodophyta), providing a potential host for long‐term persistence (Bringloe et al., 2018; Garbary et al., 1999a, 1999b; Sasaki et al., 2003). Previous studies from Washington State, United States, observed kelp gametophytes living within 17 different red algal species, with Pleonosporium vancouverianum and Scagelia pylaisaei as consistent hosts across sites (Garbary et al., 1999b). Moreover, Nereocystis luetkeana gametophytes have also been observed growing both endo‐ and epiphytically with the red macroalgae Griffithsia pacifica and Antithamnion defectum, with epiphytic gametophytes growing faster and larger than endophytic gametophytes (Hubbard et al., 2004). To date, exploration of analogous gametophyte hosts has not been undertaken in southern hemisphere kelp forests.

Advances in molecular methods since the 1990s have led to new techniques that can be employed to detect cryptic kelp gametophytes, including those growing endophytically within red macroalgae in the field, and may overcome the limitations of using laboratory cultures and microscopy (Garbary et al., 1999a, 1999b; Hubbard et al., 2004). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is a technique used to amplify DNA through a multitude of repeated temperature cycles, directed by oligonucleotide primers that flank the region to be amplified, which can also be species‐specific (Gabriyan & Avashia, 2013). Although PCR product detection can be through gel electrophoresis, quantitative PCR (qPCR) provides much greater detection sensitivity and quantification accuracy of the amplified target DNA (Gabriyan & Avashia, 2013). This, in turn, has enabled the application of PCR methods to trace‐level sources of DNA in the environment, termed environmental DNA (eDNA; e.g., Ward et al., 2025). A further enhancement is provided by digital PCR (dPCR), in which the sample is split between thousands of partitions that undergo individual PCR reactions, with the number of positive amplification partitions over the total used to quantify the target's original concentration (Quan et al., 2018). Digital PCR is used when the target DNA is expected to be especially rare or subtle, and it has a higher tolerance to PCR inhibitors, which is useful when analyzing DNA from crude environmental sources (Kuypers & Jerome, 2017; Quan et al., 2018).

Macrocystis pyrifera (order Laminariales) is a large surface‐canopy‐forming kelp, with a widespread distribution in the temperate southern hemisphere and northeast Pacific (Steneck & Johnson, 2013). This species has undergone significant population declines in Australia over recent decades (Butler et al., 2020; Forbes et al., 2025; Johnson et al., 2011) resulting in its listing under the Australian Government's Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 as a "threatened ecological community"1999 in southeastern Australia (Evans et al., 2017; Layton et al., 2020). Reefs once dominated by M. pyrifera have transitioned to dominance by smaller stipitate kelp species such as Ecklonia radiata and the fucoid Phyllospora comosa. Interestingly, giant kelp forests in Tasmania are characterized by high cover and diversity of understory red algal assemblages (Hurd et al., 2023), whereas E. radiata forests are characterized by lower cover of foliose species and higher cover of encrusting species due to the abrasion from their stipitate morphology (Ruiz‐Ruiz et al., 2025). Given the importance of foliose red macroalgae as propagule banks in the northern hemisphere, this transition in canopy and understory assemblages raises questions about the propagule bank potential for giant kelp forests in Tasmania, particularly in light of expanding restoration efforts.

Here, we assessed the presence of Macrocystis pyrifera gametophytes, living on (epiphytic) and within (endophytic) red macroalgae, to understand the potential for these understory habitats to act as propagule banks for kelp forests. We used dPCR to test for M. pyrifera gametophytes living on red macroalgae at four sites in Tasmania, Australia, including two remnant M. pyrifera forests and two sites where M. pyrifera occurred historically but which are now dominated by Ecklonia radiata. We compared the richness and biomass of red algal assemblages at sites with and without M. pyrifera forests to test whether red macroalgae composition varies with the presence/absence of M. pyrifera relative to E. radiata forests. The results from this study will help identify species with potential to harbor kelp gametophyte banks in the wild that could facilitate ongoing recovery and restoration of kelp forests.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study sites and macroalgal collection

Sampling took place during April–May 2024 at four sites in Tasmania, Australia (Figure 1). Sites included two remnant natural Macrocystis pyrifera forests—Actaeon Island (43.528° S, 146.994° E) and Sisters Bay (43.418° S, 147.020° E)—with moderate densities (79 ± 14 stipes · 100 m−2, mean ± SE, and 509 ± 40.4 stipes · 100 m−2, mean ± SE, respectively) of sporophytes. There were also two Ecklonia radiata‐dominated sites with no M. pyrifera at the time of sampling—Fortescue Bay (43.128° S, 147.961° E) and Bicheno (41.869° S, 148.299° E). In Fortescue Bay, M. pyrifera was historically dominant and still present as recently as 2017, whereas in Bicheno, it was historically present up until the 1990s (Butler et al., 2020; Sanderson, 1990). All sites have rocky reefs at between 8 and 12 m depths, with mixed and diverse understory macroalgal assemblages of Rhodophyta, Chlorophyta (e.g., Caulerpa spp.), and Heterokontophyta (e.g., Zonaria spp.). Both E. radiata‐dominated sites have been subject to M. pyrifera restoration efforts, but there were no established or reproductive sporophytes present or within 20‐km radius at the time of sampling.

Map of Tasmania, Australia showing the four sites used in this study. Bicheno and Fortescue are dominated by Ecklonia radiata (black), whereas Sisters Bay and Actaeon Island are dominated by Macrocystis pyrifera (gray).

At each of the four sites, six 0.25‐m2 quadrats were placed on the seafloor by divers, and all foliose and fleshy understory macroalgae inside each quadrat were collected and put into a calico bag. Canopy forming, encrusting, and turfing macroalgae were not collected. At the Ecklonia radiata‐dominated sites, quadrats were placed haphazardly along a transect line, within E. radiata canopies. At the Macrocystis pyrifera dominated sites, quadrats were placed at the base of six different M. pyrifera sporophytes. The calico bags containing the macroalgae were transported in a cool box to the laboratory and kept in a cool room at 12°C before they were processed.

Relative abundance of understory red and brown macroalgae in Macrocystis pyrifera versus Ecklonia radiata forests

To characterize the understory macroalgal composition at each site, the fresh weight (g) of each species was recorded from each of the six quadrats collected from each of the four sites. Macroalgae from each quadrat were sorted and grouped by species, shaken in seawater to remove loose invertebrates and debris, pat‐dried with paper towel, and weighed to the nearest gram.

Detection of Macrocystis pyrifera gametophytes epiphytic and endophytic red macroalgae

For each of the red macroalgal species collected from the four sites, two individuals were taken and two ~20‐cm2 pieces were cut from each, resulting in four subsamples for each species. One subsample from each individual (two subsamples per species) underwent a cleaned treatment, and the other underwent an uncleaned treatment inside a sterile laminar flow hood to avoid DNA contamination from other sources. No DNA extraction was performed for Polyopes constrictus, as only a single sample was collected.

The cleaned treatment aimed to remove epiphytic material and involved wiping the sample surface thoroughly with paper towel, followed by scraping with a scalpel, then dipping sequentially into three beakers of UV‐treated (20‐min) artificial seawater with 5 mL · L−1 iodine (Betadine©) solution. Although scraping with a scalpel was attempted on all samples, fragile species such as Ballia callitricha and Plocamium leptophyllum were vulnerable to breakage, resulting in lighter scraping than robust species such as Sonderophycus coriaceus. Samples were then rinsed in a beaker with artificial seawater only and pat‐dried with paper towel. For the “uncleaned” treatment, macroalgae were rinsed sequentially in three beakers of artificial seawater, without thorough wiping, scraping, or betadine‐solution washes. Although differences in scraping vigor among species has the potential to influence the persistence and detection of epiphytic Macrocystis pyrifera DNA on the host tissue, the comparison of cleaned and uncleaned treatments enabled intraspecific differences in detection rates to be compared and control for this possibility.

All samples were put into a sterile zip lock bag with 1.5 teaspoons of silica beads to dry, after which tissue samples were removed from the silica beads, freeze‐dried, and stored at −20°C. Species that test positive for Macrocystis pyrifera DNA in the cleaned treatment suggest the presence of endophytic gametophytes, whereas a positive test in uncleaned samples suggests the presence of endo‐ and/or epiphytic M. pyrifera DNA.

DNA extraction, polymerase chain reaction, and sequencing

DNA extraction and PCR were undertaken at the Central Science Laboratory (CSL), University of Tasmania. All equipment was sterilized under UV light before use, and pipetting was done in a fume hood with filtered pipette tips to minimize potential contamination. Approximately 20 mg of the freeze‐dried sample was ground into a fine powder in a 2‐mL screw cap tube with two stainless steel ball bearings, using a TissueLyser grinder. Freezing in liquid nitrogen was used if necessary to aid the process. DNA was extracted from the samples using an ethanol precipitation method (Durrant et al., 2015); however, Epoch spin columns and extra washes were omitted from that protocol. A NANODROP 8000 Spectrophotometer V2.1 was used to confirm the presence of high molecular weight DNA. To confirm that the DNA extracted by this approach could facilitate PCR amplification (positive control), traditional PCR was conducted for a subset of samples using primer pairs T16N/T24U and P23SrV‐F1/P23SnewR (targeting red algae). This PCR was visualized on an agarose gel.

Digital PCR (dPCR) of Macrocystis pyrifera used the same primers as Ward et al. (2025), mpy_F3_166/mpy_R3_166. Before the main dPCR run, a dPCR of multiple different dilutions of positive control sporophyte‐derived M. pyrifera DNA was run to identify the most appropriate concentration during dPCR (1/100,000,000). A 26,000‐partition approach and 24‐sample plate was used for the main dPCR. The reaction conditions were optimized to 40 cycles with an annealing at 57°C, maximizing the contrast between positive and negative signals. Each 24‐sample plate included a positive control (diluted M. pyrifera sporophyte DNA) and a negative control comprising UV sterilized and filtered (0.22 μm) seawater. Digital PCR results were only assessed if the positive controls resulted in amplified DNA partitions and the negative controls did not.

Results from the dPCR were additionally assessed through DNA sequencing to ensure amplification signals represented Macrocystis pyrifera. Eight DNA samples were sequenced and compared to the M. pyrifera target sequence: a positive control based on M. pyrifera sporophyte DNA, six red algal DNA samples that dPCR determined contained M. pyrifera DNA, and one red algal sample that the dPCR determined did not contain M. pyrifera DNA. The selected samples were from different sites, different red algal species extracts, and both cleaned and uncleaned treatments (for details see Table S1). Sequencing used the Applied Biosystems (ABI) Big Dye Terminator Version 3.1 chemistry, with separation on an ABI 3500 Genetic Analyzer.

Data analysis

A nested analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test for difference between the total biomass of the understory red and brown macroalgae between dominant canopy type (fixed factor, two levels), with sites nested within canopy type (four sites in total, two per canopy type). Assumptions of independence, normality, and homogeneity of variances were met. Differences in biomass composition of understory species between dominant habitat types were assessed using non‐metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and permutational multivariate ANOVA (PERMANOVA) in R (vegan package). Fresh weights (g) were summed by species within each replicate quadrat (0.25 m2) and square‐root–transformed prior to calculating Bray–Curtis dissimilarities. Non‐metric multidimensional scaling was performed using metaMDS with multiple random starts, and ordination quality was assessed using the final stress value. Species contributing the most to multivariate patterns were identified using envfit with 999 permutations. Differences in community composition between dominant habitat types were tested using PERMANOVA (adonis2, 999 permutations, Bray–Curtis dissimilarities), and homogeneity of dispersion was verified using betadisper to ensure homogeneity of variances. To assess differences in positive Macrocystis pyrifera DNA detection rates within understory species, dPCR data were transformed to be binomial, with “1” being a positive detection of M. pyrifera DNA and “0” being a negative/no detection. The threshold for M. pyrifera presence was determined by reference to the number of positive partitions in positive and negative controls; the concentration of the lowest positive control (4 copies · μL−1) selected as a conservative threshold. A chi‐squared test was used, with an alpha value of 0.05, to determine if there was a difference in M. pyrifera detections with habitat (dominant canopy: M. pyrifera and Ecklonia radiata), as well as if there was a difference between unclean (endo‐ or epiphytic) and clean (endophytic) only treatments. All data were analyzed in R Version 4.1.0.

RESULTS

Relative abundance of understory macroalgae in Macrocystis pyrifera versus Ecklonia radiata forests

A total of 19 species of fleshy and foliose macroalgae were recorded living in the understory at the four sites (Figure 2a), with an average of 5.3 species per 0.25 m2. The number of species per quadrat did not significantly differ between sites or with dominant canopy species (ANOVA, F 1,12 = 0.424, p = 0.917). In contrast, the biomass of macroalgae was significantly higher at sites dominated by Macrocystis pyrifera (303 ± 33 g · 0.25 m−2, mean ± SE) compared to Ecklonia radiata (142 ± 21 g · 0.25 m−2; F 1,23 = 16.98, p < 0.001; Figures 2, 3).

The NMDS ordination of understory species composition showed clear separation between dominant canopy types (Stress = 0.173; Figure 2b). The PERMANOVA revealed a significant effect of the dominant canopy on community composition (pseudo‐F = 8.12, R 2 = 0.425, p = 0.001). Species vector fitting (envfit) identified Halopeltis australis, Plocamium dilatatum, and Sonderophycus coriaceus as the strongest contributors to this separation, with increasing biomass of P. dilatatum and H. australis associated with Macrocystis pyrifera‐dominated sites, and higher biomass of S. coriaceus characterizing Ecklonia radiata‐dominated sites. At M. pyrifera‐dominated sites, P. dilatatum dominated the understory accounting for 33% ± 7% and 34% ± 14% of total biomass at Sisters Bay and Actaeon Island, respectively. Sonderophycus coriaceus dominated the understory biomass at E. radiata‐dominated sites, accounting for 43% ± 11% and 59% ± 10% of total understory biomass at Bicheno and Fortescue Bay, respectively.

Detection of Macrocystis pyrifera gametophytes associated with red macroalgae

At the Macrocystis pyrifera canopy‐dominated sites, 21.1% of red macroalgae samples tested positive for M. pyrifera DNA, compared to 12.5% of samples at the Ecklonia radiata‐dominated sites, but there was no significant difference between sites (χ2 = 0.947, df = 1, p = 0.331). Across all sites, the number of positive M. pyrifera DNA detections differed among red algal species (Figure 4). Ballia callitricha had the highest number of positive M. pyrifera DNA detections (66.7%), followed by Halopeltis australis (50.0%), and Phacelocarpus peperocarpos had the lowest detection rate (6.3%) among the species when M. pyrifera DNA was detected (Table 1). No M. pyrifera DNA was detected in five (Callophyllis lambertii, Delisea pulchra, Plocamium leptophyllum, P. mertensii, and Sonderophycus coriaceus) of the 13 species tested.

Overall, there was no significant difference in Macrocystis pyrifera DNA detections between the cleaned and uncleaned treatments (χ2 = 0.055, df = 1, p = 0.814). Equal numbers of positive detections were observed in clean and uncleaned treatments of three species, Ballia callitricha, Halopeltis australis, and Plocamium dilatatum. In the clean treatments, there were more positive detections of M. pyrifera DNA recorded for Phacelocarpus pepperocarpus, Plocamium angustum, and Ptilonia australasica; however, more positive M. pyrifera DNA detections from uncleaned samples were found in Gloiocladia halymenioides and Ptilonia subulifera (Table 1). Positive and negative detections of M. pyrifera DNA were verified through additional DNA sequences from a positive control and six positive samples detected by the dPCR. Positive dPCR detections matched the target M. pyrifera DNA sequence with identities exceeding 95%. Similarly, the DNA sequence of the sample with a negative dPCR detection confirmed no resemblance to M. pyrifera (Table S2).

DISCUSSION

The fate of kelp gametophytes following spore release remains an enigma to kelp forest ecology in many parts of the world despite its critical role toward the maintenance, recovery, and restoration of these ecosystems. Here we detected Macrocystis pyrifera DNA within eight species of red macroalgae in Tasmania, Australia, suggesting that these species may host kelp gametophytes on the reef and could represent a propagule bank for giant kelp forests in the region. Similar detection rates between cleaned and uncleaned treatments suggest that gametophytes are living endophytically within their red algal hosts. Detection rates differed markedly between red algal species, suggesting that although common, this function is not ubiquitous among understory species. Ballia callitricha was the standout host for M. pyrifera DNA, indicating that this species may play an important role facilitating the recruitment and recovery of giant kelp in the region.

Macrocystis pyrifera DNA in red macroalgae

The prevalence of Macrocystis pyrifera DNA in the branching, filamentous species, Ballia callitricha is consistent with observations from the northern hemisphere, in which (Garbary et al., 1999a) observed that the top hosts for kelp gametophytes were from the morphologically similar branching filamentous species, Pleonosporium vancouverianum and Scagelia pylaisaei. In contrast, several red algal species in the current study, including Sonderophycus coriaceus and Delisea pulchra did not record M. pyrifera gametophytes despite being relatively abundant on the reef. Sonderophycus coriaceus has a thick, leathery, cartilaginous thallus that radiates circularly from the stipe, and it hosts a multitude of sessile invertebrate epiphytes on its shaded underside, potentially limiting M. pyrifera gametophyte recruitment. In contrast, D. pulchra is a finely branched species and is morphologically similar to several species in which M. pyrifera gametophytes were detected (e.g., Phacelocarpus pepperocarpus). However, D. pulchra is well known for its chemical defenses, including a range of halogenated compounds that defend it against grazing, bacterial colonization, and the settlement of macroalgal propagules on its surface (Dworjanyn et al., 2006; McClintock & Baker, 2001). The absence of detections in these two species suggest that morphology and chemical defenses may be important traits for the recruitment of M. pyrifera gametophytes to red algal hosts.

Interestingly, however, of the four Plocamium species tested, two species had positive detections, and two did not. These four species differ in their morphology, including branch width (~1 to ~4 mm) and thallus thickness. There was no pattern in branch width or thickness between the species that displayed positive detections and those that did not. This incongruence may reflect natural variation in spore settlement or gametophyte survival among individual hosts, as species with positive detections also had higher replication than the species that did not. Alternatively, it might suggest that taxonomic and morphological traits are poor predictors of gametophyte host species.

Detection of epiphytic and endophytic Macrocystis pyrifera gametophytes within red macroalgae

For the red macroalgae studied, Macrocystis pyrifera gametophyte detections were similar for untreated and cleaned treatments. Although a DNA assay cannot distinguish between living gametophytes and sporophytic or extracellular DNA, all samples were subjected to sequential rinsing in multiple beakers of artificial seawater, which would have strongly diluted any non‐associated M. pyrifera DNA. It is therefore likely that detections in cleaned treatments reflect gametophytes that are living endophytically within the host species, whereas uncleaned treatment may represent epiphytic and/or endophytic gametes. The similarity in detection rates between cleaned and uncleaned treatments therefore suggests epiphytic gametophytes did not make a significant contribution to detection rates. Nevertheless, the presence of surface‐associated gametophytes cannot be entirely excluded. Particularly for fragile species, which were only subject to light scraping, the Betadine treatment and rinsing alone may have been insufficient to eliminate all surface gametophytes or extracellular Macrocystis DNA from the sample (Veenhof et al., 2025). Robust species such as Halopeltis australis, however, that were thoroughly scraped and displayed the same frequency of positive DNA detections on cleaned and uncleaned tissue, support the notion that endophytic gametophytes are present within the host tissue.

Our results are consistent with both Garbary et al. (1999a) and Sasaki et al. (2003) who observed kelp gametophytes only living endophytically in red macroalgae hosts. Endophytic growth has a multitude of potential benefits for gametophytes, including protection from herbivores such as gastropods and amphipods that primarily graze the surface of red algae (Aumack et al., 2010; D'Antonio, 1985). Further, resources from the host macroalga, like sugars from photosynthesis, may be available to the endophyte (Garbary et al., 1999a). Other benefits include protection from sediment burial, buffering from the external environment, and overgrowth by other benthic algae (Hubbard et al., 2004).

Kelp propagule bank potential of understory macroalgae

The interest in the potential for kelp gametophytes to live endophytically within red algae is because of their potential to remain vegetative until environmental conditions become favorable for reproduction, thereby forming a propagule bank (Hubbard et al., 2004). The long‐term persistence of viable gametophytes in understory habitats would facilitate kelp forest recovery following disturbance by reducing the reliance of recovery on reproductive seasonality. Additionally, this mechanism may allow gametophyte density to accumulate on the benthos over time, sourced from a diversity of parental (i.e., genotypic) origins. Although the current study could not determine the longevity or viability of kelp gametophytes living within red algal species, it identified the host species involved, paving the way for future studies to further interrogate this function.

A key feature of the understory environment and red macroalgae that makes them suitable hosts for propagule banks is the low‐light environment under kelp canopies. Under low‐light conditions in controlled laboratory environments, kelp gametophytes can remain in a vegetative state for years—enabling long‐term preservation and the use of gametophyte cultures (i.e., propagule banks; Visch et al., 2025). Upon exposure to blue light, gametophytes undergo gametogenesis and fertilization and develop sporophytes. In the wild, kelp forests create analogous low‐light conditions, blocking out >90% of irradiance compared to canopy‐free conditions (Bennett, Wernberg, Bettignies, et al., 2015). Red macroalgae contain pigments that harvest light in the green region of the spectrum, allowing them to inhabit low‐light environments (Gantt, 1980; Hurd et al., 2014; Voerman et al., 2022), making them suitable potential hosts for propagule banks. In this way, kelps and red algal hosts may share a mutualistic relationship, in which the red host provides protection for kelp gametophytes, and the kelp creates suitable habitat for the host by shading and reducing water motion.

A surprising result from this study was the presence of Macrocystis pyrifera gametophytes in Bicheno, where giant kelp forests have not been locally present for over 3 decades (Hurd et al., 2023). Moreover, detection was not an isolated case, with M. pyrifera gametophytes recorded in five of the eight red algal species tested. There are several possible explanations for the putative presence of M. pyrifera gametophytes at Bicheno despite the absence of M. pyrifera. The most feasible explanation is that spores are recruited from passing, floating, detached M. pyrifera sporophytes, which were observed at the study location at the time of sampling. Detached M. pyrifera sporophytes can float >450 km from their source, and in addition to sporophylls, blades can bare mature reproductive sori (Layton et al., 2022; Leal et al., 2014). Spores released from sori can disperse up to 1 km (Gaylord et al., 2006). Within host species in which detections were generally high, such as Ballia callitricha, rates of positive detection were indeed lower in Bicheno (25%) than in the two giant kelp dominated sites (75%–100%).

The presence of kelp gametophytes and absence of sporophytes in Bicheno raise questions about the viability of these gametophytes to fertilize and recruit locally. In the absence of giant kelp, macroalga canopies are dominated by dense cover of stipitate species, namely Ecklonia radiata, and the fucoid Phyllospora comosa. Giant and stipitate kelp forests differ markedly in their physical abrasion of the understory environment, which in turn influences the cover and composition of understory assemblages. In the current study, although site‐level replication of the different canopy types was low (n = 2), red algal biomass was higher in Macrocystis pyrifera understory communities than stipitate E. radiata assemblages. Furthermore, the M. pyrifera understory was dominated by fine branching filamentous and foliose species that generally displayed higher kelp detection rates than the thick leathery species (e.g., Sonderophycus coriaceus), which dominated E. radiata sites. As such, the efficacy of propagule banks is likely to differ substantially between reefs, based on the composition of local understory assemblages. Moreover, giant kelp recruitment under stipitate canopies is naturally very low due to competition and abrasion from the adult canopy (Ling et al., 2025). As such, in the absence of a significant disturbance to the existing canopy, giant kelp recruitment is unlikely. It is also possible that local conditions in Bicheno are viable for gametophyte survival but not for sporophyte development, due to higher thermal tolerance (Le et al., 2024) and lower metabolic costs under low‐nutrient conditions (Garbary et al., 1999a).

The current study raises several interesting questions for further research about the viability and longevity of kelp gametophytes living within red macroalgae. Specifically, in terms of viability—what is the mechanism by which endophytic kelp gametophytes successfully reproduce to form juvenile sporophytes capable of attaching to the rock substratum? Do juvenile sporophytes detach from their host and subsequently settle onto rock, or do they simply overgrow their host and attach to the reef in spite of the red algal host—as was observed with artificial substrates used in kelp restoration (Ling et al., 2025). With respect to longevity—how is the propagule bank maintained given the ephemeral nature of red macroalgae or herbivory pressure? If red algal hosts are similarly or more sensitive to disturbance (e.g., marine heatwaves) than kelps, then the benefit of any propagule bank may be compromised. These outstanding questions could be addressed through targeted experimental approaches, such as laboratory cultivation of red algae with fertile kelp sporophytes, controlled inoculation of red algal tissue with kelp zoospores, and subsequent monitoring of gametophyte persistence and sporophyte development under varying light regimes (mimicking shading/opening of the surrounding canopy). Complemented with field‐based studies, involving collection of red algal hosts to assess the occurrence of juvenile kelp sporophytes associated with host tissues.

CONCLUSIONS

This study showed that red macroalgae may form propagule banks for Macrocystis pyrifera gametophytes in Tasmania, Australia, and that these microscopic gametophytes may have preferred species of red macroalgae as hosts. It also demonstrated that there was a larger abundance of red macroalgae at sites with M. pyrifera populations. Greater amounts of red macroalgae (especially those preferred host species) may increase the potential for gametophyte propagule banks, and with more propagule banks, there will be a higher abundance of gametophytes in stasis and ready to grow when the opportunity presents. Therefore, red algae may be an important component of self‐sustaining M. pyrifera populations and represents an important consideration for active restoration efforts.
