Atrial Leadless Pacemaker to Replace Atrial Pacing From a Failed Conventional Transvenous Atrial Lead
Authors: Ghazaleh Goldar, Edward M Powers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeadless atrial pacing provides a novel solution for patients with atrial lead failure where extraction is not feasible. The integration of leadless systems with existing cardiac devices offers a promising approach for managing patients in whom lead extraction is considered too risky.Case SummaryA 73-year-old man with heart block, after having undergone cardiac resynchronization therapy pacemaker implantation 10 years ago, presented with atrial lead failure. Owing to recent double-lung transplantation and high surgical risk, lead extraction was avoided. A leadless atrial pacemaker was successfully implanted, restoring atrioventricular synchrony by using the atrial sensing from the existing right atrial lead.DiscussionThis case demonstrates the integration of conventional right atrial lead sensing with leadless pacing technology, offering a safe and effective alternative when lead extraction is too risky.Take-Home MessageLeadless atrial pacing integrated with existing cardiac device leads offers a safe and effective alternative for restoring atrioventricular synchrony in high-risk patients in whom lead extraction or revision is not feasible.Key words: AV synchrony, leadless pacemaker, pacemaker malfunction, transvenous pacemaker

History of Presentation

The patient is a 73-year-old man who presented for further evaluation and management of a malfunctioning atrial lead in his cardiac resynchronization therapy pacemaker (CRT-P) system. He has a history of complete heart block, for which he underwent emergent CRT-P implantation (Boston Scientific) in 2015. Device interrogations over the years revealed paroxysmal atrial arrhythmias without attempts at rhythm control. His underlying rhythm remained complete heart block, with no intrinsic R waves during testing. Additionally, interrogation noted a rising atrial threshold of 1.6 V at 1.0 ms, though consistent capture was still observed at that level.

Medical History

The patient has a history of paroxysmal atrial arrhythmias, complete heart block, hypertension, and interstitial lung disease status post double-lung transplantation.

Investigation

In 2024, the patient underwent lung transplantation owing to severe interstitial lung disease. Since the transplantation, he experienced an increased atrial arrhythmia burden, reaching 86%. Device interrogation revealed adequate atrial sensing with marked sinus bradycardia. Atrial threshold testing was equivocal owing to lack of intrinsic atrial activity during pacing to confirm noncapture. Additionally, intermittent prolonged retrograde conduction, considered a trigger of atrial arrhythmias, was noted in a review of episode logs (Figure 1).

Management

Management of these issues was deferred until 12 months post-transplant, when an electrophysiology study was performed. Retrograde conduction with a ventriculoatrial time of 420 ms was noted, and atrial noncapture at maximum output were both confirmed (right atrial lead: Boston Scientific Ingevity, model 100536). Atrial sensing was confirmed to be 0.8 to 1.2 mV. An atrioventricular (AV) node ablation was performed that abolished the residual retrograde conduction. His underlying sinus rate was noted to have improved to 45 to 50 beats/min during the 8 months between initial evaluation and electrophysiology study (Figure 2). The patient's lower rate limit was decreased to 50 beats/min, and his device was programmed DDDR at 50 to 130 beats/min to promote AV synchrony while allowing for ventricular rate response given his significant sinus node dysfunction. Atrial output was set to 0.1 V to minimize battery drain.

Underlying Rhythm After Electrophysiology Study With DDDR Pacing and LRL of 50 beats/min

Electrophysiology study showed atrial pacing without capture and biventricular pacing at 50 beats/min. VA conduction has been abolished, and some spontaneous atrial activity is now present, which the device is able to sense and track. LRL = lower rate limit; VA = ventriculoatrial.

Given the lead capture failure and the subsequent loss of synchrony, a discussion was held regarding options for lead abandonment versus extraction and reimplantation. Because of his immunocompromised status and history of bacteremia, lead abandonment was not recommended. The patient elected to undergo a cardiac surgery consultation to assess his candidacy for surgical backup in the event of lead extraction.

A multidisciplinary discussion was held between the electrophysiology, cardiac surgery, and transplant teams regarding options for lead abandonment. The surgical team recommended abandoning the atrial lead rather than extracting it owing to the patient's prior lung transplant and the potential for catastrophic complications if an extraction were attempted. Alternative strategies were explored, and it was ultimately decided that a leadless atrial pacemaker (Aveir AR, Abbott) would be the best approach to restore AV synchrony. Device interrogation reconfirmed persistent atrial noncapture and appropriate sensing.

The engineering teams at Boston Scientific and Abbott were consulted to confirm there would be no significant negative interaction between the systems. The patient was brought back to the electrophysiology unit, with representatives from both companies present. The Aveir device was placed in standard fashion in the right atrial appendage, with preference for close proximity to the atrial lead. The electrophysiology study was performed, which confirmed atrial capture of the Aveir and sensing on the atrial lead. The Aveir helix was deployed, which temporarily increased the threshold to 5.0 V at 1.0 ms. During this window of time, sensing of the pacing spike was performed to allow for programming in the future should tissue sensing be lost. The atrial lead was reliably sensing noncapture pacing spikes at 1.5 V at 0.4 ms. The device was released without complication (Figure 3). The devices were programmed AAIR at 60 to 130 beats/min and DDD at 50 to 130 beats/min.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At his 6-month follow-up check, the patient's atrial arrhythmia burden had fallen to zero, and his CRT-P showed 100% atrial sensing (Figures 4 and 5).

Discussion

This case highlights the evolving role of leadless pacemakers in complex electrophysiological management, particularly in high-risk patients in whom traditional lead extraction poses significant risks. Current guidelines from the Heart Rhythm Society and European Society of Cardiology acknowledge the increasing utility of leadless pacing systems, especially in patients with contraindications to transvenous leads.1,2 However, widespread use of leadless atrial pacing is still emerging, with limited long-term data. Recently, several reports in the medical literature have highlighted the use of leadless pacemakers as a bailout strategy. For instance, Zhao et al3 described a case involving a stable patient with a perforated atrial lead tip protruding from the pericardium near the inferior wall of the main right pulmonary artery who declined lead extraction; a leadless pacemaker was successfully implanted as an alternative. Similarly, Coppola et al4 reported a patient with multiple prior device replacements and extractions, complicated by a fibrous membrane between the ventricular lead and the superior vena cava, who underwent successful leadless pacemaker implantation after transvenous system failure. Furthermore, a larger case series of 17 patients demonstrated the feasibility of leadless pacemaker implantation after explantation of infected conventional systems, including patients with recurrent device infections and pacemaker dependency. Both early (<1 week) and delayed (>1 week) leadless implantations were performed, with no subsequent device infections during follow-up, supporting the role of leadless systems as a viable bailout option in the context of device infection.5

The integration of leadless pacing systems with existing cardiac devices presents new opportunities for managing lead failures, particularly in cases where extraction poses significant risks. In our patient, a leadless atrial pacemaker successfully restored AV synchrony by using the existing right atrial lead for sensing while also minimizing infection risk in an immunocompromised, post-transplant setting.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates the successful integration of existing right atrial lead sensing with a leadless atrial pacemaker to restore AV synchrony while mitigating the risks associated with both lead abandonment and extraction in a high-risk, immunocompromised patient. By leveraging leadless technology, we provided an effective solution that minimized infection risk and avoided potential complications from extraction or additional transvenous leads. This approach highlights the evolving role of leadless pacing in complex device management and underscores the importance of individualized strategies for patients with challenging clinical scenarios.
