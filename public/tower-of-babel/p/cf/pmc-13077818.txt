Application of vegetation restoration technology in ecological restoration of urban water pollution
Authors: Lei Shi, Ting Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsTo achieve ecological restoration of urban water pollution, this study uses vegetation filter strips to filter and remove pollution caused by aquiclude in vegetation restoration technology.MethodsThis study focuses on the main pollution factors of urban water pollution, nitrogen and phosphorus, and conducts comparative experiments on different vegetation filter belts to analyze their removal effects on pollution factors.ResultsWith the decrease of runoff intensity, the removal rates of TSS and TP significantly increased by 22.97% and 20.04%, respectively. At different pollution concentrations, the removal effect of the experimental group exceeded that of the control group. The removal rate also improved with the added pollution concentration. The removal efficiency of experimental group 3, which used Adiantum capillus-veneris as the remediation zone, was the highest. The maximum removal rates in TP, NH4+-N, TN, NO3−-N and TSS pollution factors reached 88.34%, 85.35%, 85.04%, 84.92%, and 87.21%, respectively.ConclusionsThe application of vegetation restoration technology in the ecological restoration of urban water pollution has confirmed its important role and practical significance in controlling urban water pollutants, providing theoretical basis and practical reference for the ecological restoration of urban water pollution.

Background

As an essential component of social and economic development as well as human life, water resources have been deteriorating due to the impact of economic and social development and excessive consumption of water resources. As a result, China is facing water scarcity and increasing water pollution [1]. Urban water pollution is mainly affected by non-point source pollution, which comes from various factors, including soil sediment, nutrients, pesticides, and atmospheric particulate matter. These factors enter the water, soil, and atmospheric environment through soil erosion, surface runoff, and farmland drainage [2]. In urbanization, hardened ground makes it difficult for rainwater to penetrate, thereby affecting hydro-logical cycles and natural replenishment, exacerbating the impact of urban water non-point source pollution [3]. In recent years, non-point source pollution of urban water is the main cause of urban water pollution. Therefore, the key to solving urban water pollution problems lies in controlling non-point source pollution. Urban water pollution mainly comes from various aspects such as domestic sewage, industrial wastewater, urban runoff, and atmospheric sedimentation [4]. Morin-Crini N et al. pointed out that the wastewater from urban sewage treatment plants was the main source of newly emerging pollutants in water. Therefore, filtering out emerging pollution factors is an imperative task in current urban ecological restoration [5]. At present, traditional water environment restoration measures include improving feeding management, constructing ecological filters, and wetland restoration. However, these traditional measures have limitations such as slow effectiveness, high economic costs, and short duration of action [6, 7]. Zhao M et al. proposed that balancing land restoration and water resource protection required sustainable ecological restoration strategies, particularly emphasizing the importance of ecological restoration [8]. Begum W et al. described the pollution status of river networks and its impact on the spatial distribution of benthic macro invertebrates, and elucidated the application of macro invertebrate community indices in determining the pollution status of river systems. The results indicated that the macro invertebrate community index was an effective tool for determining river pollution status [9]. Chawla et al. discussed the sources and impacts of pollutants in aquatic ecosystems, as well as restoration techniques to reduce their impact on aquatic ecosystems. They pointed out that biotechnology such as plant remediation, algal mediation, biofilm use, and ecological floating bed construction could be used to increase the number of aquatic organisms and improve the overall ecological health of aquatic ecosystems [10]. Vegetation restoration technology includes methods such as soil restoration, plant introduction, and natural regeneration. Its core idea is to restore vegetation cover to exert the self-purification function of vegetation to filter and remove pollution factors [11]. In vegetation restoration technology, vegetation filter strips can intercept and purify surface runoff. The residence time of surface runoff in it affects the runoff directly discharged into the river, thereby affecting the water quality and quantity of the catchment river [12]. Although the purification mechanism of vegetation filter belts has been widely explored, their purification efficiency is influenced by various factors such as vegetation type, pollution concentration, and hydraulic conditions. The optimal configuration in actual urban runoff scenarios still needs further exploration. At present, there is still a lack of research on the comparative purification performance of different herbaceous plants under actual runoff conditions. Therefore, in this context, this study focuses on the removal of typical pollutants in urban surface runoff, systematically comparing the purification performance of four common herbaceous vegetation filter belts under different concentrations and runoff intensities, and exploring the influence of surface infiltration flow ratio (IFR) on removal efficiency, to provide more operational vegetation screening and design basis for ecological restoration in high-density urban areas.

Materials and methods

Experimental environment settings

Although emerging pollutants such as drug residues and microplastics are becoming increasingly prominent, traditional pollutants are still the main factors leading to eutrophication of water bodies and degradation of ecosystems, and their control is the foundation of water environment governance. Therefore, this study mainly focuses on the removal of typical traditional pollutants such as nitrogen and phosphorus, as well as total suspended solids (TSS) in urban surface runoff. To verify the removal effect of vegetation filter strips on pollution factors, an experimental site is constructed. The experiment uses urban sewage configured in the laboratory for simulation. To simulate the concentration of urban surface runoff sewage, the sewage configuration concentration is divided into three gradients: high, medium, and low. The configured pollution factors mainly include the most common nitrogen and phosphorus in urban water pollution, including total phosphorus (TP), ammonia nitrogen (NH4+-N), total nitrogen (TN), nitrate nitrogen (NO3−-N), and TSS. The urban sewage is composed of substances such as ultrapure water, potassium nitrate, ammonium chloride, and sludge in different concentration ratios [13]. The experimental environment is located at the experimental site of Agricultural University, with a surface soil thickness of 300 mm. To weaken the impact of natural rainfall, a transparent ceiling is constructed for research. The experimental site is in the Jianghan Plain, belonging to a subtropical monsoon climate with distinct four seasons and abundant rainfall [14, 15]. The experimental environment consists of a simulated runoff device and a simulated soil device. The simulated runoff device is composed of a plastic bucket equipped with a peristaltic pump, while the simulated soil device is composed of a glass groove with a specification of 150 cm×30 cm×10 cm. The filter strips composed of different vegetation lay up in the middle of the simulated soil device, with specifications set at 120 cm×30 cm×10 cm. To simulate the seepage and runoff of soil under natural rainfall conditions, a collection channel is set at one end of the soil simulation device, and a drainage outlet is set at the other end for simulation. The slope utilizes adjustable metal frames to place the soil trench on a 2.5° slope. The vegetation filter belt uses common herbaceous plants for greening, such as Festuca, Manilagrass, Adiantum capillus-veneris, and Ophiopogon bodinieri [16, 17]. These vegetation types have strong adaptability, are tolerant to shade and drought, and grow rapidly, and are often used in vegetation restoration projects [18]. These four vegetation are cultivated on the experimental site. Water pollution removal experiments can be conducted when their growth reaches a coverage rate of over 90%.

Experimental methods

The experiment is divided into a control group (CG) and an experimental group (EG) for comparative testing. The latter consists of soil covered with vegetation filter strips, while the CG consists of soil not covered with filter strips. The EG is divided into EG 1, EG 2, EG 3, and EG 4, which correspond to the soil covered with Festuca, Manilagrass, Adiantum capillus-veneris, and Ophiopogon bodinieri, respectively. At the beginning of the experiment, the soil surface is moistened first, and different concentrations of sewage are discharged to the CG and EG at the same water volume and flow rate. To determine the impact of various runoff intensities on the removal efficiency of pollution factors, a valve is used to control the flow rate of sewage. It is set to three different intensities of runoff, high, medium, and low, for comparative experiments.

Determination of pollution factors

The pollution factors TP, NH4+-N, TN, and NO3−-N can be measured by spectrophotometry [19]. TP can react with inorganic phosphorus and other substances under acidic conditions to form complexes with a blue state. The TP content can be inferred by measuring the complex using spectrophotometry [20]. Similarly, NH4+-N can also generate complexes that can be measured by spectrophotometry under specific conditions, and the NH4+-N content can be calculated based on this [21]. TN uses potassium persulfate as an oxidant under alkaline conditions to oxidize organic nitrogen compounds in water samples into nitrates, and uses ultraviolet spectrophotometry to measure absorbance to calculate the TN content [22]. The absorbance of NO3−-N can also be determined by ultraviolet spectrophotometry. TSS is measured by the weight method, which involves filtering and drying the water sample to a constant weight to calculate its concentration.

Results

The removal effect of pollution factors under various pollution concentrations

The removal rates of pollution factor TP under different pollution concentrations are displayed in Fig. 1. From Figs. 1 (a) and (b), the TP removal rate of the pollution factor in the EG exceeded that of the CG at various pollution concentrations (P < 0.05). From Fig. 1 (a), under surface flow conditions, different pollution concentrations had little effects on TP removal efficiency. EG 3 had the highest TP removal rate, with an average of 90.04%, 88.16%, and 97.01% at high, medium, and low pollution concentrations, respectively. From Fig. 1 (b), under seepage conditions and different pollution concentrations, the TP removal rate of EG 3 was still the highest, at 88.34%, 85.64%, and 84.51%. Besides, with the increase of pollution concentration, the removal rates of TP in the CG and EG also increased (P < 0.05).

The influence of surface infiltration flow ratio on the removal rate of pollution factors

The effect of surface IFR on the removal rate of nitrogen pollution factors is shown in Fig. 6. From Fig. 6, as the surface IFR ratio increased, the removal rates of various nitrogen pollution factors showed a negative correlation trend. From Fig. 6 (a), when the surface IFR was 0.2 and 1.0, the removal rates of pollution factor TN were 91.43% and 77.58%, respectively, a decrease of 13.85% (P < 0.01, R²= 0.231). A lower surface IFR is more conducive to remove TN. A lower flow rate prolongs the residence time of water in the treatment system, enhancing biodegradation and physicochemical adsorption. From Fig. 6 (b), when the surface IFR was 0.2 and 1.0, the removal rates of the pollution factor NO3−-N were 87.61% and 82.34%, respectively, with a decrease of 5.27% (P < 0.05, R²= 0.136). Although the decrease is small, the increase in surface IFR still significantly reduces the removal rate of NO3−-N. From Fig. 6 (c), when the surface IFR was 0.2 and 1.0, the removal rates of NH4+-N pollution factors were 92.45% and 78.15%, with a decrease of 14.30% (P < 0.01, R²= 0.285). The surface IFR has a relatively large impact on the removal rate of R², because NH4+-N is more easily adsorbed and transformed by microorganisms at low flow rates.

Discussion

With the development of social economy, the demand for natural resources by humans is increasing. Urban construction and resource development have exacerbated water pollution. Especially in the urban construction, the impermeable layers have led to increasingly serious urban non-point source pollution problems [23, 24]. During rainfall, pollution factors in the city will enter the water body with runoff, exacerbating the urban water pollution. Research has shown that vegetation filter belts purify runoff through physical, chemical, and biological processes such as sedimentation, filtration, dilution, infiltration, and absorption, thereby reducing non-point source pollution [25]. Vegetation filter strips usually include multiple vegetation such as grasslands, trees, shrubs, etc. These vegetation help slow down water flow velocity, increase the contact area between water flow and soil, and improve the removal efficiency of pollutants [26]. The main functions of vegetation filter belts include reducing the pollution of agricultural wastewater on water bodies, consolidating soil, stabilizing riverbanks, and protecting biodiversity [27]. Vegetation filter strips originated in the United States as measures for preventing and controlling non-point source pollution, which have been widely used in research on soil and water conservation and non-point source pollution prevention [28]. Therefore, this study conducts comparative experiments on vegetation filter strips prepared for different plants to analyze their removal effects on pollution factors and runoff reduction effects, to provide support and reference for ecological restoration technology. Although vegetation filtration technology itself is not a new concept, this study has clarified the superior performance of Adiantum capillus veneris under typical urban pollution concentrations through multi pollutant and multi gradient experiments, and quantified the impact of surface IFR on removal efficiency, providing specific references for vegetation screening and hydraulic condition regulation in urban ecological restoration.

This study found that under different pollution concentrations, the removal effect of the EG with vegetation cover exceeded that of the CG without vegetation cover. The removal rate also improved with the increase of pollution concentration. EG 3, which used Adiantum capillus-veneris as the remediation zone, performed the best in removing nitrogen, phosphorus, and suspended solids. It indicates that Adiantum capillus-veneris has a significantly better retention effect on pollution factors than other plants. The CG without soil cover also had a removal rate of over 50% for various pollution factors, because the soil itself has retention effects on pollutants. The vegetation filter significantly improves the runoff reduction rate, thereby enhancing the removal efficiency of pollutants. From the perspective of runoff reduction effect, the runoff reduction rate of the EG exceeded that of the CG, especially the best reduction effect of EG 3, reaching 63.67%. This indicates that vegetation coverage can effectively reduce surface runoff, enhance water infiltration, and reduce surface runoff loss. In the CG, runoff mainly existed in the form of surface flow. In each EG, seepage was the main form, further proving that vegetation coverage improved the infiltration capacity of surface water. From the perspective of the removal rate of pollution factors, as the runoff intensity decreased, the removal rates of TSS and TP significantly increased by 22.97% and 20.04%, respectively. This is because low-intensity runoff prolongs the residence time of water in the filter strip, enhancing physical sedimentation and biodegradation processes. From the correlation analysis, the correlation coefficients between runoff and NH4+-N, TN, and TP were all above 0.9, indicating a significant correlation between runoff reduction rate and the removal rate of these pollutants. This means that increasing the runoff reduction rate directly enhances the interception and removal efficiency of nitrogen and phosphorus pollutants. The increase in surface IFR significantly reduces the removal efficiency of nitrogen, phosphorus, and suspended solid pollution factors. This is mainly because the lower surface IFR prolongs the residence time of water in the treatment system, enhancing the effectiveness of physical sedimentation, chemical adsorption, and microbial degradation processes. Therefore, in practical applications, to improve the efficiency of pollutant removal, it is recommended to use a lower surface IFR. This study confirms the superior performance of Adiantum capillus veneris in removing various pollutants, which is consistent with the conclusion obtained by Chawla et al. [10] that plant remediation is a key technology for improving the health of aquatic ecosystems, and further clarifies the practical potential of ferns as an efficient species. Meanwhile, the significant control effect of surface IFR on purification efficiency reveals a core mechanism: Vegetation filter belts enhance biogeochemical processes by slowing down water flow and prolonging hydraulic retention time. This provides a key procedural explanation for the study proposed by Begum et al. [9] on using biological indicators to evaluate river health. Therefore, the quantitative results of this study not only respond to the call of Zhao M et al. [8] for sustainable ecological restoration strategies, but also implement macro strategies into specific vegetation screening and hydraulic regulation parameters, promoting the substantial transformation of urban water pollution control from end of pipe treatment to ecological management. It should be pointed out that this study reveals the purification efficiency and regulatory mechanism of vegetation filter belts on typical pollutants under controlled conditions, but does not include macro variables such as climate change and regional land use differences. These factors will significantly affect vegetation growth and hydrodynamic processes in field applications, thereby regulating the long-term stability and universality of the technology. Therefore, in practical engineering design and promotion, it is necessary to adaptively adjust the parameters obtained in this article based on the local climate background and underlying surface characteristics.

The results of this study are applied in a broader field, and its significance goes beyond a simple comparison of four herbaceous plants. This study provides empirical support and specific design references for nature-based solutions and the application of green infrastructure in urban water management by quantifying the removal efficiency of vegetation filter belts for traditional pollutants and their key regulatory parameters (surface IFR). Especially in high-density urban areas, this study confirms that even narrow vegetation belts can significantly improve water quality and regulate runoff, providing feasibility for flexibly embedding ecological restoration measures into the grey infrastructure of “sponge cities”. In addition, the identified advantageous plant Adiantum capillus veneris not only has high purification efficiency, but also has good landscape aesthetic value. This suggests that in future ecological engineering, both functionality and aesthetics can be considered, promoting a shift from single pollution control to comprehensive ecological services and spatial quality improvement. Therefore, the findings of this study contribute to the evolution of urban water pollution control from the “end of pipe treatment” model dominated by grey engineering to a more resilient “ecological management” model.

In summary, reasonably designing and implementing vegetation filter strips can not only effectively reduce runoff, but also significantly improve pollutant removal efficiency. Therefore, to further optimize the removal efficiency of pollutants in the future, measures such as increasing vegetation cover and optimizing filter belt design can be taken to improve the runoff reduction rate, thereby improving the interception and removal efficiency of pollutants. This study not only provides direct vegetation screening and design basis for ecological restoration in high-density urban areas, but also establishes a purification efficiency evaluation framework and key control parameters, laying a necessary theoretical foundation and methodological support for further exploring the interception and transformation potential of vegetation filter belts for emerging pollutants in the future. However, the study only analyzes common pollution factors such as nitrogen and phosphorus in urban sewage, and the experimental results are still not comprehensive enough. Therefore, in future work, based on the efficiency evaluation framework and key hydraulic parameters established in this study, typical emerging pollutants should be further included as detection indicators, and macro factors such as climate change and land use should be coupled to construct a more comprehensive and forward-looking urban water ecological restoration technology system.

Conclusions

In summary, the application of vegetation restoration technology in urban water pollution ecological restoration has confirmed the effectiveness of vegetation restoration technology in runoff reduction and pollutant removal, providing support and reference for urban water pollution control. The findings of this study have direct operational significance for urban water pollution control in the real world. The efficient vegetation and key design parameters verified by the research provide specific and quantifiable basis for designing and constructing efficient and small-scale vegetation filtration systems in limited urban spaces. In terms of scalability, this modular solution can serve as a distributed management strategy, forming a networked pollution interception system by “point-like” layout at multiple key runoff inflow points in the city, thereby achieving large-scale control of non-point source pollution. Compared with traditional grey infrastructure, vegetation filter belts have significant cost-effectiveness advantages, with maintenance costs much lower than the operating and energy consumption costs of grey facilities. In addition, vegetation filter belts can simultaneously provide synergistic benefits such as enhancing biodiversity, beautifying urban landscapes, and mitigating heat island effects.
